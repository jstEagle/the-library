## 2. Low Tide, High Trouble

The river told Yara things it told no one else, and on the morning of the ninth of Frostmoon, it told them something impossible.

They had been awake since the gray hour, as they always were, hands working the mooring lines before their mind fully arrived in the day. The *Kestrel* sat low in the water with her hold full of millstone grit and salted pork, bound downriver to Ferrow's Landing, and the Verre moved beneath her hull in that slow, brown, muscular way it had in autumn — a river thinking its long thoughts, carrying the season's first cold down from the high country where Yara had never been and never intended to go.

They were cinching the last line when they noticed the birds.

Gulls, mostly, and the mud-hens that worked the shallows below Widow's Bend, a half-mile downstream. There were too many of them, and they were wrong in their behavior — not wheeling and crying over a school of fish, but standing. Standing on ground that should have been ten feet under the current, heads cocked, picking at a mudflat that had no business existing.

Yara had run this stretch of the Verre for eleven years. They knew its sandbars the way other people knew the lines of their own palms: the shifting shoal below the old ferry pilings, the snag that surfaced only in drought years, the deep channel that hugged the eastern bluff where the water ran green and cold even in high summer. The river was a ledger they kept in their body, and every entry balanced. The flats below Widow's Bend were never dry. Not in the worst drought their father had ever described, not in the stories the oldest pilots told over cheap brandy at Ferrow's Landing, stories that got more impossible with each retelling. The Verre did not give up that ground. It was a law, like the debt at the guildhouse was a law, like winter coming down from the Sable Range was a law.

Yara stood at the rail with a mooring line still in their hands and watched the water pull back.

It was not dramatic, at first. That was the unsettling part. The river withdrew the way a person withdraws from a conversation — gradually, then all at once. The current slackened, eddies reversing, and then the water simply began to leave, sliding away downstream in long glassy sheets, uncovering gravel bar after gravel bar, exposing the pale ribs of deadfall and the sunken hulks of two old barges Yara had only ever known as hazards marked on charts with little inked crosses and the word *wreck*.

By midmorning, the flats below Widow's Bend lay open to the sky for a quarter mile, steaming faintly in the cold sun, and the whole river town of Millbrook had turned out on the bluff to look at it.

Yara did not go to the bluff. Yara went down to the flats.

* * *

The mud took their boots to the ankle and held them with a wet, sucking reluctance, as though the ground itself were reluctant to give up what it had kept. Yara walked anyway, past the gulls, past the stranded fish gasping in the shrinking pools, past a drowned fence row that must have been a hundred years old, its posts black and soft as cheese. The smell was enormous — river-bottom smell, old silt and rot and minerals, the smell of a place that had been holding its breath.

Other people had come down too, of course. Salvagers with their buckets. Boys from the mill with their trousers rolled, whooping, throwing things. A knot of guild men in their gray-and-green sashes standing at the edge of the flats with clipboards, already arguing about salvage rights, because the guild men could make a claim on anything, including, apparently, a miracle.

Yara walked past all of them, and kept walking, because they had seen it.

The cave mouth was at the base of Widow's Bend itself, where the bluff came down steep and red into the waterline. It was a black arch, maybe eight feet at its highest, half its mouth still curtained by the retreating water, which poured out of it in a thin, strange waterfall, as though the river were draining out of the hill's throat. The stone around the arch was slick and dark and veined with something pale — old shell, old lime, the bones of a sea that had never been in the Verre valley in anyone's memory.

Yara stood before it for a long moment, hands on hips, breath fogging.

Every sensible instinct they owned said *no*. The tide — because that was the only word for it, though the Verre had no tides, had never had tides, the sea was four hundred miles away — every sensible instinct said the water would come back, and it would come back fast, and caves that drank rivers did not give them back politely. The *Kestrel* was loaded and waiting. There was a delivery window, and a penalty clause, and Odile Voss's clerks did not forgive penalty clauses.

And beneath all of that, older and less sensible than any of it, was a voice that sounded like their mother's, twenty years gone, saying: *when the river shows you its bones, Yara, you look. You always look.*

Yara went back for the skiff.

* * *

They rowed in standing up, punting off the gravel with the boathook, because the water in the arch was barely a foot deep and thick with stirred silt. The light failed within six feet of the entrance. Beyond that it was black stone and the sound of the thin waterfall behind them and the drip, drip, drip of a hundred small seeps, and the cold coming off the rock like a held note.

Yara lit the lantern they'd had the sense to bring and held it high.

The cave was not large. It ran back perhaps forty feet, the floor rising gently, the ceiling coming down to meet it, and the whole chamber had the look of a place the river used and abandoned long ago — an old channel, a throat the water had once worn and then forgotten. The walls were marked with the pale shell-veins, and here and there with darker marks that Yara chose not to examine too closely, because they might have been handprints, and they might have been centuries old, and either answer was more than they wanted on a working morning.

The chest sat at the far end, on a natural shelf of stone above the old waterline, as though someone had carried it in and set it there deliberately, out of reach of any ordinary flood.

It was an iron-strapped chest, small, the wood gone silver-gray with age. It was not crusted with a century of silt. That was the detail Yara's mind kept snagging on, turning over, refusing to file. The chest was *clean*. Someone had kept it clean, or it had been placed here recently, or the river had kept it the way some people kept a grave.

And on the lid, burned into the wood with a steady hand, were three letters:

**A.O.**

Yara did not touch it for a long time.

They stood in the black cold with the lantern trembling slightly in their grip — they noticed the trembling and hated it — and they looked at those two initials, and the river roared very quietly in their ears, which was strange, because the river was barely a trickle behind them.

Adaeze Okafor. A.O. Twenty years dead, by every account that counted. Drowned, they said, or lost, or taken by the mountain — the stories changed depending on who told them and how much they'd had to drink, but the ending never changed. Gone. Vanished on an expedition into the Sable Range with a hired guide and six others, chasing a pass that most sensible people said didn't exist, and Yara — eleven years old, left behind with an aunt in Millbrook who took the guild's stipend and said *your mother was a foolish woman* — had learned to stop asking.

Yara had learned a great many things in twenty years. They had learned to read water and weather and the faces of guild clerks. They had learned that the debt their mother left was real and growing and would outlive them if they let it. They had learned to answer to whatever the world called them and to correct it quietly, once, and let it go if the correction didn't take. They had built a life on the *Kestrel* that was narrow as a coffin and snug as one too, and they had told themselves for a long time that snug was enough.

And now here was a box in a hole in the earth with their mother's initials on it, and every snug wall Yara had ever built around themselves developed a crack at once.

*Don't,* they told themselves. *It's a coincidence. Half the women on this river are A.O.s. Ama Osei. Old Ansa Odom. It's nothing. It's salvage, and the guild men up on the flats are already arguing about salvage, and if they see you carrying this chest out they'll attach it against the debt, and—*

Their hand was already on the lid.

The iron was cold enough to burn. The straps were sound. There was no lock — there was a hasp, and folded into the hasp, wedged there with a care that made Yara's chest hurt, was a strip of oilcloth. Someone had wrapped the hasp so it wouldn't rust shut. Someone had expected this chest to wait a long time, and had wanted it to open easily when it did.

Yara unwrapped the oilcloth. The hasp lifted without a sound.

Inside: dry straw, packed with a housekeeper's thoroughness. A bundle of oilcloth larger than the rest, tied with cord. And on top of it, face-down, a small square of paper gone brown with age — a river pilot's license, the old kind, the kind issued before the guild standardized them, with a name inked across it in a hand Yara knew better than their own.

*Adaeze Okafor. Licensed pilot, Verre and tributaries.*

Her license. Her *pilot's license*, which she would never have surrendered while alive, which should have gone into the guild's archive when she was declared dead, which had no business in a cave under Widow's Bend unless she had put it there herself.

Yara sat down on the cold stone without deciding to. The lantern light swung and steadied. Somewhere behind them, out at the mouth of the cave, a gull screamed, and the sound came in off the water and echoed strangely off the shell-veined walls, and Yara sat in their mother's grave-room and did not move for what felt like a long season.

They were not a person who cried easily. They had trained that out of themselves early, the way you train a boat to answer the tiller, small correction by small correction. But their eyes burned now, and they let them, because there was no one to see, and because some part of them understood — in the way you understand a river by feeling the pull against the oars — that whatever this was, it was not an ending. It was a current, and they had already been caught in it, and the only question left was where it meant to take them.

* * *

They opened the oilcloth bundle back on the *Kestrel*, in the stern cabin with the door shut and the lamp trimmed low, because some things you do not do in front of gulls and guild men.

The straw had kept the contents dry. There was a woman's coat, good wool, moth-eaten but whole, too small for Yara and smelling of stone and cold air rather than river water. There was a leather roll of tools — climbing tools, ice tools, the iron toothed things Yara had only ever seen on the belts of the range guides who came down to Ferrow's Landing in spring with frost still in their beards. And there was a compass.

It was a beautiful thing and a wrong thing, and Yara knew it was wrong before they ever touched it, because the needle was moving. Moving steadily. Not swinging the way a needle swings when you carry it — but *turning*, slow and deliberate, around and around, like a slow animal pacing the inside of its cage, and every time it paused, it paused pointing the same direction: south and west. Inland. Toward the Sable Range, which stood up behind the western hills on clear days like a wall at the end of the world, its high pass hidden in cloud.

Yara turned the compass in their hands. North, the needle ignored. They turned it upside down, sideways, held it near the iron stove — the needle did not so much as tremble from its purpose. South and west. Toward the mountains. The way, Yara thought with a cold spreading under their ribs, that a dog strains toward a home it has been taken away from. Or the way a needle might point at a person, if a needle could be made to love.

There was a letter too, folded into the coat's inner pocket, and Yara took it out with hands that had gone clumsy, and that was when the knock came at the cabin door — two raps, crisp, official, the knock of a person who has never in their life waited to be invited.

"Captain Okafor." A woman's voice, level and pleasant and carrying the particular warmth of a banked fire in a room you are not permitted to leave. "Odile Voss, Ferry Guild. Might I have a word about your salvage?"

Yara looked down at the letter in their hands. Their mother's handwriting on the outside, their own name — the name they'd been born with, the name they'd spent years setting down and picking back up — written in faded ink across the front.

They slid it, unopened, into their shirt, against their skin, where it lay like a coal.

"One moment," Yara called, and blew out the lamp, and put their hand flat on the compass until the needle stilled beneath their palm, still straining south and west, still pointing at whatever was left of their mother at the end of the world.
