## 3. The Compass That Lies

The chest came open the way old things do — reluctantly, with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

Yara had carried it up from the cave at first light, when the tide was still thinking about coming back. It was heavier than it looked, that kind of weight that isn't wood or iron but history, and they had rowed it out to the *Marrow* with hands that wouldn't quite steady, then sat in the stern for a long while doing nothing but listening to their own pulse. The cave mouth behind them had swallowed itself again by midmorning, the sea climbing back over the rocks at Widow's Bend as if nothing had ever been exposed there, as if the low tide had been a mistake the ocean was eager to correct.

Now, past noon, with the boat tied off in a cove where no guild patrol bothered to look, Yara set the chest on the deck boards between their knees and worked the hasps.

The lock had rusted through years ago. The lid fought them anyway.

Inside: a smell like cedar and cold stone and something faintly sweet, dried flowers maybe, or paper that had given up trying to be anything else. A folded oilcloth. Beneath it, two objects, laid side by side with a care that said someone had placed them there on purpose, in a hurry, but on purpose all the same.

The first was a letter. Or what remained of one. Half of it was gone — burned away along one edge, the char so clean it looked deliberate, like whoever destroyed it had lost their nerve halfway through, or changed their mind about which half mattered more. The surviving portion was written in a hand Yara knew.

They knew it the way you know a scar. Not because you look at it often, but because it's yours.

Their mother's handwriting slanted left, letters crowded together like people sheltering from rain. Yara had seen it last on a shopping list pinned to the door of a house they'd been taken from at six years old, and before that on the inside cover of a storybook, *for my little current*, and the sight of it now — twenty years later, risen out of a drowned cave like everything else the sea gives back when it feels like it — hit them somewhere below the ribs, in the place where grief keeps its accounts.

They didn't read it yet. They couldn't. Their eyes kept sliding off the ink the way water slides off oiled cloth.

So they picked up the other thing instead.

* * *

It was a compass.

Brass, palm-sized, heavier than any compass had a right to be, its casing worn smooth at the edges by years of a thumb worrying it — and Yara felt an absurd, unwelcome kinship with that absent thumb, because their own found the worn spot immediately, fit into it like a key into a lock. The face was covered by a hinged lid, and the hinge still moved, silent, well-oiled against all reason after two decades underwater. Someone had maintained this instrument. Someone had loved it.

Yara opened the lid.

The needle swung.

Not the way needles swing — not the lazy, damped oscillation toward north that every compass Yara had ever owned performed, the honest little servant pointing home to magnetic truth. This needle moved like something alive. It swept once around the full circle, fast, then snapped back and *held*, quivering at the tip, straining toward —

Yara turned. The needle turned with them, correcting, insistent.

Toward the mountains.

They were sitting in a cove on the Verre River, forty miles from the nearest slope worth the name, and the needle pointed unerringly east-northeast, upriver, inland, into the high country where the range stood up white and indifferent against the sky. Yara rotated the compass a quarter turn. The needle swung smoothly, without wobble, back to its bearing. They walked it around the deck — three steps to port, four to starboard, holding it level, watching — and the needle never faltered, never drifted, never once glanced toward north the way a proper compass should.

"Where are you going?" Yara asked it quietly, and hated how much their voice shook.

Compasses didn't lie. That was the whole covenant of the thing — you trusted a compass the way you trusted a river, because both went where physics sent them and neither cared what you wanted. A compass that ignored north wasn't a broken tool. It was a different kind of tool entirely. And tools like that answered to something other than the earth's slow magnetism.

Tools like that answered to people. Or were made by them. Or were *meant* to find them.

Yara sat down hard on the cargo boards. The river murmured against the hull. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher rattled its blue laugh and was gone.

They thought about the chest's initials — **A.O.** — cut deep into the lid in a slanted, crowded hand. Adaeze Okafor. Their mother, who had walked out of Yara's life twenty years ago chasing a rumor, a legend, a pass through the mountains called the Sable Current that supposedly ran free when every other road in the world had learned to charge admission. Who had been declared dead two winters later when her expedition's gear washed out of a gorge, though no body had ever come home. Who had left behind a child, a debt, and a hole shaped exactly like herself that Yara had spent twenty years learning to walk around without falling in.

And here was her handwriting. Her initials. Her compass — because whose else could it be? — pointing east-northeast with the insistence of a dog pulling a leash.

Pointing, if Yara let themselves think it, which they were trying very hard not to, at a person.

"No," they said aloud, to the compass, to the cove, to the whole arrangement of the morning. "No, she's dead. Dead is settled. Dead is the one thing the river got right."

The needle quivered at its bearing and did not dignify this with a response.

* * *

They made tea because their hands needed a job. The small spirit stove hissed; the kettle ticked; the ritual of it — measure, pour, wait, the bitter green smell rising — steadied something in their chest that had been listing badly since dawn. Yara had survived twenty years on routines. Routines were ballast. You loaded them in the morning and they kept you upright through whatever weather the day brought.

But even drinking tea, they kept the compass in view, the way you keep an eye on weather that's decided to turn.

The brass casing bore markings. Up close, in the flat noon light, Yara could make out engraving worn nearly to invisibility: a ring of fine ticks around the dial, finer than any navigational scale they'd seen, subdividing the circle into degrees far smaller than a helmsman would ever need. And at the base of the dial, half-hidden under the pivot, a stamped mark — a seal of some kind, a circle enclosing a shape Yara almost recognized. A bridge? A gate? The metal was too rubbed-down to say. It nagged at them, that shape. Like a word on the tip of the tongue.

There was a name engraved inside the lid, tiny, in that same left-slanting hand:

*It doesn't point north. Don't ask it to.*

Yara laughed — one short, cracked note that startled a gull off the pilings. Trust their mother to leave instructions instead of explanations. Twenty years gone and still managing, from beyond all reasonable hope, to tell her child what to do rather than why.

"Fine," Yara muttered. "Then what do you point at?"

The needle held its line east-northeast, trembling slightly, like a hand raised in a classroom that knows the answer and can't understand why no one's calling on it.

* * *

The letter waited. It had waited twenty years in a flooded cave; it could wait another hour. But Yara knew that was cowardice dressed as patience, and they had promised themselves, rowing back from Widow's Bend with the chest between their feet, that they were done being careful about this one thing. Careful had gotten them a boat, a debt, and a life measured out in cargo manifests. Careful was how you survived. It was not how you lived, and lately — since the tide had gone out farther than any tide in living memory, since the sea had peeled back its skirt and shown them a door — careful felt less like wisdom and more like a habit they'd outgrown without noticing.

They unfolded the letter on the oilcloth.

Half of it was simply gone. The burn had taken the top right corner and eaten diagonally down, so the lines began intact at the left margin and dissolved into char partway across, sentences amputated mid-word. What remained was enough.

*My child —*

Yara stopped breathing for a moment. Then made themselves continue.

*My child, if you are reading this, then the sea has done what I asked it to, or failed to, depending on how you count such things. I told it to keep you safe. I did not tell it to keep you ignorant. I find, writing this, that I cannot decide which I wanted more.*

*I have found the Current. It is real — not the story, but the thing under the story. A pass through the Sable Teeth that belongs to no guild, charges no toll, and answers only to the weather. There are people who mean to change that. They will call it stewardship. They will call it maintenance. They will put a gate on it and a price on the gate and call the price fair, and in ten years no one will remember it was ever free.*

*I mean to stand in front of that for as long as I can. I know what this costs you. I know what it cost me to write that sentence. If you are angry — be angry. Anger is honest. It is better than the alternative, which is to grow up believing you were not worth staying for. You were. You are. That was never the question.*

*The compass will find me. It was made for that — [burned] — the needle does not point north, it points — [burned] — do not let Voss take it. Whatever she offers, whatever she threatens, the needle must not —*

The rest was char.

Yara read it twice. Three times. The fourth time they weren't really reading; they were just looking at the words, letting them settle into the sediment of themselves, displacing twenty years of carefully deposited certainty.

*Voss.* Odile Voss. Everyone on the Verre knew the name — guild captain, enforcer, the woman whose climbers had "found" the wreckage of the Sable Current expedition in that gorge, the woman who had personally delivered the news to the Okafor household, hat in hand, voice like poured honey: *I'm so sorry. The mountain took her. The mountain takes us all eventually.* Yara had been six. They remembered Voss's gloves. Fine grey leather. They remembered thinking, even at six, that a woman wearing gloves that fine had no business looking sorry about anything.

And now their mother's letter, half-burned, said *do not let Voss take it* in a hand that shook on the final word.

The past hadn't knocked. Yara understood that now, sitting in the cove with the tea gone cold in its cup. The past had picked the lock, come inside, and sat down at the table, and it was waiting for Yara to acknowledge it.

* * *

The rest of the afternoon passed in the particular trance of a life rearranging itself. Yara hauled anchor and moved the *Marrow* upriver a few miles, mostly to be moving, mostly because sitting still felt suddenly dangerous. They checked the compass at every bend. East-northeast. Always east-northeast, correcting patiently whenever Yara turned it, faithful to a bearing that led away from the river, away from the cargo runs and the debt ledger and the berth they rented by the month, away from everything they had built out of the wreckage of their childhood — and toward the Sable Teeth, white on the horizon, growing whiter as autumn leaned in.

Snows would close the passes within weeks. Everyone knew that. It was the one law older than the ferry guild.

They ate standing up. They didn't taste it.

What kept circling was the sentence their mother had chosen to survive the fire: *You were. You are. That was never the question.* Twenty years Yara had built a life on the assumption that the answer to the unasked question was *no* — not worth staying for, not worth writing to, not worth coming back for. They had made peace with it the way you make peace with a limp: completely, functionally, and never once actually. And now a scrap of burned paper was telling them the limp had been unnecessary. That the question had always had a different answer, and the answer had been sealed in a box at the bottom of a cave, waiting for a tide unprecedented in living memory to expose it.

Either that, or it was a crueler trick than any the river had ever played.

Yara weighed the possibilities the way they weighed cargo — honestly, without sentiment. Option one: their mother was alive, somewhere east-northeast, dying or hiding or guarding something, and had spent two decades silent by choice. That choice was its own wound, and Yara would need years to decide whether forgiveness was even on the table. Option two: the compass and letter were bait — planted, seeded, arranged for a low tide that someone had predicted, left for the one person in the Verre delta with the initials to match. But bait for what? Voss's enforcers already had salvage rights over anything pulled from the Bend; if the guild had planted the chest, they'd hardly have left it for Yara to find first. Unless finding it first was the point. Unless the trap wasn't the chest but the direction it pointed — into the high country, into guild territory, where a debtor with no family and no witnesses could vanish as thoroughly as her mother had.

Both options ended with Yara walking into the mountains. That was the ugly symmetry of it. Hope and trap wore the same boots.

The light was going amber when Teo's face surfaced in their thoughts — the cartographer's apprentice from the guild office, the young man with ink-stained fingers who had watched Yara load cargo last week with the hungry attention of someone memorizing an escape route. He'd asked odd questions. About the high country. About old survey routes. He'd flinched, visibly, when Voss's clerks came near.

Yara filed the thought away without deciding anything about it. One problem at a time. The river taught you that too: you don't fight the whole flood, you fight the water in front of your hull.

* * *

Dusk. They anchored in the lee of a willow island, lit no lamp, and sat in the dark with the compass open on their knee, watching the needle catch the last grey light.

East-northeast. Unblinking.

"You'd better be worth it," Yara told the dark, meaning the mountains, meaning the letter, meaning the woman who had written *be angry* and signed nothing because the fire had taken her name along with everything else. "One of you had better be worth it."

The needle trembled at its bearing, patient as tide, certain as current.

And downstream, faint but unmistakable on the evening air, came the sound Yara had been dreading all day: the low thrum of a guild launch engine, running without lights, moving upriver toward the cove at Widow's Bend — toward the cave that was no longer there, toward the salvage that had already been claimed, toward the one boat on the water carrying a chest marked **A.O.**

Yara closed the compass lid and started weighing anchor in the dark.
