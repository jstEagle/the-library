## 4. Guild Men at the Dock

The compass had not stopped pointing at the mountains.

Yara had tried to make it stop. They had turned it face-down beneath a coil of rope, buried it in the flour sack, wedged it behind the iron stove in the *Marrow's* cramped cabin where the needle could not possibly know which way was anything. Each time they had gone back to look — and each time, through brass and flour and iron, the needle swung its slow, patient arc until it found the northwest again, and held there, trembling faintly, like a dog scenting a road it meant to walk whether anyone followed or not.

Two days since Widow's Bend. Two days of running cargo downriver with their hands doing the work their mind refused to do: checking lashings, minding the soundings, trading salt pork and lamp oil at the landing stages while the needle pulled at them from below decks like a second heartbeat. The river did what the river always did. It asked nothing of Yara but attention, and attention was the one thing they could not give it.

They were moored at Tallowmouth on the third morning when the guild came for them.

Yara knew before the boat touched the pilings. You learned to read a dock the way you read water — the weight of men standing on planks that should have been empty, the way gulls lift off all at once when something two-legged and wrong comes up the gangway ramp. Three figures in grey oilskins stood at the head of the Tallowmouth pier, and though the guild wore no uniform, they might as well have. There was a way guild men stood: feet planted, hands loose, faces arranged into the particular blankness of people who had been paid to be patient.

And behind them, at the landward end of the dock, a fourth figure who was not patient at all.

Yara tied off the stern line first, then the bow, taking the extra seconds because the extra seconds were theirs to take. Then they straightened, wiped their palms on their trousers, and walked up the ramp into the cold morning light.

"Captain Okafor." The man in front spoke first. He was broad, red-faced, with the broken-veined cheeks of a man who drank his wages, and he wore his guild badge on a chain outside his coat so no one could mistake him for anything honest. "Ferry Guild, Verre District. Name's Hobb. This is Ferren." A nod at the younger one, lean and quiet, whose eyes never left Yara's hands. "We'd like a word about salvage."

"Salvage," Yara said.

"The cave at Widow's Bend." Hobb smiled as if they were sharing a joke. "Exposed by the low tide. Anything raised from unclaimed waters belongs to the guild under charter law. You'll want to declare your findings."

"I found mud," Yara said. "And a dead seal. I left both."

Hobb's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was how Yara knew the conversation had already happened without them — already been decided somewhere warm, over ledgers, before Hobb ever smelled the river. Behind the three enforcers, the fourth figure began to walk.

She came up the dock the way cold water comes into a bilge: quietly, from below, filling every space. She was perhaps fifty, tall, dressed in good grey wool cut for travel rather than ceremony, and she moved like someone who had spent her life being obeyed and had long ago stopped noticing it. Her hair was iron-grey, bound back tight. Her eyes were pale, almost colourless, and they went past Yara entirely — swept the *Marrow* stem to stern, the cargo netting, the stovepipe, the patched jib — the way an assessor looks at a thing she is already pricing.

"Captain Okafor." Her voice was low and even, with a rasp underneath like a hull dragging sand. "Odile Voss. District captain."

Yara had heard the name the way river folk heard weather warnings — often enough to be tired of it, never enough to be ready. Voss ran the middle Verre for the guild. Toll schedules, cargo levies, the debt collectors who had taken Yara's father's second boat and would have taken the *Marrow* too if the arithmetic had ever quite worked out in their favour. Twenty years of stories, and not one of them kind, and not one of them stupid.

"Captain Voss." Yara kept their voice flat. "I'm behind on nothing. My quarter payment isn't due till—"

"I know when your payments are due." Voss stopped an arm's length away. Close enough that Yara could see the fine scarring at the edge of her jaw, old and white, like something had once tried to take her throat and settled for a taste. "I'm not here about your debt."

"No?"

"You went into the cave at Widow's Bend."

It was not a question, and Yara understood suddenly that it could never be a question again. The tide charts were guild property. The bend was guild-watched — a toll point, a choke in the river where every hull paid its passage. Someone had seen the *Marrow* anchored there through the freak low water. Someone had seen the rowboat go in. Or perhaps it was simpler than that, worse than that: perhaps the guild simply knew everything the river knew, because the river had learned to tell it everything.

"I looked," Yara said. "There was nothing worth looking at."

"There was a chest."

The wind came off the water and pushed between them, smelling of tar and coming frost. Down the dock, Hobb shifted his weight, and the young one, Ferren, let his hand drift toward his belt with the unhurried confidence of a man who had never once needed to hurry.

Yara thought about lying properly. About the flour sack and the stove and the coil of rope, and the small brass weight of the thing sleeping below decks, pulling northwest, pulling northwest, always northwest. And then they thought about what Voss's eyes were doing — reading them, reading the pause, reading the half-second too long — and understood that this woman had made a life out of other people's half-seconds.

"A chest," Yara said. "Rotted through. Empty."

Voss studied them for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled. It was not a cruel smile. That was the worst part. It was almost warm, the way a ledger is warm — balanced, complete, everything in its column.

"May I see your boat, Captain?"

* * *

She went through the *Marrow* like a tide herself.

Yara stood in the companionway and watched Odile Voss work, and hated how good she was. She touched nothing roughly. She lifted the flour sack and set it back with the seam aligned. She opened the locker where the spare line lived, ran her thumb along the planking, checked the bilge with a glance rather than a lantern. She was not searching for the compass. Yara realized that halfway through, and the realization sat in their stomach like swallowed anchor-chain. She was searching for what else the compass might mean — letters, papers, proof of anything — and finding none of it, she seemed, if anything, satisfied.

At last she stood in the little cabin, under the swinging lamp, and turned to face Yara in the doorway.

"My mother sailed this stretch," Voss said. "Did you know that? Before the charter. When the passes were open and the ferries ran free and everyone starved equally." Her tone did not change; it was the flattest sentence Yara had ever heard carry that much weight. "People forget what the river was like before the guild. Feuds. Wrecks. No charts worth trusting, no law worth the name. The guild brought order. Order has costs. Everyone pays them, and mostly everyone is better for it."

"And if they're not?"

"Then they pay anyway." She said it without apology, and somehow that was more honest than anything Hobb had said, and Yara hated her a little for the honesty. "You owe the guild four hundred and sixty crowns, Captain Okafor. Your father owed three hundred when he died, and interest has done its quiet work since. At your current margins, you will clear that debt in eleven years, assuming no bad seasons. You've had six bad seasons out of the last ten."

Yara said nothing. The numbers were right. That was the trap of the guild — it never lied to you. It only told you the truth in a voice that made the truth feel like a hand around your wrist.

"So here is my offer." Voss reached into her coat and produced a purse — soft leather, heavy, the particular heaviness of real money counted out in advance. She set it on the chart table between them. "One hundred crowns. For the contents of the chest. Whatever they were."

"They were nothing."

"Then one hundred crowns for nothing is generous, and a woman eleven years from solvency can't afford to refuse generosity." Voss's pale eyes held steady. "Or — refuse. Keep your nothing. And I return to Tallowmouth and file a salvage claim against the *Marrow*, which the magistrate will honour, because the magistrate is paid by the same people who pay me. The claim takes your boat. The debt transfers to you personally. You will spend the next twenty years working guild docks for guild wages to pay for a boat you no longer own." She paused. "I am told the northern quarries are hiring."

The lamp swung gently overhead. Somewhere upriver a barge horn sounded, long and low, and neither of them turned toward it.

"Why?" Yara asked. It came out quieter than they intended. "A rotted chest full of nothing. Why does the district captain come up a dock herself for it?"

For just a moment — a flicker, a hairline crack in plaster — something moved behind Voss's eyes. Not guilt. Something older and more tired than guilt. Recognition, maybe. She looked at Yara the way she had looked at the boat: assessing, pricing, and finding, briefly, something she hadn't expected to find.

"Because twenty-two years ago," Voss said, "a woman named Adaeze Okafor took something out of these mountains that did not belong to her, and the guild has been paying for it ever since. In money. In men. In—" she touched her jaw, lightly, the scarred place, and let her hand fall. "Whatever she left in that cave, Captain, I have spent my career cleaning up after it. I would very much like to be finished."

The name landed in Yara's chest like a dropped cleat. Their mother's name, spoken aloud in this cabin, in this voice, after twenty years of silence — spoken not as grief but as grievance, as a line item, as a cost.

"She drowned," Yara said. "Twenty years ago. Everyone knows it."

"Everyone knows what we wrote down," Voss said. "That is not the same thing, and you are smart enough to know it isn't."

The cabin felt suddenly very small. Yara became aware of their own breathing, of the compass below the floorboards pulling northwest with its patient idiot faith, of the purse sitting on the chart table with its hundred crowns of bloodless mercy.

"I don't have anything of hers," Yara said.

Voss held their gaze a long moment. Then she nodded, once, as if closing an entry.

"Then you'll forgive the intrusion." She stepped past them into the companionway, and paused there, half-turned, the grey light catching the iron in her hair. "The offer stands for six days, Captain. After that, the salvage filing goes to the magistrate, and the magistrate and I are old friends." A beat. "Six days is longer than she'd have given you. Consider that my professional courtesy."

She went up the ramp. Hobb fell in behind her, and Ferren, and the three of them and then the fourth figure walked away down the Tallowmouth dock, and the gulls came back down to the pilings, and the ordinary noise of the ordinary morning resumed as though nothing had passed through it.

* * *

Yara did not touch the purse.

They stood in the cabin until the cold drove them out, and then they stood on deck until the dark drove them below, and the whole time the hundred crowns sat on the chart table like a guest who would not leave. Once, near dusk, they picked it up. It weighed what it weighed. Four hundred and sixty crowns of debt, and here was a fifth of it, handed over freely, needing only one small lie — *take it, say nothing, keep the boat, keep the river, keep the life you built because you had no choice* — and all it would cost was the needle, and whatever the needle was pointing at.

They set it back down.

Below the floorboards, wrapped now in an old shirt, the compass pulled northwest. Yara knelt and lifted the plank and looked at it in the lamplight, this small brass inheritance from a mother the world said was drowned and a guild captain said was a debtor of a different kind. The needle trembled against its glass, straining, certain.

"Six days," Yara said aloud, to the boat, to the river, to nobody.

And that was when they heard the thump.

Soft. Forward, near the bow, beneath the tarpaulin over the spare crates — a sound like a knee knocking a hull plank, quickly stifled. Yara went still, one hand drifting to the boathook by the door. The *Marrow* rode quiet at her lines. The dock was empty. The lamp hissed.

Slowly, keeping to the shadows, Yara moved forward along the deck, took a fistful of tarpaulin, and pulled it back.

A pair of wide brown eyes stared up at them out of the dark between the crates — a boy, sixteen maybe, thin as a winter eel, clutching a satchel of rolled paper to his chest like a shield, his mouth opening and closing twice before any words came out.

"Don't give me to them," he whispered. "Please. I know what the letter says."
