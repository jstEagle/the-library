## 5. Teo Jumps Aboard

The guildhouse in Corvane kept its maps the way a miser keeps coins: locked, catalogued, and never touched without three signatures and a witness. Teo Alvarado had spent four years learning to draw coastlines he would never see and mountain ranges he would never climb, copying the work of dead men onto fresh vellum while Master Ellery checked his line weights with a jeweler's loupe and sighed at every wobble.

He had also spent four years learning where the keys were kept.

That was the thing nobody understood about apprenticeship — it wasn't training, it was proximity. You learned the craft, yes, but you learned everything else too: which clerks drank at which taverns, which night watchman dozed after the second bell, which drawer held the correspondence that was supposed to be burned. Teo had a memory like flypaper. Things stuck to it whether he wanted them to or not. The smell of the sealing wax in the chancery. The exact phrasing of Captain Odile Voss's requisition orders. The way her voice went flat and pleasant right before she ruined someone.

And now, folded against his ribs inside a stolen oilskin wrap: three pages of a half-burned letter, copied out in his own careful hand because he hadn't dared take the original.

*Voss must never hold the needle.*

He'd read that line forty times since yesterday, and each time his hands did the same humiliating tremble.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Debt* — and what a name for a boat, Teo thought, what an honest, miserable name — sat low in the water at the Corvane cargo dock, taking on crates of dyed wool under a sky the color of dirty pewter. Teo watched it from behind a stack of salt barrels for most of the afternoon, and what he saw matched everything he'd pulled from the guild's shipping ledgers during the long boring months when they let him file things.

Yara Okafor. Twenty-eight, maybe thirty. Ran cargo on the Verre between Corvane and the delta towns. Carried the ferry guild's debt like a stone sewn into their coat — eleven years old now, per the ledgers, accruing interest at a rate Teo found frankly obscene. No family listed. No crew. A single-hander working a boat that wanted two, which meant Yara Okafor was either very good or very stubborn, and the dock gossip said both.

They moved like the river had taught them personally. Teo watched them read the current coming off the bend, watched them shift a mooring line before the eddy even arrived, anticipation rather than reaction, and felt something twist in his chest that he refused to name. He knew what it looked like when someone loved their work. He'd just never been allowed any of his own.

The plan, such as it was, had assembled itself out of panic and opportunity. Voss had summoned him yesterday evening — not to the chart room but to her private office above the tollhouse, where she kept no maps at all, only a desk, a lamp, and a window facing the river. She'd asked him about salvage law. She'd asked him, casually, terribly, whether he could think of any reason a brass compass might be worth more than its weight in gold to the Ferry Guild. And then she'd shown him the report from her enforcers at Widow's Bend: a cave exposed by an unprecedented low tide, a chest bearing the initials *A.O.*, and a riverboat captain who had rowed out of it carrying something small enough to fit in a pocket.

"I want you to find out what Okafor has," Voss had said, in that flat pleasant voice. "You're clever, Alvarado. Clever people make good listeners. You'll be on the next boat south regardless — I've arranged passage. Consider it a promotion."

She'd smiled when she said *promotion*, and Teo had spent the whole walk home understanding that he was being made into a knife, and that knives got used up.

So instead he'd gone back to the guildhouse after midnight, through the door with the tired lock, and copied the one document in Voss's own files that she didn't know mattered: the marginalia on a twenty-year-old expedition charter, the Sable Current survey, signed by Adaeze Okafor. The margins were full of cipher — a grid cipher, homebrewed, the kind cartographers used for coordinates they didn't want clients reading. Teo had cracked ciphers like it since he was nine years old, alone in the map room while the other apprentices slept. It was the only part of the trade that had ever felt like it belonged to him.

The margins named a pass. The margins named a route. And one line, half-erased by fire damage, repeated like a heartbeat: *the road stays free or there is no road.*

Now all he had to do was get aboard the *Kestrel's Debt* without being caught by either the woman who owned it or the captain who wanted him as her spy.

* * *

Getting aboard was the easy part. Cargo boats took on strays the way rivers took on rain; a boy with a coil of borrowed rope over his shoulder and a look of purpose could walk up any gangplank in Corvane if he walked like he belonged there. Teo stowed himself in the lazarette beneath the stern deck, wedged between a spare tiller and a nest of rotting fenders, and arranged the stolen oilskin around himself like a cocoon.

Then he waited, and waiting turned out to be the hard part.

The boat came alive around him. He heard the mooring lines come off, heard Yara's voice — low, unhurried, talking to the boat itself, a running commentary of small curses and encouragements that Teo found himself memorizing despite every intention not to. The engine — no, not an engine, he realized, listening harder; a chain drive to a stern paddle wheel, hand-cranked for the quiet stretches, which explained the debt and the exhaustion both — clattered into motion. The hull began to speak its long sentence of water, and Teo lay in the dark doing sums.

Three days to the delta town of Pell's Landing, if the wind cooperated. Voss's fast packet would follow within a day. Which meant he had maybe four days to convince a stranger with no reason to trust anyone that he was worth more free than delivered.

He rehearsed it in the dark. *I can read the letter.* No — too abrupt, no context. *I know what you found in the cave.* Worse — threatening. *I'm fleeing Voss and I—* True, but self-pitying, and self-pity was a currency nobody accepted twice.

He was still rehearsing when the hatch above him opened and lamplight fell across his face like a verdict.

* * *

"You've been breathing wrong for an hour," Yara Okafor said.

Teo blinked up at them. They stood silhouetted against the dusk, one hand on the hatch coaming, the other holding a boathook — not raised, exactly, but present, the way a fact is present. Behind them the sky had gone the deep bruised violet of early night on the river, and the first stars were out, and Teo thought absurdly that this was the first time in four years he'd seen the sky from anywhere except a window.

"Breathing," he managed.

"Stowaways breathe shallow. They hold it when the deck creaks, then let it out too fast." Yara tilted their head. "You breathe like you're counting. Also, you sneezed at the second bell. From the dust in my fenders, I'd guess."

Teo sat up, banging his head on the tiller, and decided dignity was beyond recovery. "I need to talk to you about the compass."

The boathook shifted. Not much. But it shifted.

"Careful," Yara said, and their voice had changed — still low, still unhurried, but with something underneath it now, bedrock under topsoil. "Keep talking."

"My name's Teo Alvarado. I'm — I was — an apprentice at the Corvane chart house. Odile Voss knows you have it. She knows about the cave, she knows about the chest, she has enforcers' reports with your boat's name in them, and she sent me to spy on you." He made himself say it all in one breath, because if he stopped he'd start editing, and editing was how liars were born. "I'm telling you that because I'm not going to do it. I copied something out of her files instead. Something about your mother."

The silence that followed had texture. The paddle wheel turned. Somewhere ahead a night bird called across the water, once, and got no answer.

"Sit on the hatch coaming," Yara said finally. "Where I can see you. Hands where I can see them. Then show me what you copied."

Teo climbed out of the lazarette stiffly, every muscle reporting complaints from hours of compression, and settled on the coaming with the oilskin bundle. Unwrapping it felt like undressing in public. Three pages of his own handwriting, dense and small, the cipher transcribed into plain text down the left margin with his reconstruction notes down the right. He'd worked on it for six hours straight and it showed — crossings-out, question marks, one whole section flagged *incomplete, fire damage*.

Yara took the pages without touching him. They read by lamplight, and Teo watched their face while they did, because faces were another thing he'd learned to read in the map room, alone, watching the clerks decide things.

What crossed Yara Okafor's face was not surprise. That was the worst part. It was confirmation — the expression of someone who had carried a suspicion so long it had worn smooth, seeing it suddenly given edges and ink. Their thumb stopped on the line *the road stays free or there is no road* and pressed there, hard, until the paper dimpled.

"She wrote in cipher," Yara said quietly. "All the time. Little codes on grocery lists. I thought it was a game." A pause. "I was eight."

"I'm sorry," Teo said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

"Don't be sorry yet. Be useful." They looked up, and their eyes in the lamplight were steady and appraising and very tired. "Voss sent you. Why should I believe you turned?"

Teo had expected the question. He'd rehearsed answers — noble ones, strategic ones — and found, sitting here under actual stars with an actual person, that he only had the true one.

"Because I've spent four years drawing other people's discoveries for men who sell them back to us," he said. "Because Voss asked me about compasses in a voice she uses when she's already decided someone's going to be hurt. Because—" His voice cracked and he pushed through it, furious at it. "Because I know what those margins say. Your mother hid a free pass across the range, and the guild has spent twenty years building toll gates, and I helped draw the charts they use to tax the roads. Every road. Even the footpaths. There's a fee schedule for the *view* of Mount Verre from the tollhouse balcony, Captain Okafor. Two coppers. I filed it."

The paddle wheel turned. The river ran dark and patient toward the sea.

"You ran away from the guildhouse with paper in your shirt," Yara said at last. "You understand what happens to you if they catch you. What happens to me if I keep you."

"Yes."

"And you got on my boat anyway."

"There wasn't another boat," Teo said. "And even if there was—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Everyone always says there'll be another chance, another boat, another year. I've watched chances get catalogued and shelved my whole life. I didn't want to shelve this one."

Yara studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, they laughed — a short, rough sound, surprised out of them, aimed mostly at themselves.

"A cartographer who jumps," they said. "The river sends me a cartographer who jumps." They folded his pages along his own creases and handed them back. "Here's what's going to happen. You sleep in the lazarette tonight because I haven't decided about you yet. Tomorrow you eat at my table and you tell me everything you know about Voss, the guild, the charter, and that cipher, in that order. If anything you said turns out to be a lie, I put you off at the next landing and I don't slow down."

"That's fair," Teo said.

"It's not fair. It's survival. Fairness is for towns." Yara stood, and paused, and something in their posture eased by a degree — a knot loosening that they'd probably have denied was ever tied. "You said you cracked her cipher. Alone. How old were you when you started doing that?"

"Nine," Teo admitted. "It was the only thing I was good at that nobody wanted done."

Something moved behind Yara's eyes — recognition, quick and unguarded, there and gone like a fish under the hull.

"Yeah," they said. "Me too." They nodded at the lazarette. "Get some sleep, Teo Alvarado. Dawn comes early and the current won't wait."

* * *

He didn't sleep, not really. He lay in the dark among the fenders and listened to the boat live — the tick of the cooling chain drive, the whisper of water along the strakes, Yara's footsteps overhead making their rounds, checking lines, checking him, checking the world. Once, near midnight, he heard them stop directly above the hatch and stay there a long time, and he understood that they were looking at the mountains.

He could feel it too, even lying down. The range was somewhere out there past the eastern shore, invisible in the dark, and the pages against his ribs seemed to lean toward it slightly, the way a compass needle leans, the way everything in this story leaned now — him, the letter, the woman who'd written it, the child she'd left behind who was not a child anymore.

*My child, whoever you become,* the margins hadn't said, but the spirit of them had. Teo had spent four years becoming what the guild house needed: quiet, precise, invisible, useful. Tonight he'd jumped, and jumping was the first thing he'd ever done that nobody had assigned.

Above him, Yara's footsteps moved aft again, unhurried, keeping their own kind of watch. Two loners on one small boat, Teo thought, drifting at last toward sleep. The river carrying them both toward the same mountains.

At dawn, Yara woke him with coffee and one word that changed the shape of everything:

"Tell me what else the letter says."
