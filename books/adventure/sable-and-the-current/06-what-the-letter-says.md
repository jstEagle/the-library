## 6. What the Letter Says

Teo had not slept. He sat cross-legged on the deck of the *Kestrel's Debt* with the half-burned letter spread before him on a board, weighted at the corners with four brass buttons he'd unscrewed from his own coat, and the lantern turned low so the flame wouldn't gutter in the river wind. Around him the boat creaked and ticked as it settled against the pilings, and somewhere above, Yara was pacing — Teo could hear it, that particular rhythm of boots, two steps forward, one step back, like water finding the shape of a channel.

The letter had defeated him for six hours.

Not because it was hard to read. The hand was beautiful, actually — a flowing, confident script that leaned slightly rightward, the kind of penmanship taught by someone who cared about teaching it. Adaeze Okafor had written like a person who expected her words to matter. No, what defeated him was the margins. Down both edges of the page, in columns so narrow they were almost vertical lines, ran strings of characters: numbers mixed with letters, letters mixed with symbols Teo didn't recognize until he did — and then he felt his whole body go cold, because he recognized them from the guild chart-rooms, from the locked drawers of Master Ferren's desk, from the cipher key that apprentices weren't supposed to know existed but all of them knew existed, the way everyone knows where the keys are kept even if they've never held them.

It was a surveyor's cipher. Guild-made. Guild-taught. And Adaeze Okafor had used it to hide something inside her own letter, which meant she'd known the guild would read it, which meant she'd written it knowing she might be caught, knowing it might be intercepted, and she'd hidden the true message in plain sight, folded into the spaces between sentences like coins sewn into a hem.

Teo flexed his fingers. They ached. He'd been copying charts since he was nine years old; his hands knew how to work past tiredness. He dipped the pen Yara had lent him — a cheap thing, a barge-man's pen, but it wrote — and began again at the top of the left margin.

The trick of the surveyor's cipher was that it wasn't really a cipher at all. It was a selection. Every character in the margin pointed to a word in the main text: the number gave the line, the letter gave the position, and the strange symbols — those were the part Master Ferren had called "the cartographer's mercy" — indicated whether you counted from the start of the line or the end. A map was only ever useful if you could find yourself on it. That was what Ferren said, every term, in his dry dust-of-chalk voice: *A map is only ever useful if you can find yourself on it.* Teo had thought it was about maps. It had taken him six hours to understand it was about this.

He worked. The river breathed against the hull. Once, a night heron cried out over the water, a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and Teo looked up and saw the mountains faintly visible even here, even this far south — a jagged darkness on the northern horizon, deeper than the sky around it, with a smear of white at its crown that never melted. He'd drawn that range a hundred times. He'd drawn it from other people's surveys, secondhand lines, guesses dressed up as knowledge. Nobody had mapped the high passes. The guild said they couldn't be mapped. The guild said a lot of things.

The first decoded words came out of his pen just after midnight, and they made no sense, and then they made terrible sense:

*...the Sable Current is real. I have seen it.*

Teo sat back. His heart was going oddly, too fast and too slow at once, the way it did when he stood at the edge of a height and his body hadn't decided yet whether to be afraid.

He read the sentence again. Then he read the whole decoded passage as it stood so far, pieced together across three hours of work, fragments waiting for their neighbors:

*...they will tell you the range has no crossing...*

*...the Sable Current is real. I have seen it...*

*...a pass above the icefields where the wind runs like a river...*

*...free. It must stay free...*

Free. Teo turned the word over. In the guild towns, *free* was a word people said quietly, the way you'd say a prayer or a curse. Free roads belonged to the old stories, the time before the tolls, before the chart-houses bought up the passes and put gates on them and charged a winter's wage to walk through. Teo had grown up drawing borders for things that shouldn't have borders. He'd inked the little toll-station glyphs onto maps of valleys where children had never known an ungated road existed. He'd told himself it was just work. Everyone told themselves it was just work.

*...if you are reading this, then I did not come back, and the needle found you instead...*

Teo glanced at the compass. It lay on the board beside the letter, brass casing dull with twenty years of dark, its glass clouded at one edge like an eye going blind. All evening the needle had done its impossible work, swinging away from north whenever Yara came near, settling toward the mountains with a persistence that had stopped feeling like a malfunction and started feeling like attention. Like being watched by something patient.

*...do not trust the guild with what I am about to tell you...*

*...Voss must never hold the needle.*

There it was. Teo read the name twice, three times, making sure. Voss. Odile Voss, who had come aboard the *Kestrel's Debt* two days ago with her grey coat and her grey eyes and her voice like a ledger being balanced, offering to buy a dead woman's compass with a smile that never reached the arithmetic behind it. Voss, whose enforcers had been asking questions at every dock since. Voss, whom Teo had served for eleven months, carrying her instruments, drafting her charts, watching her mark the unmarked ranges with little stamped claims — *GUILD SURVEY PENDING* — as though ownership were a weather system moving in from the west.

His hands had gone still on the paper. He noticed them the way you notice your own breathing when it changes.

He thought about the night he'd run. Not the decision — decisions were clean, and that night had not been clean — but the running itself: the chart-room window he'd oiled weeks earlier without letting himself think about why, the satchel of stolen blanks, the wet streets, the way his lungs had burned and some animal part of him had sung. He'd told himself he was fleeing Voss. Now, decoding a dead woman's handwriting by lantern-light on a stranger's boat, he understood he'd been fleeing toward something, and the something had a name, and the name was in front of him in faded iron-gall ink.

The last section of the margin took him another hour. By then the sky beyond the cabin roof had gone from black to the color of wet slate, and Yara had come down twice without speaking, once to set a tin mug of chicory beside him — hot, bitter, exactly right — and once simply to stand in the doorway and watch, arms crossed, saying nothing, which Teo was beginning to understand was a language Yara spoke fluently. *I'm here. Take the time you need. I'm here.*

When the final fragment came out of the pen, Teo read it aloud, because silence suddenly seemed wrong for it:

*"A map is only useful if you can find yourself on it. Find yourself, child. Then find me."*

* * *

Yara took the decoded pages and read them standing up, the way people read things they can't sit still for, and Teo watched their face do something complicated — grief and fury and a fierce, disbelieving joy all trying to occupy the same features at once, none of them winning.

"Say the middle part again," Yara said.

"The Sable Current is real. I have seen it. A pass above the icefields where the wind runs like a river."

"And it says free."

"It says free four times." Teo tapped the pages. "Your mother counted on someone counting. She wanted no ambiguity. Free means no tolls, no gates, no claim. She uses the guild's own legal phrasing here—" he turned to a fragment near the end, "—'unencumbered passage in perpetuity,' which is the exact wording the charter-house uses when it grants itself a route. She inverted it. She wrote the pass into freedom using their own language."

Yara stared at him. "You're telling me my mother hid a legal claim inside a letter."

"I'm telling you she hid a *map*, a *claim*, and a *warning* inside a letter, and disguised all three as a mother's goodbye." Teo's voice came out faster than he intended. Eleven months of holding his tongue were coming loose all at once. "That's what the cipher is for. That's what the guild uses it for — burying terms of grant inside correspondence so rival houses can't contest them. If this letter exists, if it can be produced and deciphered before witnesses, then the Sable Current isn't a legend. It's a documented, granted, *free* passage. And the compass—"

"The compass."

"The casing." Teo picked it up carefully, turning it in the grey morning light. "Look at the base plate. Under the grime. There's a stamp."

Yara leaned close. Faintly, almost erased, a seal was pressed into the brass: a circle crossed by a stylized current-line, the old mark of the founding charter, the one that appeared on documents worth more than most towns.

"That's a founder's seal," Teo said. "This compass isn't an instrument. It's a *deed*. Your mother didn't send you a keepsake. She sent you proof."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The river moved under the hull with its endless patient sound, and the light strengthened, and the mountains on the horizon sharpened from smudge to fact.

Then Yara laughed — one short, cracked note with nothing amused in it — and sat down heavily on the cargo crate across from Teo.

"A deed," they said. "Twenty years I thought she abandoned us. Twenty years I built my whole life in the shape of that hole. And she was writing me a deed." Their jaw worked. "Do you know what the worst part is? I'd have traded it. All of it. The pass, the claim, the proof — I'd have burned every page for one more year of her."

"I know," Teo said quietly. And he did know, in the way of someone who had spent his childhood being useful to an institution instead of loved by a family. "But she didn't send you a year. She sent you a burden."

Yara looked up sharply.

"That's what this is," Teo went on, gesturing at the pages, the compass, the whole impossible inheritance laid out on a cargo-board between them. "She saw the Sable Current. She saw what it meant — a road nobody could own, a crossing nobody could gate. And then she spent twenty years paying for having seen it. Look at the warning. *Voss must never hold the needle.* She wasn't writing poetry. She was naming the exact person who would come for it, twenty years before Odile Voss stood on this deck and offered to buy it." He shook his head slowly. "Treasure gets found and spent. This doesn't get spent. This gets *carried*. Whoever holds the needle holds the pass, and whoever holds the pass spends the rest of their life keeping it out of other hands. That's not a treasure, Captain. That's a watch."

Outside, boots on the dock planks. Two sets, heavy, unhurried, stopping at the gangway.

Yara rose without a word and blew out the lantern, though the daylight made it pointless — an old instinct, Teo thought, the instinct of someone who had learned early that some things are safer unseen. They tucked the decoded pages inside their coat, against the chest, and closed their hand around the compass, and Teo saw their thumb move once across the founder's seal, slow, like touching a scar to check it's still there.

"You said the guild buries grant-terms in correspondence," Yara murmured. "Which means they'll know how to read this. Which means if they take it—"

"They don't need to take it," Teo said. "They need to make sure it never surfaces. There's a difference, and the difference is a fire."

The boots on the dock shifted. A knuckle rapped twice against the hull — polite, deliberate, the knock of someone who already knew they'd be let aboard.

Yara exhaled through their nose, and something in their face settled, the restlessness of the river finally finding its current.

"Well," they said, moving toward the gangway. "Let's go lie to Captain Voss."
