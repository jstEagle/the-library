## 7. Burning the Berth

The *Kestrel* had a name before Yara owned her, and they had never learned it. The ferry guild's ledger listed her as Lot 114, thirty-one feet of river oak and patched canvas, repossessed from a man who'd drowned with her debt still wet on the books. Yara had bought her at auction for less than the cost of the rope in her rigging, and for eleven years she had been the only honest thing in their life: she floated when you asked her to float, she carried what you loaded onto her, and she never once pretended to be anything other than what she was.

Yara stood on her deck at first light and ran a hand along the rail the way you'd touch the face of someone sleeping.

"You could tell me not to do this," they said to her.

The river said nothing. It was doing its morning work, sliding gold-colored light up from the east, carrying last night's cold down toward the delta. Somewhere upstream a fish jumped — one clean slap of sound — and Yara flinched like it had been a word.

Behind them, on the roof of the cabin where he'd slept under an oilskin because there wasn't room below, Teo sat up with his hair flattened on one side and his eyes already too awake. He was nineteen and built out of elbows and questions, and he had been aboard four days now, long enough that his stowaway smell had been replaced by the boat's smell — bilgewater, tar, the ghost of dried coffee — which Yara supposed meant something, though they didn't examine what.

"You're talking to the boat," Teo said.

"I'm saying goodbye to the boat."

"Same thing." He swung down onto the deck, careful of the loose plank by the mast step that he'd already learned to avoid, and Yara felt that small competence land somewhere under their ribs like a stone dropped into a well. Four days. He already knew about the plank.

They were tied up at Miller's Landing, which was barely a landing — a coal-stained dock, a chandlery that opened when it felt like it, and a gravel yard where three boats sat waiting for buyers the way dogs wait outside butcher shops. Two of them were barges. One was the *Kestrel*.

The buyer was named Hobb. He came down the dock at midmorning wearing a coat too good for the mud and a smile too practiced for his face, and he walked around the *Kestrel* twice, kicking tires that didn't exist, thumping hulls, making sounds like a man appraising a horse.

"She's tired," Hobb said.

"She's sound," Yara said. "New garboard planks two seasons back. I did them myself."

"That so." He thumped again, harder, as if trying to raise a bruise. "River boats are a dying trade, Okafor. Rail's coming up the valley. I'm doing you a favor even looking at her."

Yara looked at him. They had spent eleven years learning to read water — the way current lied around bends, how a smooth surface could hide a snag that would open your hull like a letter — and reading men like Hobb was easier than reading water. He wanted the boat. He wanted it cheap. Every insult was a coin he was palming.

"Two hundred forty," Yara said.

Hobb laughed. "I'll give you ninety."

"The debt is two hundred ten," Yara said, "plus the guild's filing fees, plus interest that accrues whether or not you stand here insulting my caulking. Ninety doesn't cover it. Ninety means they take the boat anyway and sell her to someone like you for a hundred twenty, and I walk away owing the difference. So let's skip the part where we pretend you're doing me a favor, and you give me a number that ends this."

Something shifted behind Hobb's smile — respect, maybe, or just the recalibration of a man who realized the animal in front of him had teeth after all. He looked at the boat again, really looked, and Yara watched him find the things Yara had put into her over eleven years: the fair seams, the straight tiller, the way she sat in the water balanced as a held breath.

"One sixty," he said.

"One ninety-five, and I leave the anchor and chain."

"One seventy-five, and you leave everything, including whatever's in that chest you keep glancing at."

Yara went very still. It was a small thing, the glance — they hadn't known they'd done it — but Hobb had been watching them watch the cabin, and men like Hobb collected other people's tells the way the river collected driftwood.

"The chest isn't part of the sale," Yara said.

"Didn't say it was." He shrugged, expansive, innocent as silt. "One eighty. Final. And I want her off my dock by tomorrow noon, because I've got a barge coming in."

Yara thought: *one hundred eighty against two hundred ten.* They would still owe thirty marks and the filing fees, call it forty all told. Forty marks was six weeks of cargo runs they no longer had a boat to run. Forty marks was a hole with nothing left to fill it.

Forty marks was also freedom, if you squinted, if you held it up to the light and turned it until the shape looked like something else.

"Done," Yara said.

Teo made a sound behind them — half a word, strangled — and Yara didn't turn around.

* * *

Papers took an hour. Hobb's man wrote them out in the chandlery while flies investigated the inkwell, and Yara signed their name — *Y. Okafor*, the compact version, the one that fit in ledgers — and watched the boat stop being theirs in real time, clause by clause. The guild's lien would be paid directly from Hobb; the remainder, minus fees, would come to them in coin, which Hobb counted out onto the counter in little towers like a man building something he intended to knock down later.

Forty-one marks and change. Yara swept it into a leather pouch and felt how light it was. Eleven years. Forty-one marks.

"You're being robbed," Teo said on the dock afterward, loud enough that Hobb's man, walking away, snorted.

"I'm being finished," Yara said. "It's different."

"It's robbery with extra steps."

Yara almost smiled. Almost. "You've never sold anything you loved."

"I've never had anything to love." Teo said it flatly, without self-pity, the way he said most true things, and then seemed to hear it himself and looked away down the river. "That came out wrong."

"No," Yara said. "It came out right. That's the problem with it."

The *Kestrel* sat at the dock in her new ownership already, though nothing visible had changed. That was the obscenity of it, Yara thought. A boat didn't know whose hands were on her lines. She'd carry Hobb's cargo with the same patience she'd carried theirs. The river wouldn't note the difference. Only Yara would, and only for a while, and then not even that — some future morning they'd remember the loose plank by the mast step and not be sure they weren't inventing it.

They spent the afternoon stripping her, because the contract said *everything*, and because leaving things aboard would have meant coming back for them, and Yara knew with a certainty that lived in the body rather than the mind that they would not come back. Tools first. Then the charts — eleven years of hand-copied river charts, every sandbar and snag annotated in their own cramped script, worthless to anyone else, priceless to them, and therefore unsellable and unkeepable both. They stacked them in the cabin stove.

"What are you doing?" Teo said from the doorway.

"Burning the berth."

"You can't burn those. Those are—" He crossed the cabin in two steps and put his hand flat on the stack, protective, absurd, his fingers spread over the top chart like a man shielding a candle. "Do you know what these are worth? To me? To anyone? Half these annotations don't exist on any guild survey. The channel shift below Widow's Bend — you've got three years of migration mapped in pencil—"

"They're mine," Yara said. "And I'm not going to need river charts where I'm going."

"And where is that, exactly? Because you haven't actually said. You've got a compass that points at a dead woman and a letter that says a mountain pass is going to kill us, and your entire plan appears to be *walk toward it*. You're selling the only thing keeping you alive and fed, and burning the only professional skill you own, and I'd like to register, formally, as the only person aboard with any cartographic training, that this is insane."

Yara looked at him. He was breathing hard, his hand still flat on the charts, and there was something underneath the outrage that Yara recognized because they'd been carrying it themselves for days: fear. Not of the mountains. Of the water going away. Of the last legible thing in either of their lives being fed into a stove.

"Light it or don't," Yara said. "Your choice."

Teo stared at them. Then, slowly, he took his hand off the charts, picked up the stove lid iron, and held it like it might bite.

"Why does it have to be burned?"

"Because if I keep them, I'll come back." Yara fed the first chart into the firebox, face-down, the annotations going dark. "And I can't afford to come back. Coming back is how Voss finds us. Coming back is how this ends with me poling barges for the guild until the debt eats the rest of my life. My mother chose the mountains over the river, and everyone said she was lost. Maybe she just couldn't afford to come back either."

The paper caught. Heat breathed up out of the stove, and the pencil lines bloomed brown at the edges, curled, went white. Yara fed another. Sandbars they had memorized through years of low water turned to ash in the order they'd learned them. The dogleg at Tern Shoal. The sunken barge above Ferryman's Rest that no chart but theirs recorded. Each one went up like a small funeral, and Yara stood at the stove and attended every one.

Teo lit the rest. Neither spoke. Outside, the river kept moving, indifferent, enormous, going about its business of carrying everything away.

When the last chart was ash, Yara closed the stove and stood with their palm flat on the warm iron, and found that the grief was not where they expected it. It wasn't sharp. It was old — older than today, older than the auction, older than the debt itself. It was the grief of a life chosen by necessity and mistaken, for eleven years, for a life chosen at all. They had loved the river because the river was there. They had mastered it because mastery was cheaper than drowning. And now, standing in the cabin of a boat that belonged to a man named Hobb, Yara admitted the thing they'd never said aloud even to themselves: given any choice at all, they might have gone. Might have been one of the ones who left. The river had been a berth, not a home. You could grieve a berth. People did it all the time. Nobody sang songs about it.

"I'm sorry," Teo said quietly.

"Don't be. You were right. It is insane." Yara took their hand off the stove. "Insane is just a plan other people wouldn't survive."

* * *

They carried their belongings off the *Kestrel* at dusk: two bedrolls, the brass compass wrapped in oilcloth inside Yara's shirt, the half-burned letter in its oilskin sleeve, a single duffel between them that weighed less than the anchor chain they'd surrendered. Yara did not look back at the boat. They made a study of not looking back — examined the dock boards, the mooring cleats, the far bank going purple — and it worked right up until Hobb came down to take possession, keys of some kind jingling in his fist as if a boat needed keys, and Yara heard his boots cross *her* deck, heard the cabin door close on hinges they had oiled every spring, and their whole body turned like a needle finding north before they could stop it.

She looked smaller with someone else standing on her. That was the worst of it. Not gone — smaller. As if eleven years had been a trick of scale.

"Come on," Teo said, and touched their elbow, brief as a bird landing.

They walked north along the towpath while the light died. The road out of Miller's Landing climbed away from the water within the mile, and Yara felt the separation physically, the way you feel a tooth come loose — the river falling behind them by degrees, its sound thinning from presence to memory, until the last thing they heard of it was the slap of water on pilings, and then not even that, just wind in the grass and their own boots and Teo's, slightly out of rhythm with theirs.

They camped in a hay barn whose owner charged two coppers and asked no questions, which was the going rate for no questions in guild country. Yara sat against a post with the compass in their lap, unwrapped, its brass case drinking the last lantern light. The needle didn't waver. It lay pointing west-northwest, into the darkening wall of the range, steady as a pulse.

"Say it," Teo said. He was lying on his bedroll with his arms behind his head, watching the compass instead of the ceiling. "Whatever you're chewing on. Say it, or you'll chew it all night and I'll get no sleep listening to you not say it."

Yara turned the compass over in their hands. "I keep thinking I should feel lighter. Debt's paid. Nothing anchoring me. That's the whole story people tell — cut loose, go free." They huffed. "I feel like a man who amputated his own leg to escape a trap and keeps waiting for the trap to matter more than the leg."

"It will matter," Teo said. "For a while. Then it'll just be the thing you did." He rolled onto his side. "My master used to say grief is just love with nowhere to put itself. He said it about maps, mostly. Drafts you have to throw away. But I think it goes for boats."

"Your master sounds like he talked too much."

"He died talking. Voss's auditors came for his surveys and he stood in the doorway lecturing them about the ethics of information while they carried him out." A pause. "So. Mountains."

"So. Mountains."

"In the morning."

"In the morning."

But Yara sat up long after Teo's breathing went slow and even, turning the compass, watching the needle hold its line through the dark. Forty-one marks in a pouch. A letter naming a pass that had swallowed their mother whole. A warning in a dead woman's handwriting — *Voss must never hold the needle* — and somewhere behind them, surely already, a guild captain discovering that her salvage inquiry had flown.

The needle pointed into the black where the mountains were, patient as water, and Yara finally understood that they had not sold the boat to buy freedom.

They had sold it to buy the right to follow.
