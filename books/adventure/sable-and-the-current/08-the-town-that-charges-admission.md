## 8. The Town That Charges Admission

### Yara

The town was called Ferrier's Rest, and it had the look of a place that had stopped being a town and become a tollbooth with houses attached.

Yara saw it from the ridge road first: a scatter of slate roofs in the valley cup, chimney smoke standing straight in the windless morning, and around it all — like a noose drawn neat and deliberate — a timber palisade with a single gate. Not a wall to keep wolves out. Wolves didn't carry ledgers.

"Gate toll," Teo read aloud from the notice board they'd passed a mile back, his finger tracing the painted letters. "Two coppers a head, one for children under ten, five for carts, eight for anything with wheels *and* cargo." He lowered the paper's memory like it had a smell. "There's more. Lodging tax. Hearth tax. A *road-wear* fee."

"They charge you for wearing out their road by walking on it," Yara said.

"That's not even the worst part." Teo dug into his satchel and produced a second notice, this one printed on better stock, the guild's compass-rose seal stamped in blue wax at the bottom. He'd lifted it off the same board while Yara pretended to study the horizon. "Look at this one."

Yara took it. The printing was fine work, expensive work. **THE VERRE PANORAMA**, it announced. *Guild Survey Point No. 4. Admission: three coppers. Children free on rest-days.* Below the text was an engraving of mountains — the range they were walking into, rendered in loving crosshatch, snowfields and couloirs all made safe and picturesque behind a frame.

"They're selling the view," Yara said slowly.

"They're selling the view." Teo's voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when he was angriest. Yara had learned that about him in the two days since he'd jumped aboard — no, since they'd burned the berth and started walking; the boy's anger never shouted. It got quiet and precise, like a blade being honed. "There's a platform built up on that spur — see, there, the bare knob above the town? Stairs, railing, a little roofed shelter. My old master surveyed it. Guild Survey Point Four. You pay your three coppers, you climb forty steps, and you're allowed to look at mountains that nobody built and nobody owns."

Yara said nothing. They walked. The road dropped through pine shadow, and the mountains came and went between the trunks, white shoulders rising higher with every switchback, and every time the range showed itself Yara felt the compass shift against their chest — not physically, it was wrapped in oilcloth inside their coat, but some other sense of it, the way you feel a current under a calm stretch of river. Pulling east. Pulling up. Toward the Sable Current, toward whatever was left of Adaeze Okafor at the end of twenty years.

Toward Voss, too, maybe. The needle didn't distinguish.

"You're doing the thing," Teo said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you go quiet and steer." He squinted up at them. "You've been doing it since the boat. I used to watch Captain Enzo do it when we ran the gorge in flood season. Hands steady, face empty, everything happening underneath."

"I'm not steering anything. There's no boat."

"There's always a boat," Teo said, with the serene confidence of someone who had been a stowaway exactly once and considered himself an authority on vessels now. "You just haven't told us what it is yet."

Yara almost smiled. Almost. Then the trees opened, the road straightened, and Ferrier's Rest showed them its gate, and whatever smile had been building died of exposure.

The palisade was new timber, pale as bone, and the gatehouse above it flew the guild colors — blue and white, the compass rose — beside a second banner Yara hadn't seen before: a mountain peak inside a circle of chain links. The Chain-and-Peak. The guild's land division, Teo had called it. The arm that held territory.

At the gate stood two men in guild gray with padded vests and short iron-tipped staves, and behind them a table. An actual table, set across the road, with a strongbox, an inkwell, a ledger open to a fresh page, and a young woman behind it with the patient expression of someone who processed human beings the way a mill processes grain.

"Welcome to Ferrier's Rest," she recited. "Entry toll is two coppers per adult. If you intend to purchase goods or lodging, the toll is credited against your first transaction over one silver. If you are merely passing through to the eastern road, the toll is not credited. Do you declare cargo?"

"My coat," Yara said.

"Do you declare *trade* cargo?"

"No."

"Then two coppers each, four total, receipt issued at the gate, valid for seven days' passage." She dipped her pen. "Names?"

Yara paid. It wasn't the money — four coppers was nothing, less than nothing, they'd gotten a fair price for the *Marrow* and the coin purse had weight now for the first time in years. It was the ledger. Watching their names go down in guild ink, watching the pen scratch *Okafor* onto the page — they'd given the name without thinking, out of habit, and only heard it as the woman read it back — "Okafor, party of two, eastern passage" — and felt the word travel somewhere. Filed. Somewhere in a guild archive, a line now existed connecting that name to this gate, this day, this road east. Toward the pass.

Voss would have clerks who read such things. Voss had already shown she could find a person fast when there was brass involved.

They walked through, and Yara kept their hood up and their pace unhurried, and thought: *I just paid the people hunting me for permission to flee them.*

It was, they had to admit, a well-built machine.

### Teo

Teo had grown up inside the machine, which was why he recognized its gears.

His master, Aldous Ren, had been a guild cartographer for thirty years, and Teo had spent six of his own years grinding pigments and stretching vellum in Ren's workshop above the Ferrier's Rest survey office — before the survey office moved to the panorama platform, because views sold better than maps. Six years of learning that a map was never just a map. Every chart the guild printed carried its seal in the corner, and every seal was a claim, and every claim was a fence you couldn't see until you tried to cross it.

He had loved the work anyway. That was the shameful part, the part he hadn't told Yara yet. He had loved the inks and the instruments and the feeling of holding a coastline in his hands. He'd have gone on loving it forever if Ren hadn't asked him to copy the letter.

*The letter.* Teo touched his satchel strap, under which the half-burned pages rode wrapped in the same oilcloth as the compass. Three nights now he'd worked the coded margins by firelight, and three nights the margins had given up a little more: the Sable Current named plain, the words *free passage, in perpetuity*, and the warning repeated like a heartbeat — *Voss must never hold the needle.*

He knew things now that Voss wanted. He was walking through Voss's town carrying those things in a canvas bag. And the town itself — his town, once, the streets he'd sprinted down as an apprentice with dye-stained fingers — had rearranged itself around him like a face he knew that had learned to sneer.

"Ticket?" said a boy at the foot of the panorama stairs.

Teo stopped. He couldn't help it. The stairs were exactly where Ren's old office had been; they'd torn the workshop out and built a staircase in its footprint, forty steps up the spur to the viewing platform, and at the bottom stood a child of perhaps nine with a wooden cash box on a strap and an official guild apron cut down to his size.

"A ticket," the boy repeated, patient, practiced. "For the Verre Panorama. Three coppers. Best view in the valley. You can see the whole range."

"Can you," Teo said.

"The Sable Peaks." The boy pointed with a professional flourish. "That notch there — the low one, left of the twin horns — they say that's the Sable Current, but nobody goes up. Closed route. Dangerous." He delivered it all in one breath, a catechism. "Three coppers."

Teo looked at the notch. Even from here, even through the valley haze, something in his chest pulled toward it — the cartographer's itch, the part of him that saw any gap in a ridgeline as a question demanding an answer. *Closed route. Dangerous.* Meaning: uncharted. Meaning: *mine to chart.*

He looked at the boy instead. "Who taught you that speech?"

"The steward. You get extra bread if you say it right." The boy shrugged, suddenly just a kid. "It's a good job. My da says it's a good job."

"Does he pay the gate toll to come hear you say it?"

The boy's face did something complicated and then smoothed over, the practiced mask reassembling. "Three coppers," he said again, quieter. "Or you can move along."

Teo moved along. His hands were shaking, which embarrassed him. It wasn't the boy's fault. It wasn't even really the guild's fault, he thought, and then corrected himself: no. It was. It was exactly their fault, and the precision of it was what made him furious — that they'd found a way to make a nine-year-old sell the horizon, and made the boy grateful for the work, and made everyone else grateful for the stairs.

He found Yara at the provisioner's, haggling over dried apples and hardtack with the focused economy of someone who had fed themselves on thin margins for a long time. Marisol was supposed to meet them at the border saloon two days east; until then it was the two of them, and Teo still sometimes caught himself surprised that the tall river-captain with the careful eyes had let him stay.

"Ren's office is gone," Teo said, when the haggling was done and they were walking again. "They turned it into the staircase. To the ticketed view."

Yara glanced at him. "I'm sorry."

"He used to let me draft the river bends. Said my hand was honest — that most people draw what they're paid to draw, but mine drew what was there." Teo kicked a stone off the road, harder than necessary. "You know what the guild does with a surveyor who draws what's there? They promote him to management. That's how they get you. They don't burn the honest maps. They bury them in ledgers."

"And you?"

"I stole the letter and jumped a boat." He said it lightly, but it landed heavy in his own ears, heavier than he'd meant. "So. There's that."

Yara stopped walking. In the middle of Ferrier's Rest's main street, between a cooper's yard and a shrine to some river saint whose niche had been repurposed as a guild notice board, they stopped and looked at him with that level, weighing gaze that made Teo feel either very safe or very caught, depending on the day.

"Why did you jump the boat, Teo? Truly. Not the letter. The letter's the excuse. Why'd you run?"

No one had asked him that. He'd asked himself, in the dark of the cargo hold, and had answered with practicalities: Voss's men at the workshop, the coded margins, the chance. But Yara was waiting, and the street noise seemed to fall away, and Teo heard himself say:

"Because I drew a map once. When I was fourteen. Of the toll roads — all of them, every gate from here to the coast, with the fees written in. I did it for practice. Ren pinned it up in the workshop." His throat tightened. "And a guild assessor saw it and bought it. Paid real silver. And they printed it — a thousand copies, *Traveler's Companion to the Guild Roads* — and my map became the thing people used to calculate how much of their lives they'd spend paying to walk. And I got a bonus, and a title, and everyone said, *what a clever boy, what useful work.*"

The stone he'd kicked sat in the dust between them. Teo stared at it.

"I was proud," he said. "That's why I ran. Not the letter. I ran because I finally understood what I'd helped build, and I couldn't stand in the workshop one more day drawing fences and calling them rivers."

Yara put a hand on his shoulder — brief, rough, real. "Come on," they said. "We're leaving through the east gate. I want to see what they charge to get *out*."

### Yara

The east gate charged one copper. Exit fee. Yara paid it with a smile that made the tollwoman lean slightly away from the table, and then they were through, on the eastern road, and Ferrier's Rest fell away behind them with its pale palisade and its nine-year-old selling the sky.

But the anger stayed. Yara knew anger well — had carried it since the day a guild clerk had explained, with genuine sympathy, that Adaeze Okafor's debts had passed to her next of kin, and that *next of kin* meant a twelve-year-old in a borrowed coat. Twenty years of anger, worn smooth like river stone, so familiar Yara barely noticed its weight anymore.

This was different. Walking east with the mountains growing, the compass pulling, Teo's confession still hanging in the air between them — this anger had edges. It had a shape. It was crystallizing, drop by drop, into something with structure.

Because here was the thing Yara understood, standing on the eastern road with the tollgate's shadow still cold between their shoulder blades: the guild didn't just take money. Money was nothing. Yara had been poor their whole life; poverty was weather, you dressed for it. What the guild took was *permission*. Permission to enter, to exit, to look, to pass, to exist on a road your grandfather's grandfather had walked free. And they had built a world where people thanked them for the privilege — where a boy sold tickets to the horizon and called it a good job, and meant it.

And somewhere up in that notch — the low saddle left of the twin horns, the last free way through — someone had spent twenty years keeping a door open. Getting old. Getting sick, maybe. Alone.

"We'll make the border saloon by dark tomorrow," Yara said.

Teo, scribbling in his notebook as he walked — he was always drafting something, the boy's hand never still — looked up. "Marisol."

"Marisol." Yara watched the notch glint between the horns, far off, impossibly high. "And then we climb."

The compass lay against their chest, patient as water. Somewhere ahead, its needle's other end rested in a cabin Yara couldn't imagine yet, held by a woman Yara had mourned for twenty years. And somewhere behind, in a town that charged admission to the world, a ledger bore the name *Okafor* in fresh ink, drying.

Let it dry, Yara thought. Let them read it. By the time Voss's clerks traced the line from that gate to this road, there would be three of them on the mountain, and the name in the ledger would mean something the guild hadn't counted on.

Not a debtor.

An heir.
