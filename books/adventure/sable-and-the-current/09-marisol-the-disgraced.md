## 9. Marisol the Disgraced

The saloon in Puerto Umbral had a name once, painted in gold leaf above the door: *La Aguja del Norte*. The Needle of the North. The paint had flaked until only *del Norte* remained, and then a flood season's damp had eaten even that, so now the sign read nothing at all, which Marisol Ferreyra considered the most honest thing about the place.

She sat at her usual table — third from the back, near the wall where the plaster bulged with water damage like a healed bruise — and watched the afternoon light crawl across the floorboards. The light was the color of cheap whiskey. Everything here was. The whiskey itself, the sawdust, the faces of the mule drivers who came down off the border roads to spend their wages and their dignity in equal measure.

Marisol had spent hers years ago. She was down to drinking on credit, which the barman extended because she'd once been somebody, or because he pitied her, or because pity and credit were the same currency in a border town. She didn't ask which. Asking questions was how you found out things you couldn't live with.

"Another," she said, and slid the glass along the wood.

The barman — Tomás, gray at the temples, patient as a stone in a river — filled it without comment. That was another kindness of this place: nobody commented. In the city they would have known her name. Here they knew it too, probably; guides were rare enough that word traveled, and what word traveled fastest? Not the summits she'd bagged in her twenties, not the winter crossing of the Col de los Huesos that had made her reputation by thirty. No. What traveled was the expedition of twenty years ago. The one that went up with nine souls and came down with four. The one whose leader walked into the lowlands carrying three names on her back and set them down in every cantina between there and the coast, trying to give them away, and finally gave up and kept them, and drank.

*Adaeze Okafor.* Even now, twenty years later, the name arrived in Marisol's chest like weather. Not pain exactly. Pressure. The kind of pressure that builds ahead of a storm you know is coming and cannot turn aside.

Adaeze Okafor had hired her in the spring of the year the guild first started talking about closing the high passes. A riverboat captain's wife, some said; others said the captain was her wife, and others said Adaeze needed no one, which was closest to true. She'd come to Marisol's office with a map drawn in her own hand — beautiful, obsessive, covered in marginal notes in three languages — and asked one question: *Can you get me to the Sable Current?*

And Marisol, young and hungry and certain that no mountain could refuse her, had said yes.

Nine souls. Four came down. And the woman who'd asked the question was not among them, though they never found a body, never found gear, never found anything but an empty camp at the tree line and a wind that seemed, to Marisol's fevered mind in the weeks after, to be saying something over and over in a language she almost understood.

She drank. The whiskey did what whiskey does, which is nothing, which is everything.

* * *

They came in near dusk, when the saloon was filling with the smell of fried onions and the particular silence of strangers.

Marisol noticed them the way she still noticed everything, despite the drink — old habits of observation dying harder than she was. Two figures in the doorway: a tall person in a riverboat captain's coat, salt-stained at the cuffs, with the wide-shouldered stance of someone who'd spent their life compensating for a moving deck. Younger. Early twenties, maybe. Behind them, a boy — no, not quite a boy, a young man with ink-stained fingers and the haunted, darting look of someone being followed, or who believed he was, which amounted to the same posture.

They scanned the room the way travelers scan a room when they're looking for one person specifically. Then the tall one's eyes found Marisol, and stopped, and Marisol felt the look land on her like a hand on her shoulder.

She knew before they crossed the room. She knew by the color of their skin, deep brown under the road dust, and by the shape of their jaw, and most of all by the way they held their chin — lifted slightly, defiantly, as if daring the world to say something about any part of them. She'd seen that chin tilted at her across a map table twenty years ago.

"Are you Marisol Ferreyra?" the tall one asked. Voice low, careful. A voice used to being mistaken for other things and correcting the record.

"Depends who's asking."

"Yara Okafor."

The name went through her like a spear through wet canvas. She set down her glass because her hand had become unreliable, and looked — really looked — at the face above that defiant chin. Twenty years. The child would have been two, maybe three, when Adaeze left. Now here stood the grown shape of that absence, wearing its mother's eyes and its mother's stubbornness and a coat that had seen better rivers.

"Okafor," Marisol said. Her voice came out rougher than intended. "Sit down before I fall over."

* * *

They sat. The boy — Teo, he said, and said it quickly, glancing at the door — ordered tea, which told Marisol something about him. The captain ordered nothing, which told her more.

"You led the expedition," Yara said. Not a question.

"I led the expedition." She let the words sit there, ugly and public. Around them the saloon noise continued, mule drivers arguing about freight rates, dice clicking somewhere near the window, but at their table a small cold country had formed with borders of its own. "Nine went up. Four came down. Your mother wasn't one of the four. That's the whole story, and if you've come looking for a different ending, I don't have one to sell."

"I'm not looking for an ending. I'm looking for a guide."

Marisol laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle like old ice. "You've got the wrong drunk. Ask anyone. Ask the guild — they struck my certification. Ask the families of the men I lost. Ask—"

"I'm asking *you*."

There it was again, that chin. And beneath the defiance, visible now that Marisol forced herself to actually see rather than drink and glance, something else: exhaustion. The specific exhaustion of someone who has been running for days and has decided, somewhere on the road, to stop apologizing for it.

"Tell me why," Marisol said. "Not the polite version. Why me, out of every guide on this side of the range, when my name is mud from here to the coast?"

Yara reached into their coat and set something on the table between them.

A compass. Brass, old, dented along one side as if it had been dropped from a height or lived through a fall Marisol didn't want to imagine. Its lid was open, and the needle inside was doing something Marisol had never seen a needle do in thirty years of mountains: ignoring north entirely. It swung slowly, deliberately, and settled pointing east-northeast. Toward the range. Toward the high country. Toward—

"Where did you get this?" Her mouth had gone dry in a way the whiskey hadn't caused.

"A cave at Widow's Bend. Low tide opened it. There was a chest with my mother's initials and this inside, and half a letter we're still reading." Yara turned the compass so the needle stayed faithful to its impossible heading. "It doesn't point north. It points at her. Or at whatever she left up there. Teo thinks—" a nod at the boy, who flushed and studied his tea "—Teo thinks the needle responds to something in the pass itself. Something she built or found or guarded. We don't know yet. But it points, and it hasn't stopped pointing since I pulled it out of that cave."

Marisol stared at the needle. Twenty years ago she had stood at a windswept col with Adaeze Okafor while the other woman held a battered brass instrument against the gale and frowned at it and said, quietly, *it wants to go home*, and Marisol had laughed and called it a broken tool and a charming quirk, and Adaeze had smiled in a way Marisol had thought of many times since, usually at three in the morning, usually mid-bottle.

"It's hers," Marisol said. "I've seen it before. On the mountain. She had it in her hand the last day I ever saw her alive."

Yara went very still. "What did she say?"

"That it wanted to go home." Marisol heard her own voice as if from a distance, flat and strange. "I told her it was broken. God help me, I told her to leave it behind if it slowed us down."

The saloon noise rushed back in around the edges of the silence. Somewhere a bottle broke; someone laughed; the evening went on being an evening. Marisol looked at the compass, and at the face of the child she had orphaned by incompetence, and felt the full weight of twenty years settle onto her shoulders like a pack she'd never been allowed to put down.

"Here's what I can offer," Yara said. "Coin, if you want it. We have some. Not much."

"No."

"Then—"

"No coin." Marisol pushed her glass away, all the way to the table's edge, as far from herself as it could go without leaving her entirely. It was a small gesture. It cost her more than the whole journey would. "You misunderstand what I've been doing for twenty years, Captain Okafor. I haven't been drinking because I liked it. I've been drinking because there's nowhere to put it down. Every guide in the range knows the story, and every guide in the range looks at me and sees the woman who killed nine people, and I let them, because the truth is worse. The truth is I was good. I was the best, and I was arrogant, and when the weather turned I made choices, and the choices were mine, and your mother paid for them. So no coin. You want to know my price?"

Yara waited.

"My price is the summit." Marisol's voice steadied as she said it, the way a rope steadies under load. "My price is standing where she stood, and finding out what happened up there, and if there's anything left of her — a grave, a cairn, a journal, a bone — bringing it down or leaving it right, whichever she'd have wanted. My price is walking back into those mountains as a guide instead of a ghost. Absolution isn't sold, Captain. It's climbed for. So you'll take me up that mountain and you'll work me like a mule and you won't be gentle with me, and when we reach the Sable Current — *if* we reach it — maybe I'll be able to sleep. Maybe. That's my fee. That's all I've got."

For a long moment Yara just looked at her. Then they glanced at Teo, and something passed between them — the quick silent negotiation of people who'd been making decisions together for days, short as their acquaintance was.

"The snows are coming early," Yara said. "We leave tomorrow, dawn, before the guild's enforcers pick up our trail. They want the compass. Their captain offered to buy it, and when I refused, she stopped offering."

"Odile Voss." The name tasted like copper. "She's climbing after you?"

"She's climbing after us."

Marisol nodded slowly. Of course Voss was. Voss had wanted the high passes since before Marisol had a reputation to lose; Voss had sat across from Adaeze Okafor in a guild office twenty years ago and offered money for route knowledge and been refused, politely, in three languages. If the compass pointed at Adaeze, then Voss would follow it the way water follows gravity.

"Then you'll need someone who knows the hidden routes," Marisol said. "Because Voss's climbers are good, and the main trails will be watched, and there are ways through the range that aren't on any chart the guild owns." She paused, and the old shame stirred, and she let it speak, because hiding it hadn't worked for twenty years either. "Some of those ways I know because of the expedition. Because we cut them fleeing. People died learning them. But I know them, and I'll get you through them, and I'll stand at that pass if it kills me, and honestly, Captain, it might."

Teo spoke for the first time, quiet, not looking up from his tea. "Is that supposed to reassure us?"

Marisol barked a laugh — a real one this time, surprising all three of them. "No, boy. It's supposed to be honest. Reassurance is for people with clean records." She stood, and the room tilted, and she rode the tilt the way she'd once ridden scree slopes, letting it move under her without fighting it. "Dawn, then. Where's your boat?"

"We sold it," Yara said. Something moved behind their eyes when they said it — grief, fresh and private. "To cover a debt. We're walking out of here."

"Good," Marisol said. "Boats make people soft. Meet me at the east gate, and bring that compass, and Captain — one more thing."

Yara looked at her.

"When we're on the mountain, you do what I say on the ice. Not because I'm better than you. Because I'm the one who's buried people who didn't." She held their gaze, mother's eyes and all, and did not look away. "And when we find her — if we find her — you let me carry whatever needs carrying. I've been training for it twenty years."

Outside, the light had gone fully blue, and far to the east the range stood black against the last of the sky, its high passes already wearing the season's first white like a warning. Marisol looked at them a long moment, the way she used to read weather, and felt something in her chest that was not whiskey and not quite hope, but was related to both.

Tomorrow, dawn. She turned back to the table, to the two unlikely companions gathering their things, and raised two fingers toward Tomás.

"One for the road," she said. "And then none. None after this one. I mean it this time."

Tomás looked at her the way bartenders look at twenty years of meaning-it, and poured anyway, and Marisol drank it slow, watching the mountains darken, feeling the old pull in her blood — the pull she'd drowned for two decades — waking up like a muscle remembering its use.

Somewhere up in that darkening white, a woman she'd failed was alive or dead, and a needle of brass knew which, and Marisol Ferreyra was going up to find out, sober or close to it, with the child of her worst mistake watching her every step.

It was, she thought, the fairest sentence she'd ever been given.
