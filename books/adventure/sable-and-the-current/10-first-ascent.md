## 10. First Ascent

The mountain did not care that Yara Okafor had spent twenty years reading water.

That was the first lesson of the morning, delivered without cruelty and without mercy, somewhere above the tree line where the trail gave up pretending to be a trail and became a suggestion of loose stone climbing toward a sky the color of unpolished tin. On the river, Yara could feel a sandbar forming before it broke the surface — could read the way current puckered over a submerged log, the way a channel's mood changed three bends before the change arrived. Their hands knew rope the way other people's hands knew their own names.

But scree was not water. Scree was water's mean cousin, the one who'd been drinking all night.

Yara took a step and the mountainside moved with them — an entire slope of gray fragments shifting, sliding, whispering downhill in a sound like slow applause from an audience that wanted them to fail. They caught themselves on a boulder the size of a coffin, palms scraped raw through thin gloves, heart slamming against their ribs in a rhythm they hadn't felt since the flood year, since the Verre had tried to take the *Marrow* and everything they owned in one brown swallow.

"Weight on your whole foot," Marisol said behind them. She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she had the flat, unhurried delivery of someone who had learned that shouting on a mountain was a confession of fear. "Not the toe. You're walking like you're stepping off a dock onto a boat. This isn't a boat."

"It moves like one," Yara said, breath ragged. The air up here was thin and tasted of iron and old snow. "It shifts under you. It has moods."

"It has gravity," Marisol said. "That's the only mood it has, and it is always the same mood." She came past them with the economy of a woman descending stairs she'd climbed ten thousand times, boots finding purchase where Yara saw none, her gray-streaked braid swinging under her hat. Up close, in daylight, sober — or mostly sober, on her second day of it — the ruin of her face was easier to read: the deep lines around her mouth, the eyes that checked the skyline every few seconds out of habit older than thought. She looked like a woman carrying a pack full of something heavier than gear.

Teo scrambled past them both, twenty years old and made entirely of elbows and enthusiasm, sketchbook already out despite the grade of the slope, pencil scratching as he walked. "The switchback below us — I think it's the same one on the 1841 survey, but the surveyor drew it wider, so either the trail's eroded or he lied about how bad it was—"

"Teo." Yara's voice came out sharper than they intended. "If you fall off this mountain while drawing it, no one will ever read your map."

He grinned at them, that open, unguarded grin that had disarmed Yara completely back at the border saloon and still disarmed them now. "Then I'd better draw fast."

Yara watched him go and felt the strange new thing move in their chest again — the thing that had been growing since the night they'd found a stowaway curled among the cargo tarps, clutching a stolen roll of charts like a life preserver. It wasn't trust yet. Trust was a mooring line, tied off at both ends, load-tested. This was more like the first time you let a stranger take the tiller while you went below for five minutes. You stayed half-listening the whole time. But you went below.

They climbed.

* * *

By midday the sun had come out and turned the slope into a furnace of reflected light, and by late afternoon it had gone again, swallowed by cloud, and the temperature dropped like a stone off a bridge. Yara learned the mountain's second lesson: weather up here did not arrive. It *condensed*. One moment the world was blue and enormous; the next, a gray ceiling sat on their shoulders and the wind came sideways off the ridge carrying grit and the smell of coming snow.

They made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder, a shallow scoop in the rock that Marisol pronounced acceptable with the enthusiasm of a customs inspector. While Teo wrestled with the small stove — his hands better with a pen than a flint, though he refused to admit it — Yara stood at the edge of the shelter and looked back the way they had come.

The valley opened below them in long green folds, and far down, a thread of silver caught the last light. The Verre. Their river. From this height it looked exactly like what it had been: a small thing. A beautiful, small thing that had been their entire world — the width of their ambition, the measure of their days, the road that led everywhere and nowhere, because every road on a river leads back to the same docks.

Twenty years of cargo runs. Twenty years of counting coin against the guild debt, of knowing every eddy between Corvane and Widow's Bend, of being the best captain nobody important had ever heard of. They had told themselves it was enough. Some nights they'd even believed it.

"You're doing the thing," Teo said beside them. He'd appeared without sound, the way he did, holding two tin cups of something hot and terrible.

"What thing?"

"The looking-back-like-you-left-a-body thing." He handed them a cup. Steam rose off it and tore away instantly in the wind. "Marisol does it too. Different direction, though. She looks up."

Yara wrapped their hands around the cup and said nothing, because there was nothing honest to say that wasn't either too much or not enough. Below them, the river caught another sliver of light and held it.

"I used to think the river was the biggest thing there was," Yara said finally. "Not big like — size. Big like, it contained everything. Every kind of day. Flood and drought and fog and glass-calm. I thought if I mastered it, I'd mastered weather itself." They took a sip and grimaced. "What is this?"

"Tea-adjacent."

"This is tea's enemy." They drank it anyway. Warmth was warmth. "And then you get up here and the mountain doesn't even notice you. The river at least pretended. When it wanted to kill you, it looked you in the eye first."

"That's why you're good at it," Teo said. He was looking down at the valley too, his young face lit faintly by the last light. "On the boat. You watch things. You noticed me breathing wrong in the cargo hold before I'd been aboard six hours. Most people wouldn't have noticed a person at all."

"You were breathing like a man being hunted."

"I *was* a man being hunted."

"And now you're a man drinking tea-adjacent on a freezing rock," Yara said, "which is arguably worse." And Teo laughed — really laughed, the sound startled out of him, bright and cracked and young — and the sound did something to the cold. Made a pocket in it. Yara found themselves thinking, with a captain's instinct for assessing cargo: *this one is worth carrying.*

Below, the light left the river entirely. Somewhere down there was the berth they'd sold, the debt they'd paid, the life they'd burned like a signal fire to light the way up here instead. Grief and freedom had turned out to be the same weight, carried in different arms.

"Get some sleep," they said. "Tomorrow we cross the scarp, if Marisol says the weather holds."

"If Marisol says," Teo echoed, and there was something in his tone — not mockery, exactly. Curiosity. He'd noticed it too, then. How quickly they'd both started routing decisions through the disgraced guide, as if she were a lock and the mountain a door.

Yara pretended not to hear it. But they filed it away, the way they filed away everything: current, cargo, crew.

* * *

The third day, the mountain taught its third lesson, and this one drew blood.

It happened on the traverse above the frozen waterfall — a long, exposed shelf of tilted slab where the trail narrowed to boot-width and the drop below went down and down into a white silence that made Yara's stomach climb into their throat. Marisol went first, moving with that infuriating ease. Then Teo. Then Yara, facing the rock, hands pressed flat against cold stone, telling themselves the same thing they told green deckhands in rough water: *look where you're going, not where you're afraid of falling.*

The rock sheared.

It gave no warning. No puckering of current, no change in the channel's mood — just a crack like a rifle shot and a slab the size of a door swinging out from under Yara's leading foot, and then they were falling, the world rotating, sky and stone trading places—

—and stopped. Wrenched to a halt by their own hand, which had shot out on pure animal reflex and closed around a nub of granite barely bigger than a fist. Their whole body hung from that grip, boots skating on loose rubble, the drop yawning beneath them, and their arm screamed, and their mind — their treacherous, river-trained mind — kept trying to read the fall like water, kept waiting for the current to tell them what it would do next, and the fall said nothing. Falls don't talk.

"Yara." Marisol's face appeared over the edge, framed against the gray sky. Calm. Absolutely calm. "Don't pull. Feet first, find the wall. Walk yourself down to the ledge — there's a ledge, four feet below your right boot. Can you see it?"

"I—" Their voice came out in pieces. "I can't read it. I can't read this. There's nothing to read."

"There's nothing to *predict*," Marisol corrected. "There's plenty to see. Look at the wall, not the drop. Where's your right boot?"

Yara looked. Found the ledge. Moved the boot. Found friction. Moved the other. The wall accepted them the way walls do — indifferently, but solidly — and thirty seconds later they were lying on the ledge shaking, palm bleeding freely now, Teo's anxious face joining Marisol's above them like two moons.

"You caught yourself," Teo said. "You just — you grabbed it. That was incredible."

"That was luck."

"That was *fast*," Marisol said. She'd already dropped a rope down; her hands were busy coiling the slack with automatic precision. "River reflexes. Your body decided before your head did. That'll keep you alive up here, eventually — once your head catches up to your body and stops trying to treat rock like water." She paused, and something shifted behind her eyes, some door opening briefly on a room Yara wasn't invited into. "Adaeze fell on rock like this once. Caught herself the same way. Tore the skin off her forearm to the elbow and laughed about it after."

Yara went very still, hanging between the ledge and the rope. "You remember that."

"I remember everything about her," Marisol said quietly. "That's rather the problem."

She said nothing else, and hauled them up.

At the top, while Teo bound Yara's palm with a strip of linen and fussed with the focused intensity of a boy who needed something to do with his hands, Yara sat against a boulder and let the shaking finish its business. The mountain rose above them, indifferent, immense, wearing its clouds like a crown it hadn't asked for. Two days ago it had humiliated them. Today it had nearly killed them. And still — Yara examined the feeling honestly, the way they'd examine a hull for rot — still they did not want to turn around.

Because the compass lay wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and twice today, when they'd checked it in shelter, the needle had pulled harder. Not north. Not toward the river, toward safety, toward everything known. Toward the pass. Toward whatever waited at the top of all this indifference.

Toward her.

"Your grip will be weak for a few days," Marisol said, crouching beside them. She nodded at the bandaged hand. "Rope work goes to Teo and me. You navigate, you carry light loads, you keep your eyes working — eyes are your real tool anyway, and yours are better than mine ever were." She hesitated. Then, gruffly, like paying out a line against her own will: "You listen well. On the icefield tomorrow, when I say stop, you stop. When I say run, you run. Do that, and I can get us up this thing."

It was not an apology for anything. It was not warmth, exactly. It was a contract, offered in the only language Marisol seemed to speak.

Yara looked at her — at the drunk from the saloon, the guide whose expedition had ended in names on cairns, the woman who flinched at her own reflection in the word *leader* — and understood that trust, on a mountain, worked differently than it did on a river. On the river, you trusted the water because you'd tested it a thousand times. Here, you trusted the person, because the mountain would test everyone whether you liked it or not, and the only question was who'd be standing next to you when it did.

"All right," Yara said. "When you say stop, I stop."

Marisol nodded once, satisfied, and shouldered her pack.

It was Teo who spotted it, an hour later, from the saddle of the ridge — a smudge of movement far below and behind, at the edge of the tree line they'd left two days ago. Tiny figures. Moving with purpose. Too many of them, and too evenly spaced, to be lost hunters.

"Yara," he said, and his voice had gone quiet in a way that stopped both of them mid-stride. "Is that... do climbers usually travel in groups of eight?"
