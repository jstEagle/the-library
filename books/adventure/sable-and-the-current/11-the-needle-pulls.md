## 11. The Needle Pulls

Teo had learned to read the compass the way he'd once learned to read his master's moods: by watching it when it thought no one was looking.

It was the third morning above the treeline, and the world had been reduced to its essentials — grey stone, white sky, the sound of wind worrying at the scree like an old woman at a knot. Marisol led, because she was the only one of them who knew what she was doing on this kind of ground. Yara carried the heavy pack and said little, which Teo had come to understand was how they carried everything. And Teo walked last with the compass in his coat pocket, one hand cupped around it like a candle flame he was afraid to lose.

The needle had been pulling since dawn.

Not swinging — that was the thing about it that never stopped unsettling him. A compass ought to swing, ought to waver and settle and waver again, alive with small indecisions. This one moved with intent. When Teo held it flat in his palm, the needle swung toward the mountains and *held*, quivering at the tip like a dog's nose pressed to a door. Toward the pass. Toward whatever waited there.

Toward Adaeze Okafor, if half of what the letter promised was true.

"Stop fidgeting with it," Marisol called back without turning around. She had eyes in the back of her head; Teo was convinced of it. "You'll walk off a ledge."

"I'm not walking off a ledge."

"You're walking like a man reading a letter while crossing a river. Same thing up here." She paused on a shelf of rock no wider than a table, waiting for him with the particular patience of someone who had buried people who didn't listen. "Give it here if you can't leave it alone."

Teo's hand closed tighter around the brass case. It wasn't rational. He knew it wasn't rational. But the compass had been in his pocket for eleven days now, through the border town where even the mountain view came ticketed, through the saloon where Marisol drank herself upright, through the first terrible nights of the climb when Yara had slipped twice on wet stone and caught themselves both times with a river-boatman's reflexes and a mountaineer's bad luck. The compass had become, without his permission, the fixed point of the whole enterprise. The one object in the world that knew where it was going.

"I've got it," he said.

Marisol looked at him a moment longer than the answer required, then grunted and moved on.

* * *

They made camp at midday in the lee of a granite shoulder, because Marisol said the weather was thinking about changing and she liked to be settled before the sky finished its sentence. Teo didn't know how she read it — the clouds looked identical to yesterday's clouds, and the day before's — but he'd stopped doubting her readings. She'd been right about the wind funnel in the col. She'd been right about the ice on the north-facing slabs. Being wrong, Teo suspected, had cost her too much ever to risk it casually again.

He built the fire while Yara melted snow, and it was during this ordinary labor — hands busy, mind loose — that he noticed the needle behaving strangely.

He took the compass out to check, certain he was imagining it. He wasn't. The pull had changed character. Before, it had been a steady insistence, a drawn line toward the pass. Now it trembled, quickened, the way a tuning fork hums when its twin is struck across a room. The needle strained against its pivot as though the thing it pointed toward were close enough to touch.

"We're near something," he said.

Yara looked up from the pot. Wind-flushed, hair escaping from under their cap, they had the wary expression they wore whenever Teo announced anything about the compass — the expression of someone who wanted to believe and had trained themselves out of wanting things. "Near what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Teo stood, turning slowly with the compass flat in both hands. The needle held its bearing through the whole rotation, stubborn as scripture. "It's stronger than this morning. Stronger than an hour ago. Whatever it's pointing at—" He stopped. Turned another quarter-circle. The needle didn't waver. "It's not just direction anymore. It's proximity."

Marisol had gone very still by the packs. Teo had catalogued her stillnesses over the past week the way he catalogued everything — it was how he'd survived eight years as Voss's apprentice, learning which silences meant boredom and which meant danger — and this one was new. Not fear exactly. Recognition.

"There's a cairn," she said slowly. "Up ahead. Half a day, maybe less, if the ledge holds."

"A cairn," Yara repeated.

"A shrine, really. Pilgrims built them along the old free routes, before the guild choked off the trails. Waymarkers, offerings, letters sometimes. People leaving word for whoever came after." Her jaw worked. "I haven't been up here since—" She didn't finish. She never finished that sentence. The expedition hung behind her like a coat she couldn't take off. "If your compass is pulling toward a shrine, then someone left something there. Someone who knew what the needle responds to."

"And who would know that?" Yara asked, though Teo could hear in their voice that they already knew. They all did.

"Someone who carried it first," Teo said quietly.

* * *

They broke camp early, which Marisol hated — you didn't move fast on tired legs at altitude, she said, tired legs were how people died — but none of them suggested waiting. There are some pulls that don't permit patience.

The ledge Marisol had mentioned proved to be a long traverse across a face of grey rock streaked with snowmelt, wide enough for one, exposed enough that Teo kept his eyes fixed on his boots and his hand on the rock and let the others' breathing tell him where they were. Below the ledge the mountain fell away in a series of grey steps into a valley full of cloud. Above it, the rock rose sheer. They moved through a horizontal world, a seam between floor and ceiling, and Teo thought — not for the first time — that maps lied. His master's maps, the guild's maps, the beautiful engraved sheets he'd spent his apprenticeship copying, all of them rendered this as a blank margin or a decorative squiggle. None of them conveyed the smell of cold stone, the way sound traveled oddly, the sense of being a scratch on the surface of something vast and indifferent.

None of them conveyed the compass going mad in his pocket.

By the time the ledge widened into a shallow alcove, the needle was shivering so hard he could feel it through the fabric, a faint buzzing against his ribs like a trapped insect. He pulled the compass out and the needle spun once, twice — and then snapped to a point six feet away and *locked*, rigid, quivering at its tip.

The cairn stood in the alcove's deepest corner, sheltered from the worst of the weather, and someone had built it to last. Not a casual heap of stones but a careful construction, flat slabs fitted together, waist-high, crowned with a capstone. Years of wind had polished it. Someone had sealed the joints with something dark — pitch, maybe — and carved into the capstone, in letters worn nearly smooth but still legible, a single line:

*FOR WHOEVER THE ROAD BRINGS.*

Nobody spoke. The wind filled the silence, pouring over the lip of the alcove above them and streaming down the rock face in visible ribbons of rime — the first hint, Teo realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, of the phenomenon the legends called the Sable Current. Snow that flowed like water. A river in the sky. His cartographer's soul ached to see it properly, and his apprentice's caution noted that they were standing directly beneath whatever fed it.

"It's her work," Yara said. Their voice was strange — flattened, careful, the voice of someone holding something fragile very still inside their chest. "The fitting of the stones. She built dry-stone walls behind our house. I used to—"

They stopped. Teo watched them press their palm flat against the capstone, over the carved words, and saw their shoulders rise once and hold.

"Do you want me to?" he asked softly.

Yara shook their head. Took a breath. And began, with the same unhurried competence they brought to knots and cargo manifests, to lift the capstone.

Beneath it, the cairn was hollow. A cavity lined with more flat stones, dry despite twenty years of mountain weather — the pitch-sealing, Teo thought, the woman had engineered the whole structure to shed water. And in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff and amber with age, a bundle.

Yara lifted it out as if it might dissolve. Kneeling in the scree, they unwrapped the cloth with fingers that weren't quite steady, and Teo knelt beside them without deciding to, and Marisol came and stood at the mouth of the alcove with her back to them, keeping watch, giving them privacy the only way the mountains allowed.

Inside the oilcloth lay a second letter. Thicker than the first, sealed with wax that had cracked but held, and beneath it — Teo's breath caught — a small square of oilcloth containing a spare needle, a vial of oil, a folded diagram of the compass's mechanism in a precise, confident hand. She had expected the instrument to need repair. She had planned for decades of distance.

"She knew someone would follow," Teo whispered.

"She hoped," Yara said. "There's a difference." They turned the letter over. The wax bore the impression of a thumbprint, pressed deliberately, and below it two initials: *A.O.* "She sealed it with her own print. So there'd be proof it was hers. So no one could claim it was forged." A sound escaped them that was almost a laugh. "Twenty years dead to the world and she's still thinking about evidence."

"Open it," Marisol said quietly, without turning around. "Before the light goes."

Yara broke the wax.

* * *

The paper was soft with age, the ink faded to brown, but the handwriting was strong and slanted and sure of itself. Yara read it aloud, because Teo asked them to — because he understood, in the way of someone who had lost people and never gotten to hear their voices again, that a letter read silently is a different thing from a letter given air. Yara's voice roughened on the first line and steadied after that, the way a boat steadies once it commits to the current.

*To my child, whoever you become.*

"If you are reading this, then the needle found you, which means you are mine by blood or by choice, and either is enough. I have written this letter many times in my head over many years, and every version began with an apology, and I have decided that apologies are a coward's opening. So instead I will begin with the truth:

*I am alive, or I was when I wrote this, and I chose to let you believe otherwise.*

"I will not explain myself here. Explanations belong to the living, and I hope — I pray — that we will speak face to face, and that this letter will seem a clumsy rehearsal for a conversation I have imagined ten thousand times. But if we do not speak — if the mountain keeps me, or worse, if the guild does — then I need you to know three things, and I need you to know them cleanly, without the fog of grief.

"First: the pass called the Sable Current belongs to our family. Not to the guild, whatever their charters claim. My grandmother carried the grant papers over these very stones, and the seal that proves it is set into the compass casing itself, hidden where no clerk would think to look. The pass was granted in perpetuity to be free for all who travel. Free, do you understand? That word is the whole inheritance. Everything else is stone and snow.

"Second: the man who wants the pass is not evil, which is worse. Odile Voss believes she is saving something. Do not hate her. Hate wastes warmth, and you will need every degree of warmth you have up here.

"Third — and this is the one I have rewritten most, because it is the hardest —

"I do not know who you became. I left a child and the world will have made you into someone else, and I grieve every version of you I did not get to watch. Whoever you are — whatever name you carry, however you move through the world, whoever you love and whatever you believe — you were seen. Before all of it. You were seen by me, completely, and I loved what I saw, and nothing that happened since has changed that. If you have remade yourself, then remake yourself proudly. A self that is chosen is worth ten that are given.

"The needle points to me because I charged it to. There is a lodestone in the shrine at the summit, and its twin is in the casing. I wanted — forgive me, I wanted one thread that could not be cut. One way home that did not depend on roads or charts or anyone's permission.

"Come find me if you can. Forgive me if you cannot. Either way, you are seen.

"— Your mother, A.O."

The wind poured over the alcove and streamed down the face in ribbons of white, and for a long moment nobody moved.

Teo found that his eyes were burning. He looked away, out at the cloud-filled valley, and pretended to study the weather, and was not fooled for a second by himself.

*Whoever you became.* He thought of his own mother, who had sold him to the cartographer's guild at nine years old with a kiss and a promise she hadn't kept. He thought of eight years of being *the apprentice*, a function rather than a person, useful and interchangeable. He thought of the night he'd run, taking nothing but his field kit and a stolen glance at Voss's private correspondence, and how for three days afterward he had felt not free but simply *unowned*, which was a colder thing.

And this woman — this stranger twenty years vanished — had sat down somewhere above the clouds and written *whoever you become* as though it were a blessing instead of a loss. As though the becoming itself were the point.

"You're crying," Yara said. Not unkindly. Surprised.

"No I'm not."

"All right." Yara folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, and Teo saw that their own eyes were wet and that they had also decided not to be crying. Something passed between them in that moment — not sympathy exactly, something drier and more durable. Two people agreeing, without negotiation, that some things would be acknowledged later or never.

Marisol turned from the mouth of the alcove. Her face was doing something complicated. "The summit shrine," she said. "I know it. Or I knew of it. The old guides called it the Anchor. We were supposed to reach it on the expedition." Her voice snagged on the last word. "We never got that far."

"Then Voss doesn't know about it," Teo said. The thought arrived fully formed, the way his best map-insights always did, sudden and complete. "If your party never reached the Anchor, and the guild's records show the route ending where your expedition ended—"

"Then Voss is climbing blind above the treeline," Marisol finished. A grim light entered her face, the first expression resembling hope Teo had seen her wear. "She'll have hired lowland climbers. Good ones, maybe, but they'll be following my old survey notes, and my old survey notes stop at the rope bridge."

"Then we have time," Yara said. They rose, tucking the letter inside their coat, next to the first one, and Teo watched them stand over the opened cairn for a moment — a person receiving a message across twenty years of silence, and standing straighter under its weight rather than buckling.

But Teo was looking at the compass.

Because the needle had changed again.

During the reading it had eased — the frantic trembling settling into that steady, patient pull he'd come to think of as its resting state. Now, as Yara tucked the letters away, the needle stirred, strengthened, strained — and Teo realized with a slow, rising chill that it wasn't pointing at the summit shrine anymore.

It was pointing down-trail. Back the way they'd come.

Toward something following them up the mountain.

"Yara," he said. His voice came out flat and quiet, the way voices do when they're trying not to startle anyone, including themselves. "Take a look at this."

Yara crouched beside him. Marisol crowded in. The three of them stared at the needle straining southward like a finger accusing the horizon.

"That's the direction of the border towns," Marisol said slowly. "That's the direction of the guild."

"Or," Teo said, and his mouth had gone dry, "it's the direction of whoever's climbing behind us."

Above them, far up the rock face where the alcove's shadow ended, the wind shifted — and brought with it, faint and impossible at this altitude, a sound Teo had heard every working day of his apprenticeship and had hoped never to hear again in his life.

A whistle. Three notes, descending. The guild's trail signal.

Voss was on the mountain.
