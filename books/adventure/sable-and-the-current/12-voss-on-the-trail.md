## 12. Voss on the Trail

The rain had followed Odile Voss out of the lowlands for three days, and she had stopped pretending to mind it. Rain was honest. Rain told you exactly what it wanted and then took it. It was the guild that lied — with its brass plaques and its ledgers and its talk of *service* — and Voss had spent thirty years learning to read between its lines.

She stood at the edge of the staging yard in Harrowgate, watching her hired climbers load mules under canvas awnings, and let the water run off the brim of her oilskin hat without wiping it away. Six of them so far. She needed eight. Good ones cost coin; cheap ones cost more, later, when they froze or panicked or ran. She had learned that arithmetic on her first contract, at nineteen, hauling toll crates up a switchback while a man named Pell slipped on wet granite and fell two hundred feet into fog. The guild had docked her pay for the lost crate.

She had not forgotten the crate. That was the trouble with her, people said. She never forgot anything.

"Captain." Dren, her second, came up under the awning shaking water from his shoulders like a dog. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, but his hands were still sure on rope, and he had never once asked her a question she didn't want answered. "The ferry guild's man is here. From Verre Landing. Says the Okafor woman sold her boat."

Voss turned. Rain sheeted off the awning's edge between them, a gray curtain.

"Sold it? To whom?"

"A broker named Cassel. Paid off her debt in full, by all accounts. Then bought passage east with a boy — young, bookish, carrying a map case. And a third joined them outside town. A woman. Big shoulders, drinking like she's trying to drown something."

Voss was quiet for a moment. In her mind she laid the pieces out the way she laid out route plans: three travelers, one of them a disgraced guide, moving east toward the range weeks before any sane person would climb it. Carrying a compass that did not point north.

*Marisol Ferro.* Of course. Voss had put the name together two nights ago from a saloon keeper's description and an old expedition roster she'd had copied from the guild archive at considerable expense. Marisol Ferro, who had led Adaeze Okafor's last expedition into the mountains twenty years ago and come back alone, minus four climbers and minus every scrap of credibility. The guild had struck her name from the rolls. Voss had read the inquiry transcript twice. It was a masterpiece of blame distributed so evenly that no one could tell where the fault actually lived — which usually meant it lived somewhere expensive.

And now Ferro was climbing again, toward the pass everyone believed was a myth, beside the drowned woman's child.

"The boy," Voss said. "Teo. Describe him again."

"Seventeen, maybe eighteen. Guild livery under a borrowed coat — cartographer's division. Voss—" Dren hesitated, and she watched him decide whether to say the next thing. He always decided yes, eventually. "He was assigned to your office last spring. The survey copying. You signed his work papers yourself."

"I know I did." She remembered him, too. A thin, serious boy with ink-stained fingers who'd asked her once, apropos of nothing, why the guild's maps showed the eastern range as blank above the tree line. *Because no one has surveyed it,* she'd told him. *Because it isn't worth the cost.* He'd looked at her the way apprentices look at you when they think you're lying and are too polite to say so. Apparently he'd grown less polite since.

"So we chase them," Dren said. "Into the high country, early season, after a guide who knows the ground better than any of us."

"We follow them," Voss corrected. "There's a difference. They'll make mistakes. We won't have to make any — we can just wait for theirs."

"And if they reach the pass first?"

Voss looked east, where the rain blurred the foothills into a suggestion, a charcoal smudge under a lower sky. Somewhere past that smudge, in country no guild map acknowledged, a corridor of wind-carved stone supposedly existed where snow streamed like a river and no toll had ever been collected. Twenty years ago she had stood in a room with the guild's founders and listened to them explain, with the patience of men discussing drainage, why such a pass could not be permitted to exist. Not because it was impossible. Because it was *free.*

A free road through the range would gut the northern tolls in a single season. It would gut the ferry charters, the waystation leases, the escort fees. It would gut everything Voss had spent her life maintaining, and it would do it not with fire but with *convenience* — travelers simply choosing the open door over the locked one.

Unless the guild held the door.

"Then we arrive second," she said, "and we arrive with paper. Get the mules finished. We leave at first light."

* * *

That night she sat in the taproom of the Wayfarer's Rest with the old expedition file open under her elbows, and she read it again even though she knew it by heart, because there were nights when the file was the only conversation available to her.

Her father had paid tolls. That was where it started, if she was honest, though she rarely was, even with herself. Every market day of Odile Voss's childhood, her father had loaded their cart with turnips and salt fish and driven it through the Kestrel Gate, and every market day the gate clerk had taken a tenth of the load's value for the privilege of the road, and every market day her father had smiled at the clerk as if the smile might someday be worth something. It never was. When the blizzard year came and the turnips rotted in the field, the guild's relief office had offered him a loan at interest against the spring crop, and her father had signed it with the same smile, and the smile had died before the loan was paid.

Odile had been eleven. She had sat across from the relief officer, swinging her feet, watching him weigh her family's future on a brass scale, and she had thought — with the terrible clarity of children — *someone should be sitting on that side of the desk who remembers what it's like over here.*

So she had joined them. At sixteen she'd taken the guild exam and scored highest in her cohort. At nineteen she'd lost Pell off a switchback and learned that the ledger cared more about crates than bodies. At twenty-four she'd proposed a graduated toll — nothing for families under a certain means, full rate for freight — and watched the council table reject it in eleven minutes flat, one founder actually laughing, a dry sound like a drawer closing. At thirty-one she'd been made captain of enforcement, the youngest ever, and had understood finally what the position was for. Not reform. Custody. Her job was to keep the doors locked and be the face at the window, and she had done it well, and the doing of it had sanded away whatever it was in her that had once swung her feet under a relief officer's desk.

But the founders were dead now, most of them. The new council was softer, greedier, more frightened. And the pass — if it existed — was a problem arriving faster than caution could travel.

Which brought her back to Adaeze Okafor.

Twenty years ago, Adaeze had been the guild's best contracted scout. Voss had met her exactly once, in this same taproom, before the expedition departed. She remembered it with humiliating precision: a tall woman with river-gray in her braids, eating bread and cheese with the unhurried attention of someone who knew how to be hungry, and Voss, then a rising enforcement lieutenant, sent over to remind her that the guild's investment in the expedition entitled it to first claim on any routes discovered. Adaeze had listened politely, wiped her hands, and said, "Roads aren't investments, girl. Roads are promises. You don't own a promise. You keep it."

Voss had reported the insubordination. It went in the file. Two months later the expedition vanished, and four names went onto the memorial wall, and Adaeze Okafor's name went onto the drowned list when her boat was found adrift and empty below Widow's Bend. Case closed. File sealed. The guild had paid out a condolence sum to the child — Voss had signed that voucher too, she realized suddenly, she had *signed it*, her name on the paper that closed a dead woman's account — and the whole affair had been folded away like a map along its creases until the creases wore through.

Except the woman hadn't drowned. Voss was sure of it now. The compass, the letter, the child heading east with a disgraced guide — none of it made sense unless Adaeze Okafor had spent twenty years alive in the high country, keeping her promise, guarding her free road. Faking her own death had been the only way to take the secret beyond the guild's reach.

And that was the part Voss understood better than anyone on the council would ever believe she understood. Because Voss knew what it cost to give a thing your whole life and receive custody instead of ownership. Adaeze had chosen the mountain. Voss had been chosen by the guild, and had stayed, and had told herself for thirty years that staying was loyalty when it was really just the fear of finding out who she'd be without the brass and the ledgers.

They were not so different, she and the drowned woman. That was precisely why one of them had to lose.

Dren found her there near midnight, the file closed, her cup untouched.

"Mule count's at nine," he said. "We've got a tenth climber if you want him — local, says he's done the eastern approaches."

"Has he, or has he looked at them?"

"He claims a winter on the Ledges."

"Claims." She turned her cup slowly on the wood. "Take him. On a short rope, where I can see him. Anyone who volunteers for early-season ice is either very good or very hungry, and hungry men lie about their feet."

Dren almost smiled. "And the travelers? Do we take them alive if it comes to that?"

The question hung in the taproom's lamplight. Around them, off-duty teamsters argued over dice, and the fire cracked, and the rain kept up its patient work on the roof. Voss thought about the child at the memorial voucher — a name she'd never bothered to read, just a line item, *one dependent, sum disbursed* — walking east right now with a compass pointed at the mother the guild had declared dead.

"No one dies on my rope if I can help it," she said. "That's always been the rule. But we take the compass, Dren. Whatever it costs short of that."

* * *

They left Harrowgate at first light in a thinning drizzle, nine mules and ten climbers strung out along the east road like beads on a wet cord. By midday the rain quit altogether, and the clouds tore open in the west, and the range stood up in front of them all at once — white-shouldered, blue-shadowed, enormous — and Voss heard two of the hired men stop talking mid-sentence just to look at it.

She let them look. Awe was useful. Awe kept men careful.

From a ridge above the river fork, Dren found the sign: cold campfire ash, a latrine trench dug proper, hoofprints of a single pack animal, and boot prints — three sets — heading up the valley toward the old shrine trail. Fresh. A day old, maybe less.

"They're moving fast for greenhorns," Dren said, crouching over the prints.

"One of them isn't a greenhorn." Voss crouched beside him and touched the edge of a boot print where the mud had begun to crust. "Ferro sets a pace like this. Look — rest stops timed, water taken at the crossings, camp pitched out of the wind line. She's teaching them as they go." She straightened, and her knee announced itself the way it always did above two thousand feet, and she ignored it the way she always did. "A day behind. Maybe a day and a half if we push."

"Push how hard?"

Voss studied the valley ahead — the narrowing walls, the first dirty patches of old snow lingering in the shade lines, the trail threading upward into country that would kill an unprepared party without malice or effort. Above it all, faint as breath on glass, the high col where the pass was supposed to live.

Weeks early, the snows were already turning. She could feel it in the air's new edge, in the light's thinner gold. Everyone on this mountain — her climbers, the travelers, whoever waited at the top of it — was now racing the same clock, and the clock did not care whose claim was legal.

"As hard as keeps us alive," she said. "Set the pace yourself. And Dren — if you find anything on the trail that looks like a shrine, a cairn, a marker, anything with writing on it, you bring it to me before anyone else touches it."

He gave her a look. "Expecting letters, Captain?"

"I'm expecting the guild seal," Voss said, "in places the guild has spent twenty years pretending don't exist. Move them out."

The column wound upward into the shadow of the range, and behind them the valley filled with evening, and somewhere above — a day ahead, maybe less — three small figures climbed through the last of the light toward a woman who had beaten them all to the mountain by twenty years.

Voss walked at the head of her rope, counting steps, conserving breath, and did not let herself wonder what Adaeze Okafor would say if she saw her coming.
