## 13. The Rope Bridge

The bridge appeared out of the fog the way ghosts appear in stories Marisol's grandmother used to tell: all at once, and too late to turn back.

One moment they were picking their way along a shelf of gray stone above a gorge that swallowed sound — you could drop a stone off the edge and wait three full breaths before the river answered — and the next the fog thinned like a curtain drawn aside and there it was, strung across the void in a long, sagging arc of hemp and rot.

Marisol stopped so abruptly that Teo walked into her pack.

"Is that—" he began.

"Yes."

"It doesn't look—"

"No," she said. "It doesn't."

She had known it was coming. That was the worst of it. She had known since the second morning out from the saloon, when she'd finally admitted to herself where the compass was pulling them, because she had crossed this bridge before. Eleven years ago. In better weather, with a stronger rope, with six people behind her who trusted her voice more than they trusted their own fear.

Four of those people had names now on a cairn two valleys south.

"Yara." She raised her voice without turning around. "Come up here. You'll want to see this before we're in it."

Yara came up the trail at their easy river-walk, that rolling gait of someone whose body had spent its life compensating for a moving deck, and stopped beside her. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The fog moved through the gorge below them in slow white rivers of its own, and the bridge hung over it like a stitch holding two halves of a wound together.

It was maybe forty paces across. Two cables of hemp, thick as a man's wrist where they anchored into the rock on either side, sagging to a curve that would put its lowest point well below the level of the cliff tops. Between the cables, slats of split larch — some whole, some half-gone, some missing entirely — were lashed down with rawhide gone stiff and gray as old bone. The whole structure swayed very slightly in a wind Marisol couldn't feel on her face, the way a hanged man sways, she thought, and then hated herself for thinking it.

"There was a tower here," she said quietly. "On the far side. A stone anchor tower, with a winch. We rebuilt the western third of it ourselves, my expedition. Replaced eleven slats. I have the numbers in my head because I made everyone count them out loud while they worked. Kept their minds off the drop."

"And now?" Yara asked.

"Now it's been eleven years and nobody's counted anything."

Teo had come up beside them. He was looking at the bridge the way he looked at maps — with an appetite that frightened her, a hunger to understand a thing even when understanding it might be the last thing he did. He leaned out over the edge, and Marisol's hand shot out and closed on his shoulder before she knew she'd moved.

"Don't," she said. Her voice came out harder than she intended. Harder than the moment deserved, maybe. But the boy didn't flinch; he just looked back at her with those cartographer's eyes, patient, cataloguing her the way he catalogued everything.

"The cables look sound," he said. "Whoever built it understood loading. See how they're doubled at the anchors? And the sag is deliberate — it distributes weight instead of fighting it. It's ugly but it's honest engineering."

"Honest," Marisol repeated. "Eleven years of weather, boy. Nothing up here is honest. Everything is just waiting to see what you weigh."

Yara crouched at the near anchor, testing the hemp with their thumbs, pressing into the weave the way Marisol had seen them test rigging on the boat. "It's dry," they said. "No rot smell. Cold enough up here that it doesn't rot fast." They glanced up at her. "You've crossed worse."

*You've crossed worse.* She almost laughed. Yes. She had crossed worse, once, in every sense of the word, and the crossing had cost four lives and her name and everything she had been at twenty-nine years old, which had been a great deal: the most sought-after high guide in the eastern range, a woman whose word on the weather was taken as fact, whose rope teams signed on months in advance.

Adaeze Okafor had been on that expedition too. Adaeze had ordered the retreat that saved the rest of them. But that was not this story, and this was not the place, and Marisol had spent eleven years learning to keep those two things separate — the mountain that killed and the mountain that merely tested — because if she ever let them merge she would never put another boot on stone again.

Except here she was. Boot on stone. Bridge ahead.

"We cross one at a time," she heard herself say, in the guide voice, the old voice, the one that came out of her like water from a deep well whether or not there was anything left to draw. "Full kit stays on. No packs off, no unroping. Yara first — you're lightest on your feet and heaviest in judgment. Then me. Teo last, roped to me, ten feet of slack, no more."

"Why am I last?" Teo asked.

"Because if the bridge goes, I want the person I can reach fastest to be the person I'm responsible for."

He considered that. "That's not a reason. That's just arithmetic."

"Arithmetic keeps people alive on mountains. Reasons are for towns."

Something passed between them then — not warmth exactly, not yet, but the first faint friction that warmth comes from. He nodded and started coiling rope.

* * *

Yara went across the way water goes over stones: without argument, without flourish, reading each slat with their feet before committing their weight, hands riding the upper cable loose and easy. Halfway across, where the sag bottomed out and the bridge dipped toward the fog, they paused, and the whole span swung gently under them, a pendulum forty paces long, and Marisol watched them stand in the middle of that swinging nothing with the stillness of someone who had spent a lifetime being moved by something larger and had learned that the trick was not to fight the motion but to join it.

They reached the far side. Turned. Raised one hand — *come* — and disappeared into the fog beyond the anchor.

Your turn, Marisol told herself. Go on. You've done this a hundred times. You did it in a whiteout with a broken wrist. You did it carrying a man with a compound fracture strapped to your back.

But her boots wouldn't move.

It wasn't fear of the bridge. She could name every failure point on the span, could calculate the load, could tell you within a hand's width where the first slat would go. It was something older and lower than fear, something in the marrow: the memory of standing exactly like this, at exactly this kind of edge, saying *one at a time, full kit, no unroping*, and watching the arithmetic fail anyway. Watching the mountain do what mountains do, which is change its mind. Hearing the crack come up through her own boots. Feeling the rope in her hands go suddenly, terribly light — the specific lightness of a rope that has stopped holding anyone.

Four names. Four cairns. And a fifth name that should have been among them and wasn't, because Adaeze Okafor had shouted *down-rope, everyone, DOWN* in a voice Marisol still heard in the space between sleeping and waking, and had turned back up-slope into the slide zone to reach the last of them, and had not come back down that day, or any day after, according to every report and every search and every year of Marisol's guilt.

*Down-rope, everyone.*

She was gripping the anchor cable so hard her knuckles had gone bloodless. The fog breathed against her face. Somewhere behind her, Teo was saying her name.

"Marisol. Hey. Marisol."

"I'm fine."

"You're shaking."

"Mountains are cold, boy. Shake your way across and stop talking."

But he didn't clip in. She heard him set his pack down instead, deliberately, the buckles clicking, and then he was beside her at the anchor, small and stubborn, looking at the bridge and then at her.

"My mother died on a road," he said.

Marisol closed her eyes. "Teo—"

"She got sick in a toll town and they wouldn't treat her because her papers were guild-stamped from the wrong district. I was nine. I sat outside the door and listened to the number go up — the fee, they read the fee out loud every hour, like an auction." His voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone who has carried a thing so long it has worn smooth. "So when I say this bridge matters, I'm not talking about the view. Voss wants to make the whole range into that doorway. Every pass, every ford, every road. A world where the fee only goes up." He picked up the rope end and held it out to her. "I need to get across. And I think you need to remember that you're the one who knows how."

And there it was — the thing she had been running from since the saloon, offered to her plainly by a nineteen-year-old with frostbitten ears. Not absolution. He hadn't said *you're forgiven* or *it wasn't your fault*. He'd said something better and crueler: *you're needed*. Guilt was a room you could live in forever if nobody asked you to leave it.

Her hands took the rope. She watched them do it as though they belonged to someone else — the old competence moving through her like a current through a dead channel, finding its bed again.

"One at a time," she said. "Ten feet of slack. If I say down, you go flat and you hold and you don't ask why. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

* * *

The first ten paces were almost manageable. The slats creaked but held; the cable hummed under her palms with a low living note, like a mooring line in wind. Below her the fog lay in layers, and through a tear in it she glimpsed the river, absurdly far down, a thread of green-white violence coiling through black rock. Don't look. She fixed her eyes on Yara's silhouette at the far anchor, a dark shape against the pale wall of the gorge, steady as a post.

At fifteen paces the bridge began to sway.

It was nothing at first — a slow lateral drift, the whole span swinging a hand's width to the east, then back. She'd seen it before. You matched your steps to it, let the rhythm carry you, the way you walk a rolling deck. She found the rhythm. Three steps swing, three steps back. Her body remembered. For a few paces she was twenty-nine again and good at this, genuinely good, the best of her generation, and the thought arrived treacherously warm—

—and then the wind came.

It came down the gorge from the north in a single long gust, the kind the high passes send ahead of weather like a herald, and the bridge leapt sideways under her. Not a sway. A jump. Her boot came down on a slat that was no longer where her foot expected it, and the slat cracked — a short dry sound, a knuckle popping — and tilted, and her leg went through to the knee.

The world narrowed to hemp and emptiness.

She caught the upper cable with both hands and hung there, one leg through the gap, the torn larch biting the back of her calf, and below her boots the fog parted as if in courtesy and showed her the whole drop at once: eighty feet of gray air, then the boulder field, then the river going by without any interest in her whatsoever.

*Down-rope,* said a voice in her skull that was not hers. *Everyone, DOWN—*

Move. *Move.* She knew the sequence — elbow lock, knee up, roll onto the cable, redistribute — she had taught it to thirty green climbers in a meadow below the tree line, patiently, a hundred times. But her body would not take the instruction. Her hands were locked on the cable with a grip that had nothing to do with technique, and her breath was coming in shallow sips, and somewhere very far away Teo was shouting her name from the near side, and the bridge was still swinging, gently now, horribly gently, like a cradle.

This is what it was like for them, she thought. This is the last thing. The rope going light. The sky where the mountain used to be.

"MARISOL."

Yara's voice, from the far anchor, cut through everything — not loud so much as absolutely certain, the voice of a captain calling a heading in bad water. "Look at me. Not down. Me. Now move your elbows. One motion. Do it on my count."

And because Marisol had spent her whole adult life obeying competent voices in dangerous places, her body — mercifully, traitorously — began to respond. Elbow. Lock. Knee.

She got her leg up through the gap on the third try. Rolled onto the cable. Lay there straddling it, cheek against rough hemp, breathing fog, while the bridge swayed beneath her like a thing deciding.

Then she heard the crack from behind her.

Not one crack. Several, rapid, in sequence — the sound she knew better than her own heartbeat, the sound of a structure giving up its argument one member at a time. She twisted around on the cable.

Teo was on the bridge.

He was supposed to be waiting at the anchor until she reached the far side — that was the plan, that was the arithmetic — but he was eight paces out, roped to nothing, moving fast, too fast, because the section behind him was going. Slats were letting go in a spreading run like ice breaking off a ledge, dropping away into the fog without even a splash to mark them, and the boy was running for solid ground with his arms out and his face white and his eyes fixed on her with a trust so total it made her sick.

The gap reached him at pace five.

She saw it happen the way you see things in nightmares, frame by frame: the slat tilting under his trailing foot, his ankle turning, his body pitching forward onto the lower cable, both hands slapping hemp and catching — catching — his whole weight snapping onto the line with a groan of fibers, his legs swinging free over eighty feet of nothing, his pack dragging at his shoulders like an anchor.

"Teo!" Her voice tore out of her. "Hold! Hold the line, feet up, wrap your legs—"

He wrapped his legs. Good boy, good boy, he'd listened, he'd listened — but the cable he hung from was anchored to the same failing section, and she could see it, could see the load path running back through the rotten slats to an anchor lashing that was fraying strand by strand with every swing of his body, and there was nothing under her feet, nothing between her and him but air and eleven years of cowardice.

Her hands were frozen on the cable. Her legs would not close around it. Down in the black pit of her, the old voice was saying what it had said for eleven years: *you froze then too, you froze and Adaeze went up instead of you, and it will happen again, it always happens again, you are the reason the rope goes light—*

The frayed lashing gave one more strand with a sound like a snapped tooth.

Eight paces of broken bridge between her and the boy.

She could not move.

Behind her, impossibly far and impossibly close, she heard boots hit the far anchor platform — Yara, already moving, already shouting something she couldn't parse over the wind and her own blood — and the last strands of the lashing began, slowly, softly, to part, one fiber at a time, like a promise being unwritten.
