## 14. Yara Catches

The bridge held Teo for exactly four steps.

Yara heard it before they saw it — a sound like a harp string snapping somewhere in the mountain's chest, a single high note that had no business existing at this altitude. Then the near anchor post lurched, the whole span of braided hide and rotted hemp swinging out over the gorge like a pendulum cut loose from its clock, and Teo went with it.

They were twenty feet behind him on the ledge. Twenty feet of scree and frost-slicked stone between Yara and the moment when the boy's boots left the planks.

"Teo!" Marisol's voice cracked across the wind, but she was frozen — Yara saw it plainly, saw her standing at the bridgehead with her hands half-raised and nothing coming out of them, the same look Yara had caught flickering across her face since the saloon, the look of a woman watching something happen twice.

The bridge swung. Teo clung to the side ropes with both arms wrapped, his satchel of maps and instruments banging against his hip, his legs kicking over a drop that went down so far the river at its bottom was only a rumor of white.

Yara did not think. That was the truth of it, and they would carry the truth of it afterward: there was no decision, no weighing, none of the careful arithmetic they applied to cargo loads and tide tables and guild debts. There was only the body knowing what it knew — twenty years of reading water, of feeling where a current wanted to take a thing and putting yourself where it would have to come past you.

The bridge's swing had a rhythm. Everything did. Rivers, ropes, falling people. Yara ran the ledge, boots skidding on shale, and threw themselves flat at the point where the arc of the swing would bring Teo closest to the rock, arms out over the void.

His hand hit theirs like a gaff hook finding flesh.

The weight of him nearly took them over. For one long, screaming second Yara felt their whole body become a lever with the world on one end and nothing on the other, and then their boots found a crack in the stone, an old fault line filled with ice, and they jammed their toes into it and held.

Teo's fingers were slipping. His palms were wet with cold sweat and the rope-burn of the side cables, and every second he hung there the grip got worse. Below him the gorge breathed its cold up at them, patient as deep water.

"I've got you," Yara said. They didn't know if it was true. "Look at me. Not down. Me."

Teo looked. His face was gray, his lips moving around words that wouldn't form, and his eyes — his eyes were fixed on Yara's face with the absolute, drowning trust of someone who has no other option, and that trust weighed more than his whole body.

"Marisol." Yara's voice came out steady, which surprised them. "The anchor. The far side is still fastened. If you can get to the low rocks below us, we can swing him to the ledge."

Marisol moved. Whatever had locked her broke all at once, like ice going out on a spring river, and she was scrambling down through the boulders with a competence that made clear exactly how much skill lived under the drink, calling measurements up to Yara in a guide's clipped shorthand — *two arm-lengths of slack, mind the frayed strand, on my count* — and when she said *now*, Yara swung Teo in a wide arc, once, twice, building the rhythm back into the broken thing, and let go.

He hit the ledge beside Marisol hard enough to knock the breath out of him, and then he was just a shape on the stone, curled small, breathing in great whooping gulps, alive.

Yara lay back on the cold rock and stared at the sky and laughed. It came out of them sideways, ragged, not entirely sane — the laugh of a person whose body has just informed them, in arrears, of everything it was afraid of.

* * *

They didn't cross the gorge that day. There was no crossing it; the bridge was gone, its far anchor still knotted to a post on the opposite cliff like a severed tendon, and the detour Marisol mapped out would cost them half a day's climb north to a shallower ford of scree. They made camp instead in a hollow below the broken bridgehead, out of the worst of the wind, and Yara built a fire while Marisol checked Teo for damage.

He was mostly fine. Rope burns across both palms, a bruise blooming on his ribs where the satchel had swung into him, and a tremor in his hands that he kept trying to hide by holding them near the flames. He'd lost his ink bottle over the edge. He mentioned it three times, each time in the same stunned tone, as though the ink were the true catastrophe and the fall merely incidental.

"It was the blue one," he said. "I mixed it myself. You can't buy that shade anymore, the formula came from my master's master, and now it's in the river."

"The river will make good use of it," Yara said.

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. Ink goes back to water eventually. Everything does." They fed a stick to the fire. "You'd know that better than anyone right now."

Teo flinched. Yara watched the flinch land and regretted it even as they understood why they'd said it — because the fear needed somewhere to go, and they were angry, and anger always looks for a door.

Marisol saw it too. She settled onto her bedroll with the deliberate calm of a woman positioning herself between two dogs. "Nobody died," she offered.

"Somebody almost did," Yara said. Their voice came out harder than they intended. "That's what I keep landing on. Almost."

"So we don't do it again," Teo said quickly. "We find another way around. There's always another way around—"

"There isn't." Marisol said it quietly, without cruelty, the way you report weather. "Not ahead. The range narrows from here. Every route to the Sable Current crosses something that wants to kill you — bridges, icefalls, ledges no wider than your boot. I've been telling you both since the saloon. This is what the mountain is. It doesn't negotiate."

"Then maybe," Teo said, and his voice shook, and he pushed through it, "maybe we should say it out loud. What it costs. Because nobody has. We've been walking for weeks and nobody has once said, plainly, that one of us might die out here. That *all* of us might."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind worked at the broken anchor post with a low wooden groan.

"You want it said plainly?" Yara turned to face him. "Fine. Plainly: yesterday I woke up thinking about whether we had enough salt. Today I stood on a ledge and let a boy put his life in my hands over a hole in the ground, because if I hadn't, I'd have spent the rest of mine listening to him fall. That's the price. That's what it costs. Are you satisfied?"

"No," Teo said. "Because that's not an answer. That's just — you being brave at me."

"I'm not brave."

"You jumped off a cliff!"

"I read a current!" Yara heard their own voice rise and couldn't stop it. "That's all I did. The bridge swung, and swings have a rhythm, and I've spent my whole life learning rhythms because the river doesn't forgive you for guessing wrong. I didn't jump off a cliff out of courage. I did it because my hands knew the motion before I did. And my hands learned it hauling cargo for a guild that owns my debt, and if I'd guessed wrong today, Teo, if that crack in the rock had been an inch to the left, then my mother dies alone in a cabin on a pass I never reached, and you die with your maps unfinished, and Marisol—" they stopped, because Marisol had gone very still, "—Marisol gets to watch it happen twice."

Silence. The wind filled it, worrying at the fire, dragging sparks sideways into the dark.

"That's not fair," Teo said finally, but softly now. "What you said about Marisol."

"No," Yara admitted. "It wasn't."

But Marisol spoke before either of them could mend it. She was looking into the fire, and her voice had the flat evenness of a story told many times to oneself in the dark.

"On the expedition," she said, "there was a crossing too. A meltwater channel, nothing like this — wider, but gentler. I made the call to send people across in pairs, to save time. We were behind schedule. My schedule." Her jaw worked. "The third pair went in. A man named Ovid and a boy of nineteen, Fenn. The channel took Fenn. Ovid went in after him. Neither came out, and I stood on the bank and did the arithmetic — four more lives against two, the mission against the men — and I chose the mission, and I have been doing that arithmetic every night for twenty years, and I will tell you what I've learned about it."

She looked up. Her eyes were dry and terrible.

"The arithmetic never balances. There is no version of the sum where you come out clean. You only get to choose which weight you carry." She nodded toward the broken bridge, black against the stars. "Today I froze. First time in twenty years I've been on a crossing, and I froze, and this child—" a rough gesture at Teo, "—nearly died in the space where my courage used to be. So when you ask whether the journey is worth dying for, captain, understand that I'm not asking the mountain that question anymore. I asked it already. The answer was no. And then your mother pulled me out of a bottle in a border town and gave me one road left, and I'm walking it because the alternative is carrying the arithmetic forever."

Teo had gone pale again, but differently now. "You could have told us," he said. "Before."

"And you'd have what? Left me at the last village? Sent me back?" Marisol almost smiled. "Apprentice, I am the last guide willing to walk this range. You need me the way I need this. Let's not pretend otherwise tonight."

"I'm not pretending anything," Teo said, and then, quieter, staring at his burned hands: "I ran away from Voss because she wanted to own what I could see. That's all cartography ever was to her — territory, and who controls the chart controls the toll. But I keep maps because—" he stopped, started again. "My father was a ferryman. On the Ilse. He drowned when I was six, and nobody ever drew the stretch of river where it happened accurately, because the guild wouldn't pay for a survey of water they didn't tax. So there's a place where my father died, and officially it doesn't exist. I make maps so places exist. So people exist. That's my whole reason. And today I almost became a place that doesn't exist, and the only person who'd have drawn it was me, and my blue ink is at the bottom of a gorge."

He laughed — a wet, cracked sound, halfway to crying — and Yara found themselves laughing too, helplessly, the two sounds tangling together over the fire until Marisol shook her head and muttered something about madmen and apprentices and reached over to turn Teo's watered wine into unwatered wine.

* * *

Later, when Teo slept — truly slept, the boneless sleep of the emptied-out — Yara sat up keeping the fire and found Marisol awake beside them, as she seemed to always be, whittling nothing in particular off a stick of pine.

"He's tougher than he looks," she said.

"They usually are." Yara fed the fire. "I was harsh with him."

"You were honest with him. He'll decide which one it was by morning." The knife whispered. "For what it's worth — what you did on that ledge. Reading the swing. I've climbed with guild men who trained their whole lives and couldn't have done it. Instinct like yours can't be taught. Only inherited or drowned for."

"My mother had it," Yara said, before they could stop themselves. "On the river. Everyone said so. She could feel a sandbar shift before the water showed it." They stared into the flames. "Twenty years I thought she abandoned us. Ran off chasing a legend and left a child and a boat and a debt. And now I'm following her — same mountains, same obsession, same refusal to turn around — and today I finally understand it wasn't abandonment. It was inheritance. She gave me the wanting and I've been carrying it my whole life without knowing what it was."

Marisol was quiet a moment. "And is that a comfort?"

"It's a wound that finally has a name." Yara huffed something like a laugh. "Which is most of what comfort is, out here."

The wind shifted, and with it came a sound from the north — faint, rhythmic, almost lost under the mountain's noise. Yara raised their head. Marisol had gone still, knife stopped mid-stroke.

"Pitons," she said. "Hammer on stone. More than one hammer." She met Yara's eyes across the fire, and the old shame in her face had hardened into something sharper. "Somebody's climbing behind us. Fast."

"How far?"

"A day. Maybe less, if they're pushing nights." She spat into the coals. "Voss."

In the dark beyond the firelight, Teo slept on, one bandaged hand curled near his satchel, and the broken bridge groaned overhead like a thing mourning itself, and Yara looked north into the black where the hammers rang and felt the current change beneath them — the way they always felt it first in their bones, before the water ever showed its face.
