## 15. Weather Turning

Teo woke to a sound he had spent three years learning to dread: the soft, wrong silence of snow falling on canvas.

It was not rain. Rain ticked and ran and argued with the tent. Snow said nothing. It simply accumulated, the way debts accumulated, the way a map's blank spaces accumulated until someone had to go and fill them or lose the territory entirely. He lay still in the dark of the tent, listening to Marisol's snores from the far side and Yara's quieter breathing between them, and he understood, with the particular clarity of a boy who had grown up in a cartographer's office, that the window was closing.

He unzipped the tent flap an inch and looked out.

The world had been rewritten overnight. What had been a grey bowl of scree and lichen-stained stone, the high basin they'd climbed into the evening before, was now white from rim to rim — not deep snow, not yet, but a skin of it, an inch maybe two, enough to erase every path they'd picked out by lamplight. Enough to make the cairns they'd built look like small, foolish gravestones. The flakes still came down, fat and unhurried, spinning out of a sky the color of wet slate.

"First snow," he said to no one. "Weeks early."

In the survey office in Corvane, where Teo had apprenticed under Master Aldric Vane before Voss's people had come sniffing around the guild's mapping contracts, there was a ledger of first snows. Forty years of them, kept in a spidery hand by a clerk who had nothing else to live for. The high passes around the Sable Current, according to that ledger, opened in late spring and closed in the second or third week of Frostmonth. Teo had memorized the column without meaning to. It was the kind of fact that lodged in him the way river silt lodged in a boat's seams.

It was not Frostmonth. It was not even close to Frostmonth. And it was snowing.

He sat back on his heels and did the arithmetic he'd been doing in fragments for two days, the arithmetic he hadn't said out loud because saying it would make it a fact instead of a fear. The pass, if Marisol's memory and the compass needle could be trusted together, was four days' hard climbing away. Five, if the weather turned properly bad. Six, if someone twisted an ankle or the scree field below the col had iced overnight. And behind them —

Behind them, he thought, and his stomach did the small cold flip it always did — behind them was Odile Voss.

* * *

Yara emerged from the tent the way they emerged from everything: all at once, already moving, as if sleep were a berth they'd only docked at briefly. They stood in the falling snow with their arms crossed, breath steaming, and looked at the basin the way Teo had seen them look at a river gone strange — reading it, weighing it, deciding what it was going to be allowed to do to them.

"Tell me I'm wrong," Teo said.

"You're not wrong." Yara crouched and pressed two fingers into the snow, then rubbed them together, tasting it the way they'd taste silt. "Dry snow. Cold snap came down fast. This isn't a squall passing through — this is the season arriving early and meaning it."

Marisol crawled out last, hair wild, one boot unlaced, and stood blinking at the white basin like a woman who'd been shown a photograph of a place she'd buried someone. Teo had learned to watch for that look on her face — the flicker where the present slid sideways into the past. It came often now, the higher they climbed. He suspected it came even more often than she let him see.

"Early snow," she said. "On the expedition — Adaeze's expedition — we had a dusting like this on the fourth day out. Someone said it was a fluke. A joke. *The mountain's saying hello.*" Her jaw worked. "Eleven days later we couldn't see our own hands."

"Cheerful," said Yara. But they said it gently, and Teo noticed — he noticed everything about the two of them now, the way he noticed contour lines — that Marisol almost smiled.

They broke camp fast, the three of them moving in the wordless economy that had grown up among them without anyone announcing it. Yara struck the tent. Marisol coiled rope and checked the pitons with a grim, professional tenderness. Teo spread his field map on a flat stone, weighted the corners with pebbles, and marked their position with a numb blue thumb.

The map had changed since they'd left the river. That was the thing he couldn't stop thinking about, lying awake at night in whatever shelter they'd found: the map was no longer a copy of anything. Master Vane had taught him from plates, from surveys, from the guild's great locked folios, and everything Teo had ever drawn had been a rendering of someone else's knowing. This map — this one, inked in the margins of a salvaged survey sheet, corrected nightly by firelight — was his. It was wrong in a dozen places and he knew exactly how it was wrong, which was the beginning of every map that had ever mattered.

The needle had pulled them up the eastern flank of the basin all yesterday, steady as a current. This morning, when he took the compass from its oilcloth wrap, the needle swung and settled toward the northwest notch in the ridge — toward the pass — with a faint, insistent tremble, like a dog straining at a leash.

"Still pointing," Yara said, reading it over his shoulder.

"It's always pointing. It's the *snow* that's new." Teo wrapped it away again. He'd taken to handling it carefully, not because it was fragile — though it was — but because it had started to feel less like an instrument and more like a voice. *This way. Hurry. She's still there. She's still there.*

He didn't say that part out loud either.

They climbed.

The snow made everything harder and everything simpler at once. Harder because every foothold had to be tested, because scree under snow was a liar, because the cold found the gap between Teo's gloves and his sleeves and lived there. Simpler because there was no longer any question of route-finding by eye — the basin had been erased — and so they moved by compass and by Marisol's memory and by Yara's uncanny habit of knowing, before anyone else, when a slope was about to argue with them.

By midday the snow had stopped falling but the sky stayed low and iron-colored, and the wind had come up from the west with a knife's edge to it. They ate cold flatbread and dried fish in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and it was there, chewing and shivering and watching Marisol chip ice out of the water skin, that Teo saw the cairn.

Not one of theirs.

It stood on a shelf of rock fifty yards upslope, half-swallowed by snow: a man-height stack of stones, old, weathered, its top stone darkened with what might have been pitch or tar. It was the kind of marker guild route-wardens built. Teo had catalogued a hundred of them in the folios. He was on his feet before he'd decided to stand.

"That's not one of ours," Marisol said, and there was something in her voice — not fear exactly. Recognition.

They climbed to it together. The stones were fitted in the guild manner, but crudely, hurriedly, the way a warden builds when he's been ordered to build fast. And wedged under the capstone, sheltered from the worst of the weather, was a strip of oilcloth.

Yara unwrapped it. Inside was a paper — a guild requisition chit, the kind used to draw supplies at way-stations — and on the back, in a clerk's quick hand, a note.

*V. party, 14 souls, resupplying. Moving on the col at first light. Snow early — captain says press anyway. — R.*

Teo read it twice. Then a third time, because the numbers wouldn't sit still.

"Fourteen," he said. "Fourteen climbers and Voss."

"Resupplying where?" Marisol took the chit, turned it over, held it to the flat light. "There's a way-station at the Grey Ladders, two days back. This is a Grey Ladders chit, see the stamp." She looked up, and Teo watched her do the same arithmetic he'd been doing since dawn, watched it land on her face. "Two days back. If they resupplied yesterday morning and pressed hard—"

"They're a day behind us," Yara said. "Maybe less."

The wind gusted across the shelf, and the snow on the cairn lifted in a thin white breath and scattered, and for a moment Teo thought of the ledger of first snows, of the spidery clerk's hand, of all the small dry records that were really just ways of writing *hurry* without saying it.

"They'll have the same problem we do," Marisol said. "The snow erases everything. They'll be route-finding by compass and guesswork, same as us. That buys time."

"Voss doesn't need much time," Yara said. "Voss hires people whose whole trade is mountains."

Teo looked at the chit again. *Captain says press anyway.* He thought about what that meant — that Voss had looked at the same early snow he had, and instead of feeling the cold dread he'd woken with, she had felt opportunity. A closing window cut both ways. It cut against the pursued and the pursuer alike, but Voss had fourteen climbers and guild rations and, Teo suspected, a much smaller reserve of mercy for anyone who slowed her down.

He took out his pencil and marked the cairn on his map. A small x, and beside it: *enemy, day behind, pressing.* The pencil felt heavier than it had that morning.

"We can't outrun them to the pass," he said. "Not with fourteen against three. If it comes to a race, we lose the race."

"Then it doesn't come to a race." Yara was looking northwest, at the notch in the ridge where the needle pulled, where the snow streamed off the high rocks in long grey banners. "Marisol. That hidden route of yours. The one you traded to buy us out of the ambush at the col — the one Voss knows you know."

Marisol was quiet for long enough that Teo looked at her. She was watching the banners of spindrift stream off the ridge, and her face had gone still in the way it went still when the past came close.

"Voss knows I know *a* route," she said at last. "She doesn't know which. And there's a difference," she added, "between a route and the route. The one I'd take us on, Voss couldn't follow if she had a map of it in her hand. It doesn't forgive mistakes." A pause. "It doesn't forgive much of anything."

"Neither does the guild," said Yara.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a black seam in the mountainside that Marisol found by some instinct Teo decided not to question. Yara got a small fire going — small, because fuel up here was lichen-crusted dwarf pine that burned fast and smoked worse — and the three of them sat close enough that their shoulders touched, which was, Teo had noticed, how they arranged themselves now without discussing it.

The firelight made a warm room of the cold seam. Beyond it, the snowfield glowed faintly under a sky that had half-cleared, stars showing in ragged patches between the clouds. Teo got his map out and worked by firelight, correcting the day's route, and it was while he was doing this — head down, pencil moving — that he heard Marisol say, quietly, to Yara:

"You know they'll reach the cabin before or with us, if we take the long way. Fourteen of them. They could be sitting inside it when we arrive."

"I know."

"And you're still—"

"I'm still going the way that gets us there alive," Yara said. "A dead heir signs nothing."

Teo's pencil stopped. *Heir.* He'd heard Yara use the word before, back in the lowlands, back when it had been a legal argument — *the compass proves inheritance, the seal proves the grant* — but here, a day's climb from the pass, with the snow coming down and Voss pressing behind them, the word had changed weight. It wasn't a legal term anymore. It was a family one. Somewhere over that ridge was a woman who had spent twenty winters alone in a cabin keeping a road free, and she was dying, and the person sitting three feet from Teo with firelight on their face was her child, and the snow was falling weeks early, and none of the maps in any of the guild's locked folios had ever once recorded anything that mattered this much.

"How long," Teo said, before he could stop himself, "before the snow closes the pass? Really. Not the ledger answer. Your answer."

Marisol fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch.

"The ledger says third week of Frostmonth," she said. "The ledger was written by men who measured from the valley. Up here, the pass closes when the wind decides, and the wind has been deciding early all year." She looked up at the ragged stars. "I've seen it shut in a single night. A corridor like the Sable Current — it's a wind-carved thing, a throat. When the storm sets in, the throat fills. Days to dig out. Weeks, if the snow keeps coming." She turned her head and looked at Teo, and her eyes were very steady. "So the honest answer is: we have maybe eight days of window. Maybe six. After that, nobody goes in and nobody comes out until spring. Not us. Not Voss. Not Adaeze."

*Not Adaeze.*

Teo felt the words go through him like cold water through a hull. He looked at Yara and saw that they had gone very still, the way they went still at the wheel when the river did something the river shouldn't do. Their face didn't change. But their hand, resting on their knee, had closed slowly into a fist, and Teo understood that inside that fist was the whole argument they'd been having with themselves since the cave at Widow's Bend — *hurry, hurry, she's still there* — and that the snow had just picked a side.

"Then we don't take the long way," Yara said. "We take your route, Marisol. The one that doesn't forgive. We take it tomorrow, first light, and we don't stop."

"It's three days of climbing," Marisol said. "Hard climbing. Teo—"

"I'm not the slowest anymore," Teo said, and was surprised to find it was true, and truer still that he didn't mind saying it. "And I'm the one carrying the map. If we go through country no one's charted, you want me where I can see it."

Marisol looked at him for a moment, and something moved behind her eyes — the thing that had been happening more and more between the three of them, the slow, unspoken agreement that they were not, anymore, three strangers sharing a fire. She nodded once.

"First light, then."

They banked the fire and settled into the tents, and Teo lay in the dark listening to the wind come down off the ridge and thinking about clocks. Down in the lowlands, time was kept in bells and ledgers, in the ferry guild's schedules, in the neat columns of first snows. Up here time was kept in snowfall and daylight and the number of days a dying woman had left in a cabin at the top of the world, and the clock had just been set down in front of them where all three of them could see it, ticking in white.

He must have slept, because when he woke the tent was grey with pre-dawn and Yara was already up, and Marisol was standing outside with her head tilted back, looking at the ridge.

Teo crawled out into the cold and followed her gaze.

The banners of spindrift were streaming off the notch again — but thicker now, longer, and the sky behind the ridge had gone a strange bruised yellow at the horizon, the color the sky went before weather, real weather, the kind Marisol had gone quiet about. And below them, far down the basin they'd climbed out of, small and dark and unmistakable in the first light, moved a line of figures.

Fourteen, maybe fifteen. Tiny as ink-marks. Climbing.

"They're through the basin already," Marisol breathed. "They pushed through the night. That's not a day behind us anymore." She turned, and her voice came out flat and hard. "That's half a day."

The wind rose, and the yellow on the horizon deepened, and somewhere above them, in the throat of the mountain, the snow began to fall again — faster now, and finer, and it did not look like it intended to stop.
