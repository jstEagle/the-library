## 16. The Hermit of the Ledges

The ledges appeared the way old sins do — gradually, then all at once.

Marisol saw them first from the col above the frozen waterfall: a staircase of gray stone stitched into the mountainside, each step wide enough for a mule and narrow enough to make a mule think twice about it. Above them, the rock rose in pale shelves like the pages of a book someone had dropped and left where they fell. And tucked into the third shelf, so small she'd have missed it if she hadn't known what to look for, was a curl of smoke.

Smoke meant a fire. A fire meant fuel, and fuel at this altitude meant someone had carried it up, log by log, or had spent twenty years learning which dwarf pines gave up their roots without dying. Either way it meant a person, and a person meant supplies, and supplies meant they might live long enough to see the pass.

"There," she said, and pointed with her chin because her hands were too cold to trust.

Yara followed her gesture and went very still. Teo, half a rope-length behind, nearly walked into them both.

"Someone lives here?" Teo asked. "Up here? On purpose?"

"People live everywhere, boy. That's the whole tragedy of people." Marisol started down toward the ledges before her courage could reconsider. "Keep your hands visible when we get close. Hermits up here are usually hermits for reasons."

She did not say the other thing, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the smoke appeared, heavy as a stone in a pack pocket: *I know whose hermit this is.*

Because there was only one person Adaeze Okafor had trusted on this side of the range after the expedition broke. Only one man who'd been paid in maps and silence and told never to come looking. Marisol had drunk herself stupid trying to forget his name, and had failed at that too, the way she failed at everything except walking uphill.

*Ezra Vane.* Twenty years, and she could still see him sharpening his ice axe by the fire at base camp, humming some lowland tune, saying nothing while everyone argued about whether to turn back.

* * *

The climb down to the ledges took two hours and cost Marisol most of what remained of her composure. The steps were iced in their shadows, and once her boot skated out from under her and only Yara's quick hand on her pack strap kept her from going over the edge into a blue-white nothing. Yara didn't say anything about it afterward. They just re-anchored the rope and kept moving, and somehow that was worse than scolding. It was the courtesy of someone who had already decided how much of her failures they were willing to carry.

Teo counted the ledges aloud as they climbed, because counting calmed him — Marisol had noticed that on the second day, the way he numbered everything, switchbacks, breaths, the seconds between lightning and thunder. "Fourteen," he said now. "Fifteen. Is the cabin on sixteen?"

"Seventeen," Marisol said. "It's always seventeen."

"How would you know? Have you been here?"

Marisol looked at the boy's wind-scoured face, at the frost in his lashes, at the way he held the compass case against his chest like a bird he was afraid of crushing, and she decided the truth could wait until after tea. "Lucky guess," she said.

The seventeenth ledge opened onto a shelf the size of a chapel floor. The cabin sat at its back wall, built of driftwood-gray timber and stone, roofed in slabs of shale lapped like feathers. Smoke rose from a chimney made of stacked tin cans, which should have been ridiculous and instead looked like the most sensible thing Marisol had ever seen. Firewood was stacked under an overhang in cords so neat they might have been planed by machine. Dried herbs hung under the eaves. There were three window boxes, and in them, impossibly, grew small green lettuces under panes of ice-glass.

Yara stopped walking.

Marisol watched them take it in — the orderliness of it, the patience, the particular stubborn tenderness of a garden at nine thousand feet — and she watched something move across their face that she recognized from her own mirror twenty years ago. Hope is a terrible thing to watch happen to someone. It makes them look younger and more breakable at the same time.

"You know this place," Yara said quietly. Not a question.

"I knew the man who built it." Marisol stepped past them and raised her voice. "Ezra Vane! It's Marisol Reyes. I'm coming in slow, and I've got two young ones with me who are colder than I am, and one of them is an Okafor!"

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Marisol to count eleven of her own heartbeats. Then the door opened, and a man stood in it holding a crossbow that he did not lower, and time did its cruel trick of folding twenty years into a single stride.

Ezra Vane had been a big man once, broad through the shoulders, with a beard he kept trimmed sharp as a blade's edge. Now he was all weathered leather and white stubble, thinner, one eye clouded milky with old snow-blindness, but the good eye went straight past Marisol — past her, always past her, even now — and fixed on Yara.

"Saints above," he said. The crossbow dipped. "You have her walk."

* * *

He fed them first. Marisol had forgotten that about him — that Ezra Vane answered every crisis with soup. He set them around a table scarred with knife-marks and map-burns and ladled out a broth of dried mushrooms and barley and something sharp and green that tasted like the mountain had decided to be kind. There was hard cheese. There was flatbread warmed on the stove iron. Teo ate like a boy who had learned not to trust abundance, one careful bite at a time, eyes flicking to the pot to confirm there was more.

Yara ate slowly, watching Ezra the whole time.

"Say it," Yara finally said. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it."

Ezra sat down across from them, folded his ruined hands on the table, and looked at Marisol first — a glance, brief, almost apologetic — before turning back to Yara.

"Your mother is alive."

The words landed in the cabin like a dropped lantern. Marisol heard Teo stop chewing. She heard the stove tick. She heard her own pulse, loud and ashamed, because she had known — had suspected since the shrine cairn, had *known* since Ezra's face at the door — and had said nothing for two hours of climbing, telling herself she was waiting for the right moment, when really she was afraid of being the one who handed Yara hope, because everything she had ever handed anyone had come back broken.

"We found her letters," Yara said. Their voice was steady in the way water is steady right before it goes over the falls. "In the cairn. Written to me. But letters can be old. People write things and then die."

"Not her." Ezra shook his head slowly. "Not yet. She was here in the spring. She'll come again before the deep snows, or I'll go down to the ledges below the pass and leave marks on the stones, the way we do." He tapped the table. "Every year. Twenty years. She comes down twice, maybe three times if the winter is merciful, and we sit at this table, and she eats my terrible soup, and she asks me questions she already knows the answers to, because asking is easier than hoping."

"What questions?" Yara asked.

Ezra's good eye softened. "Whether the river still floods in the Verre valley. Whether the ferry guild's tolls have gone up again. Whether—" He stopped. Started again, gentler. "Whether anyone ever came asking after her. Whether anyone remembered."

The cabin was very quiet. Marisol looked at her bowl because she could not look at Yara's face, and even the bowl blurred.

"She thinks you're dead," Ezra said. "Or she has to pretend she does. Those aren't quite the same thing, but out here they weigh the same." He leaned forward. "Listen to me, child, because this is the part that matters. Your mother didn't vanish. Your mother *hid*. There's a difference, and the difference is the guild."

* * *

He told it in pieces, the way old men tell things, circling, coming back, filling in edges. Marisol had heard fragments of it before — everyone on the broken expedition had carried home a fragment and buried it somewhere private — but hearing it assembled, in this cabin, with Yara's face getting whiter by the minute, was different. It was like seeing a body after knowing only the wound.

Adaeze Okafor had found the Sable Current as a young woman, before Yara was born. Not stumbled on it — *earned* it, guided up by an old woman of the high valleys whose name Ezra still wouldn't speak, because the high valleys kept their names the way banks kept gold. The pass ran free then. Anyone could cross. Herders, pilgrims, smugglers, lovers running from bad marriages — the mountain didn't ask questions. Adaeze had sworn to keep it that way.

Then the ferry guild had grown teeth. Tolls on the river roads. Fees at the bridges. And when the lowland routes were bled dry, the guild had turned its eyes upward, to the last free crossing in the range. They'd offered Adaeze money. Then partnership. Then, when she refused, they'd offered her something else entirely — Marisol saw Ezra's knuckles whiten around his spoon at this part, and understood that whatever came next had been done to someone, and that Ezra had perhaps seen the results.

"So she died," Yara said flatly. "On paper."

"On paper, in a river, witnessed by three men the guild believed were hers." Ezra shrugged. "One of them was me. I wrote the report. I'm rather proud of it; I cried real tears while writing it, which helped. The guild closed the file. And Adaeze Okafor walked up into the mountains and became a ghost with a rifle."

"Why?" Teo asked. His voice cracked on it. "Why stay dead? Why not come back? Why let—" He caught himself, glancing at Yara, but the damage was done, the question was in the room now, huge and cold: *why let her child grow up grieving?*

Ezra didn't flinch. Marisol respected that. Most men would have flinched.

"Because grief ends," he said. "Torture doesn't. If the guild had ever believed she lived — if they'd ever gotten their hands on her, or on anyone who loved her — they'd have taken the route out of her piece by piece. She wasn't choosing to abandon you, child. She was choosing the only shape of protection left to her. It was a monstrous choice. She knows it's monstrous. She carries it like a stone. But she made it, and she's kept it, and every year she asks me whether anyone remembers her, and every year I lie and say no one does, because that's what keeps her safe." He paused. "And every year I think: someday this lie is going to be the cruelest thing I've ever done."

Marisol set down her spoon. "You knew I was coming."

Ezra looked at her then, finally, fully, and his clouded eye and his clear one both held her without mercy. "I knew someone was coming. Word travels on the mountain faster than people do. And I knew that if anyone climbed this far in this season, following that compass, it would either be an Okafor or a fool or both." He exhaled through his nose. "I hoped for both, frankly. An Okafor alone would freeze. A fool alone would fall. Together they have a chance."

"And me?" Marisol asked. "What am I, in your arithmetic?"

"The debt," Ezra said simply. "You're the debt. You were there when it happened, Marisol. You were the guide. Whatever you've done with the twenty years since, the mountain sent you back up it, and the mountain doesn't waste moves." He turned back to Yara before she could decide whether she'd been forgiven or sentenced, and perhaps those were the same thing. "Now. The part you won't like."

Yara laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "There's a part I won't like."

"She doesn't want to be found." Ezra said it plainly, kindly, the way you set a broken bone. "I need you to hear all of it before your hope runs ahead of you. She guards that pass with a rifle and twenty years of practice at shooting near people. She has turned back climbers, surveyors, guild scouts, and last autumn a party of hired men with guild money in their purses. She will not know who you are at first. She may not believe it when you tell her. And even if she believes it—" his voice roughened— "she may send you away anyway. Because sending you away is the last act of love available to her, and she has practiced it a long time."

Yara sat with that. Marisol watched them sit with it, hands flat on the scarred table, jaw working, and thought: *there it is. Hope and hurt in the same breath, exactly as promised.* The child had crossed half a range on the belief that the needle pointed at a grave, and graves at least stayed put. Now the grave had a rifle and a will of its own.

"If she sends me away," Yara said at last, "then she sends me away knowing I'm alive. Knowing I climbed to her. That's not nothing." They looked up. "That's not nothing, and I won't pretend it is."

"No," Ezra agreed softly. "It isn't nothing. It might be everything. It might be the thing that finally lets her put the stone down." He pushed back his chair. "But it will also bring Voss's people to her door, and that, child, is the part that frightens me. Because I've seen what Odile Voss does with things she wants, and I've read the guild's old charters, and if she reaches that pass before the snows seal it—"

He stopped. Outside, the wind changed. Marisol felt it before she heard it — a pressure shift, a flattening of the air, the mountain inhaling — and twenty years of instinct stood up inside her spine.

"That's not wind," she said. "That's weather behind the wind."

Ezra was already moving to the window, squinting his good eye at the western sky, where the blue had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. When he spoke again, all the softness had gone out of his voice, and what remained was the old guide, the one Marisol had followed once into hell.

"Storm's coming early. Two days, maybe less. Which means Voss's climbers will push hard to beat it." He turned from the window and began pulling supplies from his shelves — flour, salt meat, a coil of good rope, a battered tin lamp. "Which means you leave within the hour. And it means, Marisol Reyes, that you're going to have to tell them the rest of it. About the hidden route. About what happened on your expedition. About what Adaeze ordered you to do." His good eye held hers, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible fairness of old men who have run out of time for kindness that lies. "Because if they're going to meet her, they deserve to arrive knowing whose footsteps they're walking in."

Marisol looked at the flour, the salt meat, the rope. At Yara's face, waiting. At Teo's, frightened and fierce.

Outside, the first flakes came down fine as sifted ash, and the mountain began, very quietly, to close its accounts.
