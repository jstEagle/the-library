## 17. Yara's Name

The storm had passed in the night, leaving the sky scoured clean and cold as river glass. They made camp early that evening in a hollow below a granite shoulder, where a stand of wind-bent pines broke the worst of the gusts and a dry streambed offered a fire ring of stones someone else had stacked long ago. Marisol declared it good ground with the authority of a woman who had slept on worse, and Teo built the fire with the fussy precision he brought to everything — kindling sorted by thickness, flame coaxed upward like something being taught to stand.

Yara sat back against their pack and let the heat find their face.

They were three days above the tree line now, or nearly; the pines here were stunted things, more shrub than tree, clawing sideways out of the rock. Below them the valley they'd climbed out of was already a rumor, a blue smear between ridges. Above them the mountain went up and up into a sky going violet at the edges, and somewhere past all that white wall waited the Sable Current, and — Yara would not quite say it yet, even inside their own skull — *her*.

Three days. Maybe four, if the weather turned again.

Marisol was cooking. She cooked the way she did everything now, with a grim attentiveness, as though each pot of barley and salt pork might be the one that redeemed her. The smell was better than it had any right to be. Teo had his journal open across his knees, drafting by firelight, his pencil making small industrious scratches. He'd been quiet all day. Not sullen-quiet, which Yara knew well from him, but thinking-quiet, the kind that meant a question was being carried around like a stone in a pocket, turned over and over until its edges wore smooth enough to ask.

Sure enough, when the barley was eaten and the plates scraped and Marisol had lit her pipe with the ceremony she allowed herself exactly once per day, Teo set down his pencil and said:

"Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

"You've been winding up to it since the switchbacks," Yara said. "Ask."

Teo glanced at Marisol, who blew a smoke ring and studied it with elaborate disinterest, which was her way of saying *I'm listening too but I'll die before admitting it.*

"The name," Teo said. "Yara. Marisol called you by your old name once, back at the saloon, before she knew better, and you didn't yell, but you went still. Like a heron. And I've been wondering ever since — not what it was, I don't care about that — but how you chose this one. Where it came from." He turned his pencil over in his fingers. "I ask because names are my whole trade, sort of. Maps are mostly names. Somebody has to decide what a thing is called, and then everybody downstream lives inside that decision for two hundred years. So I think about it. Who got to choose. Whether they chose well."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, snowmelt ticked through the rocks.

Yara looked into the flames for a while. It wasn't reluctance, exactly. It was that the story lived in a part of them they rarely visited — the way you don't visit a room in your own house because everything in it is already arranged just so, and moving anything would mean deciding, again, whether the arrangement was right.

But these two had earned the visit. Teo had jumped aboard a stranger's boat rather than draw toll maps for Voss. Marisol had walked back into the mountains that ate her reputation, carrying twenty years of guilt like ballast. They had both, in their different ways, remade themselves. It seemed only fair to show them the place where Yara had done the same.

"All right," Yara said. "But it starts on the river, so bear with me."

* * *

"I grew up on the Verre, or near enough. You know that much. What you don't know is that the woman who raised me — after my mother vanished — was a net-mender named Bess Okafor, who took in half-orphaned river kids the way other people take in strays, except she charged rent and kept accounts, so nobody could call it charity. She was hard and fair and she smelled of tar and fish scales, and I loved her the way you love a dock: without ever really looking at it, because it was always there.

"She never asked me about myself. That was her gift, actually. Other adults asked constantly. Asked why I wouldn't wear dresses, why I flinched at 'girl,' why I stood wrong, laughed wrong, was wrong. Bess just handed me a mending needle and said, 'You'll do fine,' and left me alone to become whatever I was becoming.

"But the name followed me around like a debt collector. The one I was given at birth. Every time somebody said it, it fit like a boot two sizes small — you can walk in it, but every step reminds you. And on the Verre there's no escaping your name. The river towns talk. Your name travels ahead of you faster than any boat, and it comes back to you wearing other people's opinions the way driftwood comes back wearing moss.

"So when I was sixteen I started collecting names. Not stealing them — collecting. Listening for them. Names I heard at markets, names off cargo manifests, names carved into lock gates by bored deckhands. I kept a list in my head the way Teo keeps his drafts. Most of them didn't fit either. Some were too big — I tried one for a week that belonged to a famous smuggler, and I felt ridiculous, like a minnow wearing a pike's name. Some were too small. Some were pretty but hollow, like painted river glass.

"And then one morning, coming down from Widow's Bend — this was years ago, before the guild had squeezed that stretch dry — I heard an old towman talking to his mule. Just talking, the way lonely men do. And he said, 'Easy, Yara, easy now,' and I stopped dead on the towpath.

"It wasn't even a person's name. It was the mule's. This gray, stubborn, sure-footed beast that had hauled barges up that river for twenty years, that knew every shallow and every snag better than the men steering her, that had been kicked and overloaded and underfed and had simply *kept going*, patient as stone, ornery as weather.

"I know how that sounds," Yara said, and both of them were leaning forward now, Teo with his pencil forgotten, Marisol with her pipe paused mid-draw. "A mule. But listen. It wasn't the animal I wanted. It was what the name sounded like when he said it. He said it soft. He said it like a name you say to something you trust with your whole livelihood. 'Easy, Yara.' Not *faster*, not *harder*. Easy. Steady. I know you. Keep going.

"I'd never been named like that in my life. So I took it. Walked home that evening and told Bess, and she looked at me over her nets for a long moment, and she said, 'Well. Suits you. Hand me that shuttle.' And that was it. That was the whole ceremony. Best day of my life up to then, and it lasted four seconds and involved a fishing shuttle."

Teo laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him. Marisol smiled around her pipe stem, and the smile did something complicated to her face, softened it and aged it at once.

"The pronouns came later, and harder," Yara went on. "That took longer to figure out, because there wasn't a mule for it. There was no moment on a towpath. It was more like... you know how you learn a river? Not from a chart. Charts help, but you learn it by running it, grounding once or twice, learning which bend lies and which eddy tells the truth. That's what pronouns were. I ran a few. 'She' grounded me every time. 'He' — I tried it for a season, passing as a boy on a lumber barge, and it floated better but it still wasn't my river. And then one day I thought: I'm not either bank. I'm the water between them. The current itself.

"'They' isn't nothing, whatever people say. 'They' is what you call a crew. A muster. Everybody pulling together toward one shore. When someone says 'they' about me, I hear: this person is more than one thing, and all those things are headed the same direction. That's mine. I chose it the way you choose a heading — not because it's the only possible one, but because it's true north *for this vessel*."

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Teo hadn't written any of it down, which Yara noted with something like gratitude; some things shouldn't become map marginalia.

"My mother used to call me a word I've never found again," Yara said quietly. "Not a name. Something in her own language, from wherever her people came from before the river. I was small. I remember the shape of it in her mouth more than the sound. Two syllables, maybe three, warm at the front and dark at the back, like a boat sliding out of sunlight into a bridge's shadow." They shook their head. "When she disappeared, that word went with her. I couldn't keep it. Nobody else ever said it. So I had to build a name from salvage, the way you build everything on the river. Salvage and stubbornness."

"That's not building from salvage," Marisol said. Her voice came out rough, like it always did when she'd been quiet too long. "Salvage is what's left over. What you did was more like—" she gestured with the pipe, searching, "—like dredging a new channel. The river didn't give it to you. You cut it yourself, and now the water runs where you decided."

Yara blinked at her. In twenty-some years of knowing people, no one had ever put it that way, and the truth of it landed in their chest with an almost physical weight.

"That's why I'm telling you this," Yara said. "Because we're walking into a mountain full of my mother's choices. Her pass, her letters, her war with the guild. Everything up there is inheritance — hers to me, mine to figure out. But I need you both to remember, when it gets hard, that I've done this before. Made myself. Chosen what I'm called and what I'm for. Whatever we find at the top of this mountain, whoever she turns out to be, whatever she wants — the name Yara comes first. The name I built. Before daughter, before heir, before whatever the compass says I am. I chose me before I ever knew she was alive to choose me back."

Teo nodded slowly. Then he said, in the careful voice he used for corrections on a chart, "Can I tell you something? About names?"

"Go on."

"When I ran from Voss, the first thing I did was burn my indenture papers. Second thing — and I've never told anyone this — I crossed out my name in my master's ledger before I left. My apprentice name, the one he registered me under. I wrote *stet* next to my real one. The one my mother gave me. Because he'd renamed me, did you know that? Apprentices get renamed. He said my name was too long for the ledgers." His jaw tightened. "So I understand. A little. Not the same, but adjacent."

"Adjacent counts," Yara said.

"And I'll write you properly," Teo went on, warming now, the pencil already back in his hand as if the conversation had been leading here all along. "On the new map. Every free route gets named by the people who open it. That's the old rule, before the guild corrupted it. So if we make it — when we make it — there'll be a pass, and a col, and probably a chute Marisol will refuse to have named after her, and somewhere on that sheet, in ink, it'll say *Yara*. Spelled right. Said right. For everyone downstream, for two hundred years."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face, and behind him the mountain stood black against the first stars, vast and indifferent and, tonight, almost kind.

"You'd do that," Yara said. It wasn't a question, but it needed saying anyway.

"I'm already doing it," Teo said. "Page nine. Don't look, it's not ready."

Marisol knocked out her pipe against a stone and stood, joints cracking, and for a moment her silhouette blotted out the stars. "Names," she said. "Huh. I had one once that meant something. 'Marisol of the High Way.' Earned it young, lost it in a crevasse with four other people." She looked down at Yara, and the firelight caught the deep lines of her face, and something in her expression was naked and unguarded for the first time since the saloon. "Maybe when this is done, I'll earn a new one too. Seems only fair. If a mule can lend you a name, the mountain owes me at least one."

She ducked into her bedroll before anyone could answer, which was her way.

* * *

Yara took first watch. They sat with the fire rebuilt to a modest, honest size, the compass in their lap, its brass casing drinking the light. The needle didn't point north. It hadn't since Widow's Bend. It pointed up-valley, into the dark, steady as a towline, toward whatever waited at the top of the world.

They thought about the word their mother had used. Two syllables, maybe three, warm at the front and dark at the back. They thought about how, in a few days, they might hear it again — spoken by the mouth that made it, aimed at the person they'd become instead of the child they'd been. Would it still fit? Could a word built for a small child in a river town possibly stretch to cover a boat captain, a name-collector, a self-made channel?

And if it didn't fit — if Adaeze reached for that old word and found it no longer true — what then?

Yara turned the compass over in their hands. On the back, beneath grime they hadn't dared scrub, there was engraving they still hadn't fully deciphered: letters, and beneath them a date, and beneath the date a line of smaller script that the firelight kept sliding off of, as if the metal itself were shy.

They tilted it toward the flame.

The small script resolved, letter by letter, and Yara's breath went out of them slow and cold.

It wasn't a maker's mark. It was a dedication. And it wasn't addressed to Adaeze at all.

*For the child I have not met yet — may you find your own way to be called.*

Yara sat very still, the fire cracking, the needle trembling faintly toward the summit, and understood that they had been carrying an inheritance meant for them — specifically, personally, by name — since the night they pulled it from a drowned cave.

Their mother had known. Twenty years ago, sealed in brass, she had known there would be a child, and had guessed — somehow, impossibly — that the child might need permission to become someone new.

Yara closed their fist around the compass and looked up at the mountain, and for the first time since the cave, the fear in their chest was edged with something that felt almost like being welcomed.

Two days later, at dawn, they found the cairn.
