## 18. Ambush at the Col

The col announced itself an hour before they reached it, in the language of wind. Yara had learned to read rivers by the way foam gathered against stone, and mountains, it turned out, spoke a cousin tongue: the wind funneled through a notch carried its own hiss, a long exhalation that meant *low ground here, the only low ground for miles*. Any party crossing this stretch of range would have to come through it, the way any boat on the Verre had to pass Widow's Bend.

Which was exactly why someone would be waiting there.

Yara said nothing about it while they ate the morning's hardtack, but they watched Marisol chew slowly, her eyes on the notch between two granite shoulders ahead, and knew she'd done the same arithmetic. Teo, bless him, was still chattering about contour intervals, sketching in the margin of his map with a stub of pencil he kept behind his ear like a carpenter keeps a nail.

"Wind's wrong," Marisol said at last.

"It's a col," Yara said. "Wind's always wrong."

"Wind's *too* right." She spat a fleck of biscuit into the scree. "Funneling clean through. No eddies. Means nothing's up there breaking the flow." She looked at Yara, and her face did something complicated — guilt and resolve wrestling in the same small space. "Or means whoever's up there is lying flat."

Teo stopped drawing. "Voss."

"Voss," Marisol agreed.

Yara turned the compass over in their palm without opening it. The needle had been pulling harder all morning, tugging northeast toward the pass like a dog at the end of a rope, and every mile closer made the pull feel less like guidance and more like a countdown. They were two days from the Sable Current if the weather held. Voss had been a day behind them at last sighting. A day behind, and now possibly not behind at all.

"She could go around," Teo offered, without conviction.

"She could," Marisol said. "She won't. I trained half those climbers myself, back when I still had a reputation to lose. Odile Voss doesn't go around things. She goes through them, and she sends other people first." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and looked older than she had at the saloon, older than she had on the rope bridge, older than she had anywhere except maybe the night she'd finally told them about the expedition — though even then she'd told it slantwise, like light through dirty glass. "Question is whether we meet her here or make her chase us."

"There's no other way across," Yara said. "You said so yourself. This is the throat."

"This is *a* throat." Marisol squinted at the col, at the broken ramparts on either side, at the grey sky pressing down like a lid. "There's another one. But we're not talking about that yet."

Yara heard the *yet* and filed it away, the way they filed everything Marisol said these days — in the drawer marked *she knows more than she's telling*, which had gotten crowded enough to jam.

They moved out single file along the moraine, Teo in the middle because he was lightest and least sure-footed, Yara leading because their eyes were best, Marisol sweeping the rear because she refused to do anything else. The sun came and went behind ragged cloud. Somewhere below them a glacier groaned, a sound like a door opening in a house where no one should be home.

The col rose ahead: a saddle of shattered rock perhaps forty yards wide, walled on the left by a cliff streaked white with birdlime, on the right by a slope of talus that ran down and down toward a valley floor too distant to judge. Snow patches clung in the shadows like dirty lace. Through the notch, Yara could see the next valley, blue and empty and impossibly far, and beyond it, faint as a pencil line, the wall of peaks that hid the Sable Current.

Their mother was somewhere past that line. Dying, maybe dead already. The thought hit Yara the way cold water hits a swimmer — not gradually, all at once, the whole body flinching — and they pushed it down and kept walking, because that was what you did. You put your head down and pulled the oar.

They were thirty yards from the notch when Marisol grabbed Yara's shoulder.

"Down."

Yara dropped without arguing, pulling Teo with them, and a heartbeat later the wind changed — or seemed to — and underneath it Yara caught it: the click of a carbine bolt being worked, somewhere up among the rocks to the left. Then another. Then a voice, amplified by the cliff walls until it seemed to come from everywhere:

"That's far enough!"

Marisol closed her eyes briefly, like a woman hearing a song she'd hoped was forgotten. "Kessler," she muttered. "Of course it's Kessler."

"Who's Kessler?" Teo whispered.

"A man I taught to read snow. Who is currently reading ours very well." She raised her voice. "Kessler! It's Marisol Reyes! Come down here and look me in the eye if you're going to point that thing at me!"

Silence. The wind hissed through the notch. Yara lay flat against the frozen ground, cheek against stone that smelled of lichen and old ice, and counted the shapes that began to rise along the cliff edge — one, two, four, six — dark figures against the pale rock, rifles cradled with the casual competence of people who used them often. Six. Maybe more still hidden. On the talus slope to the right, two more figures stood up out of nowhere, cutting off retreat. Eight. Nine.

And then, stepping to the lip of the col itself, framed against the sky like a figurehead, a woman in a guild-grey coat with her hands clasped behind her back.

Odile Voss did not look like Yara had imagined. Yara had imagined cruelty, some visible signature of it in the face. Instead there was only tiredness — deep, structural tiredness, the kind Yara recognized from dockworkers at the end of a double shift — arranged around a pair of eyes that missed nothing. Her gaze swept the three of them lying in the scree and settled, with terrible precision, on the compass case at Yara's belt.

"Captain Okafor," she called. Her voice carried without effort, pitched like someone who'd spent years making herself heard over crowds and storms. "I'd like to talk."

"You've got a strange way of asking," Yara shouted back.

"I've got a practical way." Voss crouched, bringing herself nearer their level, and even at eighty yards Yara could see the frost in her eyebrows, the raw windburn on her cheeks. She'd climbed hard to get here. She'd spent her climbers' strength to arrive first. "Three of you. Nine of me. Two of my people are above you on the ridge — don't bother looking, you won't find them, that's rather the point of them. I can end this conversation before it starts, or we can have it. I know which option I'd prefer, and I suspect you're sensible people."

"Sensible," Marisol repeated softly, bitterly. Beside Yara, Teo had gone very still, the particular stillness of prey animals and apprentices who'd seen what Voss did to people who disappointed her.

Yara got slowly to their feet, hands open and empty, heart hammering against the ribs like something trying to get out. "Then talk."

"Walk forward. Just you. My people will keep their pieces pointed elsewhere." A pause, dry as chalk. "For now."

Yara walked. The twenty yards took a year. Scree shifted underfoot, announcing every step, and Yara thought absurdly of the river, of how noise traveled differently over water, of how they'd never once been ambushed on the Verre because the river showed you everything coming. Mountains lied. Mountains let nine armed people lie flat in plain sight and call it invisibility.

Up close, Voss was smaller than her voice. Fifty, maybe older, with grey coming in at her temples and a scar through one eyebrow old enough to have gone silver. She studied Yara the way a customs officer studies cargo — thoroughly, impersonally, looking for what was being smuggled.

"You have your mother's chin," she said.

The words landed like a slap. Yara kept their face still, mostly. "You knew her?"

"I met her once. Twenty-two years ago, at a guild hearing, where she stood up in front of the whole council and told them the Sable Current belonged to no one." Something moved behind Voss's eyes — not warmth, but the ghost of warmth, a room where a fire had been and wasn't anymore. "Half the hall laughed. The other half wrote her name down. I was in the second half." She glanced back at the col, at the three figures waiting in the scree. "Here is what happens now, Captain. You give me the compass and the letters. You give me whatever your mother left you. In exchange, you and your companions walk out of these mountains alive, and I pay your ferry guild debt besides — all of it, cleared, with interest. You go back to the river. You buy a better boat. You live a long unremarkable life."

"And the pass?"

"The pass becomes what it should always have been: managed. Maintained. Safe." Voss's voice didn't rise, but it hardened, the way ice does. "Do you know how many people die on that route every year because no one maintains it? Because anyone can wander onto it, free, unprepared, and fall into a crevasse nobody's mapped? I'm not offering to sell the mountain, Captain. I'm offering to *keep* it. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Yara said. "Not once the tollbooths go up. Not once it's cheaper to stay home than cross. I've watched the guild do it to the river my whole life. Ferry rates up, ferry traffic down, and the towns on the far bank just — fade. Like somebody turned off a tap." They heard their own voice shaking and didn't try to stop it. "My mother didn't fake her death and spend twenty winters freezing on top of the world so the guild could put a gate on her pass."

Something flickered across Voss's face at *twenty winters* — a quick, involuntary calculation, confirmation of what she'd perhaps only suspected. "So she is alive."

Yara bit down on the mistake, too late, tasting it.

Behind them, Teo made a small sound. One of Voss's climbers shifted, rifle butt grinding on stone.

"Captain Okafor." Voss's voice dropped, and for a moment — just a moment — the customs officer was gone and someone else looked out, someone exhausted, someone standing in a place she'd climbed to at great cost for reasons she might not fully own. "Give me the needle. That's all. The letters, the casing, the needle. Whatever claim it carries, I'll honor your family's name in the charter — a stipend, a monument, anything you want carved in stone. But the route comes under guild control, or people will keep dying on it, and their blood will be on the hands of everyone who could have prevented it and didn't." She extended one gloved hand, palm up. "Please. Don't make me take it."

"*Please,*" Marisol's voice cut across the col like a whip crack, and she was standing now, arms spread wide, walking forward with the loose-hipped swagger of a woman who had decided to be either magnificent or dead. "Nine rifles and a *please*. Odile, you always did learn manners in the strangest places."

Voss went rigid. For the first time, something like genuine emotion crossed her face — and it was anger, hot and personal. "*Reyes.*"

"Hello, Odile." Marisol stopped a few paces short, close enough that Yara could see her hands were trembling and hear that her voice wasn't. "Still sending other people up the scary parts first?"

"You were banned from guild territory. You were banned from—"

"From guiding, from membership, from the rolls, from the memorial wall — yes, I've had twenty years to memorize the list." Marisol smiled, and it was a terrible smile, all teeth and no joy. "But here's the thing your nine riflemen and your two ghosts on the ridge can't help you with, Odile. You need to reach the Sable Current before the snows close it. And there is exactly one known route through the eastern approaches." She tapped her own temple. "And it lives in here. In the head of the woman you exiled. Your maps are wrong, Odile. All of them. I've seen your surveyors' charts — Teo showed me, back when he still worked for you — and they route you through the Meridian Shelf, which hasn't existed since the icefall came down in the spring. You'd find that out in about four days, at altitude, with the weather turning. If you're lucky, you'd find out in time to turn around."

The silence that followed had texture. Yara could feel the climbers on the cliff shifting, glancing at each other, doing their own arithmetic. Rifles were for bandits and beasts; nobody wanted to shoot over a navigation dispute.

Voss's jaw tightened. "You're bluffing."

"I'm many things, Odile. A liar, a drunk, a coward — you signed the documents saying so yourself, and I keep them framed." Marisol's voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and Yara saw her gather it back like spilled grain. "But I am not bluffing about the mountain. Ask your apprentice. He copied those charts himself. He'll tell you the shelf notation was twenty years old."

Every head turned to Teo. He stood up slowly, brush still behind his ear, looking smaller than ever and somehow steadier than Yara had ever seen him. "It's true," he said, and his voice only shook a little. "The shelf data predates the collapse. I flagged it. Nobody reads an apprentice's flags."

"So." Marisol spread her hands. "Here's my offer, Odile, since you're so fond of offers. Nobody shoots anybody today. We walk — all of us, separately, your people trailing if it makes you feel safe — and I guide both parties to the Current by the route that actually exists. Because I'll tell you a secret." She looked at Voss, and her voice went quiet enough that the wind nearly took it, and Yara leaned forward to catch it anyway. "I owe a debt on that mountain. Eleven names' worth. And I'd rather pay it getting everyone there alive than winning some stupid standoff on a col. But if you take that compass by force, Odile — if one shot fires today — I will sit down right here on this rock, and I will wait for the snow, and neither of us will ever find that pass. Test me. Everyone says I'm a coward. Let's find out together."

The wind screamed through the notch. Somewhere below, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

Voss stared at her for a long moment. Then at Yara. Then at the compass case, and Yara watched her weigh it — watched her actually weigh it, the way she'd probably weighed every choice of her life, cost against cost, toll against toll — and Yara understood suddenly that this woman was not their opposite. That was the worst part. This woman was what happened when you loved a thing so much you tried to own it.

"No shots today," Voss said finally. It came out like a concession torn from her with pliers. "We follow. At a distance. And Reyes — if your route turns out to be a fantasy, there won't be a rock remote enough."

"There never has been," Marisol said. "That's rather been my problem."

Voss turned and barked orders — positions, intervals, the two ghosts on the ridge recalled — and the col began, slowly, to exhale. Rifles went to shoulders. Climbers descended from the rocks with the fluid economy Yara was beginning to hate, that mountain competence they themselves would never have.

Yara walked back to Teo and Marisol on legs that felt borrowed. Teo's face was grey. "How much of that was true?" they murmured.

"The shelf?" Marisol was already packing, movements sharp, not looking at either of them. "True. The rest—" She cinched a strap hard. "There is a route. I didn't lie about that. But I haven't been on it in twenty years, and the last time I was, I came down alone." She shouldered her pack and finally looked at them, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "So pray I remember it, children. Pray I remember every step. Because we just promised Odile Voss a road, and if I can't deliver it, she'll take the compass off your corpse and mine both."

She started up through the notch, past the disarmed riflemen, past Voss, who watched her go with an expression Yara couldn't read — and turned east, away from the trail, toward a wall of broken country that appeared on none of Teo's charts.

Toward the route that lived only in her head.

* * *

They climbed for the rest of the day into country that grew steadily less like a map and more like a rumor. The known trail fell away below them within the hour; after that there was only Marisol's memory, tested step by step against the stone. Twice she stopped for ten minutes at a time, staring at a spur of rock, lips moving, counting years. Once she led them half a mile down a dead-end gully before stopping, swearing with a fluency that would have embarrassed a stevedore, and climbing back out.

By late afternoon they traversed beneath a hanging glacier the color of old bone, and Marisol halted at a cairn so ancient it had fused with lichen into the landscape.

She stood before it a long time. When she spoke, she didn't turn around.

"This is where it starts," she said. "The hidden route. And this is where I tell you the rest of it — the part I left out by the fire, the part about your mother." Her shoulders rose and fell once. "Because if we're going down this road, you deserve to know whose footsteps we're following. And why she ordered me to run."

The wind moved through the stones of the cairn with a sound almost like speech, and Yara waited, heart pounding, for the story that would change everything again.
