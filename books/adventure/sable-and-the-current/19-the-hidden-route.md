## 19. The Hidden Route

The hidden route began where the map ended, which was, Marisol thought, the only honest place for any route to begin.

She had marked it in her mind twenty years ago and never spoken of it since. Not to the guild surveyors who'd offered her a pension to forget it. Not to the drunk priests and sober liars of the border towns who bought her rounds hoping she'd trade them a mountain. It lived behind her eyes like a splinter worked too deep to dig out, and now she was leading two children straight into it.

They left the col before dawn, moving west along a shelf so narrow that Yara walked with their back to the rock and their arms out like a tightrope artist, and Marisol watched them do it and thought: *river legs.* The child had balance bred into their bones from twenty years of decks that moved underfoot. It would save their life up here, eventually. Or it would make them overconfident, and the mountain would collect its fee.

Below them the world was still dark. Above them the sky went from ash to rose in slow increments, lighting the face of the peak they were skirting — a wall of granite streaked with ice, so tall that looking up at it gave Marisol the same vertigo as looking down. They traversed for three hours without speaking. That was the discipline of bad ground: you saved your breath for your feet.

Then the shelf narrowed to nothing, pinched out against an overhang, and Marisol stopped and said, "Here."

Yara looked at her. Teo looked at her. The wind looked at her, shoving at her shoulders like a debt collector.

"Here what?" Yara said.

"Here's where you trust me or turn around." She pulled the coil of rope off her shoulder. "There's a chimney behind this buttress. It doesn't show from below. Doesn't show from above either, unless you're standing exactly here, which is why Voss's people will walk right past it on the main trail and wonder why we vanished."

"And if we fall?"

"We won't," Marisol said, and heard the lie in her own voice, smooth as river stone, worn by twenty years of handling.

* * *

The chimney was brutal. That was the word for it, brutal, and also beautiful, though Marisol only admitted the second part after they were through it.

It was a crack in the mountain's shoulder, wide enough at the bottom to stand in and narrowing as it rose, floored with loose rubble and walled with stone the color of old iron. Water had carved it over ten thousand years and then frozen in it, so that climbing it was like ascending the throat of some enormous sleeping animal — ribbed, slick, breathing cold air down at them. In one place a fin of ice had grown across the gap and they had to chop through it with the adze end of Marisol's axe while the chips rang off the walls like bells.

Teo went second and hated every minute of it, narrating his hatred in a thin, determined voice that echoed strangely in the stone. Yara went last and said nothing, but Marisol, climbing above them, kept glancing down and catching the set of their jaw — that particular stubbornness she'd seen once before, on another face, in another lifetime.

At the top the chimney opened into a hanging valley no map had ever recorded, because no cartographer had ever been paid enough to suffer for it.

Marisol heard Teo stop breathing when they came out into it.

It was a bowl of stone perhaps half a mile across, ringed by walls, floored with old snow gone blue at the depth of it. A stream ran through the middle, emerging from under a glacier tongue at the head of the bowl and vanishing again into a sinkhole at its foot, and the light came down into the bowl at a single angle, mid-morning only, so that everything in it existed in a kind of held breath. Edelweiss grew in the cracks near the outlet, absurdly, impossibly white. A herd of blue sheep stood on the far wall like ornaments someone had glued there, watching them with total indifference.

"It's beautiful," Teo whispered, as if loudness might break it.

"It's a trap," Marisol said. "That's what makes it beautiful. Nobody comes here because nobody can leave easily. We're committed now. From here the only way is up and over, two days' hard travel, and if weather closes in—" She shrugged. "The mountain keeps what the mountain takes."

Yara crouched by the stream and drank, cupping water in both hands. When they looked up, their eyes were bright and hard. "My mother found this place."

"My mother found this place," Marisol corrected gently, and then, because the lie had gotten heavy enough to crush her, she sat down on a boulder and said, "Sit down, both of you. It's time I told you what happened. All of it. You've earned worse than you've gotten."

* * *

They built a small fire — there was dead dwarf pine twisted in the lee of the wall, twenty years' worth of it, brittle as biscuit — and Marisol wrapped her hands around the heat and told them.

"There were six of us," she said. "Your mother, Adaeze. Myself. Two guild surveyors named Brandt and Okonkwo. A young porter called Emet, seventeen years old, wanted to see the high country so badly he carried double loads to be allowed along. And a woman named Sefu, who was our ropemaster and the best climber I ever knew, better than me, better than anyone."

She watched the fire. She could feel them watching her.

"The guild sent us to find the Sable Current and chart it. Officially. Unofficially, we were carrying a grant document — Adaeze's family claim, the original, sealed — to be lodged with the guild archive at the summit station. That was the story. But Brandt and Okonkwo had other orders, orders that didn't come from Adaeze. The guild founders wanted the pass. Wanted it controlled, tolled, gated. And the claim was the one thing standing in the way. If the claim disappeared on the mountain — lost, burned, buried in a crevasse with whoever carried it — the pass became open ground. First-claim land. Guild land."

Yara had gone very still. "The expedition wasn't exploring. It was an ambush."

"It was a funeral waiting for its date," Marisol said. "I didn't know. I swear to you I didn't know, not then. I was the guide. I knew the route, the weather, the snow. I didn't know I was leading my friends into a knife fight."

"What happened?" Teo asked softly.

"The third night, at the high camp, I woke and Brandt was gone from his bag. So was the document satchel. And when I got up to look, I found Okonkwo standing over Adaeze's tent with an ice axe in his hand." Her voice did not shake. She had practiced this part alone, in a hundred saloons, in a hundred empty rooms, until it came out flat as slate. "We fought. He was bigger. I lost the argument and most of my blood stayed inside me, which was the day's one victory. Adaeze came out of her tent and settled it differently. And then the storm came down off the summit ridge early — a week early, a killer, the kind you don't argue with — and Adaeze made the call."

"She ordered the retreat," Yara said.

Marisol looked at them. "How could you know that?"

"Because you're alive. Because you've spent twenty years drinking yourself to death over something you think you did. Because that's not what a coward's guilt looks like." Yara fed a stick to the fire. "Tell me what she actually ordered."

Marisol laughed — a short, broken sound, surprised out of her. "You are your mother's child, God help us all. Fine. Here's what happened, straight, no varnish. The storm hit and Emet fell into a crevasse on the descent. Sefu went down after him on rope. I was anchoring. The anchor held, but the snow bridge over the crevasse was failing, and Brandt — Brandt was screaming that we should cut the rope, save ourselves, the storm was killing us, every minute cost lives. And Adaeze stood in that white hell and said no. She said: *we hold the rope until the rope fails, not before.* We held it forty minutes. Long enough for Sefu to get Emet to the lip. Not long enough for both of them to get out. The lip collapsed. Took them both."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the hidden valley with a sound like distant surf.

"Four survived that night," Marisol said. "Me, Adaeze, Emet, and Brandt. And here is the thing I need you to hear, Yara, because I've needed to say it for twenty years and never found ears that mattered. On the descent, Brandt tried again for the satchel. Tried to take it and take Adaeze with it, off the Icefall Ledges. And Adaeze — wounded, half-frozen, carrying more grief than any one spine should hold — she made a choice. She saw the storm closing and she saw that Brandt would follow us anywhere the document went, and so she ordered the retreat split. She sent me down ahead with Emet, fast, the safe route, the route I knew. She took Brandt with her onto the ledges, into the storm, drawing him away from us. Drawing the knife away from the throat."

"She led him to his death," Yara said quietly.

"She led him to *his own choice*, which is a different thing. He went onto those ledges chasing a document and a murder, and the mountain settled his account. Adaeze walked out of that storm three days later alone, half-dead, and the first thing she said to me was not *I almost died*. It was *did Emet get down*. And then she looked at me — I'll never forget it, boy, girl, whatever you were to her, she looked at me like I was the last living proof her plan had worked — and she said, *Marisol, you live now. You live loudly and stupidly and visibly, so they watch you and not me.*"

Teo made a small sound. "She faked her death."

"She let the mountain fake it for her. The guild reported all hands lost. Adaeze Okafor, dead. The claim presumed destroyed with her. And she went up instead of down, into the one place the guild would never pay enough to search properly, and she has been sitting on that pass like a dragon on its hoard ever since. Guarding it. Keeping it free the only way she could — by being its ghost." Marisol spread her hands. "And me? I drank. Because she ordered me to live and I couldn't figure out how to do it without hating myself for surviving people better than me. Because I thought my failure was that I anchored well and it wasn't enough. Twenty years to understand that the failure was never mine. The retreat was hers. The order was hers. And the order was *right*, because I'm sitting here, and so are you, and the pass is still free."

Yara was crying. They didn't wipe their face or hide it; they just let it run, staring into the fire, and Marisol understood that these were not entirely tears of grief. Some of them were rage, and some were relief, and some were the terrible tenderness of a child learning their parent had loved them in the only language available — absence.

"She let me believe she was dead," Yara said. "Twenty years. Every offering I burned at the river shrine. Every year I—"

"Yes," Marisol said. "And she spent those same twenty years freezing and starving and bleeding on a mountain so that no guild man would ever put a toll gate between you and the sky. I'm not telling you it was fair. Love at that altitude isn't fair. It's just load-bearing." She leaned forward. "But hear the rest of it, because it matters. She didn't send you nothing. She sent the compass. She spent years engineering that tide cave, that chest, that letter — a trail only her own blood could read, because she knew the guild watched me and she knew she couldn't come down. She chose the moment. She chose you. Whatever else she owes you, Yara Okafor, she trusted you with the hardest thing she had."

The fire burned down. The blue sheep had gone. In the east, past the valley's rim, the sky was doing something ominous and beautiful with cloud — building towers of it, gray-violet, lit from within.

Marisol felt it before she could name it: the pressure drop, the ache in her old break, the specific silence birds make before they shut up entirely.

"Teo," she said. "Your sky-reading. What does that look say?"

Teo studied it, and Marisol watched the color leave the apprentice's face. "That's not passing weather. That's a front. A real one. Days, maybe — but if it breaks tonight—"

"Then we move now," Marisol said, already kicking snow over the fire. "High camp before dark, the saddle tomorrow, and we pray the Current stays open long enough for us to walk through it." She paused, one boot on the scree, and looked back at the two of them — the captain's child and the runaway mapmaker, her unlikely penance and her unlikelier hope.

"And pray for one thing more," she added. "That whatever's driving Voss up the main trail, she doesn't find the chimney. Because if she does, she won't be a day behind us anymore."

Above the hanging valley, the first flakes had already begun to fall — fat, silent, wrong for the season, like the mountain clearing its throat before it spoke.
