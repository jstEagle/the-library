## 20. What Voss Wants

The wind on the col had a voice Odile Voss knew better than her own mother's, and it was saying the same thing it had said for thirty years: *turn back, pay the toll, turn back.*

She stood at the mouth of her tent with a tin cup of tea gone cold in her hands and watched the hidden route's entrance — a seam of shadow between two buttresses that Marisol Reyes had walked into an hour before dawn like a woman returning to a grave she'd dug herself. Voss did not trust it. She did not trust Marisol. But she trusted arithmetic, and the arithmetic was simple: three climbers ahead of her, one compass, one dying legend of a woman at the top of the Sable Current, and perhaps four days before the snows closed everything for good.

Behind her, Brenner was coiling rope with the exaggerated care of a man who wanted to be asked nothing. The others were quieter still. They had been quiet since the col, since Marisol had stood in front of six armed guild enforcers and talked them out of murder with nothing but a map drawn from memory and a voice like gravel settling.

Voss drank the cold tea anyway. It tasted like the lowlands. It tasted like home.

*You were a toll child once,* she told herself, the way she did every morning, a small liturgy of self-reminder. *Remember what you are climbing for.*

But memory, she had learned, was a river with two banks, and she could not always tell which one she was standing on.

* * *

The first thing Odile Voss ever owned was a ticket stub.

She was five years old, and her mother had let her hold it — the ferry passage across the Verre at the Halloway crossing, two coppers, paid in coins worn smooth as river stones — because holding the ticket made Odile feel important, and her mother needed her to feel something other than hungry on market days. The stub was pink and cheap and printed with a guild seal: a wheel inside a circle, the spokes radiating outward like a promise.

*The Guild Carries You,* the seal said underneath, in letters so small you had to squint. Odile had squinted. She had asked her mother what it meant.

Her mother had laughed — not the good laugh, the tired one, the one that came out sideways. "It means we pay," she said, "so they carry us."

"But the river carries us," Odile had said, with the terrible logic of five-year-olds. "The river's free."

Her mother had gone quiet then, in the way adults went quiet when a child has accidentally said something true and expensive. She had taken the ticket stub back. And Odile had learned her first lesson about the world: that things which were free by nature could be made to cost, if someone built a gate across them and hired men to stand behind it.

Her childhood was a catalogue of gates. The bridge at Ferrow's Mill — a half-copper each way, more if you had a cart. The road over the shoulder of the Graybacks where the guild had posted collectors who called themselves *wardens* and wore gray sashes and never once, in all Odile's girlhood, cleared a single rockslide without first passing a hat. The winter ferry surcharge. The market tax dressed up as a "safety levy." Her father had been a cooper, a good one, barrels that held water for years without a whisper of leak, and every year the guild took a little more of him — not in coin alone but in the particular posture of a man who has stopped standing up straight because he is always calculating what he owes.

Odile used to watch him do the sums at night, lips moving, candle guttering, and she used to think: *there has to be another way through the mountains.* Because there was. Everyone knew there was. The old people spoke of it in the tone they reserved for saints and floods — the Sable Current, a high pass where the wind combed the snow into rivers and no man had ever set a toll, because no man had ever held it. Free passage, coast to coast, over the spine of the world. A rumor. A song. A thing children drew on slate when the schoolmaster wasn't looking.

Her father died owing eleven coppers to the Ferrow's Mill bridge fund. Eleven. The wardens came to the funeral and stood at the back with their hats off and their ledgers open, and afterward they spoke to her mother gently, the way you speak to someone whose house is already burning, and the debt passed down like eye color.

That was the year Odile decided she would join the guild.

Not to serve it. To *change* it. She was fourteen and magnificent in her certainty, and she told anyone who would listen — which was mostly her mother, mostly at night, mostly while mending nets — that the guild was not evil, only rotten, and rot could be cut out by someone who understood where the roots ran. Her mother would look at her with an expression Odile could not read for years, and would only understand much later, after it was too late to ask: grief, arriving early, for a person who had not yet left.

"You want to fix the wolf," her mother said once, "by becoming its teeth."

"I want to hold the leash."

"Child." Her mother tied off a knot, bit the thread. "Nobody holds that leash. You'll see. The leash holds *you*."

* * *

The guild took her the way institutions take clever poor children: completely, and with excellent manners.

She was quick with figures, quicker with people, and quickest of all at the specific alchemy of making powerful men believe an idea was theirs. By twenty-two she was running the Halloway crossing — the very crossing of the pink ticket stub — and she had reduced the toll twice, and the boatmen drank to her name, and she lay awake at night feeling like a surgeon who has removed a tumor and found the organ riddled with others.

Because here was the thing she learned in those years, the thing no one tells you from outside: the tolls were not greed. Or rather, greed was only the smallest muscle in the body. The guild *ran* on the tolls — the pensions, the patrols, the dredging crews, the widows' fund that had kept her own mother fed through two bad winters. Every copper at the Ferrow's Mill bridge bought rope somewhere and bread somewhere else. Cut the tolls and you didn't starve the fat men at the top; they had cellars. You starved the widow two doors down from your childhood, the one who swept the ferry dock for her pension.

The system was a knot, and every thread was holding up something soft.

So Odile stopped trying to cut the knot and started trying to *own* it. If the tolls must exist, then let them exist under a hand that remembered being five years old with a ticket stub. Let the wolf have teeth that remembered being a lamb. She climbed. She compromised. She signed things. There was a morning — she could date it still, the frost on the guildhall windows, the smell of sealing wax — when she approved a rate increase she had sworn she never would, because the alternative was the patrol going unpaid and the mountain roads going unpatrolled, and unpatrolled roads meant dead travelers, and she had done the sums, she had *always* done the sums, and the sums said yes.

Her mother heard about it from a neighbor before Odile could explain. When Odile came to visit, the door was shut, and through it, flat and final, like a stone dropped down a well:

"The leash holds you. I told you."

She had wanted to say: *it isn't like that, I'm different, I'm still me.* But standing on that step she had reached for the proof and found her pockets full of sealing wax and signatures, and the words wouldn't come, and she stood there in the cold until her feet ached, and then she went back to the city and worked harder, because work was the only prayer she had left.

* * *

And then — Adaeze Okafor.

Odile met her twice. That was all. Two meetings, twenty years ago, and they had rearranged the architecture of her life more thoroughly than any promotion or any loss.

The first time was in the guildhall at Verre Landing, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old paper. Adaeze Okafor was a river captain with a reputation for running cargo through water other boats wouldn't float on, and she had come to the guild with a proposition that made the senior clerks exchange glances over their spectacles: she claimed to have found corroboration — a survey fragment, a guide's testimony — of a viable route to the Sable Current, and she was asking the guild to fund an expedition.

Not to claim it. That was what had struck Odile even then, even young, even ambitious. Okafor stood in front of the council and said, in a voice like a keel cutting current: *"Fund the mapping, and the route goes in the public charts. Free passage. In perpetuity."*

The room had reacted the way rooms do when someone proposes giving away a fortune: polite horror. Old Chairman Aldous had stroked his beard and spoken of maintenance costs, of liability, of the sad necessity of administration. And Odile — junior seat, bottom of the long table, invited to listen and not speak — had felt something turn over in her chest like a boat coming about.

Because she had done this exact calculation. Alone. A hundred nights. *If the pass exists, and it's free, the tolls lose their justification. The whole rotten argument collapses. No more leash.*

She should have spoken. She knew, later, in the sleepless archaeology of regret, that this was the hinge of her whole life: a moment when the girl with the ticket stub and the woman she had become were both in the same room, pointed at the same door, and she said nothing. She told herself she was waiting for the right moment. She told herself the council would never approve it and a lost vote would kill the idea forever. She told herself stories, the way everyone does, and the expedition was voted down, and Adaeze Okafor stood, thanked them with terrifying courtesy, and left.

The second time was three weeks later, at the dock, in the rain. Odile had gone down — she never fully admitted why, even to herself; some leftover instinct, some debt of conscience — and found Okafor loading her boat for the mountains anyway, alone, unfunded, buying supplies with the look of a person spending money they couldn't spare on a thing they couldn't not do.

"You know they'll call it piracy if you cross guild land without papers," Odile said.

Okafor had looked at her — a long, level look, rain running off the brim of her hat — and said: "I know what they'll call it. I'm doing it anyway. My child deserves a world with one free road in it."

Then, softer, almost kind, in a way that Odile would spend two decades taking apart: "You're the one who argued for the lower tolls at Halloway. I've heard the boatmen talk. You're not one of them yet, guildwoman. Don't let them make you one."

And she had shipped her oars and gone upriver, into the range, into the song and the rumor, and she had vanished, and the guild had declared her dead within the season, and Odile had stood at the memorial service among men who seemed relieved, *relieved*, that the inconvenient dream had drowned, and she had looked at their faces and understood that she had watched the last door close.

After that, something in her went quiet and efficient. If the free road would not be given, then it must be *held* — held, priced, administered, because anything unadministered was chaos, and chaos killed travelers faster than any toll. That was the story she told herself. She told it so many times it grew true the way scar tissue grows true. She took the mountain portfolio. She raised the rates. She sent surveyors who came back with nothing, and then she stopped sending surveyors and started sending watchers, because a legend you cannot find is a legend you cannot control, and control — she had come to believe this with the whole scarred weight of her — control was the only mercy an institution could offer.

*If I hold the pass,* she told herself, climbing now, twenty years late, *then no one else can fence it worse. If I set the price, the price stays fair. If I am the wolf, at least the wolf remembers being a lamb.*

It was a good story. She had told it at her mother's deathbed, even — had gone, gray and powerful, to the little house by the net-mends, and said, *Mother, I'm going to hold the pass, and when I hold it I'll lower every toll on the range, you'll see, it will be worth it* — and her mother, blind by then, had reached up and touched her face with fingers like paper and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than the leash speech, worse than anything, and Odile had carried that silence up every mountain since.

* * *

"Captain." Brenner, at the tent flap. "Reyes is back."

Marisol came into the firelight looking like a woman who had argued with a ghost and lost. Snow in her hair. Something raw around her eyes. She dropped onto a crate and accepted tea she didn't drink.

"Well?" Voss said.

"It's real. The route. Brutal, but real — it puts us a day behind them, maybe less if the weather holds." Marisol stared into the fire. "There's a shrine partway up. Cairn, letters. The Okafor woman's been leaving messages on that mountain for twenty years like a woman writing to someone she expects."

"She was writing to her child," Voss said.

Marisol looked up sharply. "How would you know that?"

Because Odile knew. Because she had sat in that guildhall and heard a woman say *my child deserves a world with one free road in it*, and she had understood, even then, that Adaeze Okafor hadn't climbed for glory or gold or freedom in the abstract. She had climbed the way Odile had joined the guild — for a child not yet grown, for a future she wanted to build with her own hands because no one would hand it over. They were the same animal, she and the ghost on the mountain. One had chosen the wilderness and one had chosen the institution, and both had told themselves stories about protection, and both had lost things they could not name in daylight.

The difference — the whole difference, the difference that had kept her awake on the col — was that Adaeze Okafor had built her wall *against* the guild, and Odile had become the thing the wall was built against.

"Captain?" Brenner prompted.

"We move at first light," Voss said. "Full party. We take the hidden route, and we take it fast." She looked at the fire, at the sparks going up and winking out like ticket stubs burning. "And nobody touches the Okafor woman. Nobody touches the child either. We're not murderers. We're administrators."

Marisol made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Is that what we're calling it now?"

"That's what it's always been." But her voice came out flatter than she intended, and Marisol heard it, and looked at her a moment too long, with the expression of a guide reading terrain — reading *her* — and finding the ground less stable than the maps suggested.

Voss turned away and checked her gear by lamplight: ropes, pitons, the writ of claim with the guild seal heavy in its oilskin sleeve, and beneath it, wrapped separately, a folded pink paper gone brown with age, soft as cloth. A ferry stub. Halloway crossing. Two coppers. She had carried it for thirty-five years, through every promotion, every signature, every compromise, and she had never once been able to explain to herself why.

*The leash holds you,* her mother had said.

Tomorrow they would climb into the hidden route, and at the end of it there would be a cabin, and a dying woman, and a child who had inherited a compass instead of a debt, and Odile Voss would stand in the wind-carved corridor where snow ran like a river and finally learn whether she had spent her whole life escaping the toll — or building the largest one of all.

She snuffed the lamp. Outside, the wind began to speak, and for the first time in thirty years, she could not make out what it was saying.
