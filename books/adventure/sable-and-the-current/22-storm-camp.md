## 22. Storm Camp

The storm came down on them like a lid.

Yara had watched weather all their life — the green bruise of a squall line over the Verre, the way wind told the truth before clouds did. But mountain weather lied. It had been a clean blue morning when they'd made the notch below the shoulder of the ridge, and by noon the sky had turned the color of wet slate and begun to shake itself apart above them. By the time they'd thrown up the tent in the lee of a granite fin, the snow was coming sideways, dry as salt, hissing against the fabric like sand poured over a drumhead.

"Inside," Marisol said, and it wasn't a suggestion. She shoved Teo through the flap first, then Yara, then backed in herself with the stove and the fuel can clutched to her chest like an infant. "Nobody goes out again until this breaks. Nobody argues."

Yara didn't argue. They were too busy being quietly horrified at how much of the world could disappear. An hour ago there had been a view — the whole western face of the range laid out in tiers, blue and gold and merciless. Now there was a circle of white maybe ten feet across, and beyond it nothing, not even the memory of anything. The wind found every seam in the rock and played them all at once, a hundred flutes, none of them in tune.

Teo was shivering. Not the ordinary shiver of cold but the deep, stuttering kind that comes when the body has stopped trying to be polite about it. Yara got him into the sleeping bag nearest the stove wall and started working his boots off with numb fingers while Marisol lit the burner and set the dented pot on it with hands that were steadier than her voice.

"Not frostbite," she said, mostly to herself, pressing her thumb to the pad of Teo's big toe and watching the color come back. "Not yet. He walked us half a mile past where I wanted to camp because he swore he saw cairns."

"There *were* cairns," Teo said through chattering teeth.

"There were rocks shaped like cairns. There is a difference, and the difference is called judgment." But she said it gently, and she kept one hand on his ankle as if to make sure he stayed real.

Yara sat back on their heels and looked at the two of them in the orange light of the stove flame and felt something turn over in their chest that they didn't have a name for yet. On the river, they had known exactly what every person aboard was worth: what they could haul, what they could mend, how many days they could be trusted not to drink the water ration. Worth was weight and work. Here, in a nylon bubble pinned under a screaming sky, worth had gone strange. It was just — these two. Breathing. Here.

* * *

The storm did not break that night or the next day or most of the day after that. It settled in the way storms do at altitude, the way a debt settles, becoming less an event than a condition. The tent became the world. The world was nine feet long and five wide and smelled of paraffin, wet wool, and three unwashed human beings who had stopped apologizing for it around noon of the first day.

They rationed because rationing was the only prayer anyone still believed in. Half rations of the flatbread, a thumb of cheese each, tea made from the same leaves three times until it tasted less like tea than like the idea of tea. Marisol counted everything out with the grim precision of a woman who had once run out of food higher up than this, and nobody joked about it, because everyone knew she had.

It was during the second evening, in the long blue hour when the stove was banked low and the wind outside had dropped from a scream to a moan, that Teo said it.

"Tomorrow's my birthday."

He said it to the ceiling of the tent, in the tone of someone mentioning a fact about the weather. Yara looked up from mending a strap. Marisol, who had been doing sums with a stub of pencil on the back of a map margin, stopped mid-figure.

"How old?" she asked.

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen." Marisol put the pencil down. "Nineteen years old, and you ran away from Voss, crossed two provinces, climbed onto a glacier with a stolen barometer, and you mention your birthday like it's a train delay."

"It's not important—"

"It's absolutely important." Marisol was already moving, digging through the food bag with a purpose that made Yara nervous. "What have we got. What have we *got*." She held items up to the stove light like a merchant appraising jewels: the heel end of the cheese, wrapped in wax cloth. A paper twist of dried apricots, four of them, saved from the last resupply. A knuckle of dark sugar Yara had been carrying since the river town without quite knowing why — bought on impulse from a stall, the kind of purchase they never made, and now understood they had been saving without knowing that either. And at the bottom of the bag, one small tin of condensed milk, dented, ancient, the kind of thing that travels at the bottom of packs for years waiting for its moment.

"The milk," Marisol said, holding it up. "We're opening the milk."

"That's for emergencies," Teo protested.

"This *is* an emergency. You've been alive nineteen years and we're going to mark it properly if it kills us, which, given the altitude, is not out of the question."

Yara found themselves laughing — really laughing, the kind that hurt a little at this elevation, a thin cold laugh that steamed in the air. It startled them. They couldn't remember the last time laughter had come out of them unasked. On the boat, humor had been a tool, deployed to keep deckhands calm in weather. This was different. This was laughter escaping custody.

* * *

They cooked the birthday feast in shifts because the stove was small and patience was smaller. Marisol shaved the cheese heel into slivers so thin they were translucent and arranged them on the tin plate with the gravity of a chef plating for a duke. Yara cut the apricots into quarters — sixteen pieces, which meant four each, which meant abundance, technically — and heated them with a spoonful of water until the whole tent smelled of orchards, impossibly, obscenely, ten thousand feet above any orchard on earth. And then Marisol opened the condensed milk, and the smell of it — sweet, thick, milky-sweet as childhood — filled the little dome of nylon and canvas, and all three of them went quiet at once, because it was the smell of a world that no longer existed for any of them.

"To Teo," Marisol said, raising her tin cup of third-steeped tea. "Who is nineteen, and alive, and here, which given his judgment is frankly miraculous."

"To Teo," Yara echoed.

Teo's ears had gone red. He looked down at the plate of cheese slivers and apricot quarters and the pool of condensed milk in the shared cup, and his jaw worked once, twice, and then he said, very fast, "My mother used to make a cake. Every year. Even the bad years, even when we didn't have flour, she'd make something, she'd call it cake, it was basically sweetened paste, and she'd put one candle in it and say, *one candle is enough, the wish doesn't know the difference.*"

The wind moaned against the fly. Nobody spoke.

"She's why I know maps," Teo went on, staring at the milk. "She drew them. Survey maps, for the county office. She'd bring home the misprints and I'd draw roads on them that didn't exist. Whole cities. She never once told me to stop." He swallowed. "I haven't thought about that in — I don't let myself think about it much."

"You can," Yara said. "Not here. Not now. But someday, somewhere lower and warmer, you can think about it all you want. That's part of what we're climbing toward, I think. Places where remembering doesn't cost anything."

Teo looked at them then, really looked, the way he looked at contour lines when he was deciding whether a slope was climbable. "You don't talk about your mother much either," he said. "Even now. Even with the compass right there in your pocket pointing at her."

"No," Yara agreed. "I don't."

"Why?"

Yara turned the tin cup in their hands. Outside, the storm pressed its whole weight against the fabric, and inside, the stove ticked as it cooled, and the question hung there, patient as the weather.

"Because for twenty years she was a story I told myself," they said finally. "A shape I built out of almost nothing. A woman who left. And I knew — I *knew* — that whatever I found at the top of this mountain would be smaller than the shape, or different from it, or worse, and some part of me has been trying not to arrive this whole time." They looked up. "But she's real. At the end of the needle there's a real woman, old and sick and stubborn enough to live alone above the clouds for twenty years, and I'm going to sit across from her, and she's going to look at me and see — I don't know what she'll see. Someone she left. Someone she doesn't recognize. And I'll have to be a person, not a story, and so will she, and that—" Their voice caught, and they let it, because hiding it would have cost more. "That frightens me more than Voss does. More than the icefall. More than this storm."

Marisol reached over and put her hand flat on the floor of the tent between them, palm down, the way guides do — grounding, anchoring, an old habit written into her body deeper than the drinking had ever managed to write itself.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the woman I remember — Adaeze — she was exactly that stubborn. And when she looked at people, she saw them. All the way through. It was the most frightening thing about her, and the best thing." She paused. "She'll see you, Yara. Whoever you are. That's not something to fear. That's the whole prize."

* * *

Later, when the feast was eaten down to crumbs licked off the plate and the stove was out and the three of them lay in their bags in the dark listening to the storm, Teo spoke again from his corner.

"I used to think family was the thing you got born into," he said. "And that if yours was broken, that was just — the hand you were dealt. Like weather."

"Mm," said Marisol, who was already half asleep.

"But this isn't weather," Teo said. "You two. This isn't weather at all. This is — I chose this. You chose me. That's not the hand you're dealt, that's—" He fumbled for the word, and Yara, lying awake in the dark with the compass a small hard weight against their ribs, supplied it.

"Cargo," they said. "The good kind. The kind you lash down double and carry all the way."

Teo laughed sleepily. "Family," he said. "I was going to say family, but sure, cargo works. Everything on this expedition is cargo."

"Watch it," Marisol murmured. "I'm the oldest cargo aboard."

"Valuable cargo," Yara said. "Both of you. Lashed down double."

And in the dark, in the wind, at the top of the world, they let themselves believe it — that this was a thing that could be carried, that it wouldn't tear loose, that the mountain could take everything else and leave them this. Yara lay listening to the two of them breathe, slow and even, and thought: *whatever happens at the pass, whoever she turns out to be, I already have more than I sailed out with.* The thought was warm. It was the warmest thing they had owned in years.

* * *

It was near dawn, in the gray hour when the storm had thinned to a whisper and the cold came down hardest, that Yara woke for no reason they could name — the old river instinct, the sense of a current shifting under the hull.

Teo slept curled like a comma. Marisol's bag was empty.

Yara lay still and listened. Outside, the silence was enormous, the post-storm silence that rings in the ears. Then, faintly, from beyond the tent wall: the crunch of boots on crusted snow. One set. Moving away from the tent, downslope, toward the saddle where the terrain dipped out of sight.

Yara sat up slowly, heart tapping out a rhythm they didn't like. People stepped out into the night for reasons. To relieve themselves — but not downslope, not in the open, not in boots. To check the weather — but the storm was over; the stars through the clearing gap in the clouds said so plainly.

Or to signal something. Or someone.

Yara told themselves there were a dozen innocent explanations, and dressed anyway, and picked up the compass, whose needle — even here, even now — pulled steady and true toward the summit, toward the cabin, toward the woman at the end of everything.

By the time Marisol crunched back into camp, cheeks red with cold, breath ragged, eyes finding Yara's in the dark a half-second too late to arrange themselves into innocence, the eastern sky had begun to pale. Behind her, far below in the saddle, something glinted once in the first light — metal, or glass, or a mirror flash — and was gone.

"Couldn't sleep," Marisol said. "Went to check the route."

Yara looked at her for a long moment, the compass warm in their fist, and said nothing at all.
