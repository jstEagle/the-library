## 23. Betrayal of Convenience

The storm broke in the small hours of the morning, and Marisol lay awake in the tent listening to it die the way a wounded animal dies — not all at once but in stages, each gust weaker than the last, until what remained was only wind, ordinary wind, the kind that meant nothing.

She had not slept. None of them had, really. Teo had slept in fits, curled against his pack with the new map rolled beneath his jacket like a child sleeping on a hoarded sweet, and Yara had slept the way river people sleep, half-anchored to the world, one ear always listening for water that wasn't there. But Marisol had lain on her back staring at the frost-feathered canvas and doing arithmetic.

Three days behind them, if Voss had made good time through the col. Two days, if the guild captain's hired climbers were better than Marisol believed they were. One day, if the weather had favored them as it had just now favored everyone — because this storm, this beautiful killing storm that had pinned Yara and Teo and Marisol in their tents for two days, had also pinned Voss, and every hour it raged was an hour the gap between them held steady.

And the window was closing. The snows had come weeks early. Every guide Marisol had ever known would look at these skies, at the load sitting heavy on the upper slopes, at the way the wind had begun to speak in the register it used before avalanches, and would say: *turn back*. The pass would close. Not with a door slamming but with a slow white suffocation, and anyone caught above the icefall when it did would become a story told in lowland taverns for a season and then forgotten.

Marisol knew all of this the way she knew her own hands. And she lay there in the dark doing the arithmetic anyway, because she had been doing it for two days, and because the answer never changed, and because she could not stop asking the question.

*If we don't move tomorrow, we die up here. If we do move, we cross the icefall with Voss a day behind us, and if anything goes wrong — if the seracs shift, if someone falls, if we lose even half a day — she catches us on the glacier below, where there is nowhere to hide and nowhere to run.*

*And I am the only one who knows the route.*

That was the thought. That was the thought she had been circling like a bird over carrion. She was the only one who knew the hidden route, the ugly beautiful knife-cut of a path through the eastern ledges that Adaeze Okafor had shown her twenty years ago under oath of silence, and that knowledge was worth more than rope or food or luck. It was leverage. It had bought them a stalemate at the col. And leverage, Marisol knew from long and bitter experience, was a thing you could spend — or a thing that could be taken from you by people who wanted it badly enough.

Voss wanted it badly enough.

Marisol turned onto her side and looked at the shapes of the other two in the dim grey light seeping through the canvas. Teo's breathing, quick and light. Yara's, deep and deliberate, a boat at anchor. These two. This strange little family that had assembled itself out of wreckage — a runaway apprentice, a disgraced guide, a riverboat captain chasing a ghost with a brass needle. Twenty years ago Marisol would have laughed at the idea of herself belonging to anything. Twenty years ago she'd had a team instead, seven strong climbers and a client with a dream, and she had led five of them into the mountain and brought two home, and she had learned exactly what belonging cost.

*You froze on the rope bridge,* she reminded herself. *Yara caught him. You stood there like a woman watching it happen again.*

That was the other arithmetic. The one underneath. Because it wasn't only the icefall ahead that frightened her. It was the knowledge of what she was — a guide whose nerve had gone, whose body remembered falling even when her mind insisted she hadn't fallen, who had watched Teo swing over that black drop and felt her hands forget how to work. On the icefall, if something went wrong, they would need her to be the guide she had been. And she did not know — could not know until it happened — whether that woman still existed inside the drunk who had spent fifteen years drinking herself toward oblivion in border saloons.

So the fear had two faces, and both of them looked at Voss.

* * *

She rose before the others. Old habit; guides rise first, melt the snow, heat the water, let the clients wake to warmth. She moved quietly in the blue pre-dawn, boots in hand, and slipped out through the tent flap into a world scoured clean and silent.

The storm had left the camp buried to the guy-lines. The sky above the eastern ridge was turning the color of a healing bruise, gold bleeding up from underneath, and the peaks around them stood sharp and pitiless against it, wearing their new snow like fresh linen on a corpse. Beautiful. It was always beautiful. That was the mountain's oldest trick, and Marisol had never once been immune to it.

She stamped a platform clear by the cook fire ring, set the pot on to heat, and told herself she was simply up early. Simply being useful. Simply doing the job.

Then she took out the signal mirror.

It was a small thing, a sliver of polished steel no bigger than a playing card, worn nearly smooth at one corner where her thumb had worried it across twenty years and a thousand miles of trail. Guides carried them for rescue work: catch the sun, flash three times, hope someone with a telescope was watching. Every climbing party in the range used them. There was nothing suspicious about a guide checking her mirror, cleaning it, holding it up to the strengthening light.

There was nothing suspicious about angling it east.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake, and they didn't, and some cold detached part of her noted that too — *so this is how easy it is, after all these years; the sin hasn't rusted shut* — while the rest of her watched the sun's reflection leap off the steel and swing toward the distant shoulder of the col, toward the notch of shadowed rock where, if Voss's party had survived the storm and was moving at all, they would be making their morning camp.

One flash. Two. Three. Pause. One. Two. Three.

Not a distress signal. A distress signal was three-and-three, evenly spaced, the universal call. What Marisol gave them was different — a rhythm old guides used among themselves in the years before radios, a cadence that meant *I see you, hold your position, I will come to terms.* No climber alive would read it by accident. But Odile Voss had grown up in these mountains paying tolls, and her hired guns were professionals, and professionals knew the old codes even if the guild pretended they didn't.

She flashed the sequence twice more, then lowered the mirror and stood very still in the freezing gold light, listening to her own heartbeat.

*I can make it right,* she told herself. *This isn't betrayal. This is insurance.*

The reasoning unspooled with terrible fluency, the way it always did, the mind supplying justifications the way a river supplies water — endlessly, without effort, from some deep source. *I'm not giving her the route. I'm opening negotiations. If I wait until she catches us on the glacier, she takes everything — the compass, the letters, the boy's map, maybe worse. But if I come to her now, while she still needs me, I can set terms. Amnesty. Safe passage down for the three of us. Maybe — maybe I can even talk her out of it entirely, buy Yara time, tell her the hidden route is washed out, collapsed, gone—*

Even as she thought it, she heard the lie in it. Voss was not a woman you lied to twice. And the hidden route was real, and Voss would know it was real, and the only coin Marisol actually possessed was the truth of it. Everything else was packaging.

Below the camp, far down the eastern flank, something glinted.

She went cold. Then she raised the mirror again — casually, the way a person checks the light — and found it. A thread of reflected sun, tiny with distance, answering from the rocks below the col. Once. Twice. Three times. Then a pause, and a single long flash.

*Received. Send your man.*

Marisol lowered the mirror. Her breath came out in a long white plume and hung in the dead air, refusing to disperse, as though the morning itself were holding its breath to see what she would do next.

They had seen her. They had answered. Somewhere down there, Odile Voss was standing in the ruins of her own storm camp reading a signal she must have prayed for, and thinking: *the disgraced guide has cracked. They always crack. Now I only have to name my price.*

And the worst of it — the thing Marisol would carry afterward, the splinter that worked deeper the more she pressed at it — was the relief. For one whole breath, standing there with the mirror warm in her fist, she had felt *lighter*. Fifteen years of fear, of waiting for the past to collect its debt, and here at last was a door marked EXIT, and her hand was already on the latch.

Then she turned and looked back at the camp.

* * *

Teo had emerged from the tent, wrapped in every layer he owned, hair frozen into a dark corona. He was crouched over the cook fire Marisol had laid, feeding it twigs with the fussy concentration he brought to everything, and he was talking — of course he was talking, the boy talked the way rivers ran, constantly and in one direction.

"—and if the load has set overnight the upper crossing might actually be safer than the lower, counterintuitively, because fresh snow is what lubricates the slides, old consolidated snow holds together, so arguably the storm did us a favor—" He glanced up, saw her, and smiled. It was an unguarded smile, the smile of a boy who had decided, against all evidence and instinct, to trust her. "Morning. Did you sleep?"

"Bits," Marisol said.

"Yara's still out. I didn't want to wake them, they were up half the night re-rigging the sled ropes." He held his hands to the flames. His fingers were chapped raw and he flexed them carefully, wincing. "Two days in a tent. I thought I'd lose my mind. But I finished the eastern sheet." He patted the roll under his jacket, and there it was again, that fierce quiet pride. "Every ledge, every cairn, the whole hidden route. If we get down, if any of this works, there'll be a map of the free road that doesn't belong to the guild. First one ever. My master would—" He stopped. Something crossed his face. "My master would hate it," he finished, and grinned.

Marisol sat down heavily on a rock beside the fire and looked at him.

He was nineteen, maybe twenty. He had run away from the only life he'd known with nothing but a stolen apprenticeship's worth of skill and a conviction that maps should tell the truth. He had nearly died on a rope bridge because the guide responsible had frozen, and he had forgiven her for it inside a day, easily, the way the young forgive — not because he understood but because he hadn't yet learned what it cost to carry.

*He trusts you,* said the voice in her head, the one that sounded like Adaeze Okafor, the one she had been hearing for twenty years. *Both of them trust you. Yara handed you their mother's route. The boy shows you his map. And you stood in the dawn flashing signals to the woman hunting them.*

"It's a good map," Marisol said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

Teo looked at her, startled by the tone. "Thanks. I mean — it's not done. The icefall section's going to be guesswork until we're through it."

"The icefall." Marisol fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch. "Tell me honestly, boy. When we get there. Are you afraid?"

Teo considered it with the seriousness he brought to everything, which was one of the things about him that had begun, quietly, to matter to her. "Terrified," he said. "But it's a different terrified than the bridge. On the bridge I was afraid of dying. At the icefall I think I'm mostly afraid of failing. Of getting so close and—" He shrugged. "Yara says fear's just information. That river people read it like current. Is that true? Is that what it's like?"

*No,* Marisol thought. *Sometimes fear is just a hole in the world with your name on it, and sometimes it makes you do things in the dark that you spend the rest of your life explaining.* 

"Sometimes," she said.

Behind them, the tent flap moved, and Yara came out into the morning — moving stiff, face hollowed by two bad nights, but awake in that way they were always awake, fully present, a person who had never once in Marisol's company wasted a moment of consciousness. They looked at the sky first, then the slopes, then the two of them by the fire, and something in their expression eased.

"Storm's done," Yara said. "We move today. Icefall by midday, high route across, camp on the far moraine tonight if the mountain lets us." They crouched by the fire, took the pot Teo offered, drank, and closed their eyes briefly at the heat of it. When they opened them, they were looking at Marisol. "Today's the day we need the guide you used to be. Can I have her?"

Just that. No speech, no pressure. A question, asked plainly, in the voice of someone who had already decided to trust the answer.

Marisol met their eyes. Yara Okafor's eyes, dark and level, Adaeze's eyes, and behind them the whole weight of what Marisol had done and what she was doing and what she was going to spend today pretending she wasn't.

"You've got her," Marisol said.

*God forgive me,* she thought. *God forgive me, I'm sorry, I'm sorry.*

* * *

They struck camp in the hard bright cold, and the mountain received them back the way it always did, indifferently, taking their weight onto its shoulders without comment. Marisol led. That was the arrangement and she was grateful for it, because leading meant facing forward, facing uphill, facing anywhere but the two people following in her tracks, trusting her line, calling on her judgment with every step.

The route climbed northeast along a rib of broken rock toward the shoulder above the icefall. Below them, coming into view as they gained height, lay the glacier itself — and there it was, the thing she had been dreading since the storm began. The icefall was not a single fall but a staircase of chaos, a quarter mile of shattered glacier where the ice poured over a buried cliff and broke itself into towers and crevasses and bridges of dubious faith. In a normal year, crossed early, it was dangerous work. This year, loaded with weeks-early snow, warmed and split by the storm, it was a different animal altogether.

Marisol stood at the shoulder and studied it through the long glass, and her stomach performed a slow revolution.

The lower crossing — the standard route, the one on every guild chart — was choked. A serac wall on the western flank had shed during the storm, and the debris lay across the approach like a dropped handful of teeth. The upper crossing was open, barely, a thread of a line threading between two leaning towers that any honest guide would call suicide by Wednesday. And between them, invisible from here but burned into her memory, lay the third option. The one Adaeze had shown her. The one that wasn't on any chart because Adaeze had sworn her to silence, the cut through the eastern lateral moraine and along the shelf beneath the peak — brutal, exposed, slow, but outside the fall zone entirely.

*The hidden route.* Her card. Her leverage. The truth she had sold in the dawn.

"Talk to me," Yara said, crouching beside her.

Marisol pointed with the glass. "Lower route's buried. Upper's a gamble — those two towers are leaning on each other and the storm shook them. We could try it fast, first light tomorrow, before the sun softens anything." She paused. "Or there's the shelf route. East side. Longer, harder, slower. But nothing hangs over it."

Yara studied the glacier for a long moment. "How much slower?"

"Half a day. Maybe more, if the shelf is iced."

"And Voss is a day behind us." Yara's jaw worked. "Half a day puts her in sight of us on the far glacier. A gamble keeps the gap." They looked at Marisol. "Your call. You're the guide."

*Your call.* As if the words weren't a knife. As if she hadn't already made her call, alone, in the dark, in the gold light of the dawn, with a piece of polished steel in her hand.

"We take the shelf," Marisol heard herself say. "The upper crossing is a lottery and I don't buy tickets for other people's children."

Something flickered across Yara's face — relief, and beneath it a question they were too disciplined to ask aloud, because Marisol had spent the last week earning exactly this much authority and not one ounce more. "The shelf it is. Teo! Get the rope coiled, we're moving."

Teo scrambled up the slope toward them, cheeks burning red with cold and effort, and Marisol watched him come and felt the splinter twist.

Because here was the shape of it, plain as the glacier below: she had signaled Voss at dawn, and now she was going to lead her people onto the hidden route — the very route she had offered to sell — and somewhere in the next hours she would have to decide, finally and irreversibly, which side of her own treachery she stood on. Either she would find a way to warn Voss off, to misdirect, to spend her signal on nothing; or she would mark the turns for the guild's eyes as they passed, a broken branch here, a cairn nudged there, small treasons disguised as trail craft. Or—

Or she would confess. Stand in front of Yara Okafor and say: *I flashed the mirror this morning. I offered them the route. I was afraid, and fear bid higher than loyalty, and I am telling you now because I cannot carry it another hour.*

She imagined their face. She imagined Teo's. She imagined the rope bridge again, the moment of Teo falling, and Yara catching him — Yara, who had never once frozen, who read fear like current and acted on it, who had asked her this morning for the guide she used to be and believed, wholly, stupidly, generously, that the woman existed.

Marisol turned up the slope and began to climb, and set herself to the only penance available: to be, today, hour by hour, the guide she had promised. To find the line, place the protection, keep them alive. And all the while, at the edge of hearing, the mountain spoke in its avalanche register, and far below on the white stair of the icefall, the sun glinted off something small and metallic and moving — a party of climbers, tiny as insects, entering the lower route and finding it buried, and beginning, with the patience of professionals, to traverse west toward the upper crossing.

Toward the lottery. Toward the towers.

Toward them.

"Marisol," Yara called from above, and there was something new in their voice — not alarm, not yet, but its first cousin. "Come look at this. Quickly."

She climbed. And when she reached the ledge where Yara knelt, she saw it in the trampled snow: boot prints, many sets, hours old at most, cutting across the shelf route from the east — the guild's advance scouts, faster than anyone had guessed, already above them, already between them and the pass.

The hidden route, it seemed, was hidden no longer.
