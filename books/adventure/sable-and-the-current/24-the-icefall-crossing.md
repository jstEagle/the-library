## 24. The Icefall Crossing

The glacier announced itself long before they saw it — a sound like a giant clearing its throat somewhere beneath the world, a groan that traveled up through their boots and settled in the teeth. Yara had heard rivers talk all their life. They knew the vocabulary of water: the chuckle of shallows, the mutter of silt, the deep bell-note of a hull striking a submerged log. But this was a river with a different tongue, one made of pressure and time, and every syllable of it said *move*.

They stood at the lip of the icefall at first light, breath hanging in ragged flags, and looked down into a chaos of blue.

It was nothing like a frozen river, though Marisol kept calling it one, because it was the only way she knew to teach it. A river ran; this river *had run*, once, centuries ago, and then been arrested mid-motion, its rapids and eddies translated into towers and chasms. Seracs leaned against each other like drunks holding each other upright, some the size of the ferry guild's warehouse, some no bigger than a chapel, all of them glazed in that high-altitude blue that seemed lit from inside, as if the ice remembered the sky it had swallowed. Between them yawned crevasses whose bottoms they could not see. The whole slope poured downward from the col above in a cataract of broken ice, and somewhere on the far side of it, if the compass needle could be believed, lay the last approach to the Sable Current.

Yara held the compass open in their palm. The needle did not waver. It pointed across the icefall, dead level, toward a notch in the upper wall where the seracs thinned to slabs, as though their mother were standing over there waiting, patient as tide.

"Two ways through," Marisol said. She crouched at the edge, gloved fingers tracing lines in the snow crust. "The low route follows the trough there — see where the debris has funneled? Faster, flatter, but you're walking the riverbed. Anything above you comes down, it comes down *on* you." Her finger climbed. "Or the high route, along that spine of rock. Slower, exposed to wind, one bad slip and you're a red smear on the moraine. But nothing falls on you from above, because there is no above."

"And which would you take?" Teo asked. He had his sketchbook out already, mapping the icefall from memory before they'd taken a single step into it, the way he mapped everything — compulsively, tenderly, as if the world might be lost otherwise.

Marisol didn't answer right away. Yara watched her face do something complicated. Since the ambush at the col, since the hidden route, since the night she'd finally told them about the retreat her mother had ordered — told them, and let them decide what to do with her shame — Marisol had carried herself differently. Not lighter. More deliberately, like a woman carrying water across ice who has decided she will not spill it again.

"Twenty years ago I'd have taken the low route," she said. "Fast and flat. That's how we lost people." She stood, knees cracking. "We take the high route."

But the mountain disagreed with her plan before the morning was an hour old.

* * *

The spine of rock lasted exactly as far as the first serac field, and then it didn't. A wall of ice the color of deep water blocked the way forward, split by a ramp of packed snow that switchbacked up its face. Beyond it, Yara could see the route continue: another spine, another ramp, descending in stages toward the flat white shelf on the far side of the fall. It was climbable. It was even, in its terrible way, beautiful. What it was not was quiet. Every few minutes the glacier spoke — a crack like a rifle shot echoing off the walls, a hiss of settling snow, and once, horribly close, a boom and a shudder as somewhere behind them a block the size of a house let go and thundered down into the trough they were *not* walking through. Teo flinched so hard he nearly dropped his pencil.

"It's just talking," Marisol called back. "Ice talks when the sun warms it. Expansion. Mostly harmless."

"Mostly," Teo repeated, in a small voice, and wrote the word down anyway.

They roped up — Marisol insisted, and after the rope bridge none of them argued about rope anymore — and started up the ramp. Yara went second, Teo third, Marisol leading, cutting steps with the adze of her axe in short, economical strokes. Yara found their river-legs failing them again, the way they had on the first ascent: the instinct to read current, to lean into flow, to trust the moving thing beneath them, was worse than useless here. Ice did not flow. Ice waited. Ice was a river that had learned patience, and patience, Yara was learning, was the most frightening quality a landscape could have.

Halfway up the ramp, the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the icefall full-on.

The change was immediate and total. The blue ignited. Shadows swung. And the glacier, warmed through its skin, began to speak in earnest — cracks rippling away in every direction, long reports rolling down the valley, the whole frozen cataract ticking and groaning like a ship working loose from its moorings. Snow hissed off the serac faces in long smokeslides.

"Faster," Marisol said, and for the first time since Yara had known her, there was no performance in her voice at all. Just a guide doing arithmetic she hated. "Faster now. Move when I move, freeze when I freeze."

They moved. Steps crunching, axes biting, rope paying out between them in taut silver curves. Yara counted their own breathing the way they counted oar-strokes, four counts in, six counts out, and fixed their eyes on Marisol's heels and refused to look at the towers leaning over them, towers that had been falling for ten thousand years and were not finished falling yet.

They were twenty feet below the top of the ramp when the sound came.

Not a crack. Not a report. A *tear* — deep, wet, enormous, the sound of something vast being ripped at the root — and Yara felt it through the soles of their boots before they heard it, a shudder rolling up through the ramp itself.

"Above us!" Marisol screamed. "Off the ramp! *Move!*"

Yara looked up despite themselves, because a body looks at the thing that will kill it, and saw the serac.

It stood maybe two hundred feet above and to the left, a leaning tower of ice taller than the guild hall, and it was coming apart. A fracture line raced across its base like lightning drawn in ink. Chunks the size of carts calved off its flank and cartwheeled down the face below it, gathering speed, gathering mass, and the whole tower leaned further, further, surrendering at last to the gravity it had argued with for centuries.

There was nowhere to go. The ramp was a funnel. Above them the crest was too far, below them the base too near, and left and right were sheer blue walls. Yara's mind, trained on floods and snags and the hundred emergencies of the Verre, seized the problem and found no channel for it — and in that half-second of paralysis, with the roar building overhead like the sky tearing open, Marisol made the decision for all of them.

She pointed her axe downhill and to the right, at a shallow alcove where the ramp met the serac wall — a pocket of shadow, barely big enough for three bodies, sheltered under an overhang of older, denser ice.

"There! *Now!*"

She shoved Teo. Yara grabbed the rope and hauled, and the three of them went down the ramp in a controlled fall, boots skidding, axes dragging, and crammed themselves into the alcove as the world came down around them.

* * *

Later, Yara would try to describe the sound and fail. There was no describing it. It was the flood they'd survived at nine years old, multiplied by winter. It was the sky collapsing in installments. Ice hit the ramp where they had stood and the ramp ceased to exist; the shockwave slapped the alcove like a physical hand, driving the wind from their lungs, filling the air with a blizzard of shattered crystal that cut light into a million screaming fragments. Yara pressed their face into Marisol's shoulder, arms locked over their head, and rode it out — thirty seconds, forty, an eternity measured in heartbeats — while the mountain disassembled itself above them and the cold roared and the world turned white and then, slowly, unbelievably, quiet.

Snowdust hung in the air like fog. It settled on their shoulders, their eyelashes, in the folds of their collars. Yara coughed, tasted ice, and took inventory: Teo, pressed against the wall, eyes enormous, alive, whispering something that might have been a prayer or a list of map coordinates. Marisol, already moving, checking the rope, checking their limbs, her hands steady in a way her voice wasn't.

"Talk to me," she said. "Both of you. Injuries."

"I'm fine," Yara managed. Their voice came out strange, flattened, as if the avalanche had taken part of it. "Teo?"

"Fine. Fine." Teo laughed, high and cracked. "I think I'm fine. Is the ramp—"

Yara turned and looked back the way they'd come, and the words died in their throat.

The ramp was gone. Not damaged — *gone*, erased, along with the lower third of the serac field. Where their route had climbed through a corridor of leaning towers there was now a raw scar of new fracture, steaming faintly in the cold, choked with rubble blocks the size of cottages. The high route, the safe route, the route Marisol had chosen precisely because nothing fell on you from above — the mountain had reached out and deleted it.

And the low route, the trough, the riverbed — Yara looked down at it and felt their stomach drop. The avalanche debris had filled the trough's upper reaches, yes, but the trough itself still ran, a clean channel winding down through the icefall toward the far shelf. One way in. One way out. Under the towers.

"No," Marisol said quietly. She had followed Yara's gaze and arrived at the same arithmetic. "No, we are not splitting up. We go together or we don't go."

"Through that?" Teo pointed at the scar. "We can't climb that. Those blocks aren't stable, they're sitting on fresh fracture — one more release and—"

"I know what one more release does," Marisol snapped, and then closed her eyes, and Yara watched her gather herself the way she'd learned to, visibly, deliberately, a woman carrying water across ice. When she opened her eyes again she was a guide. "The trough is faster. An hour, maybe less, if nothing falls. The scar is a day of climbing unstable rubble, and I won't stake three lives on rubble. But the trough means we're in the riverbed, and if anything else lets go up there—" she tipped her axe toward the wounded serac field, "—we're in the path."

Silence, except for the glacier ticking as it cooled.

"We could wait," Teo offered. "Let it settle. Camp here and—"

"We don't have a day." Yara heard their own voice and was surprised by how certain it sounded. "Voss is behind us, the window's closing, and this alcove is a coffin with a nice view. We move." They looked at the trough, at the towers looming over it, and made themselves say the rest. "But Marisol's right. Three bodies in that channel is three times the target. If it comes down while we're in there, it takes all of us. Split the risk."

"Split the—" Marisol stared at them. "You want to *split up*. On the ice. After everything—"

"After everything, I know you'll get him through." Yara met her eyes. "You got us through the bridge. You got us past Voss's climbers. You've spent twenty years paying for people you couldn't save — you're not going to lose anyone today, because today you're not leading an expedition that trusted the wrong route. Today you're leading two people who chose it with their eyes open." They swallowed. "You take the trough with Teo. Fast and flat, your old way — but now you know what lives above it, and you'll read it better than anyone alive. I take the scar. Slow and ugly, alone, nothing to crush. We meet on the shelf."

"Absolutely not," Teo said. "No. You don't leave me, we don't— that's not how this works, we're—"

"We're a crew," Yara said gently. "And crews divide labor. Teo. Look at me." He looked, and Yara saw the fear in him, the same fear from the rope bridge, the fear of being the one who fell, the one who was left. "I am not leaving you. I am going sixty feet above you, in sight the whole way, shouting bad jokes down at you until we're all sick of each other. And tonight we eat on the far shelf, all three, and you show me whatever you've been drawing in that book when you think nobody's looking."

Teo's ears went red. "I don't— there's nothing—"

"Your pencil's been moving since the col," Yara said. "Whatever it is, it matters. So keep it dry and keep yourself alive, and I'll see you at the bottom." They turned to Marisol before he could argue further, because argument was a luxury the glacier taxed heavily. "Lead him well."

Marisol held their gaze for a long moment. Something passed between them that had no name either of them would use — gratitude, apology, absolution, all braided together like the ropes on their harnesses. Then she nodded, once, and became entirely business.

"Rope stays with me and Teo. You take the spare coil and the second axe. Whistle signals — one blast, all clear; two, danger, freeze; three, come running." She gripped Yara's shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Three blasts, Yara. Don't be proud."

"I've never been proud a day in my life," Yara said. "It's why I'm poor."

That got a bark of laughter out of Teo, wet and shaky, which had been the point.

* * *

The scar was worse than it had looked from the alcove, which Yara should have expected, because everything on the mountain was worse than it looked. The avalanche had left a staircase of shattered blocks, tilted at hostile angles, glazed with a skin of new ice that squeaked under the axe picks. Yara climbed the way they bailed a flooded hold: methodically, without heroics, testing every hold twice and trusting nothing, least of all their own confidence. Below and to the left, tiny as figures in a woodcut, Marisol and Teo picked their way down into the trough and became two dark stitches moving along a white seam.

Yara kept them in sight. That was the bargain, and they honored it — pausing on every third block to check the seam, to count two stitches, to raise a hand and catch Teo's answering wave. Once, crossing a gap between rubble mounds, they slipped, both picks skating off blue glass, and for one lurching instant hung on friction and stubbornness alone before the pick bit home — and in that instant they understood, with total clarity, what they had asked of Marisol and Teo: to watch someone you loved dangle over a crevasse and keep walking. It was a debt they intended never to collect from anyone again.

The glacier, meanwhile, talked. Every report rolled down the valley and Yara flinched, and froze, and counted two stitches, and climbed on. Twice the towers above the trough shed minor loads — smoke-slides of powder that hissed harmlessly into the channel — and twice Yara whistled two blasts and watched the two figures below press themselves against the channel wall and wait, patient as stone, until the air cleared.

An hour became two. The sun crossed toward noon. And then, near the bottom of the fall, where the trough bent eastward toward the shelf, Yara saw it happen.

There was no warning sound. That was the obscenity of it — after all the groaning and cracking, the killing blow came silent, a serac on the upper wall simply tilting, unhurried, dignified, like a man rising from a chair, and then letting go. It did not fall so much as pour, a brown-white torrent of ice and old stone flooding down into the trough exactly where the bend hid the channel from view, exactly where the two stitches had been seconds before.

Yara's whistle was at their lips before thought caught up — three blasts, shrill, frantic, over and over — and they were scrambling down the scar, sliding, falling the last ten feet, sprinting the moraine toward the bend with the spare coil streaming behind them, screaming names into the settling dust.

The cloud thinned. The trough emerged from it, half-buried, transformed.

And out of the dust, coughing, clinging to a rubble block at the channel's edge, came two figures. Two. Marisol hauling Teo up by the collar, both of them plastered white, both of them *moving*, both of them alive — the serac had gone into the bend behind them, missed them by the length of a boat, buried the channel they'd just walked through under forty feet of new glacier.

Yara reached them and did the only reasonable thing, which was to grab them both and hold on, all three of them shaking, laughing, swearing, a knot of arms and rope and snow-crusted wool in the middle of a landscape that had just tried to erase them.

"You stopped," Yara gasped into Marisol's shoulder. "At the bend. Why did you stop?"

Marisol pulled back. Her face was gray, her eyes wet, and her voice, when it came, was steady as bedrock.

"Because your whistle said two blasts, thirty seconds before the tower went. Something in the ice changed pitch. I heard it." She exhaled, long and slow. "Twenty years ago I didn't hear it. Today I did." She looked up at the wounded icefall, at the whole groaning white ruin of it, and then back at them, and something in her face had settled — not healed, nothing would ever heal it, but *settled*, like cargo lashed down properly at last. "We're through. All three. Get your things. The shelf is fifty yards, and I intend to sleep there until my hands stop shaking, and then—" she paused, and glanced at Teo, and something almost like a smile touched her mouth, "—then somebody is going to show me what he's been drawing in that book."

Teo went scarlet to the ears. But he didn't deny it this time. He hugged the sketchbook to his chest, looked at Yara, and said, quietly, "When we make camp. I want to show you both. It's — I think it's why we're going to win."

Above them, the icefall groaned and settled into its long afternoon silence, and the needle in Yara's pocket pulled steadily onward, toward the pass, toward the cabin, toward whatever waited at the end of the white.
