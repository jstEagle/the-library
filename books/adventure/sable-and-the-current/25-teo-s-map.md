## 25. Teo's Map

The high route was a rumor made of stone.

Teo had been walking it for two days, and still he could not decide whether it was a path or an argument the mountain was having with itself. The ledge clung to the eastern face of the col like a scar that hadn't finished deciding what it was, half a meter of broken shale stitched to a wall of blue-gray granite that went up forever and down considerably longer. Below them the icefall spread out in the morning light, a river of shattered glass frozen mid-sentence, and somewhere on its far side — if she had made it, if the low route had held — Marisol was walking the same direction through a different country.

Yara moved ahead of him with the careful, deliberate stride of someone who had learned balance on a moving surface. Teo had watched them for weeks now, and he had finally understood it: the river had taught Yara that the ground was never to be trusted, only read. Every step was a sentence in a language of weight and give. On scree, on ice, on the rotten ledges of the hidden route, they moved like the stone was water and they were poling a barge through it.

Teo moved like a boy carrying too many books.

His pack was the reason, though he'd never said so. Everyone assumed the weight was rope and rations, and some of it was. But strapped against his spine, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas and then, because he was his mother's apprentice and paranoia was hereditary, wrapped again, were forty-one sheets of paper and the tools that made them: three pencils worn to stubs, a nib pen in a leather sleeve, a tin of ink that he'd learned to keep warm against his body so it wouldn't freeze, and a folding rule Voss herself had given him, three years ago, on the day she'd said *you have a cartographer's eye, boy, don't waste it on dreams*.

He'd been wasting it on dreams ever since. Deliberately. Gleefully. At first out of spite, and then — somewhere on the rope bridge, or maybe the night around the fire when Yara had told them about their name — out of something that felt less like spite and more like faith.

He'd been drawing the whole way.

Not the guild charts, with their toll stations marked in red and their careful blank spaces where free routes used to run before the guild bought the paper they were printed on. A new map. *The* new map. The Sable Current as it actually was: every ledge, every cairn, every spring, every place the snow bridged and every place it lied. He had bearings and distances and elevation estimates in a shorthand of his own devising, and last night, huddled in the wind shadow of an overhang while Yara slept, he had finally laid the sheets out and joined the sections, and what he'd seen had kept him awake until the stars wheeled toward morning.

It was beautiful. That was the word his mother had taught him to use for a map that was true — *beautiful*, because truth at scale was the rarest kind of beauty there was. And this one was true. He'd checked it against the needle, against the cairns, against the hermit's scratched directions, against the dead reckoning he kept in his head like a second heartbeat. The hidden route ran clean from the western foothills to the col, and the col ran to the pass, and the pass — the Sable Current, the wind-carved corridor where the snow streamed like a river, Marisol had said, her voice going strange and reverent — the pass was free. Had always been free. The guild's charts showed a blank there, a white nothing with a printed toll line at the base, as if the mountain simply stopped and commerce began.

Teo had spent his apprenticeship filling in blanks. This one was going to end the guild's whole way of being.

He just had to tell Yara. And he'd been not-telling Yara for three weeks, which was its own kind of cartography — the careful charting of a thing you were afraid of.

* * *

They made camp at noon in a hollow below the final headwall, a scoop of gravel where the wind went over them instead of through them. Yara got the stove going with the economy of long habit, and Teo sat on his pack — sat on it, deliberately, the way you sat on a confession — and watched the steam rise off the pot and thought about how every important thing he'd ever said had come out sideways first.

"Your hands," Yara said, without looking up. "They're shaking."

"It's the cold."

"It's not the cold." Yara glanced at him then, and their eyes were the gray-green of the Verre in spring, water over stone, and Teo had long since stopped pretending he could hide anything in them. "You've been sitting on that pack like it owes you money since the icefall. Since before the icefall, if I'm honest. Whatever's in there, you should take it out. Things carried too long get heavy past their weight."

Teo laughed — a short, startled sound. "That's exactly what my mother says. Said. Says." He still hadn't sorted out the tense. "She says a secret is just a debt with no due date."

"And you're bad with debt."

"I'm *terrible* with debt." He unslung the pack, worked the waxed canvas free, and laid the oilcloth bundle on the gravel between them with the care of a man setting down something that might go off. "Okay. Okay. Before I show you this, you should know I started it the first night. Before you even agreed to take me. I was in the hold of your boat with a pencil stub and a scrap of chart paper I'd stolen from Voss's own desk, and I drew the Verre from Widow's Bend to the sea, and then I wrote at the top of it —" he took a breath — "I wrote *Free Water*."

Yara went very still. It was a stillness Teo had learned to read as attention, not anger. Usually.

"Show me," they said.

He unwrapped the bundle.

Forty-one sheets, and he'd joined the first thirty-eight with paste made of flour and snowmelt, so what came out was a scroll as long as his arm and nearly as wide, and he weighted the corners with stones and unrolled it in the gravel between the stove and the headwall, and then he made himself stop talking and let it do its work.

The mountain unrolled itself in graphite and ink. The western foothills with their toll gates, drawn small and mean, each one marked with a tiny crossed-out circle — his own private notation, *obsolete, pending*. The border town with its ticketed viewpoints. The saloon where Marisol had been drinking herself into a shape that fit her shame. Then the range proper, rising in careful hachures, the first ascent, the shrine cairn with its second letter, the ledges, the rope bridge drawn with a small honest note — *failed under load, 2 spans — rebuild before publication* — the icefall in both its crossings, high and low, the hidden route in a dotted line that he'd inked only two nights ago because he hadn't trusted it to be real until it was under his feet.

And above it all, at the top of the sheet, the col, and beyond the col the pass, and the pass was not blank. He'd drawn it as Marisol described it, from her drunk, broken, half-mad telling in the saloon: the corridor, the streaming snow, the small cabin at its heart. He'd drawn the cabin. He had no right to draw the cabin. He'd drawn it anyway, and labeled it in his best lettering, the hand his mother had drilled into him for map titles:

**THE SABLE CURRENT — FREE PASSAGE IN PERPETUITY — BY GRANT AND BY BLOOD**

Yara knelt over it for a long time. The wind came over the headwall in slow gusts and made the corner of the scroll lift and settle, lift and settle, like the map was breathing.

"You drew this," Yara said finally. "The whole way. While we walked."

"While we climbed. While we starved. While I was terrified." Teo rubbed the back of his neck. "Especially while I was terrified. My mother says fear is when the hand is steadiest, because the rest of you has nothing left to waste. She's —" he stopped, corrected himself, started again. "Voss used to say the opposite. She said fear was when you made mistakes. She kept her best cartographers frightened on purpose. Kept us paid just enough, kept us replaceable just enough, kept us grateful just enough. Fear as a management tool." He looked at the map. "I started drawing this the night I decided I'd rather be frightened for myself than comfortable for her."

Yara reached out and touched the dotted line of the hidden route, tracing it with one finger the way they'd trace a current on a chart. "You could have told me."

"I know."

"You could have told me a *month* ago."

"I know." Teo pulled his knees up. "Here's the thing. Here's the whole stupid thing. If I told you, it became real. And if it became real, it could fail. And if it failed —" he laughed, not because it was funny, "— do you know what happens to a cartographer who publishes a false chart? Not a guild cartographer. A real one. An honest one. Their name goes on a list. Every harbor office in the lowlands has it. *Do not trust the work of this hand.* My mother has a colleague on that list. He drew one river two miles wrong, one time, in fog, and a ferry went aground and three people drowned, and now he repairs clocks in a town where nobody knows his name and he signs his work with someone else's initials. I've been carrying that story since I was nine years old. I've been carrying this map the same way. Both hands full."

The stove hissed. Yara fed it a twist of scrub pine and watched the flame take.

"And now?" they asked.

"Now it's real." Teo spread his hands over the scroll, over the whole impossible length of the free road. "Because it's not just mine anymore. It's got your mother's route in it, and Marisol's memory of it, and the hermit's warnings, and your —" he hesitated, then said it — "your river sense. Every time you read the stone, I wrote down what you did. *Traveler's instinct, do not chart, but note.* I've got a whole column of notes that are just you. You're in the margins of this map, Yara. You've been in them since Widow's Bend."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Something moved through their face that Teo couldn't chart — something between grief and laughter, the way a river between banks is neither water nor land but both at once.

"Each of us," Yara said slowly, "carrying somebody else's inheritance. My mother's pass. Marisol's dead. Your —" they gestured at the scroll — "your whole profession's honor. And mine, I suppose. The compass. The letters. The name."

"That's what an inheritance is," Teo said. "It's not treasure. It's a job nobody asked you to do, left to you by someone who couldn't finish it."

"My mother would like you."

"Your mother," Teo said, "is going to have a lot of opinions about my lettering." He pointed at the title block. "She's going to say the descenders are too long. All the old guides say that. They want labels you can read at arm's length in a wind."

Yara smiled — the small, real one, the one that had been arriving more often since the col, since the ambush, since the argument on the ledge about whether any of this was worth dying for, which they had somehow survived and somehow not finished having. Then the smile faded into something more careful.

"Publishing it," they said. "That's not the same as drawing it. The guild will call it forgery. Voss will call it forgery. They'll say you fabricated the route, that the grant is fake, that the whole thing is a scheme to —"

"To what? Make a mountain free?" Teo shook his head. "No. That's the point of the map, Yara. That's why it has to be *good*. Not pretty — good. Every bearing checkable. Every distance defensible. I've kept a verification log — every measurement, every source, every correction. When we get down, anyone with a theodolite and a month can walk my line and find I'm true. That's the whole religion of it. My mother's religion. You don't argue a map into being believed. You make it so that disbelief costs more work than belief."

He pulled the verification log from the bundle — a smaller, fatter book, its cover soft as cloth from handling — and set it beside the scroll. Two objects. The claim and the proof. He thought, and did not say, that it was strange how much that pair kept appearing on this journey: compass and letter, seal and grant, the thing asserted and the thing that made it stand.

"Teo." Yara's voice had changed. Quieter. "When we reach the pass — if my mother's alive —"

"She's alive." He said it simply. "The needle says so. The hermit says so. The smoke we saw two nights ago, thin and gray, from a chimney that's supposed to be abandoned — the map says so. I marked it." He touched a small symbol near the cabin, a wisp of ink like a breath. "I've been marking every smoke."

Yara closed their eyes for a moment. When they opened them, they were wet, and they didn't hide it, and Teo understood that this too was part of the inheritance Yara was building — the right to be seen exactly as large as you were.

"Then when we reach her," Yara said, "I want her to see this. Before anything else. Before the letters, before the compass, before I even —" their voice caught and steadied. "Before I say who I am. I want her to see that her route is going to live. That someone drew it so it could never be owned again."

"She'll say your descenders are too long," Teo said.

"Whose descenders?"

"Yours. On your signature. When you sign the grant claim. I'm going to make you sign the map, by the way. Right there, under the title. *Charted by.* It has to be two names. Mine for the drawing and yours for the —" he searched for the word — "for the *standing*. A map of a free road needs a free hand and a free claim. That's how my mother would draw it up. That's how it would hold."

Yara looked at the empty space beneath the title, the blank he'd left there — he'd left it the first night, in the hold of the boat, before he'd known why — and something passed over their face like weather.

"Charted by," Yara said, tasting it. "I've never signed anything in my life. Debts get signed by other people. You sign things when you own them, or when you owe them. I've only ever owed."

"Then this is the other kind," Teo said. "The first one."

* * *

They struck camp an hour later, and the scroll went back into its wrappings, and the wrappings went back against Teo's spine, and the weight of it had changed — he felt it immediately, the way you feel a debt forgiven, the same pack, the same mass, a different animal entirely. Secrets were heavy. Shared things were just weight, and weight was only a problem if you were carrying it alone.

The headwall took them the rest of the afternoon, a long traverse on broken ground with the wind rising by degrees, and Teo climbed it thinking about publication, about the print shop in the lowland capital where his mother had once taken him and let him smell the ink, about the run of copies they'd strike — five hundred, a thousand — and the harbormasters and the caravan chiefs and the ferry pilots who would unroll it and see, for the first time in twenty years, that the blank at the heart of the range had a name and a road and no toll.

He was so full of the future that he nearly missed the present.

It was Yara who caught it — a hand on his shoulder, a stop, a look toward the col. The light was going long and copper, and the wind had swung, and on the wind, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Teo had learned to hate in Voss's employ: the flat crack of a signal flare, followed, ten seconds later, by another.

Two flares. From the low route. From the direction Marisol had gone.

"Distress," Teo said. "Or a signal. Or —"

"Or a trap," Yara said. But they were already turning, already reading the icefall's far edge, already calculating, and Teo saw the thing he'd seen on the rope bridge and at the ambush: the moment when Yara Okafor stopped being afraid and started being *fast*.

"Marisol," Teo said.

"Marisol," Yara agreed, and shouldered the rope. "Get the map wrapped tight. Whatever's down there —" they glanced back, and their eyes were river-gray and certain — "whatever happens next, that scroll gets to the pass. That's not a request, Teo. That's the whole point of us."

They started down toward the flares, and the wind carried the sound of a third — and this one, when it came, was not a flare at all.

It was a rifle shot.
