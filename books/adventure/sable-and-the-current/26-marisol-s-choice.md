## 26. Marisol's Choice

The rifle shot echoed off the icefall walls and came back wrong, flattened by wind, so that for a moment Marisol could pretend it had been nothing — a serac cracking, a rope snapping taut, any of the hundred small deaths a mountain offered up for free.

She did not get to keep the pretense. A second shot followed the first, and this one she felt in her teeth.

"Down," she hissed, and pulled Teo behind a fin of blue ice older than either of their grandmothers. Yara was already moving, low and fast across the glacier's wrinkled skin, and Marisol watched them go with an old, familiar sickness rising in her throat. Twenty years ago she had watched another figure move like that across another stretch of ice — Adaeze Okafor, walking point with that same unhurried certainty, as if the mountain were a river she'd already learned to read.

Adaeze had walked into the white and not come back. Marisol had spent two decades telling herself it was the mountain's doing, the weather's doing, the expedition's bad luck. She had drunk enough to make the story hold its shape.

It was not holding its shape anymore.

* * *

They found the first flare burning in a hollow below the serac field — Voss's signal red, guild-stamped, still hissing where the wind couldn't reach it. Beside it, planted upright in the snow like a flag of surrender, was a second marker: a slat of pine with charcoal writing, blocky and official.

**MARISOL QUINTERO. TERMS STAND. AMNESTY FOR ROUTE. COME ALONE OR NOT AT ALL.**

Teo read it twice. "Terms," he said. "What terms?"

Marisol said nothing. The silence said it for her, the way silence always did, and she saw the understanding arrive on Teo's face in stages — confusion, then hurt, then something colder.

"You signaled them." His voice didn't rise. That was worse. "At camp. While we were sleeping. You — after everything —"

"I was going to tell you."

"When? At the pass? After they'd killed us?"

"I was going to trade the *route*, Teo. Not you. Not Yara. A way through that dead-ends at the chute if you don't know the turn — I'd have given them the false fork and taken my amnesty and walked away, and none of you would ever have been the wiser." She heard how it sounded even as she said it. The lie inside the lie. "That was the plan."

"And then?" Yara had come back to them, snow crusted in their eyebrows, breathing hard. They looked at the marker, then at Marisol, and their face did something terrible and gentle at once. "And then what changed?"

Marisol opened her mouth and found nothing there but truth, which was the one thing she'd never learned to carry uphill.

"The chute," she said finally. "You want to know what changed? I stood at the top of the Hidden Route yesterday and I looked down at that chute and I remembered Elena Vasquez falling into it. I remembered the sound. And I thought: Odile Voss is going to lead six climbers into that chute believing my word, and when the route fails them they're going to be angry, and frightened, and armed, and they are going to come back up that chute looking for the woman who lied to them. And every one of those climbers has paid tolls since they were children. Every one of them is exactly the kind of person this whole thing is supposed to be *for*."

"So you're protecting them," Teo said. "By betraying us slower."

"No." She made herself look at Yara. "I'm done protecting myself. That's what changed. I sent the signal because I was afraid, and fear is the only thing I've been loyal to for twenty years. I'm finished with it. Here's what happens now: I meet Voss alone, like the note says. I give her the route. The real one. And while she's committing her climbers to it, I'll take them somewhere they can't come back from quickly — the long way around the Col, through the old survey line. It adds a day. Maybe two. It buys you the pass before the storm window closes."

"You'd burn your amnesty," Yara said. Not a question.

"I'd burn it." Her voice cracked on the words and she let it crack; she was too tired and too high up to care about dignity. "Twenty years ago I traded other people's lives to save my own, and I've been paying interest ever since. This is the last payment. After this I'm either free or I'm dead on a mountainside, and honestly, captain, both of those sound quieter than what I've been living."

Yara studied her for a long moment. Wind moved between them, lifting the fine powder off the glacier in threads.

"If Voss catches you lying," Yara said, "she won't arrest you. There's no law up here. You understand that."

"I led six people onto this mountain and buried four of them. I understand exactly what I am to her."

"She might shoot you."

"Yes."

"And you're going anyway."

Marisol almost laughed. "Captain, I've been going anyway my entire life. It's the only direction I know."

* * *

They argued for another hour, because Yara was Yara and would not send anyone anywhere without a plan folded into thirds, and Teo was Teo and could not forgive quietly — he forgave loudly, in paragraphs, with citations. But by late afternoon the plan existed, drawn in charcoal on the back of Teo's spare map sheet: Marisol would descend to the lower moraine where Voss's party had camped, deliver the route, and lead them east along the survey line while Yara and Teo took the Hidden Route straight for the pass. If all went well, they'd converge at the summit within a day of each other. If nothing went well —

If nothing went well, Marisol had made her peace with the mountain, which was more than most people managed.

At the edge of the moraine she stopped. Yara stood beside her, and for a moment neither spoke, and the wind filled the space with the sound it makes over ice, which is the closest thing the world has to a held breath.

"My mother trusted you once," Yara said. "On the expedition. She ordered the retreat that saved your life, and you've hated yourself for surviving it ever since. I know how that works. I spent twenty years hating myself for staying behind on the river while she went." They turned, and their eyes were steady. "Don't do this to die clean, Marisol. Do it to live dirty. Come back down off this mountain with us afterward and be someone who has to keep going. That's the harder sentence."

Marisol's throat worked. "You sound like her."

"I'm told that's genetic."

A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob, and then she pulled her hood up and started down the moraine, boots crunching, each step taking her further from the only people in twenty years who had looked at her and seen anything worth saving.

Behind her, faint under the wind, she heard Yara say: "We'll see you at the pass, guide. Both of us. Don't make me climb down to fetch you."

She raised one hand without turning around, because if she turned around she wouldn't leave.

* * *

Voss received her at the edge of the guild camp with a rifle slung casual across her back and two climbers flanking her, and Marisol noted — professionally, the way she noted everything now, the way she should have noted things twenty years ago — that the camp was smaller than it should have been. Three tents where five had set out. Two men sitting out a turn of frostbite by the fire. The mountain was taxing Voss's army the same way it taxed everyone, and there was a bitter comfort in that.

"Quintero." Voss's voice was gravel poured slow. "You came alone. Good. I wasn't certain you would."

"You wrote *or not at all*. I figured you meant it."

"I did." Voss studied her with eyes the color of slate runoff. "The route. All of it. No omissions, no clever forks. And then you walk south with amnesty papers and never climb again. That was the arrangement."

"That was the arrangement." Marisol crouched by the fire, held her hands to the heat, and bought herself three breaths. Then she took out the charcoal sketch she'd prepared — the real Hidden Route, accurate in every detail except one, the single omission folded into the eastern section where the survey line branched. "Here. The full passage. Crampon work above the seracs, fixed lines at the headwall, and the col crossing here—" she tapped the map "—through the old survey traverse. It's longer than the direct line but it's stable, and it puts you at the pass a day behind the others. You'll want to move tonight, before the next system comes in."

Voss took the sketch and read it the way she read everything — completely, suspiciously, twice. Marisol watched her and kept her face arranged into the tired honesty of a woman who had sold the last thing she had left.

"The survey traverse," Voss said. "Why haven't I heard of it?"

"Because it's on no map. Because the man who surveyed it died before he could publish, and because the guides who knew it kept it for emergencies." She met Voss's eyes. "This is an emergency."

Something passed over Voss's face then — not belief, exactly, but the hunger of a woman two days from her goal with a shrinking window and a shrinking team, the hunger that eats caution. Marisol had seen that hunger before. She had fed it before. Twenty years ago she had fed it with a shortcut, and four people had gone into the ice.

"This better be true, Quintero."

"It's true." And it was — every line of it, every anchor point, every elevation. Only the destination was a lie: the survey traverse did not reach the pass. It reached a hanging valley walled in by rock on three sides, a beautiful green amphitheater in summer, a stone coffin in a storm. There was a way out — a scramble up the western wall that would cost Voss's climbers a full day and their tempers — but by the time they found it, the window would be shut, and Yara would be standing at the pass with papers in hand.

Voss rolled the sketch and tucked it inside her coat. "Move at dawn," she said to her climbers. Then, to Marisol: "You'll lead."

Marisol had known this was coming. She had counted on it, actually — a guide who wouldn't lead her own route was a guide selling something. Still, hearing it landed like a stone under the ribs.

"I'll lead," she said.

"Then rest. We break camp at first light." Voss turned toward her tent, then paused. "For what it's worth — I read the old reports. The Vasquez expedition. I know what they say about you."

"They say the truth. I was the guide. People died."

"People die on mountains. Guides get blamed." Voss's voice was flat, unreadable. "I grew up paying tolls, Quintero. I've spent my life watching this guild squeeze travelers because some family holds a pass hostage. Whatever you were, whatever you did — I only care whether you can get me to that summit."

*And I only care,* Marisol thought, *whether you can live with what happens when I don't.*

"Get some sleep, Captain," she said aloud. "Dawn comes early up here."

* * *

She did not sleep. She lay in the borrowed tent listening to the wind reorganize itself against the canvas, and she thought about Elena Vasquez, who had sung hymns badly on the approach march and laughed at Marisol's terrible jokes and fallen nine hundred feet into a chute because her guide chose speed over sense. She thought about Adaeze Okafor, who had ordered the retreat with a voice like iron and gone back into the white alone so that the others could live, and whom the world had called dead for twenty years while she sat up here keeping a door open for strangers.

And she thought about Yara, saying *come back down off this mountain and be someone who has to keep going.*

Around midnight she crawled out of the tent. The sky had cleared hard and cold, stars in their thousands, and to the north — high, faint, catching starlight along its rim — she could see the notch of the Sable Current, a thin bright seam in the black shoulder of the range. Somewhere up there, a cabin. Somewhere up there, a dying woman and her child, reunited after two decades, and a map that could set travelers free.

Somewhere up there, her absolution, waiting to be claimed or forfeited.

Voss's sentry nodded to her from the fire. "Can't sleep either?"

"Old habit," Marisol said. "Mountains talk at night. I like to hear what they're saying."

"What are they saying tonight?"

She looked north at the bright seam of the pass, and the answer rose in her chest with a weight that felt, for the first time in twenty years, less like guilt than like purpose.

"They're saying the storm's coming early," she said. "And that some roads aren't for sale."

The sentry laughed, thinking it a joke. Marisol smiled, and fed the fire, and waited for dawn with her hands open and empty — no compass, no claim, no amnesty, nothing left to lose and one true thing left to do.

At first light, she pointed the guild's climbers east, toward the survey line, toward the long beautiful lie, and began to walk them into the consequence she had chosen.
