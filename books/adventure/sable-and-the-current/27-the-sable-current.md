## 27. The Sable Current

The last pitch was the kind of slope that made a liar out of every map Teo had ever drawn. It looked like nothing from below — a gray ramp of stone and old snow, maybe two hundred meters of vertical — but it climbed at an angle that forced them onto all fours, and the wind came off the summit ridge in long horizontal gusts that pressed against their backs like hands trying to shove them up faster than was safe.

Yara went first. They had learned that much about themselves in three weeks of mountain: on the river they steered from behind, from the stern, where they could see everything; on the mountain they had to lead, because if they followed anyone else's line up loose rock they would second-guess it into paralysis. Marisol came second, roped to Yara with a length of line she'd salvaged from the icefall crossing, her breath coming in short disciplined puffs. Teo came last, which he'd insisted on, because — he said — someone had to make sure nobody's pack straps worked loose, and because he was still drafting as he climbed, one gloved thumb hooked through his notebook's spiral binding, sketching the slope's features in shorthand only he could read.

"Ridge in twenty minutes," Marisol called over the wind. "If the col is what I remember."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we're lost above eight thousand feet and I'll be too tired to be ashamed."

Yara laughed, or something like it — the altitude had flattened their laugh into a kind of exhalation — and kept climbing.

They crested the ridge just after noon, and the world opened.

Yara had spent their whole life inside valleys. The Verre River ran between walls of green hills so close that the sky was always a ribbon, always something you looked *up* at, never something you stood inside of. They had thought they understood mountains from the water — those white-crowned shapes on the horizon that the cargo buyers pointed at, that the guild's toll ledgers treated as a wall, a boundary, a thing to be gone around or paid through.

Nothing had prepared them for standing on top of the wall.

The range spread out in every direction like a frozen sea — and Yara thought in river terms even now, couldn't help it: this was a flood of stone, an ancient current caught mid-surge and turned to granite, wave after wave of it running west into haze. The light was enormous. It fell without obstruction, without mercy, filling every fold and couloir with a clarity that made distances meaningless. A peak that looked close enough to touch was a day away. The glacier below them was a mile wide and moved, Marisol said, though it moved so slowly the motion was invisible, a river that had forgotten how to hurry.

But none of that was what stopped Yara's breath.

Ahead, cutting through the summit ridge like a wound, ran a corridor.

It was perhaps fifty meters wide and half a kilometer long, walled on both sides by rock towers streaked black and white, and down its whole length the snow was *moving*. Not falling — streaming. The wind, funneled by the corridor's walls, lifted the surface snow and carried it eastward in a continuous silver flood, a river of powder pouring through the gap in the mountains as if the sky itself were draining through it. Where the light hit it, it glittered. Where it passed into shadow, it glowed faintly, the way deep water does. It made a sound — a low, ceaseless rush, like the Verre in spring flood heard from far off, like breathing, like the ocean Yara had never seen.

Marisol stood beside them, snow crusted in her eyebrows, and said quietly, "There it is."

"The Sable Current," Yara said. Their voice came out strange. Thin. Young.

"That's what your mother called it. Everyone else called it the Wind Gap, or the Col, or nothing at all, because most people never got here." Marisol's jaw worked, like there was more she wanted to say and twenty years of not saying it had rusted the hinge. "She named it because of how it looks at dusk. When the light goes low, the streaming snow turns dark against the sky. Like ink. Like a current of black water running uphill." She shook her head slowly. "I told her once it was the most beautiful thing I'd ever seen and she said — she said, *it's the most useful thing I've ever seen, and I intend to keep it that way.*"

Yara looked at the old guide. Marisol's face was turned toward the pass, toward the streaming silver, and her eyes were wet, and she didn't wipe them, and Yara understood that some kinds of crying were done standing up, in the wind, where no one could offer a handkerchief, and you let them be.

* * *

They crossed the col in single file, roped, moving fast because the wind in the corridor was a living force that could take an unbraced person off their feet. Yara felt it grab at them twice — a sudden lateral gust that leaned them hard against the rope and made Marisol shout something about leaning into it, *lean, don't fight it*, and Yara leaned, and the wind held them up instead of knocking them down, and they thought: *the river taught me this too. You don't fight the current. You read it. You use it.*

Their mother had known that. Had built her life around it, apparently. Had named a mountain pass the way a boatman names a boat.

And then they came around the last rock tower, out of the worst of the funnel, and saw the cabin.

It was small — smaller than the wheelhouse of the *Perdika*, smaller than any home Yara had lived in. Stone footwalls, timber upper walls gone silver-gray with weather, a roof of overlapping slates weighted down with rocks along every edge. Smoke-stain fanned up the eastern face above a chimney cap. There was a woodpile under an oilcloth lean-to, stacked with a precision that spoke of long habit. A line strung between two posts held frost-stiffened cloth — clothing, flags, something. A cairn stood near the door, waist-high, topped with a wooden post from which hung a board painted with characters Yara could not read at this distance except to understand, instinctively, that they were a warning.

Someone lived here. Someone had lived here for a long time. Twenty years of winters' worth of firewood discipline. Twenty years of smoke.

Teo stopped beside Yara and stared, and then did the thing he always did when the world exceeded him: he took out his notebook. But his hand was shaking too much to draw, and after a moment he put it away and just said, very quietly, "It's real. All of it. She was really here."

"She's here," Yara said. And then heard their own voice say it, and felt the words land in their chest like a dropped anchor — that sudden, terrible settling weight, the thing that stops a drifting boat dead in the water.

Because that was the truth they had been climbing toward for three weeks, through ambushes and icefalls and storms and betrayals, and now it stood in front of them wearing a slate roof, and Yara discovered that they did not know how to walk the last hundred meters.

* * *

Marisol looked at them. "Do you want me to go first?"

"No." Yara said it before thinking, and then understood why it was true. "No. This part's mine."

Teo put a hand on their shoulder — briefly, awkwardly, the way he touched people, like he was checking whether they were solid — and stepped back. Marisol did too. They stayed by the rock tower, two small figures against the enormous streaming light, and gave Yara the corridor.

Yara walked.

The ground rose gently toward the cabin across a shelf of packed snow. Wind-scoured patches showed bare earth, and in one of them, absurdly, impossibly, grew a cluster of small alpine flowers — purple, star-shaped, huddled in the lee of a stone. Yara stopped and looked at them for longer than they meant to. Something alive, up here, in the killing cold, blooming anyway. They thought of their mother's face in the one portrait that had survived: a woman laughing at something outside the frame, daring the world to be as funny as she found it.

Fifty meters. Thirty. Close enough now to see details: the axe buried head-down in a chopping block; a tin cup left on the windowsill, mouth-down, drained; boot prints in the snow near the door, fresh enough that the edges hadn't feathered. Fresh. Hours old, maybe less. Yara's heart began to do something it had never done on any river, in any storm, with any debt collector's fist an inch from their face. It began to *shake*.

Twenty years ago, Adaeze Okafor had kissed a six-year-old child goodbye at a dock on the Verre and walked into these mountains and died — that was what everyone said, that was what the guild's records said, that was what Yara had built their whole careful, unglamorous, debt-ridden life on top of, the way you build a house on a grave. *Your mother abandoned you,* the dock gossips had said when they thought Yara was too young to understand. *Your mother is dead,* the ferry guild's clerks had written in the file that determined who paid her debts. *Your mother chose the mountain over you.*

And the compass in Yara's coat pocket — cracked casing, bent needle, guild seal exposed like a wound — had been pointing at this cabin, at whoever stood inside it, since the day a freak tide showed Yara a cave full of their own inheritance.

Ten meters. Yara could hear, through the wind, a sound from inside the cabin. Faint. Rhythmic. Wood being split? Water being carried? Or — they realized, and their stomach dropped — humming. Someone was humming. A tune Yara didn't know and knew at the same time, the way you know a word you haven't heard since childhood, stored somewhere below memory, in the body, in the bones. Six-year-olds are sponges for melodies. Whatever Adaeze Okafor had sung while packing for her last expedition, it had soaked into her child and stayed there for twenty years, waiting.

Yara stood at the door and could not raise their hand.

This was worse than the icefall. Worse than Voss's climbers, worse than the rope bridge, worse than the night Marisol signaled the enemy and Yara lay awake listening to the wind and wondering if loyalty was a thing that could be bought out from under you. Because all of those dangers had been *out there*, and this was in here, behind a plank door, and it was the thing they had wanted most in the world for twenty years without letting themselves want it, and wanting it had nearly killed them twice, and now it was four planks of weathered timber away and their hand would not move.

What if she wasn't in there. What if someone else was. What if she was, and she looked at Yara and saw a stranger. What if she was dying right now, on the other side of this door, alone, having guarded a free road for two decades while her child hauled cargo for the guild that hunted her. What if the first thing Yara said was wrong. What if the first thing *she* said was wrong. What do you say to a ghost who turns out to have a heartbeat?

The needle of the compass, Yara realized suddenly, was trembling. They could feel it through the coat — not pointing anymore but *quivering*, straining, the way a dog strains at a leash held by someone too grief-frozen to unclip it. As if the brass itself had more courage than its bearer.

*Come on,* Yara told themselves. *You've rowed floodwater. You've stared down Odile Voss. You've carried a stranger's secrets and a dead woman's letters and your own name like ballast for twenty years. Open the door.*

They raised their hand. Knocked — three times, the dockhand's knock, the knock you use on a locked boathouse, and they almost laughed at themselves for it, almost wept, because of all the possible knocks in the world their body had chosen the professional one, the transactional one, *hello, I have business here.*

The humming stopped.

Silence — total, except for the Current's endless rushing breath behind them. Then footsteps, unhurried, crossing a small room. Then a sound Yara identified a half-second too late: the working of a rifle bolt.

Of course. Of course. A woman alone at eight thousand feet, guarding a pass the richest syndicate in the lowlands had spent twenty years trying to buy or break, hearing a knock at her door in the last week before winter closed the route. She had survived up here by assuming every visitor was a threat, and she was right to, and Yara loved her already for it and was also about to die of it.

"I'm armed," said a voice through the wood. Low. Roughened by altitude and years. Steady as a keel. "So are you, or you wouldn't have knocked. State your business."

Yara opened their mouth and every language they knew failed them. Their business was twenty years old. Their business was a chest in a drowned cave and a half-burned letter and a name chosen in secret and a compass that lied about north because north had never mattered. How did you state that through a door, to a rifle?

"My name is Yara," they said finally, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect, river-trained, load-bearing. "My mother's name was Adaeze Okafor. I think you've been waiting for me a long time."

A pause. Long enough for the wind to change direction, for the streaming snow in the corridor behind to shift from silver to gold as a cloud released the sun.

Then, through the door, so quiet Yara almost missed it beneath the Current's roar:

"...Show me the needle."

Yara's hand went to their coat pocket. It was shaking. Everything was shaking — their hand, their heart, the compass needle straining toward the woman on the other side of four planks of pine. They drew it out, cracked casing and all, guild seal glaring, and held it up as if the door could see, as if brass could testify, and in a way it could, in a way it had been testifying since Widow's Bend, and Yara understood in that moment that they had not carried it up a mountain.

It had carried them.

The bolt slid. The latch lifted. And the door of the cabin at the heart of the Sable Current began, inch by inch, to open.
