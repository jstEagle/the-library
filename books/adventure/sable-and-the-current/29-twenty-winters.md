## 29. Twenty Winters

The rifle was still in her hands when the trembling started.

Adaeze Okafor had not held it raised at a living soul in nineteen years — not since the last desperate season, when a pair of guild scouts had climbed too high and she had fired a warning shot into the blue and watched them turn back with their maps half-drawn and their greed intact. She had told herself then that the next time she aimed this gun at a person, it would be the last time, because there would be no one left to climb for. The guild would forget her. The pass would outlive her interest in it. She would simply become part of the mountain's weather, one more wind among winds.

She had not counted on her own face coming up the trail.

The child standing in her doorway — no, not a child anymore, a grown person with her own father's stubborn jaw and her own mother's river-gray eyes — stood beside two strangers, and all three of them were breathing hard from the altitude, and none of them was running. That was what undid her first, before the compass, before the letters, before any of it: they were not running. They had walked up to the door of a woman who had spent twenty years making herself unfindable, and they had stopped, and waited, as if being found were a thing they had every right to.

Adaeze lowered the rifle an inch. Then another inch. Her hands shook so badly the barrel described a small, helpless arc against the floorboards, tracing nothing, threatening no one.

"You have her eyes," she said, and hated herself for saying it, because it was not a greeting, it was an accusation dressed as tenderness. *You have her eyes* meant *you should not be here.* It meant *I know exactly who you are and I need you to understand that I chose this.*

Yara did not flinch. Of course they didn't. They had been raised by a river, by dockhands and debt collectors and the long gray patience of the Verre, and a river teaches you early that water does not apologize for the shape of its banks. Yara looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a charted reef you've only ever heard about — and said, quietly, "We brought your letter back. The one from the cairn. I thought you'd want—"

"I wrote four," Adaeze said. "There are two more above the ledges. One under the icefall, if it hasn't been swallowed." She heard her own voice and marveled at it: steady, dry, almost administrative, as though she were reporting on stores rather than confessing to twenty years of deliberate erasure. "I wrote them the way you'd leave markers on a route. In case someone ever came looking who deserved to find me."

"And us?" Marisol asked from the doorway, her voice rough with something older than apology. "Did we deserve to?"

Adaeze turned and looked at the disgraced guide for a long moment. The last time she had seen Marisol Vane, the woman had been thirty years old and weeping into a rope coil at base camp, swearing she'd never lead anyone anywhere again. Now she was grayer, softer in the middle, her hands still scarred along the knuckles where the mountain had taken its tolls. But her eyes were the same eyes, and they were asking the same question they had asked on the worst night of both their lives: *Was it my fault?*

"Sit down," Adaeze said. "All of you. The stove is warm and the tea is terrible and I am going to tell you a story I have never told anyone, and when I'm done, you can decide what you think of me. I already know what I think."

* * *

The cabin was small — one room, a sleeping loft, walls lined with split cedar she had cut herself the first summer, when she still believed the exile would be temporary. Everything in it spoke of long habitation without comfort: the mended windowpane, the third chair built after the second broke, the row of journals bound in waxed canvas occupying an entire shelf like a second family. On the wall above the hearth hung a framed scrap of river-chart, water-stained, showing a bend in the Verre with a settlement marked in faded ink. Widow's Bend. Home. She had put it there the first winter and never taken it down, because a person needed to remember what they were guarding their way back *to*, or the guarding became merely habit, and habit was a kind of dying.

She set the rifle in its corner — a ritual, laying down the sentry's burden, if only for an hour — and poured tea from a pot that had seen better decades. Teo, the young cartographer, sat closest to the shelf of journals and could not stop looking at them, and Adaeze found she did not mind. A mapmaker's hunger was a respectable hunger. It had been her own, once.

"Twenty years ago," she began, "the Ferry Guild was not what it is now. It was worse, in a different way. Smaller. Hungrier. Odile Voss hadn't yet learned to wear her ambition like a uniform; she wore it like a knife. And the guild's founders — the old families, the ones whose names are still carved over the tollbooths — had decided that the mountains were the next river. Rivers you can charge for crossing. Mountains you can charge for passing through. All they lacked was a route."

She wrapped her hands around her cup, and the heat of it felt like holding something living.

"I was a guide. A good one. Better than good — I had found things other people said weren't there, and I had come back from places that kept other people, and that reputation was worth more to the guild than any cargo I ever carried. They wanted the Sable Current. The high pass. Every trader, every pilgrim, every family with a wagon and a dream had heard the stories: a corridor through the range where the wind runs like water, where you can cross in three days instead of three weeks around the southern shoulder. Free, they said. Always free. That was the legend's whole spine — a road no one owned."

Marisol's cup had stopped halfway to her mouth. She knew this part. She had lived this part. But knowing and hearing were different organs, and Adaeze watched her old friend brace herself like a woman about to take a wave broadside.

"The guild offered me a contract. An extraordinary one. Lead their survey team to the pass, map it precisely, and in exchange — money, yes, but also legitimacy. My name on the charter. A house in the lowlands. Security for my family." Her voice caught, just slightly, on that word. *Family.* "I had a child then. Small. Sharp-eyed. Already more comfortable on the deck of a boat than on solid ground. I thought I was negotiating for their future."

She set the cup down. Her hands were steadier now; the story, once begun, carried its own ballast.

"What I didn't understand — what I refused to understand until it was too late — was that the contract had teeth hidden in its margins. Clause eleven. Once mapped, the route became guild property. Toll gates at both approaches. Tariffs scaled to cargo weight. Patrols to enforce them. And the clause beneath that, clause twelve, the one they made me initial separately, in red ink: the guide who mapped the pass would be legally bound to reveal every approach, every shelter, every seasonal variation — and forbidden, on pain of guild prosecution, to ever guide anyone across it *unlicensed* again. Do you understand what that meant? It meant that once I drew the map, the free road died, and I became its jailer. They weren't buying my skill. They were buying my silence, forever, and calling it honor."

Teo leaned forward. "Clause twelve. I've seen modern guild contracts. They still use that structure. They just bury it deeper."

"They learned," Adaeze said grimly. "From me. From what happened next."

* * *

Here the story bent toward the place Marisol dreaded, and Adaeze took a breath before following it down, because some truths you carry so long they grow roots, and pulling them up hurts even when the ground wants them gone.

"The expedition went wrong in the way high expeditions go wrong — slowly, then all at once. Weather. A crevasse field that had shifted since my scouting notes. And a decision I made at the col, on the fourth day, with the barometer falling and two of our party already frostbitten: I ordered the retreat."

"We would have died." Marisol's voice came out cracked. "You said it yourself. You said the mountain doesn't negotiate."

"The mountain doesn't negotiate," Adaeze agreed. "But the guild does, and that's the part no one tells you. When we came down — five of us alive out of nine — the guild didn't mourn. They *recalculated*. Four deaths, they said, regrettable, but proof of the route's difficulty, which justified higher tolls for guided passage, which justified the whole enterprise. They took my dead and turned them into arithmetic. And then they came to me privately, and this is the conversation I have replayed every winter for twenty years, and they said: *Adaeze. The route is half-mapped. Finish it. Sign the full charter. Or we will assume your loyalty lies elsewhere, and we will act accordingly.*"

She let that sit in the warm air of the cabin, let the word *accordingly* do its cold work.

"I asked what *accordingly* meant. They smiled. They mentioned my child. They mentioned the boat we lived on, and how easily a mooring license could lapse, and how easily a hull could take on water in a storm with no witnesses. They didn't threaten me directly. They never had to. They just listed everything I loved in a calm voice, the way you'd read a manifest, and then they wished me a pleasant evening."

Her eyes went dark for a moment, remembering.

"So I did the only thing left. I lied. I told them I'd finish the mapping in the spring, alone, to clear my head after the losses. They liked that — grief makes a worker docile, they believe. And that winter, I staged my death."

Teo's breath actually caught audibly. Yara had gone very still, the stillness of a person listening with their whole body, afraid that moving might break whatever spell was letting these words exist.

"A storm on the lower glacier. I left my pack half-buried in a collapse zone, my identification inside it, my jacket snagged on serac debris where searchers would be sure to find it. I knew the terrain well enough to make the evidence convincing — I'd spent years learning how the mountain eats things, so I simply fed it a meal shaped like me. The guild searched for six days. Marisol—" she glanced at her old friend, gently, "—Marisol identified the jacket. She wept over it. She carried the news home to my child, and I have never forgiven myself for that, and I never will, and I don't ask forgiveness for it. I ask only that you understand it."

"I carried it," Marisol whispered. "God help me, I carried it to the dock myself. I told a seven-year-old their mother was gone."

"You told the truth as you knew it," Adaeze said. "That's the cruelty of it. Everyone in that story told the truth except me. I was the only liar, and the lie was the only mercy available." She looked at Yara now, directly, and forced herself not to look away. "I let my child grow up believing I was dead rather than let the guild torture the route out of me. Because that's what it would have been — not a threat made once, but pressure applied daily, year after year, until I gave them what they wanted or broke trying. They would have used you, my love. They told me as much, in the language of manifests and moorings. Distance was the only wall thick enough. So I built it, and I hid behind it, and I called it protection, and some nights it even was."

* * *

The fire had burned low. Adaeze rose to feed it, and the simple act — splitting kindling, arranging it, coaxing flame — steadied her the way it always had. Work was her oldest prayer.

"When they declared me dead," she continued, settling back onto her bench, "something unexpected happened. The guild's claim collapsed. No living guide held knowledge of the route. The half-map was useless without the woman who could read it. The Sable Current passed back into legend — *unmapped, unnegotiated, unowned.* For a few years, they tried to mount their own surveys. Two parties turned back at the icefall. One didn't turn back at all. And then the founders died, one by one, and the whole scheme gathered dust, and the pass stayed free the only way a thing stays free: because no one could prove it belonged to anyone."

"But you stayed," Yara said. Their voice was quiet, almost bewildered. "Twenty years. Alone. Guarding a road nobody was attacking."

"At first, staying was penance," Adaeze admitted. "Then it was vigilance. Then, somewhere around the tenth winter, it was simply my life, and I stopped being able to imagine another one. I patrolled the approaches each season. I dismantled cairns that surveyors built. I moved the shelter stones so their maps wouldn't reconcile. Twice I fired warning shots. Once — I'm not proud of this — I let a party believe the route was cursed, walked ahead of them in the fog rattling stones, singing in a register the wind distorted until they fled. I became a ghost on purpose. It seemed fitting. I had already become one everywhere else."

She looked at her hands, brown and cracked and strong, hands that had hauled ropes and split wood and buried strangers' dead and written letters to a child who might never read them.

"The loneliness was the price. I won't pretend it wasn't enormous. There were winters I talked to the stove. Winters I wrote letters I couldn't send and burned them one by one, reading each aloud first, so the words existed somewhere besides paper. Winters I nearly walked down to the valley just to hear a voice that wasn't mine echoing back off the ice. But every time I weighed it — my solitude against the open road — the road won. Because I had seen what tolls do. I grew up watching ferrymen bleed villages dry at the crossings. I watched a woman once choose between paying the toll and feeding her children, and pay, and I never forgot her face. A free pass isn't an abstraction. It's that woman's children eating. It's the pilgrim and the peddler and the runaway all walking the same corridor with nobody's hand in their pocket. If the cost of that was my name, my history, my child's memory of me — then the ledger balanced, and I paid it, and I have paid it every single day for twenty years, and I would pay it again tomorrow."

Yara's eyes were wet. They didn't hide it. Something in their expression had shifted during the telling — not softening exactly, but a kind of terrible comprehension, the look of someone who has finally been shown the full architecture of a wall they'd spent their life pressing against.

"You let me think you drowned," Yara said. Not accusing. Measuring. "For twenty years. You let me grieve you."

"Yes."

"And the letters — the chest at Widow's Bend, the cairn — those were for me. If I ever came."

"If you ever came, and if you came *free*," Adaeze said, leaning forward, and here her voice acquired an urgency that twenty years of solitude had kept sharpened like a blade. "That was the condition, my love. Listen to me. The compass, the letters, the seal — I left them where the tide could give them up only to someone patient enough to wait and brave enough to follow. I could not know who would find them. I could only make sure that whoever did would inherit not just the route but the *reason* for it. The whole truth. Clause eleven and clause twelve and the red ink and the manifest-voice threats and the woman who paid twenty winters so the road would stay unbought. If you inherited the pass, you had to inherit the war that comes with it. Otherwise the inheritance is just a treasure, and treasures get stolen."

Outside, the light was changing. Through the mended windowpane, Adaeze could see the far wall of the pass catching the late sun, and the wind beginning its evening run down the corridor — the Sable Current itself, streaming snow off the cornices in long silver ribbons, the river in the sky that had named the place. She had watched it ten thousand times. She would watch it ten thousand more, if the mountain granted them. It still moved her. After everything, it still moved her, the way a free thing moving freely always moves the people who have paid to keep it so.

But her chest hurt. It had been hurting since midsummer — a deep, grinding ache beneath the sternum that worsened with altitude and effort, that had begun waking her at night, that she had diagnosed herself with the blunt honesty of a woman with no doctor within a hundred miles. She knew what it was. She had watched it take her father. She had perhaps a season in her, maybe less, maybe more if the winter was gentle and she was careful, and careful was a word she had never once managed to live by.

And now, tonight, with her child sitting at her table and the truth finally spoken and the journals on the shelf waiting for younger hands — now the vigilance felt less like duty than like a relay race run alone for too long, hearing footsteps finally approaching the exchange point.

She was saved from having to say any of this by the weather, which had opinions of its own. The wind outside shifted quarter, swung north, and dropped ten degrees in as many minutes, and the ribbons of spindrift off the cornices thickened into something with intent. Adaeze had read this sky for twenty years. She knew what it was building.

"Storm by morning," she said, rising to check the shutters. "A real one. We'll want extra wood in before dark, and the animals secured, and—"

She stopped.

Because down the eastern approach, small against the gathering white, moving with the disciplined spacing of trained climbers under orders — a line of figures was ascending toward the pass. Six of them. Maybe seven. Led by a woman whose stride she recognized even at that distance, even after twenty years, because some ambitions walk with a signature all their own.

Odile Voss had found the route.

Adaeze set down the lantern, took the rifle back out of its corner, and loaded it by feel in the dimming light while her child watched her with questions rising in their eyes like floodwater.

"There," she said quietly, "is the rest of the story. Come and see."
