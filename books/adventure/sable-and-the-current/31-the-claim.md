## 31. The Claim

The wind at the pass had a voice. Yara had learned its grammar over the last three days — the way it whistled through the corridor of stone like breath drawn across the mouth of a bottle, the way it carried snow in long silver threads that streamed eastward as if the mountain itself were a river running uphill toward the sky. The Sable Current, Adaeze had called it once, in a letter Yara had read by lamplight with trembling hands. Now they stood inside it, and it was more beautiful than any letter could hold.

But this morning there was no time for beauty.

Voss was coming.

Yara had known it since the storm broke two nights ago, since the moment they'd pulled her climbers shivering and half-frozen into the cabin's low doorway and watched Odile Voss herself arrive an hour behind them, frostbitten and furious and still, somehow, unbowed. She had sat on the far side of the fire that night, saying nothing, watching everything. Her climbers had slept in the loft among the cured fish and the bundles of kindling. Voss had not slept. Yara had seen her eyes in the dark, open, fixed on the ceiling beams, calculating.

Now the sky had cleared to a hard, rinsed blue, and the pass lay open between its walls of granite like a road made of light. There would be no weather to hide behind. Whatever happened next would happen in full view, under the sun, with everyone watching — Adaeze, frail as a candle in her chair by the stove; Marisol, standing at the cabin's edge with her old rope coiled around her shoulders like a penance; Teo, hunched over the table with ink-stained fingers, copying something; and six of Voss's climbers, their loyalty stretched thin as wire, waiting to see which way it snapped.

Yara stood outside the cabin door and breathed the cold until it hurt, and then breathed it some more, because they wanted to hurt. They wanted every part of this morning sharp.

Inside, on the table scarred by twenty years of knife-work and candle-drip, Yara laid out the papers.

They had carried them the whole way up without fully understanding what they carried. That was the strange arithmetic of inheritance: you haul a thing for weeks through scree and icefall and fear, and only at the end do you weigh it properly. The compass casing — cracked down one side where the fall at the col had split it open, brass gone green at the seams, but intact enough, whole enough — sat first. Inside its lid, exposed now like a secret that had been waiting decades for someone clumsy enough to break it open, was the seal.

Yara had almost missed it. Teo hadn't. He had been the one who held the broken casing up to the firelight three nights ago and gone quiet in a way that frightened Yara more than shouting would have. *This is a guild seal,* he had said. *The old guild. The founding guild.* And beneath it, engraved so fine you needed the right angle of light to catch it: a grant number, a date, and a name.

*Okafor.*

The letters came next. Yara spread them in order — the half-burned letter from the chest at Widow's Bend, its edges charred brown and curling, rescued from a fire someone had started and someone else had smothered before it finished its work; the second letter from the shrine cairn, addressed to *my child, whoever you become*, its paper soft as cloth from twenty years of mountain cold; and the bundle Adaeze herself had pressed into Yara's hands two mornings ago, when she had begun to speak of endings, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a knot Yara recognized from a hundred cargo manifests, the knot river people used when a thing must survive water.

Deeds. Correspondence. A surveyor's report signed by a man whose name appeared in the guild's own founding charter — Teo had checked, cross-checked, and checked again, because Teo trusted nothing he hadn't verified twice, and Yara loved him for it. And at the bottom of the bundle, the document itself: the grant. The Sable Current Pass, conveyed in perpetuity to the family Okafor, to be held open, maintained, and kept *free of charge to all who travel*, in exchange for which the family bore the burden of its keeping.

Free for all who travel. Yara read those four words again and again until they stopped looking like ink and started looking like a promise. Somewhere in the lowlands, a clerk in a guild office had written them a hundred years ago, and somewhere between then and now the guild had decided the promise was inconvenient, and somewhere in the last twenty years Adaeze Okafor had climbed alone into this wind-carved corridor and kept the promise alive with her own failing body because no one else would.

Paper. It was all paper. Faded ink, brittle fiber, wax seals cracked with age. Yara had spent their life on the river, where worth was measured in cargo tonnage and hull integrity, where a thing was real if it floated and worthless if it didn't. Paper did not float. And yet here, at the top of the world, paper was about to be the heaviest object in the room.

* *

Adaeze woke mid-morning. She woke slowly, the way she did everything now, surfacing through pain like a swimmer coming up through cold water. Yara sat beside her while she gathered herself, and when she asked — in a voice worn thin as the letters — what the day would bring, Yara told her the truth.

"Voss will contest it," Yara said. "She'll say the grant lapsed. She'll say the family abandoned the pass. She'll have lawyers' arguments memorized, even up here, even without lawyers."

Adaeze smiled, and the smile cost her something; Yara could see it in the way her hand tightened on the blanket's edge. "She'll say whatever she has to. But claims aren't argued, child. They're shown." She looked at the table, at the papers lying in the sun that fell through the cabin's single window. "Show her."

The word *child* landed differently now than it had two days ago. Two days ago it had been a stranger's word, a word from a woman Yara had spent twenty years building a fortress against, brick by brick, memory by memory. Now it was simply true. Yara was her child. Whoever they had become — and they had become themselves, deliberately, painfully, choosing the name Yara the way a boatwright chooses timber — they were hers, and she was theirs, and the papers on the table were the bridge between the person Yara had invented and the person history insisted they had always been.

That was the thing about the claim, Yara realized, standing at the table while Adaeze dressed with Marisol's help, moving like a ship taking on water but refusing to sink. The claim wasn't really about the pass. The pass was stone and wind and snow; it would be here whether anyone claimed it or not. The claim was about the thread. The unbroken line from the founding charter to the grant to Adaeze's twenty winters alone to the compass in a drowned cave at Widow's Bend to a nonbinary riverboat captain who had once believed they had no family at all except a debt and a boat.

The guild had spent twenty years assuming the thread was cut. They had declared Adaeze dead, closed the file, absorbed the pass into their toll roads by simple administrative inertia. Nobody had ever proven the thread cut. Nobody had ever bothered to check. And now Yara stood holding both severed ends in their hands, and the ends matched.

* *

Voss came up the corridor at noon.

Yara watched her come, from the cabin doorway, and had to admit — privately, grudgingly, in the place where they kept the truths that didn't help — that there was something magnificent in it. She walked the pass like a woman walking into a courtroom she intended to burn down. Her frostbite had been tended by her own climbers, her face raw and peeling at the edges, and she moved anyway, steadily, without complaint, the way people move when they have decided that dignity is the last possession they will surrender.

Behind her came her six. Yara counted them, the way they counted everything now — a habit the mountains had taught them in a single season, sharper than any the river ever managed. Six. There had been eleven at the col. The icefall had taken its toll, and the storm had taken more, and two had turned back below the ledges with the honesty of men who knew their limits. Six remained, and of the six, Yara suspected — had suspected since the night in the cabin, since the way they ate the stew Adaeze's stores provided and thanked her for it, since the way two of them had quietly helped Marisol shore up the woodpile without being asked — that perhaps three of them still believed in what they'd come to do.

Voss stopped ten paces from the cabin door. Not closer. She chose her ground the way she chose everything, deliberately, and Yara understood that the distance was a statement: *I am not entering your house. I am meeting you on the mountain.*

"Captain Okafor." Her voice carried over the wind without effort. She had a voice built for dockyards and council chambers, a voice that had probably never once been caught off guard. "I've had a night to consider your mother's condition, your presence here, and the situation generally. I'm prepared to make my final offer."

"We're prepared to decline it," Yara said, "but go ahead."

Voss's mouth twitched. Almost a smile. "The guild will fund the pass. Maintenance, staffing, a warden's station, emergency shelter — everything this route needs to be safe. In exchange, the guild administers it. Tolls, modest ones, scaled to cargo. Your mother retires with a pension and medical care in the lowlands. You receive a position — warden, if you want it, or a seat on the routes committee. Everyone gains. No one freezes."

It was a good offer. That was the trap of Odile Voss; she never offered bad ones. Every offer she had made since Widow's Bend had been structured to be survivable, reasonable, the kind of thing a tired person with a dying parent might accept at the end of a long road. Yara had lain awake two nights ago turning it over, honestly turning it, because they refused to become the kind of person who dismissed things just because the person offering them was the enemy.

And the answer was still no. Because the offer contained, folded small inside its generosity, the same word it had always contained: *tolls*. Scaled, modest, temporary-sounding. But the founding grant said free, and free was not a price point. Free was a principle, and principles did not scale.

"You're offering to buy something that isn't for sale," Yara said.

"Everything is for sale."

"No." Yara stepped forward, out of the doorway, onto the packed snow of the corridor floor, and felt the wind take their coat and press it flat against their back like a hand pushing them onward. "Some things are held. This is one of them."

Voss's eyes went narrow. Yara watched her reassess — watched the dockyard mind run its calculations, weighing the stubbornness of a riverboat captain against the odds of the terrain, and finding, perhaps, that the numbers had shifted. "Then show me why," she said. "Show me something better than sentiment. I've climbed past sentiment all the way up this mountain. Show me a claim."

So Yara showed her.

* *

They did it outside, in the open, because Yara wanted the mountain to witness it. They brought the table out — Marisol and Teo hauling it through the doorway with the practiced clumsiness of people who had learned each other's bodies and rhythms over a thousand miles of bad footing — and set it on the snow, and weighted the papers with stones from the corridor walls, gray stones veined white, stones that had been falling into this pass since before there were guilds.

Teo spoke first. Yara had asked him to, the night before, and he had agreed with a seriousness that made Yara certain they'd chosen rightly. He stood beside the table in his patched coat, the apprentice cartographer who had stowed away on a cargo boat to escape a woman he now faced across a table of old paper, and he did the thing he did best: he made the invisible legible.

"The founding charter of the Ferry Guild, year one," he began, his voice thin in the wind until he found its register and steadied. "Article nine. The guild shall identify, maintain, and hold open for the common use of all travelers such high routes as may be necessary for the free passage of trade and persons across the range. The Sable Current is named in the annex. Named. By the founders themselves."

He turned the page. "Twenty-two years later, the grant. Conveyance of the Sable Current Pass to the family Okafor, in perpetuity, in consideration of the family's undertaking of the pass's keeping. Clause two: the route shall remain free of charge to all who travel, without exception, in perpetuity. Clause three: should the family fail in its keeping for a period exceeding five consecutive years, the grant reverts to the guild." He looked up, and let the silence sit. "Five years. The family has not failed for five years. The family has not failed for one. Adaeze Okafor has kept this pass for twenty."

The wind gusted and lifted the corner of the oldest letter, and Yara put a hand on it, and felt — absurdly, undeniably — their mother's handwriting under their fingers, the pressure of a pen applied two decades ago by a woman who had been writing to a child she might never meet, sealing instructions into paper the way other people sealed them into stone.

"And the seal?" Voss said. Her voice had changed register. It was flatter now, harder, the sound of a woman who had stopped performing confidence and started checking her foundations. "A broken compass casing is not a deed, Captain."

"No," Yara agreed. "It's proof of custody." They held the casing up, turned it so the sun struck the green-brass lid, and pointed with one finger at the impression stamped inside — the old guild seal, the founders' mark, the same mark that appeared on the grant's wax pendant, matching it mark for mark, curve for curve. "The grant was issued together with this instrument. The compass that finds its holder's kin. It passed from founder to Okafor, and from Okafor to my mother, and from my mother into the sea, and from the sea into my hands. Chain of custody, Captain Voss. Unbroken. Documented. Witnessed." They nodded toward Marisol, who stood at the table's far end with her jaw set like a woman holding a door against a flood. "By a guide who was on the last expedition my mother led. Who can testify to what was carried, and what was lost, and what was hidden."

Marisol said nothing. She didn't need to. Her presence was testimony enough, and everyone present knew it, including Voss, whose gaze flicked to the disgraced guide and away again with the wariness of a woman who remembered exactly what Marisol knew.

Then Adaeze rose.

It took her a long time. Yara stood close, not helping, because Adaeze had asked them not to — *I will stand on my own feet when I say this, or I will say it lying down, but no one will lift me* — and the rising took nearly a minute, hand over hand up the back of a chair, breath sawing, body swaying, until finally she was upright in the corridor of her pass, facing the woman who had hunted her signature for twenty years, and the wind took her gray hair and streamed it east with the snow, and for a moment she looked less like a dying woman than like a standard planted in the ground.

"I am Adaeze Okafor," she said. "Holder of the grant. Keeper of the pass. Alive, Captain Voss, despite your guild's very thorough paperwork to the contrary." Her voice was wrecked and quiet and it carried anyway, the way truth carries, badly and irresistibly. "I have read your offers. I read them twenty years ago, when your predecessors made them to me in a room with the windows nailed shut, and I declined them, and they locked me in, and I escaped, and I came here, and I have declined them every winter since. I will decline yours today. My child holds my claim. The papers are true. The seal is true. The chain is true. And the clause your lawyers will reach for — abandonment, lapse, reversion — dies on this table, because I am standing on my pass, and I am not abandoned, and neither is it."

She paused, and gathered breath, and gave Voss the only gift she had left to give: clarity.

"There is no version of this where you win, Odile. There is only the version where you lose slowly, in court, over years, while travelers pay your tolls on a road that was never yours — or the version where you lose today, quickly, and choose what you become afterward. I know which one I'd choose. I've had twenty years to think about it."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing on the mountain.

Yara watched Voss's climbers watch their captain. Watched the youngest of them, a broad-shouldered man with frost-split lips, look from the papers to Adaeze to the pass itself — the corridor streaming its snow eastward, the free road lying open in both directions, the shelter cabin with its door that had taken in strangers during the storm without asking a single question — and watched something move behind his eyes that Yara recognized, because they had felt it themselves at the shrine cairn, reading a dead woman's letter by firelight: the feeling of being seen by something older and larger than yourself.

Paper as weapon. But also paper as witness. Every faded line on that table was a sentence someone had written believing it mattered, and it had mattered, and here it was, doing its work a century late, in the wind, at the top of the world.

Voss looked at the table for a long time. Then at Adaeze. Then, briefly, unexpectedly, at Yara — and there was a flicker in her expression that Yara would spend the rest of the descent trying to name. Recognition, maybe. Or grief. Or the specific exhaustion of a woman seeing, clearly, for the first time in years, the shape of the cage she had built around herself and called a career.

"This isn't settled," Voss said at last. Her voice was quieter now, stripped of the dockyard authority, down to something rawer. "Claims can be disputed. Grants can be challenged. I have counsel in the lowlands and the guild has deeper pockets than your family will ever see. I'm not conceding anything on a mountaintop because a dying woman stood up straight."

"I wouldn't expect you to," Adaeze said, and sat down again, slowly, in the chair her daughter held steady, and the audience was over.

But as Voss turned to go — as her climbers gathered their gear and began picking their way back down the corridor toward whatever camp they'd made below the eastern wall — Yara saw her stop. Saw her stand motionless in the streaming snow, facing down the pass, her back to the cabin, her shoulders carrying something heavier than her pack.

Saw her turn halfway back, and look at the youngest climber, the one with the frost-split lips, and see that he was no longer following her.

He was standing at the table, hat in his hands, reading the grant.
