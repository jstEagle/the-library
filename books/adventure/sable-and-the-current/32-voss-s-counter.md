## 32. Voss's Counter

The wind at the col had a voice Odile Voss had learned to read the way sailors read water. It came down the Sable Current in long, flattened sheets, hissing over the ice like a blade drawn across a whetstone, and when it changed pitch — when it dropped to that low, hollow moan — it meant the storm was still a day out, gathering its strength behind the eastern peaks. She had heard that moan twice in her life. Both times, people had died.

She stood now at the mouth of the pass with eleven climbers arrayed behind her in a ragged crescent, ropes coiled at their shoulders, ice axes planted in the crust like a fence of iron flowers. The cabin sat forty paces ahead, small and black-timbered against the white, smoke rising from its chimney in a thin, defiant thread. And between her and the door stood Yara Okafor, holding up a broken brass compass as though it were a writ of empire.

Voss had seen many things held up like that. Deeds. Charters. A guild seal pressed into wax by men who had been dead before she was born. She knew what paper was worth at altitude: nothing. Paper froze. Paper blew away. Paper could not hold a rope.

And yet.

* * *

She had not slept. That was the first thing she would admit, if she ever admitted anything. She had sat through the night in the snow cave below the col with her back against the ice, listening to her climbers breathe, and she had done the arithmetic she always did — rations, rope, daylight, the margin of error that separated a managed expedition from a burial party — and the arithmetic had come back wrong for the first time in twenty years. Not because of the mountain. Because of what had happened at the ambush site two days ago, when Marisol Reyes had looked her in the eye across thirty feet of scree and told her, calmly, that she knew a route Voss did not, and that the price of that knowledge was amnesty.

Voss had agreed because agreeing was cheaper than bleeding. But lying awake in the blue dark, she had turned the moment over and over like a stone in a pocket, and she kept arriving at the same uncomfortable grain of truth: Marisol hadn't negotiated from strength. She had negotiated from shame. And shame, Voss knew something about. Shame was the guild's oldest currency. It was what they paid their enforcers in when coin ran short, and it was what they had paid her in, all those years ago, when a girl from the toll towns with frostbitten heels and a head full of fire had signed her name on the guild's line because it was the only line anyone offered her.

Now here was Yara Okafor, standing in the wind, offering a different line.

"The pass was granted to Adaeze Okafor's family in perpetuity," the captain said. Their voice carried strangely in the corridor of stone and moving snow — not loud, but level, the way a river is level no matter how fast it runs. "Free passage for all travelers. The seal is the guild's own. Your founders stamped it themselves."

"Show me," Voss said.

Yara turned the broken compass in their hands. The casing had split along its seam where it had taken the fall on the hidden route, and inside, etched into the inner plate where no one was ever meant to look, was the seal: the ferry wheel, the crossed poles, the founding date. Around it, in a hand Voss recognized from a hundred toll ledgers — the old clerk's hand, the one that had written out the original charters before the guild's script standardized into something more like a brand — ran a line of text. Grant. Perpetuity. Free passage for all who travel.

Voss stepped closer. Her climbers watched her do it, and she felt them watching, and she understood, with the cold clarity that had made her captain, that this was the real battlefield. Not the cabin. Not the claim. The eleven people behind her, standing in the wind with numb toes and opinions they had never been asked for.

She took the compass. She turned it under her magnifier, the one she carried for reading contracts in bad light, which was most light, at altitude. The etching was genuine. She knew the founder's mark the way a priest knows scripture — she had spent three years in the guild archive as a young clerk, cataloguing exactly these documents, dreaming even then of the day she'd hold one that mattered. The grant was real. The date was real. The perpetuity clause was real, and it was ironclad, and it named not the guild but the family Okafor as holder of the pass, with the single condition — written in the clerk's careful, dying hand — that the route remain open and untolled forever.

The condition was the whole point. Voss had known that since the letter. *Voss must never hold the needle.* Adaeze Okafor had understood, twenty years ago, what Voss had spent twenty years failing to understand: that the guild did not want passes. It wanted gates. And a gate is only worth what you can charge to open it.

She handed the compass back. "It's authentic," she said, and her climbers heard her say it, and that was the beginning of the end of everything she had built.

* * *

"You're confirming the claim?" That was Hessler, her senior climber, a granite-faced man from the toll town of Brasse who had been paying the guild's fees since he was nine years old. He asked it quietly, the way men ask questions they already know the answers to, hoping the answer will change if enough silence surrounds it.

"I'm confirming the document," Voss said. "Documents aren't claims. Claims are enforced."

Hessler nodded slowly. Behind him, the others shifted. Voss read them the way she read weather, and what she read frightened her more than the storm coming down the eastern wall.

There was Perrin, nineteen, the youngest, whose mother sold rope at the Verre ferry docks and whose entire expedition wage would go to cover the toll debt on his father's boat. There was the twins, Anselm and Greta Kroll, who had grown up in a toll town so far upriver that the guild's fee booth was literally the first building you saw on the road in — a painted shack with a slot for coins and a sign that read PASSAGE IS A PRIVILEGE. There was Okonkwo, who never spoke on expeditions, who had once told her, drunk, at the bottom of a bottle of plum brandy in a border saloon, that his grandfather had walked the high passes free his whole life and called them *the people's stairs*, and that the first toll gate had gone up the year after the grandfather died, and that the family still talked about it the way other families talked about wars.

Eleven climbers. Eleven histories of the toll. Voss had recruited them the way the guild trained her to recruit — on wages, on loyalty, on the quiet understanding that the guild's enemies were their enemies too. But standing here, at the top of the world, watching a nonbinary riverboat captain hold up a piece of brass that proved every toll they had ever paid had been theft — not law, not custom, *theft*, dressed in wax and ribbon — she could feel the ground shifting beneath her own expedition. Not the literal ground. The other kind. The kind that decides whether men follow you back down the mountain or leave pieces of themselves on it.

"They're saying the pass stays free," Perrin said. His breath came out in a white plume. "If we take the pass for the guild — what happens tomorrow morning?"

Voss didn't answer. She didn't have an answer that wasn't the truth, and the truth was: a fee booth. Painted timber, a coin slot, a sign. Passage is a privilege. She had helped write that sign once, early in her career, before she understood that she wasn't writing signs, she was writing walls.

"And the day after that?"

"Tolls," she said. "Assessed by the guild. Collected by the guild. Administered—" she paused, and the pause cost her, because everyone present knew what word was coming, "—by me."

Okonkwo shifted his weight. The crunch of his boot on the crust was very loud in the wind-lull.

* * *

Here was the thing Odile Voss had never said aloud, not to the council, not to her diary, not to the frozen face of the woman she had been before the guild finished making her: she had joined to break the tolls. Every enforcer in the guild's history had a story like that, probably; hers was at least true. She had grown up in Brasse, third of six children of a bridge carpenter, and she remembered — she could still feel it in her palms, the way some memories live in the body instead of the mind — the winter her father couldn't pay. The fee collector had come on a Tuesday. Her father had knelt in the snow outside the booth, actually knelt, hat in his hands, and begged for a season's grace, and the collector — a soft man, a desk man, someone's nephew — had looked down at him with the particular patience of a person who has never once been cold in a way that mattered, and had said: *The guild does not grant grace. The guild grants extensions. At interest.*

Her father had paid. It took four years. Her sister had gone to work in the rope lofts at twelve. And Odile, who was fourteen and angry in the way that builds rather than breaks, had decided that the problem was not the toll. The problem was that people like her father had no power inside the system that tolled them. So she would get power. She would climb. She would become, from within, the correction.

Twenty-six years later she was standing at the summit of everything she had climbed, holding the correction in her hands like a dead bird, and the correction was a broken compass and a dead woman's letters and a riverboat captain who had nothing — no guild backing, no climbers, no money, no rope worth the name — except the one thing Voss had traded away somewhere on the ascent without noticing the exact ledge where she'd set it down.

The pass was supposed to be the answer. Control the pass, and the guild's revenue would fund the guild's mercy — that had been her proposal to the council, five years ago, delivered in the archive room with charts and projections and her own reputation staked across the table like a thrown glove. Tolls on the Sable Current would subsidize free passage on the lowland ferries. The poor would cross free because the rich would pay to climb. It was elegant. It was arithmetic. It was also, she saw now, with the terrible clarity of altitude, just the same booth with better math. Her father kneeling in the snow would kneel again. Only the sign would be newer.

Adaeze Okafor had seen it in one glance, twenty years ago, and chosen exile over arithmetic. Had faked her own death, abandoned her child, climbed into the teeth of the range, and spent two decades keeping a door open with her body. Voss had thought it madness when the file crossed her desk. Sacrifice of that scale read as either madness or sainthood, and the guild's taxonomy had no column for sainthood.

Standing here, she was no longer certain it was madness.

* * *

"So what's your play, Captain?" Hessler again, blunt now, done with quiet. "We've got eleven. They've got three and a sick woman. We take the cabin, we take the papers, we take the pass. That's the job. That's what we're paid for."

"That's what you're *paid* for," Voss said. "Yes."

The wind picked up again, that flat sheeting hiss, and for a moment nobody spoke, and in the silence Voss became aware of a sound she had not expected: the sound of her own climbers looking at each other. Not at her. At each other. Boots shifting. Eyes meeting sideways. The small, furtive choreography of people discovering they are a congregation and not an army.

Perrin spoke first, because the young always speak first when the old are busy calculating. "My mother pays the dock toll," he said. "Every cargo run. Every year. She's got a ledger at home — actual paper, she keeps it herself — twenty-two years of payments." He swallowed. "If the claim's real, then that ledger's a record of theft. Our theft. I'd be climbing up here to make sure my own mother keeps getting robbed."

"It's not theft if it's legal," Greta Kroll said, but her heart wasn't in it; Voss could hear the boilerplate running on empty. Greta had argued the guild's side in tavern debates for years, quick and fluent, and Voss had liked that about her, had marked her for promotion. But Greta had never argued it at the top of the thing itself, with the booth she was defending visible only in her imagination, and the imagination was failing her. "Is it?" Greta added, quieter, and it wasn't a question aimed at Voss. It was aimed at the twins' shared conscience, at her brother, at the wind.

Anselm said nothing. Anselm had a habit, on hard pitches, of touching the little tin medal of the Ferry Saint that hung under his shirt, and he was touching it now, working it between gloved fingers like a rosary bead. The Ferry Saint — Voss knew the iconography better than any living member of the clergy — was depicted carrying travelers across the water for free, having refused the king's gold. The guild had adopted him centuries ago and quietly cropped the refusal of gold out of the popular prints. Anselm, Voss realized, was praying to the uncropped version.

Okonkwo finally spoke, and when he did, his voice was the low one, the brandy one, the true one. "Captain. My grandfather called these mountains the people's stairs." He looked past her, up the corridor of the pass, where the wind-streamed snow poured along the rock like a river pouring along its bed — the Sable Current itself, alive, audible, a thing you could watch flowing. "I'm looking at it right now. And I'm thinking about a fee booth."

Nobody said *mutiny*. Nobody had to. Mutiny at altitude doesn't announce itself; it arrives as hesitation, as slack ropes, as the half-second delay in obedience that kills orders. Voss felt it arrive. She had spent her career manufacturing that hesitation in other people's crews — she knew its taste, its texture, the precise way it spread from the youngest to the oldest like damp through cloth — and now she stood inside it, and it was the loneliest weather she had ever stood in.

* * *

Yara Okafor watched all of this happen, and Voss watched Yara watching, and she understood that the riverboat captain had already counted her crew the way Voss had counted her own — had read the same cracks, the same shifting, the same congregation forming inside the army — and had simply decided to let it happen. Not to force it. To stand there, level as a river, holding the proof, while gravity did its work.

That was the move. Voss recognized it because it was her own best move, used against her. Don't argue. Don't threaten. Present the document and wait. Truth, given time and cold, outperforms force. She had built a career on the inverse principle — that force, given time and cold, outperforms truth — and she had believed it because she had never once stood on the losing side of it until now.

"Captain Voss," Yara said, and their voice was steady, and Voss hated how steady, hated the way steadiness in other people's mouths always sounded like a verdict. "You confirmed the seal yourself. In front of your own people. Whatever happens next, they all heard it."

"I know what they heard."

"Then you know the choice isn't mine to offer you anymore. It's theirs." Yara tilted their head slightly toward the eleven. "You can order them up this hill against a claim they now know is real, to install a toll booth their own families have bled under. Some of them will follow you. Men always follow the last order they were given. But not all of them. And the ones who don't will walk down this mountain and tell everyone in every toll town what they saw up here — that the guild's own seal says the pass was never theirs to charge for."

Voss looked at her climbers. Perrin had turned to face the pass. Okonkwo had not moved his eyes from the flowing snow. The Kroll twins stood shoulder to shoulder, and Greta's chin had come up, but not in defiance — in the specific way a person lifts their chin when they are trying to see something far away clearly. Hessler alone still faced Voss, waiting for the order, loyal the way granite is loyal: structurally, without opinion.

She could give the order. She calculated it honestly, because she owed her career that much honesty: seven would go up. Four would refuse, openly or otherwise. Seven climbers against a cabin defended by a dying woman with a rifle, a riverboat captain, a runaway apprentice, and a disgraced guide who knew the mountain better than anyone alive — plus the storm, which was now less than a day out and would kill the wounded first. Best case, she seized the cabin, held it through the storm, lost two or three climbers to cold and collapse, and owned a pass whose legal claim she had personally authenticated in front of witnesses. Worst case, she lost the storm, the climbers, and the pass, and the story that came down the mountain was of a guild captain who ordered her people to die enforcing a fraud her own eyes had confirmed.

And underneath the arithmetic, in the place where she kept the girl from Brasse with the frostbitten heels, another calculation ran, older and colder: *her father kneeling in the snow.* The booth. The slot for coins. *Passage is a privilege.*

"Captain," Hessler said. "The order?"

The wind dropped. In the sudden hush the Sable Current was audible — that long exhale of snow streaming through the corridor, the mountain breathing, the free road doing what it had done for centuries before anyone thought to put a price on it.

Odile Voss opened her mouth to give the order she had climbed twenty-six years to be able to give.

What came out instead surprised them both.

* * *

"No," she said. "Not yet. Not like this." She raised her voice, not to command but to carry, the way you do when you want every ear on the mountain to hear the same words at the same time. "The claim stands unchallenged in substance. I'll not dispute the seal — I verified it myself, and any dispute I filed would be a lie with paperwork on it. But I don't surrender the guild's interest on a mountainside because a riverboat captain recites a charter. There's a process. There's a council. There are forms." She almost smiled at the absurdity of it — invoking procedure at nine thousand feet, in the teeth of a storm — and then stopped, because she saw that Yara understood immediately why she was doing it. Procedure was the last weapon she had left. Procedure bought time. Time bought a way down that didn't end in mutiny or massacre.

But Yara Okafor, who had spent a lifetime dealing with the ferry guild's enforcers, knew procedure too — knew it as the shape the noose comes in — and answered before the echo of Voss's voice had died off the rock.

"Then we go down together," Yara said. "All of us. Before the storm closes the pass and turns your process into a tomb. You, me, your eleven, my people, and the compass — and we put the claim in front of the council while the ink is still warm and the witnesses are still standing."

Behind Voss, she heard Perrin exhale — a long, shaking breath that sounded like a boy putting down something heavy. She heard Okonkwo say, softly, to no one, "The people's stairs." She heard Greta whisper to her brother in the twin-language they thought no one knew, and caught only the last word, repeated twice, like a bell: *free, free.*

Her army was dissolving into a congregation, and the congregation was singing the other side's hymn.

Voss stood in the wind at the mouth of the Sable Current, twenty-six years of climbing ending at a door she could not open, and looked at the broken compass in Yara Okafor's hands — at the guild's own seal, staring up at her from inside the brass like an eye that had finally opened — and understood that the next chapter of her life would be written not on this mountain but in a council chamber far below, where the documents lived.

"All right," she said. "We descend together. But understand me, Captain — I'm not conceding. I'm adjourning."

Yara nodded once, river-calm. "So am I."

And then, from the direction of the cabin, came a sound that stopped them all — a rifle shot, fired straight up, the report cracking off the corridor walls and rolling away down the Current like thunder deciding where it belonged. A signal. A summons. From the doorway of the small black cabin, wrapped in wool, leaning on a stick, stood a gaunt figure Voss had last seen in a guild file marked *presumed dead, twenty years missing* — Adaeze Okafor, alive, upright, and watching them with eyes that had guarded this pass through twenty winters and intended, whatever the councils said, to keep guarding it through one more.

The storm was less than a day out. And now there were seventeen people on the mountain, and only one cabin, and the snow had already begun to fall.
