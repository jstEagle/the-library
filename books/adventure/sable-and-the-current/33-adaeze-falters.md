## 33. Adaeze Falters

The cabin smelled of pine resin and old smoke, of twenty winters pressed into the grain of its walls. Yara had been inside for less than an hour, and already they had memorized it: the iron stove with its dented flue, the shelf of tinned goods arranged by some private logic only Adaeze understood, the cot in the corner where their mother slept beneath a quilt patched so many times it had become a map of its own mending. A rifle hung above the door on two pegs. A brass lantern sat cold on the table. And everywhere — pinned to walls, folded into drawers, stacked in a crate by the window like cordwood — were journals.

Dozens of them. Leather-bound at first, from the early years, when she'd still believed someone might come looking. Then oilcloth-wrapped bundles, then cheap school notebooks bought from traders who climbed up to sell her flour and lamp oil. Twenty years of weather recorded in a hand that had started confident and slanted and ended cramped and careful, as though each word cost something now.

Yara stood among them while Teo catalogued what he could and Marisol fed the stove, and they tried to understand what they were seeing. Not a hermit's clutter. An archive. A woman alone at the top of the world, writing down everything — snowfall dates, wind directions, the comings and goings of guild scouts, the small daily heroics of keeping a pass open and unclaimed — so that someday, if anyone ever asked, there would be proof.

*She was waiting for us*, Yara thought. *All this time. She was building an argument we could inherit.*

But the thought curdled almost as soon as it formed, because Adaeze herself did not look like a woman who had won a twenty-year argument. She looked like a woman the argument had nearly finished.

---

It happened in the late afternoon, without ceremony.

Adaeze had insisted on walking Yara out to the eastern lip of the pass, to show them the cairn line — the row of stones she'd built every autumn, marking where the drifts would come, where the wind funneled, where a traveler caught in whiteout should turn left or die. It was the tour of a lifetime's work, delivered with the quiet pride of someone who knew she might never give it again. She walked slowly, one hand skimming the rock like a rail. Yara matched her pace and said nothing, because saying nothing was a kind of respect, and because their mother had spent two decades teaching herself not to need anyone's voice beside her.

They reached the third cairn. The Sable Current streamed overhead — that impossible river of snow moving through the sky, pouring through the col in a silver ribbon that never touched the ground — and Adaeze turned to point at it, the way she must have pointed at it ten thousand times before, explaining to no one.

"The wind comes off the western icefield," she said. "It hits the col here and has nowhere to go but through. That's why the pass stays clear even in deep winter, most years. The mountain sweeps its own road." Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just enough. "I used to think it was beautiful. Now I mostly think it's lonely."

Yara looked at her — really looked, for the first time since the cabin door had opened on a rifle barrel and a stranger's face — and saw what they had been refusing to see all day. The grayness under her skin. The way her breath came shallow even standing still, at an altitude where Yara themselves had been winded for two days and had stopped noticing. The tremor in the pointing hand that wasn't age. Age didn't do this. Age didn't hollow a woman out from the inside like a river undercutting a bank.

"Sit down," Yara said quietly.

"I'm fine."

"You're not."

Adaeze smiled then, and the smile was worse than any symptom, because it was the smile of someone who had known for a long time and had decided, deliberately, not to say. "No," she agreed. "I'm not. I haven't been for about three years. But you don't tell people things like that when you live alone at nine thousand feet, Yara. You'd have them climbing up here every month to watch you die, and I had work to do."

She took two more steps toward the fourth cairn. Then her knees simply stopped being hers.

Yara caught her before the stone did — caught her around the shoulders, went down with her, took the impact on their own knees against the scree. It was not graceful. It was the catch of a person who had spent their life pulling drowning things out of rivers and had never once pulled their own mother out of anything. Their hands shook. Adaeze's weight settled into them, lighter than it should have been, and that lightness frightened Yara more than the fall.

Marisol came running from the cabin with the sled they used for hauling supplies, and between the three of them they got Adaeze back inside, onto her cot, under her patchwork quilt. Teo put water on to heat. Marisol, who had seen men die on mountains and had once frozen completely at the sight of it, did not freeze now; she knelt and pressed her ear to Adaeze's chest and listened, and what she heard she described in plain terms: lungs working hard for too little return. Fluid, probably. Altitude makes everything crueler. Whatever it was, it had been coming for years, and it had arrived.

"She needs a doctor," Teo said. "There isn't one within four days' walk."

"There isn't one anywhere," Adaeze said, from under the quilt, in a voice that was trying very hard to be the old voice. "And I'm not going down the mountain to die in a town with toll gates. Don't be absurd."

Yara sat on the edge of the cot. The stove ticked. Outside, the Current poured through the col, indifferent and eternal, and the light was going gold and then gray, and none of it mattered at all compared to the small terrible fact of their mother's hand lying open on the quilt, waiting for theirs.

Yara took it.

"Tell me," they said. "All of it. How long. What it is. What you know."

Adaeze studied their face for a long moment — searching it, Yara realized later, for the child she'd left behind and finding instead the adult who had chosen their own name, and perhaps that search itself was a comfort, because she seemed satisfied by what she found.

"A growth," she said finally. "In my chest, I think. The trader's wife who comes up in summer had some nursing training before she married; she listened once and told me what I already suspected and told me there was nothing to be done about it at this altitude, which I also already suspected. It's slow. It's been slow for years. But this summer it stopped being slow." She tightened her grip on Yara's hand, briefly, like a knot being tied. "I meant to hold on until spring. Long enough to see you settled here. Long enough to know the pass was safe. I'm sorry I'm going to miss my own deadline."

"Don't apologize."

"Everyone apologizes at the end. It's the one honest thing left." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "So let me skip ahead to the part that matters."

* * *

What followed was the longest hour of Yara's life, and it happened entirely indoors, by lamplight, with snow beginning to sift past the window like ash.

Adaeze talked. She talked about the journals — where they were, how many, which ones mattered, which ones were just weather. She talked about the claim: the original grant, sealed with the guild's own mark generations ago, given to her grandmother in perpetuity, free passage guaranteed to all travelers forever. She explained — patiently, the way you explain arithmetic to someone you love — how the compass casing Teo had discovered after the fall held that seal, how the letters completed the chain, how paper was the only weapon that had ever worked against the guild and always would be, because the guild was built on paper and could only be undone by it.

Then she stopped talking about documents and started talking about Yara.

"I watched you today," she said. "On the col, when Voss's climbers came over the ridge and you stood in front of Teo like a wall. I watched you at the icefall. Marisol told me — she talks, when she thinks I'm asleep, she tells me everything you've done since Widow's Bend — and I want you to understand that I heard it all. The rope bridge. The storm camp. The ambush. You led people, Yara. People who weren't yours, who owed you nothing, who followed you anyway." Her breath caught, steadied. "Your father couldn't lead anyone. I couldn't either, not really — I could endure, but endurance isn't leadership. You got it from somewhere else. From the river, maybe. Or from yourself. It doesn't matter where. What matters is that when I look at you, I see the only person alive who can hold this place."

Yara's throat closed. They had crossed a mountain range, buried a boat, survived an ambush, broken a compass, and lost twenty years of their mother, and none of it had prepared them for being seen like this — named, itemized, claimed.

"What are you asking me?" they managed.

"I'm asking you to decide." Adaeze pushed herself up against the headboard, propped on pillows, and the effort cost her visibly; her breath went ragged and stayed ragged. "Because I'm not going to be here to decide it, and the choice can't wait for grief. Grief makes bad decisions. I've watched it. The pass needs a steward before winter closes, not after. Someone the climbers will follow. Someone Voss will have to negotiate with rather than fight. Someone who understands that freedom isn't a feeling — it's maintenance. Cairns. Journals. Showing up every autumn when nobody thanks you." She looked directly at Yara, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "I kept this road free for twenty years because I promised your grandmother I would. I am handing you the promise. Not the land — the promise. You can refuse it. Refusing is allowed. But you have to choose it or refuse it while I'm alive to hear your answer, because I will not spend my last weeks wondering."

The stove ticked. The snow thickened outside. Somewhere below, faintly, camp-smoke from Voss's party rose in the blue dusk — the enemy, one valley away, waiting for morning.

Yara did not answer immediately. They wanted to say yes with their whole heart and they wanted to say no with their whole heart and both hearts were true. This was the life they had fled toward without knowing it and feared without admitting it: rootedness, obligation, a post you hold until you die at it. Everything they had loved about the river was motion, impermanence, the freedom to leave. And here was their dying mother offering them the exact opposite, wrapped in the language of inheritance, and calling it freedom too — the freedom of a road that belongs to everyone, guarded by one.

Marisol, washing dishes at the basin, had gone silent. Teo sat cross-legged by the crate of journals with his pencil hovering over a fresh page, not drawing, just listening, as though he understood he was present at the signing of something.

"Can I ask you something first?" Yara said at last.

"Anything."

"When you chose to stay — twenty years ago, when you faked your death and climbed up here alone — did you ever regret it? Honestly. Not the polished answer."

Adaeze considered the question longer than she had considered any of the others. Snow ticked against the glass. Her hand, the one not holding Yara's, moved restlessly on the quilt, tracing the stitching of an old patch — a square of blue cloth that Yara suddenly recognized, with a jolt that went through them like lightning through a mast, as cloth from a shirt they had worn at seven years old. She had carried a piece of them up the mountain. All these years. Every night, under her palm, a scrap of her child.

"Yes," she said finally. "Every winter, once or twice, I regretted it. When the storms came and I couldn't remember why staying mattered more than going home. When I found your initials carved into the doorframe of our old house on one of my trips down — you'd cut them yourself, deep, with a knife, and I sat on the floor of that empty house and cried." She paused. "But regret isn't the same as wishing you'd chosen differently. I regretted the cost. I never regretted the road. Because I knew — I *knew* — that if the guild got this pass, they would wall it, gate it, ticket it, and no poor family would ever again walk from one side of these mountains to the other without paying. I grew up watching them do it to the ferry crossings. I wasn't going to watch them do it to the last free road." Her voice dropped. "So: yes, I regretted it, and I would do it again, and both of those are true, and anyone who tells you a real decision doesn't contain both is selling you something."

Yara absorbed that. It was the most honest sentence anyone had ever spoken to them, and it came from a dying woman on a cot under a quilt made partly of their own childhood shirt.

Then Adaeze said the thing that broke the question open:

"But here is what I learned, and it's yours now whether you take the pass or not. I thought guarding it would mean being alone. Twenty years taught me that a road you keep alone is a road you keep badly. I lasted because strangers helped me — the hermit, the trader's wife, Marisol's old crew leaving supply caches they never knew I found. The pass was never mine. It was mine the way a lighthouse is mine: you keep it, but the light belongs to everyone it guides." She shifted, wincing, and Yara felt the shift like a wound reopening. "You wouldn't be choosing solitude, Yara. Look around this room. You'd be choosing *them*. The girl who draws maps. The drunk who found her way back. Even Voss, eventually — especially Voss, because a steward who can't redeem their enemy isn't a steward, they're just a fighter with better scenery."

Yara laughed — actually laughed, a short, cracked sound that surprised everyone including themselves — and Adaeze laughed too, weakly, and for a moment the cabin held something that was almost ordinary: a family, joking, tired, alive.

Then she coughed, and the cough went on too long, and Marisol was suddenly there with a tin cup and a steadying arm, and the moment passed the way moments do at altitude — quickly, permanently.

When Adaeze could speak again, she said, quietly, "Tomorrow. Sleep on it tonight if you need to. But not longer than tomorrow, because the storm is coming — I can smell it in the wind, and I've smelled forty of them up here — and whatever you decide, you'll decide it better warm than frozen." She turned her face toward the window, toward the darkening col. "Voss arrives tomorrow too. I've watched her party's fires for two days. So you'll make your choice with the enemy at the door, which is, I find, the only condition under which humans ever make honest ones."

Yara lay awake most of the night on the floor beside the cot, close enough to touch their mother's hand, listening to her breathe. The breaths were shallow and uneven, with long pauses that Yara timed without meaning to, counting seconds the way they counted fathoms, the way they read water. Some pauses stretched to five. To seven. Each time, she came back. Each time, Yara's own heart stopped with hers and restarted when she did.

Toward dawn, half-dreaming, they heard her murmur something — a fragment, barely a word, addressed to no one visible. Something about a river. Something about a child. Yara couldn't hold onto it and didn't try. Some things are not meant to be captured, only witnessed.

At first light, Yara rose, splashed water on their face, and walked out onto the col alone while the others slept. The Current was running hard — a blizzard-white ribbon streaming east through the notch, loud even through the wind, a sound like a river in the sky. Yara stood in it, in the edge of it, snow streaming past them horizontally, and made the decision there, standing up, in the open, the way their mother would have wanted: not kneeling, not desperate, not bargaining. Choosing.

They walked back into the cabin while the kettle was still warming and said, in front of Teo and Marisol and their mother, plainly, without ceremony:

"I'll keep it. The pass stays free. I'll take the promise — the cairns, the journals, the autumns, all of it. But I'm going to keep it the way you just taught me: not alone. With them. And when Voss gets here, I'm not going to fight her. I'm going to make her part of the answer, whether she likes it or not."

Adaeze's eyes filled. She didn't speak for a moment — couldn't — and when she did, it was only six words, spoken like a key turning in a lock she had kept oiled for twenty years:

"Then it's yours. Keep it well."

Outside, the wind changed. It came around to the north, hard and sudden, carrying the dry iron smell of high snow, and the temperature fell through the morning like a stone through water. By noon the light had gone the color of pewter. Adaeze slept, woke, slept again, each waking shorter than the last. Marisol reinforced the cabin's shutters with split shingles. Teo worked by lamplight, finishing the fair copy of his map — the free route, inked and lettered, ready to be copied — because he had understood, before any of them said it aloud, that a map was the only form in which a promise could survive its maker.

And far below, in the blue shadow of the eastern valley, the fires of Voss's party climbed higher and multiplied. They were coming up. Everyone could see it. The storm was coming too, faster than they were.

Two forces, human and weather, converging on one small cabin at the top of the world — and inside it, a dying woman sleeping under a quilt stitched from her child's old shirt, and a new steward who had just, in six words and one walk through the sky-river, inherited everything.
