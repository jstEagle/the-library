## 35. What Yara Decides

The storm had spent itself by morning, and the Sable Current lay quiet under a sky scrubbed clean — that hard, rinsed blue that only comes after the mountain has finished shouting. Snow streamed through the pass in long silver ribbons, exactly as it always did, as if nothing had happened here at all: no ambush, no collapse, no twenty years of guarding, no night when thirty-one people had crowded into one cabin and let the wind howl itself hoarse outside.

Yara stood on the cabin's stone step with a tin cup of snowmelt tea going cold in their hands and watched Voss's climbers strike their camp below the col. They worked quickly, efficiently, the way people work when they've been paid to move fast their whole lives. Tents came down. Ropes were coiled. The guild's gray pennant, which had flown from an ice axe all through the storm like a flag of occupation, was folded and stowed without ceremony.

Odile Voss was not helping them.

She stood apart, at the edge of the pass where the ground fell away toward the eastern slope, her back to Yara, her hands buried in the pockets of a coat that had seen better decades. She had not slept — Yara could see it in the way she moved, careful and slightly too deliberate, a woman walking on a floor she didn't trust. All night she had sat in the cabin with everyone else while the storm tried to peel the roof off, and all night she had listened to what happened inside a shelter when nobody was charging anybody for it: Teo reading aloud from Adaeze's journals by lamplight because there was nothing else to do; Marisol telling the story of the rope bridge for the third time, softer each time, until even Voss's hired climbers were leaning in; Adaeze, propped against the wall with her breath coming thin and rattling, correcting Marisol's memory of a route name with the gentle authority of someone who had walked it two hundred times.

Nobody had said *you can't be in here*. Nobody had asked Voss for papers. The mountain had simply opened its one door, and everyone had walked through it, and they had all lived.

Yara had watched Voss understand this. It had taken most of the night. It had arrived in her face slowly, the way weather arrives over a ridge — first a tightening at the eyes, then something loosening around her mouth, then, near dawn, a stillness that looked almost like surrender but wasn't. Yara knew enough about pride to know the difference. Surrender was quiet. What Voss wore at dawn was more like a woman standing in the wreckage of an argument she'd been having with herself for thirty years.

Yara set down the cold tea and went down to meet her.

The snow crunched underfoot with that particular post-storm squeak, dry and dense, promising more cold to come. The climbers watched Yara approach their captain and made no move to stop it. That, too, told Yara something. Last week, at the col, those same men would have put hands on them without hesitation. But men who have been saved by the thing they were sent to seize look at it differently afterward. Two of them had paid tolls since they were children. One — the big one, Halvard, with the frostbitten ear — had said so out loud last night, in front of everyone, in front of Voss, his voice flat with exhaustion: *"My mother skimped on coal every winter so we could cross to market. Every winter of my life."* And Voss had not contradicted him. She had just looked at the fire.

"Captain Okafor," Voss said when Yara reached her. Her voice was level. Twenty years of giving orders does not wash out of a voice overnight.

"Just Yara," they said. "You know that."

"I know what you prefer." A muscle flicked at her jaw. "I find I'm not sure anymore what I'm entitled to call anyone."

"That's new," Yara said. "For you."

Voss turned to face them fully. Up close, without the storm behind her and the ambush ahead of her, she looked smaller than she had at the dock at Widow's Bend — or maybe Yara had grown. There was gray at her temples that the guild's gray uniform had hidden, and lines around her eyes carved by wind Yara recognized now, having stood in it herself. She had climbed this whole range on a debt of her own, Teo had learned, digging through guild records before he fled: her father had died carrying freight over a toll bridge, drowned when the ferry capsized under too much cargo, cargo loaded heavy because the tolls ate the margin and the margin had to come from somewhere. She had joined the guild at sixteen to change it. Somewhere in the changing, she had become it.

"You're going to make me an offer," Voss said. Not a question.

"I'm going to try," Yara said. "Whether you take it is yours."

"Then say it plainly. I'm too old and too tired for the dance."

So Yara said it plainly, standing in the streaming snow of the pass their mother had kept free for twenty years, with the cabin at their back and the compass casing — cracked open now, the guild seal exposed like a wound, the paper trail that proved everything — buttoned inside their coat against their ribs.

"The pass stays free," Yara said. "Not as charity. As law. The grant is in perpetuity, to my family, for the road's keeping — and the road's keeping means it stays open to anyone who can walk it. No toll. No ticket. No gatehouse. That part isn't negotiable, and it isn't mine to negotiate anyway. It's my mother's, and she held it longer than you've held your commission."

Voss's chin lifted a fraction. "And if the council simply refuses to recognize a scrap of brass and some letters?"

"Then every climber you brought up here testifies to what they saw," Yara said. "That the seal exists. That the grant names the Sable Current. That your own expedition leader read it aloud by lamplight and didn't dispute it." They paused. "Halvard already offered. So did the Ferro twins. You heard them."

"I heard them," Voss said, and something in her voice suggested she was still hearing them, would be hearing them for a long time.

"But here's the part that's actually an offer," Yara went on. "A free road still needs keeping. Cairns need rebuilding after avalanches. The rope bridges need replacing every few seasons. The cabin needs a roof, and stores, and someone to check on them before winter closes in. My mother did it alone for twenty years and it took everything she had — you can see what it took, she's lying in that cabin right now with lungs full of scar tissue from breathing this altitude half her life. I'm not going to do it alone. I don't want to do it alone. And I know the ferry guild has money, and masons, and rope, and a warehouse full of timber it currently uses to build tollbooths."

Voss's mouth twitched. It might have been anger. It might have been the first crack of something else.

"So the guild funds the upkeep," Yara said. "Openly. On the record. Not as penance, though frankly, Captain, penance wouldn't be undeserved — but as investment. Because a free road brings traffic, and traffic brings trade to the towns on both sides, and trade fills guild coffers better than tolls ever did, and you know it, because you've run the numbers yourself. You told me at the dock you'd read every ledger the guild keeps. You know the toll revenue has been falling for six years. People are going around. They're taking the long ways. The guild is bleeding coin trying to squeeze a stone that's already given up its blood."

"And the map," Voss said. Her eyes had gone sharp again, the negotiator surfacing through the exhausted woman like a fish rising. "Your apprentice has been drawing one. I saw him sketching at the storm camp. Don't think I didn't notice."

"He's been drawing it the whole way up," Yara said. "Every cairn, every ledge, every water source, every place the cornice hangs heavy and every place it doesn't. He's good. Better than good — he redraws from memory at night, checks it against the morning, corrects himself. He's the best cartographer I've ever met and he learned it inside your guild's own drafting rooms, which means the guild trained him and then gave him a reason to run." They let that sit. "Teo's map becomes the official chart of the Sable Current. Published under his name. Distributed free to every guide station and border post on both slopes. No guild watermark claiming ownership. His name, his hand, his credit."

"And what does the guild get for funding the upkeep of a road it can't charge for, and printing a map it doesn't own?"

"A role," Yara said. "Not control. A role. Your wardens walk the pass twice a season alongside whoever my family appoints — and right now that's me, and after me, whoever comes after me, and the grant says the road answers to the family, not the purse. Your people carry supplies. Your masons rebuild the cairns. Your name goes in the maintenance ledger next to ours, as partners, not owners. And when travelers ask why the pass is free, the answer will be that the ferry guild helps keep it that way." Yara shrugged. "You spent thirty years trying to change the guild from inside and it changed you instead. This is the door out. It just happens to be a door you have to hold open for other people."

Voss was silent for a long moment. The wind moved through the Current above them, that low river-sound the pass made even in calm, snow streaming eastward in the high air like smoke from a fire no one had lit. Yara's mother had named it well. It sounded like water. It sounded like home.

"You're asking me to stand in front of the council," Voss said finally, "and argue for dismantling the thing I built my life on."

"I'm asking you to finish what you started at sixteen," Yara said. "You just took the long way around."

Something crossed Voss's face then that Yara had never seen on it — not at the dock, not at the col, not during the ambush when her climbers had arrows nocked and Yara had stood between them and their mother's cabin. It wasn't softness, exactly. It was recognition. The look of a person seeing their own reflection in someone they had decided was an enemy, and finding the reflection older than expected, and tired, and still standing.

"My father's ferry went down because the manifest was overloaded," Voss said, quietly, to the mountains rather than to Yara. "Overloaded because the tolls on the bridge upstream meant the freight had to pay for itself twice. I was eleven. I decided the guild was a machine that ate people, and I would climb inside it and stop the gears." She laughed — a short, airless sound. "I stopped the gears. Then I became one. Do you know what the founders used to say? *The road is the guild's body; the toll is its blood.* I said it myself, at ceremonies. I said it last year."

"I know," Yara said. "Teo found the minutes."

"Of course he did." Voss exhaled slowly, a plume of breath torn sideways by the wind. "And your mother. Adaeze Okafor. She faked her death rather than let us torture a route out of her. Twenty winters alone up here." Her voice caught on something — not grief, Voss didn't seem built for grief, but for the thing adjacent to it, the accounting of cost. "I sent men to find her. Twice. In the early years. I told myself it was about the guild's interest. It was about proving she couldn't beat us." She looked at Yara directly. "She beat us. She beat us by never fighting at all. She just kept the door open."

"She's dying," Yara said. Plainly, because Voss had asked for plain. "Maybe days. Maybe less. Whatever happens between us in the next hour, she won't see the council's decision. She wanted me to decide the pass's future while she could still hear it. That's why I'm standing here talking to you instead of sitting with her."

Voss closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, the negotiator and the eleven-year-old were looking out together, and for once they seemed to agree.

"Terms," she said. "If I bring this to the council, I'll need terms precise enough to survive lawyers. The grant document stays in your family's possession — the original. The guild receives certified copies. Maintenance funding set annually, reviewed publicly, never contingent on access restrictions — I want that written so explicitly that no future council can use the purse as a lever. The warden rotations joint, as you said. And the map—" she glanced back toward the cabin, where Teo was visible through the frost-furred window, hunched over his drafting board with his mittens off and his fingers red, working even now, "—the map is published within the year, or the funding clause voids. I know how councils stall. We'll starve the stall."

"You'd bind your own guild to deadlines," Yara said.

"I'd bind *them*," Voss said, "to what I should have made them do thirty years ago." She straightened, and something of the captain came back into her spine — but different now, Yara thought. Less like armor. More like posture. "One condition of my own. Not from the guild. From me."

"Name it."

"When we go down," Voss said, "when the claim is presented and the tolls fall and the towns hear about it — there will be a story told about how this happened. There always is. Stories get simplified. Heroes get invented." She looked at the cabin, at the thin woodsmoke rising from its stove pipe into the enormous clean sky. "Make sure the story is hers. Adaeze Okafor kept this pass free for twenty years alone, and no one knew, and no one thanked her, and she did it anyway. I'll tell that story myself, in front of the council, in front of whoever needs to hear it. But it should come from you too. From the family. Let her be the legend. Let me be the footnote."

Yara thought about that for a moment. About legends, and footnotes, and mothers.

"She wouldn't want to be a legend," Yara said. "She'd want the road open and her name left off the signposts. But she's earned the truth being told, and the truth is what you just said. So yes. Agreed."

They shook on it — actually shook, hand to hand, bare fingers stinging in the cold, two people who had pointed weapons at each other across a col nine days ago now sealing a bargain with nothing behind it but their word. Yara had learned on the river that a handshake in bad faith was worth less than the paper it wasn't written on, and that a handshake in good faith was worth more than any contract. They looked into Voss's eyes and judged it good faith. It was the strangest judgment call of their life, and it felt steadier than most.

* * *

Inside the cabin, the light through the little window had gone gold, and Adaeze was awake.

She was propped up on the cot Marisol had built for her out of pack frames and the good sleeping roll, and she was thinner than the mountain, and her hands lay folded on the blanket like two birds that had finally landed. But her eyes were open, and they found Yara the moment Yara came through the door, and they were clear.

"Well?" she said. Her voice was a thread, but it was her voice — the voice from before, from the kitchen in the house on the Verre, from the stories she'd told about a pass where snow ran like a river. "Did the captain of the guild accept terms from the captain of nothing?"

"From the keeper of the pass," Yara said, kneeling beside the cot. "It's done, Mama. Free road. Guild funds the upkeep. Teo's chart becomes the official map. Voss argues it to the council herself."

Adaeze was quiet a moment. Then she began, faintly, to laugh — a sound like a creek starting up in spring, unsteady at first, then running. "Twenty years," she said, "I imagined every way it might end. Fire. Arrest. Falling. Never once—" she pressed a hand to her chest, "—never once did I imagine I'd win by *negotiating*. Your father's temper, child. You got his temper and my stubbornness and somehow neither one killed you. Look at that."

"It's not done-done," Yara said. "The council still has to ratify. Voss thinks they will — the numbers are on our side, and half her climbers will testify, and she's got the stall-proof clauses written in. But there'll be fights. There's always fights."

"There are always fights," Adaeze agreed. Her eyes drifted to the window, to the streaming snow beyond it, the Current doing its endless silver work. "But the road doesn't care about fights. That's the secret I came up here to learn. The road just waits. It waits for people to stop being stupid." She looked back at Yara, and her hand found theirs, and squeezed with a strength that surprised them both. "You stopped being stupid faster than I did. Took me twenty winters."

Outside, voices — Teo calling something to Marisol about the cairn line, and Marisol laughing, actually laughing, the sound rough and rusty as an old hinge but real. Voss's climbers finishing their packing in the quiet, efficient way of people whose work has changed meaning overnight. The stove ticking. The wind above the pass singing its one long note.

Adaeze's breathing hitched, steadied, hitched again. Yara stayed kneeling, staying in the rhythm of it, learning it the way they'd once learned the moods of sandbars — not to fight it, just to read it.

"Mama," they said. "Tell me what you want for tomorrow. Anything. If the weather holds, anything."

Adaeze smiled, and closed her eyes, and for a moment Yara thought she'd fallen asleep — but her thumb kept moving, slow, across the back of Yara's hand, the way it had when they were small.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I want to watch the sun come up on the Current. Just once more. And I want you to promise me something while I watch it."

"Anything," Yara said again, and this time they meant it all the way down.

"Promise me you'll go back to the river," Adaeze whispered. "Not because the mountain doesn't need you. Because the river raised you, and a keeper should keep what they love, not what they inherit." Her eyes opened, bright and fierce and fading all at once. "The pass will keep itself for a while. It's had practice. But the river's been waiting twenty years for its captain to come home — and this time, child, come home to something you chose."

The sun slid behind the western ridge, and the light in the cabin went blue, and somewhere above them the Sable Current streamed on, patient as water, certain as morning — and Yara held their mother's hand and promised.
