## 36. Adaeze's Last Morning

The storm broke sometime in the small hours, and Yara woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath.

They had slept in the cabin's main room, on the narrow cot Adaeze had kept made up for twenty years — for no one, she'd said once, and then, later, softer: for whoever came. The wool blankets smelled of cedar smoke and old lavender, and beneath that, faintly, of the mountain itself, that cold mineral scent that got into everything above the snowline. Yara lay still for a long while, listening to the cabin settle around them, the timbers ticking as they released the day's heat into the night air.

Adaeze was in the back room. She had been failing faster since the storm, since the exertion of standing at her window to watch Voss's climbers come up the corridor, since the long negotiation that had ended not in conquest but in something Yara still didn't have words for — an agreement, a truce, a beginning. Whatever it was, it had cost her mother something she didn't have to spare. She had slept most of yesterday, waking in short intervals, her breathing shallow and rhythmic as tide.

Yara rose quietly. Teo was curled in the chair by the cold stove, asleep sitting up, his map case hugged to his chest like a child holds a stuffed animal. Marisol had taken the floor near the door, blanket pulled to her chin, her gray-streaked hair loose across the boards. They had all earned their sleep twice over. Yara let them keep it.

They crossed the main room without lighting a lamp. They knew this cabin now, three days familiar — knew where the floor creaked by the pantry, knew which of the two doors stuck in cold weather, knew the way the window over the wash basin framed the Sable Current itself, that impossible corridor where the wind funneled snow eastward in an unbroken silver stream, hour after hour, year after year, indifferent to everything human that happened beneath it.

The back room door opened without sound. Adaeze had oiled its hinges herself, Yara would learn later from her journals; she had wanted to be able to slip out to watch the pass without waking anyone, though there had been no one to wake for twenty years. Old habits, kept alive past any reason. Like the garden she still weeded every summer though nothing but the hardiest alpine herbs could survive up here. Like the second cup she set out each morning out of some stubborn ritual of hope.

Yara sat down on the stool beside the bed.

Her mother was awake. Not restlessly awake — not the anxious surfacing of the past few days, when she'd blink open her eyes and seem briefly surprised to find herself still here. This was different. This was a quiet, deliberate wakefulness, the kind Yara recognized from their own worst nights on the river, when sleep wouldn't come and they'd stopped fighting it and simply watched the dark instead, letting it be what it was.

"You're up," Adaeze said. Her voice was thin but steady. "I heard you cross the room. You walk like me. I used to think you walked like your father, but he clomped. You and I, we go soft-footed. River people."

"Mountain people too, apparently."

"We'll see." A small smile moved across her face and faded, spending energy she measured now in spoonfuls. "Come closer. My eyes aren't what they were, and I want to look at you while there's light enough."

There wasn't light yet. The window showed only the deep blue-black of high altitude before dawn, the stars still sharp and crowded overhead, the snow outside glowing faintly with borrowed starlight. But Yara leaned close anyway, close enough that their breath mingled, and Adaeze raised one hand — trembling, spotted, the nails broken and dirty from twenty years of rope work and stove wood and shovel blades — and laid it against Yara's jaw.

"There," she whispered. "There you are."

* * *

They talked in the dark for a long time, or rather Adaeze talked and Yara listened, because talking cost her and listening cost nothing, and Yara understood instinctively that these hours were not for conversation. They were for handing things over.

She told stories Yara half-knew and half-had invented for themselves over twenty years of absence. The market in Onitsha where she'd bought cloth with her first wages. The way Yara's father used to sing off-key on purpose just to make baby-Yara laugh, and how Yara had laughed anyway, every time, because babies don't know bad singing from good, they only know love when they hear it. The name she'd almost given them — Chidera, she'd meant to call them, God-writes — before deciding a child should choose their own name the way a river chooses its course, and how she'd told this to no one, kept it folded away like the letters in the chest, waiting.

"I left the compass where I knew the tide would give it back," she said. "That sounds mad, doesn't it. Sealing a chest in a cave below the waterline. But I knew the tides of that river better than I know anything else on this earth, and I knew that someday the water would pull back far enough, and I knew who would be too curious to leave it alone." Her laugh was a thread. "Curiosity is the Okafor inheritance. I couldn't leave you money. I couldn't leave you land. So I left you the thing that makes you go looking."

"And it nearly got us killed," Yara said. "Twice. Maybe three times, depending how you count the icefall."

"It did what it was meant to do, then." She turned her head on the pillow, and her eyes found Yara's in the dark with startling precision. "Nothing worth inheriting comes safe."

Outside, the sky began its slow turn. The stars over the eastern ridge thinned first, washed out by a grayness that wasn't quite light, and the Sable Current — invisible in full darkness — reappeared as a pale ribbon of moving snow, catching the first suggestion of dawn before the peaks did. Yara watched it through the window and thought, not for the first time, that it looked exactly like water. That their whole life had been spent learning to read one current, and their mother had spent hers learning another, and they were the same lesson in different languages: pay attention, respect the flow, never assume it will carry you gently.

"Tell me about the last twenty years," Yara said. "Not the guarding. I know the guarding. Tell me the ordinary parts. What did you eat? What did you do with the winters?"

Adaeze's face went distant, pleased, remembering. "I read. I had forty-one books by the end; travelers left them, mostly, the ones who came through before the guild closed the low routes. I copied out the ones I loved best, in case the originals burned. I made tea from the pine needles and pretended it was the jollof-spiced kind from home, which it was not, and drank it anyway. In winter I talked to myself. Out loud. Full conversations." She paused. "I gave both sides good arguments. It kept my mind from going strange. Mostly."

"What did you talk about?"

"The pass. Whether I'd done the right thing, staying. Whether leaving you had been cowardice dressed up as sacrifice." Her voice dropped. "I never settled it. Some mornings I was sure. Some mornings I was sure of the opposite. You live long enough alone, you learn that certainty is just a mood that lasts longer than most."

"And today?" Yara asked. "Which morning is it?"

Adaeze was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Today I'm sure I'd do it again. Because you're here. Because you came up a mountain to find a dead woman and found a living one instead, and you didn't flinch, and you took the pass and made it free, and you did it with strangers who became yours." Her breath caught, steadied. "Whatever I got wrong — and I got plenty wrong — that isn't wrong. That's the whole point of everything, done right, by someone I love. So."

So. One word, carrying twenty years.

* * *

Dawn came slowly, the way it does at altitude, the light arriving sideways and then all at once. By the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge, the whole corridor of the Sable Current lit up like a vein of molten glass, and the snow-stream threw rainbows across the cabin's south wall, tiny prismatic shivers that crawled over the timber as the sun climbed.

Teo woke first. He came to the doorway of the back room, saw Yara sitting with their mother, and stopped — not intruding, not retreating, just standing there in the slanted gold light with his map case under his arm, waiting to be invited or dismissed. Yara lifted two fingers from the bedframe: come. He crossed the room softly and sat on the floor by the window, cross-legged, and Adaeze looked down at him and smiled.

"The cartographer," she said. "You're drawing my pass."

"Your pass is everyone's pass now," Teo said. "That's the whole idea. I'm drawing it so nobody can charge for it again. A map is a kind of promise. You publish it, and it belongs to the world."

Adaeze was quiet a moment, and then she said, "Twenty years ago I thought I was protecting this place by hiding it. Hiding the route, hiding myself, hiding the truth of what the guild wanted. And maybe that was right for a while. But a secret is only safe until someone stronger finds it. A published map — that can't be unmade. You've done the sturdier thing." She reached toward him, an effort that visibly cost her, and touched the top of his head. "Thank you, boy. Whoever unmade you from Voss's employ, thank them too."

"That was mostly me," Teo said. "I ran."

"Running toward is braver than running away," Adaeze said. "Even when they look identical from behind."

Marisol came next, drawn by the smell of the fire Yara had coaxed back to life in the small stove. She stood in the doorway longer than Teo had — Marisol always stood in doorways, Yara had noticed, as if rooms had to earn her — and when she finally entered, she brought two things: a tin cup of the pine-needle tea, brewed strong the way Adaeze apparently liked it, and an apology twenty years overdue.

"I led that expedition," Marisol said, without preamble, without excuse. "The one you vanished from. I led them into the whiteout on the western col because I was too proud to turn back, and when you ordered the retreat — when you ordered *me* to take the survivors down — I obeyed, and I hated you for it, and I hated myself more, and I spent two decades drinking over it." She set the cup on the crate beside the bed. "I came up this mountain for absolution. I don't expect it. But I wanted to say it to your face while I could."

Adaeze studied her for a long moment. Then she said, "Sit down, Marisol. You look terrible."

Marisol laughed — a cracked, startled laugh, the first real one Yara had heard from her — and sat.

"You obeyed a retreat order in a storm," Adaeze continued. "Do you know how rare that is? Pride kills more climbers than ice does. Ice just finishes what pride starts. You swallowed the worst thing a guide can swallow — being led, on your own mountain — and you brought people home." She picked up the tea, hands shaking, and managed two swallows before setting it down. "I ordered that retreat to save lives. You executed it to save lives. There is no debt between us. There's only weather, and what we each did inside it."

Marisol sat very still. Yara watched something leave her face — some tension held so long it had become structure, load-bearing, and its removal made her look suddenly younger and suddenly older at once. She didn't speak. She didn't need to. She simply stayed, and stayed present, and that was its own sentence.

* * *

Morning strengthened. The light in the back room went from gold to plain white, honest light, the kind that shows everything. Yara opened the shutters fully so Adaeze could see the pass, and propped her up on the pillows she'd sewn herself from flour sacking, and for an hour or more the four of them existed in the small room together without agenda — Teo sketching in the corner, Marisol splitting kindling out front and bringing it in armload by armload, Yara beside the bed, Adaeze watching her current run.

At some point she asked for the compass.

Yara fetched it from the chest at the foot of the bed — the brass casing cracked open since the icefall, the guild seal exposed, the needle still turning faithfully toward whatever it pointed at now. Yara had checked it that morning out of habit. It pointed nowhere. It swung slow and idle, undecided, like a weathervane in dead air. Yara hadn't wanted to think about what that meant. Now, holding it, they understood: it had never pointed north. It pointed at her. And she was here, and found, and finished, and there was nothing left in the world for it to seek.

Adaeze held it in both palms like a bird she was afraid to startle.

"My mother gave me this," she said. "Her mother gave it to her. It's guild work, old guild work, from back when the guild built things instead of charging for them — back when a seal meant *this is true*, not *this is ours*. When the founders granted our family the keeping of the pass, they sealed the grant into the instrument itself. Paper burns. Brass remembers." She closed her fingers over it. "I hid it in the cave because I thought it might be found by anyone — a smuggler, a guild man, a stranger — and I couldn't risk it leading the wrong person to me. So I made it lead to no one, for twenty years. Locked under the tide." She looked up at Yara. "And then it led to exactly the right person. The tide knew. Rivers always know."

"It's pointing at nothing now," Yara said.

"No," Adaeze said. "It's pointing at everything. Nothing left to find means everything found." She pressed it back into Yara's hands and folded their fingers over it, hers over theirs, a blessing done in brass and bone. "Keep it. Repair the casing if you like, or don't. Let it be cracked. Cracked things tell truer stories."

* * *

Afternoon came, and Adaeze slept, really slept, for the first time in days — deep and even, her face relaxed in a way it hadn't been since Yara had known her, which was only eleven days total, spread across a lifetime. Yara sat with her through it, reading aloud from her own journals when they grew restless, passages she'd written in the early years: the first winter, the terror of it, the nights she'd nearly walked down and surrendered herself to the guild just to stop being alone. And then the later entries, the ones where the fear thinned and something else grew in its place — a fierce, idiosyncratic, hard-won peace, the peace of a woman who had chosen her post the way soldiers choose countries, not because it was easy but because it was hers.

*"Day 4,112,"* Yara read. *"The Current ran pink at sunset. I have decided this counts as company."*

Adaeze woke laughing, a real laugh, brief and bright. "Four thousand days of sunsets," she said. "I counted them like coins. Richer than the guild ever made me feel."

Toward evening she ate a little — broth, half a biscuit softened in it, three spoons of the preserved berries Marisol had carried up from the lowlands in her pack like treasure. She asked about the river. She asked Yara to describe the Verre in every season, and Yara did, the spring flood and the summer shallows and the autumn fog and the winter ice that locked the boats in their berths like animals in stalls, and Adaeze listened with her eyes closed, seeing it, building it back around herself after twenty years of stone and snow.

"Take me down there someday," she murmured. "When I'm strong."

"When you're strong," Yara agreed, and both of them knew it was a fiction, and both of them let it stand, because some fictions are kindnesses and this one was.

* * *

Night fell fast, as it does at the pass. The temperature dropped through the walls' cedar planks like water through a sieve. Yara banked the stove, added blankets, did all the small logistics of keeping a dying woman warm, and Adaeze accepted each ministration with the mild impatience of someone who had kept herself alive alone for seven thousand days and knew exactly how much care she required — less than Yara was giving, more than she'd admit.

Around midnight she asked them to open the shutters wide.

The night was violently clear. Cold air poured in and Yara let it, dragging the bed's frame closer to the window so Adaeze could see. Above the pass the sky was a black so dense it looked solid, stitched with stars, the Milky Way running pole to pole like a second river — and below it, the Sable Current streamed silver under the starlight, visible even at night, the only river on earth that flowed of snow and wind and never froze.

"There's mine," Adaeze said softly. "And there's yours." She found Yara's hand in the dark. "Two currents. Same sky. Do you see it, child? I never left the water. I just went upstream so far it turned to light."

Yara saw it. Would always see it now, every clear night for the rest of their life: the galaxy overhead, the snow-river below, and somewhere between them a woman in a cedar cabin who had traded everything to keep a road free, and called the price fair.

She slept after that. Yara sat up beside her, holding her hand through the blanket, and did not sleep, and did not try to. Some vigils are not endurance. They are attendance. You simply stay, and stay awake, so that the leaving happens in company.

* * *

It was Teo who noticed, a little after four, that her breathing had changed.

He'd woken and come to check the stove and drifted to the doorway of the back room, and he stood there a moment and then came and touched Yara's shoulder, once, gently, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the room didn't already know. The rhythm had lengthened. The spaces between her breaths had grown wide and quiet, like the pause between waves when the tide turns.

Yara sent Teo to wake Marisol. Both of them came and stood at the foot of the bed, and Adaeze — who had, in her long solitude, developed an almost animal awareness of presence in the room — opened her eyes.

All three of them were there. Her daughter's child, holding her hand. The cartographer who had mapped her pass into permanence. The guide whose retreat order she'd given, whose guilt she'd lifted, who had climbed back up her mountain to finish what shame had interrupted. Strangers eleven days ago. Family now, by choice and by ordeal, the only kind of family that had ever held.

"Everyone's here," Yara said. "You're not alone. You were never alone again after the day we found you. I need you to know that part. Whatever else happened — you were not alone."

"I know," she whispered. "I counted. Eleven days. Best eleven days of my life, and I'm including the ones when you were born." Her mouth curved. "Don't argue with a dying woman. We get the last word. It's in the rules."

"They're not your rules."

"All rules are made up," she said. "Might as well make the good ones."

The spaces between her breaths widened. The pre-dawn blue crept into the window, and with it the Sable Current emerged again, faint, faithful, running. Yara watched it and watched her, the two rhythms side by side, one eternal and one ending, and understood that they had always been the same motion — a current is just a river's way of continuing, and a death, done right, attended, witnessed, loved, is just a life's way of handing off.

Near the end she surfaced one more time, lucid, urgent, and used her last clear words on instructions, because she was her mother's daughter and her daughter's mother and paperwork ran in the blood:

"Bury me facing the Current. Read the grant aloud over the grave — the whole claim, every clause, so the mountain hears it and the seal is witnessed. Then go down before the snows. All of you. Take the journals. Don't look back at the pass — it doesn't need looking after anymore. It has a map now." A breath, long, quiet. "And Yara. The boat. Name it well."

"I already know the name," Yara said.

"I know you do." Her eyes went to the window, to the pale ribbon of moving snow brightening as the east grayed. "Look. It's running."

Then the spaces between her breaths grew longer than the breaths themselves, and then, as the sun's first edge cleared the ridge and struck the corridor and turned it to a blade of burning silver — at the exact minute the light reached the cabin wall, the minute she had watched every morning for twenty years — the breaths stopped altogether, gently, between one space and the next, like a boat coming alongside a dock and making fast.

Yara held her hand a long time afterward. It cooled slowly, honestly, the way stone cools after sundown. Outside, the Current ran on. It had run through the whole of it — the vigil, the silence, the turning of the light — indifferent and gorgeous and somehow, impossibly, consoling, the way a river consoles you simply by continuing.

Teo sat on the floor with his head bowed over his knees. Marisol stood at the window, facing the pass, saying nothing, her hand flat against the glass as if steadying herself against the view or leaning into it — Yara couldn't tell, and suspected Marisol couldn't either.

Grief, when it finally arrived, arrived clean. No ambush in it, no unfinished argument, no letter unsent or word unsaid. Everything had been said. Everything had been handed over. What remained was only the fact of her, gone, and the fact of her, having been, and those two facts sat together in Yara's chest without fighting, heavy and simple, like ballast.

Yara closed her mother's eyes. Folded the blanket back. Opened the chest at the foot of the bed and took out the grant, the sealed brass, the forty-one books, the journals wrapped in oilcloth — the whole inheritance, weighed and found sufficient.

Then they stepped out into the morning, into the wind, and looked down the corridor of the Sable Current, streaming eastward into the new day, and began to plan a burial.

* * *

By midmorning the three of them had carried stones.

It was Marisol who chose the site — a shelf of granite above the cabin, facing due east into the Current, sheltered from the worst of the wind by a fin of rock that Adaeze herself had surely noticed in twenty years of walking this shelf. They built the cairn in the old way, Marisol directing, each stone fitted and turned until it locked, no mortar but gravity and patience. Teo cut the marker with his cartographer's chisel, working slowly in the bitter cold, his breath freezing on the stone: ADAEZE OKAFOR — KEEPER OF THE PASS — SHE KEPT IT FREE.

Yara read the grant aloud, every clause, as instructed, voice cracking against the wind and pushing through it anyway, while the others stood witness and the needleless compass lay on the finished cairn, its cracked casing open to the sky, its guild seal facing upward like a signature.

And when it was done — when the last stone was set and the wind took the sound of their voices and scattered it down the corridor toward the world — Yara stood before the cairn and felt the strangest thing, which was not emptiness.

It was direction.

The journals were packed. Teo's map was finished to the last contour. The descent waited, and below the mountains a river waited, and below the river a berth waited, empty, for a boat that did not exist yet.

Yara turned to face downhill, into the long fall of the world, and said, "Let's go home. We have a boat to name."
