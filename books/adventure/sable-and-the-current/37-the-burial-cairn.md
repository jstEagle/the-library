## 37. The Burial Cairn

The storm passed in the night, and the mountain woke clean.

Marisol stood at the edge of the pass with her hands buried in her pockets and watched the light come up over the eastern peaks, the way it always did up here—not gradually, not the way it seeped into the river valleys she'd known most of her life, but all at once, like a door flung open. The Sable Current ran beside her, that impossible corridor of wind where the snow streamed eastward in a long silver ribbon, hissing faintly, endlessly, like water over stone. Twenty years ago she had stood on a ridge not three miles from this spot and listened to that same sound and thought it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard. Then she had led seven people into the white and brought back three.

Now she would help bury the woman who had ordered her home.

* * *

They dug the grave where Adaeze had asked, in the lee of the cabin, where the wind dropped and the snow lay deep and undisturbed, a white field that in summer, Adaeze had told Yara two nights before she died, bloomed with a kind of small blue flower that no one had ever bothered to name. Marisol had watched that conversation from the doorway, the dying woman propped on her cot and her child kneeling beside her, and had thought: *she chose her ground twenty years ago. She has been waiting for this the way a farmer waits for rain.*

Digging was slow work at altitude. The ground beneath the snow was scree and frozen grit, and every shovel stroke rang against stone. Yara dug without stopping, without speaking, their shoulders working under a borrowed coat that had hung in the cabin's cedar chest for two decades and still smelled faintly of camphor and of someone else's life. Teo dug beside them, clumsy but tireless, a boy who had never in his life held a shovel before this month and who had learned, in the space of a few weeks, to climb ice, to cross rope bridges, to bury the dead.

Marisol dug too. Her hands blistered and bled inside her gloves, and she was grateful for the pain. It gave her something to do with her grief besides carry it.

Voss stood apart. Marisol had expected her to leave—had half hoped for it, if she was honest, because the sight of the guild captain still raised the hair on the back of her neck—but Voss had stayed, and had worked the hardest of any of them through the night, breaking the frozen ground with an ice axe, methodical and silent, as though the labor were a debt she was paying down in installments. Her climbers had gone down the mountain that morning, all but two, who waited near the cabin with their hats in their hands like men at a church door.

By midmorning the grave was done: a long rectangle of dark turned earth in the white field, shallow, because the mountain does not give up much of itself, lined with stones because the mountain gives up that much.

They brought her out wrapped in the sailcloth from Yara's old boat.

Marisol had not known that detail until Teo said it, quietly, while they carried her. Yara had sold the boat to pay the debt, but the sail had come back to them—Voss had bought the vessel at auction, and had ordered the sail cut down and sent up with her climbers, folded into a neat square, without a word of explanation. Marisol had watched Yara take it, had watched them stand there holding it for a long moment, jaw tight, eyes bright, and had understood that the war between them and Odile Voss had ended somewhere on the storm-swept summit, in the cabin, in the long night when the mountain sheltered guild and free-climber alike and no one died. What remained was not forgiveness. But it was something. It was a sail.

Adaeze Okafor weighed almost nothing. Illness had spent her the way a river spends a bank, slowly and then all at once. Marisol carried her feet; Yara carried her shoulders; Teo walked ahead with the compass, which he had repaired as best he could with wire and resin, and which pointed, now, steadily, at the grave.

They lowered her into the ground, and the sailcloth went in with her, and Marisol thought: *she came up this mountain on a riverboat woman's stubbornness and she leaves it wrapped in canvas, and there is a rightness to that that no priest could improve on.*

* * *

Yara spoke first.

They stood at the head of the grave with the wind pulling at their collar, and they did not cry, or rather they cried the way stone weeps, so slowly and so deep that you only saw it afterward, in the wet tracks down the face of things. They told the story of the cave at Widow's Bend, of the low tide that should not have happened, of a chest with two letters inside it and a compass that lied about north. They told it plainly, without ornament, the way you tell a thing you have turned over so many times it has worn smooth.

"She spent twenty years keeping this pass free," Yara said. "She spent twenty years letting me believe she was dead, so that no one would ever use me to find her. I spent twenty years being angry at a ghost." Their voice caught, and held. "I'm not angry anymore. I don't know what I am. But I know that everything I am, I got from her. The stubbornness. The river. The name." They looked up, at the pass, at the streaming snow. "Rest here, Mama. You kept the door open. I'll keep it open too."

Then it was Teo's turn, and the boy unfolded a paper with shaking hands—his map, the one he had been drafting the whole way up, the free route drawn line by line in his careful cartographer's hand—and read the names of the places he had charted, and said that he had named the highest col after her, *Adaeze's Col*, because maps were how he loved things, and because a name on a map outlives every toll booth and every guild seal and every grave.

Then Voss stepped forward, and the whole mountainside seemed to hold its breath.

Marisol watched the guild captain take off her hat. She watched her stand there for a long moment, a woman in her fifties with frost-burned cheeks and a lifetime of toll ledgers behind her eyes, and when she spoke, her voice was low and rough, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years.

"I never met you," Voss said to the grave. "I spent my whole life paying your family for the right to walk on mountains, and I hated it, and I hated you, and I built a career on making sure the paying never stopped. Because I thought if the guild controlled the passes, at least the tolls would be fair. At least the money would feed someone." She paused. "I was wrong about a great many things. Your child taught me that at the top of your mountain, in a storm, when they opened the door for the people who'd hunted them." She put her hat back on. "The tolls are coming down. That's not a gift. It's a repayment. Twenty years of interest."

She turned and walked back to her climbers, and did not look at anyone, and Marisol found, to her own surprise, that her throat was tight. She had hated Odile Voss for a long time, in the abstract, the way you hate weather. It was harder to hate a woman who buried her enemies' mother and meant it.

Then everyone looked at Marisol.

She had known it was coming. She had been dreading it since the digging started, since before that, since the cabin, since the night Adaeze, propped on her pillows with her breath rattling like wind in a rigging, had gripped Marisol's wrist with surprising strength and said, *You were there. When it happened. You say the names. The dead need their names said by someone who knew them, or they turn into stories, and stories get sold.*

So Marisol stepped forward, and took off her glove, and put her bare hand flat on the frozen earth of the grave, and the cold went through her palm and up her arm like a river in flood, and she began to speak.

"I led an expedition up this mountain twenty-two years ago," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Eight of us. We were going to chart the Sable Current for the guild. I took the guild's coin because I was young and proud and I told myself the map would be for everyone. It was not for everyone. Nothing the guild has ever touched was for everyone."

The wind moved through the pass. The snow-stream hissed. She kept her hand on the ground.

"Four of us turned back at the icefall. That was my order, and it was the right order, and I have spent twenty-two years being unable to feel that it was right, because the ones who kept going—" Her voice broke, and she let it. "The ones who kept going died. And the woman who sent them up there, the guild handler who promised us the route was safe, her name was Odile Voss's mentor, and I never said that out loud before either, and it doesn't matter now, except that it does, it matters that I say it."

She looked up, at Yara, at Teo, at Voss, who had gone very still.

"Here are the names." She said them one at a time, slowly, into the wind, the way you pour out the last of something precious. "Domenic Hale, who sang off-key and carried double loads without being asked. Sefa Ili, who was seventeen and lied about his age to get on the team, and I knew, and I let him come, and that is mine to carry until they put me in the ground. Petra Voss—" she saw the flinch, and went on, "—Petra Voss, Odile's sister, who wanted to be a guide and never got the chance, and whose death her sister has been trying to make mean something ever since, and who I should have turned back at the col when she asked me to, because she asked me, and I said the summit was close. And Ruth Amari, my second, who held the rope when the cornice went, and held it, and held it, until the mountain took all three of them at once."

The names hung in the thin air. Marisol realized her cheeks were wet and did not care.

"Adaeze Okafor was not on that expedition," she said. "Adaeze Okafor was the woman who ordered us to retreat before we ever saw the icefall. She sent a warning up through the hermit at the ledges—turn back, the season is wrong, the pass is closing. I read her letter and I made a calculation about time and money and the guild's expectations, and I kept climbing." Her hand pressed harder against the frozen ground. "Three of us came home. If I had listened to her, eight of us would have. And when the guild needed someone to blame, they blamed her, and she let them, because the truth would have led them here, to this pass, and she had sworn to keep it free. She took my sin on her own back and carried it for twenty years, and I drank in a border saloon and called it shame, and shame was the easy part."

She knelt then, both knees in the snow, and bowed her head over the grave.

"So I say the names, Adaeze. Yours and theirs. Domenic Hale. Sefa Ili. Petra Voss. Ruth Amari. And yours, Adaeze Okafor, who kept the door." She lifted her head. "I carried them up here so they could be set down. That's all mourning is, I think. I learned it too late, but I learned it. You carry them until you find the high ground, and then you set them down, and you walk back into the valley lighter, and you spend the lightness on the living."

She stood. Her knees cracked. Yara crossed the snow and took her arm, and did not say anything, and did not need to, and Marisol leaned on that strong young shoulder and let herself be held up for a moment, the way she had not let anyone hold her up in twenty-two years.

* * *

They built the cairn together.

It was Teo's idea, or maybe it had been Adaeze's all along—there was a half-finished ring of stones behind the cabin, stacked with a dying woman's careful hands, waiting. They carried stones from all across the pass. Yara chose theirs from the streambed where the meltwater ran. Teo brought one down from the col that would bear his map's new name. Voss, when her turn came, walked all the way to the eastern edge of the pass and back, twenty minutes each way in the thin air, and set her stone at the base without a word, and Marisol understood it was the heaviest stone anyone carried that day.

Marisol set hers last. She had carried it up from the ledges, from the hermit's shelter, a flat gray stone she'd pried from the wall of the shrine cairn where Adaeze's second letter had waited all those years. It seemed right. Things that had waited should be together.

When it was done, the cairn stood taller than a man, white-rimmed with frost, and the wind came through the Sable Current and touched it and moved on, and the snow-stream kept running east, silver in the noon light, the river in the sky that Adaeze Okafor had guarded with her whole life.

"It's a good grave," Yara said. Their voice was raw. "She'd have hated the fuss and loved the view."

Teo laughed, and then Yara laughed, wet and cracked, and then Marisol was laughing too, helplessly, at the edge of a grave at three thousand meters, and it was the first time she had laughed at a burial in her life, and it felt like setting down the last stone.

They stood there a while longer. Then Voss touched her hat brim and turned downhill with her two remaining climbers, and Yara and Teo went into the cabin to sort what would be carried down—Adaeze's journals, her charts, the small tin box of blue flowers pressed between pages twenty years ago—and Marisol stayed.

She stayed until the shadows of the peaks had stretched long across the white field. She talked to the cairn, quietly, the way she used to talk to rivers when she was a young guide and the rivers were the only things that listened. She told it about the saloon. She told it about the years she didn't remember and the ones she remembered too well. She told it that she was going down the mountain sober, and that she meant to stay that way, and that if she failed she would come back up and apologize in person.

Then she heard boots in the snow behind her, and turned, and Yara was standing there with a lantern lit against the coming dusk and an expression Marisol had not seen on their face before—uncertain, almost shy, the river captain suddenly a child at a stranger's door.

"The journals," Yara said. "There's one from the year I was born. She wrote about me. And there's—" they stopped, and started again. "There's a page about you. From the retreat. I haven't read it yet. I thought—" They held out the small leather book. "I thought you should read it first. Whatever it says. It's yours before it's mine."

Marisol looked at the book. Her hands, scarred and steady now, did not shake as she took it.

The light was failing. Down the eastern slope, the snow waited, deep and blue in the shadows, and below that the valleys, and below that the river, and a new boat with an old name waiting to be born. But that was tomorrow's country. Tonight there was a fire in a cabin at the top of the world, and a page with her name on it, and two people inside who had, without ever saying the word, become the only family she had left.

Marisol opened the journal, and read the first line by lantern light, and her breath went out of her all at once.

*If Marisol ever reads this,* Adaeze had written, twenty years ago, in a strong and certain hand, *tell her the retreat was not her failure. It was my gift.*
