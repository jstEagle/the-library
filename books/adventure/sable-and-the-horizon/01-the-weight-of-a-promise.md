## 1. The Weight of a Promise

The storm had been building all morning the way storms did out here — not with thunder but with a kind of pressure you felt in your fillings, in the seams of your boots, in the way the dust began to move against the wind instead of with it. Emrys Okonkwo had learned to read that contradiction years ago, back when reading it wrong meant losing a rig, and she was reading it now from the cab of her chase truck as it ate up the flats at eighty kilometers an hour, the horizon ahead gone the color of old iron.

Contract footage. That was what this run was. Three hundred kilometers of open scrub for the Meridian Weather Consortium, who paid by the minute of usable storm tape and docked her for every frame of blur. The money was already spoken for — she'd spent it before she'd earned it, the way she spent everything, because the Vane Syndicate's collectors didn't accept promises and didn't care that her mother's medical bills had eaten two seasons of income whole. Forty thousand owed. Interest compounding like rust on a wet bolt. Every chase was a payment plan written in lightning.

She keyed the mic to Toma in the passenger seat. "Front's tightening. Rotation signature on the doppler feed — weak, but it's there."

"Weak," Toma said, not looking up from his tablet. He was twenty-two and treated every storm like a math problem, which Emrys both resented and relied on. "You're burning fuel chasing a maybe."

"I'm burning fuel chasing a paycheck." She let the truck drift wider, cutting the seam between the dry line and the mesocyclone's inflow, where the light went strange and green-gray and the air tasted of ozone and crushed sage. "Maybe is all we've got."

The land out here was a ledger too. Fences gone to wire ghosts. Farmhouses standing empty with their windows blown in, doors open like mouths. Three settlements within a day's drive — Corrin's Hollow, Ash Bend, the little grid of trailers they still called New Prosperity out of stubbornness or spite — and all of them shrinking, wells going brackish, kids leaving as soon as their legs were long enough. Everyone blamed the drought. Droughts ended. This hadn't.

Emrys noticed things like that. It wasn't sentiment; it was pattern recognition, the same instinct that kept her alive inside a rotating wall cloud. Things that didn't add up were data.

* * *

The storm broke east of the salt crossing at 4:17 in the afternoon, and it was a good one — a stovepipe tornado, rope-out at the tail, forty seconds of clean footage that would cover maybe six hundred of what she owed. She'd have laughed at the arithmetic if laughing weren't so expensive. Then the cell collapsed faster than the models predicted, the rain shaft slumped over them like a wet blanket, and the light died, and Emrys did what she always did when the sky closed its hand: she stopped performing and started driving, threading the hail core by feel, by the sound of the tires, by the way the rain hit the windshield in bands instead of sheets.

That was when she saw the smoke.

It wasn't storm smoke. Storm smoke was steam, white, rising off sun-warmed rock after the rain passed. This was black and low and leaning hard downwind, coming off something metal. She swung toward it against every instinct that said never leave the road, because out here the road was a rumor and the ground between wheel ruts could swallow an axle whole.

Two kilometers off the track, half-buried in a wash the rain had turned to quicksand, sat a prospector's rig. A converted hauler, the kind with a drill mast welded to the bed and sample crates stacked like a fortress. Its rear end was canted into the gully at an angle that made Emrys's stomach clench, because she knew angles, knew what they did to people inside machines. The cab was crumpled around something that used to be a door. The smoke was leaking from under the hood, thinning now, dying with whatever was left to burn.

She pulled up short, waded out into the mud to the depth of her shins, and got the door open with a pry bar and a curse.

The man inside was old. Older than anyone had a right to be doing this work — sixty, seventy, hands like cured leather wrapped around a steering wheel he no longer controlled. Blood in his hair, dried to a shell. One leg bent wrong below the knee. His lips were gray and cracked, and when Emrys leaned close she could hear the rattle in his chest, the sound of lungs filling with something they couldn't use.

"Hold on," she said, though she didn't know why she said it. People wanted words even when the words changed nothing.

He looked at her with eyes the color of the storm behind her and said, "You came anyway."

"You're bleeding out in a wreck." She hauled the first-aid kit from behind her seat, tore it open one-handed. "I came because wrecks are where the footage isn't."

Something like a laugh moved through him, faint, more breath than sound. "Honest," he said. "I'll give you that."

His name, he told her while she splinted what she could and packed what she couldn't, was Halvard Sable. Prospector. Thirty-one years walking the basin with a drill bit and a hunch. He'd been running a line survey along the deep aquifer — mapping water, tracing it, testing it — and his rig had caught the soft ground at speed during the hail front. He'd been lying in this gully for nine hours. Nine hours, in a country where nine hours was usually the difference between a story and a grave.

Emrys gave him water. Sips, because his body had stopped asking for it properly. Then she opened the kit's morphine ampoule and held it near his mouth and watched the rattle ease, watched his face unclench by degrees, and understood that she was not saving him. She was making him comfortable. There was a difference, and the difference was the rest of his life, which was measured now in hours at most, and she knew it, and he knew it, and neither of them said it out loud because saying it cost something too.

"Nearest medevac is Ash Bend," Toma radioed from the truck, voice tight. "Ninety minutes if the ford's passable. It won't be."

"Then we've got ninety minutes," Emrys said, and hated herself a little for how calmly she said it.

* * *

Sable used them well, those ninety minutes.

Somewhere past the second ampoule, pain pushed back far enough behind the fog that he could talk, and talk he did — about the aquifer, about the water table dropping three meters in five years across a basin that should have been recharging, about the samples in his crates that read wrong, that read like chemistry nobody had put there on purpose. He talked about the settlements drinking it. He talked in fragments, a man rationing his last coherence, and Emrys listened the way she listened to storms: taking everything, judging nothing yet.

Then he reached inside his coat, and his hands shook so badly she had to help him, and together they brought out a oilskin packet, sealed with wax, tied with wire. Flat. Heavy. The size of a folded chart.

"This goes to the end of the line marked on it," he said. "There's a station buried out there. Old survey cache. What I found, it confirms what's in here — no, it proves it. Proof needs a second source. Proof needs witnesses. You understand?"

"I understand paper," Emrys said carefully. "I don't understand what you want me to do with it."

"Bring it home." His grip found her wrist, and for a man ninety percent dead his strength was astonishing, and terrible. "Bring what it finds home safe. And you tell no one what it says until it's home. Not your crew. Not your creditors. No one. Swear it."

"Why me? Why not—" she gestured vaguely at the world, at Ash Bend, at anyone with less debt than she had.

"Because you stopped." His eyes were closing, opening, closing. "Everyone else drove past. I counted four rigs on that track today. Four. You turned off the road for a stranger's smoke. That's the only currency I trust anymore."

And then, quieter, with the particular gravity of a man spending his final sentence on something that mattered: "The Syndicate knows I was out here. They've known for a month. If they reach this packet before the line does, it dies with me, and so does everyone drinking that water. So don't sell it. Don't trade it. Whatever they offer you — and they will offer, they know your name already, Okonkwo, they know what you owe — remember what it costs to stop."

He died twenty minutes later, in the cab of his own rig, with the storm's runoff ticking down on the roof like a slow clock winding out. Emrys stayed with him through it. She cut him down gently when the time came, wrapped what needed wrapping, took the oilskin packet from where he'd made her tuck it inside her jacket against her skin, and stood in the mud beside a dead man's machine while the sky cleared to a bruised and blameless blue.

* * *

In the truck afterward, Toma asked the obvious question. "What's in it?"

"Not telling you," Emrys said. "Not telling anyone. That was the deal."

"A sealed packet from a dead prospector and you're just going to carry it around like luggage?" He sounded young when he was exasperated, which was most of the time. "We could open it. We could know what we're carrying. We could decide whether it's worth anything."

"That's exactly what I'm not going to do." She set the packet on the seat between them, where she could see it in the mirror, where it would ride every kilometer like a third passenger. "He bought my promise with water and morphine, Toma. Cheapest contract I've ever signed and the only one I intend to keep."

Toma stared at her, then at the packet, then at the map on his tablet — the route home, the fuel remaining, the debt numbers he kept in a folder labeled OBLIGATIONS because he was the kind of person who labeled folders. "You owe the Syndicate forty thousand," he said finally. "If that thing is worth what he implied—"

"If it's worth what he implied, it's worth more than money, and the moment I price it, I've broken my word." She turned the truck back toward the track, toward the settlement lights coming on far to the west, small and yellow and drinking poisoned water without knowing it. "Besides. He said they know my name."

The evening wind came up across the flats, dry already, the storm's moisture wrung out and gone. Emrys drove into it with the packet warm against her ribs and thought about what Sable had said — that stopping had always cost more than she could pay. It was true. Stopping for her mother's illness had cost her the good contracts. Stopping for a stranger's smoke had cost her an afternoon and most of her water. Everything she'd ever paused for had billed her afterward, with interest, and the Vane Syndicate was simply the largest of her creditors, the one that had learned to charge compound rates on other people's bad luck.

But somewhere between the gully and the track, between the last ampoule and the last breath, the arithmetic had shifted. Some debts weren't owed to lenders. Some were owed to the dead, and those didn't compound — they waited, patient as the buried station at the end of Sable's line, for whoever was stubborn enough to walk the distance.

She'd find out soon enough what walked with her. On the ridge line behind them, catching the last light, a single vehicle had appeared — too far away to read, too deliberate to be lost, holding a course that matched hers like it had been waiting for her to move first.
