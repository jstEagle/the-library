## 2. What the Dry Season Keeps

The map was drawn on something that wasn't paper — a polymer sheet, heat-fused at the edges, the kind of material that outlived the men who trusted it. Sable's hand had been steady when he inked it. Dying men's hands usually weren't.

Emrys studied it by lamplight in the rig's cab while Toma slept badly in the back, and what she saw made her want to wake him and made her want to let him sleep forever. The route ran south off the haul roads, off everything with a name, into a depression the old surveys called the sink basin — a bowl of earth two days wide where the water tables had folded in on themselves like a closing hand. At its center, Sable had marked a single glyph: a circle with a line through it, struck through twice in red.

*Buried. Do not dig casually.*

She'd promised a dying man she'd bring home whatever this led to. She hadn't promised to understand it first.

They rolled out before dawn, three vehicles running dark, headlights killed until the sun gave them permission. Emrys drove point because she always did, reading the ground the way other people read faces — the way dust sat on hardpan, the way wind scoured one slope clean and left another feathered with sand. The dry season had teeth this year. Every horizon looked like it was holding its breath.

By midmorning they'd left the last fence post behind. By noon, the last tire track that wasn't theirs.

* * *

**TOMA:** I keep the nav for this crew, which means I keep the lies too. There are eleven ways into the sink basin and ten of them are traps — soft ground that eats axles, salt crust over mud you could lose a truck in up to the mirrors. Emrys picked the eleventh without hesitating. She doesn't hesitate. That's not bravery. Bravery is being scared and going anyway. What Emrys has is something colder: she's already priced out what fear costs and decided she can't afford it. Debt does that to a person. The Syndicate owns her ledger, and a person who's owned learns to move like nothing can touch them, because flinching is a luxury.

I logged our heading and said nothing about the fact that we were driving toward a mark on a dead man's map. Some jobs you take because they pay. This one we took because a man with maybe an hour left traded his morphine for a promise, and Emrys Okonkwo — who has broken contracts with better-dressed people than Halvard Sable — signed it anyway. I watched her do it. I watched her mean it.

That scares me more than the basin does.

* * *

The ridge line came down on them like a wall at dusk — a long spine of rust-colored rock that funneled the wind into a single screaming corridor. Emrys took it at speed, feeling the rig go light over the crest, and then they dropped into the basin proper and the world went quiet in a way that felt less like silence and more like being swallowed.

The floor of the basin was cracked clay, polygon after polygon of it, stretching to every edge of sight. Heat shimmered off it in slow waves. Nothing lived here. Not even the things that lived nowhere else.

"There." Toma pointed, and Emrys saw it — a low mound breaking the geometry of the cracks, maybe four hundred meters off their line, too regular to be geology. They came at it angled, engines throttled down, and stopped fifty meters out.

It was a roof. A survey station's roof, buried to its eaves in silt and clay, half-collapsed, its solar array shattered decades ago. Someone had built this place when the basin still had a name people spoke aloud. Someone had sealed it and walked away, or been made to walk away, or never walked away at all.

"We dig tonight," Emrys said. "Cool hours only. We're done before the sun gets ideas."

They dug through the night by work lights, six of them passing buckets down a chain, the clay coming away in slabs that held the shape of the shovels like pressed flowers. Around midnight the roof gave way to a hatch, and the hatch gave way to darkness that smelled of dust and machine oil and time.

Toma went down first. He always volunteered for the dark places; Emrys had stopped asking why years ago.

* * *

**TOMA:** The station is small — one room, mostly, plus a shaft. Racks of core samples. A bank of dead monitors. And a filing cabinet, steel, sealed with a padlock someone had welded shut from the inside, which tells you everything about how afraid they were.

I cut it open with a plasma torch and the drawer stuck and then surrendered all at once, spilling folders across the floor like a card trick. Water quality reports. Aquifer charts. Dates going back nineteen years. And then the thing that stopped my hands cold:

A memo. Internal. Vane Syndicate letterhead. Signed by a name I knew — a regional director who now sits on the settlements' water council, the body that decides who gets to drink.

The memo says, in language so careful it might as well be a confession: *continued extraction at current rates will introduce contaminant migration into the shallows aquifer serving Ash Hollow, Meridian Flats, and Calloway's Rest. Projected affected population: 4,100. Recommended action: proceed. Public disclosure not advised.*

Proceed. They proceeded. Nineteen years ago they read their own poison in the numbers, wrote it down, filed it, welded the cabinet shut, buried the station under a basin nobody visits, and kept pumping.

Kids in Ash Hollow have been born sick for a generation. We all blamed the drought. The drought was innocent.

I carried the folders up into the starlight and stood there holding them, and I'll be honest with whoever reads this someday: my first thought was not justice. My first thought was a number. Evidence like this, sold back to the Syndicate quietly, clears every debt in this crew twice over and buys us out of this life entirely. My second thought was Emrys's face when she sees what I'm holding, and I knew the answer before I asked the question, and I asked it anyway, because a man owes himself the attempt.

* * *

Emrys spread the documents across the hood of the lead rig as the sun rose, weighting the corners with stones, and the crew gathered around them the way people gather around a fire.

She read the memo twice. Then she stood very still, looking east across the basin, where the light was coming up the color of old brass, and Toma watched her decide.

"Four thousand one hundred," she said finally. "That's the number they wrote down. Like it was tonnage."

"It *is* money," Toma said. "I know what I'm saying. I've run the figures. One meeting, one buyer, one signature, and we walk away rich and free. Or—" he gestured at the folders, "—we become the story. And stories get people killed. You know who kills them."

"I know exactly who." Her voice was flat. "Halvard Sable knew too. That's why he was dying alone in a wrecked rig instead of rich in a tower somewhere. He found this. He tried to bring it home. It cost him everything he had, and he still spent his last hour making sure it wouldn't stop here." She picked up the memo and folded it against her chest. "I promised him. Not the map. Not the payout. Him. Whatever's at the end of this goes home safe, and what's at the end of this isn't paper, Toma. It's four thousand people drinking poison and calling it weather."

"And your debt?"

"My debt is a number. This is people. I've spent my whole life confusing the two." She almost smiled. "Not anymore."

Toma looked at her a long moment, and then he laughed — short, rough, defeated and relieved at once. "Then we do it loud. Council, broadcast, witnesses. If we're dying either way, we die expensive."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

**TOMA:** Here's the part I should have seen coming, and didn't, and will hate myself for.

We broke camp an hour later, and Emrys walked the perimeter the way she does, checking the ground, and came back with her jaw set wrong.

"Our tracks from the ridge line," she said. "There's a second set alongside them."

Nobody believed her at first. Six of us, and we'd watched nothing but empty country for two days. But Emrys reads ground like scripture, so we went and looked, and there it was: a parallel track, lighter tread, wider axle, keeping our exact line through the funnel of the ridge — matching our pace, matching our stops, hanging back just far enough to stay over the horizon. It hadn't appeared today. The wind had preserved it. It had been there since the ridge.

Someone had followed us in. Someone had watched us dig up the Syndicate's grave and carry out its confession, and had waited, patient as the dry season itself, to see which way we'd run.

Emrys stood at the edge of camp facing south, where the tracks vanished into the shimmer, and I watched her do the arithmetic she does — distance, fuel, light, wind, odds — and I realized I already knew the answer, because it was written in how still she was standing.

They weren't following us anymore.

They were ahead of us, at the salt crossing, waiting for us to come to them.

And we had no road home that didn't pass through their hands.
