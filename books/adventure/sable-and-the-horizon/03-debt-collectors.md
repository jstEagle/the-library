## 3. Debt Collectors

The salt crossing looked like a mirror that had given up on being a mirror.

Emrys brought the rig down off the ridge at first light, tires crunching over crust that rang like broken glass under her wheels. The basin spread out ahead, white to every horizon, stitched with the faint blue veins of brine pools. Beautiful, in the way of things that wanted you dead. Salt ate metal and lungs alike, and there was no cover for forty kilometers in any direction.

Which was why Sable's map had said *cross it fast or don't cross at all.*

"We're exposed," Toma said from the nav seat, not looking up from his instruments. He'd been saying it since the ridge line, where he'd found tire tracks paralleling theirs, patient as a shadow. "Whoever's been following us knows exactly where we have to funnel."

"Then we funnel fast." Emrys kept her eyes moving — sky, horizon, sky again. The air had that thick, grainy stillness she'd learned to hate in ten years of chasing: pressure falling, dust hanging suspended like the whole world was holding its breath. A storm was building somewhere west. She could feel it in her back teeth.

"Emrys." Toma's voice had gone flat. "Dust plume. Two o'clock. Moving too straight for weather."

She saw it a heartbeat later — a dark line rising off the flats, resolving into three vehicles running abreast, kicking rooster tails of white powder. Syndicate rigs. She'd know that formation anywhere; she'd driven second position in it once, two lifetimes ago, before the debts and the cameras and the slow grinding arithmetic of owing Vane money.

"Hold on," she said, and floored it.

The salt crossing turned into a chase measured in heartbeats. The rig bucked over pressure ridges, brine spray fanning from the wheels. Behind them the lead vehicle closed with terrible patience, like something that had done this a hundred times and never once lost.

They made it four kilometers before the shot took out their rear axle.

The rig slewed sideways, dug into the crust, and rolled. Emrys came to hanging upside down in her harness, blood ticking off her eyebrow, the world outside the shattered windshield nothing but white and spinning dust. Somewhere behind her, Toma was cutting himself free. Somewhere closer, boots were hitting salt.

"*Scatter*," she rasped at him. "Take the case. Go."

"The promise—"

"Is no good if we're both in a hole. *Go.*"

Toma went, low and fast, vanishing into a fold of terrain she'd have sworn didn't exist until he dropped into it. Emrys popped her harness, crawled out through the windshield frame, and got six steps before the world went white at the edges and hands closed on her arms.

* * *

Rask Vey watched them carry Emrys Okonkwo across the salt and felt the old familiar weight settle onto his shoulders — the one that came with doing the Syndicate's work correctly.

He'd known her name before he'd ever seen her face. Okonkwo. Storm chaser, contract footage, deep in the red to Vane Credit. And once — though almost nobody living remembered it — a survey tech out of Meridian Station, same intake year as him. Same instructors. Same brutal first winter learning to read wind the way other people read faces.

She'd been better at it than him. That was the part nobody mentioned anymore.

Vey stood by the lead rig while they zip-tied her wrists and set her on an equipment crate, and he studied her the way you study a problem you used to call a friend. Dust in her hair. Blood drying black at her temple. Eyes already working — reading the sky over his shoulder even while kneeling captive on salt.

"You know who I am," he said.

"I know what you are," she said. "The face is older."

Something in his chest pulled tight. He let it go. Sentiment was a debt like any other, and he'd paid his off years ago, the day he'd chosen the badge over the report — the day he'd buried a water survey because loyalty paid better than truth. He'd told himself it was pragmatism. Some nights he still managed to believe it.

"Here's how this goes," he said, crouching to her level. "You give me the survey station's location and everything you pulled from it. In exchange: your debts clear. All of them. Every credit you owe Vane, gone. You walk away free, rig replaced, contract honored. Or—" he nodded toward the horizon, where the western sky had begun to turn the color of old rust— "we wait out the storm together, and I find out what I need from your navigator instead. He strikes me as a man who can be reasoned with."

"He can't," Emrys said. "Neither can I."

"You haven't heard the number."

"I don't care about the number." Her voice was steady, but Vey had spent enough years around her to hear the thing underneath it — not fear. Grief. "A dying man gave me something and asked me to bring it home. That's the whole transaction. There's no clause where I sell it."

"Sable." Vey almost laughed. "Halvard Sable was a prospector with a grudge and a terminal diagnosis. You traded morphine for a piece of paper. That's not a promise, Okonkwo, that's a purchase."

"A promise is exactly what it is when it costs you more than the paper's worth."

The wind was rising now. Vey felt it against his neck, tasted salt and iron. His own storm-reading — rusted, but not gone — told him what the sky was assembling out west: a haboob, a big one, walls of dust tall enough to bury the sun. Two hours, maybe less. Enough time to break her, if it came to that. Not much time to be clever.

"Last chance," he said. "Where's the station?"

"Buried," Emrys said, "under your conscience. Dig there."

One of his riders stepped forward with a baton, and Vey raised a hand to stop him — not mercy, just procedure. They had time. They had shade rigged off the truck bed. They had all the patience of an organization that owned the water in three settlements and the law in none of them.

What they didn't have, he realized twenty minutes later, was her.

* * *

Emrys had spent the twenty minutes doing what she did best: watching.

The zip ties were industrial grade, but the crate they'd seated her on sat beside a brine pool, and brine mist had been drifting off it all morning, settling fine and corrosive over everything. She'd dragged her bound wrists through the wettest edge of the crate's metal rim, slow, casual, like shifting her weight. Salt water eats through coating, then steel, then patience. She'd learned that from a wrecked rig and a long walk home, years before Vey ever put on the badge.

And the sky — the sky was hers. She'd been reading its grammar since before Vey knew letters. The haboob wasn't coming straight on; it was curling, hooking east, which meant the leading gust front would hit from the southwest first, then swing. Everyone else would brace for the wall. She'd brace for the turn.

When the ties finally parted — a soft snap lost under the rising wind — she waited one more minute, watching the riders huddle at the trucks, checking straps against the coming blow. Then the gust front arrived early, a slap of hot grit, and in the half-blind scramble she rolled off the crate, behind it, and ran — not away from the storm, but *into* it, angling hard southwest, letting the dust swallow her while they were still looking north for a runner.

Running blind in a haboob should kill you. But she wasn't blind. Pressure against her left cheek meant the wind was quartering; she corrected two degrees. The grit thinned for three seconds — a gap between cells — and she read the ground beneath it from memory: the fold of terrain where Toma had vanished. She counted paces, trusted the math, and dropped into the fold just as the full wall of dust arrived and erased the world.

Toma was there, case strapped to his chest, a tarp strung between two salt crags for shelter. He looked at her, then at the storm, then at her bleeding wrists.

"They offered me the same deal," he said quietly. "Radioed my earpiece before the crash. Cleared accounts, new start." He paused. "I told them the promise matters more than the payout. Took me a while to decide. But watching them roll up on us like we were cargo — I decided."

Emrys sat down hard against the crag, and for a moment she couldn't speak. Betrayal she'd expected. This — Toma choosing the promise without being asked — was the thing she hadn't let herself hope for since the ridge line.

"Vey will figure the storm's cover in an hour," she said finally. "We move inside it, east, then cut south along the dry channel to the council road. We make the settlements before the season's last winds die."

"And if he catches us?"

Emrys looked at her raw wrists, then at the case against Toma's chest, and felt something in her go quiet and certain, a needle swinging true.

"Then he learns what I learned today," she said. "I'm done paying debts. From here on, I only keep promises."

Above them, the haboob howled — and somewhere inside it, engines were turning, hunting.
