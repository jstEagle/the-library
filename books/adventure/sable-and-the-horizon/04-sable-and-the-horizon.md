## 4. Sable and the Horizon

The last dry winds came off the flats at dawn, hot as an open furnace door, and Emrys Okonkwo drove straight into them with the survey case strapped to the seat beside her like a passenger she couldn't afford to lose.

Toma rode shotgun, the broadcast rig balanced across his knees, solder still cooling on its housing. He'd built it from three broken radios and a transmitter he'd pried out of a dead relay station two nights ago, working by headlamp while the rest of them slept in shifts. It was ugly. It was heavy. It would work.

"Range is maybe forty kilometers," he said, for the fourth time. "Open channels only. Once we key it, everyone hears it. Syndicate, settlements, herders, anyone with a receiver and a grudge."

"That's the point."

"It's also irreversible." He looked at her sideways. "You sure you want to be the one who says it?"

Emrys kept her eyes on the horizon, where the sky had gone the color of old bone. "Sable asked me to bring what this finds home safe. Home isn't a vault, Toma. Home is people knowing."

The rig's engine screamed against the wind. Behind them, dust hung in a long brown curtain — Vey's riders, still back at the ridge line, still coming. They'd lost six hours to the storm that broke her free of the salt crossing, and Vey had spent none of them sleeping.

* * *

They hit the settlement road at midmorning, passing the first of the marker cairns that told them they were close enough to matter. The land here was dying in plain sight — wells drawn down to mud, orchards standing bare and gray, a playground where the swings didn't move because no children came anymore. Three settlements fed by one aquifer, and the aquifer was poison. She'd read the survey numbers until they burned behind her eyelids. Benzene plumes mapped decade by decade. Internal memos dated years before the first sick child. The Vane Syndicate had known, and drilled anyway, and buried the proof under forty meters of sink basin silt where a dying prospector happened to find it.

Halvard Sable. Even his name sounded like something the wind was carrying away.

"Ten minutes," Toma said. "We're in range of the settlement repeaters."

Emrys pulled over on the high ground above the dry riverbed, engine idling, and took the mic. Her hands were steady. That surprised her more than anything else about the morning.

"This is Emrys Okonkwo, broadcasting open on all channels." Her voice carried flat and clear over the wind noise. "I'm holding the Halvard Sable survey — hydrological records from the basin station, sealed since before the dry season began. They show the Vane Syndicate knowingly contaminated the aquifer beneath Ashfall, Meridian Flats, and Coldwater Crossing. Dated memos. Test results. Maps of the plume. I'm bringing copies to the settlements' council at noon today. Anyone who wants to see them, come and see them. There will be too many of us to bury."

She keyed off. For a moment there was just the wind, and then Toma's rig lit up with returning signals — dozens of them, then hundreds, herders and well-keepers and council clerks and ordinary voices crackling back across the flats: *Received. Received. We're coming. We hear you.*

"You did it," Toma said softly. "No takebacks now."

"Never wanted any."

* * *

They reached the council grounds as the sun stood two hands high, and Emrys saw what witnesses actually looked like: not a crowd yet, but a gathering — trucks and wagons and people on foot strung along the road for half a kilometer, moving toward the council hall with the gravity of a funeral and the hunger of a trial. Old women with canes. Well crews in dusty coveralls. A man carrying a child who had never known water that didn't smell of chemicals.

Vey's riders came up the west road twenty minutes later.

Emrys watched them from the hall steps — nine machines, engines snarling, Rask Vey leading on the black bike she remembered from another life, when they'd both chased weather for wages and he'd taught her how to read a wall cloud. He slowed at the edge of the crowd. Stopped. Sat there with his hand resting on the grip of his sidearm, looking at what stood between him and the council doors.

Three hundred people. Maybe four hundred now. Still arriving.

He could have pushed through. He was good enough, and mean enough, and the Syndicate had never once paid him to be gentle. But weapons fire into a crowd of witnesses wasn't leverage; it was a confession with a body count, and even Rask Vey understood arithmetic like that. Every person here had heard the broadcast. Every person here was a copy of the truth that couldn't be recalled.

He sat there a long time. Long enough that Emrys found herself walking down the steps toward him, alone, because some debts you settle face to face.

"Rask."

He didn't look at her. "You should have sold it back, Okonkwo. I'd have cleared your ledger myself. Walked away clean."

"I was never going to walk away clean," she said. "I was going to walk away paid — in the only currency the Syndicate can't counterfeit."

That made him look at her. His eyes were the same eyes that had once chosen loyalty over truth on a ridge outside Meridian Flats, back when they were young and the choice hadn't cost anyone everything yet. Almost the same.

"There'll be others after me," he said. "You know that."

"The season's ending. The dry winds die tonight, and after that every road runs wet and slow, and every day the story spreads further from anyone who could stop it." She put her hand flat on the survey case. "It's done, Rask. You're not chasing evidence anymore. You're chasing a memory, and memories don't take bullets."

Something moved through his face — not surrender, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man doing math he'd been avoiding for years. He pulled his hand off the sidearm, turned his bike around in a long slow arc that four hundred people watched in silence, and rode west without a word. Behind him, the crowd let out a breath it had been holding since dawn, and someone started to clap, and then everyone did, and the sound rolled out over the flats like thunder with no storm attached.

* * *

The council session ran past dark. Emrys laid the survey out table by table, page by page, and watched the room change — watched disbelief become anger become resolve, watched the council president of Coldwater Crossing stand up with shaking hands and say, "We knew something was wrong. Now we know we were right, and we know who, and we know when." Lawyers were called. Filings were begun. By midnight there were three settlements' worth of claims against the Vane Syndicate, and the Syndicate's name was being spoken on every channel in the territory, not whispered but shouted.

Her debt died somewhere in those hours. Not forgiven — rendered worthless. You couldn't hold paper over a woman whose testimony four hundred witnesses needed, and the Syndicate's enforcers knew it better than anyone. Leverage dies in daylight. She'd learned that the hard way, and the hard way had finally paid out.

Toma found her on the hall steps afterward, when the crowd had thinned to stragglers and the stars had come out hard and cold over the flats.

"So," he said. "What now? Storm season starts again in a few months. Contracts. Footage. Debt—"

"No debt." She said it slowly, tasting the shape of the words. "First time since I was nineteen."

"And?"

Emrys looked east, toward the basin, toward the wrecked rig where an old man had traded his last hours for a stranger's promise. Somewhere out there, under the wind, Halvard Sable was still keeping his own appointment with the horizon.

"Now I stay," she said. "Not because running got cheaper. Because some things are worth standing still for."

Toma nodded, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the wind moved through the settlement like it was learning gentleness.

Then a runner came up the road with a lantern held high — a kid from Coldwater Crossing, breathless, grinning, holding out a folded paper like it was treasure. "Message for the survey woman. Fella brought it in an hour ago, wouldn't say who sent him. Wouldn't leave a name."

Emrys opened it. One line, in handwriting she'd last seen on a sealed map in a dying man's rig:

*There's a second station. Deeper. He marked it on the back of the map — check the fold.*

She unfolded Sable's map, and there it was, in cramped pencil along the crease she'd never thought to open: coordinates, a depth, and four words that turned the quiet night into a question.

*This one names them all.*
