## 1. The Weight of Moving On

The rain in Vessik came sideways, the way it always did in the weeks before the monsoon turned serious, and Ulyana Dravenmoor had learned to read it the way other people read clocks. Sideways rain meant the season was early. Early meant the lowlands would flood before anyone expected them to. Early meant work — for someone like her, work was always a disaster that hadn't finished happening yet.

She stood under the awning of the ferry office with her pack between her boots and her hands in her pockets, watching the Sable's tributary run brown and fast through the middle of town. Vessik wasn't a town so much as an argument between two countries, a border crossing that had grown customs sheds and noodle stalls and a jail the way a wound grows scar tissue. On one side of the river, the Republic. On the other, the Protectorate. Ulyana had been blacklisted from both, which made Vessik the only place on the map where she could legally stand — a strip of mud and corrugated iron that belonged to neither government because neither wanted the expense of claiming it.

Three years she'd been coming through here. Long enough that the woman who ran the noodle stall stopped asking her name. Not long enough for anyone to offer her anything better than a bed above the ferry office and a rate for guiding that shrank every season.

"You're still here." The noodle woman said it without looking up, ladling broth into a chipped bowl. "I thought you'd have gone south by now."

"I'm always about to go south."

"Then why aren't you?"

Ulyana took the bowl and sat on the crate beside the stove. "Because going south costs money I don't have yet."

That was the honest answer, or close enough to it. The fuller answer was that she didn't know how to stop moving, and also didn't know how to keep doing it, and both of those facts sat in her chest like ballast. She was thirty-eight years old and had spent eleven of those years being paid to walk people into wild country and bring them back out. It was good work when you could get it. It was impossible work when two governments had your name on a list, because the kind of clients who needed a guide in monsoon country were exactly the kind who checked lists first.

The first blacklist had come from the Protectorate, six years back. Officially: unauthorized entry into a restricted survey zone. Unofficially: she'd led a mapping expedition into the upper Sable basin, found something the government hadn't put on its maps, and reported it anyway instead of taking the money they offered her to forget it. The second blacklist had come from the Republic two years later, after a rescue job went wrong — a party of engineers stranded by a flash flood, three of them dead, and a review board that decided a guide with her history must have cut corners. She hadn't. She'd done everything right, and the water had killed them anyway, and the board needed someone to blame who couldn't afford a lawyer.

Since then: small contracts. Poacher patrols who didn't ask questions. A mining outfit that paid late. A widow who'd wanted her husband's body recovered from a gorge and paid her in chickens and grief. The work kept her fed. It did not keep her anywhere.

The rain eased toward evening, and the light over the river went the color of old tin. That was when the man from the survey office found her.

* * *

He came down the ferry ramp in shoes too good for the mud, holding his briefcase off the ground like it might catch something. Young-ish, maybe forty, with the damp, worried look of a bureaucrat who'd been sent somewhere he didn't want to be by people he couldn't refuse. His badge said R. Ostrand, Department of Hydrographic Affairs, and beneath the lamination the card was already warping from the humidity.

"Ms. Dravenmoor?" He said her name carefully, like he'd practiced it. "I was told you'd be at the ferry office."

"You were told right."

He looked her over the way men in his line of work looked at everything — checking for damage, assessing resale value. Her jacket was patched at the shoulder. Her boots were resoled twice. Her pack was military surplus older than some of the countries she'd worked in. None of it bothered him, apparently, which told her something about how desperate his office was.

"I have a contract," he said. "An escort contract. A survey team going into the lower basin. Nine days, maybe twelve if the weather holds against us. We need a guide who knows the channels."

"The lower basin floods this time of year."

"That's rather the point. We're mapping flood lines." He set the briefcase on a dry crate and opened it with the fussy precision of a man who alphabetized things. "The department has funding for a full hydrographic revision. The last survey data is forty years old and the river has moved since then. Moved considerably."

Rivers did that. Rivers especially did that when governments built levees upstream and pretended the water downstream wouldn't notice. Ulyana knew the Sable's moods better than most people knew their own families, and even she wouldn't have claimed to know where its channels lay anymore. Forty years was nothing to a river. Forty years was a rumor.

"So hire a local pilot," she said. "Half the ferrymen here could draw you flood lines in their sleep."

"They're not licensed for government work. And frankly —" he paused, and the pause had weight in it, "— we were given a short list. Your name was on it. Which surprised me, given your file."

Given my file. He'd read it, then. Both blacklists, probably, and whatever else the departments attached to a name like hers. She waited for him to explain why a man reading that file would still come looking for her, and he didn't explain, and the not-explaining was more interesting than any explanation would have been.

"Who gave you the list?"

"A coordinator in the capital. I don't have clearance to know how the list was assembled." He said it lightly, as though it were normal, and maybe for him it was. "Look — Ms. Dravenmoor. I'll be direct, because I've been in Vessik four days and everyone here has been direct with me and I find I prefer it. This contract is not popular inside the department. Half the section thinks the survey is unnecessary. The other half thinks it's a waste of money. Nobody wants to lead it. The team we've assembled is five officials who volunteered for reasons I'd describe as mixed. What they need is someone on the water who won't get them killed, and the licensed guides all want triple what we can pay."

"And I want less than triple?"

"I'm told you want work at all."

That landed true enough to sting. She looked at him for a long moment — at the warped badge, the good shoes, the briefcase held like a child holds a bag lunch on the first day at a new school — and felt the particular exhaustion of a person being offered the only door available and knowing it's the only door available.

"What's the pay?"

He named a figure. It was decent. Not good — decent, the kind of number that covered food and a room and a little set aside, but nowhere near what the risk deserved. She'd taken worse for less. She'd also walked away from better, once, on principle, and principle had cost her six years.

"Twelve days," she said. "Downriver and back. I take the team in, I take them out. If the water rises faster than forecast, I call the schedule, and my call is final. That goes in the contract."

"Agreed."

"And nobody touches the boat but me and whoever I say."

"Agreed."

"And if I tell them to turn around, they turn around."

Ostrand hesitated there, visibly, the way a man hesitates when he's remembering instructions he was given and deciding whether to repeat them. "Within reason," he said finally. "It's a survey. They'll need to reach certain points."

"Every point on your map is underwater part of the year. That's why you're surveying it. So yes — within reason. My reason."

He signed off on it. Of course he did; he wanted this over with. They wrote the terms on the ferry office's only table while the noodle woman watched from across the room with the expression of someone who'd seen every kind of paper pass through this town and trusted none of it. Ulyana read the contract twice before signing. There was a clause about confidentiality — standard, she'd have signed it anyway — and a clause about restricted zones that listed coordinates she recognized, and that recognition sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

She signed. Ostrand folded his copy into the briefcase, relieved in a way he tried to hide and failed to, and told her the team would muster at the dock in two days' time. Then he shook her hand — a wet, bureaucratic handshake, firm the way forms are firm — and picked his way back up the ramp toward the customs sheds, briefcase held high, shoes ruined anyway.

The noodle woman refilled her bowl without being asked.

"Government work," she said. "You've done government work before."

"Once or twice."

"How did it go?"

"It went fine until it didn't."

* * *

That night, above the ferry office, Ulyana packed.

Packing was the one ritual she'd never lost, the one piece of home she'd carried from place to place until home became the packing itself. She laid everything out on the narrow bed the way she always did: rope, dry rations for longer than the contract needed, flares, the medical kit she restocked obsessively because she'd once watched a man die for lack of a tourniquet she'd left behind to save weight. Charts of the basin, soft at the folds. A knife, a compass, a waterproof notebook with her own soundings penciled in the margins — corrections to official charts that no official had ever asked her for.

She packed light. Everyone assumed guides packed heavy, that the job was hauling, but the job was judgment, and judgment traveled best unburdened. Eleven years of transit had taught her that everything you owned was either tool or anchor, and she'd spent those eleven years throwing anchors overboard. An apartment in the Protectorate, gone. A marriage, gone — he'd wanted a wife who stayed, and she'd wanted a horizon, and neither of them had been wrong. Friends, thinned to a handful of names she owed letters to. Even her reputation had become a thing she carried rather than a thing she stood on.

At the bottom of the pack, wrapped in oilcloth, there was a photograph.

She took it out and held it a while under the bare bulb. It was creased down the middle from years of folding, the colors gone amber, the edges soft as cloth. Four people standing on a riverbank, squinting into sun, arms around each other in the way people do when they believe the day will end well. She was one of the four. She could name the others: Miro, who'd taught her to read water. Dessa, who'd kept the accounts and the jokes. And a fourth face she found herself unable, tonight, to look at directly — a face connected to the first blacklist, to the restricted zone, to the thing she'd found and reported and been punished for finding.

She never explained to anyone why she kept it. She barely explained it to herself. Throwing it away would have meant deciding what it meant, and keeping it meant never having to decide. So it rode along, year after year, border after border, the way a splinter rides in a palm — too deep to dig out, too familiar to feel unless the weather changed.

Tonight the weather had changed. Tomorrow she'd be on the water again, headed into the same basin, the same channels, the stretch of river that had already cost her a career and very nearly cost her more. She thought about leaving the photograph behind. Deliberately, consciously thought about it — propped it against the lamp, told herself this trip was different, told herself a person couldn't carry everything forever.

Then she wrapped it back in the oilcloth and put it in the pack, because she always did, because not doing it would have required becoming a different person, and she didn't have time to become a different person before morning.

* * *

Two days later the survey team mustered at the dock, and Ulyana saw the boat before she saw the people.

It was a converted patrol launch, thirty feet, diesel, a cabin aft and a flat working deck forward. Old paint over older paint. The hull lines were honest but the maintenance wasn't — she could see the rust bloom at the waterline seam, see the way the rub rail sat proud on one side, and when she pressed her thumb to the planking near the bow the wood gave slightly, the way wood gives when it's been wet and dried and wet again too many times. She'd known boats like this her whole life. Boats like this were fine until they weren't, and then they were very much not fine, very quickly, usually in weather.

The five officials were loading equipment with the enthusiasm of people who had never carried their own luggage. Crates of instruments. A folding theodolite in a case lined with green felt. Two radios, a generator, boxes marked FRAGILE in three languages, and — this was the detail that caught in her throat — far more crates than a flood-line survey required. She counted. Nineteen crates, plus personal gear. A hydrographic revision needed instruments, paper, fuel, food. Nineteen crates needed explaining.

Halvard, the team leader, was the one who tried. He was broad, silvering, with the calm of a man used to being obeyed in rooms where obedience mattered, and when Ulyana asked him what was in the crates he smiled and said, "Equipment," in a voice that believed itself.

"Which equipment?"

"Survey equipment."

"I loaded survey equipment for nine years," she said. "This is more than survey equipment."

He looked at her then — really looked, the first of the team to do it — and there was a flicker in his expression, quick as a fish rising, that she'd seen before on clients with secrets. Not guilt. Guilt she knew. This was closer to fear wearing competence as a coat.

"Departmental redundancy," he said. "We carry spares."

Nobody carried nineteen crates of spares into flooded country. But the contract was signed, the pay was half in advance, and the rain was holding off, and a guide who interrogated her employers on the dock didn't eat in October. So she stowed her own gear, tested the bilge pump — it worked, sluggishly — and cast off at midmorning with five officials, nineteen crates, and a hull she didn't trust, downriver into the basin.

The current took them. The banks widened. The last roofs of Vessik fell away behind the bend, and the country ahead opened into drowned forest and slow brown water and marker posts sticking up out of the shallows — old posts, rotted gray, stamped with a survey mark from a mapping expedition forty years dead.

Ulyana had seen those marks before. Once. From the other side of a blacklist.

She stood at the wheel and let the river carry them toward it, and told herself, the way she always told herself, that this time would be different.

The river, which had heard that sentence before, said nothing.
