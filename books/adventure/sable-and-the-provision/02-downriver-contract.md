## 2. Downriver Contract

The harbor at Vessik smelled of diesel and rot and rain that hadn't arrived yet but was already owed. Ulyana stood at the end of the pier with her pack at her feet, watching the survey boat sit low in the gray water like something that had given up on floating years ago and was only going through the motions.

It was a river barge, forty feet if it was generous, hull patched in three places with plates that didn't match the original metal. The paint had been white once. Now it was the color of old teeth. A crane arm bolted to the stern sagged under its own rust. The name on the bow had been painted over so many times that the letters beneath bled through like scars: *Sable Daughter* over *Kestrel's Grace* over something older she couldn't read at all.

She'd been hired to guide this.

*This,* she thought, and felt the familiar tightening behind her eyes that meant a job was already going wrong before it started.

The five officials came down the gangplank one at a time, each carrying more than the last. Two of them carried instrument cases heavy enough that they walked with their arms fully extended, like men bearing a coffin between them. One carried a rifle he clearly did not know how to hold — stock against his bicep, muzzle wandering toward the sky, toward the water, toward Ulyana herself for one uncomfortable second before she stepped left and let him figure out where his own barrel pointed.

They were government men. She could tell from thirty feet. Not because of the badges — half of them weren't wearing any — but because of how they moved. Bureaucrats on field assignment move like tourists who've been told there are crocodiles: carefully, theatrically, and with no actual understanding of what they're being careful about.

The leader came last. Halvard. Tall, silvering at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome long enough to become an institution. He wore field clothes that were new — tags still stiff, boots unscuffed — over the posture of a man who had never once slept on the ground. His handshake was firm and warm and completely without information in it.

"Madam Dravenmoor," he said. "Thank you for coming. I know your reputation precedes you."

"Which reputation?" she asked.

He smiled. It was a good smile. Practiced. "The favorable one."

"Then you haven't read my file."

"I've read everything relevant." He said it lightly, the way a man says something damning when he believes you won't notice the weight of it. She noticed. She always noticed. *Everything relevant* meant he knew about both blacklists, which meant someone above him had signed off on hiring a woman two governments had formally declared unreliable, which meant either desperation or a plan that required someone expendable.

Both, probably. Both was usually the answer.

"Your team," she said, nodding at the five men arranging themselves on the dock like cargo being inventoried. "Who are they?"

"Hydrologists, mostly. Survey specialists. A cartographer's assistant."

"Names?"

He gave them, quickly, in the flat cadence of a man reciting from a list rather than introducing colleagues. Ostrer. Pell. Marn. Duvall. And Tamsin — the only woman among them, small and quiet-eyed, standing slightly apart from the others as though she'd already worked out that she would be doing the actual work while they took credit for it.

Ulyana looked at each of them in turn. Then she looked at the equipment.

That was where the trouble lived.

There was too much of it. She'd guided survey teams before — real ones, flood-mapping teams, the kind that measured water levels and sediment loads and drew lines on charts that other people would use to not drown. Those teams traveled light. A theodolite, a sounding line, waterproof notebooks, rations for the days planned plus two. You could fit a legitimate hydrographic survey into four crates.

Halvard had brought eleven.

Eleven crates, stenciled with numbers that didn't correspond to any manifest he offered her. Two of them were long — rifle-length or longer — and sealed with government wax stamps she recognized from the customs house in Karst, the border crossing where her second blacklist had been signed. Three were heavy enough that it took two men and a block-and-tackle to swing them aboard. One was small, steel, and locked, and Halvard carried it himself, personally, and set it down inside the cabin where he could keep his hand on it while talking.

"What's in the small one?" she asked.

"Calibration instruments."

"For what?"

"The survey instruments."

Ulyana looked at him. He looked back with the serene confidence of a man who believed his answers had been adequate. They hadn't been. Calibration instruments for survey gear were brass and glass and weighed nothing. Whatever was in that case had weight to it — she'd watched him lift it, watched the way his wrist compensated, watched him set it down with the care people reserved for things that were either fragile or dangerous or both.

"Mr. Halvard," she said, and stopped, because she was deciding whether to use his first name or make him give it. He gave it without prompting — Aldous — which told her he'd been coached, prepped, drilled by someone whose job was making him sound cooperative. "Aldous Halvard. Fine. Here's how this works. I'm escorting you upriver through monsoon country on a boat I wouldn't cross a harbor in. That means when something goes wrong — and something will go wrong — I'm the one who has to get you out of it. So I need to know what I'm getting you out of it *for*. Flood mapping doesn't need eleven crates. What's the actual mission?"

"Flood mapping," he said again, and this time his voice cracked slightly on the second word, the way voices do when they're holding a shape they don't believe in.

Ulyana had interrogated smugglers, deserters, and one customs official who'd tried to bribe her with her own confiscated rifle. She knew what a bad lie sounded like. Halvard's lie wasn't even well-constructed. It was a screen door over a vault door — you could see straight through it if you stood at the right angle, and he seemed genuinely unaware that angles existed.

But she also knew what happened to guides who pushed too hard before the contract started. There was no contract yet. There was a handshake and a number — a very good number, insultingly good, the kind of number that existed because someone wanted her committed before she asked questions. Once she signed, she was theirs until the river said otherwise.

So she didn't push. She filed it. Every answer he failed to give went into the ledger she kept behind her eyes, the one with columns for *suspicious*, *very suspicious*, and *start planning your exit now*. Halvard was filling the third column faster than anyone she'd worked for in six years.

* * *

The boat's owner was a wiry old man named Ferren who smoked through a hole in his throat and spoke by cupping his hand over it, producing a voice like wind through a fence. He showed Ulyana the engine room without being asked, which she appreciated; owners who hid their engines were owners lying about their engines.

Inside, the situation confirmed every suspicion the hull's exterior had raised. The main engine was a marine diesel older than Ferren, kept alive by cannibalized parts and prayer. The backup was missing entirely — its mounting bolts still in place, its absence explained by a plywood sign reading *IN REPAIR*, which in Ferren's dialect apparently meant *sold*. The bilge pump worked. The secondary bilge pump was a bucket.

"You'll want to stay south of the Sable bend," Ferren wheezed, handing her a wrench she hadn't asked for. "Past the bend, in monsoon, this hull takes water like it's thirsty. I patch her every season. Every season she finds new ways to drink."

"How many ways this season?"

"Three that I found." He looked at her with the honest fatalism of a man who had made peace with drowning professionally. "How many I didn't find is the interesting question."

Ulyana climbed back on deck and did the arithmetic she hated doing. Five officials, herself, Ferren, eleven crates, fuel for nine days, and a hull with at least three leaks and a single engine, heading into the Sable basin during the earliest forecast monsoon window in a decade.

The forecast itself was posted at the harbormaster's office, and she'd read it twice. *Depression forming over the highlands. Movement west-southwest. Expected landfall: four to six days. Rainfall totals: extreme.* Four to six days. The trip upstream to the survey grid, per Halvard's own itinerary, was five days. Which meant they would be somewhere in the middle of the flooded lowlands — the drowned country, the place where the river spread wide and shallow and treacherous and ate boats for breakfast — when the sky opened.

She mentioned this to Halvard at the gangplank, laying the forecast paper on a crate between them like a card played face-up.

"Weather delays are built into the schedule," he said.

"Built in how?"

"Contingency days."

"With one engine and a bucket for a bilge pump?"

His jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Almost. She caught it because catching things was the entire trade. "The vessel is certified."

"By whom?"

"The department."

"Your department?"

"Yes."

"So the people who chartered the boat certified the boat." She folded the forecast and put it in her jacket pocket, where he could see her doing it, which was its own kind of message. "Noted."

He looked at her then with the first honest expression she'd seen on him — a flash of something wary, almost resentful, the mask slipping just enough to show the man underneath didn't like being read. Then the mask came back down, smooth as water closing over a dropped stone, and he resumed being the pleasant, evasive administrator she'd met ten minutes ago.

Good, she thought. Now I know he knows I know.

* * *

They cast off at midday, into a sky the color of wet slate, the air thick enough to chew. Ulyana took the helm for the harbor approach while Ferren handled the engine, and the officials arranged themselves on deck with their crates like men fortifying a position — which, she was beginning to suspect, was exactly what they were doing.

Tamsin was the only one who helped. Small, quick, quietly competent, she coiled lines without being told and stowed cases where they wouldn't shift in chop, and when Ulyana called a correction on the loading — the heavy crates amidships, low, or the boat would list like a drunk — Tamsin was the one who made it happen, moving the others' gear with a firmness that suggested she'd been managing incompetent men her entire career.

"You've done river work," Ulyana said to her, not quite a question.

"Lake work. Survey grade." Tamsin paused in her hauling. "Rivers are new to me."

"They'll be new to you once. After that they're familiar forever, mostly in the sense of knowing how they kill people." Ulyana nodded at the crate Tamsin was securing. "What's in those long ones? Everyone's been very careful not to tell me."

Tamsin looked at her — really looked, the way people do when they've decided whether to trust you and have come down, tentatively, on yes. "I wasn't briefed past my own tasking," she said. "Water sampling and depth charts. That's mine. What's in the sealed crates isn't in my tasking, and I've stopped asking, because asking got me reassigned once before."

"Reassigned from what?"

"From a better job." She said it evenly, without self-pity, and Ulyana marked her down as the most dangerous person aboard after herself — dangerous meaning *competent and paying attention*, which in Ulyana's private taxonomy was the highest compliment short of trust.

Ostrer, Pell, Marn, and Duvall, by contrast, spent the afternoon being seasick in rotation, which at least kept them from asking questions of their own. Halvard stayed in the cabin with his steel case, emerging hourly to check the horizon and retreat again, and Ulyana began to understand the rhythm of him: a man rehearsing. Whatever he was supposed to say or do upriver, he was practicing it alone in a cabin, with a locked box for company, and the practice wasn't going well, because his lies were getting worse, not better, as the day went on.

By evening they'd cleared the harbor bar and entered the Sable proper, the water going from harbor-gray to river-brown, wide and slow and muscular, carrying silt from countries upstream that Ulyana had been banned from entering. The banks narrowed into drowned forest — trees standing to their shoulders in water that used to be their soil, black trunks reflected so perfectly that the world seemed doubled, forest above and forest below with only a hairline of surface between.

She knew this water. That was the thing she'd been circling around since the dock, the thing she hadn't let herself think about directly, because thinking about it directly meant admitting what it meant.

Six years ago she'd guided an expedition up this same stretch. Different client, different flag, same river. That expedition ended badly — ended with her arrest at the border, her equipment seized, her name entered into a registry that followed her across two borders like a smell. She had never learned what the expedition actually found. She'd been paid to take them to a confluence and wait, and the waiting turned into three days, and the three days turned into soldiers, and the soldiers turned into a cell, and the cell turned into the rest of her life running.

The route Halvard had filed matched that route. Not roughly. Exactly. Same waypoints. Same overnight anchorages. Same final destination: a section of the upper basin that appeared on no commercial chart, marked on the government surveys only by a blank space where topography should be.

Guides noticed things like blank spaces. Blank spaces were where the interesting work happened, and also where guides got disappeared.

At dusk she anchored in the lee of a drowned grove, following the itinerary Halvard had provided, and stood at the rail watching the light die over water that remembered her. The officials ate ration packs and complained about the heat. Ferren smoked through his throat-hole and listened to the engine tick as it cooled. Tamsin cleaned the sampling equipment with the focus of someone keeping her hands busy to keep her thoughts still.

And Halvard stood apart at the stern, looking north, upriver, toward the blank space, with an expression Ulyana had seen exactly once before — on the face of the previous client, the night before the soldiers came. It was the look of a man who knows what's ahead of him and is afraid of it in a way he cannot say out loud, because saying it out loud would be treason, or worse, would be true.

She went to him. Casual, professional, the approach of a guide checking tomorrow's plan.

"Early start?" she asked.

"Dawn." He didn't look at her. He looked at the water. "We'll make the confluence by midafternoon if the current holds."

"The confluence." She said the word slowly, tasting it. "The one off the charts?"

"It's on our charts."

"Everyone's charts are on their charts, Mr. Halvard. Mine aren't yours. Mine have the places marked where people like me get arrested." She waited. He said nothing. The silence had texture — fear in it, and obedience, and something underneath both that might have been shame.

Then, unexpectedly, he spoke. Quietly. As if the words cost him something.

"There's a man on the river," he said. "Ahead of us. At the confluence. We've been told to expect him. A cartographer, freelance. He's charting the flood lines independently — that's the story. If he approaches us, we're instructed to cooperate with him. Fully."

"Instructed by whom?"

He looked at her then, finally, and the look lasted one full second, which in Halvard-time was a geological age. "You'll want to be careful of him," was all he said.

Ulyana stood at the rail a long time after that, running it through. A freelance cartographer, waiting at a confluence, whom a government survey team had been ordered to cooperate with fully. Freelancers didn't get cooperation orders. Cooperation orders meant the freelancer was working for someone — or against someone — and either way, the man at the confluence was not what he claimed to be, and Halvard knew it, and Halvard was afraid of him, and Halvard had just handed her that fear wrapped in a warning like it was a gift.

Maybe it was. Maybe the man underneath the mask was trying, clumsily, to protect her. Or maybe it was bait. Maybe they wanted a witness disposed of, and a curious guide walking willingly into a stranger's skiff was the cleanest disposal method ever devised.

Either way, dawn was coming, and the confluence with it, and the river was already rising.

She checked the bilge before sleeping. An inch of water where there'd been none at noon. Ferren's three found leaks, plus whatever else the old hull was hiding.

One inch wasn't drowning. One inch was a message.

The river was telling her something. It always did. The problem — the problem that had defined her whole life — was that she never stayed long enough to hear the whole sentence.
