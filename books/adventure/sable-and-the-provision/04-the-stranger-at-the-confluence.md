## 4. The Stranger at the Confluence

I had been waiting three days for the survey boat to reach the confluence, which is a strange thing to admit, so I never admitted it. I told myself I was charting flood lines. I told the river, too, in case it kept records.

The Sable basin doesn't forgive improvisation. The water here moves like a bureaucracy — slow, patient, and willing to drown you over paperwork you didn't know you'd signed. So when I saw their boat coming down the eastern channel, riding too low and steering too confidently, I did what any careful man would do.

I let them come to me.

My skiff sat in the shadow of a drowned kapok tree, canvas hull gray as the sky, and I sat very still with my chart tube across my knees and my hands folded on top of it like a man with nothing to hide. A man with nothing to hide is the most suspicious creature on any river. But suspicion is a currency, and I've learned to spend it slowly.

They came around the bend at half past morning, five figures under a tarpaulin and one at the tiller who moved like she'd been born holding a rudder line. Ulyana Dravenmoor. I knew her by reputation before I ever saw her — two blacklists, one burned bridge, and a way of reading water that made grown officials nervous. The government had hired her anyway, which told me how badly they wanted this survey done quietly. You don't hire a woman like that for routine work. You hire her because no one else will go where you're going, and because if things go wrong, she's easy to disavow.

Halvard stood at the bow pretending to study the far bank. He was doing it wrong. Men who actually study riverbanks look at the waterline; men who are afraid of being seen look at the treeline. Halvard hadn't looked down once since they'd rounded the bend.

I waited until they were fifty meters out, then shipped my oars and let the current carry me into the open, casual as driftwood.

"Ahoy," I called, and raised my chart tube like a toast. "You're about to row into a channel that doesn't exist."

* * *

The effect was immediate and satisfying. The boat wobbled. One of the officials — the young one with the clipboard, whose name I would later learn was Pell — grabbed the gunwale like the river itself had spoken. Ulyana didn't flinch. She turned her head, measured my skiff, measured me, and brought them alongside with the unhurried precision of someone deciding whether to help a drowning man or finish him off.

"You're claiming a channel doesn't exist," she said. Not a question. An invitation to embarrass myself.

"I'm claiming that channel exists on maps and not in the water." I tapped the tube. "The eastern fork shifted after the last deluge. Your chart is four years old. Four years is a long time in a floodplain." I smiled the smile I keep for customs officers. "I'm Kestrel. Ivo Kestrel. Cartographer. Freelance."

"Freelance cartographer," repeated the man at the bow. Halvard. Up close he had the damp, overworked look of a man who slept in his coat. "There's no contract for independent surveyors in this basin."

"There's no contract for anything in this basin," I said pleasantly. "That's rather its charm."

Ulyana watched me while Halvard blustered. She had the particular stillness of people who have survived things by noticing. Her eyes went from my face to my hands to the chart tube and back, cataloguing. I made sure my hands looked boring. Hands tell stories; mine were telling one about rope calluses and oar work, which was true, and nothing about the six weeks I'd spent upstream asking questions, which was also true but less useful right now.

"Flood lines," she said finally. "That's your work?"

"That's my work." Also true. Just not all of it. I've found that the best lies are load-bearing walls built entirely out of true statements.

"The eastern fork," she said. "Show me."

* * *

We rafted the boats together in the lee of the drowned kapok, and I unrolled my chart across both gunwales, weighting the corners with a tin cup and Halvard's own water flask, which he surrendered without being asked and which told me something about him too — men who guard everything don't notice small thefts, and men who notice small thefts guard the wrong things.

My chart was genuine. That mattered. I'd spent two seasons on it, sounding the false channels, marking the sandbars that migrated like herd animals, noting where the old villages had gone under and where their roofs still caught debris at high water. It was good work. It was also, in three places, deliberately incomplete, because there are things you chart and things you leave blank on purpose, and the blank places are where I live.

"The false channel here," I said, tracing the eastern fork, "looks deeper than the main stem. Deeper water, greener banks, birds working the surface. Every instinct says take it. Take it and you're in a maze of dead channels within the hour, and the monsoon comes up from the south, and you'll spend a week going in circles watching your food rot."

"And this one?" Ulyana touched the main stem.

"Boring, shallow in spots, ugly. Correct."

She looked at me for a long moment. "Who paid for this chart?"

"Nobody yet. That's what freelance means." I rolled it up before her finger could wander toward the blank places. "Though I'm open to offers. Guides get lonely. Rivers get repetitive. And you—" I glanced at the tarpaulin, at the equipment crated and stenciled with ministry markings, at the five officials who had all suddenly become very interested in the scenery— "you look like a man short on local knowledge."

"We have charts," Halvard said stiffly.

"You have charts," I agreed. "How are they working out?"

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Pell coughed. A woman near the stern — Tamsin, though I didn't know her name yet, only that she was the one who watched the water instead of the banks, which marked her as either the smartest or the most frightened of them — said nothing at all.

Ulyana broke it. "What do you charge?"

"For what?"

"However much of your time we need."

I named a figure roughly triple what I'd have accepted and watched her not blink. Good. A negotiator. Someone who understood that price was information.

"Half now," she said. "Half when we clear the delta."

"Done." We shook on it, and her grip was dry and hard and brief, a handshake like a signature on a document she intended to hold me to. "One condition," I added. "I eat in your boat. My stove died in Vell's Landing and I'm tired of raw fish."

Another test, another pass. She almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, and I noted it the way I note sandbars: present, mobile, likely to shift without warning.

* * *

That first evening, rafted at anchor in a backwater thick with drowned cane, I learned the shape of the expedition by learning the shape of its meals. Rations stamped with a ministry seal I recognized and didn't like. Instruments wrapped in oilcloth with more care than flood-survey gear deserves. And Halvard, who ate last and fastest and kept his journal inside his coat even while eating, which is the behavior of a man carrying orders, not notes.

I charmed them, because charming people is easy and useful. Pell wanted to talk about the capital and I let him. The fat one — Doss? Ross? — wanted an audience for his opinions on river fish and I gave him one. Tamsin asked me two questions about silt loads, intelligent ones, and I answered honestly and watched her decide I was trustworthy, which was either a victory or a tragedy depending on how the next week went.

And through all of it, I watched Halvard watch me.

He thought he was subtle. He wasn't. Every time I spoke, his eyes came to me like a compass needle finding north, and every time I mentioned the upper basin — casually, incidentally, the way you mention weather — his jaw did a small tight thing, like a man swallowing a word.

He knew what the survey was for. More than that, he suspected what I was for, or feared it, which amounts to the same thing in the dark.

So I fed him exactly what he expected. I complained about mosquitoes. I showed Tamsin how to read the debris lines for coming water. I drew a little sketch of the confluence for Pell, who treated it like a holy relic. By full dark I was, as far as they could tell, exactly what I claimed: a bored freelancer glad of company and hot food.

Only Ulyana didn't quite settle. She took the middle watch, and when she thought I was asleep — I sleep the way cats do, in thin slices, always listening — she sat at the tiller and studied me across the gap between the boats for a long, long time.

I let her. There's no better cover than being someone's open question. As long as she was wondering what I was, she wasn't wondering what I knew.

Because here is what I knew, and why I had waited three days in the shadow of a dead tree for this boat:

Six years ago, an expedition came up this river. Nine people. Officially, they never existed. Unofficially, they mapped the upper basin more thoroughly than anyone before or since, and then they were erased — names struck, families notified of nothing, records sealed under a ministry stamp that officially means *misadventure* and actually means *stop asking*. My sister was among them. Mira Kestrel. Twenty-six years old, better with a sextant than I'll ever be, and gone so completely that even grief couldn't find a body to bury.

Last spring, the deluge came early and hard, and the basin gave something back. I heard the rumors in Vell's Landing: survey teams sent upriver, quiet ones, mapping not the floods but whatever the floods uncovered. And then I heard that a new team was forming, and that the guide they'd hired was Ulyana Dravenmoor, who had been up this river once before, on the job that ended her first career.

Two threads, tied in a knot. I followed them here.

I lay in the bottom of my skiff and listened to the water slap the hulls and rehearsed, again, the version of myself I'd be tomorrow. Charming. Useful. Harmless. A man who asks nothing and notices less.

It's exhausting, being harmless. But the alternative is being honest, and honest men on this river end up in the ministry's ledgers between *misadventure* and *stop asking*.

Near midnight, rain began — soft, preliminary, a monsoon clearing its throat. Ulyana shifted at the tiller. In the dark I heard her say, quietly, whether to me or to herself I couldn't tell:

"This stretch. I've been here before."

I kept my breathing slow and even, and stared up at the rain, and thought: *so have I, in every way that matters.*

Tomorrow we'd enter the eastern reaches, past the marker posts of the erased expedition, toward whatever the water had given back. Halvard would grow more careful. Tamsin would grow more frightened. And I would keep smiling, and keep counting, and keep my hand near the chart tube where the real map lived — the one with nine names on it, eight of them crossed out, and one left blank because I refused, all these years, to write the word *dead*.

The river carried us on. I had chosen this meeting, chosen this boat, chosen this lie.

Now I just had to survive being right.
