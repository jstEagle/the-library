## 5. Gear and No Tracks

I woke before the light did, the way I always did on a river, some animal part of me keeping the old schedule even when the work didn't require it. The rain had stopped in the small hours. That was the first wrong thing. On the Sable in monsoon season, rain didn't stop. It paused the way a held breath pauses — and I lay in the stern under the tarp and listened to the silence and felt the hairs on my forearms lift.

The second wrong thing was the birds. Or rather, the absence of them. The drowned forest around our campsite usually started up at first gray with a racket of herons and something shrill I'd never bothered to name. This morning the trees stood dripping and dumb.

I sat up. The fire pit was ash and nothing. The tarp over the equipment cache sagged with water it shouldn't have been holding, because someone should have been on watch, and no one had woken me for my shift.

"Veyra," I said, to the dark. Then, louder: "Halvard."

Nothing. The river said nothing back. It had that quality I'd learned to distrust in my first year of guiding, the quality of a room where people have just stopped talking about you.

I got the lantern lit with hands that were steadier than I deserved. The camp resolved out of the dark in pieces, and each piece was wrong in a way I catalogued the way other people catalogued insults — precisely, and with a rising heat behind the sternum.

Five bedrolls. I counted them twice, because the first count couldn't be true. Five bedrolls, and every one of them cold. Not cool — cold. Cold the way things get after hours of empty air, the canvas damp through, the ground beneath them dark with old rain and no body heat. Whatever had happened, it hadn't happened at dawn. It had happened in the deep of night, while I'd lain ten meters away listening to rain and congratulating myself on my vigilance.

*So have I, in every way that matters.* I thought of Ivo saying it, and wanted, absurdly, to hit something.

The boots were gone. That was the detail that stopped my breath. Five pairs of boots — the heavy government-issue kind, the kind Halvard had complained about for three days straight — gone. But the weapons were there. Halvard's sidearm sat in its holster on top of his pack, buckled. Veyra's rifle leaned against a tree where she'd propped it, dry under the eave of bark. The survey team had walked — or been walked — out of camp barefoot and unarmed, in the dark, in monsoon country, and had left behind everything a person leaves behind only when they don't intend to come back.

Or when they intend to go somewhere boots would only slow them.

I didn't let myself finish that thought. Not yet. Not in the dark.

I circled the camp the way I'd been taught to circle a camp that has gone quiet, wide at first, then tighter, lantern held low. The ground was the Sable basin's gift to trackers: black alluvial mud, knee-deep in the low spots, the kind of muck that holds a print like wax holds a seal. I could have read a whole night's story in it. Who went where, how fast, how heavy, whether they went willingly — all of it written there in the language I'd been reading since I was old enough to be useful to people who shouldn't have hired me.

The mud was blank.

Not disturbed. Not swept. *Blank* — smooth as poured wax from the edge of the fire pit to the treeline, from the treeline to the water. Five adults had walked out of that camp and the river basin had simply declined to record it. There was one set of prints anywhere in the soft ground, and they were mine from the evening before, going to check the boat lines, and they ended at the water like a sentence with no verb.

I stood at the treeline for a long time with the lantern shaking just enough that the shadows moved when my hands didn't want them to.

I have seen disappearances. That's not a confession; it's a résumé. In the border country where I learned the trade, people vanish the way weather vanishes — a man steps out to check his snares and becomes a story his mother tells for forty years. I've walked search grids for three days and found the answer in the fourth hour of the fourth day, and the answer is almost always ugly and almost always legible: a print, a drag mark, a torn strap, blood in the wrong place. The basin always talks. The basin is a gossip.

This was not that. This was five people erased from a page that had been specifically designed to keep their writing.

I went back to the camp because there was nothing else to do, and that was when I found the journal.

It was sitting on Halvard's bedroll. Not beside it, not under the edge of the tarp — *on* it, centered, the way you'd set a place at a table. The cover was soaked through, swollen fat with water, and when I picked it up water ran out of the binding and down my wrist, cold as the mud. Every instinct I had said *don't open it, this is a message and messages on empty camps are bait* — and I opened it, because I have never once in my life taken my own good advice when the alternative was not knowing.

The pages were soaked. They should have been pulp. Government paper dissolves; I've watched reports turn to gray porridge in less rain than this. But the ink had held — every line of it, dark and sharp, as if the water had gone around the words rather than through them. I told myself it was good ink, lacquered paper, a bureaucrat's expensive habits. I told myself that twice. The third time I stopped telling myself anything and read.

The early entries were the ones I'd expected. Soundings, channel notes, complaints about the food, a long grudge against the boat's engine that matched the grudge Halvard voiced aloud. His hand was cramped and precise, a man who'd learned to write small to fit into forms.

Then the entries changed. I found the seam on the third page — a shift in the pressure of the pen, the letters getting taller, the margins abandoned. And the content changed with it. No more soundings. Instead, lines like:

*The posts confirm it. The old survey wasn't lost. It was withdrawn. Everything past the third marker was struck from the record by order, not by flood.*

And below that, in a hand pressed so hard the paper had nearly torn:

*It surfaced with the last deluge. The water gave it back and we were sent to say it hadn't. Confirm and report nothing. That is the whole of our commission. I have signed my name to a lie with five witnesses.*

I sat with the journal dripping in my lap and the gray light finally coming up through the trees, and I understood that I was holding the reason five people were gone, and that it was also, somehow, the only thing they'd left behind — the one object in camp that could hang them all.

You don't leave the evidence. Not if you're fleeing. Not if you're taken. You leave the evidence when someone wants it found — when the leaving *is* the message. And the message, written in a drowned camp with no tracks and cold bedrolls and weapons stacked neat as a parade, was this:

*We were never here. And neither, officially, were you.*

I heard the skiff before I saw it — the particular soft knock of a paddle against a gunwale that Ivo Kestrel had never once managed to correct, though I'd begun to suspect the error was deliberate, a courtesy to whoever might be listening. He came out of the morning mist off the channel with his canvas hood back and his face doing what it always did, which was being pleasant at a level just below sincerity.

He grounded the skiff, stepped out, looked at the camp for the space of one breath — the bedrolls, the weapons, the blank mud — and I watched something move behind his pleasantness. Not surprise. *Confirmation.* He looked like a man checking a box.

"Ulyana," he said. "Where is everyone?"

"You tell me." I didn't get up. I kept the journal closed under my hand. "You were on the water last night. You told me you don't sleep much."

"I don't." He crouched at the edge of the fire pit, touched the ash, and rubbed it between his fingers like a man testing flour. "Cold a long time. Before midnight, I'd say." He glanced at the mud, and I watched him not react to it — watched him perform not reacting, which is its own kind of reaction. "No tracks."

"No tracks," I agreed.

"Five people." He said it lightly, testing the weight of it. "Barefoot, by the look of the boots. In this water." He shook his head slowly. "The Sable takes people, but it's never been tidy about it."

"No," I said. "It hasn't." I stood, finally, and the journal stayed in my hand, and I let him see that it stayed in my hand. "You came looking for us at dawn. Before dawn. Before anyone could have known the camp was empty."

"I came looking for the camp," he said. "I follow survey teams the way gulls follow boats. It's nothing personal."

"Everything about you is personal, Ivo. You just haven't told me what yet."

He smiled at that, and it was the first smile he'd given me that didn't cost him anything, and that frightened me more than the blank mud had. Because it was the smile of a man whose plan had arrived exactly on schedule.

"You should get out of here," he said, and for one moment the charm dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was tired and old and serious. "You have a boat. You have a channel south. In two days, maybe three, there will be people on this river looking for what's left of this survey, and they won't be looking to rescue anyone. Take the journal or don't. But go."

"And the team?"

"The team," Ivo said, looking out at the drowned trees, at the water that had declined to leave a single mark, "is exactly where it chose to be."

I looked down at the journal in my hand, at the water still seeping from its spine, at words that should have dissolved and hadn't. I thought about the nine names on my own private map, eight crossed out and one left mercifully blank. I thought about how the basin had taken them and never once been this clean about it.

The river said nothing. The birds still hadn't come back.

I opened the journal to the first page of the changed hand, and I started to read it aloud, so that whatever had taken five people from a guarded camp would know that at least one of us was still taking notes.
