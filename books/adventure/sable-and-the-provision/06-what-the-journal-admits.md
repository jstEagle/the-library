## 6. What the Journal Admits

The journal was Halvard's. That much was obvious from the brass plate on the cover — *H. HALVARD, SURVEY LEAD* — stamped in the same officious typeface as the contract that had dragged me downriver. It lay face-down in the mud at the edge of the cold camp, as if someone had dropped it mid-stride and kept walking.

Nobody walks away from a journal. Not a survey man. Not a bureaucrat who'd spent three weeks annotating river depths in a hand so precise it looked printed. I turned it over with two fingers, the way you'd turn over something that might bite.

The pages were soaked. The monsoon had gotten to them before I did, swelling the paper into a soft brick, ink feathering at the edges of the early entries. But Halvard had written small and pressed hard, and the later pages — the ones that mattered — had survived.

I sat down on his empty bedroll to read them. It felt like theft. It felt like the only sane thing left to do.

*Day 1. Departed Kessel's Landing 06:40. River gauge at 4.1 meters and rising. Dravenmoor competent, insubordinate in manner. Note for file: her presence is a condition imposed from above, not a recommendation.*

I almost laughed. Almost. My laugh came out as a single dry click in my throat, like a lock testing itself.

*A condition imposed from above.* So they'd known who I was when they hired me. Not despite the blacklists — because of them. A guide nobody would miss asking questions about. A woman whose word against a ministry's weighed exactly nothing.

I kept reading.

*Day 3. The false channel east of the drowned church matches the 1971 survey within half a meter. Remarkable. The old expedition's markers are intact where the water hasn't taken them. Dravenmoor recognized the route. She said nothing, but she went pale at the second marker post. She knows more than her file suggests. Watch her.*

My hands weren't steady. I told myself it was the cold — dawn on the Sable in monsoon season could chill you through a wool coat — but my hands knew better than my pride did. The marker posts. The route. The job six years ago that had ended with my first blacklist and a body count the government had filed under *flood casualties, natural causes.*

They hadn't hired me for my knowledge of the river.

They'd hired me because I'd already been to the place they were going, and survived it, and been punished for surviving it. I was proof of concept. I was the control group.

* * *

I made myself tea I didn't want, because my father used to say a person who stops making tea has stopped expecting tomorrow, and I wasn't ready to test whether that was true. The fire was small and wet-smoking. Around me the camp sat in its terrible neatness: five bedrolls rolled tight, five mess kits stacked, the survey instruments crated and lashed exactly as they'd been the night before. Boots gone. Weapons gone. Five men and women walked out of here barefoot or in whatever they slept in, and the mud around the camp held no prints deeper than bird tracks.

Mud doesn't lie. Mud is the one witness on this river that has never once been bought.

I read by firelight while the sky went from grey to bruise-colored.

*Day 5. Confluence reached. The cartographer Kestrel appeared precisely when the charts said he shouldn't be able to. Halvard notes: coincidence is a luxury this operation cannot afford. Recommend we do not share the true objective with him. Also recommend we do not share it with Dravenmoor beyond what is necessary for navigation.*

So there it was, confirmed twice over: I was cargo with a good sense of direction.

But it was the next entry that stopped my breath.

*Directive received via sealed packet, Kessel's relay. Text as follows: "Confirm visual status of Site Provision-9. If surfaced, establish perimeter, log coordinates, and report nothing further by any channel. Do not approach the interior. Do not photograph the interior. Previous expedition personnel are not to be discussed with local inhabitants. This directive supersedes all prior instructions and does not exist."*

*It does not exist.* Halvard had copied the phrase out with what I could only describe as relish, underlining it twice, as though the absurdity of it delighted him. A directive that forbade its own existence. An order to find something and then unfind it.

Site Provision-9.

I stared at the words until they stopped being words. Six years ago, on my first run through this basin, an old ferryman had gotten drunk on rice spirit and talked about "the provision" — supplies, he said, left in the deep country by the expedition before last, meant for whoever lasted long enough to need them. I'd thought it was a story about rations. Men always make their ghosts smaller than they are.

The village elders would know better. The village that wasn't on charts. Ivo's charts, maybe — the ones he drew at night with his back turned, the ones he thought I didn't see him correcting.

*Day 6. Aerial reconnaissance impossible; cloud deck at 400 meters and lowering. Ground team reports the eastern levee breached sometime in the last decade. Water standing eight feet over what the 1971 maps mark as a maintenance road. If the site has surfaced, it has surfaced as an island. Or a roof.*

A roof. I set the journal down and looked north, where the drowned forest thinned into open water and the horizon had that particular flatness that means depth without bottom. Somewhere under that grey sheet was a structure built by a regime that had specialized in making people disappear — and now the water that had hidden it for fifty years was giving it back.

And five government surveyors had been sent to look at it and swear they hadn't.

* * *

The rain started again around midmorning, a fine needling rain that couldn't decide whether to commit. I read on under the boat's canvas awning, journal balanced on my knees, and the entries got shorter as the days got stranger.

*Day 7. Dravenmoor asked directly why a flood survey requires radiation dosimeters. I told her mineral sampling. She accepted this with a facial expression I can only describe as patient. I do not enjoy being lied to by professionals; it feels like being graded.*

Good, Halvard. At least you knew you were being graded.

*Day 8. Voss found a marker post with fresh rope tied to it. Knot style consistent with the 1971 expedition. Rope is sound. Someone has maintained these posts within the last year, possibly within the last month. Recommendation to command: we are not the first party sent to confirm. We may not even be the fifth.*

I remembered the rope. Of course I remembered the rope — I'd cut a length of it off that same post six years ago and used it to lash a cracked fuel drum, and the ferryman had gone quiet in a way that had frightened me more than any shouting. *You don't take from the provision,* he'd said. *You take from the provision, the provision takes notice.*

Superstition. I'd filed it under superstition and paddled on, and two weeks later I was blacklisted and three men were listed as drowned.

*Day 9. Night sounds from the northeast. Not animal. Rhythmic. Voss says machinery; Okafor says water in a pipe. I say neither. I have heard water in pipes and I have heard machinery and this is closer to breathing. Command will call this fatigue. Command is not here.*

Closer to breathing. I read that line four times. Halvard had been afraid. Not bureaucratic-afraid, not career-afraid — afraid the way a man is afraid when the world shows him a seam and he realizes the whole surface might be stitched on.

And then the entries changed.

*Day 10. Last night I dreamed of my mother's kitchen in Vellmark, which was demolished in '68 along with the block and everyone in it, including her. In the dream the kitchen was underwater but lit, and she was setting a third plate. I woke with the taste of bread. I have not tasted bread since the ration years. I am recording this because Okafor says we should record everything now, and because I do not trust my own mind to hold it straight.*

I lowered the journal. The rain ticked on the canvas. Out on the water, a heron stood in the drowned trees with the patience of something that has never once needed to hurry.

Halvard dreamed of the disappeared. His mother, erased with her whole city block, serving bread underwater. And the others — I flipped forward, looking, and found it in Voss's neat appended note:

*All five reporting the same class of dream. Shared content. Okafor's brother. Tamsin's father. Mine: my daughter, whom I have not seen since the custody order. They are setting places for us. Tamsin says: they're not haunting us. They're inviting us.*

Inviting us.

Five government functionaries, sent to confirm a hole in the world and report nothing, dreaming every night of the people the regime had swallowed — and the dreams setting tables, lighting lamps, leaving doors open. You could build a machine to erase people. Apparently you could also build one that learned to call them back.

Or the site could. Whatever the site was. Whatever it promised.

*Day 11. We have located the structure. Coordinates logged per directive. It stands approximately two meters above current waterline — a concrete headwall with a steel door, marked with the old regime's archive seal. The door is ajar. The mud before it holds footprints going in. None coming out. Per directive, we have established perimeter and reported nothing. Per directive, we have done nothing else.*

*Per directive.*

*I am writing this entry off the record, in the space after the directive's shadow ends. I want it known, whoever finds this, that we were not ordered to go in. We were ordered not to. We went in anyway. All five of us, at dusk, without discussion, as if we had discussed it for years. As if we had been walking toward this door our entire careers and only now admitted it.*

*Tamsin stayed outside. She said someone should. She was crying and she wouldn't say why. She is the youngest of us and the only honest one.*

*Tamsin stayed outside.*

I read the name again. Tamsin. The one who'd cried at the door. The one who'd stayed. The one whose boots were gone from camp like everyone else's — but whose bedroll, I realized now, turning, was the only one still damp inside, as if she'd left in the night rather than at dusk with the others.

Or as if she'd come back. Come back alone, in the dark, taken nothing, left everything, and walked out of the mud without printing it.

The mud doesn't lie. But mud also doesn't hold a print once the rain gets serious, and the rain had been serious for hours before dawn.

* * *

By afternoon the rain committed. It came down in ropes, hammering the awning, erasing the camp's neatness into a single smeared idea of a camp. I sat inside it anyway, at the edge of the canvas, journal wrapped in oilcloth except for the page I was reading, and I finished the last entry.

*Final entry. For whoever is reading this — and it will be Dravenmoor, because she is the only one left on this river who reads other people's mail, and I find I am glad. Glad, do you understand? I am glad it's you.*

*You were right about everything and we lied to you about all of it, and I am sorry in the way men are sorry when sorry costs them nothing, which is to say: insufficiently. So instead I will give you the truth, which is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit.*

*The site is real. The regime's archive is real. Everything they disappeared — the people, the records, the verdicts, the villages — it's in there, preserved, catalogued, waiting. Including, I believe, the files on the 1971 expedition. Including, I am certain, the file on a mountain guide named Dravenmoor, blacklisted for a flood she tried to prevent and blamed for a drowning she tried to stop.*

*We were sent to confirm it had surfaced so it could be buried again — flooded deliberately, sealed, forgotten a second time. There is a plan. There is always a plan. The water is their accomplice; it always has been.*

*We chose to stay. Write that plainly: chose. No one took us. Nothing dragged us. We walked through that door toward our own dead, and our own dead had kept the lights on, and I am a grown man and I know what longing does to a person's judgment and I went in anyway.*

*Tamsin refused. Tamsin is out there somewhere, and she knows what we know, and they will come for her the way they came for the 1971 expedition — quietly, with paperwork. Find her, Dravenmoor. Not for us. We are past finding. Find her because she is the only witness this river has ever produced who tells the truth, and because someone has to carry what's in that vault past the waterline.*

*The cipher for the provision cache is on the last page. The old expedition hid evidence against exactly this day. Use it. Burn nothing. Trust the cartographer — or don't, but watch him, because he watches everything.*

*Tell my mother's ghost the third plate was unnecessary. I was always coming.*

*— H. Halvard, Day 11, writing from inside the thing we were ordered not to find*

I closed the journal and sat in the rain and let it hit me like weather, because that was easier than letting it hit me like grief.

Betrayal first — clean, administrative betrayal, the kind I'd been marinating in for six years: they'd hired me as a deniable instrument, aimed me at the exact stretch of river that had already ruined me, and planned to bury it all a second time with me standing on the lid. That anger arrived on schedule, right on time, familiar as an old boot.

But underneath it, something else was setting. Something harder.

Resolve. Not the hot kind — I'd had the hot kind at twenty-five and it had gotten three men killed. The cold kind. The kind that makes tea, checks the hull, counts the rounds, and picks a heading.

Because here was the arithmetic, plain as a chart: a survivor named Tamsin, somewhere on this water, hunted by the same machinery that had blacklisted me twice. A cache of evidence, hidden by dead people against exactly this day, locatable by a cipher sitting in my lap. A vault full of names — mine among them, maybe, the file that would prove what really happened on my first run through this basin.

And a leaking boat, and a stranger who claimed to be a cartographer and watched everything, and a monsoon arriving early like a debt collector.

I stood up, wrapped the journal in oilcloth, and stowed it in the dry box with the ammunition. Then I looked south, where the safe channel ran — deep water, fast current, three days to a port that would ask no questions and remember no faces.

Then north, where the door stood ajar and the footprints only went in.

The rain came down like a verdict. I started checking the hull.

Behind me, out where the drowned trees stood in their rows like a congregation, a canvas skiff slid out of the grey — and Ivo Kestrel began paddling toward camp, unhurried, as if he'd known exactly when to arrive.

As if he'd been reading over my shoulder the whole time.
