## 7. Leaking Boat, Rising Water

**Ulyana**

The sound came at dusk, on the second day after the camp that wasn't there anymore.

Not a splash. Not the slap of debris against the hull. Something softer, wetter — a rhythm under the floorboards, like the river had found a way in and was learning to breathe there. Ulyana knelt in the bilge with her palm flat against the planking and felt it: cold water, rising past her wrist, carrying silt from somewhere it shouldn't be.

"Boat's leaking," she said to Ivo, who was crouched over Halvard's journal by the lantern, drying pages one at a time above the flame like a man feeding a fire he didn't want too big.

"I know."

"You know." She sat back on her heels. "How do you know?"

"Because I've been listening to it since noon." He didn't look up. "It started slow. It's not slow now."

That was the thing about Ivo Kestrel she was beginning to hate — or admire; the two feelings hadn't sorted themselves yet. He noticed everything and said almost nothing. A cartographer, he claimed. Charting flood lines for an institute whose name he'd given without being asked and then never mentioned again. She'd checked his gear the first night while he slept: no institute letterhead, no permits, no instruments worth the name beyond a brass compass older than either of them and a roll of paper he guarded like a man guards letters from home.

A cartographer who traveled light and watched heavy.

She found the leak where the keel met the garboard strake — a seam gone soft, caulk rotted to black thread, the wood around it gray as old bone. The boat was thirty years past its survey, maybe forty. Whoever had issued this contract hadn't cared whether the escort came home. That thought arrived with a small cold clarity: *they didn't expect me back either.* Five officials vanished into mud that held no tracks, and the government's response would not be a search. It would be a sweep. And sweeps didn't leave witnesses standing in leaking boats on the Sable.

"We can't patch it properly," she said. "No pitch, no spare planking. We can slow it."

"With what?"

"The tarred canvas off the cargo cover. Melted down. Stuff the seam, clamp a board over it, pray."

Ivo finally looked up. Lantern light caught the planes of his face — a face built for being forgotten, unremarkable in the way that made him dangerous. "Praying is new for you," he said.

"It's not praying. It's arithmetic." She pulled the canvas cover free and folded it into strips. "We have maybe six hours before the seam opens wider. The monsoon was supposed to be three days out. The barometer fell this morning faster than any guide I've worked with would like."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we swim in water full of drowned trees, and the current takes us wherever the basin wants us." She fed the first strip of canvas toward the small flame she'd built in a tin lid. "Which, based on recent evidence, is not somewhere good."

He handed her the journal pages, dry now, wrapped in oilcloth. Their fingers touched on the transfer and neither of them acknowledged it. "You should keep these," he said. "You read the river better than I do. You'll know when they matter."

"They already matter. They're the only reason anyone will believe five men walked into a swamp and left no footprints."

"Do you believe it?"

The question hung between them like smoke. Ulyana worked the melted canvas into the seam with a blunt knife, pressing it deep, feeling the wood drink the tar. "I believe the river took them," she said. "Or something using the river."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

**Ivo**

She worked through the dark hours the way water works stone — patiently, relentlessly, without complaint. Ivo watched her from his corner of the boat and revised his estimate of her upward, which he did rarely and never aloud.

Ulyana Dravenmoor. He'd known the name before he ever saw her face. Two countries' worth of blacklist, both earned in the same basin, both for the same sin: she'd seen something and refused to unsee it. The first time, seven years ago, guiding a mineral survey up the northern channels. The second time, four years ago, running supplies for a mapping expedition that officially never happened. Both times the punishment was the same — erasure. Not prison. Prison leaves records. Erasure leaves nothing but a woman who can't get work, can't cross borders, can't exist on paper anywhere the government's ink reaches.

And still she kept coming back to the water. That was the part he couldn't solve. A rational woman runs from a place that has ruined her twice. Ulyana Dravenmoor kept taking contracts that led here, as though the basin were a debt she intended to pay in person.

The seam held. Barely. By midnight the inflow had slowed to a seep she could bail with a cup, and she sat back against the gunwale, hands black with tar, and said, "Your turn."

"My turn for what?"

"Honesty. I gave you mine about the journal. Your turn." She nodded at the roll of paper lashed under his seat. "Cartographers don't guard their charts like love letters. What are you actually doing on this river?"

Rain began then — not the monsoon proper, but its herald, fat drops ticking against the canopy, finding every gap in the boat's cover. Ivo considered his options. He was good at considering options; it was most of what he did. He could lie. She'd catch it. He could deflect. She'd wait him out — she had the patience of a woman who had once spent eleven days pinned in a ravine waiting out a patrol.

Or he could give her a piece of the truth, sized so it bought trust without spending everything.

"Flood lines," he said. "That part's true. But not for an institute."

"For whom?"

"For me." He unlashed the roll, let her see the top sheet — careful pencil work, channel depths, bank heights, dates going back years. "I've been charting this basin on my own time since before your second blacklist. Because someone erased the last map. Officially, the earlier expedition never existed. Unofficially, its charts were burned, its members written out, its route struck from every archive." He rolled the paper closed again. "I'm rebuilding what they destroyed."

"Why?"

The rain thickened. Somewhere across the black water, a tree went down with a sound like a door closing on a very large room.

"Because maps are how you find people," he said. "And someone I was looking for was on that expedition."

Ulyana was quiet a long moment. He could feel her deciding how much of that to believe, weighing it against everything else she knew about him — the timing of his arrival at the confluence, the way he'd watched Halvard, the questions he asked that a flood-line mapper had no business asking.

"You chose our meeting," she said finally. Not a question.

"I chose the confluence. You chose the rest."

"Convenient."

"Everything about this river is convenient for someone," he said. "The question is always who's paying."

She laughed — short, hard, more exhale than humor. Then she picked up the bailing cup and handed it to him. "Then bail. If you're going to be on my boat, you pull weight."

* * *

**Ulyana**

They bailed in shifts through the worst of the pre-storm rain, and somewhere in the third hour, bailing became talking, and talking became the closest thing to confession either of them could afford.

She told him about the first blacklist because he asked the right question — not *what did you do* but *what did you see.* There was a difference, and after twenty years on rivers, she trusted people who knew it.

"A shaft," she said. "Northern channels, seven years back. Mineral survey, legitimate on paper. We broke camp early to beat a storm and cut through a gorge that wasn't on my charts. At the bottom of the gorge there was a doorway. Cut stone, regime-era markings, sealed with poured concrete from the inside." She turned the cup over, drained it over the side. "My client photographed it. Three days later his report came back stamped and his company dissolved him. I filed my own account with the border authority — honest mistake, I thought they'd want to know. Six weeks later I was blacklisted for falsifying route documentation. My account was the falsification. The doorway never existed."

"And the second time?"

"Second time I was smarter. I said nothing. Guided a supply run for a mapping team that had permits signed by a deputy minister. Halfway upriver I recognized the gorge." She stared at the rain. "I got them out. All of them. Refused the return leg, reported the route deviation, and got blacklisted for abandoning a contract. The deputy minister's signature turned out to be forged — by his office. They forge well. It's practically a ministry of its own."

Ivo bailed, listened, said nothing for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was careful. "You keep coming back."

"Don't romanticize it. I come back because it's the only work left. Blacklisted guides don't get choice; they get offers nobody clean will take." But even as she said it, she heard the shape of the lie — not in the words, in the weariness underneath them. She had taken this contract with a bad boat, a lying client, and a forecast she didn't trust, and some part of her had known exactly what she was walking toward. Some part of her had been walking toward it for seven years.

"What about you?" she said. "Who was on the erased expedition?"

The rain answered first — a wall of it, sudden, total, the monsoon arriving hours ahead of every forecast, as if the sky had decided to stop pretending. The river rose around them in visible surges, brown water climbing the banks, swallowing the marker posts from the old expedition one by one. Ulyana watched a post she'd tied off to that morning disappear, and felt the boat swing loose into the current, and understood that decisions were now being made for them.

Ivo fought the tiller. She fought the tarp over the cargo. Between gusts he shouted the answer she'd asked for:

"My sister!"

* * *

**Ivo**

Her name was Lira. He didn't say it aloud — saying it made it real in ways he wasn't ready for, and real things could be taken away twice. He said *my sister* and let Ulyana fill in the rest, and she did, because she was the kind of person who filled silences with the right questions instead of wrong assumptions.

"How long ago?"

"Six years. Survey assistant. Hydrographer's apprentice, technically. Twenty-three years old and certain she was going to redraw the basin." The bow swung wide and he hauled it straight, arms burning. "Last letter came from a trading post two days below the confluence. After that, nothing. No body, no effects returned, no record. The expedition was struck from the rolls eight months later. Every family got the same letter: personnel records lost in a warehouse fire."

"There was no fire."

"There's never a fire." He gritted the words against the wind. "There's a filing cabinet and a man with a stamp."

Ulyana braced beside him, shoulder to the gunwale, and together they held the bow into the current while the river tried to turn them broadside. This was the arithmetic she'd promised him earlier, except now it included a variable neither of them had priced: the storm had come early, the leak was holding by a hand's width, and the only shelter within reach was whatever the drowned forest offered before full dark.

"There," she shouted, pointing upstream-left where the canopy thickened over a rise of higher ground — a levee crest, maybe, or the shoulder of an old embankment. "If we can reach the lee side, we can tie off and wait out the worst of it."

"And if the seam lets go overnight?"

"Then we take turns sleeping," she said. "One of us bails while the other dreams."

They reached the lee as the light failed completely, working the poles against the current in shifts, and Ivo learned something about Ulyana Dravenmoor that no file in any ministry contained: she was stronger than she looked and more frightened than she showed, and both of those facts lived in her hands. Her hands never shook. Everything else did.

Tied off among the drowned trunks, engine dead, rain hammering the cover, they shared cold rice and the last of the tea, and the conversation resumed where the storm had interrupted it — quietly now, two people in a small dry-ish space telling each other the truth in installments.

"You said you chose the confluence," Ulyana said. "Why that spot? Why us?"

"Because Halvard's permit listed a resupply at the confluence trading post. I'd been watching the post for a week. I knew the survey was coming; I didn't know it carried a passenger." He paused. "When I saw you, I recognized the name. Dravenmoor. From the incident reports I'd bought off a clerk in the capital — the ones that were supposed to be burned with everything else." He looked at her directly for the first time since the storm began. "I needed a guide who'd already survived this river's worst trick. You were the only one on the water."

"So you used me."

"I hired you. With money I mostly didn't have." He shrugged, unashamed. "I've been funding this with savings and forgery. Call it what it is."

"Forgery."

"Clerks sell copies. Copies need provenance. Provenance needs paperwork. Paperwork needs a forger." He folded his hands. "I'm a very good forger. It's why the maps are good. Same discipline."

Ulyana absorbed this with the expression of a woman adding a new animal to a cage she'd believed secure. "You realize how that sounds. To someone with two blacklists."

"I realize exactly how it sounds. I also notice you haven't put me ashore."

"Because the boat leaks and the shore is worse."

"Because the boat leaks and the shore is worse," he agreed. "But also because you think I might be useful. And you're right. I know where the old expedition went. I've spent six years reconstructing their route. If your vanished survey followed it — and Halvard's journal says they did — then I know where they were headed when the river took them."

"Where?"

He told her. A branch channel, thirty kilometers north, marked on none of his charts until he'd drawn it himself from fragments: a fisherman's story, a drowned church bell, a stretch of bank where the reeds grew in a line too straight to be natural. The old expedition's last confirmed camp had been near it. After that, silence.

"The same place the doorway was?" she asked. "Seven years ago? The gorge?"

"Different channel. Same formation." He watched her face change. "You felt it too, didn't you. The pattern. The basin keeps having the same accident in different places."

Ulyana didn't answer immediately. She bailed the cup twice, slowly, and Ivo understood she was thinking, and that her thinking looked like work because for her it was.

"If I help you find them," she said at last, "and it turns out you're not what you say —"

"I am what I say. I'm just not only what I say."

"Nobody is." She set the cup down. "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch. And Ivo — if the seam goes, you wake up swimming, so sleep in your boots."

* * *

**Ivo**

He slept in his boots. He dreamed of his sister, which was new; usually he dreamed of paper.

In the dream she was sitting on a stilt porch drawing a map, and she looked up and said, "You're late," and he wanted to tell her he'd been trying for six years, that he'd sold his career and his name and most of his honesty to buy this far upriver, but in the dream there was no time for explanations because the water was coming through the floorboards, warm and patient, the way it comes for everything eventually.

He woke to Ulyana's hand on his shoulder and the specific quality of grey that precedes dawn, and to her voice, low and level, delivering information the way she delivered everything — without decoration.

"Seam's holding. Storm's worse. And there's a light on the water downstream."

He came alert instantly. "Patrol?"

"Too early to say. Single lamp, moving slow against the current, which no fisherman does at this hour in this weather." She handed him the field glasses. "Tell me what you see."

He saw a longboat, shallow-draft, six figures, a lamp hooded to throw its beam forward and nothing sideways. He saw the posture of the man at the bow — scanning, methodical, sweeping the banks in overlapping arcs. He had seen that posture before, in another life, on other rivers, wearing a different government's uniform.

"Sweeping for a boat," he said. "Or a body. Or witnesses."

"Or a journal." Ulyana lowered the glasses. "Halvard's journal names the site. If the survey's disappearance is starting to look like a problem instead of a tragedy, someone will want that journal found and everyone who's read it found with it."

"We could run."

"Downstream? Into them?" She shook her head. "Upstream we stay in the flooded timber where a longboat can't follow. Downstream we're a target on open water." She was already coiling rope, already moving, decision made, the way she made all of them — fast, final, without ceremony. "We go north. Toward the branch channel. Toward whatever took five men out of their boots."

Ivo took the pole. The current fought them and the rain kept falling and somewhere behind them the lamp moved on, patient as the water, patient as everything else in this country.

"You understand," he said, as the drowned forest swallowed them, "that we're not escaping anymore. We're approaching."

"I've known that since the camp," Ulyana said. "I just hadn't told you yet."

Ahead, through the rain, the reeds along the northern bank stood in a line too straight to be natural — and beyond them, half-heard under the storm, something that might have been a bell.
