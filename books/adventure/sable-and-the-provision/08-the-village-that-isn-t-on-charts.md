## 8. The Village That Isn't on Charts

The bell came again as they pushed through the reeds, and this time Ulyana could place it: not a church bell, not a buoy, but something struck by hand — a slow, deliberate tolling, spaced like a man counting.

"Someone's announcing us," she said. "Or warning them."

"Can those be different things here?"

"Everywhere, they're the same thing."

Beyond the reed line the water shallowed over what had once been a road — you could feel it through the pole, a smoothness under the silt, the ghost of gravel. Then the trees opened and the village was there, standing on the flood like a congregation that had learned to hold its breath.

Two dozen houses on stilts of blackened ironwood, some thirty feet tall, platforms lashed with rattan and roofed in tin flattened from fuel drums. Walkways ran between them at the height of the waterline, sagging catwalks of split bamboo. Lanterns hung under every eave, and in their light Ulyana counted faces — old men mending nets, women hauling rainwater, children perched on railings watching the skiff come in with the flat, unafraid attention of kids who had seen strangers before and knew exactly how much they were worth.

Nobody spoke. The bell stopped.

A woman stood on the nearest walkway, sixty maybe, arms crossed, hair gone white at the temples but black everywhere else, wearing a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too big. She looked at Ulyana the way Ulyana herself looked at weather: reading it, not greeting it.

"You're running the patrol," the woman said. Not a question.

"We are."

"How many behind you?"

"One lamp. Maybe two boats."

The woman nodded slowly, as if confirming a figure already entered in a ledger. "Tie off at the third house. Kesh will take your boat apart and hide the pieces. If you have papers, give them to me now."

Ulyana's hand went to the waterproof roll inside her jacket before her mind caught up — the blacklist notices, both of them, folded soft as cloth from years of carrying. The reflex of a woman who had been asked for papers in four languages on three borders.

"I don't give my papers to anyone," she said.

The woman almost smiled. "Good. Then you'll fit in." She turned and called something up the walkway, and the village exhaled back into motion around them — nets shaken out, a pot lid clattering, a child laughing somewhere above like the whole evening was an ordinary one.

Ivo leaned close as he tied the skiff. "No names," he murmured. "Did you notice? She didn't ask."

"I noticed."

"That worries me more than the patrol does."

"It should worry you less," Ulyana said. "Places that ask no names are places that have needed the silence themselves."

* * *

The third house belonged to the woman — her name, when someone finally used it, was Maren, though she offered it the way you offer a boat: useful, not precious. She fed them fish stew with river greens and a sourness Ulyana couldn't identify, and she did it without ceremony, setting the bowls down and sitting across from them with her own, eating in silence until the worst of their hunger was past.

Only then did she speak.

"The survey team," she said. "Government boats, five men, one woman. You were their guide."

Ulyana set down her spoon. "How do you know that?"

"Because they passed through here eleven days ago. Because Halvard bought rope from us and lied about why he needed so much. And because the patrol has been asking after them since." Maren's eyes were steady, unhurried. "You're the guide. So either you lost them, or they left you. Which is it?"

"They left," Ulyana said. "In the night. No tracks. No bodies. Boots gone, weapons left."

Something moved through the house then — not just Maren's face but the walls themselves, a stillness, the particular quiet of people listening through bamboo. Ulyana realized there were others on the platform outside, or below it, and that everything said here traveled.

"And you went looking," Maren said.

"The journal said to."

"What journal?"

"A dead man's journal. From the first expedition."

Maren put down her spoon. For a long moment she studied Ulyana with an expression Ulyana had last seen on her own face in a mirror, years ago, in a border town that wouldn't have her: the look of someone deciding how much truth a stranger can carry without dropping it.

"Come," Maren said finally. "There's something you should see before you eat any more of my stew. It goes down easier after."

* * *

They climbed. The village stacked itself upward as the water rose each year — new walkways built over old ones, so that climbing through it was climbing through its own history, decade by decade, platform over platform, until Maren stopped at a house near the top whose stilts were thickest and whose door wore a lock older than anything else Ulyana had seen on the river.

Inside, dry and dim, the room held no furniture worth mentioning. It held paper.

Crates of it. Oilcloth-wrapped bundles, tin boxes soldered shut, ledger books stacked spine-out like firewood. And on the wall, pinned in rows, maps — dozens of them, hand-drawn, layered, corrected, some so old the ink had gone brown, some so new the pencil shone.

Ulyana stepped forward before she could stop herself. She was a guide; maps were scripture. And these were wrong in the way that made her heart beat faster — every one of them showed the basin as it had been *before*, channels that no longer existed, villages that weren't drowned yet, and in the northeast quadrant, where the government charts showed nothing but blank floodplain, a dense knot of annotation. Survey marks. Depth soundings. A route.

She knew that route. She had poled a government team down a version of it six years ago, the job that ended with her first blacklist, and she had never understood why the orders took them where they took them.

"The first expedition," Ivo said quietly beside her. He'd gone very still, the way he did when he was working hard at seeming calm. "These are theirs."

"Some of them," Maren said. "Some are ours. We've been adding to theirs since." She lifted a tin box, blew dust from the lid, and set it open on the crate between them.

Rations. Sealed tins, waxed crackers, salt in glazed jars, medical kits with gauze still white. Decades old and preserved like relics, because that was what they were.

"When the first expedition understood what was going to happen to them — that their work would be erased, their names struck, some of them perhaps worse — they did two things," Maren said. "They hid their records where the government would never think to look: with the people the government had already forgotten. And they left supplies. For whoever came after. Whoever survived long enough to need them."

"The provision," Ulyana said.

Maren's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You've heard the word."

"The elders—" Ivo started, and stopped, because Ulyana had heard it too, years ago, from a dying boatman on her first trip down this river, a word he'd repeated like a prayer: *the provision, the provision, wait for the provision.*

"It means what it says," Maren said. "Provision. What is provided. They meant it for anyone the state decided to erase. Blacklisted guides. Disappeared clerks. Families of the taken. Anyone who ends up on this river with nothing but the clothes they're hunted in." Her voice didn't rise; it didn't need to. "We have kept it forty years. We have never once had to use it for ourselves, because we are careful, and because we are not on any chart, and because everyone here gave up their name willingly when they came."

Ivo was staring at the maps on the wall, and his hand had drifted toward one in particular — the newest, the one with fresh pencil — and Ulyana watched him stop himself, watched the calculation run behind his face like current under ice.

"And now?" Ulyana asked. "Now that we're here, and the patrol is behind us, and whatever took five people out of their boots is out there in the water — do we get to use it?"

Maren closed the tin box.

"You already are using it," she said. "The stew. The roof. The hidden boat. That's the provision too. It was never only tins." She looked at Ulyana with those weather-reading eyes. "It was always meant to be spent on people like you. The question is whether you'll spend yourselves the way they hoped — on what's buried out there — or whether you'll take the rations and run south like sensible people."

Outside, the rain thickened against the tin roofs, and beneath it, far off across the black water, Ulyana thought she heard the lamp again. Or a bell. Or nothing at all.

Down on the lowest walkway, a child began ringing the evening bell — slow, deliberate, counting — and Ulyana understood at last what it was for. Not warning. Not announcement.

Roll call. Every soul accounted for, every night, in a village that officially did not exist.

"Sleep," Maren said. "Tomorrow I'll show you the rest of what they left. There's a page in the first expedition's logbook with your river on it, guide — the route you were ordered down six years ago. And there's a note in the margin, in my grandmother's hand, that you'll want to read before you decide anything."

"What does it say?"

Maren paused at the door, lantern light catching the white at her temples.

"It says: *If the second boat comes asking, tell them the provision was never meant to be kept. It was meant to be finished.*"

* * *

Ulyana didn't sleep. She sat on the walkway outside Maren's house with her jacket collar up and the rain coming down soft and endless, and she listened to the village breathe — the creak of stilts, the mutter of generators, a baby crying and being sung to, life stacked on top of life on top of the drowned world.

Ivo found her there sometime past midnight, lowering himself onto the wet bamboo beside her with a cup of something hot he'd charmed out of a neighbor. He handed it over without comment.

"Your hands were shaking at dinner," he said.

"Were they."

"When Maren opened the tin box. Just the left one. Small tremor." He blew on his own cup. "Cold, or the journal cipher, or the fact that a village full of ghosts just adopted us. I can treat any of the three."

"None of the three." Ulyana drank. The cup held tea brewed from something piney and local, bitter enough to be honest. "Six years ago I poled a team down this river and when we came back I was blacklisted for asking what we'd found. Two years ago I got blacklisted again for refusing a contract I'd already guessed was the same contract. My whole adult life I've been paid to move and punished for stopping." She turned the cup in her hands. "And tonight a woman who doesn't exist gave me stew off a shelf stocked by people who were erased, and told me it was mine if I wanted it. Do you know what that does to a person who's been in transit for twenty years?"

"No," Ivo said. "But I'm beginning to suspect I'm about to find out."

He was quiet a moment. Down the walkway, the last lanterns were being shuttered one by one, the village folding itself into sleep.

"My sister drew maps," he said suddenly, to the rain rather than to Ulyana. "That's all I'll say tonight. But tomorrow, when Maren shows us that logbook — watch my face, and don't ask me anything in front of her."

Ulyana looked at him. In the dark his charm was switched off, and what was underneath was younger and more frightened and more determined than anything he'd shown on the water.

"All right," she said.

"And Ulyana." He stood, brushing rain from his coat. "The margin note. *Meant to be finished.* You understand what that means. The provision isn't a cache. It's a debt. And tomorrow we find out who it's owed to."

Below them, far out on the flooded plain, the light appeared again — patient, unhurried, closer than it had been the night before — and this time it didn't pass by.

It stopped. Directly across the water from the village that wasn't on any chart, it hung there, burning steady through the rain, like something taking a long look before it decided.

Then, one by one, lanterns along the highest walkway began to go dark — the villagers dousing their own lights, silent, practiced, a community closing its eyes in the dark together — and Ulyana sat alone with her cooling tea, watching the light watch them, and understood that tomorrow's choice had already been narrowed to one question:

Whether the village's invisibility could survive being spent on her.
