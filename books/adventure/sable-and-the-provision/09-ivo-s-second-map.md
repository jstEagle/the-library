## 9. Ivo's Second Map

I had a rule about drawing in front of people. It wasn't superstition, exactly. A map is an admission — every line you ink is a line you've already walked or intend to walk, and either one tells a stranger more than you want them to know. So I drew at night, when the camp was asleep, and I kept the pages turned away from the firelight like a man shielding a wound.

The stilt house we'd been given was the largest in the village, raised on blackened pilings above water that breathed and shifted all night. Ulyana slept on the far side of the room with her back to the wall and her knife under her hand, which told me she'd been betrayed before by rooms like this one. Tamsin was gone — taken, held, baited — and Halvard was gone too, though gone meant something different for him now. That left the two of us and a floorboard that creaked no matter where you stepped, so I'd learned its language: three boards from the window, weight on the outside edge, slow.

I waited until the lamp guttered low. Then I unrolled my second map.

Not the flood chart I showed the world — that one was honest work, contour lines and current speeds, the kind of thing any cartographer would carry. The other map lived folded into eight panels inside my oilskin jacket, against my ribs, where body heat kept the paper from stiffening. I'd been drawing it for eleven years. It was the Sable basin as I imagined it, not as it was charted: every false channel marked in red, every drowned village named with names the government had struck from the record, and at the center, circled twice, a blank space where the old expedition had last reported before their transmissions stopped.

Eleven years of ink. My sister had been dead to the record for ten of them.

I worked by feel mostly, adding what the elders had told us that evening — a channel they called the Swallow's Throat, where the river ran backward during high water, and a stand of drowned ironwood that marked, they said, the edge of the old survey grid. My pencil moved in small strokes. The village below me creaked on its pilings like a ship at anchor, and somewhere out in the dark a fish jumped, once, and the river swallowed the sound.

That was when the board spoke.

Three boards from the window. Wrong rhythm. Weight on the inside edge. Someone who hadn't learned the floor yet — or someone who didn't care to.

Ulyana crossed the room without a lamp, which should have been impossible, and stood over me with her hair loose and her eyes already awake, the way some people are awake at three in the morning because they've never fully been asleep. She looked at the map. Not a glance. She read it, top to bottom, the way you'd read a letter addressed to someone else.

"That's not a flood chart," she said.

"No."

"Those aren't official markers." Her finger hovered over the red channels without touching them. "These villages have names. The charts I've seen call this whole stretch *uninhabited*."

"The charts are wrong on purpose," I said. "Erasure is a kind of mapping too. You just draw nothing where something used to be, and keep drawing nothing until everyone forgets there was ever anything to forget."

She sat down across from me, cross-legged, close enough that I could see the lamplight catch the scar along her jaw. She didn't ask permission. She didn't need to; the map was already open between us, and pretending otherwise would have insulted us both.

"How long have you been carrying this?"

"Eleven years."

"And the blank space?" She tapped the air above the double circle. "Center of everything. You've left it empty."

"Because I don't know what's there." I turned the pencil over in my fingers. "Or because I know exactly what's there and I can't make myself draw it. I haven't decided which."

Ulyana studied me for a long moment. She had a way of looking at a person like she was reading water — checking depth, current, what lay under the surface. Most people found it unnerving. I found it honest. Everyone else in my life had looked *through* me; she looked *at* me, and being seen was a currency I'd almost forgotten how to spend.

"You're not charting flood lines," she said. "You never were. The confluence — you intercepted us. Skiffs don't find boats on rivers that size by accident. You knew our route before we did."

"I did."

"Why?"

Here it was, then. The moment I'd been circling for days, the way you circle a channel you're afraid to enter. I could give her another half-truth — I was good at those, better than Halvard had ever been, and look where his lying got him. Or I could put the real thing on the table and see if she took it or burned it.

Vulnerability as currency. My sister taught me that, though she taught it from the wrong side of a disappearance.

"My sister was on the earlier expedition," I said. "The erased one. Nineteen years ago. Sixteen people went into this basin to do what your survey team claimed to be doing — actual hydrography, actual charts. They were good. The best the service had. And then the government realized what they were finding, and the expedition stopped existing. No recall order in the archives. No inquiry. Their families were told they'd drowned. Compensation paid, records sealed, names removed from the rolls within a year." I smoothed the map flat with both hands. "My sister was Mira Kestrel. Assistant cartographer. Twenty-six years old. She wrote me letters for six weeks and then the letters stopped, and then the man who delivered the last one told me she'd died in a flash flood, and he said it while looking at the floor, because even the liars couldn't quite meet my eyes."

Ulyana didn't say anything for a while. When she finally did, her voice was lower.

"They told you flood."

"They told everyone flood. It's the basin's favorite lie. The river takes what the state wants gone, and nobody questions a drowning here — the Sable has an appetite, everyone knows that." I laughed, and it came out wrong, bitter at the edges. "I became a cartographer because of her. I learned false channels because if she'd known the false channels, maybe she'd have come home. And then I learned the truth instead: she probably knew them better than anyone alive. She didn't drown. She was disappeared. There's a difference, and the difference is a filing cabinet in a ministry basement."

"You came back for her."

"I came back for proof. Those aren't the same thing, but they travel together." I tapped the blank space again. "Whatever surfaced after the last deluge — whatever your survey team was sent to confirm and report nothing about — it's connected to where Mira's expedition ended. Same grid. Same blank space on every chart since. The government doesn't send five officials and forty cases of equipment to measure rainfall, Ulyana. I watched Halvard load that boat. I counted the crates. Nobody packs that much brass and paper to chase a flood."

Her jaw tightened. "You knew what they were before I did."

"I suspected. You *knew* — you read the river better than any of them, you saw the marker posts from the old expedition, you recognized the route from the job that cost you your first blacklist. We were both reading the same water. We just pretended not to, for different reasons." I paused. "Yours was pay. Mine was access."

"And now?"

"Now the team is gone, Tamsin is bait, and the patrol is hunting witnesses, which means us." I began folding the map along its worn creases. "Which means the question isn't whether we go after the site. The question is whether we go after it together, or whether one of us goes alone and the other one becomes a story the river tells."

Ulyana reached out and stopped my hands. Just that — her palm flat over the folding paper, holding it open.

"Show me the route," she said. "Mira's route. From the last transmission to the blank space."

So I did. I spread the map wide across the floor between us, weighting the corners with two tin cups and a coil of rope, and I traced it for her: the confluence where the letters had last been postmarked, the drowned ironwood stand, the Swallow's Throat running backward at high water, the ridge of silt the elders called the Sleeping Levee, and past all of it, the gap where the old grid simply stopped — sixteen sets of coordinates, then silence, then nothing drawn for nineteen years.

"She was here," I said, my finger resting on the last coordinate. "This is where the final letter came from. Postmark dated the ninth of the wet season. She described the light on the water. She said the basin was 'more beautiful than the maps deserved.' That was the last sentence. Eleven days later, the service declared them lost."

Ulyana was quiet for a long time. Then she said something I didn't expect.

"I kept a photograph," she said. "From before. I still have it. I've never been able to explain why I carry it, and I've never been able to throw it away." She looked at the map instead of at me. "I understand carrying paper now. What it costs. Why you do it anyway."

"It's not paper," I said. "It's ballast. You carry it so you don't drift."

Outside, the rain thickened — the monsoon arriving in earnest now, drumming on the thatch, filling the dark with sound. The river rose beneath the floorboards, patient and enormous. Somewhere south, a patrol boat was moving through that rain with searchlights and a prisoner, and somewhere north, a blank space waited to be filled with whatever had actually happened to sixteen people and five officials and one sister who described beautiful light in her last letter.

Ulyana rolled up her bedroll and moved it closer to the map side of the room, which was as close to a treaty as either of us had ever signed with anyone.

"Dawn," she said. "We leave at dawn. Upstream, through the Swallow's Throat, while the water's still high enough to run it backward."

"That channel will try to kill us."

"Everything on this river tries to kill us." She pulled her knife from under her hand and laid it beside the map, an offering or a guarantee. "But we'll have something the others didn't. Your sister's route, and mine, and eleven years of ink that finally has somewhere to go."

I looked at the blank space one more time before we slept. Tomorrow I would draw it — or learn why I never could.

And that was when the lamp flared once, guttered, and died — and in the sudden dark, from the water below the floorboards, came the sound of oars.
