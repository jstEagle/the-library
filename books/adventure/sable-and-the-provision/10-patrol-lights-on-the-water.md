## 10. Patrol Lights on the Water

The storm that had been threatening all morning finally broke at dusk, and Ulyana almost welcomed it. Rain meant the patrol boats would run slow. Rain meant their own wake would vanish into chop. She'd spent eleven years learning what weather forgave, and a monsoon squall forgave almost everything except panic.

They'd left the stilt village before first light, over the elders' objections. The old woman named Sefa had pressed dried fish and a clay jar of river water into Ulyana's hands and said only, "Come back before the high water takes the low channels." Not if. Before. As though she already knew how this would go.

Now they were three hours downriver from the village, tucked into a drowned forest where the flood had swallowed an orchard whole — dead mango trees standing to their shoulders in brown water, branches bare and black as wire. Ivo had found the pocket. Of course he had. He read drowned country the way other men read street signs.

"Kill the lamp," he said, though it was already out. "Kill everything."

Ulyana shipped the oars and let the boat drift among the trees, nudging it with one hand against a trunk thick as a church pillar. The rain came down in ropes. Above them, through the canopy of branches, the sky was the color of wet slate, going darker by the minute.

Ivo sat across from her with the journal wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, both arms around it like a man holding a sleeping child. He hadn't let go of it since the cache. Since the photograph. Since the last frame showed his sister alive, squinting into a sun that had set twenty years ago.

"They'll sweep the main channel first," Ulyana said. "Then the false channels. They'll want witnesses more than they want us specifically."

"You don't believe that."

"No," she admitted. "But it's useful to say."

Lightning flickered far off to the north, silent, like something turning over in its sleep. Ulyana counted the seconds out of habit. Too far to matter yet. Storms moved fast up here, but not that fast.

She thought about the safe channel south. She'd been thinking about it for two days now, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. South was the delta, the delta was the coast, the coast was a freighter and a new name and a country that had never heard of Ulyana Dravenmoor. She knew the route the way she knew her own hands. Eleven years of running built routes the way rivers built silt: patiently, everywhere, without being asked.

And Tamsin was somewhere north of them, in a patrol post, alive. Probably. Radio fragments were not promises. But the voice on the intercepted band had said *one survivor, keep her breathing* — and *keep her breathing* was language you used about bait.

Ulyana put her hand flat on the gunwale and felt the river breathing under it.

"We should talk about what happens when they find us," Ivo said quietly.

"If."

"When. You're the one who taught me the river reads better than people." He tilted his head toward the darkening channel behind them. "Listen."

She listened. Under the rain, under the wind worrying the dead branches, there was another sound — engines. More than one. Low, steady, disciplined, the kind of engine noise that came from government money rather than smuggler desperation. Coming up the basin from the south.

Ulyana's stomach did something cold and old.

"Off the channel," she breathed. "Deep in the trees. Now."

They paddled without lights, without splashing, drawing the skiff back among the drowned trunks until the branches closed overhead like a cage. Ulyana wedged the bow between two mango trunks and tied off to a root that surfaced from the water like a knuckle. Then she pulled the canvas tarp half over the boat — not covering them, just breaking their outline — and settled into the bilge with her back against the hull.

Ivo crouched beside her, close enough that she could hear him breathing. He'd stopped talking. That was how she knew he was frightened. Ivo talked when he was comfortable, talked when he was calculating, talked even when silence served him better. Silence on him looked like a man wearing someone else's coat.

The engine noise grew.

* * *

Two boats came up the main channel with searchlights already burning, white columns sweeping the banks, the water, the drowned fields on either side. Government hulls — the flat-bottomed patrol craft the Ministry used, gray-green, each carrying six or eight men in dark slickers. A third boat trailed behind, smaller, faster, running without lights.

Ulyana went very still.

Not because of the boats. Because of the man standing in the bow of the lead one.

Even at two hundred meters, even through rain and searchlight glare, she knew the silhouette. Broad shoulders gone soft with desk years. The way he stood with his hands clasped behind his back, weight forward on the balls of his feet, like a man inspecting a field he owned. Deputy Director Aurel Voss. The man who had signed her second blacklist — who had read her statement about the Karst expedition with polite attention and then, with the same pen, erased her from two countries' worth of legal existence.

He hadn't aged well. Or maybe he'd aged exactly as much as a man ages when his work is making people disappear.

The searchlight swung across the drowned orchard. Ulyana pressed lower. The beam passed over the tarp, over the branches, caught the rain in its column like a curtain of white insects, and moved on.

Voss's voice carried over the water. He wasn't shouting; he didn't need to. It was the voice of a man accustomed to being relayed. "Sweep the east bank. Every structure, every hull, every fire pit. If anyone's sheltering in this basin, the storm will have driven them to ground. Find them before the high water does."

A lieutenant repeated the order. Engines throttled. The second boat peeled away toward the east bank, its light raking across the flooded fields.

Ivo's hand found her wrist in the dark. Not gripping. Just resting there. Asking a question.

"I know him," Ulyana whispered.

"I assumed. Your face changed." A pause. "How?"

"He signed my blacklist. Both times, technically — the second one was his office. The first time, I never saw who held the pen." She kept her eyes on the lead boat, tracking it through gaps in the branches. "Karst expedition. Six years ago. I guided a mineral survey into the high plateau. Halfway in, I realized we weren't surveying minerals. We were confirming a site. I told the team leader I'd walk out and report it. Two days later our camp burned, our records burned, and when I made it back over the border, there was a warrant waiting with my name on it. Arson. Endangerment. Things I didn't do."

"And Voss?"

"Voss interviewed me for nine hours. Very courteous. Very thorough. At the end he thanked me for my cooperation and signed the paper." Her jaw tightened. "He told me something at the end of that interview. He said, 'Miss Dravenmoor, some things are lost on purpose, and the losing is the point.' I've carried that sentence for six years like a stone in my boot."

The rain thickened. Through it, the third boat — the small, fast one — cut across their view of the channel, and Ulyana saw that Voss had transferred to it. It ran north. Deeper into the basin. Toward the confluence. Toward the site.

Toward Tamsin.

"There," Ivo breathed. "That's the forward post direction. The radio fragment—"

"I heard it too."

They watched the small boat's running light shrink and vanish into the northern rain. The two larger patrol craft continued their methodical work, sweeping banks, prodding structures with poles, occasionally firing a flare to burn away the dark over some stretch of reed bed. Methodical. Patient. Funded.

One of the flares burst directly overhead — close enough that the drowned orchard lit up like noon for four full seconds. Ulyana didn't move. Neither did Ivo. The light fell through the branches in coins and shadows, painted their faces white, found the tarp, the tied-off bow, the still water around them — and slid off. Dead trees. Dead water. Nothing the light could name.

The flare died. Darkness rushed back in, thicker than before.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Ivo exhaled, very slowly, and Ulyana realized she'd been holding her breath along with him.

"They're hunting witnesses," he said. "Not us specifically. Anyone who's seen the basin. Seen the marker posts. Seen—" He touched the oilcloth bundle. "Seen what the journal describes."

"They're hunting the journal." Ulyana turned to look at him. In the dark his face was just an arrangement of shadows, but she could feel the shape of his attention, aimed at her like the searchlights aimed at the banks. "Halvard's journal is the only written record outside the vault. If Voss gets it, the provision means nothing. The evidence burns, the inquiry dies, and everyone in that archive drowns twice."

"Then we don't let him get it."

"It's not that simple."

"It never is. Say it anyway."

Ulyana leaned her head back against the hull and watched the rain come down through the branches. Somewhere south, the safe channel waited, wide and quiet and empty of searchlights. Eleven years of instinct pointed at it like a compass needle. *Run. Run now, run light, run alone. You've done it before. You're good at it.*

But the compass needle had been wrong before, too. It had pointed her out of the Karst plateau, out of two countries, out of every room where someone might ask her to stay. Running had kept her alive. It had also, she was beginning to understand, kept her *small* — a woman sized precisely to fit through exits.

"Tamsin is at that post," she said. "Bait. And Voss is sailing straight to her."

"Yes."

"If we go after her, we're not running anymore. There's no exit route out of a patrol post. You either win or you're in a cell, or worse — you're in the basin when they seal it." She said it flatly, laying out the arithmetic the way she'd lay out a route. "If we run south tonight, we live. Probably. And Tamsin becomes a line in someone's report. *Witness eliminated.* Like Halvard. Like the others."

Ivo was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its charm entirely, which somehow made it more trustworthy. "My sister stayed. Everyone tells me she was taken, or tricked, or lost. But I've read the journal now. I think she chose it. And I've spent twenty years running toward that choice trying to undo it." He shifted, and the oilcloth crackled. "You asked once why I know the false channels. Because she mapped them. Because learning her maps was the closest I could get to learning her." A breath. "I'm not telling you to stay. I'm telling you I can't run anymore either. Whatever you choose — I'm done choosing exits."

The patrol boats were moving off now, their lights dwindling down the channel, sweeping the eastern shore. The rain softened from ropes to needles. In the silence they left behind, the basin sounded enormous — water dripping from ten thousand branches, the river sliding past in the dark, patient as anything that has already won.

Ulyana looked north, where Voss's boat had gone. Then south, where the safe channel opened like a door.

Eleven years ago she'd learned that survival was the only thing worth paying for. Lately the price had started to feel different. Higher. Paid out in names she couldn't remember anymore, in rooms she'd left before anyone could ask her to remain, in a photograph she'd packed and unpacked a hundred times without ever looking at it straight.

"The post will be lightly manned while the boats are out sweeping," she said at last. "That's not sentiment. That's tactics. If we're going to do something stupid, the stupid window is open right now, and it closes when those boats come home."

Ivo's smile appeared out of the dark. "So we're doing something stupid."

"We're doing something tactical that will look stupid in the report." She reached down and took up the oars. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. "North. Quiet water along the west bank. And Ivo — if I tell you to run, you run. One of us should survive this to testify."

"Noted," he said, taking the other oar. "Ignored, but noted."

They backed the skiff out of the drowned orchard, slow as a secret, and turned its bow north — toward the searchlights, toward the post, toward the man with the pen. Behind them the safe channel slid away into the rain, and Ulyana found, to her own astonishment, that she didn't watch it go.

Ahead, far off through the murk, a single light burned on the water where no light should be. Steady. Green. A post beacon — or an invitation.

She rowed toward it.
