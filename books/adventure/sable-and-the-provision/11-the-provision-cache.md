## 11. The Provision Cache

The green light wasn't a beacon. It was a lantern hanging from a drowned church bell tower, and it had been burning long enough to grow a beard of moss down its wire.

Ulyana killed the skiff's lantern and let them drift the last hundred meters, paddle across her knees, listening. Rain on water. Rain on tin — the church roof, somewhere above the flood line. Rain on leaves. Nothing else. No engine, no voices, no boots on wood.

"You see it too," Ivo said. Not a question. He'd been staring at the tower for a full minute without blinking.

"Someone wanted it found." She didn't like that. Things that wanted to be found were usually bait, and bait was usually set by people with pens. "Or wanted it *seen*. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"Seen means it's a warning. Found means it's an inheritance."

Ivo turned his head slowly, and in the dark his face was all angles and rain. "Which do we want it to be?"

"Whichever one we can survive."

She brought the skiff alongside the bell tower's base, where a stone ledge broke the water at knee height, crusted with old flood lines like the rings of a tree. The tower's door had once opened onto dry ground. Now it opened onto a black rectangle of water that breathed in and out with the river's pulse.

The cipher from Halvard's journal had taken them two days and one near-drowning to crack. *The provision sleeps where the river keeps its first promise.* Ulyana had assumed it was poetry. Then Ivo, hunched over the soaked pages by lantern light, had gone very still and said, "First promise. The first thing the surveyors marked on the old charts. Every expedition marks the same thing first — a fixed point. High ground. A church."

The bell tower of Saint Aldric, drowned in the second year of the reservoir, according to a chart so old its paper had gone the color of tea.

Ulyana tied off the painter to a rusted iron ring and stepped onto the ledge. The stone held. She'd learned a long time ago never to trust stone that held — stone that held was stone that hadn't been tested — but there was nothing for it. She went up.

The church's interior was reachable through a window on the second level, where the flood had once crested and left a tide mark across the saints' faces. She hauled herself through, boots first, and lay on the sloped roof of the vestry catching her breath while the rain drummed the tiles above like a thousand small insistent fingers.

Ivo came through a minute later, wetter, quieter. He crouched beside her and didn't speak. He was doing his listening thing again — the thing he did where his whole body went still and his eyes went somewhere else, some interior map only he could read. She'd stopped finding it unsettling somewhere around the third day. She'd never admit when.

"Below," he said finally. "The vestry floor. There's an air gap under it — I can hear the difference when the water moves."

"You can hear—"

"Shh."

He wasn't listening to the water. He was listening to the *absence* of it. Ulyana had known river men who could do that — read the hollows through their ears — but she'd never known a cartographer who could. Then again, she'd never known a cartographer who watched government men the way Ivo watched them, either.

They pried up a vestry board with a boat hook and the flat of her knife. Beneath: darkness, and a smell. Not rot. Something drier. Paper. Oilcloth. Old metal.

And cold air, moving. The cache breathed.

* * *

IVO

The space under the vestry was a stone vault the size of a chapel crypt, and it was dry.

Not damp-dry. Not sealed-tomb-dry. *Kept* dry — there was a system, crude and brilliant: a clay channel cut into the floor that fed seepage down to a sump, a vent shaft disguised as a drain, and walls lined with waxed canvas that someone had maintained, because the canvas was newer than the vault. Someone had come back. More than once. Ivo's hands were shaking and he made them stop.

Rations in oilcloth, stacked in ranks — hardtack, salt meat, tins with labels worn to ghosts. Medical stores. A crate of lamp oil. And against the far wall, under its own canvas, the thing he'd crossed a country and lied to five strangers and nearly drowned twice to find:

Records.

Dozens of them. Field journals, survey ledgers, photographic plates in paper sleeves, a bundle of letters tied with wire, and — his breath went strange in his chest — a chart case, brass-cornered, with a stenciled number he knew. He knew it because he'd spent eleven years looking for that number in archives that swore it didn't exist.

Expedition Nine. The erased one. The one that had gone into the basin in the spring of a bad year and come out of every record in the country, its personnel struck, its findings burned, its route redacted down to a single black rectangle on a single surviving map.

His sister had been on it. Mira Kestrel, assistant surveyor, twenty-six years old, last seen in a photograph holding a ranging pole and laughing at something outside the frame.

He'd been seventeen when the government men came to the house. He remembered the shape of their briefcases more than their faces. He remembered his mother's hands. He remembered that afterward, the family photograph album had a rectangular gap in it, and no one ever said why, and no one ever said her name again, and Ivo had learned to become a person who was very good at maps and very bad at staying anywhere.

"Kestrel." Ulyana's voice, low. She'd come down the ladder without him hearing — of course she had. "How much of this can we carry?"

All of it. He wanted to say all of it. He wanted to sit down in this dry stone room with his sister's ghost and never leave.

"Two cases' worth," he said. "The journals. The plates. The letters." His voice came out level, which he counted as a professional achievement. "The rations we take what we need. The rest stays — someone built this to last. It'd be a sin to break it open for nothing."

"Sin." Ulyana almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth he'd seen from her since the village. "You've been spending too much time with the elders."

"They're good teachers."

He knelt at the chart case. His fingers had gone clumsy, which annoyed him, and he made them be precise, which took longer. The latches were stiff with disuse but sound. Inside, in a nest of oiled wool:

Charts. The originals. Expedition Nine's full survey — the basin, the false channels, the drowned villages, and the site, marked and annotated in a hand he recognized from a single surviving letter his mother had hidden inside a Bible for thirty years. *Mira's* hand. He'd know it anywhere. He'd traced it off that letter so many times he could draw it in the dark, and here it was, whole, alive, covering yards of paper.

And beneath the charts, a photograph.

He knew before he turned it over. Some part of him knew from the weight of the paper, from the year of the stock, from the way the world had arranged itself in the seconds before he lifted it out of the wool — the way a river knows a waterfall before the sound arrives.

It was a group photograph, taken on a barge or a dock, eleven people in field clothes squinting into hard sunlight. Expedition Nine, whole. He found her the way you find a face in a crowd — suddenly, and then everywhere, as if she'd always been the only person in the picture.

Mira. Older than the photograph in his memory. Tanned, windburned, standing slightly apart from the others the way she always did, one hand shading her eyes. Laughing. Still laughing at something outside the frame, eleven years and a government erasure later.

And on the back, in pencil, in that hand:

*Last frame before we went down. Don't come looking, little brother. But if you're reading this, you didn't listen, and I'm glad. The provision is for whoever's stubborn enough to need it. — M.*

Ivo sat down on the cold stone floor of a drowned church and held a photograph of his sister and did not make a sound, because he had spent eleven years learning to do that too, and because the alternative was a sound that would never stop.

She'd been alive when it was taken. Alive, and dry, and laughing, on the far side of the black rectangle.

*Don't come looking.*

*You didn't listen, and I'm glad.*

He became aware of Ulyana crouched nearby, not touching him, not speaking, just present, the way you stand near a wild animal you don't want to spook. He almost laughed at that. He was the wild animal. He'd always been the wild animal; the maps were just the cage he built around himself.

"She's in the last frame," he said. His voice was wrecked and he let it be. "Alive. They took this the day they went down to the site."

Ulyana took the photograph when he offered it. She studied it for a long time — not Mira, he realized. The others. Her eyes moved across the eleven faces like a reader scanning a page for one word.

"Your sister," she said. "And these two—" she tapped two men at the edge of the frame, "—were on the first job. The one that got me blacklisted the first time. They were the ones who told me to forget what I'd seen." She looked up. "They were still alive when I knew them. Years after this photo."

The arithmetic of it landed between them and neither of them flinched from it. Down was not death. Down was something else. The site didn't kill people; it *kept* them. And the government didn't erase the dead — the dead made no trouble. It erased the living.

"Hope," Ulyana said quietly, "is worse than grief. Grief you can carry. Hope carries you somewhere you didn't agree to go."

"You're telling me not to hope."

"I'm telling you what it costs. I've paid it." She handed the photograph back with a care that surprised him. "Hope anyway. But know the price going in."

* * *

ULYANA

They worked by lamplight in the dry dark, and Ulyana let herself, for one hour, feel something like peace.

It was an unfamiliar sensation and she distrusted it on principle, but there was no arguing with the facts of it: a dry room, a wall of food, a task with clear edges. Sort, pack, protect. Work she'd been doing her whole life in one form or another — keeping things alive through weather. Ivo moved through the cache like a man in a cathedral, and she let him have his cathedral. She took the rations and the medical crate and the practical business of survival, because that was her half of any church.

It was in the ration crate that she found the thing that ended the peace.

A radio. Government make, recent vintage, wrapped in a soldier's blanket — not expedition stock, not Nine's. Someone else had found this cache. Someone from the survey team. Halvard, or one of his five. They'd stashed it here and meant to come back for it.

It was still powered. Still tuned.

She almost didn't touch it. Then she remembered the green lantern burning over black water, the invitation of it, and thought: whoever left this wanted the cache *watched*. And a watched cache watches back.

She turned the volume to its lowest whisper and held the set to her ear.

Static. The long, oceanic static of an open band in monsoon country. And beneath it, threading through like a fish under ice, a voice. A woman's voice, repeating, with the flat exhaustion of someone who has said the words too many times:

*—any station, any station, this is Survey Team Six, member Tamsin Vey, held at forward post Kestrel— no. Post Kestrel is— this is Tamsin Vey, I am at the forward post, I am— they're using me, do you understand, they're using me as—*

The signal dissolved. Came back. Dissolved.

Ulyana sat very still in the lamplight with a dead government's bread and a living woman's voice in her hands.

Ivo was watching her from across the vault, the photograph still in his grip, his face doing the thing it did when he'd already read her mind and was waiting for her to catch up.

"How much did you hear?" she asked.

"Enough." He came and crouched beside her. "Tamsin Vey. The junior hydrologist. She was the one who argued with Halvard about the route — I liked her." A pause. "The forward post. That's the patrol. That's *him*."

"I know whose post it is."

"They're not holding her. They're *baiting* with her." His voice had gone quiet and hard, the charm burned off it. "Bait for whoever has the journal. Bait for us."

"Yes."

"And you're going."

It wasn't a question, and that was the worst of it, because she hadn't decided yet — she was still standing at the fork in herself, the runner and the other one, the one she'd only just met — and he'd already seen the answer in her face before she'd found it in her chest.

She looked down at the radio. At the woman's voice caught in it like a moth under glass.

*They're using me, do you understand—*

"Yes," Ulyana said. "I'm going."

Ivo nodded slowly, and began, without another word, to repack the cases — lighter now, faster, making room.

Outside, above the vestry, the rain changed its mind and came down all at once, and the river under the church floor lifted its voice, and somewhere south of them, in a post lit green against the dark, a woman who'd said *any station* too many times waited to find out if anyone had been stubborn enough to listen.
