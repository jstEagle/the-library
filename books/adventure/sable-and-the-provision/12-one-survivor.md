## 12. One Survivor

The radio came alive at dusk, and it was almost polite about it.

Ulyana had been nursing the boat's patched hull through the last of the drowned forest when Ivo's hand-crank set crackled from the bow — a frequency he shouldn't have known, a band width no cartographer would bother memorizing. She'd stopped asking him how he knew things two days ago. The answers were always technically true and completely useless.

*—repeat, all units, forward post Kestrel's Reach confirms custody of one (1) survey personnel, designation Tamsin Vole, alive and ambulatory. Holding pattern authorized until recovery of sensitive material. Bait status confirmed by—*

The transmission cut to static, then picked up again lower, a man's voice reading coordinates like a grocery list. Post Kestrel's Reach. The name snagged in her ear like a fishhook. She looked at Ivo. He was cranking the handle with the flat expression of a man listening to weather.

"Kestrel's Reach," she said.

"Common enough name for a post." He didn't look up. "There are probably six on the basin charts."

"There are four posts on the basin charts, and none of them is called that." She let that sit between them like a knife on a table. "You named yourself after a patrol station."

"I named myself after a bird," Ivo said mildly. "The government named a station after the bird. If anything, they're copying me."

Ulyana laughed despite herself — one short, hard sound, gone as fast as it came. Then she pulled the crank radio onto her knees and listened properly, because what the voice had said mattered more than any name: *one survey personnel, alive.* Tamsin Vole. She'd catalogued the team's gear on the first morning out of the border town — five bedrolls, five mess kits, and the junior hydrologist who'd been kind to her without needing anything back, who'd asked real questions about the river instead of pretending she already knew its answers. Tamsin Vole had been the only one of the five who'd looked at Ulyana like a person with a history rather than a hired pair of hands.

And now the government had her, and was saying so out loud, on an open band, in language so clean it could only be deliberate.

"They want us to hear this," Ulyana said.

Ivo kept cranking. "They want whoever has Halvard's journal to hear it."

"We have Halvard's journal."

"Yes," Ivo said. "We do."

* * *

They tied off that night in the hollow of a dead cottonwood, the trunk wide as a chapel door, its crown long since swallowed by the rising water. Ulyana rigged the tarp into a lean-to against the rain, which had settled in for the season the way an unwanted guest settles into your best chair. The monsoon wasn't coming anymore. It had arrived, unpacked, and started rearranging the furniture.

She read the journal again by lamplight while Ivo pretended to sleep. She'd read it four times now, and each pass peeled another layer off Halvard's careful bureaucratic handwriting until what remained underneath was just a frightened man taking dictation.

*Day 9. Confirmed subsurface anomaly at grid reference the Ministry will recognize. Ordered to log as sediment displacement. We did not log it as sediment displacement.*

*Day 11. Direct instruction: find the site, confirm the site, report nothing. The word "nothing" was underlined twice by the Undersecretary himself. I have begun to wonder what we were sent to not-report.*

*Day 13. Tamsin asked me point-blank why the survey coordinates match the 1971 expedition that officially never happened. I told her coincidence. She is twenty-six years old and she did not believe me, and I respected her more for it than I have respected anyone in this ministry in eleven years.*

Ulyana closed the journal. The rain drummed the tarp. Somewhere out in the black water, something moved — a branch, a caiman, the river settling its new borders. With the Sable in flood you learned to stop flinching at every sound, because if you flinched at everything you'd be exhausted before the thing worth flinching at finally came.

But the words wouldn't settle. *Bait status confirmed.* She turned the phrase over the way she'd turn a river stone, looking at what lived underneath.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: the patrol wanted the journal. They couldn't find it, because the people who'd taken the survey team hadn't taken the journal, and the journal had ended up folded inside her waterproof kit like a debt she hadn't agreed to cosign. So the government had done what governments do — they'd found the softest point of leverage available and put a light on it. One junior hydrologist, alive, held at a forward post, broadcast on a frequency only someone close would catch.

Come get her. Or don't, and live with it.

"You're doing the math," Ivo said from under his canvas. His eyes were open. They always were, when he claimed to be sleeping.

"The math is simple."

"The math is never simple. That's the first lie math tells." He sat up, and the lantern threw his shadow huge against the cottonwood's bark. "Walk it through anyway."

"One survivor means one witness. A witness who saw whatever took the others. Right now she's a liability in a holding cell." Ulyana fed another inch of wick to the flame. "Unless the people holding her decide she's more useful as bait. Which they've decided. So either the journal walks into that post, or Tamsin Vole stops being a person and starts being a problem, and problems at forward posts get solved with a shovel and a flood story."

"And the alternative?"

"The safe channel south." She said it evenly, because she'd earned the right to say things evenly. "Two days' paddle, past the old ferry crossing, into territory where my face isn't on a board yet. There's a fuel cache at the mission station. From there, a truck, a border, a life. I've run that arithmetic before. I'm good at it."

Ivo watched her across the small yellow circle of lamplight. Rain ran off the tarp edge in a beaded curtain between them.

"You've been running since before I met you," he said. "That's not a criticism. It's an observation, the way you'd note current speed. You're very fast. It's genuinely impressive. And every time you run, someone else stays behind and pays the fare."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is also not a currency the river accepts. But you asked about the math, and the math includes the part where you already know what happens to witnesses at forward posts, because you were nearly one."

She had been. Six years ago, the second blacklist — the job she still didn't talk about, the survey crew that went into the high tributaries and came back one man short, and the report she'd signed that said nothing because she'd been told to say nothing, and the officer who'd taken her signature and then taken her name off the rolls when she tried to unsign it. She knew exactly what happened to people who knew too much near government water. She'd spent six years being the proof.

"Tamsin Vole is twenty-six," Ulyana said finally. "She asked real questions."

"She did," Ivo said. "I read the journal too."

"I hate that you read the journal."

"I know." He lay back down. "Sleep on the channel south. It'll still be there tomorrow. That's the whole trouble with escape routes — they're patient."

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the worst kind of monsoon morning, the air thick enough to chew. Ulyana stood in the shallows up to her knees and looked south down the channel Ivo had marked on his private map — the safe line, the clean exit, two days of quiet water to a border and a life.

Then she looked north, toward Kestrel's Reach.

She made herself do the thing she told clients never to do, which was stand still and feel it. Both directions. South: safety, anonymity, the long gray transit she'd been living for six years, one more country that would eventually grow tired of her. North: a patrol post, an officer whose signature had ended her career, a cell, a trap built specifically for her — and inside it, a young woman who'd once shared her tea ration and asked what the drowned villages had been like before the water took them.

The price of survival. She'd been paying it in installments her whole adult life, and she'd always told herself the installments were cheap. A name here. A hometown there. A photograph left behind in a border-town room because keeping it meant admitting there was somewhere she was from. Small payments. Manageable payments.

Tamsin Vole was not a small payment. Tamsin Vole was the whole bill come due at once.

"Fuel first," Ulyana said aloud, to no one, to the river. "We take the safe channel as far as the mission cache. Then we turn north."

Behind her, Ivo said nothing, but she heard him begin rolling the tarp, and that was answer enough.

* * *

They reached the mission cache by midafternoon, poling through a lane of drowned fence posts — a farmstead swallowed whole, roof ridge just breaking the surface like the spine of some patient animal. The mission itself stood on the last high ground before the southern flats: a stilted chapel-house with a tin roof gone orange with rust, tended now by a single lay brother named Anselm who traded fuel and rice for news and didn't ask for names, which Ulyana suspected was less charity than policy.

He filled their jerry cans in silence, then paused, funnel in hand.

"Patrol came through yesterday," he said. "Buying fuel, asking questions. About a woman guide. They had a drawing of you." He said it the way he'd report a change in the weather — no judgment, no alarm. "Not a good drawing. The chin was wrong."

"They always get the chin wrong," Ulyana said. Her heart was going hard and slow, a drumbeat under the rain-noise. "Which way did they head?"

"North. Toward the Reach." Anselm screwed the cap down on the last can. "There was a girl with them. Young. Walking on her own feet but not looking at anything. One of the soldiers gave her water and she didn't drink it." He handed over the cans. "In my experience, people who won't take water are still deciding whether they want to keep living. Worth knowing, if she matters to you."

"She doesn't matter to me," Ulyana said. "I met her for nine days."

Anselm looked at her with the mild patience of a man who'd heard every possible lie told in his own kitchen. "Then it's strange," he said, "that you're filling extra cans."

* * *

They camped that night at the channel fork where south became north, and Ulyana did not sleep. She sat in the stern with the journal open on her knees and the rain coming down like a verdict, and she read the last entry again, the one written in a different hand, hurried, the ink feathered with water:

*T.V. — If anyone finds this, I got separated during the second night. I don't know where they took the others. I don't think it was violent. I think they were invited. I hid in the root ball of a fallen fig for two days and then I made the mistake of a fire. Tell my mother the river didn't get me. Tell her it was men. — T*

Men. Not the river, not the thing beneath the basin, not whatever longing had pulled the officers under. Men with orders, holding a twenty-six-year-old in a post with her name on a manifest, broadcasting her existence on a frequency like dropping meat into moving water.

Ulyana closed the journal and looked north, where the dark was total and the rain made the whole world one sound.

Every instinct she'd sharpened over six years of running said go south. Every instinct, and the whole hard-won architecture of her survival: never walk toward the trap, never negotiate from weakness, never give the people who burned you a second chance to hold the match. The blacklist existed because she'd once walked toward a fire to pull someone out, and the fire had been a government fire, and the government had punished her for getting burned.

She knew how this story went. She'd lived the earlier draft.

But she sat in the stern of a leaking boat with a stranger who'd chosen to stay, in a flooded country that had already cost her everything once, holding a dead man's confession and a living girl's last message, and the arithmetic had changed somewhere along the way without her permission. Survival used to be the whole ledger. Now there were other lines in it.

Ivo appeared beside her in the dark, silent as the man always was when it mattered, and set something in her lap: the crank radio, tuned and ready.

"One more broadcast before we commit," he said. "Listen to the whole cycle. If they move her, the traffic will say so."

They waited an hour. At the top of the cycle, the band crackled and the clipped voice returned, and this time the message was shorter, and colder, and aimed:

*—forward post Kestrel's Reach advises: material recovery window closes in seventy-two hours. Custody subject to standard disposition thereafter. End of advisory.*

Standard disposition. Ulyana had worked around soldiers long enough to know what the phrase meant when attached to a human being: whatever's easiest to explain later. Seventy-two hours, and then Tamsin Vole would become a flood statistic, one more body the Sable politely absorbed, one more name the government had never issued.

Seventy-two hours. Two days north against the current, if the hull held, if the patrols slept, if the rain relented enough to see the channels. It would be close. Close was the best the river ever offered anyone.

Ulyana handed the radio back and took up her paddle.

"South is that way," she said, pointing with her chin, not turning around. "In case you were unclear."

"I'm never unclear," Ivo said, and pushed off from the bank beside her.

They paddled north into the rain, two small shadows against a wall of water, toward the post, the officer, the trap built to catch them — because the price of survival had finally been itemized, and Ulyana Dravenmoor found, to her own considerable surprise, that she was no longer willing to pay it.

The current fought them the whole way. She barely noticed. Ahead, somewhere past the drowned trees and the searchlights yet to come, a girl who wouldn't drink her water was running out of hours, and for the first time in six years, Ulyana was paddling toward the fire on purpose.
