## 13. Bargaining with the Blacklister

The post sat on a rise of ground the river hadn't swallowed yet — a squat concrete blockhouse with sandbags stacked to its windows and a radio mast leaning like a drunk against the storm. Two patrol boats were moored at the flooded dock, engines idling low, rain hammering their canopies flat.

Ulyana left Ivo in the treeline two hundred meters out, crouched among drowned saplings with her spare paddle across his knees and an expression she chose not to look at too long.

"You're sure," he'd said. Not a question. He'd said it the way people say things they already know the answer to, just to hear it spoken aloud.

"I'm sure."

"And if he doesn't honor the parley?"

"Then you'll know what kind of man he is before I do." She'd checked the seal on Halvard's journal one last time — wrapped in oilcloth, strapped inside her jacket, heavy as a grudge. "If I'm not back by full dark, take Tamsin's location from my head if you can get it, and go south without me."

"I won't."

"I know," she said. "Say it anyway so I can pretend I have a plan B."

Now she stood alone on the mudflat below the post, hands empty and visible, rain running off her chin, and raised the white cloth she'd torn from the lining of her pack. It was already gray with river water. A flag of parley was supposed to be clean. Hers looked like what it was: a surrender staged by someone who had never once surrendered in her life.

A voice crackled over a loudspeaker, half-drowned in static. "Drop the flag. Hands higher. Walk forward slowly."

She walked. The mud sucked at her boots. Somewhere behind her, Ivo's skiff was a ghost among the trees, and ahead of her the blockhouse door opened onto a wedge of yellow light and a silhouette holding a rifle with the casualness of a man who had never needed it.

They took her weapons at the door — the knife at her hip, the hatchet lashed to her pack, even the flint she kept on a cord around her neck, which wasn't a weapon but which the soldier confiscating it seemed to find suspicious on principle. They patted her down thoroughly enough to find the journal, and when the soldier's hand closed on the oilcloth bundle under her jacket, Ulyana said, quietly, "That stays with me until I speak to your commanding officer. That's the parley. Touch it again and there's no parley."

The soldier looked at her. She looked back. There is a language older than rank, and she spoke it fluently: *I am not afraid of you, and that should frighten you.*

He stepped aside.

* * *

Commander Aldous Vrenn had aged badly, or perhaps he had always been old and she'd simply been young the last time she stood in front of him. The office was a converted storeroom: a desk bolted to the floor, a map of the basin pinned to the wall with the site's coordinates conspicuously absent, a cup of coffee gone cold beside a stack of signal flimsies. Rain ticked against the single window like something patiently trying to get in.

Vrenn didn't look up for a long moment. When he did, there was no surprise in his face. That was the first thing she noticed, and it told her more than anything he would say all night.

"Dravenmoor." He pronounced it correctly, which somehow made it worse. Most people mangled it. He had signed her file twice; he knew how it sounded. "Six years. You look terrible."

"The monsoon does that."

"The monsoon," he agreed, "does many things." He gestured at the chair across the desk. She stayed standing. His mouth twitched — acknowledgment, or amusement, hard to say. "You're here about the survey team."

"One member of it. Tamsin Orelle. Your people pulled her out of the water three days ago. She's in your infirmary right now, refusing food, because someone told her that cooperating means disappearing and not cooperating means disappearing slower." Ulyana kept her voice level. It cost effort. "I'm offering a trade. The journal Halvard kept — the real orders, the real purpose, everything your ministry will want burned — for the girl. You put her on a boat south tonight, and tomorrow morning the journal is where I say it is, and you never see me again."

Vrenn leaned back. Outside, thunder rolled down the basin like furniture being dragged across a very large room.

"You've read it, then."

"Every page."

"Then you know what it confirms." He turned his chair toward the window, toward the black sheeting rain. "You know what's sitting under forty meters of floodwater out there, and you know why five government officials walked into it and never came back out."

"I know what Halvard wrote. I don't know what he believed by the end. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," Vrenn said softly. "They rarely are."

He was quiet long enough that she almost spoke. Then he swiveled back and folded his hands on the desk, and the officiousness dropped away like a coat, and what was underneath was tired in a way she recognized — the specific exhaustion of a man who has done a thing he cannot un-do and has stopped pretending he wants to.

"Here's what happens now, Dravenmoor, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you decide I'm your enemy. Because I was going to be, either way. Orders are orders. But you came in under a flag, and I'm enough of an anachronism to honor it for as long as this conversation lasts." He tapped the desk once. "In seventy-two hours, engineers arrive from the capital. They're going to collapse the channel at the north cut and drop another six meters of water over the site. Permanently. Whatever the survey team found, whatever they *became*, goes under and stays under. The inquiry dies unborn. The names stay erased. That is not my decision. That is a decision made by men whose faces I don't know, communicated through channels I'm not permitted to remember."

"And Tamsin?"

"Tamsin Orelle is a loose end." He said it without relish, which was somehow worse than relish. "Loose ends get tidied before the engineers arrive. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless someone gives me a reason to report her dead instead." He opened a drawer and set something on the desk between them. Her file. Her actual file, thick as a brick, stamped twice in red — the border authority's mark, then his own. BLACKLISTED. PERSONA NON GRATA. EXPULSION ORDERED. Six years of her life in a folder, priced at less than the rifle leaning against his wall.

Ulyana stared at it and felt nothing, which frightened her more than fear would have.

"That's not the trade I came to make."

"No. It's better." Vrenn leaned forward. "Listen to me carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and I mean every word. You walk out of here tonight with a clean record. Both strikes struck. Every border from here to the coast opens to you. Work permits. Licenses. Your name off every list my ministry feeds, and we feed most of them." He slid the file an inch toward her. "All it costs is silence. You never saw the journal. You never saw the site. You take your boat south, you take the girl — yes, the girl, I'll even throw her in, she's worth more to me gone than dead — and you live the life you lost. Six years, Dravenmoor. Six years of sleeping in boats and eating what the river gives and being turned away at every crossing in two countries. One word from you makes all of it unhappen."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere outside, a soldier shouted an order and boots splashed past the window.

Ulyana found that her hands had gone cold. Not from the wet.

Because here was the thing she had never said aloud, not to Ivo, not to herself, not to the photograph she still couldn't explain keeping: she had imagined this offer. For six years, in the worst hours of the worst nights, she had rehearsed it. The clean record. The open borders. The life handed back, no questions asked. She had imagined taking it so many times that the taking had worn a groove in her, smooth and easy, a path her whole self could slide down without effort.

And now it was on the desk in front of her, and the groove led straight to it, and all she had to do was nothing. Say yes. Stay silent. Take the girl and go.

It would even be defensible. Tamsin lived. That was the mission. That was the whole point of paddling toward the fire.

Except.

Except the journal in her jacket held the names of eleven people from an expedition that officially never existed, and somewhere under the flood those names were carved into a vault that Vrenn's engineers were going to bury forever in three days. Except Ivo's sister was one of those names, and Ivo was crouched in the treeline right now trusting her with the only grief he had. Except the village that wasn't on charts had fed her without asking her name, and the elders had spoken of the provision like a promise, and promises made to strangers were the only kind she'd ever honored.

Except belonging, she was learning, was not a place on a map. It was a debt you accepted. And she was standing in a room with a man offering to erase all her debts, and she understood suddenly, completely, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she did not want them erased. She wanted to pay them.

"The journal," she said, "stays buried with everything else. Or it comes out. Those are the only versions of this story."

Vrenn studied her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — disappointment, or respect, or the particular sadness of a man watching someone make the mistake he'd have made himself at her age.

"You understand what you're choosing."

"I understand exactly what I'm choosing. That's new for me. I'm rather enjoying it."

"The girl stays where she is until the engineers leave. After that—" He spread his hands. "After that, the basin takes care of its own loose ends. You have my word on nothing, because my word is worth precisely what yours is."

"Which is why I brought insurance." Ulyana reached inside her jacket — slowly, telegraphing every motion — and drew out the oilcloth bundle. She set it on the desk between the file and the cold coffee. Vrenn's hand twitched toward it and stopped.

"It's sealed," she said. "Wax and wire. If it's opened by anyone but me, a dye tablet inside ruins every page beyond reading. Halvard was thorough. So am I. The journal sits in your safe, untouched, as collateral. Tamsin walks out of this post alive and free, and I come back for it after the engineers leave — publicly, under witness, to testify to what's in it. That's the trade. That's the only trade."

"And if I simply keep both?"

"Then you keep a ruined journal and a living witness, and I spend the rest of my considerably inconvenient life making sure the capital hears the word 'provision' from more directions than you can plug." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a nice smile. "I've been blacklisted twice, Commander. Threatening me with consequences is like threatening the river with rain."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — she thought he might actually laugh.

Instead he pressed a buzzer under the desk, and the door opened behind her, and two soldiers came in, and Vrenn's face settled into official stone.

"Aldous," she said quietly, using his first name for the first time in six years, watching him flinch. "You signed my file twice. Did you ever read past the charges?"

He didn't answer. The soldiers took her arms.

* * *

They put her in the supply room at the end of the blockhouse corridor — a cell by any other name, with a cot, a bucket, and a padlock on the outside of the door. Through the wall she could hear the generators, the rain, the radios muttering their encrypted nonsense. Through the barred vent near the ceiling, a slice of sky the color of wet slate.

She sat on the cot and did the arithmetic she should have done in the treeline.

He'd taken the journal. Of course he'd taken the journal — she'd known he would, had counted on it, because a man like Vrenn could not resist locking a dangerous thing in a safe, and a locked thing in a safe was a thing with a known address. The dye tablet was real. The rest of her bluff was scaffolding and nerve, and she had maybe hours before he confirmed how much of it was hollow.

But the journal was inside the post now. And so was Tamsin. And so, she realized, listening to the change in the generator's pitch as the night watch rotated, was every boat at the dock — including, if a certain cartographer was half as good as he claimed to be, soon one fewer.

She lay back on the cot and closed her eyes and let herself feel, for exactly thirty seconds, the shape of the life she had just refused. Clean papers. Open borders. The photograph finally explained, the transit finally ended, the running finally done — purchased at the price of eleven erased names and a vault drowned twice over.

Thirty seconds. Then she got up and started testing the vent frame with her fingertips, because the life she'd refused was worth having, and the fact that she couldn't have it honestly meant she'd have to take the other one dishonestly instead.

Outside, thunder cracked close enough to rattle the padlock in its hasp.

And beneath it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable to anyone who had grown up reading rivers — came the sound of a hull being cut loose from its moorings.
