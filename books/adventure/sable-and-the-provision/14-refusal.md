## 14. Refusal

The officer's name was Drennik. I'd known it before he said it, the way you know a scar in the mirror — by shape, not sight. He sat behind a folding table in the forward post's main hut with his hands laced and his voice pitched to the register men use when they believe patience is the same thing as power.

"Ulyana Dravenmoor." He said it like an inventory item. "Two blacklists. Three countries of residence in four years. And yet here you are, walking into my post under a parley flag, offering me the one thing my superiors want more than your silence."

"The journal," I said. "And Tamsin Vole. Alive. That was the arrangement."

"That was the bait." He smiled without warmth. "The journal is the hook. You're the fish. But fish don't negotiate, so let's dispense with the theater."

The hut smelled of wet canvas and kerosene. Rain hammered the roof in long sheets, and through the slatted window I could see the patrol boats moored at the post's dock, six of them, riding low with armament. Somewhere on the water, Tamsin was being held — or so the radio fragment had claimed. Radio fragments could lie. Men like Drennik lied better.

"I'll give you the journal's location," I said. "In exchange for her, delivered to the confluence village. Unharmed. Then I go south and you never see me again."

"And if I said yes?"

"I'd know you were lying, because you'd have said it too fast."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Recognition. The look of a man meeting an old opponent across a table he'd expected to be empty.

"You haven't changed," he said. "Still reading people like current charts."

"You signed my second blacklist," I said. "You wrote that I abandoned a government contract in dangerous waters. What you didn't write is that the contract was to ferry surveyors into a basin your own ministry had already condemned as unstable, because someone upstairs wanted the incident report before the monsoon made the question academic. I didn't abandon anything. I refused to drown for a filing deadline."

"And now you're back." He spread his hands. "Fate has a sense of humor."

"Fate isn't here. I am. Because you have a woman in custody who did nothing but take a job, and I have something you want. Let's not dress it up as destiny."

He leaned back. Outside, the rain thickened until the world beyond the window became a gray smear. A lantern swung on its hook, throwing our shadows against the wall in slow arcs.

"Here is what I can offer," Drennik said. "Not the woman. Not yet. First: a clean record. Both blacklists struck. Your guide's license restored. Passage anywhere you care to go. In exchange — the journal, and your silence about the site beneath the basin. You never saw it. You never speak of it. As far as any inquiry is concerned, the survey team drowned in the early flood, tragic loss, case closed."

I let the offer sit in the air between us like a coin on a table.

A clean record. Two words struck from two ledgers. Four years of border towns that wouldn't have me, of contracts offered at half rate or not at all, of landlords who counted my money twice. All of it erased for the price of saying nothing.

It would have been cheaper than anything else on the table. It would have cost me nothing except everything I'd found since the water took the survey team — the stilt village that asked no names, Ivo's map drawn in lamplight, the provision cache with its records of the disappeared, and somewhere out there in the rain, a woman named Tamsin Vole who had done nothing wrong except survive.

"No," I said.

Drennik's expression didn't change, but the room did. The temperature of it. The weight.

"No?" he repeated.

"You're offering me my old life back," I said. "My old life was a man running from every place that learned her name. I've seen what this basin does to people who stop wanting things. I'm not going to trade a living woman for a stamp on a document."

"The document is your life."

"It was. It isn't anymore." I stood. "You have until dawn to hand over Tamsin. After that, the journal goes to the capital, to the press bureau, to anyone who'll read it. And I won't be alone when it does."

He rose too, and two soldiers moved in from the corners where they'd been standing the whole time, patient as furniture.

"I was hoping you'd say no," Drennik said quietly. "It simplifies the paperwork."

* * *

They put me in the supply hut because it had a lock and the others didn't. A single cot, a crate of tinned rations, a high window barred with rusted iron. Through the gaps in the wall planks I could hear the river working at the pilings, patient as erosion.

I sat on the cot and did the arithmetic I should have done before walking in. Six patrol boats. Maybe twenty men. One of me, locked in a shed, with a journal hidden in a hollow tree three kilometers upriver and an ally whose last known position was a stilt village that wasn't on any chart.

I had walked into this knowing it might end here. Knowing that the safe channel south was still open, still waiting, still mine if I wanted it badly enough to let Tamsin Vole pay the difference.

That was the old arithmetic. Survival first, everything else priced against it. I had lived by that ledger for four years and it had kept me alive and it had kept me alone and it had never once, not once, kept me whole.

I pressed my forehead against the wall planks and listened to the rain.

Somewhere past midnight, the sound changed. Not the rain — under it. A low grinding, then a splash, then voices raised at the dock, sharp with alarm. Boots on planking. A whistle blown twice and cut short.

I got to the window. Through the bars I could see torchlight bobbing along the dock, men running, and beyond them — the mooring lines of the patrol boats hanging slack, cut clean, the boats themselves drifting sideways into the current with their crews shouting from the bank.

Six boats. Untethered. Going wherever the Sable wanted them.

And standing at the end of the dock, hip-deep spray whipping around him, a figure in a canvas slicker raising one hand — not waving. Counting. Ivo Kestrel, who had told me once that he traveled alone because maps were better company than people, standing in the middle of a chaos he had built with a knife and a swim in monsoon water.

Then the torchlight found him, and the shouting turned ugly, and he went off the end of the dock into the black river like he'd been born to it.

* * *

The lock on the supply hut was old, and the hinge pins were older. I'd spent four years learning how buildings came apart; it took me eleven minutes and a tin lid sharpened against the cot frame. When I came out into the rain, the post was in disarray — half the garrison chasing drifting boats down the bank, the other half searching the waterline for a cartographer who was already a kilometer gone.

Tamsin's holding cell was the radio hut. She was sitting on the floor with her knees drawn up, and when I got the door open she looked up at me with the flat, uncomprehending stare of someone who has stopped expecting doors to open.

"Up," I said. "Can you walk?"

"I— who—"

"Dravenmoor. I was the escort. Can you walk?"

She could, barely. We went out the back, past the fuel stores, down the mud slope to the waterline where the current ran fast and brown and loud. And there, exactly where he said he'd be if he said anything at all, was Ivo — hauling the post's smallest skiff out of the reeds, bailing it with his hat, soaked to the bone and grinning like a man who has just discovered religion.

"You cut loose their entire fleet," I said.

"They'll spend till morning recovering them." He handed me an oar. "Some longer. The current's taking them toward the falls."

"Toward the—" I looked at him. "You sent six armed patrol boats toward the falls."

"I sent six unattended hulls toward the falls. Where they end up is between them and the river." He nodded at Tamsin. "She comes with us?"

"She comes with us."

We pushed off into the dark. Behind us the post dissolved into torchlight and shouting; ahead the river opened wide and black, running hard against us, because we weren't going south. We were going up. Upstream, into the teeth of the flood, toward the site beneath the basin and whatever waited there — Halvard, the others, the archive, the truth.

Tamsin huddled in the bow, shivering, watching the shore slide past. "Where are we going?" she asked.

"Away," I said.

But it wasn't true, and I think she knew it, and I think Ivo knew it, because the current pulled at the oars like a hand on the wrist, and the rain came down harder, and somewhere ahead of us the water was rising over something the government would burn the world to keep buried.

The storm was going to peak tonight. And we were rowing straight into the middle of it.
