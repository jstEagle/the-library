## 15. Monsoon at Full Voice

The rain came down like a verdict.

Tamsin Oduya had grown up in a country where rain was an event — a season, a celebration, something you planned around. This was not that. This was water with intent. It hit the tarpaulin over the skiff's stern in fists, it filled her ears until she could hear nothing else, and it turned the Sable from a river into a rumor of land. Somewhere below them, drowned villages sat in their long dark sleep, and above them there was only gray, and the boat, and the three of them.

She had not spoken since they pulled her out of the patrol post's holding shed. That was two days ago, or maybe one, or maybe four — the storm had eaten time whole. She sat wedged between crates of the archive cases they'd carried out of the vault, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of mildew and someone else's woodsmoke, and she watched the woman at the tiller fight the current with her whole body, and she said nothing.

Ulyana Dravenmoor. Tamsin knew the name before she ever saw the face. Everyone on the survey team had known it, though Halvard pretended otherwise. "Our guide comes recommended," he'd said at the dock, smooth as oiled rope, while the dossier open on his desk back at the capital had read: *Dravenmoor, U. Blacklisted twice. Do not disclose mission parameters.* They'd all known what she was to them. A tool with a good left arm and a criminal memory of this river.

Now that same woman was keeping her alive.

The man called Ivo crouched at the bow, bailing with a dented pot, working in a rhythm so steady it was almost meditative. Scoop, fling. Scoop, fling. The hull groaned beneath them like something dreaming. Every hour or so he'd glance back at Tamsin — not staring, just checking, the way you check a lamp that might gutter out.

On the third day of the storm — she was fairly sure it was the third — he stopped bailing, wrung out his sleeves, and sat down across from her without asking permission.

"You should eat," he said.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're shaking."

"I'm cold."

"It's not cold." He held out a tin cup. Tea, weak and gritty, but hot. She took it because her hands wanted something to hold, and the heat of it surprised her so much her eyes stung.

Ulyana shouted something over the rain from the tiller. Ivo translated by leaning close: "She says we'll make the high ground at Marker Nine by nightfall if the river doesn't change its mind."

"The river changes its mind," Tamsin heard herself say. Her voice came out rusted. "That's the first thing you learn here. The Sable doesn't flow. It *decides*."

Ivo smiled faintly. "You know it well."

"I mapped it." The words came up like something dislodged from a drain. "That was the job. Flood lines, channel shifts, silt loads. Boring work. Good work. Work where nobody disappears." She stopped. The rain filled the silence for her.

Ivo waited. He was good at waiting; she'd noticed that even through the fog. Most people asked questions like they were pulling teeth. He asked questions like he was opening doors and letting you decide whether to walk through.

"What happened to your team?" he asked now, gently. "Not the official version. Not what the patrol told you to say. What actually happened that morning when you woke up and their beds were empty?"

Tamsin looked at the tea. Looked at the gray wall of rain. And found, to her surprise, that the answer had been sitting in her chest for days like swallowed glass, and that she was tired — so tired — of holding it.

* * *

It hadn't been violence. That was the thing nobody would believe, the thing she still half-didn't believe herself. There had been no struggle, no blood, no dragged footprints. There had been singing.

No — not singing. She corrected herself every time. Not singing. *Humming.* Low, harmonic, coming up through the ground itself, through the stilts of the camp, through the soles of her boots, starting around midnight on the fifth night past the confluence. She'd woken to it and lain there thinking it was the river, some resonance of water over stone, and then she'd realized the sound had *words* in it, or almost-words, syllables in no language she knew but shaped like language, shaped like an invitation.

She'd gotten up. That was the part that shamed her. Five officials' bedrolls, five sets of boots gone, weapons left behind neat as parade kit — and her, awake, hearing it too, and getting up anyway.

"They walked," she told Ivo, and her hands tightened around the tin cup until it bit. "That's what I've never said out loud. Nobody took them. They *walked*. I followed them. Halvard was in front, moving fast, like a man late for a train he'd been promised his whole life. Voss and Adeyemi were holding hands. Holding hands — they couldn't stand each other the entire trip, they fought about the radio schedule twice a day, and there they were, fingers laced, walking into the flooded forest like children going somewhere wonderful."

The rain hammered. Ivo didn't move.

"There was a path," she went on. "Underwater, mostly — shin-deep, then knee-deep — but firm underneath, like pavement. Old pavement. And lights under the water ahead of us, green-white, steady, not lightning, not anything natural. And the humming got louder, and it started to sound — God help me, it started to sound *kind*. Like a mother calling you in from the cold. Like somewhere warm after a long trip."

"And you turned back," Ivo said.

"I fell." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Isn't that pathetic? The only reason I'm here is I slipped off the path into a channel and went under, and the water knocked the — whatever it was — out of my head. Cold water. Fear. I came up coughing and the spell was broken, and I lay in the mud watching them go, and I *screamed* at them. Halvard turned around once. Just once. He looked right at me, and he smiled — he *smiled*, Ivo, like I was the one who was lost — and he kept walking."

She set the cup down because it was rattling against her teeth.

"I went back to camp. I told myself they'd come back by dawn. They didn't. I told myself they were dead, and honestly I prayed for it, because dead was better than what I'd seen in their faces. Then the patrol found me two days later, and the officer — the tall one, the one who signed all the orders — he sat me down and gave me a choice. Say the team drowned. Say the guide abandoned you. Or go in a shed until I decided I liked the first two options better." Her jaw tightened. "So I said what he wanted. And then you two showed up and burned all of it down anyway."

* * *

Ulyana brought the skiff in under the drowned trees at Marker Nine as the light failed, tying off to a strangler fig whose roots stood bare and black above the floodline like the ribs of something enormous. They rigged the tarpaulin into a lean-to, ate cold rice, and listened to the storm try to take the roof off the world.

Later, in the dark, Tamsin heard Ulyana settle across from her. The woman moved quietly for someone built like a dock crane.

"Tell me about the site," Ulyana said. No preamble. "What did Halvard's orders say? What were we escorting them toward?"

Tamsin pulled the blanket higher. "You read the journal."

"Half of it. The cipher pages are beyond me."

"Halvard wasn't mapping floods." Tamsin stared into the black. "The floods were the cover story. The basin sits over a sealed installation from the old regime — you know this, everyone who's worked this river knows the rumors. Archive vault. Records division. The regime disappeared people, thousands of them, and they kept files. Photographs. Confessions. Everything. When the regime fell, they flooded the vault rather than let it be opened. Sealed it, sank it, erased it from every map. The deluge last spring cracked it open. The site surfaced. And the government needed to know how bad the leak was before anyone else found it."

"So the survey was a containment assessment."

"A burial assessment." Tamsin's voice hardened. "Confirm the vault breached, catalogue what's exposed, and recommend permanent resealing. Which means flooding it again, deeper this time, with the records inside. With *people* inside, the ones in the files — their names, their faces, everything they were, drowned a second time so the state never has to account for them." She heard Ulyana exhale slowly through her nose. "Halvard believed in it. That's what I want you to understand. He wasn't cynical. He thought forgetting was mercy. 'Some archives are better as tombs,' he told me. His exact words."

"And then he walked into one."

"And then he walked into one." Tamsin laughed again, that wrong laugh. "Do you want to know the worst part? The part I can't make fit? They weren't tricked. Not exactly. The humming, the lights, whatever it is down there — it doesn't lie to you. It shows you what you want. Voss lost a daughter, years ago, before the survey. Never talked about it, but everyone knew. Adeyemi had a brother the old regime disappeared — he joined the civil service to find out what happened to him, spent fifteen years hitting walls. And in the water, on that path, Adeyemi turned to me — I was closest, I was the one still half-sane — and he said, *'They have him, Tamsin. He's in there. I can hear him.'* And his face—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "His face was the happiest I ever saw a human being. That's what took them. Not violence. *Longing.* The site doesn't kill you. It just asks what you can't live without, and then it holds the door open."

The rain roared. Somewhere out in the dark, a tree went over with a crack like artillery.

Ulyana was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was lower. Rougher. "And you? What did it show you?"

Tamsin hadn't expected the question. She'd been bracing for it for days, and it still landed like a blow.

"Nothing," she whispered. "That's the horror of it. It hummed for me and I felt it *looking*, feeling along the seams of me for a hook, and there wasn't one. No dead family. No lost love. No great wound. I'm an orphan raised by the state; there's nothing behind me to grieve. It found no purchase. So it let me go." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I used to think having nothing to lose made you free. Out here I learned it just makes you invisible. Even to monsters."

"No," Ulyana said, and the word came flat and certain, cutting through the rain. "It makes you the one who walks back out. The one who carries the names." A pause. "That's not nothing. That's the whole job."

Tamsin looked at her — the blacklisted guide, the woman with two countries' worth of exile in her file, the last person on earth who should be lecturing anyone about belonging — and something in her chest shifted, some load-bearing grief settling into a new position.

* * *

Sleep came in fragments. In the small hours, wedged between the archive cases, Tamsin dreamed of the path under the water, of green-white light, of Halvard's smile — and woke to Ivo's lantern and his hand on her shoulder, saying her name twice, patiently, until she surfaced.

But it wasn't the dream that had her heart slamming. It was what she'd remembered inside the dream. Something she'd seen and filed away and forgotten, the way shock files things away.

"Ivo." She grabbed his wrist. "The vault. When the patrol brought me near it — they marched me past the breach entrance, to scare me into silence. I saw inside. Just for a second."

He went very still. Even Ulyana, at the edge of the lantern light, turned.

"There are shelves," Tamsin said. "Kilometers of shelves. And the water hasn't reached the upper levels yet — the regime built it to survive a flood, it's *rated* for this, the seals are holding on the upper tiers. The records are still dry. All of them. Every name." Her voice climbed. "And there are people living in there. Not ghosts. People. I saw lamplight moving between the stacks. I heard voices. Your team isn't dead, Ulyana. They're *home*. They're guarding it. And the government's next move — the officer told me, he thought I'd never repeat it — the next convoy goes in with demolition charges. To bring the upper levels down and drown it all for good."

The lantern flame bent in a draft. Rain drummed like fingers on a door.

Ulyana rose. Tamsin watched her look out at the black roaring river, and watched her shoulders set in a way Tamsin had already learned to read — the set of a woman done calculating exits.

"How far," Ulyana said, "to the breach entrance?"

Tamsin wiped her face, and gave her the bearing.
