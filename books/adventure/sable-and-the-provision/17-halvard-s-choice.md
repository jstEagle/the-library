## 17. Halvard's Choice

The vault breathed.

That was the only word Halvard had ever found for it, in the eleven days since he'd walked back through its doors and told the others he wasn't leaving. The air inside moved — slow, tidal, in time with the river pressing against the far wall — and it smelled of cold stone and old paper and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a sealed jar. Like things kept.

He stood at the edge of the flooded lower gallery with a lantern held high, watching Ulyana Dravenmoor pick her way down the iron stairs. She came alone. He'd heard her arguing with Kestrel on the surface — heard the argument end not in agreement but in surrender, hers or his, hard to say — and then her boots on the rungs, one careful step at a time, because she was the kind of person who never trusted a stair she hadn't tested.

She stopped three steps from the bottom. Water to her shins. Lantern light caught the scar along her jaw, the one the border files described in clinical language: *laceration, cause unrecorded.*

"Halvard."

"Ulyana." He kept his voice level. He'd rehearsed this. Not the words — you couldn't rehearse words for a thing like this — but the posture. Open hands. No retreat toward the archive shelves behind him, though every nerve in him wanted to turn and stand between her and the records the way a man stands between a wolf and the den. "You came down alone. That took courage."

"It took stupidity," she said. "Kestrel thinks so too. We disagreed about which of us it applied to." Her eyes went past him, up into the dark where the shelves climbed out of lantern range, row after row of them, the whole drowned architecture of a regime's memory. "Five people walked out of that camp, Halvard. I read your journal. I know what was ordered. I came to hear why."

"You could have just asked on the surface."

"On the surface I'd have been shouting over the storm, and you'd have been performing." She waded another step closer. "You lie badly in daylight. Let's see what you do in the dark."

* * *

He had been a surveyor for nineteen years before any of this. Before the Sable, before the contract, before the night in the camp when the water rose around their bedrolls and none of them woke — or rather, when they all woke, and found they couldn't move, and understood with a calm that still shamed him that the choice had already been made somewhere deeper than will.

He told her about the beginning instead. It was easier, and it was true, and truth was the only currency he had left that she might accept.

"I had a family once," he said. "A wife, Petra. A daughter, Mirele. Six years old." The lantern trembled slightly in his grip and he steadied it against his thigh. "This was under the old regime. You were a child yourself, maybe. It was the era of corrections. People didn't die, officially. They were *relocated.* Their names came off the registries. Their houses got new tenants. If you asked after them, you were asking after nothing, because there had never been anyone there to ask after."

Ulyana said nothing. But she had gone very still, the way she went still on the river when the current changed under the boat — reading something beneath the surface of him.

"Petra wrote a pamphlet," Halvard continued. "It sounds absurd now. Fourteen pages, hand-copied, about grain quotas. Fourteen pages, and one night there was a knock, and then there was no knock, and then there was no Petra. They took Mirele too. Children of the corrected were placed in state care, which is a phrase that means the records stop." He looked up at the shelves. "I spent two years writing letters. Petitions. I was a good citizen. I had a steady post. I wrote forty-one letters and received three replies, all of them telling me that no persons matching my description had ever been registered at my address. My own address. As if the house had raised itself."

"And then?"

"And then the regime fell, the way they do — loudly, briefly, and without opening a single door. There was a commission. There was an amnesty. There was a fund for victims' families, which required documentation, which required proof, which required the very records that had been sealed or burned or drowned." His laugh came out wrong, bent in the middle. "They erased people so thoroughly that even the apology couldn't find them. Do you understand? My wife doesn't exist enough to be mourned by her own government. My daughter is a blank line in a ledger that says *no persons matching.*

"So when the commission sent a survey team into the Sable basin — when the floodwaters dropped last spring and the old regime's emergency archive surfaced out of the mud like a bone working free of soil — I volunteered. I lied on the application. I said I wanted to map flood lines. What I wanted was to walk into that vault and put my hand on the shelf where my family would be filed. Because they *would* be filed. That's the terrible joke of it. The regime was meticulous. They erased people from the world and recorded the erasure. Every disappearance has a folder. Every folder has a name. Somewhere in this building is a card that says *Petra Halvard, relocated,* and one that says *Mirele Halvard, placed,* and as long as those cards exist, my family existed. As long as they exist, someone can be made to answer for them."

The water lapped against the stairs. Somewhere above, thunder worked at the roof like a patient carpenter.

* * *

"You've read your journal," Halvard said. "You know our orders weren't to recover anything. They were to confirm the site's condition and report nothing. Sealing order pending. The ministry wants this place underwater again by season's end — permanently, this time, with the approaches charted as false channels so no one ever finds it." He shook his head. "They'll drown it with everyone inside it still unnamed. Petra and Mirele will go under a second time, and this time there won't even be a rumor of a door."

"That's the government's sin," Ulyana said. "Not yours. You could have copied the cards. You could have carried out evidence. Instead five grown officials vanished from a guarded camp without a track in the mud, and a guide with two blacklists got left holding the journal. You made me the witness whether I wanted it or not."

"Yes." He didn't flinch from it. "And I'm sorry for the manner of it. Truly. But you should know what happened that night, because you keep saying we were *taken,* and we weren't. We were *called.*"

Her eyes narrowed. "Explain."

"The site does something. I don't have the word for it — the old technicians' notes call it resonance, the village elders call it the provision's voice, and both are guesses. But when the water reached a certain mark on the outer wall, all five of us woke at the same hour and walked to the same place, and none of us could tell you afterward why, only that it felt less like being summoned than like being *remembered.* Like something in here knew our names before we gave them." He set the lantern on a dry shelf ledge. "Inside, we found the galleries. And each of us found our own gallery. Tamsin found hers first — her brother, conscripted, lost in the eastern purges. Voss found a cousin. I found—" His throat closed. He waited it out. "Third gallery, north aisle, shelf fourteen. *Halvard, P., relocated, spring of the corrections. Halvard, M., placed, same season.* Two cards. Two sentences. Nineteen years, and everything I had of them fit in my two hands."

He turned and lifted a small oilcloth packet from the shelf beside the lantern — not the archive copy, but his own, recopied in his careful surveyor's hand, worn soft at the folds from handling.

"I stayed because these can't drown twice," he said. "Because if the ministry floods this vault, the erasure completes itself. First they took the people. Now they'll take the proof the people were taken. And I am not — I am *not* — going to stand on some safe southern bank while the water closes over my daughter's name again." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. He was done performing composure; she'd seen through it anyway. "Begging your pardon, Ulyana. Begging it sincerely. But don't ask me to leave. Ask me anything else. Ask me to carry cases until my back gives. Ask me to stand between you and the patrol when they come. But don't ask me to walk out of here and let it drown."

* * *

For a long moment she didn't speak. The water shifted around her calves, and the lantern flame leaned, and Halvard watched her face do the thing he'd seen it do on the river — the rapid, involuntary arithmetic of a woman who calculated exits the way other people breathed.

Then she said, quietly: "I ran for eleven years."

He blinked. "What?"

"Two blacklists. Two countries. I crossed the Sable basin the first time running from a job gone wrong, and the second time running from the man who signed the warrant, and every year in between running from smaller versions of the same thing. I owned one boat, one kit, one photograph I couldn't explain keeping, and nothing else, because nothing else can't be left behind in ninety seconds." She looked up at the shelves, at the thousands of folders climbing into the dark. "I told myself it was freedom. Moving light. Staying ahead. It took me until this month to admit what it actually was — that I'd made a religion out of not belonging anywhere, because belonging means something can be taken from you, and I'd already learned what taking costs."

"Yes," Halvard said, barely breathing the word.

"So I understand you better than you think, and it frightens me." She waded forward until the water was at her knees and they stood close enough that he could see how tired she was — not body-tired; soul-tired, the fatigue of a woman who had finally stopped moving and discovered what had been chasing her was grief. "You stopped running for the same reason I never could. Because there was a name you couldn't leave behind. Mine's a photograph of people I can't even account for anymore. Yours is two cards on shelf fourteen. We're the same animal, Halvard. You just found your reason and I found mine in the same season."

"Then help me save them," he said. "Both sets. Yours and mine and Tamsin's brother and every name on every shelf. The patrol is coming to seal this place — days, maybe hours. Help me carry out what we can. Not everything. Enough. Enough that the inquiry can't bury it, enough that the commission has to open every folder, enough that Mirele Halvard gets to have been real." He held out the oilcloth packet, foolishly, uselessly — she couldn't take his copies, they meant nothing to her — and then understood the gesture for what it was and lowered his hand. "I'm begging. I don't beg well. You've seen me lie badly; I beg worse."

Ulyana looked at the packet. At the shelves. At him.

"Kestrel has a map," she said at last. "His sister was on the earlier expedition — the erased one. He's been tracking this vault for years. If his sister's file is in here, he'll want it, and he'll want it even if the building comes down around us." She turned her lantern toward the flooded gallery, measuring the waterline against the lowest shelves with a surveyor's eye he recognized because it lived in him too. "How many cases can two people and a leaking boat carry?"

"Twenty, if we're ruthless about it."

"We'll need to be more than ruthless. We'll need to be lucky, and the storm's spending all the luck in the basin." She was already moving, shrugging off her pack, pulling out rope and empty sacking with the brisk economy of a woman converting grief into logistics. "We start with the index. If we take the master registry, every other case can be reconstructed. Where is it?"

"Central gallery. Second floor. Dry, still — for now." He hesitated. "Ulyana. Why? You could take the safe channel south tonight. I'd understand it. After what we did to you—"

"Don't," she said, not unkindly. "Don't make me explain it while I still believe it. Ask me again when we're on the water."

She started up the stairs. Halvard followed, the oilcloth packet pressed against his chest like a second heartbeat, and it was only when they reached the surface — where the storm had opened the sky and Ivo Kestrel stood waiting in the rain with his sister's map already unrolled — that Halvard saw the lights.

Three of them. Far down the channel, low on the black water, sweeping slow and deliberate across the drowned trees.

The patrol had come early.

"They're sealing it," Halvard said. "Tonight."

Ulyana watched the lights a long moment, rain streaming off her jaw, and then began coiling rope with terrible calm.

"Then we've got until they reach the landing," she said. "Move."
