## 19. High Water

The river gave them one day of mercy, and they spent it badly.

They spent it sleeping — all three of them at once, which Ulyana would later call the first honest mistake of the journey, because you did not sleep all at once on the Sable in monsoon season, not ever, not if you'd read the water the way she had been reading it since she was nine years old. But exhaustion is its own current, stronger than any river, and when dawn came gray and quiet over the drowned forest, Tamsin was curled in the bow like a child, Ivo was slumped against the mast step with his map case still strapped to his chest, and Ulyana sat at the tiller with her eyes open only because closing them felt like surrender.

The boat was the patrol post's longboat, cut loose by Ivo in the dark, and it was better than anything they'd had before — until it wasn't. By midday she'd found the first seep along the starboard strake, slow as a lie, and by afternoon the bilge sloshed at her ankles when she shifted her weight.

"Leak," she said.

Ivo opened one eye. "How bad?"

"We'll find out."

* * *

Tamsin spoke for the first time in two days that evening, watching the water rise around the stilt village where they'd stopped to bail and patch.

"It wasn't violent," she said, to no one, to the river. "That's what nobody understands. It didn't take them. They went."

Ulyana kept working the pitch into the seam. "I know."

"You don't. You think you do, but—" Tamsin's hands were shaking around a tin cup of tea nobody had sweetened. "Halvard stood in front of that vault door and he wept. Not from fear. I've seen men weep from fear. This was — recognition. Like he'd found something he'd lost so long ago he'd stopped believing it existed." She looked up. "What does that to a man?"

"The same thing that empties him out," Ulyana said. "Depending on what he finds."

She thought of Halvard then — of his bad lies on the dock in the border town, of his secretive nights, of the way he'd stood in the flooding vault with his arms spread like a man defending a country that had never defended him back. She thought of how he'd finally let the water push him toward the boat, howling, dragged by Ivo and Ulyana both, and how even then his eyes had stayed on the archive cases going under.

He was asleep in the stern now, hollowed out, forty years older than his file said he was.

The sky to the north turned the color of a bruise going bad.

* * *

Ivo saw it first, because Ivo always saw weather first — a cartographer's habit, reading cloud formations the way other men read coastlines. He was on the village platform checking the patch when the wind changed direction without transition, the way it does in the basin, and he came down the ladder two rungs at a time.

"That's not the tail of the last storm," he said. "That's a new one. It circled the basin. They do that here — the old charts show it. The Sable catches them and spins them."

"How long?" Ulyana asked.

"If we're lucky, an hour. If we're not—"

"Lucky," Ulyana said. "Right."

They loaded the boat in twenty minutes. Three survivors, one half-mad survey leader, four archive cases wrapped in oilcloth, the journal, Ivo's maps, and enough rations for five days if nobody ate generously. The villagers wouldn't come with them — the elders had made that clear with the gentle finality of people who had survived every regime by refusing to be anywhere regimes were looking — but they gave the boat a fresh patch, a jar of rendered fat for the seams, and a carved marker post that the eldest woman pressed into Ulyana's hands without explanation.

"When you reach the capital," the woman said, "plant this where the surveyors can see it. Tell them the basin keeps its own records too."

Ulyana took it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

They pushed off into a river already quickening, brown water climbing the stilts, and behind them the village lamps came on one by one like a constellation deciding, at the last moment, to witness what happened next.

* * *

The storm hit them ninety minutes downstream, in the worst possible place: the narrows below the confluence, where the Sable pinched between drowned ridgelines and ran fast and deep over submerged fences, roads, and — according to Ivo's second map — the roof of a church.

There was no steering it. There was only surviving it.

Ulyana took the tiller. Ivo took the bail bucket. Tamsin took the bow line and Halvard took nothing, because Halvard sat with his arms around the archive cases as though they were children and he was the last adult awake in a burning house.

The rain came sideways. The lightning came from inside the clouds, sheeting the whole sky white so that for half a second at a time Ulyana could see the entire river at once — a mile-wide animal in flood, carrying trees, roofs, a dead cow turning slowly, and one thirty-foot boat with four fools aboard.

"Hold her off the ridge!" Ivo shouted, pointing left, where the water broke white over something just beneath the surface.

"I see it!"

She didn't, quite. The boat struck glancing, wood shrieking against wood or stone or bone, and the patch on the starboard strake tore free like a scab. Water came in the way truth comes in — all at once, after a long time of almost.

"Bail!"

"I'm bailing!"

"Tamsin — the forward compartment — there's a plug—"

Tamsin found it. The inflow slowed to a trickle the boat could outrun, barely, and they shot through the narrows into the wider dark beyond while the storm walked beside them, keeping pace, patient as a creditor.

* * *

It was past midnight when the boat began to die in earnest.

Not suddenly. Boats rarely die suddenly; they die the way people do, by degrees, each failure making the next inevitable. The patched seam wept. Then the strake beside it flexed and started to open. Then the bilge pump — a hand pump scavenged from the patrol post — choked on silt and gave up with a sound like a cough.

Ulyana measured the water rising against her shin, then her knee, and did the arithmetic she'd done a hundred times in a hundred boats, and the answer was the same every time: too much weight, too little boat, too much river.

"Cases," she said. "We lighten or we sink."

"No." Halvard's voice came out of the dark, flat and absolute. "No."

"Halvard—"

"They are *names*." He was on his knees among the oilcloth bundles now, lightning strobing his face white and black, white and black. "Do you understand? Every case is names. People this country erased so thoroughly their own mothers died not knowing. If those go under, they're erased twice, and the second time is forever, and I will not—"

"The boat sinks, we all go under, and they're erased anyway!" Ulyana heard her own voice crack across the water and hated the truth in it. "Two cases stay. Two go. That's the math."

"There is no math," Halvard said quietly. "There's only what a man will drown for."

And then the wave came.

It came out of the confluence proper — a standing wave where two flooded currents met and quarreled — and it took the boat amidships and threw it sideways, and everything happened in the order such things happen: the port strake opening like a mouth, the river pouring in, Tamsin screaming, Ivo catching the bow line as the mast step cracked, Ulyana losing the tiller and finding it again, and Halvard —

Halvard lifting the two cases he'd chosen, the ones nearest the vault door, the ones he'd slept with his arms around for three days, and holding them above the water as the boat rolled under them all.

* * *

Ulyana surfaced gasping, blind with rain, and found debris — the boat hadn't sunk so much as come apart, generous in death, offering planks and cases and rope to anyone still fighting. She caught a thwart. She caught Tamsin's collar. Ivo appeared beside her with a case strapped to his back and his map case clenched in his teeth like a dog crossing a flood, and for one full minute none of them spoke because speaking meant drowning.

Then the current separated them, the way currents do, and Ulyana learned in the next hour what the river wanted.

It wanted everything.

It took her under twice and gave her back once. It took the thwart. It took her knife, her compass, her left boot. It gave her, in exchange, a drowned tree to cling to, and Tamsin, who had caught the same tree six feet away, and eventually — impossibly, absurdly — Ivo, hauling himself along the trunk with the case still on his back, spitting river water and laughing, actually laughing, the unhinged laugh of a man who has decided that if the world insists on being ridiculous he will be ridiculous back.

"Where's Halvard?" Tamsin shouted.

Ulyana looked upstream.

In the lightning flashes she saw him — a hundred meters up, maybe more, riding a section of hull like a raft, both archive cases lashed to his chest with rope he must have tied in the dark, in the water, while drowning. He wasn't swimming for shore. He wasn't swimming at all. He was steering — leaning his weight, correcting, using the last of his strength not to save himself but to keep the cases out of the main channel, away from the snags, aimed at the slack water near the bank where a body might live.

Aimed, Ulyana understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the rain, at *them*.

"He's coming to us," she shouted. "Stay on the tree!"

* * *

He nearly made it.

Later — in the capital, in the inquiry room, in the years afterward whenever the memory arrived uninvited — Ulyana would replay those last minutes frame by frame and search for the moment it could have gone differently, and she would never find one. There was no mistake to correct, no choice to remake. There was only a man, two cases, and a river that had already decided.

He came through the last stretch of fast water upright and deliberate, a barge pilot to the end, and the current set him toward the tree exactly as he'd calculated, and Ulyana reached out with both arms and got one hand on the rope lashing the cases and one hand on his wrist, and for a moment — one bright, impossible moment — she had him.

Then the snag field took hold.

The drowned trees below the high-water mark formed a lattice underwater, and the current drove Halvard's raft-section into it, and the impact tore him half free of Ulyana's grip. The cases swung. The rope — his rope, his knots, tied well, tied perfectly — caught on a submerged limb and held, and held, and held him under by the chest, and Ulyana felt the full weight of the river come down his arm like a hand trying to take something from her.

"Let go of the cases!" she screamed into the water. "*Let go!*"

His face came up once, close enough that she could see his eyes, and they weren't afraid. They were apologetic. His mouth moved — she would spend years deciding whether he'd said *take them* or *my family* or simply her name — and then the river pulled, and the choice he had been making since the vault door opened was finished for him at last, made permanent, made simple.

He let go of her wrist.

He did not let go of the cases.

The water took him down between two snags, and the rope went taut, and then the limb above it cracked with a report like a rifle and the cases came free and bobbed up lashed together, and Halvard did not come up at all.

* * *

They got the cases. Of course they got the cases — Ivo swam for them through water that should have killed him, because grief makes swimmers of us all, and hauled them to the bank where the flood had pushed a wall of debris into a kind of ramp. Ulyana dragged Tamsin ashore by main force. And then the three of them lay in the mud of the riverbank under the dying edge of the storm, breathing, while the Sable ran past in the dark, enormous, indifferent, paid in full.

It was Tamsin who said it, finally, her voice scraped raw.

"He could have let go. At the end. He could have let go of the cases and come up."

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"Why didn't he?"

Ulyana watched the river. Somewhere upstream, in a vault now sealed under ten feet of new floodwater, were the rest of the names — and somewhere deeper than that, in whatever country the drowned inhabit, was a man reunited at last with the family the archive had promised him.

"Because he'd already let go of everything else," she said. "A man holds on to the one thing he has left. You can't fault the grip. You can only wish he'd had more hands."

Ivo said nothing. He unlashed the cases, checked the seals, found them dry inside — dry, after all of that, the oilcloth holding like a promise — and then he sat down heavily in the mud beside Ulyana and put his head back and laughed again, or cried, or did whatever it is that people do when triumph and grief arrive in the same envelope and cannot be separated even by the person feeling them.

"Three survivors," he said at last. "Four cases. Enough."

"Enough," Ulyana agreed.

It was true, and it wasn't, and it was the only sentence the night allowed.

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, and with it the reckoning.

They inventoried themselves like wreckage: Ulyana, one boot, no knife, ribs bruised but whole. Ivo, soaked, bleeding from a gash on his scalp he insisted was cosmetic, both map cases intact — including the second map, the private one, the sister's map, dried inside its oilcloth by some mercy Ulyana chose not to examine too closely. Tamsin, alive, which two days ago had seemed the least likely entry in any ledger. And the archives: four cases, the two Halvard carried and the two Ulyana had saved from the sinking boat by diving into a collapsing hull for them, an act she could not explain and did not try to.

Enough. The word kept returning, patient as the river. Enough names to reopen the disappeared. Enough evidence to burn the erasure down. Enough to testify.

Downstream, maybe sixty kilometers, the capital waited with its committees and its inquiries and its officers suddenly very interested in hearing from witnesses. Upstream, the basin settled back into its green silence, keeping its own records, as the elder had said.

Ulyana stood at the water's edge and planted the carved marker post the village had given her, driving it into the mud with the heel of her one boot, where the recovery expeditions would someday pass and read it and know someone had been there first.

Then Ivo, crouched over the cases, went very still.

"Ulyana," he said. His voice had changed — stripped down, careful, the voice of a man handling something explosive. "The seal on this one. Halvard's knot. It's been retied."

"So?"

"So I've been studying his knots for three days." Ivo looked up at her, and the light in his face was terrible and bright. "This isn't his tie-off. Someone else lashed these cases — recently, inside the vault. And there's a page tucked under the cord. Waterproof paper." He drew it out with two fingers, like extracting a splinter. "It's addressed to you."

The river ran past. The morning held its breath.

Ulyana took the page.
