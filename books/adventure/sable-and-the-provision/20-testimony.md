## 20. Testimony

The capital smelled of wet stone and diesel, the way it always did after a long rain, and Ulyana stood outside the Hall of Records for twenty minutes before she could make her feet carry her through the doors.

She had been summoned here once before. Seven years ago. A different inquiry, a different room, the same gray men with the same gray folders, and at the end of it a stamp coming down on paper like a small guillotine: *blacklisted*. She remembered the sound it made. She had heard it twice now in her life, and both times it had followed her out of the building and down every road afterward.

This time there was no stamp waiting. This time there was a hearing — an actual one, with clerks who looked nervous instead of bored, because three weeks ago a guide and a cartographer and a half-drowned survey technician had walked into the provincial office carrying forty kilos of documents that were supposed to have drowned with everything else.

Tamsin had testified first. Then Ivo. Today was hers.

A clerk met her in the marble vestibule, a young woman with ink-stained fingers and the careful blankness of someone instructed not to react to anything. "Dravenmoor. They're ready."

"They've been ready for seven years," Ulyana said, and followed her up the stairs.

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than she expected. Not a courtroom — a long table under high windows, six commissioners behind it, a stenographer, a scattering of observers in the back rows. No gallery packed with press; the government had learned that much from the old regime, that erasure worked best when nobody was watching. But there were more people than she'd guessed would come, and among them, in the third row, sat faces she knew from photographs in the archive cases. Old women. A man holding a folded cap. People who had spent decades not knowing, and had come to sit as close as they were allowed to the place where the knowing might finally happen.

Ulyana's chest did something complicated about that. She put it away. There would be time later, or there wouldn't.

"State your name and occupation for the record," said the chairman, a dry-voiced man named Ostrevik whose reputation for incorruptibility was apparently the only reason this hearing existed at all.

"Ulyana Dravenmoor. Mountain guide. River guide, most recently." She paused. "Blacklisted twice. You'll have the files."

"We have the files." Ostrevik's mouth twitched, which on him might have been humor. "We also have them under review. Proceed with your account. Begin wherever you believe it begins."

Wherever you believe it begins. Most witnesses would start with the contract, or the vanishing, or the patrol lights. Ulyana had thought about it for three sleepless nights in the provincial guesthouse, listening to rain, and she had decided.

"It begins with a name," she said. "Mirela Voss. She was a hydrologist on the Sable expedition eleven years ago. The one your government erased. She was the first person to log the anomalous water levels at the confluence, and when the expedition was struck from the record, her work was struck with her. Everything I found in that basin — everything Halvard's team died for, everything we carried out — starts with her measurements. So if this hearing is going to mean anything, it should start by saying her name back."

Silence. The stenographer's keys hung still.

Ostrevik leaned forward. "You understand this inquiry is concerned with the conduct of Officer Brandt and the disposition of the recent survey team."

"I understand you want to keep it narrow," Ulyana said. "Narrow is how it happened the first time. Narrow is how forty people become a redaction. I'm not here for vindication. I don't need you to tell me I was right to refuse Brandt's deal — I know what I did and why. I'm here so the disappeared get their names again. All of them. That's the only testimony I have worth giving."

She opened the satchel at her feet and began laying the documents on the table, one by one, in the order she and Ivo had spent nights arranging them. The expedition manifest. Mirela Voss's field logs. The provision inventory, annotated in two hands. And last, the roster — the full roster of the erased expedition, forty-one names, which the old regime had reduced to a single line in a flooded-file index: *expedition concluded, records sealed.*

"Forty-one," she said. "Read them into your transcript. All of them. Slowly."

* * *

They read them. It took eleven minutes, and Ulyana stood through all of it, watching the commissioners' faces go from procedural patience to something less comfortable. In the third row, the man with the folded cap held it against his chest like a heart he'd been keeping safe. When the reader reached *Voss, Mirela*, an old woman closed her eyes.

When it was done, Ostrevik said quietly, "Thank you," and it sounded like it cost him something true.

Then came the hard hours. Questions about the site — what she'd seen in the vault, how much they'd carried, whether copies existed beyond those produced. Questions about Brandt: the parley at the forward post, the offer of a clean record for silence, the night he'd signed her second blacklist seven years ago over a border crossing she'd made to move a sick child across a river that had since changed course entirely. She told it plainly. She had learned long ago that plainness was the only armor that didn't weigh anything.

"You refused a full pardon," one commissioner said, not quite a question. "Restoration of standing, compensation, clearance for any contract in the territory. Refused, in exchange for silence regarding the site. Why?"

Because the pardon was priced in other people's names. Because she had spent seven years learning exactly what survival cost when you bought it alone, and the bill had finally come due in a flooding vault where a man named Halvard chose drowning archives over his own exit. But she didn't say any of that. She said:

"A clean record bought with a lie isn't clean. It's just laundered. I've seen what this government does with laundered things. I'd rather be dirty and accurate."

Someone in the back rows laughed — short, startled, quickly smothered. One of the commissioners almost smiled.

* * *

They broke at midday. Ulyana ate bread and hard cheese on the hall steps, alone, watching the traffic, and Ivo found her there without seeming to look for her. He sat down at the far end of the step, close enough to talk, far enough to pretend they weren't together. Habit. Theirs, apparently, now.

"How was it?" he asked.

"They said my name right. First try." She handed him half the bread. "Yours?"

"I told them about the map. About Nadia." He turned the bread over without eating it. His sister's name still sat strangely in his mouth, like a word in a language he was relearning. "There was a moment — when I said she stayed willingly — where one of them wrote something down and then crossed it out. Like even now they're deciding what's allowed to be true."

"They'll decide right, this time. Or they'll decide wrong and we'll make them regret it in print." Ulyana shrugged. "That's the whole art of testimony. You just have to outlast their comfort."

Ivo looked at her sideways. "You've gotten philosophical since the monsoon."

"Drowning does that."

He almost smiled, then didn't, because neither of them had fully recovered the ability. Three weeks out of the water and Tamsin still woke screaming some nights in the provincial infirmary, calling names — Halvard's mostly, and sometimes, horribly, none at all. The river had taken its fee. Everyone paid differently.

"Tamsin's being released tomorrow," Ivo said. "Medically cleared. She asked if you'd come see her off."

"After the ruling."

"If there is one."

"There'll be one," Ulyana said. She believed it the way you believe a rope will hold — not because ropes are trustworthy, but because you've checked every strand yourself.

* * *

The ruling came on the fourth day, and it came unsweetened, which was the only way rulings ever came that meant anything.

Ostrevik read it standing, which commissioners did not do, and which everyone in the room understood to be deliberate. The inquiry found that the Sable basin survey had been directed toward locating and suppressing evidence of the prior regime's disappearance program; that Officer Dain Brandt had conducted an unlawful cover operation including coercion of witnesses, false imprisonment, and the obstruction of rescue; that the blacklisting of Ulyana Dravenmoor — both entries — rested on fabricated findings and was hereby struck from the record in its entirety; that the forty-one members of the erased expedition were restored to the historical register with their work credited; and that Officer Brandt was relieved of command, effective immediately, pending criminal referral.

No apology. Governments didn't apologize; they amended. But the clerk who brought Ulyana the certified copy of her struck blacklists also brought a plain envelope, unmarked, containing a single sheet in Ostrevik's own hand: *The commission acknowledges that this restoration is overdue by seven years and eleven years respectively. That debt cannot be repaid. It can only be stopped from accruing further.* She kept the note. She wasn't sure yet why. Maybe because it was the first piece of official paper she'd ever held that admitted fault instead of assigning it.

Brandt, she learned afterward, had watched his relief from the same kind of gray office where he'd once stamped her life shut. He had said nothing in his own defense. There was nothing left to defend with — the journal, the cipher, the provision cache, and forty kilos of carried truth had seen to that. She felt, examining herself carefully, not triumph. Something flatter and more durable: the specific quiet of a door closing on a room you never have to enter again.

In the third row, the man with the folded cap was crying without noise. The old woman — Mirela Voss's mother, someone whispered; no, her aunt; the records disagreed and it no longer mattered — touched the transcript page where the forty-one names ran in columns, as if checking they were warm.

Ulyana stood at the back and let it happen around her. This was the part she'd come for. Not the striking of her own file — though when the clerk handed it over, thin now, gutted, she'd felt seven years of weight leave her shoulders so suddenly she'd nearly staggered — but this. Names read aloud in a government hall until they stuck. The disappeared, getting their names again.

It wasn't sweet. Nothing about it was sweet. Halvard was dead, drowned saving a case of records that proved his own family had been erased, and no ruling would give him back. The site beneath the basin was sealed now — properly sealed, flooded and concreted and mapped, its seductive archive locked away from anyone else who might hear it promising belonging at the price of everything else. Tamsin would carry the sound of the water for the rest of her life. And somewhere out there, Ivo's sister's final entry waited in a satchel, unread by him, because he hadn't been able to open it yet.

Quiet justice. Unsweetened. It would have to do. It was, Ulyana thought, the only kind that ever lasted.

* * *

Evening found her on the river embankment below the Hall, where the capital's channel ran wide and slow and utterly tame compared to the Sable. She had her old papers out — the originals, the ones she'd carried through two countries and one monsoon out of a superstition she'd never examined. Blacklist copies, transit denials, a deportation notice with her name misspelled. Seven years of proof that she was unwelcome everywhere.

She didn't burn them here. That was for the riverbank that mattered, and the Sable was four hundred kilometers north, and there was one thing left to do before she went back to it.

Ivo came down the embankment steps and stood beside her, hands in his pockets, looking at the water the way he looked at all water: reading it.

"The commission approved the recovery expeditions," he said. "Spring season. They need guides who know the basin." A pause. "They asked for you by name. Ostrevik himself."

"Did they ask for a cartographer too?"

"They asked for a cartographer who knows the false channels." He glanced at her. "I may have mentioned one."

Ulyana looked north along the tame river, toward the country where the real one waited — the drowned villages, the stilt village that wasn't on charts, the confluence where a lone man in a canvas skiff had once intercepted a boat and called it chance. There was a satchel in Ivo's room with a final entry in it that he still hadn't read, and a river up there that had known his sister's name before either of them did.

"Finish your map first," she said. "All of it. Every channel. Then we'll see what the water wants."

Ivo nodded slowly, and for the first time since the basin, since the vault, since the storm — he reached into his coat and drew out the folded second map, the private one, and held it a moment before putting it away again. Like a man deciding he was finally ready to open a letter.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I'll read it tomorrow. Her last entry. Will you—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "Come with me. When I do."

Ulyana looked at the water, and at the man beside her who had chosen this meeting on a confluence long before she ever knew his name, and she found that the answer cost her nothing at all.

"Yes," she said. "Then north. The river's waiting, and I'm done making it wait for me."
