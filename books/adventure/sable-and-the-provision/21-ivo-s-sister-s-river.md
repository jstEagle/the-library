## 21. Ivo's Sister's River

The capital smelled of diesel and wet stone, and Ivo hated it the way he hated all cities — the way a bird hates a room.

He had been in the archive annex for six days. The inquiry had taken the cases Ulyana carried out of the basin, catalogued them, stamped them, and begun the slow bureaucratic miracle of turning names back into people. Tamsin testified in a gray room with a fan that clicked. Ulyana testified twice, once for the record and once, furiously, when a clerk suggested the site's location be redacted. Halvard's name went into the ledger as drowned in service — which was true, Ivo thought, if you allowed that the river could be a government.

Ivo did not testify. He was not on any list. That had been his whole life: not being on lists.

What he did was sit in the reading room of the recovered records with white gloves on his hands and his sister's name on a request slip, waiting for them to bring him her final entry.

They brought it at four o'clock on the sixth day, in a gray folder, and the archivist watched him with the particular pity reserved for men about to learn something they already knew.

* * *

Her handwriting had not changed.

That was the first thing. Eleven years, an erased expedition, a sealed vault under a flooded basin — and Vera Kestrel still wrote her *r*'s like little flags, still crossed her *t*'s too far left, still pressed hard enough to dent three pages down. He had spent half his life learning to read maps because she used to draw them in the margins of her letters, and here she was again, drawing him one last map in the margins of her last words.

*If you are reading this, you found the provision, which means you found my map, which means you never stopped looking, which is the most Ivo thing there is.*

He laughed. It came out wrong, a dry cracked sound in the silent reading room, and the archivist looked up from her desk.

*I need you to understand something before you do anything brave,* the entry continued. *We were not taken. I know what the reports will say, what the families were told. We found the vault in the second week and we were given a choice, and I am telling you plainly, little brother: I chose.*

*You will want to make this into a crime so you can grieve it properly. Grief needs a villain. But the truth is worse and better: the thing down there doesn't take anyone. It shows you everything you lost and then it asks. And everyone I loved was in those files. Mama's deportation order. Papa's real name, the one he died without. Our street, before. I sat on the floor of that vault and read our family back into existence, and then they asked me to stay and keep it safe, and Ivo — what was I supposed to walk away from? My own name?*

*You always thought I ran ahead of you. I didn't. I stopped. There's a difference, and it took me eleven years and a flood to learn it.*

*The map is not a summons. I drew it as a goodbye. If you're reading this, don't come to the basin. It's going under again, permanently, and what's worth saving is already in your hands. Go somewhere and stay somewhere. That's all I ever wanted for either of us — one place where our names meant something.*

*Draw your own rivers now. You were always better at it than me anyway.*

*V.*

Below the signature, in the margin, a small sketch: two figures in a boat, rendered in her flag-like r's and overcrossed t's, moving toward a confluence where two lines became one.

Ivo read it four times. Then he took off the gloves, folded the pages along their original creases, and asked the archivist for a copying fee schedule, because some things you carry and some things you leave in official custody, and he had finally learned the difference.

* * *

Ulyana found him on the embankment steps at dusk, eating nothing and watching the river traffic, which was how she found him most evenings now. She sat down without asking, one step above him, the way she always arranged herself — slightly uphill even on flat ground. Habit of a woman who'd spent a decade reading terrain.

"You got the folder," she said.

"You checked."

"I checked." She turned a pebble over with her boot heel. "Tamsin told me you'd been in the reading room since morning. She said you came out looking like a man who'd won an argument with a ghost."

"That's roughly accurate."

"And?"

Ivo looked at the water. Barges, ferries, a patrol cutter running lights it no longer needed to run. Somewhere south, the Sable was doing what the Sable did, rising over its banks with the patience of something that had buried villages before and would again.

"She stayed," he said. "Willingly. She wanted me to know that. She wanted me to stop making it a crime scene in my head." He worked the folded copy out of his jacket and held it a moment, then put it back. "Eleven years I built a rescue around a woman who didn't want rescuing. I charted false channels for a living because she disappeared into one. And her last message is basically: go home, idiot."

"What are you going to do?"

"Something unprecedented." He glanced up at her. "Obey her."

Ulyana almost smiled. On her face it registered as weather — a brief clearing. "The inquiry offered me work," she said. "Recovery expeditions. Guide contract, seasonal, basin-based. They want someone who reads the river better than the charts do."

"They'd be idiots not to."

"They nearly were, twice." She flicked the pebble into the water. "It's a posting, Ivo. A fixed point. A house, eventually, maybe. I haven't had an address since—" She stopped, recalculated. "I've never had an address I chose."

"The village would put you on their chart," Ivo said. "They told me. Elders talk faster than bureaucrats."

"The elders also told me you cried at dinner."

"The elders are liars and will answer for it."

She laughed — actually laughed, short and rusty, like a door opening after years. Below them the river kept moving, indifferent and generous at once, carrying everything toward the sea except the things that decided to stay.

* * *

They left the capital together because they were both headed south, and parted — or almost parted — at the confluence, because that was where the routes divided, or appeared to.

The morning was monsoon-washed and clean, the air scoured, the Sable running high and brown-gold. His skiff sat low in the water, newly caulked; her boat rode beside it, patched and stubborn, like its owner. The stilt village was two days downriver. The recovery staging camp was three days up the eastern channel. Between them lay the confluence, wide as a lake, where every current they'd followed all season braided together and sorted itself out.

"So," Ulyana said.

"So."

"This is where you say something charming and get in your boat."

"It is where I say something charming," Ivo agreed, "and then fail to get in my boat, apparently, because my feet have stopped taking orders."

She looked at him. He had learned, over leaking hulls and searchlights and one long night among drowned trees, to read her silences the way he read contour lines — the close ones meant steep country, and hers were very close just then.

"Your sister's map," she said. "The original. The one from the provision. May I see it?"

He handed it over. She unrolled it across both gunwales, weighting the corners, and studied it for a long time. Her finger traced the main channel, the false channels, the mark at the vault, the small annotation in Vera's hand that Ivo had never been able to parse.

"*For whoever survived long enough to need this,*" Ulyana read aloud. "That's what it says under the title. Not 'for whoever comes looking.' For whoever survives." She looked up. "She drew this for strangers, Ivo. For whoever made it. She just hoped it would be you."

"I know."

"Do you?" She rolled the map and gave it back. "Because you've been treating it like a letter addressed to one man, and maps don't work that way. Maps are addressed to everyone. That's the whole point of them." She stepped back onto her own deck. "The recovery expeditions will need charts. Real ones. Of the channels that shift, the ones that lie. Someone who knows the river's false channels intimately."

"That's a suspiciously specific job description."

"I stole it from a cartographer I met at a confluence," Ulyana said. "He claimed to be charting flood lines. He watched everyone too carefully. I hired him on suspicion and never fired him."

Ivo stood in his skiff and felt the current take hold of both boats equally, gently, like a hand offering rather than pulling. His sister had drawn two figures in a boat at a confluence where two lines became one. He had assumed the drawing was memory. Standing here, he understood it might have been instruction.

"The pay is terrible," he said.

"The pay is a village that puts your name on its chart."

"The hours are monsoon."

"The hours are monsoon," she agreed. "And the benefits include a guide who will absolutely leave you behind if you slow her down."

"Noted. I'll keep up." He shipped his oars and let his skiff drift alongside hers, close enough that the hulls touched, knocked, settled — two boats finding the same heading. "Confluence rules," he said. "Two channels become one. Nobody loses their name."

"Nobody loses their name," Ulyana said, and pushed off, and the current took them together down the wide gold water toward the village that wasn't on any chart yet — toward the place where, tomorrow or the day after, an elder with a steady hand would add two new lines to the old map, side by side, running the same way.

Behind them the capital shrank. Ahead the basin opened, flooded and fertile and full of names coming back.

Ivo took out his sister's map one more time, and instead of folding it away, he laid it flat on the thwart and began, in the empty margin below her two small figures, to draw a third line — the new channel, the one that hadn't existed until this year, the one they would chart together.

His pen moved easily. For the first time in eleven years, he wasn't tracing where someone had gone.

He was drawing where they were going.
