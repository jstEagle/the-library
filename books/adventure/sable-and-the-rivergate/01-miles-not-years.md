## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Sable River did not keep calendars. It kept accounts.

Gideon Dunmore had learned that in their ninth year of salvage work, kneeling on the deck of the *Kestrel* with a grease pencil and a ledger of their own—not the kind that recorded debts owed to banks or landlords, but the kind that measured a life in fathoms and river miles. Three hundred and forty miles from the delta bar to the last navigable bend above Saltmarsh. Eleven tollgates, four of them honest. A hundred and six marked wrecks in their private chart, each one a small wound in the river's skin where something had gone down and stayed down.

That was how you counted a life when you didn't want it counted any other way.

They rolled the pencil between their fingers and looked out over the water at first light, when the Sable went the color of old pewter and the mist came off it in slow ribbons, like the river exhaling after a long night of holding its breath. The *Kestrel* sat at anchor off Cobbler's Shoal, riding low and patient, her hull patched so many times she was less a vessel than an argument—teak arguing with ironwood arguing with three generations of someone else's caulking. She'd been a grain barge once, before Gideon's time. Now she was home, workshop, and coffin-in-waiting, though Gideon tried not to think about that last part before breakfast.

"Bottom's at nineteen feet," Bram said behind them, spitting over the rail out of habit even though there was nothing down there but water and silt. "Nineteen and a half if you believe the lead line, which I don't, because Nils re-marked it drunk."

"I re-marked it *accurately*," Nils called from the wheelhouse, where he was hunched over his charts with a cup of chicory going cold beside him. "There's a difference."

"There is not a difference."

"There is a *moral* difference."

Gideon let them argue. It was a good sound, better than the alternative, which was silence—and Gideon had done enough years of silence to know its exact weight, how it settled on a boat like ballast you couldn't jettison. The bickering meant the crew was awake. Awake meant alive. Alive meant today would be a working day, and working days were the only kind worth having.

Sefa emerged from the engine hatch wearing the expression she reserved for machinery that had personally insulted her. She was a small woman built like a knotted rope, forearms scarred with burns older than Gideon's career, and she carried a wrench the way other people carried opinions: constantly, and ready to use.

"Dredge winch is sulking," she announced. "Third gear's got play in it. If we pull anything heavier than mud today, it'll strip."

"How much play?"

"Enough." She wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last season. "I can shim it. Give me an hour."

"You have forty minutes," Gideon said. "Tide turns at eight."

"Tide turns at eight," Sefa repeated, in the tone of a woman filing the information somewhere it could be used against them later, and dropped back into the hatch.

Gideon turned back to the water.

Cobbler's Shoal wasn't on any official chart worth trusting. The Crown surveys showed it as a shallow, unremarkable stretch where the river spread wide and lazy around a gravel spine, good for nothing but reeds and herons. But the river kept its own books, and Gideon had learned to read them—the way the current dimpled over a buried hull, the way the reeds grew in a slightly different green where the substrate shifted, the way gulls argued over a stretch of water that should have held nothing worth arguing about. Something lay under the shoal. Something with edges. And this morning, for reasons Gideon couldn't have explained to anyone without sounding like a fortune-teller, they wanted it up.

Not for treasure. Treasure was a word for people who hadn't yet learned what the river actually gave you, which was mostly mud, mostly grief, and occasionally—rarely, beautifully—a thing that could be sold for enough coin to buy diesel, rope, food, and another month of being no one in particular.

Being no one in particular was the whole trade, really. That was the secret nobody put in the pamphlets they handed out at the dockside hiring halls. Salvage wasn't about diving. Diving was just the labor. The actual profession, the one Gideon had practiced for nine years with the devotion of a monk, was *unbelonging*. You belonged to no port, no family register, no guild roll, no name.

Especially no name.

"Gid." Bram had come up beside them, close enough that Gideon could smell the pipe smoke in his beard. He was the biggest man aboard and moved like he was trying to take up less room than he did—a habit from some former life he never discussed, though the way he stood, always angled toward exits, always cataloguing hands and hip lines for weapons, told its own story. "You've been staring at that water since before the birds woke up. What are we doing today?"

"Working the shoal."

"The shoal." He said it flatly. "The shoal that every salvager between here and the delta has worked twice and found nothing but eel bones."

"They worked it with grapples and greed." Gideon nodded at the water. "Nobody's dragged it slow. Nobody's listened to it."

Bram looked at the river, then at Gideon, then back at the river, in the manner of a man who had long ago stopped expecting explanations and had learned to be content with results. "Forty minutes, then. I'll get the lines rigged."

He went aft. Gideon stayed at the rail a moment longer.

Below the pewter surface, nineteen feet down, the light would be the color of weak tea. The silt hung in it forever, never settling, so that diving the Sable was like swimming through amber with something always just at the edge of vision—always almost a shape, always almost a face. Most divers hated it. Gideon had made their peace with it years ago. Down there, you couldn't see far enough to be afraid of what was coming. You could only deal with what your hands found. There was a cleanliness in that. A mercy.

Their mother had hated diving. That was one of the few things Gideon still allowed themselves to remember clearly—that and the smell of her study, lamp oil and paper dust and river clay, and the way she'd tap her pen twice against her teeth when a theory wouldn't come right. Miriam Dunmore had spent her life mapping what the water covered, but she'd done it from the surface, from shorelines and survey boats, sending other people down into the dark while she stayed up in the light with her charts and her theories and her expedition crews full of men whose names Gideon refused to recall.

Five years dead now. Longer since Gideon had spoken to her. Both facts were true at once, and Gideon had never figured out how to make them sit together, so they'd simply stopped trying. They'd changed ports. They'd shortened the name—Dunmore became Dun, Dun became nothing, because dockmasters didn't ask for surnames if you paid cash and didn't flinch. The *Kestrel* was registered to a shell company Nils had drawn up with a cartographer's precision, layers of paper like the strata of a riverbed, and somewhere at the bottom of all those layers, if anyone ever dug, they'd find a name Gideon hadn't spoken aloud in years.

It was easier this way. That was what they told themselves. Easier for everyone. A famous archaeologist's estranged child, dredging wrecks for coins and copper, would be a story somebody would eventually want to print, and stories brought inspectors, and inspectors brought questions, and questions were the one cargo the *Kestrel* could never carry.

The bell on the foremast rang once—Bram, signaling lines rigged—and Gideon pushed off the rail and went to work.

* * *

By half past eight the sun had burned the mist off and the Sable had turned a heavy, opaque green, and Gideon stood on the dive platform in the suit Sefa had rebuilt from three dead suits, checking seals by feel while the crew watched with the particular boredom of people who had seen this ritual two hundred times and would still watch it two hundred more, because watching was how you proved you cared without saying so.

"Current's slack," Nils reported from the rail, a lead line dripping in one hand. "One-eighteen feet per second at surface, less below. You'll drift maybe two yards east by the time you're down."

"Two yards. Generous of it." Gideon pulled on the helmet, and the world went quiet and round and smelled of rubber and old brass. The last thing they saw before the faceplate sealed was Sefa's face, upside-down from their perspective, mouthing the words she mouthed every single time: *Come back up.*

Then the river took them.

Down through the green, hand over hand along the shot line, ears popping, the light dying by degrees from tea to umber to a brown so complete it was less a color than a texture. At twelve feet the cold found the seams of the suit. At sixteen feet Gideon's boots touched bottom and the silt bloomed up around them like ink poured into water, and for a long moment there was nothing in the universe except the fog of it and the sound of their own breathing, loud as surf inside the helmet.

They waited. Patience was the whole craft. The silt would settle or it wouldn't; either way, rushing it bought nothing. Gideon knelt in the murk and swept their hands in slow arcs, reading the bottom the way Nils read charts—by touch, by inference, by the accumulated grammar of a thousand dives.

Gravel. Gravel. Gravel, then a hard edge.

There.

Gideon followed the edge. It ran straight for six feet, then turned a clean right angle. No wreck timber broke at right angles after fifty years in a river; wood went soft and splintery, gave up its geometry. This was iron. Riveted, by the feel of the heads under their gloves, and laid flat, half-buried, running away north under the shoal like the corner of something enormous sleeping under a blanket of gravel.

A hull. A big one. Bigger than anything that should have been lost at Cobbler's Shoal, which was barely deep enough to float a loaded skiff at low water—which meant it hadn't been lost here at all. It had been carried. A flood year, maybe; the Sable in flood could move houses. Some big vessel had come apart upstream decades ago and one piece of her, one great iron plate or section of keel, had traveled down the river's long memory and lodged here, and everything else had buried it, and the river had kept the secret the way the river kept all its secrets: patiently, and without malice.

Gideon's pulse kicked up despite themselves. Not treasure—never treasure—but *size*. Size meant salvage value. An iron plate that size, cut free and winched up, would pay for Sefa's new gears and a winter's coal besides.

They were reaching for the marker buoy line to signal *found, worth cutting* when their left hand, sweeping wide along the plate's edge, closed on something that was not iron.

It was rope. Old rope, tarred, still holding a knot.

And tied to the rope, small and dense and unmistakable even through glove leather, was a ring of metal stamped with a seal.

Gideon went very still, kneeling on the grave of a shipwreck in the brown dark, holding a thing the size of a plum, and felt the whole careful architecture of their morning tilt.

Because they knew that shape. Knew it the way you know a face from a dream—know it before you know that you know it. A circle within a circle, and between them, raised under their thumb, a design like a bridge over falling water, or a gate over a river. Their thumb traced it twice, three times, as if repetition might change the answer.

It didn't. Nothing ever did.

There had been a sketch pinned above the desk in Miriam Dunmore's study, charcoal on survey paper, drawn in her quick sure hand: a seal, circular, a gate above falling water, and beneath it a word in a language Gideon had been too young to read. *Vessany*, she'd said, when child-Gideon asked. A country that wasn't a country anymore. Erased off the maps before your grandmother was born, love. Which is exactly why it matters. Maps are written by the winners, and rivers remember what maps forget.

She'd laughed when she said it. Gideon remembered the laugh. Remembered thinking it sounded like a door closing.

Upstairs, muffled through nineteen feet of green water and the hull of the world, the *Kestrel*'s engine changed pitch—Sefa testing the winch, or signaling, or just being Sefa. The sound reached down thin and distant, another country's music. Gideon knelt in the dark with the seal pressed against their palm through the glove, and the current moved faintly overhead, and the silt drifted, and somewhere above them the ordinary day went on being ordinary, tide tables and diesel and chicory, while down here the past put its cold hand around theirs and did not let go.

They should signal the buoy. Should mark the find, surface, log it, get the crew moving, treat it like any other plate of iron and length of dead rope. That was the discipline. That was the competence. Nine years of competence, worn like armor, and it fit well, and it had never once failed them.

But their thumb kept moving over the little gate above the falling water, tracing their mother's ghost in raised metal, and for the first time in five years Gideon Dunmore—who measured life in miles precisely so they'd never have to measure it in years—felt the river miles beneath them rearrange themselves into a road leading somewhere they had sworn never to go again.

They clipped the seal to their belt. They'd explain it on the surface. Or they wouldn't.

Then, because the hands knew their work even when the heart had stopped knowing anything, Gideon turned back to follow the iron plate to its end—and found, wedged where the plate met the shoal's gravel spine, a shape too regular, too square, too deliberate to be anything the river had made.

Something down here had been placed, not lost.

Gideon's breath went loud in the helmet. Above, faint and far, the winch began to turn.
