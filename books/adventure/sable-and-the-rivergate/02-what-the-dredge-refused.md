## 2. What the Dredge Refused

The river gave nothing up easily, and it never gave anything up for free.

Gideon Dunmore had learned that lesson the way the Sable taught all its lessons—slowly, at depth, with cold pressing in against the ribs like a patient creditor. Eleven years of salvage work had made a kind of liturgy out of it: the winch groan, the cable's shudder, the particular tone a dredge bucket sang when it struck something that did not want to be lifted. Most days that something was silt, or chain, or the ribs of some drowned barge older than anyone alive to remember her name. Most days you cursed, cut loose, and moved on.

But this morning the dredge had stopped singing altogether.

"Dead again," Sefa called down from the deckhouse, her voice carrying that flat irritation she reserved for machinery that had personally offended her. She leaned out over the rail, grease-black hands braced on weathered wood, and squinted at the water below as if she could shame it into cooperation by stare alone. "Third snag since the narrows. Cable's taut as a harp string and the drum won't turn. Either we've hooked the river's spine, or somebody's sunk an anchor the size of a chapel."

Gideon stood at the Kestrel's stern, one boot up on the coiled spare line, watching the brown water slide past with the unhurried confidence of something that had been here long before any of them and would be here long after. The morning fog was burning off in ragged sheets. Somewhere upstream a heron complained about its own existence.

"Where's the mark?" Gideon asked.

Sefa pointed without looking. "Forty yards off the port bow. Maybe less. It dragged us a boat-length before it set."

Bram came up from below decks rubbing sleep out of his eyes, which was a performance—he slept like a cat, one ear always open, and everyone aboard knew it. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray coming into his beard, with the settled calm of someone who had done terrible things in his youth and had made his peace with them in installments. "Could be wreckage," he offered. "There was a grain barge lost near here. Before the war. The *Merrow*, I think."

"The *Merrow* went down twelve miles south," Nils said from the bow, not looking up from the chart he had unrolled across a crate and weighted at the corners with four brass washers. Nils was twenty-three and had the pale, underfed look of someone who had spent more years indoors with maps than outdoors with weather. "I've got her position marked. This is clean bottom. Or it's supposed to be." He frowned at the chart, then at the river, as though the two were having an argument behind his back.

"Clean bottom doesn't snag a dredge," Bram said.

"No," Gideon agreed quietly. "It doesn't."

That was the thing about salvage—the whole trade lived in that gap between what the charts said and what the river knew. Every salvager Gideon had ever met carried two kinds of knowledge: the kind you could buy in a chandlery, printed neat on vellum, and the kind you earned in your lungs, in the dark, with your fingers reading the mud like a blind woman reads a face. The second kind was the only one worth anything.

Gideon pulled on the diving dress without hurry. There was no sense rushing the river; it punished haste the way it punished greed. The canvas suit smelled of tar and old rubber and the ghost of every previous dive, and when Sefa sealed the collar around their throat they felt the familiar small death of it—the world going muffled, the breath becoming a thing that happened through a hose instead of a mouth.

"Air's good for forty minutes," Sefa said, close enough now that her voice reached through the helmet's opening before the front plate went on. Her dark eyes held Gideon's for a moment longer than the job required. She had a way of doing that—checking, silently, the way a cook checks a flame. "Current's slack but it'll turn within the hour. Don't make me come down after you."

"You'd hate that," Gideon said, voice already sounding strange inside the copper.

"I'd hate losing my diver more." She fixed the faceplate herself, turned the wing nuts with quick sure fingers, and the world became a circle of glass and the sound of Gideon's own breathing, vast and tidal.

They lowered Gideon over the side on the stage line, and the river took them the way it took everything: completely, without malice, without welcome.

* * *

Down was a country with its own weather.

The light failed fast—three fathoms of amber, then three of umber, then nothing but the yellow cone of the helmet lamp pushing back the dark like a man holding a door against a crowd. The visibility was worse than usual; the recent rains upstream had fed the Sable a meal of red clay, and the water tasted of it even through the air line, iron and earth and something faintly vegetal, like the inside of an old book.

Gideon went down the cable hand over hand. That was the first rule of a snagged dredge: follow the wire. The bucket hung somewhere below, jammed against whatever had refused it, and the wire would run straight as a plumb line to the trouble.

At six fathoms the bottom rose to meet them—or rather, Gideon realized, the bottom had *changed*. The soft silt that should have swallowed the boots whole gave way to something harder, packed clay scattered with stones, and the stones were wrong. River stone is round and patient, worn into agreement with the water. These were squared. Dressed. Laid.

Gideon knelt and put a glove flat against one. Under the film of silt there was a groove—a channel cut with intent, running away into the dark. A paving joint. The lamp swung and caught more of them, receding in a line, and beyond the line a shape too regular to be geology: an arch, collapsed on one side, furred with weed and crusted with a century of river's slow cement.

Old work. Older than the Crown, older than the Trust, older than the maps Nils loved so dearly. The Sable coast had swallowed more kingdoms than it remembered, and it kept their bones the way a miser keeps coins—down where no one thought to count.

Gideon had found drowned foundations before. It wasn't rare, not really, not along this stretch where the old river road had run before the floods took it. But something about the arch made the skin crawl beneath the canvas suit—not fear exactly, but recognition's cold cousin. The feeling of standing in a room you've been in before, in a dream.

The cable ran past the arch, deeper into the hollow the current had scoured, and Gideon followed it, boots crunching softly on the drowned pavement, breath roaring gently in the helmet like surf heard through a wall.

The chest sat in the scour hole like a thing placed rather than dropped.

That was the first wrongness. Wreckage scatters. Cargo tumbles and rolls and comes to rest wherever gravity and current conspire to leave it, half-buried, askew, ashamed. But this chest lay level on a shelf of stone, oriented true, its lid facing up, as though someone had set it down carefully and stepped back—and the scouring current, which should have buried it years ago, had instead kept it swept clean, circling it like a dog told to guard.

It was lead-lined. Gideon could see the dull gray metal where the wood had split along one corner, and lead meant one thing in the salvage trade: someone had wanted the contents kept from water, yes—but also from eyes. Lead defeated the magnet-sweepers the Trust boats dragged for contraband. Lead defeated casual discovery. Lead was a choice, and choices like that were never innocent.

Gideon circled it once, slowly. The dredge bucket had bitten into the lid's edge—that was the snag—and the iron teeth had peeled back a strip of the lead sheathing like the rind of a fruit. Beneath it, wood. Dark wood, dense as ironwood, hardly swollen at all despite its drowning. And across the lid, stamped deep into the lead itself, a seal.

Gideon's breath stopped.

Not because the seal was beautiful, though it was—a device pressed with the confidence of a state that had never doubted its own permanence: a bridge spanning water, and above the bridge a star with eight points, and around the rim letters in a script that predated the colonial alphabet on these coasts, angular and assured.

It was beautiful the way a face from an old photograph is beautiful. It was familiar the way a scar is familiar.

Because Gideon knew that seal. Had known it since before knowing anything else, it seemed. A study with green light through shutters, dust turning in the slant of afternoon, and a mother who sketched when she talked, pen moving across butcher paper while she explained things too large for a child's understanding—empires, erasures, the way a nation can die twice, once in blood and once in ink. She had drawn this exact device once, hatched in careful pencil strokes, and tapped it with her finger and said words Gideon had spent eleven years pretending not to remember:

*"Vessany. They erased it, little wren. Not destroyed—erased. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. When they burned it they left records. When they erased it they left nothing—no maps, no names, no graves marked. A country that never was. And the people who do such things always leave one thing behind, because they cannot help themselves."*

"What thing?" a younger Gideon had asked.

"*A lock,*" she'd said, tapping the eight-pointed star. "*They keep the key.*"

The memory broke apart like silt in current. Gideon hung in the green dark, six fathoms down, breath hammering through the hose, staring at a seal that existed nowhere on any map or document still permitted to exist—and understood, with the cold certainty of the drowned, that the river had not snagged the dredge by accident.

Nothing about this chest was accident. Not its placement. Not its cleanliness. Not the lead. Someone had hidden it here, in the shadow of work older than memory, and hidden it well enough that a century of river traffic had passed over it like weather over a grave.

And someone else—someone with a mother's voice and a dead woman's certainty—had known it might be here. Had maybe spent years looking. Had died looking, if the rumors that drifted downriver with the flotsam of gossip were worth anything.

Gideon's hands, steady through a hundred dives, were not steady now. The wonder came first—it came like heat, like light breaking on the surface far above, the pure salvage-worker's awe of touching something genuinely unknown—and then, following it down the same wire, the dread. Because things this well hidden are hidden for reasons, and reasons like this have weight, and weight like this has a way of surfacing all at once, dragging everything attached with it.

The chest was heavy. Far heavier than its size accounted for. Gideon rigged the lifting sling with fingers that wanted to shake, double-checking each hitch the way Sefa had taught them in the first year—*the river forgives nothing twice*—and gave the signal line three sharp pulls.

Above, faintly, the winch began to turn.

* * *

The chest came up through the water like a confession.

By the time it broke the surface, the whole crew was at the rail. Nobody spoke. The Kestrel seemed to hold her breath, listing slightly toward the weight, and the river gave it up with a final reluctant surge, streaming brown water from every seam, the lead sheathing gleaming dully where the dredge had torn it.

"Stand back," Bram said, and his voice had changed—gone quiet, gone careful, the voice of a man who had handled things that exploded. He planted himself between the chest and the others without appearing to decide to. Old habits.

Sefa got the hook clear and they swung it inboard onto the main hatch cover, where it landed with a boom that Gideon felt through the soles of their boots. Water ran from it in sheets. Up close, the damage was clearer: the dredge tooth had gouged the lid, peeling lead and splitting wood in a jagged line, and through the gap showed darkness, and within the darkness—just visible—the corner of something pale and flat. Paper. Or vellum.

Paper, after a century underwater. Inside lead.

Nils arrived at the chest the way he arrived at everything, sideways and late, chart still rolled in one fist like a baton he'd forgotten to put down. He looked at the seal and went very still, in the particular way of a cartographer confronting a thing that should not exist. "That's not a merchant mark," he said finally. His voice had gone thin. "Merchant marks don't get stamped in lead. That's a *state* seal. That's—" He stopped himself, jaw working, because the name he wanted to say was a name that appeared on no chart he owned, and saying it aloud felt like stepping off an edge.

"Say it," Gideon said.

"Vessany." The word fell among them and nobody moved, the way birds don't move when a hawk's shadow crosses. "But Vessany isn't real. It's—there's no record. No treaty ever mentions it, no map, no—"

"It's real," Gideon said.

The crew looked over. Gideon hadn't meant to speak. The words had come up from somewhere below the thinking part, from the green place where a memory waited in a shuttered study, and now they were out, and Gideon's throat was tight around the shape of them.

"How would you know that?" Bram asked. Not suspicious—Bram didn't do suspicion; he did assessment, the flat weighing of facts. But the question landed hard anyway, because there was only one honest answer and Gideon had spent eleven years making sure no one asked questions that required it.

"My mother drew it once." Gideon kept their eyes on the chest. Easier than faces. "A sketch. In her study. She called it Vessany. Said it had been erased." A pause, and then, because lying to this crew was a thing Gideon found they could not make their hands do: "She was an archaeologist. Miriam Dunmore."

The name landed harder than the chest had. Sefa's wrench stopped mid-turn. Nils actually sat down on a coil of rope, abruptly, like a puppet with a cut string. Everyone on the Sable coast knew the name Dunmore—the expedition that went upriver seven years ago with nine souls and came back with four, the inquiry that found no fault in anyone, the widow-mother—no, not widow, the *mother* who never came back at all, whose effects had been seized and sold to pay the expedition's debts to the very trust that had licensed her. Everyone knew the name, and everyone aboard knew Gideon never used it, never answered to it, signed the Kestrel's papers with a single initial and a surname borrowed from nowhere.

"Gideon," Sefa said carefully, "that's the expedition that—"

"I know what it is." Sharper than intended. Gideon breathed out through their nose, made themself unclench their hands. "I know what it is."

For a moment the only sounds were the river against the hull and the creak of the chest settling, water still finding its way out of the torn seam, dripping onto the hatch in a slow count.

It was Bram who moved first. He crouched by the chest, studied the seal for a long moment, then looked up. "Lead lining," he said. "State seal. Hidden placement. Somebody went to a lot of trouble to make sure this stayed down there until it was ready to be found—or until it was never found at all." He straightened. "Question is which."

"And the other question," Sefa said, wiping her hands slowly on her rag, though they were already clean, "is who else knows. Because things like this don't stay lost by luck. They stay lost because somebody keeps them lost." She nodded at the torn seam, at the pale corner of paper showing in the dark. "And we just pulled it up in broad daylight, forty yards off a shipping lane, with a dredge that half the river heard screaming."

Nobody had an answer to that. The fog had burned off entirely now, and the morning lay open and bright around them, the Sable glittering like nothing had happened, like it hadn't just handed them a century-old secret in a lead coffin. Downriver, maybe ten miles, the toll station at Marsh Point would be logging traffic. The Trust's patrol cutter made its rounds on Thursdays. Today was Thursday.

"We should dive it again," Nils said suddenly. All of them looked at him. He had his chart out again, unrolled, and his finger was pressed flat against the spot where they floated, and there was something new in his face—the hunger Gideon had only seen in him when a map surprised him. "The site. The arch, the paving—I saw it in your face when you came up. If there's a chest, there's a reason it was put *there*. Sites mean context. Context means—" He caught himself, reined it in, but his eyes stayed bright. "We should know what else is down there before someone else does."

"He's right," Bram said. "And we should decide fast. That snag will be reported. Dredge crews talk. Dockhands talk. Everything on this river eventually flows downhill to the Trust, same as the water."

Gideon looked at the chest. At the eight-pointed star under its crust of river grime, at the bridge and the water it spanned, at the torn seam and the paper waiting inside like a held breath. Somewhere behind the ribs, in the place where grief keeps its accounts, something that had been sleeping for five years turned over and opened one eye.

*They keep the key,* Miriam had said. *They always leave one thing behind.*

"Get us underway," Gideon said. "South, toward the estuary anchorage. We'll open it behind closed water, with the hatches down." A beat. "And Nils—mark our position. Exact. To the foot."

Nils was already writing.

As the Kestrel came about and the river took her south, Gideon stayed at the stern rail and watched the site fall astern, the brown water closing over the drowned arch like a page being turned. The chest sat on the hatch behind them, streaming its last water, patient as it had been patient for a hundred years.

It had waited this long. It could wait until nightfall.

But Gideon could not shake the feeling—cold, certain, rising through them like a bubble through fathomless water—that the waiting was over for everyone. That somewhere downriver, in a tidy office smelling of ink and law, a claim form was already being filled out. That the river, which gave nothing up for free, had just named its price.

They just didn't know yet what it was.
