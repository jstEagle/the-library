## 3. The Trust's Paper

The Colonial Salvage Trust kept its offices on the third floor of the Customs Annex, in rooms that had once belonged to the harbor pilot's guild and still smelled faintly of their pipe smoke, though every one of those men was forty years drowned or dispersed. Inspector Aldous Halloran had occupied the corner office for eleven years and had never once opened the window that overlooked the river. He did not care for the smell of it—mud and diesel and something older underneath, something vegetal and patient—and he did not care for what it did to his papers, which curled at their edges on damp days like living things trying to escape.

He preferred paper that behaved.

At nine minutes past seven on the morning after the *Kestrel* hauled her strange catch out of the channel, Halloran was already at his desk, as he was every morning before the clerks arrived, working through the overnight manifests with a steel-nibbed pen and a pot of tea gone bitter from sitting. He was a narrow man, gray at the temples, dressed in a suit the color of wet slate, and he had the particular stillness of a man who had learned long ago that most information came to those who waited without moving. His office held three things of which he was proud: a framed certificate of commission signed by the Trust's founding governor; a wall map of the Sable watershed, annotated in his own small hand with every wreck, claim, and dispute of the past decade; and silence. The silence was the most valuable.

The knock came at twenty to eight. Early. Halloran noted the hour the way a fisherman notes a change in wind.

"Enter."

Dockmaster's clerk Tulliver came in sideways, hat in hand, leaving mud prints on the good floor. He was a soft-bellied man with a face like rising dough and the eyes of someone who had been paid to be somewhere he shouldn't be, and who found the transaction distasteful but not distasteful enough to refuse.

"Inspector. Thought you'd want to hear it before it goes round the taverns."

Halloran set down his pen with care, aligning it parallel to the blotter's edge. "Hear what, Mr. Tulliver."

"Dredge snag, night before last. Out past the Middle Reach." Tulliver wiped his palms on his trousers. "Gideon Dunmore's barge. The *Kestrel*."

The name landed in the room and sat there. Halloran did not react to it, because reacting was a habit he had trained out of himself decades ago, first in the customs service and then here, where the men who reacted were the men who lost claims. But beneath the desk, his thumb pressed once against the barrel of the pen.

*Dunmore.* The name was on his map. Not the barge—the mother. Miriam Dunmore's expedition permits, her bond postings, her final, catastrophic license renewal, all of it filed in the Trust's deep cabinets in Halloran's own careful hand, eleven years ago, when he'd been a junior examiner and the Dunmore name had meant money and prestige and, eventually, an inquest he'd been ordered to sit through in a room too hot, listening to testimony about a river that took people and gave nothing back.

"Go on," he said.

"They brought up a chest. Lead-lined, by the talk of it. Sealed. Some kind of old stamp on the lid nobody could read." Tulliver leaned in, lowering his voice as though the walls had ears, which, in a sense, they did; Halloran paid two of them. "Dunmore's crew hauled it aboard themselves. Didn't report it. Didn't post notice at the harbor office. Just—" Tulliver made a vague gesture, a man tucking something away. "Took it in."

"You saw this yourself."

"I heard it from Osk, on the dredge tender. And Osk doesn't lie about tonnage." A pause, the doughy face working. "There's the matter of my discretion, Inspector. Discretion isn't free these days. My wife's got her hip—"

"Yes." Halloran opened the drawer at his right hand, counted out notes without looking at them—he knew the denominations by touch, the way a blind man knows coins—and slid the envelope across the desk. "For your wife's hip. And for your continued discretion, which I trust will extend to forgetting you ever spoke to me."

Tulliver took the envelope, weighed it, and managed to look both grateful and insulted, which was the usual arrangement. He let himself out. The mud prints remained on the floor, and Halloran looked at them for a moment with genuine distaste before ringing for the boy to deal with them.

Then he sat still for a long time.

* * *

The river gave up bodies twice a year and cargo perhaps once a decade, and when it did, the law was unambiguous, because Halloran had spent eleven years making it so. The Crown's charter, renewed under the last administration and quietly broadened under this one, vested in the Colonial Salvage Trust all rights to property recovered from navigable waters within the Sable watershed: wrecks, cargoes, specie, artifacts of historical significance, and any documents therein. Salvors were entitled to a recovery fee—one-eighth, at the Trust's discretion, less costs—if and only if the find was reported within seventy-two hours and surrendered intact at a designated customs house.

Unreported finds were not salvage. They were theft from the Crown. The distinction was a line Halloran had written into the enforcement manual himself, in his own phrasing, and he was rather proud of it: *Possession is not discovery. Discovery is not ownership. The river belongs to the Crown, and everything the river yields arrives addressed to it.*

A lead-lined chest bearing an unreadable seal, raised by an unlicensed salvor who happened to be the child of the Trust's most embarrassing dead expedition leader, and not reported.

Halloran rose and went to the wall map.

The Middle Reach was there, of course, marked in blue pencil from some old survey: a wide, slow bend below the tollgate shallows, notorious for snags, silted over twice in recorded memory. He stood before the map with his hands clasped behind his back and let his mind do what it did best, which was to reduce a tangle of human mess to a clean column of facts.

Fact: Gideon Dunmore worked the lower river, alone mostly, aboard a patched barge, under a name that was not quite hidden and not quite used. Fact: the Trust had no active file on the younger Dunmore—Halloran checked this twice over the following hour, pulling drawers with the deliberation of a man defusing something—but the absence of a file was itself informative. People without files had arranged to be without them, and people who arranged things were people who understood exactly what they were avoiding.

Fact: Miriam Dunmore's last expedition, eleven years ago, had gone upriver under a Trust research license to survey "anomalous submerged structures" in the upper watershed. Four of six had come back. The inquest had ruled misadventure, weather, riverine hazard. The Trust had paid out the bonds, sealed the testimony, and struck the route from its survey schedule. Halloran remembered the sealed testimony because he had carried the box to the archive himself, and remembered thinking, even then—a junior man, ambitious, careful—that a box that heavy contained more than paper.

And now the river had coughed up a sealed chest into the hands of the dead woman's child.

Coincidence was a word Halloran did not permit in his reports. Coincidence was what other people called patterns they lacked the patience to trace.

* * *

By ten o'clock he had drafted the claim.

It was a beautiful document, if one admired such things, and Halloran did. He drafted it the way he did everything—in duplicate, in fair hand, with every statutory citation exact down to the subsection. *In re: Property Recovered from Navigable Waters, Middle Reach, Sable River.* The respondent was listed as *Kestrel (vessel), master unnamed pending service.* The grounds were the charter sections he knew better than his own birthday. The requested relief was standard: seizure of the recovered property, impoundment pending appraisal, and an order compelling the salvor to appear.

He read it through twice, adjusted one comma, and signed.

It was only afterward, standing at the clerk's window while young Prewitt stamped the filing and entered it in the day book, that Halloran allowed himself the question underneath the paperwork, the one that had been sitting in him since Tulliver's mud prints crossed his floor.

What was in the chest?

Not what the law said was in it—the law didn't care; the law would appraise it in pounds and shillings and shelve it in the bonded warehouse with the cannonballs and the church bells. What was actually in it. A seal no dockhand could read. Lead lining, which meant someone had wanted the contents protected from water, or from detection, or both. Raised from the Middle Reach, which lay well downstream of anything on Miriam Dunmore's sealed route—or did it? Halloran went back to the map and traced the river with one gray finger, up past the tollgate, past the shallows, into the blank space above the last surveyed bend where the cartographers of two governments had simply given up and written *marshes, impassable,* in fading ink.

His finger stopped there, at the edge of the blankness, and rested.

Eleven years ago, a woman had taken a licensed expedition into that blankness and four men had come back without her, and the Trust had sealed the file and paid the bonds and never asked what she had found, because asking would have cost more than not asking. That decision had been made above Halloran's head, by men whose names he now dined with. He had carried the box downstairs himself.

He wondered—not for the first time, but for the first time with this particular coldness—whether the box had ever held all of the paper there was.

* * *

The summons went out by afternoon launch: a warrant of seizure served at the harbormaster's office, posted on the board, and a copy dispatched to the mooring fields with instructions to serve the master of the *Kestrel* personally. Halloran also sent a quieter thing—a memorandum, internal routing only, to the Trust's directorate, informing them that property of possible historical significance connected to the Dunmore expedition had surfaced, and that he was acting to secure it in the ordinary course.

The word *ordinary* was doing careful work in that sentence. Halloran was a great believer in words that did careful work.

He expected the directorate to ignore it, which was fine. The memo existed so that later, whatever later held, no one could say Halloran had acted alone or for reasons of his own. He had watched better men than himself go down for initiative. Initiative was a fire; procedure was a banked stove that warmed you for forty years. He intended to die warm, at a desk, with his files in order.

What he did not expect—and this displeased him, the way a missing decimal displeases an accountant—was the reply.

It came back within the hour, by runner, in an envelope bearing the directorate's seal and nothing else. Inside, a single card in the director's own secretary's hand:

*Acknowledged. Secure the vessel's cargo. Note: the Chest matter is of particular interest to Mr. Vole. Extend him every professional courtesy.*

Below it, a name and a club address. Cassian Vole.

Halloran read the card twice, then held it near the lamp and studied the watermark, as though the paper might explain itself. It did not. He knew the name Vole the way everyone in the salvage world knew it: the expedition's surviving partner, the one who'd come back walking and talking and respectable, the one who'd testified movingly at the inquest about the storm, the hero of the tragedy, lately turned consultant to the very Trust whose license had killed his colleagues. There were men who fed on grief and men who invested in it, and Vole, by all accounts, had done the latter and prospered.

But why a consultant's name on a directorate card about a routine seizure? Courtesy flowed downhill to inspectors, not uphill from directors. Unless the courtesy wasn't courtesy. Unless it was instruction wearing courtesy's coat.

Halloran put the card in his breast pocket, which was where he kept things he had not yet decided about.

* * *

Evening came down on the river the way it always did, gilding the cranes and the masts and turning the water to hammered bronze, and Halloran stood at last at the window he never opened and looked out at it with the expression of a man auditing a ledger that wouldn't balance.

From up here the river looked orderly. Barges at their moorings, lights coming on one by one, the whole commerce of it legible, taxable, owned. Somewhere out there among the lights was a patched barge called *Kestrel*, and on it a salvor who had pulled a sealed chest out of the mud and said nothing, and somewhere upstream of that, in the blank space past the last survey mark, was whatever had killed Miriam Dunmore and however many others, waiting under the floodwater with the patience of everything the river kept.

The Trust said the river belonged to the Crown. Halloran had written the manual; he believed the sentence the way he believed his own signature. But he had stood on enough riverbanks to know the truth underneath the statute, which was that the river belonged to no one, and merely lent things out, and collected its debts on its own schedule, in its own currency.

It had lent the Crown a chest. And it had delivered it, of all the mudbanks on all the coast, to a Dunmore.

That was not coincidence. That was interest accruing.

Halloran turned from the window, sat, and drew a fresh sheet toward him. On it, in his small precise hand, he began a private list—not a report, nothing with a file number, just a list, headed with a single underlined word: *Questions.* Beneath it he wrote: *1. What does Vole know that the directorate does not? 2. What did the sealed testimony weigh, and what does it weigh now? 3. Where is the Kestrel, and how fast can she be?*

He blotted the page, folded it into his breast pocket beside the card, and turned down the lamp.

Out on the black water, far downstream, a light he could not see was already moving against the current.
