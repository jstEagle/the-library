## 4. Mother's Handwriting

The chest sat on the Kestrel's deck all night, and nobody touched it.

Gideon had draped a tarp over it—not to hide it, exactly, but because looking at it felt like looking at an open door in a house you'd sworn you'd never enter again. The lead lining made it heavier than it looked, heavier than any salvage had a right to be, and when Sefa's winch had hauled it dripping into the lantern light, the whole crew had gone quiet in that particular way river people go quiet: not with awe, but with the instinct of animals who smell weather coming.

Now it was past midnight and the barge creaked at her moorings off Cutter's Shoal, riding a slack tide, and Gideon sat alone on the deck boards with a bottle of cane spirit they hadn't opened and the tarp folded back, and the seal of Vessany stared up at them through the lamplight like an eye that had been closed for a hundred years and had chosen, of all people, to open on Gideon Dunmore.

They poured two fingers of spirit and didn't drink it.

*You're stalling,* said the voice in their head that sounded annoyingly like their mother. *You've freed snagged anchors in black water with a busted regulator. You've cut a drowned man loose from a paddlewheel in current strong enough to peel skin. And you're sitting here afraid of a box.*

"It's not the box," Gideon said aloud, because there was no one left aboard awake to hear them talk to themselves, and because saying it out loud made it smaller. "It's what's in the box."

It was the seal. That was the truth of it, and they'd known it since the moment their glove had brushed the silt away from the lid down there in the green dark, since the moment the stamped wax—wax, actual sealing wax, preserved under lead and river-mud like a fly in amber—had resolved into the shape Gideon had last seen at eight years old, sketched in pencil in the margin of a map in Miriam Dunmore's study: a river coiled around a mountain, crowned with three stars.

Vessany. The country that wasn't. The name that got you a flat look and a correction if you said it aloud, like mispronouncing a word. Erased off the charts so thoroughly that cartographers now argued about whether it had ever existed at all, whether the whole thing was a colonial-era fable, a phantom nation invented by mapmakers too embarrassed to leave blank space.

Gideon had believed the fable story for years. It was easier. Easier than believing their mother had spent her life chasing something real enough to be buried in lead and sunk in the Sable like a body.

They drank. The spirit burned going down and settled warm and useless in their chest, which was where all the useless things lived these days.

Then they knelt, took out their knife—the good one, Bram's gift, the one with the whalebone handle—and worked the hasp.

* * *

The lock surrendered without a fight. That should have been the first warning. Salvage taught you that things fought: rust fought, rivets fought, dead men's fists fought. This chest opened like it had been waiting.

Inside, packed in wool gone grey and dense as felt, lay books. Dozens of them. Ledgers, mostly—slim volumes bound in dyed leather, their spines stamped with the same coiled-river sigil—and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of documents tied with cord, and a small lacquered case that rattled faintly when Gideon lifted it and went still before they could bring themselves to shake it again.

The wool smelled of cedar and old smoke, of some spice Gideon couldn't name and one they could: their mother's study had smelled like this. Turmeric and lamp oil and the ghost of pipe tobacco she'd never admitted to. For a moment the smell put Gideon somewhere else entirely—a low room above a chandler's shop in Harrowgate, rain on the glass, a woman with ink-stained fingers turning pages by lamplight while a child pretended to sleep on the daybed and listened to her murmur numbers to herself in a language the child didn't know and would spend thirty years forgetting.

Gideon set the lacquered case aside unopened. Hands first. Hands knew what to do even when the rest of them didn't.

They lifted the top ledger onto their knees. The leather was cool and slightly tacky, like skin. Inside, the paper had survived the river the way only lead-lined things survive anything—dry, crisp, the ink unfaded, a brown-black so deep it looked wet. Columns of figures. Names in a script Gideon didn't recognize, flowing right to left in places, then breaking into trade-glyphs, then, bafflingly, into standard Common Tongue notation, as though the book had been kept by several hands across generations, each teaching the next.

A treasury ledger. Gideon understood that much without wanting to. Deposits. Withdrawals. Notations of weight and fineness. Page after page of a nation's wealth counted out in careful hands, and every entry dated in a calendar that ended—abruptly, mid-column, halfway down a page—with nothing after it. No closing balance. No final signature. Just a stop, like a heart between beats.

The country hadn't faded. It had been interrupted.

Gideon turned pages faster now, not reading so much as searching, the way you search a wreck when you know something's wrong and your hands want to find it before your mind does. The later entries shifted language again, and here the handwriting changed—looser, hurried, the numerals slanting—and dates appeared in a calendar Gideon did know, the colonial one, the one printed on every customs form and wage slip on the Sable coast. Someone had kept counting after the end. Someone had kept this ledger alive in hiding, moving numbers like moving children out of a burning city.

And then the last page.

Gideon saw the handwriting before they read a single word, and the world tilted.

They knew that hand. They knew the way the capital M broke its spine and kicked its leg out. They knew the little hook she put on her sevens, the way her r's leaned like tired men against a wall. They had watched that hand write grocery lists and field notes and, once, only once, a letter that began *My darling Gid* and was never finished and never sent and that Gideon had found years later, after the funeral, folded inside a book of tide tables, and had read so many times the creases went soft as cloth.

*M. Dunmore,* the page said. *Custodian.*

Below it, in smaller letters, cramped into the margin as though written quickly, secretly, by lamplight with someone's footsteps in the hall:

*I accept what I did not choose. Let the river keep it until the river can give it back. —M.D.*

Gideon did not remember deciding to sit down. They were simply sitting, ledger open on their knees, the bottle knocked over beside them and the spirit soaking into deck planks that had swallowed worse, and the sound coming out of them was not crying, or not only crying—it was the sound of five years arriving all at once, like floodwater finding a breach.

* * *

Five years of silence. That was the arithmetic Gideon had done, over and over, in the worst hours: five years since the telegram, four since the funeral they'd arrived late to and left early, three since they'd sold the study's contents rather than sort them, two since they'd stopped answering letters from anyone who had ever shared a table with Miriam Dunmore. Five years of measuring life in river miles because miles didn't ask questions. Miles didn't say *you look like her.* Miles didn't say *she'd be proud*—the cruelest sentence in any language, because pride from the dead is a debt you can't discharge.

And underneath the silence, the anger, which Gideon had told everyone—including themselves—was grief, and which was partly grief, but was also this: *she chose it.* She had chosen the expeditions, the absences, the years of postmarks instead of birthdays. She had chosen the river over the child, and then the river had chosen her back, taken her at Greyfall Bend along with two of her crew, and left Gideon nothing but a name that opened doors they didn't want opened and closed doors they did.

That was the story Gideon had lived inside for five years. It fit well. It had worn smooth as river stone.

But the ledger didn't fit in it.

*Custodian.* Not treasure-hunter. Not profiteer. Custodian—the word meant keeper, meant someone entrusted with a thing on behalf of others, meant duty, meant burden. Gideon turned the last page over, held it to the lantern, and saw what they'd missed: faint impressions on the reverse, writing from a sheet long removed, and among them, repeated twice, the phrase *they are not looking for the gold.*

Not the gold. Then what? What did you hunt, upriver, in the drowned country, if not the treasury everyone swore didn't exist?

Gideon thought of the sketch in the study map. Thought of how Miriam used to trace rivers with one finger while she talked, always upstream, always toward the source, as if everything worth knowing gathered at the headwaters. Thought of the last time they'd spoken—really spoken—an argument on a dock in Harrowgate, Gideon nineteen and furious, Miriam saying, *One day you'll understand why I keep going back,* and Gideon saying the unforgivable thing, the thing they'd rehearsed apologies for a thousand times in the dark and never once delivered: *I hope whatever you're looking for down there drowns you.*

The river had obliged.

Gideon pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes until the lantern became a red blur. The apology was still unsent. It would always be unsent. There was no version of this where the words arrived in time, and no amount of salvage could dredge that up and make it right, and Gideon had built a whole drifting life on the understanding that some things stay sunk.

But the chest had come up anyway. The river had given it back—or given it to them, specifically, them, out of all the miles of the Sable, out of all the dredges and divers and opportunists on the coast. Their mother's handwriting, naming their mother's name, sealed in lead and sunk where a Dunmore would eventually drag a chain through the mud. Coincidence was a word river people didn't trust. Rivers didn't do coincidence. Rivers did current, and current did delivery.

"You were keeping it," Gideon said quietly, to the deck, to the dark water sliding past the hull, to wherever the dead keep their hearing. "All this time. You weren't hunting it. You were carrying it."

And if Miriam had been custodian—if she had died holding a secret worth killing for, worth erasing a nation to bury—then the expedition hadn't been an expedition. It had been a delivery run. And it had failed. And someone, somewhere, had spent five years believing the chest was lost, or worse, knowing roughly where it lay and being unable to reach it.

Which meant someone might be looking for it now.

The thought landed cold, colder than the river at forty feet, and Gideon found they weren't afraid of it. Or rather—they were afraid, deeply, in the marrow—but the fear had a direction now, and directed fear was a different animal entirely. It was the feeling of a dive line going taut. Something to pull against.

Gideon stood, joints stiff, and looked at the chest's contents spread across the tarp: the ledgers, the oilcloth bundle, the lacquered case. The bundle next. Cord untied, oilcloth peeled back, and inside—papers. Correspondence, some of it water-damaged at the edges, some in that same broken-spined hand. A shipping manifest. A letter of introduction bearing a seal Gideon recognized from customs houses: the Colonial Salvage Trust, the Crown's own river-claim office, the institution that owned, on paper, everything the Sable yielded.

Miriam had corresponded with the Trust. Or negotiated with it. Or fled from it—the letter's tone, even half-legible, had teeth.

Gideon refolded the papers slowly. Above deck, the night wind had come around, carrying the smell of rain from far inland, that green metallic promise the air made before the wet season. Somewhere downriver a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and the Kestrel answered with a groan of rope.

There would be consequences to this find. Gideon understood that the way they understood weather—not the details yet, but the pressure dropping, the light going strange. A chest like this didn't surface into a quiet world. It surfaced into a world of claims and charters and men whose business was owning what other people pulled from the water. By morning, maybe sooner, the news would start moving through the port's capillary network of dockhands and clerks and informants, the way blood moves from a wound.

Let it move. Gideon found, to their own surprise, that they were done running ahead of their name. Miriam Dunmore had accepted a burden she didn't choose. Her child had spent five years refusing one that was lighter.

Gideon picked up the ledger, closed it gently, and laid it back atop the others with the care owed to sleeping things. Then they picked up the lacquered case, turned it over once in their hands, and set it down unopened. Whatever it held could wait for daylight. Some doors you opened with witnesses.

They climbed to the wheelhouse, woke Sefa with a boot against the engine housing, and said, "Get steam up. We're making for port before the tide turns."

Sefa squinted at them through the hatch, hair wild, already reading the change in Gideon's face the way engineers read gauges. "Port for what? We don't sell till—"

"Not to sell." Gideon looked downriver, toward the distant smudge of lights that was Harrowgate, toward the auction notices they'd seen nailed outside the customs hall—the sale of seized effects, scholarly effects, the estate of certain deceased persons of interest. Miriam's things. The Trust had inventoried her life and put it under the hammer, and Gideon had walked past those notices for a week with their jaw set and their eyes forward.

"Now," Gideon said, "we buy my mother back."

Behind them, below decks, the chest sat in the dark with its hundred-year secret, and the river carried the Kestrel toward everything that would come next.
