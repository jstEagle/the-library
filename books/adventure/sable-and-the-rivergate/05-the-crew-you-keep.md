## 5. The Crew You Keep

### Sefa

Sefa heard the claim notice before she saw it — the particular silence of Gideon reading something they didn't want to read aloud.

She was elbow-deep in the Kestrel's bilge pump, third time that week, her forearms slick to the shoulder with grease and river water, and the quiet coming from the deck above had a texture to it. She'd spent eleven years listening to engines and the men and women who stood near them, and she knew the sound of bad news the way she knew the knock of a cracked bearing: not loud, but wrong.

She wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her arms and climbed the ladder into grey morning light. The Sable coast wore its fog like a borrowed coat, ill-fitting, already slipping. Gideon stood at the rail with a paper in one hand, the other gripping the wood hard enough that the knuckles had gone pale as fish bellies.

"Trust?" Sefa said.

Gideon didn't answer, which was an answer.

The Colonial Salvage Trust's notices all looked the same — heavy cream stock, a wax stamp the color of dried blood, lettering so ornate it seemed embarrassed by what it said. Sefa had seen them nailed to dockside posts up and down the coast, over salvage yards and fishing sheds, little paper flags planted in other people's livelihoods. *By authority of the Crown, all yield of the Sable River and its tributaries, submerged or recovered, is held in trust.* She'd never read past that line before. It hadn't been hers to read.

Now it was nailed to the Kestrel's mast.

"They came out at dawn," Gideon said finally. Their voice was flat, sanded down. "Skiff, two clerks, one man with a rifle he kept pointing at the water like it might apologize for him. Nailed it up while we slept."

"You slept," Sefa said. "I was fixing your pump."

"The chest is named in the claim. The ledger too." Gideon turned the paper over as if the back might hold better news. "They're giving us ten days to surrender recovered materials at the Rivergate station. After that it's seizure, plus costs, plus—" their mouth twisted "—*obstruction penalties*. They've made a word for it. A whole word for the crime of keeping what you pulled out of the water with your own hands."

Sefa leaned against the rail and looked at the fog, thinking about arithmetic. The Kestrel owed money on her hull patch — three months of work, paid for on credit against future salvage. If the Trust took everything the dredge brought up, there were no futures to borrow against. There was only the debt, and the debt grew teeth.

She thought about the yard at Corren's Landing where she'd apprenticed. Old Master Ivo, who used to say an engine would tell you the truth if you listened long enough, and who had fired her when she told his customers the truth about his engines. She thought about the year after that, sleeping in tool sheds, eating what work came loose. And then she thought about the night two years ago when a thin, sharp-eyed stranger had rowed out to her mooring in the rain and said, *My barge's engine is dying and I can't pay much, but nobody on this boat will ever lie to you about what things cost.*

Nobody ever had.

"I'm not leaving," she said.

Gideon blinked. "I haven't asked anyone to—"

"You were going to. You get this look, like you're drafting a speech to let us off easy." She took the claim notice from their hand, folded it with more care than it deserved, and tucked it under the wax-stamped nail so it wouldn't blow away. "Ten days. That's enough time to pull the pump properly, seal the hull seams, and be somewhere the Trust's skiffs don't like to go. So stop writing my goodbye for me and start planning my route."

For the first time that morning, something moved behind Gideon's eyes that wasn't dread. It was small and it was tired but it was alive, like a banked coal deciding whether to become fire again.

"Away from the tollgates," Gideon said slowly. "Upstream."

"Upstream," Sefa agreed, and went back down to her engine, which had never once lied to her, and got to work.

### Bram

Bram counted exits the way other men counted money. It was habit older than his scars: every room, every deck, every stretch of open water, he marked the ways out. He'd been doing it since Vell's Crossing, since the day staying put through a door that should have been opened had cost four men their lives and bought him a discharge and a name he didn't use.

So when he saw the cream-colored paper on the Kestrel's mast, his first thought — honest, ugly, automatic — was *that's my exit.*

He sat in the bow with his whetstone and his knife, working the edge in slow strokes, and watched the fog burn off the river, and did the soldier's math. Ten days until seizure. A Trust that could field gunboats against a patched salvage barge crewed by four. An engineer who couldn't fight, a boy who couldn't fight, a captain who fought like someone who'd learned from books rather than bruises. And himself — a man with a rifle, a bad conscience, and a talent for violence that had never once made anything better.

Four ways off this boat. He'd counted them a hundred times. Row out at night to the coastal road. Walk away at any port with a market crowd thick enough to vanish into. Take the pay Gideon had advanced him for the hull patch and disappear south, where nobody asked ex-soldiers about their histories. Or stay, and drown, or hang, or worse — watch the others drown because of him.

Because that was the part the math kept circling back to, wasn't it. Vell's Crossing hadn't happened because Bram was a coward. It had happened because he'd been *sure*. Sure the door could wait. Sure the plan would hold. Sureness was the most expensive thing a man could carry, and Bram had been paying it off for nine years without making a dent in the principal.

"You're sharpening that knife like it insulted you."

Nils dropped into the bow across from him, knees drawn up, a chart across his lap weighted at the corners with four different objects including half a biscuit. The kid mapped everything. Rivers, coastlines, the interior of the galley, apparently conversations.

"Just maintenance."

"You've done that edge six times since the chest came up." Nils smoothed the chart flat. It was the upper Sable, hand-copied, decades old, its tributaries drawn in faded brown ink like veins on the back of an old man's hand. "I've been looking at where the Trust's stations are. Tollgates here, here, and here. Gunboat moorings here. But see—" his finger traced upstream, past the last marked gate, into country the map simply stopped bothering with, "—past Greyfall Bend, they've got nothing. Paper claims cover the whole river. Boats don't."

Bram kept working the stone along the steel. "You're saying run the gates."

"I'm saying the map says it's possible. Whether it's possible and whether we do it are different questions." The boy looked up, and there was something in his face Bram recognized uncomfortably well — the look of someone who had already decided and was waiting for permission to say so. "I ran from my family. That was the right call; they wanted to sell me to a survey guild like a breeding goat. But I've been running two years, and running starts to feel like a direction if you do it long enough." He folded the chart with precise, careful creases. "I don't want to run from this one. This one I want to *go toward*."

Bram looked at the knife. The edge was fine now, finer than it needed to be, sharp enough to shave with or to do the other thing. He thought about doors. He thought about how, at Vell's Crossing, the door he'd failed to open had been behind him — the way out for the wounded — and how he'd judged it could wait, and how the waiting had been the whole of the disaster.

If he left now, he'd be closing a door behind him on purpose. On these three people who ate at the same table and complained about the same coffee and had never once asked him what he'd done before the Kestrel, which was either kindness or the greatest gift of cowardice anyone had ever handed him.

"Kid," he said, "if we run the tollgate at night, the decoy has to be convincing. Someone has to pilot the empty barge into the searchlights while the real one slips the cut."

Nils went still. "That's a hanging offense if they catch them."

"That's why it can't be me asking anyone to do it." Bram finally sheathed the knife. "It has to be someone offering."

He stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked upstream into the fog where the river narrowed and the maps gave out and whatever Miriam Dunmore had died for was waiting in the drowned dark.

He'd counted four exits off this boat. He'd been counting wrong. There was only one way off the Kestrel that wouldn't follow him the rest of his life, and it pointed upstream.

### Nils

Nils drew maps of places he intended to keep.

That was the rule he'd made for himself somewhere between his father's house and the coast — a private superstition, unspoken even to himself most days. Every chart he copied, every coastline he inked, was a promise: *I will stand on you someday. I will know your true shape.* His family had drawn maps for money, for armies, for men who used them to move borders and soldiers and grief across other people's homelands. Nils drew them to go home to places he'd never been.

Tonight, in the Kestrel's cramped cabin, lamplight swinging gently with the current, he was drawing the Sable River's upper reaches from memory — from fragments, really. Dock talk. An old pilot's drunken description of the Greyfall rapids. Three lines in Miriam Dunmore's recovered journal that mentioned a bend, a drowned archive, a depth.

And the chest's seal, which he'd sketched from Gideon's halting description: a river rendered as a serpent swallowing its own tail, crowned with seven stars. Vessany. A nation Nils had first encountered at age eleven, in his father's library, on a map with the name struck through in red ink and a note in the margin in his father's tidy hand: *Extinct. Territorial settlement final. Do not survey.*

*Do not survey.* His father had written those words on perhaps a hundred maps, always in the same place, always about the same blank spaces, and young Nils had understood even then that a *do not survey* was the loudest possible invitation. His father had erased Vessany from the family's atlases. Somewhere upstream, the river had kept a copy.

The cabin door opened and Gideon came in, carrying two chipped mugs of the coffee everyone complained about and no one refused. They set one beside Nils's elbow without comment and lowered themselves onto the bunk, which groaned in protest, and for a while neither spoke. The lamp swung. The river murmured against the hull like a large animal settling.

"I owe you an apology," Gideon said at last. "Or a warning. Possibly both."

Nils kept inking. "Those usually come together from you."

"The Trust's claim names the chest. When I surrender it — *if* I surrender it — everything else on this boat becomes evidence. Associations. Witnesses." Gideon wrapped both hands around the mug. "People who help us could lose their salvage rights. Permanently. In some territories, their freedom of the rivers. I want you to know exactly what staying costs before—"

"Before you give me the speech where you let me off easy." Nils set down his pen. "Sefa says you write those speeches. Bram says you rehearse them."

A ghost of a smile crossed Gideon's face and was gone. "I'm trying to protect you."

"I know." Nils turned his sketchbook around and pushed it across the small table. "Look."

Gideon looked. Nils watched them look — watched their eyes move over the upper river, the unmarked tributaries, the bend where he'd drawn the ruins in dotted conjecture lines and labeled them, in his best cartographer's hand, *Vessany archive (presumed)*. Watched them reach the bottom margin, where he'd written the coordinates of Greyfall Bend as precisely as fragmentary sources allowed.

"My father erased this country from every map he owned," Nils said quietly. "Struck the name out in red. *Do not survey.* I've spent two years drawing maps of everywhere except the one place I was forbidden, and I told myself it was because I didn't have good sources." He tapped the dotted lines. "I had the sources. I just didn't have a reason. Now the reason is sitting in our hold wearing a lead lining and a dead nation's seal, and my mother's handwriting is in its ledger, and the people who erased it want it back in the dark."

"It's not your mother's handwriting."

"No." Nils met Gideon's eyes. "But it's yours. And you've spent five years erasing yourself the same way your mother erased herself — no letters, no name, no trace. I recognize the technique. It runs in families." He turned the sketchbook back around. "I'm not staying on this boat despite the danger, Gideon. I'm staying because for the first time since I ran, there's a destination worth the ink. Draw me the route. Let me make the map. That's my price for staying, and it's not negotiable."

The lamp swung. Somewhere above, Bram's boots crossed the deck in his slow patrol rhythm, and beneath them Sefa's engine turned over — a cough, a catch, then the deep steady heartbeat of machinery loved back to health.

Gideon picked up the pen. For a moment Nils thought they would refuse, would produce the speech after all. Then Gideon pulled the journal close, found the page with Miriam's final route notes, and began, in a voice gone rough and low, to dictate bearings.

Nils inked them as fast as they came. Line by line, the river's hidden shape assembled itself on the page — a map of a place that officially did not exist, drawn by a runaway son of the men who had erased it, bound for a drowned archive where a dead woman's markers still stood.

When the last bearing was set, Gideon stared at the finished chart for a long time.

"Cassian Vole," they said at last, reading the name from the journal's margins for the hundredth time. "Her partner. Her *friend*." Their jaw tightened. "There's an auction in port tomorrow. Seized effects from her last expedition. Her field journals are in the lot."

Nils felt the hair rise on his arms. "Then tomorrow," he said, "we'd better be the highest bidder."

Outside, the fog was lifting off the Sable, and far upstream, past the last tollgate, past the edge of every map his father had signed, the river waited with its throat full of stars.
