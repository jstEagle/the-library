## 6. Auction of Ghosts

The auction notice went up on the customs house board on a Tuesday, pasted crooked over a notice about cholera quarantine, and Gideon stood in front of it for a long time while the market crowd flowed around them like water around a piling.

*By order of the Colonial Salvage Trust, in settlement of debts against the estate of the late Miriam Dunmore, archaeologist: the effects of the deceased, being one crate of instruments, two crates of papers and correspondence, one crate of personal effects, to be sold at public auction at the Vell Street sale rooms, Saturday next.*

They read it three times. The words didn't change. That was the trouble with words; they never changed just because you needed them to.

Five years Gideon had kept the Dunmore name off their tongue like a stone kept under the tongue to still a hunger. Five years of measuring life in river miles instead of years, of signing manifests as *G. Marsh*, of letting the world believe the archaeologist's child had drowned or scattered or never existed at all. And now the Trust had gathered up everything their mother had left behind—everything the law could get its hands on, which was everything, because the dead own nothing and their debts belong to whoever claims them—and put a price on it, lot by lot, in neat printed figures.

Gideon touched the notice. The paste was still wet. It came away grey on their fingertip, and they rubbed it against their thumb the way they'd rub silt between their fingers on a dive, reading the river's mood.

"Vell Street," they said aloud, to nobody. The market roared on. A fishwife argued the price of eels two stalls over, her voice climbing into the register of gulls. Somewhere behind the fish stalls, the Sable moved in its brown, patient way, carrying everything toward the sea and keeping nothing.

Gideon went back to the *Kestrel* and told the crew they'd be in port through Saturday.

* * *

The Vell Street sale rooms had been a granary once, and the building hadn't gotten over it. The ceiling was high and stained with the ghosts of old harvests, and the light came down through clerestory windows in long dusty blades that turned every lot into something holy and every bidder into a silhouette. It was, Gideon thought, exactly the kind of room their mother would have hated—light and grandeur arranged over the business of selling a life by the pound.

They arrived early and stood at the back, cap pulled low, hands in pockets, wearing the face they wore for customs men and toll collectors: a salvager's face, weathered, unremarkable, belonging to no one in particular. The crowd was the usual port mixture. Dealers with soft hands and hard eyes. Clerks from the Trust in grey coats, sitting together in a block like crows on a wire, bidding only when instructed by a list. A few curiosity-seekers drawn by the name *Dunmore*, which even after five years still carried a shine in certain circles—the woman who'd gone up the Sable and hadn't come back down.

Gideon catalogued them the way Sefa had taught them to catalogue engine sounds: by threat. The dealers were noise. The clerks were routine. But there was one man, third row from the front, who sat alone and did not bid on the early lots at all, and that interested Gideon the way a wrong current interests a diver. He was broad through the shoulders, silver at the temples, dressed well but not richly—a coat cut for travel, boots made for mud. He held a catalogue and did not open it. He had, Gideon noticed, already marked it. Little pencil ticks against certain lot numbers, made before the auction began.

Lot eleven: instruments. Lot fourteen: correspondence, two crates. Lot nineteen: one field journal, leather-bound, water-stained.

Gideon's chest did something complicated and painful around that last number.

The auctioneer was a thin man with the voice of a man who'd spent thirty years making other people's losses sound festive. He moved through the lots with the rhythm of a dredge chain—clank, clank, clank—sextant, surveying tools, brass calipers worn bright by Miriam's hands, and Gideon watched a stranger pay eleven crowns for a compass their mother had used to find her way through country no map had ever named, and felt the specific violence of it, the way you feel a hook tear free.

The silver-templed man didn't move. He sat with his hands folded and waited, patient as silt.

* * *

Lot fourteen came up in the middle hour, when the room had gone warm and the bidding had gone mechanical.

"Correspondence and papers of the deceased, two crates, contents unexamined. Do I hear an opening?"

The Trust's clerks stirred. One raised a card. "Five."

"Five crowns. Six? Anyone at six?"

A dealer in the second row, a woman with ink-stained fingers and the look of someone who bought dead men's letters by the yard, went to six. The clerks went to eight. The woman went to nine, ten, twelve, and then stopped, calculating, and the clerks took it at thirteen, and the gavel fell, and two crates of Miriam Dunmore's correspondence—the letters Gideon had never answered, the letters Gideon had never opened, the letters addressed in that upright, patient hand to an address in the capital where Gideon had not lived in five years—went to a grey-coated clerk with a list, for less than the price of a used dredge winch.

Gideon stood very still at the back of the room and breathed the way you breathe when the water is cold and you must not gasp.

They had told themselves, walking here, that they'd come only to see. To stand in the room while the world did its arithmetic on their mother and to know, afterward, exactly what she had been worth to it. Thirteen crowns. That was the answer. Thirteen crowns for five years of letters.

But lot nineteen was coming, and Gideon had known since Tuesday that they were lying to themselves.

* * *

The field journal came up late, near the end, when the light through the clerestory windows had gone amber and the crowd had thinned to the serious and the stubborn.

"Lot nineteen. Field journal of the deceased, leather-bound, water-damaged, contents partially legible. A document of considerable interest to collectors of Sable exploration material." The auctioneer turned it in his thin hands with a showman's reluctance, and Gideon saw it across the room and the room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet when something in your blood is shouting.

They knew that journal. They had known it as a child, known it the way you know a thing that lives in a house with you—the cracked spine, the brass corner-caps, the water stain shaped like a bird across the cover from the crossing where Miriam's boat had swamped in the shallows above Tern Reach and she'd laughed about it for a year. Gideon had been eleven. They had sat on the floor of the study while their mother catalogued the day's notes, and she'd let them hold the compass, and she'd said, *Everything true that I know is in this book, little fish. Everything else is just weather.*

"Shall we say two crowns to open?"

Gideon's hand went up before their pride could stop it.

"Two crowns, at the back. Three?"

The dealer with ink-stained fingers went to three. Gideon went to four. Five. Six. The dealer dropped out at six with a shrug, and Gideon allowed themselves one breath of something almost like relief—

—and then the man with silver temples raised his card.

"Seven," said the auctioneer, with fresh interest. "Seven crowns. Eight?"

Gideon bid eight. The man bid nine without looking up from his marked catalogue. Ten. Eleven. The rhythm between them tightened, and Gideon understood, with the cold clarity of a diver reading a snag, that this was not a contest of enthusiasm. The man was not caught up in the fever of the room. He was spending money the way a surveyor lays out a chain—deliberately, link by link, toward a fixed point. He had come for this journal and perhaps for others, and he had a number in mind, and Gideon had no way of knowing what it was.

Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Gideon's purse had a floor, and they were nearing it, and the arithmetic of the *Kestrel*—the hull patches, the air bottles, Sefa's parts list—stacked itself up in their mind like ballast.

Fifteen, from the silver-templed man, calm as a tide table.

And then, from a corner near the front, a voice Gideon didn't know: "Sixteen."

Gideon turned. A young man stood there—thin, sunburnt, ink on his cuffs and a rolled tube of drawings under one arm, the kind of face that belonged on a dock or a drafting table and clearly didn't belong in a sale room. He looked as surprised by his own bid as anyone. He looked, in fact, like a man who had been counting coins in his pocket and had just decided to spend all of them.

The silver-templed man finally looked up. He looked at the young man for a long moment, and something passed across his face—not anger, not surprise, but a kind of courteous recalculation, a surveyor noting an unexpected obstruction. Then he raised his card again.

"Seventeen," said the auctioneer.

The young man went white, looked at his pocket, looked at the journal, and sat down.

"Seventeen crowns. Eighteen? At seventeen, then—any advance on seventeen?" The auctioneer's gavel hovered. Gideon stood with their hand half-raised and their purse empty and their mother's journal lying on a velvet cushion under a stranger's pencil mark, and the silence stretched out over the room like a held breath over deep water.

"Seventeen crowns, once. Twice—"

"Sold."

The gavel fell like a cleat coming loose. The silver-templed man rose, unhurried, and went to the cashier's table to settle his account, and Gideon watched him take the journal in both hands and open it—not to the first page, but somewhere in the middle, as if he knew exactly which page he wanted—and read it there in the amber light with an expression Gideon could not read at all. It wasn't curiosity. It was recognition. It was the face of a man finding his own name in a stranger's book.

Then he closed it, tucked it under his arm, and walked out of the sale rooms into the evening, and Gideon stood in the emptying room and understood that they had just watched someone steal a thing out from under them in full view of the law, legally, with a gavel, for seventeen crowns.

* * *

They caught up with the young man on the steps outside, because there was nothing else to do with the anger and the grief except walk, and he was walking the same direction, and he had the look of a man who'd also just watched something be taken from him.

"You bid on lot nineteen," Gideon said.

He startled, then squinted at them in the failing light. "I bid on a lot. I don't remember numbers. The journal." He said it the way people say the name of a person they've lost. "You wanted it too. I saw you. You were the one driving him up."

"Who is he?"

"I was hoping you'd know." The young man shifted his roll of drawings to the other arm. "He bought four lots. The journal, the photograph plates, the survey sketches, and a bundle of her river charts. All the cartographic material. Everything with routes on it." He said it fast, the way Nils talked when a map was involved, and then caught himself. "I'm Nils. I'm— I was going to say a cartographer, but I'm not, officially. Officially I don't exist, which is a longer story. I came for the charts. Miriam Dunmore's charts. There's no one else who surveyed the upper Sable. No one living."

The name, spoken aloud by a stranger, in the open air, hit Gideon like cold water in the ear.

"You knew her work," Gideon said carefully.

"Everyone in the trade knew her work. Everyone in the trade thought she was dead." Nils looked at them sideways, and Gideon watched the thought arrive in his face the way weather arrives over water—gradually, then all at once. "You have her chin," he said. "The portrait in the expedition announcements, years ago. The family that funded her. There was a child in the—"

"Don't," Gideon said.

Nils closed his mouth. Down at the harbor, the evening bells were ringing, and the Sable ran brown and patient under the first lamps of the quay, carrying everything toward the sea.

"The man who bought the journal," Nils said at last, quietly, "marked his catalogue before the auction started. He knew what he wanted. And he bought every lot that showed where she went." He hesitated. "I only got a look at the cover of the journal, when the auctioneer held it up. But there was a slip of paper marking a page, sticking out the top. I could read it from the second row." He swallowed. "It was a route note. *Greyfall Bend.* And underneath it, underlined twice—"

The bells finished. A gull screamed somewhere over the fish stalls, and the river kept moving.

"*Trust the river, not the crew,*" Nils said. "That's what it said. *Trust the river, not the crew.*"

Gideon stood on the steps of the Vell Street sale rooms with an empty purse and a name in their mouth they hadn't spoken in five years, and watched the last light go off the water, and thought about a chest with a broken seal of Vessany lashed under the *Kestrel*'s floorboards, and about a ledger whose last page named their mother as custodian, and about a man with silver temples who had just bought every map of the route she died on.

"Whoever he is," Gideon said, "he's ahead of us now."

"Then we'd better be faster than him," said Nils. "I know a channel he won't."
