## 7. The Journal's Route

Nils had always believed that a map was a confession. Every line drawn was a line someone else wanted hidden; every blank space was a secret wearing the costume of ignorance. So when Gideon set Miriam Dunmore's field journal on the Kestrel's chart table and said, *You're the one who reads things*, Nils felt something between dread and homecoming settle in their chest.

The lamp above the table swung gently with the river's breathing. Outside, the harbor at Corrin's Landing muttered to itself — halyards ticking against masts, the long sigh of water against pilings, somewhere a dog with opinions about the moon. Inside, the air smelled of lamp oil, tannin-stained paper, and Sefa's coffee, which she brewed strong enough to strip varnish.

Nils turned the first page and understood immediately why the dealer at the auction had wanted it so badly.

* * *

The journal was not written to be read. That was the first thing. Miriam Dunmore had composed it the way a frightened person speaks — in fragments, in code, in a shorthand that looked like a naturalist's field notes but wasn't. Entries about bird populations that named no birds. Water depths recorded for rivers that didn't exist on any chart Nils knew. A recurring notation — three small circles, then a dash — that appeared forty-one times across ninety pages, always beside measurements of things that could not be measured: *depth of silence*, *weight of the door*, *the blue under the stone*.

"She was hiding it in plain sight," Nils said, mostly to themselves. "Anyone who seized this would see a scientist's notebook. Boring. Worthless."

Gideon sat across the table with their arms folded, saying nothing, watching the pages turn as if each one might bite. They hadn't slept. Nils could see it in the raw rims of their eyes, in the way they kept touching the pendant at their throat without seeming to know they were doing it.

Sefa leaned over Nils's shoulder, smelling of engine grease and coffee. "What's the cipher?"

"Not a cipher, exactly." Nils tilted the page toward the light. The paper was good stock, salt-bleached, the ink iron-gall gone brown at the edges. "It's displacement. She writes one thing where she means another, but the substitutions aren't random — they're systematic. Look." They tapped the margin. "Every time she writes *heron*, the entry is about depth. Every time she writes *reedbed*, she means structure. Architecture. And these numbers—" they ran a finger down a column, "—they're not soundings. They're distances. Miles, I think, or maybe hours of travel. She's recording a route."

"A route upstream," Gideon said quietly.

"A route upstream," Nils agreed. "And she hid it because she didn't trust whoever might read this. Which means she didn't trust her own crew."

The words landed in the cabin like a dropped wrench. Sefa straightened. Gideon went very still.

* * *

They worked through the night, the three of them, while Bram stood the deck watch alone and pretended not to listen through the open hatch. Nils built the substitution key the way they'd once built maps for a cartography house that sold them to men who used maps to move soldiers — patiently, obsessively, with the growing nausea of someone who knows what their precision will be used for.

By the third hour the journal began to speak.

*Day 12. Heron at four reeds past the split channel. The blue under the stone is real. Vessany did not drown; it was drowned.*

Nils read that line aloud twice. The second time, their voice cracked on it.

"Say it again," Gideon said.

"*Vessany did not drown; it was drowned.*" Nils looked up. "She's saying the flood was deliberate. That the country — the city, the archive, whatever Greyfall Bend used to be — was put underwater on purpose. Someone broke the levees. Or opened the sluices. Or rerouted the river itself."

"Who drowns an entire nation?" Sefa asked.

"Whoever profits from a nation not existing," Nils said. It was the kind of thing they'd learned young, growing up in a mapping house, learning early that borders were drawn by the people who could afford ink. "If Vessany legally ceased to exist — if its records, its treasury claims, its land grants all sat at the bottom of a lake — then everything it owned became ownerless. And everything ownerless becomes claimable. By whoever holds the paper."

"The Trust," Gideon said. Not a question.

"The Trust," Nils confirmed. "Or whatever the Trust was before it was the Trust. Miriam figured it out. And then she wrote it down in a language only she could read, and hid the key somewhere, and kept moving upstream anyway."

"Why keep going?" Sefa asked. "If she knew? If she'd proven it?"

Nils turned pages faster now, the substitutions coming easier, the route assembling itself in their mind like a coastline emerging from fog. "Because proof isn't possession. There's a difference between knowing a treasure exists and holding it in your hands. She needed the archive itself. Documents. Originals. The kind of paper that can't be called forgery because it bears the seal of a government that officially never existed." They paused. "She was going to make the lie impossible."

* * *

The route, once decoded, was beautiful and terrible.

Miriam had traveled light and fast, apparently alone in the final weeks — or writing as though she were alone, which amounted to the same thing. Her entries thinned as she went upriver, the handwriting tightening, the margins filling with small anxious marks. Nils transcribed the waypoints onto a fresh sheet of drafting film, working by lamplight while the river breathed outside:

*The split channel at Kiln Rock — take the western throat, not the marked one; the eastern is sounded wrong on every chart, deliberately.*

*A drowned church spire at Tallowmere Reach, its bell still hanging, used as a range marker: pass north of the bell.*

*Three days' poling through the Serpentine, where the flooded forest stands dead but upright, "like a congregation," and the water is deep enough to float a barge but shallow enough to snag one.*

*Then Greyfall Bend.*

The last entries were different. The hand shook. The ink was applied wet and heavy, blotted, hurried.

*Reached the bend at dusk. The archive hall is intact beneath eleven fathoms. The doors are bronze. The Vessany seal is unbroken. Everything is here. Everything they drowned the country to bury.*

*I am not alone. I was never alone. C. has been reporting our position since Kiln Rock. I found his signals — mirror flashes, timed to the supply runs. He thinks I don't know. Let him think it.*

*If anything happens to me, the chest goes down with me. Better drowned than read by the wrong eyes. M.D.*

Nils stopped reading aloud after that. They read the rest silently, twice, and then closed the journal and sat with their hands flat on the cover, feeling the texture of the boards through their palms, and did not speak for a long moment.

"Well?" Gideon said finally. Their voice was carefully level, the way water is level right before it goes over a weir.

"Cassian Vole," Nils said. "Her partner. The man who's now a consultant for the Colonial Salvage Trust." They watched Gideon absorb it. "He was selling her position. Mile by mile. Whatever happened to her crew — and the ledger says there were deaths — he was the reason they walked into it."

Gideon stood up so abruptly the bench scraped. They crossed to the stern windows and stood looking out at the black water, one hand closed around the pendant, knuckles white. For a long time they said nothing. The river said nothing back. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's bell rang the half-hour, and the sound seemed to go through Gideon like a bullet through water.

"My mother knew," they said at last, not turning around. "She knew she was being followed. She knew what Vole was. And she kept going anyway, alone, up a drowned river, to a sealed door at the bottom of a lake." A breath. "She died with the truth in her hands and hid it where no one could use it. And everyone called it an accident. Everyone called it bad luck. Bad weather."

"Gideon—"

"They buried her under a headstone that says *lost to the river*." Gideon turned. In the lamplight their face was terrible and calm. "It wasn't the river."

No one argued. There was nothing to argue with. The journal lay open on the table between them, and the decoded route ran up its margin in Nils's careful hand like a spine: *Kiln Rock. Tallowmere. The Serpentine. Greyfall Bend.*

The past had become a map. All that remained was to sail it.

* * *

Near dawn, Nils climbed to the wheelhouse roof with the drafting film pinned under a brass weight, checking the decoded route against the coastal charts they'd memorized years ago and never expected to need again. The sky was doing that gray-pink thing it did before sunrise on the Sable coast, the color of old scars. Mist stood on the water in columns. The Kestrel rocked at her moorings, patched and patient.

Bram came up the ladder quietly, two mugs of Sefa's coffee in hand, and offered one without a word. Nils took it. They had learned, in the mapping house, to accept warmth where it was offered, because it was rarely offered twice.

"You've got us a way upriver," Bram said. Not quite a question.

"I've got us a way upriver that Miriam Dunmore trusted enough to encode." Nils sipped. The coffee was awful and perfect. "Which is more than any chart can say. Half those channels don't exist on Crown surveys. The other half are surveyed wrong on purpose."

"On purpose," Bram repeated slowly.

"That's the part that keeps me awake." Nils spread the film on the wheelhouse roof, weighting the corners. "Look. Kiln Rock. On the Crown chart, the safe channel is the eastern throat. Miriam says the eastern is sounded wrong — deliberately. Someone falsified the hydrography." They traced the line north. "Tallowmere. The charts show shoal water across the whole reach. Miriam floated a loaded boat through it. And the Serpentine — the Crown survey calls it impassable, seasonal marsh. She poled through it in three days with cargo." Nils looked up at Bram. "Someone spent a great deal of money making sure no one could ever follow her route. You don't falsify charts for free. You falsify them when you own the printing contract."

Bram was quiet for a while. Down in the harbor, the mist was lifting off the water in rags, and the first light caught the tin roofs of Corrin's Landing and made them briefly golden.

"When I was in service," Bram said at last, in the flat voice they used for the parts of their past they didn't like, "we burned a village's granary records so the tax assessors couldn't prove what the people were owed. Same principle. Smaller scale. Erase the paper, erase the debt." They looked at the route. "You're saying the Trust erased a country."

"I'm saying your mother thought so," Nils said, "and she died for thinking it, and she left the proof at the bottom of a lake behind a bronze door, and the only map to it is written in herons and reedbeds." They rolled the film. "So yes. I'm saying it."

Bram nodded once, slow, like a man accepting a sentence. Then: "There's a launch been circling the harbor since midnight. Steam launch, black hull, no name board. It's come past the Kestrel four times."

Nils felt the morning go cold along their spine. "Survey vessel?"

"No survey vessel circles four times without lights." Bram drained their coffee. "I'll wake Gideon."

But Gideon was already in the companionway, dressed, the journal wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to their chest beneath their coat like a second heart. They had heard. Of course they had heard; they heard everything, the way people do who grew up learning which floorboards announced which footsteps.

"How long to raise steam?" Gideon asked.

Sefa's voice floated up from the engine room, already running, already answering: "Twenty minutes if you want her gentle. Twelve if you want her angry."

"Make her angry," Gideon said.

Nils stood at the rail as the Kestrel's boiler woke below their feet, a low animal heat rising through the deck plates, and watched the black launch slide out of the mist one more time — closer now, close enough to see a figure at its bow with field glasses raised, close enough to see the glasses lower slowly, deliberately, the way a man looks at you when he wants you to know you've been seen.

Then the launch turned, unhurried, and vanished back into the fog toward the upper river.

Toward Kiln Rock. Toward the route.

"They know," Nils said. "Somehow, they already know we have the journal."

Gideon's hand went to the oilcloth bundle over their heart. When they spoke, their voice was quiet and absolutely certain, and it was the voice of someone who had stopped measuring their life in miles.

"Then we'd better get there first."
