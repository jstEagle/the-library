## 8. Old Crew, New Wages

The rain in Port Callow never fell so much as it *arrived* — a gray insistence that began before dawn and did not so much end as lose interest. Cassian Vole watched it from the third-floor window of the Colonial Salvage Trust's annex, a building that had once been a counting house for the spice trade and still smelled faintly of nutmeg and old money, and he thought, as he did every morning, that the river gave nothing away for free. Not to him. Not to anyone.

His office was a study in deliberate modesty. A desk of dark sal wood, worn at the elbows where his own forearms had polished it across eleven years. A single lamp with a green glass shade. On the wall behind him, framed under glass, a survey chart of the Sable delta drawn in a hand he would have known anywhere, even blindfolded, even dead — because he had. He had known it dead. He had helped make it so.

*M. Dunmore, Expedition Surveyor, Third Sable Reconnaissance.* The ink had faded to the color of weak tea. He kept it there not from sentiment. He kept it the way a man keeps a splinter worked deep into his palm: to remind himself that the body learns to close around a wound, and that the closing is not the same as the healing.

Below, in the annex yard, a clerk was arguing with a porter about a crate of dredged ballast stone. Cassian watched without seeing. His mind was on the wire that had come in the night before, the one he had read three times and then burned in the lamp, because that was how you handled paper in this country if you intended to keep breathing.

*A chest recovered from the riverbed off Tallow Reach. Lead-lined. Bearing the seal of Vessany. Salvage crew believed to be operating out of the Kestrel, a patched barge working the lower reaches. Claim filed. Halloran assigned.*

Vessany.

The word still had weight, even now, even after a century of erasure. He had spent a good part of his life learning what the maps refused to say — that nations did not simply die, that they were *killed*, and that the killing was done twice: once with gunboats, and once with ink. The Trust had been the ink. He had been, in his small and deniable way, some of the ink too. He had told himself for years that this was the price of proximity, that a man who wanted to know things had to stand near the people who unmade things, and that knowing was worth the standing.

Then Miriam had died, and the arithmetic had changed forever.

He turned from the window. On the corner of his desk sat the auction catalogue, its cover soft from handling. *Sale of Unclaimed and Seized Effects: Scientific Instruments, Field Journals, Cartographic Materials, and Miscellaneous Lots.* The estate of the late Miriam Dunmore, dispersed by order of the creditors. He had not gone to the sale itself. He had sent a man — a soft-footed little solicitor named Pell, who had the gift of being forgettable in a room full of people trying to be remembered.

Pell had come back the evening before with a list of lots won and a single troubling detail, delivered in his dry, apologetic voice: *There was another buyer, sir. Persistent. Kept to the back of the room. Paid cash through an agent. Took Lot 41.*

Lot 41. The field journal. Third Sable Reconnaissance. Miriam's own hand, her own route, her own — Cassian was certain of it — her own suspicions, because Miriam had never once in her life recorded a thing she could instead *conceal*, and concealment, in her, had been a form of love. She had hidden things the way other people gave gifts.

He had assumed, at first, that the buyer was a collector. There were men in the capital who paid obscene sums for the journals of dead explorers, the way other men paid for teeth of extinct animals. But Pell had described the agent: a riverman's build, a salvager's rope-burned hands, a face that kept turning toward the door. And the description of the principal — young, or youngish, broad through the shoulders in the way of someone who worked for a living, with the kind of watchful stillness that Pell said reminded him of a heron at the edge of reeds.

Cassian had gone very still at his desk, the catalogue open under his lamp.

He had never met the Dunmore child. Miriam had spoken of them exactly twice in all the years he had known her, and both times she had spoken the way you speak of a thing you have locked in a strongbox and thrown into deep water — carefully, and with your eyes on the middle distance, and with the absolute conviction that the subject was closed. *They're better off without this life,* she had said, the second and last time, on the eve of the Fourth Expedition, and she had not said another word about it, and Cassian had been fool enough, or coward enough, to let it go.

Now the strongbox had floated up. Now the child — grown, rope-handed, heron-still — was buying back pieces of the mother the Trust had stripped and sold.

He should have felt alarm. What he felt instead, and this shamed him even as he felt it, was a terrible, vertiginous *relief*. Someone else knew. Someone else was pulling at the same thread. For eleven years he had carried the truth of the Third Reconnaissance alone in the dark of his respectable life, and the weight of it had grown teeth. If the Dunmore child was digging, then the digging had begun, and a thing begun could be finished — one way or the other.

He pressed his thumb against the worn elbow of the desk and made himself look at the survey chart on the wall. Miriam's hand. Her little annotations in the margins — *beware the false channel at Greyfall*, *water rises four days after first landfall* — her tidy, stubborn, unforgiving script.

*I'm sorry,* he did not say, because he had never said it, because the dead did not accept apologies and the living did not offer them cheap. *I'm going to finish what you started. Even if I have to finish it wrong.*

* * *

The man he wanted was called Dree Halloway, and he could be found, when he wished to be found, in the back room of the Anchor and Chain, drinking arrack that could have stripped paint and playing dominoes with a patience that unnerved people. Cassian had used him twice before — once to move cargo that needed to not exist, once to discourage a journalist — and had found him satisfactory in the way one finds a well-made tool satisfactory: no sentiment, no curiosity, no interest in anything beyond the shape of the payment.

The Anchor and Chain smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular sourness of men who had been rained on and given up on drying. Cassian entered with his umbrella furled like a sword he had no intention of drawing, and the room did its usual trick of rearranging itself around him — not deference exactly, but the wary recalculation of men noting that a gentleman in a good coat had walked into their territory and was not lost.

Halloway sat alone at the corner table. He was a slab of a man gone soft at the edges, with a boxer's flattened nose and small, intelligent eyes set deep under a brow like a shelf. He did not look up from his dominoes.

"Consultant," he said. It was not a greeting. It was a classification.

"Halloway."

"Sit, then, if you're sitting."

Cassian sat. The chair complained. He arranged his hands on the sticky table — a gesture of openness, of patience, of a man who had all the time in the world, which was a lie, because the monsoon was coming and the river would close like a fist, and everything that was going to happen had to happen inside the next few weeks or not at all.

"There's a barge," he said. "The *Kestrel*. Working salvage out of the lower reaches. She pulled something off the bottom at Tallow Reach that doesn't belong to her."

"Everything under the river belongs to the Trust," Halloway said, turning a domino over with one thick finger. "That's the law. I've heard the law recited at me in courtrooms."

"And the law," Cassian said, "is about to be enforced. But enforcement is a blunt instrument, and I need something with an edge. The Trust will send inspectors. Inspectors write reports. Reports go into files. Files can be lost, mislaid, or read by the wrong eyes." He let that sit. "I need to know who is on that barge. I need to know where she goes. And when the time comes, I may need her stopped — quietly, without paperwork, without anyone standing on a dock afterward asking questions."

Halloway turned another domino. The click of it was very loud in the smoky room.

"You're asking for river work," he said. "River work's different from dock work. River men know each other. You put strangers on the water, they get noticed. You want eyes on a salvage crew, you want someone who can pass — a dredge hand, a deckhand looking for wages."

"Then find me one."

"I can find you three." Halloway finally looked up, and his small eyes were patient and bottomless. "But river work costs more. And quiet stopping costs more again. And there's a question I'd want answered before I put my people on the water, because it affects the price."

"Ask it."

Halloway leaned back. The chair groaned again, a longer complaint this time.

"Is this Trust work, or is this Vole work?"

The rain hammered the roof. Somewhere behind the bar, a bottle broke and a man laughed. Cassian held the other man's gaze and did not blink, because blinking, with men like Halloway, was a form of confession.

"The Trust pays my wage," he said. "This is off the wage."

Halloway nodded slowly, as if a sum had been confirmed. "Off the wage is double the wage."

"Off the wage is triple, and you'll never see me in this room again if a word of it travels."

"Then we understand each other." Halloway swept his dominoes into a leather bag with a sound like bones being gathered for burial. "I'll have a man aboard the *Kestrel* inside four days. And Vole —" he paused at the edge of the table, one hand flat on the wood — "the chest off Tallow Reach. The one with the old seal on it. There's talk already. Dock talk travels faster than the Trust's wires. You want to know what the talk says?"

"I want to know everything the talk says."

"It says the seal's a ghost seal. Vessany. It says there's men in this city who'd burn a barge to the waterline to keep that name out of the newspapers." Halloway's face did not change at all; it was the voice of a man reading a shipping manifest. "I don't hold opinions. But I price risk. And that chest is risk."

"Then price it," Cassian said, "and send me the number."

* * *

He walked back through the rain rather than take a cab, because walking was where he did his thinking, and because the rain in Port Callow made the city honest — stripped the paint, ran the soot down the walls in black tears, turned every gutter into a small brown river running toward the big brown river, which ran, in its turn, toward the sea, carrying everything the land wanted forgotten.

*The seal's a ghost seal.* Of course it was. He had seen it once, years ago, in a drawer of Miriam's that he had never been meant to open — a wax impression, a sketch, her notes in three languages about a treasury that had never officially existed. *They didn't lose the treasury,* she had written, and he could still see the pressure of her pen through the paper, the way her hand always pressed too hard when she was angry. *They lost the *nation*. The treasury is just what was left over. And a treasury without a nation is the most stealable object on earth.*

He had understood, that night in her study with the lamp low and her footsteps overhead, that he was holding the end of a thread that, pulled hard enough, would unravel the Trust itself. And he had put it back in the drawer. He had put it back in the drawer because he was thirty-one years old and ambitious and in love — with her, though he had never said it, and with the truth, though he had never once acted on it — and he had told himself there would be time. There would be an expedition. There would be a moment when the two of them stood in some flooded archive hall with the whole rotten architecture of the Trust laid open between them, and she would look at him, and he would finally be the man she believed he was.

Then the Fourth Expedition had gone upriver. Then the wires had stopped. Then the boats had come back with half a crew and no Miriam, and the official report had said *fever*, and Cassian had read the report and known — the way you know your own heartbeat — that it was a lie written by men whose signatures he recognized.

He had spent eleven years proving it to himself in silence. He had pulled the Trust's own records, quietly, one thread at a time, and what he had found was worse than fever. It was a decision. It had minutes. It had signatures. One of the signatures, on a requisition order dated three days before the Fourth Expedition's last wireless transmission, was his own — a routine authorization, a cargo manifest, a line of ordnance listed as *survey equipment*, and he had signed it without reading it because Cassian Vole, at thirty-one, had been a man who signed things.

He had not pulled a trigger. He had not given an order. He had simply been the kind of man who could be used, and he had been used, and Miriam had died of it, and her child had grown up an orphan with a name to be ashamed of, and the treasury of a murdered nation still lay somewhere under the brown water, waiting.

So no. It was not greed. Greed would have been simple. Greed would have been a thing he could set down. What he wanted was the one thing the Trust's money could not buy and the river could not drown: to be, at the end of this, the man Miriam had believed he was — even if she had to be dead, even if he had to be damned, even if the Dunmore child had to hate him for the way it was done. Some inheritances you claim. Some you steal back.

* * *

The wire from Pell came at dusk, delivered by hand, as all of Pell's communications were. Cassian read it standing at the window with the rain silvering the glass.

*Re: Lot 41. The agent who outbid us — traced the cash through a chandler on Rope Street. Principal is one G. Dunmore, salvage, master of the barge Kestrel. Age approximately twenty-six. No registered address. No bank. No debts on record, which is itself remarkable. Purchased the journal and nothing else. Left the sale alone, on foot, into the rain.*

*One further item, sir, which I attach for completeness. The Trust's claim on the Tallow Reach recovery was filed this morning. Assigned to Inspector Halloran. I am told Halloran is thorough. I am also told, from a man who has worked under him, that Halloran keeps his own copies of everything, and that his own copies do not always match the Trust's.*

Cassian read the last line twice. Halloran. He knew the name the way he knew all the Trust's names — from org charts and dinner-party gossip and the small, careful intelligence network that a man built when he intended, someday, to burn his own house down. Halloran was either honest, which made him dangerous, or dishonest in a way that could be steered, which made him useful. Cassian filed the question away with all the others.

Then he sat down at his desk, drew a fresh sheet of paper toward him, and did the thing he was actually good at, the thing that had made him respectable: he planned.

The *Kestrel* would run. That was certain. A salvage crew that pulled a ghost seal off the riverbed and then bought back the dead woman's journal was not a crew that intended to sit in port and wait for the inspectors. They would go upriver. They would follow her route — *his* route, the Third Reconnaissance's route, the one he had walked beside her, the one he knew every bend of the way he knew his own hands. And at the end of the route, at Greyfall Bend, where the river had swallowed the archive halls of a nation that had never officially existed, they would find what Miriam had found, or what Miriam had hidden, or both.

And he would be waiting. Not with gunboats — not yet, not first, not while there was still a chance, however thin, that the Dunmore child could be turned rather than broken. Because Miriam had believed in the child, and Cassian had spent eleven years learning to believe in whatever Miriam believed in, even from the bottom of the river where she lay.

He wrote three names on the sheet. Halloway. Pell. Halloran — with a question mark after it, and then, after a long moment, the question mark struck through and replaced with a small, cold note: *watch.*

Below, the annex yard was dark now, the clerk and the porter long gone, the crate of ballast stone standing in the rain like a headstone. Cassian capped his pen. On the wall, Miriam's survey chart hung in its frame, her annotations faded to whispers, and he stood before it the way a man stands before a grave he is not permitted to visit.

"I'm coming up the river," he told her quietly, in the empty office, with the rain coming down like the sky had finally decided to confess something. "And this time I'm reading everything I sign."

The lamp hissed. The rain did not answer. Four days' hard steaming upriver, the chart said, to Greyfall Bend — and the monsoon was coming, and the river would close, and whatever was going to be found or buried or avenged would have to be found or buried or avenged before the water finished falling.

Cassian Vole put on his coat, took up his umbrella, and went out into the rain to hire the rest of his ghosts.
