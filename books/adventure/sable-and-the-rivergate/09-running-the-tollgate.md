## 9. Running the Tollgate

Bram had learned to count in the army, and he had never unlearned it. He counted the seconds between lightning and thunder as the storm front built over the western hills. He counted the lamps along the Kestrel's deck rail — seven lit, three dark, exactly as planned. He counted the men on the toll platform at Cutter's Reach, silhouetted against their brazier fire: four visible, which meant at least two more out of sight behind the signal shed, because the Trust never staffed a checkpoint thin when there was money moving and no war to blame the losses on.

Six men. One chain across the channel. One swivel gun mounted on the platform, its muzzle catching the brazier light like a wet black eye.

Bram counted all of it twice, and then he went below to tell Gideon the numbers were bad.

"They'll see us," he said. The chart room was small and smelled of lamp oil and wet canvas, and Gideon stood over the river chart with a divider in one hand and Miriam Dunmore's field journal open beside them, and Bram thought, not for the first time, that the child had grown into the mother's shoulders without ever noticing it happening. "Six on the platform. Swivel gun. Chain's up — I can see the buoy lights marking it."

Gideon didn't look up from the chart. "And the decoy?"

"Old Marta's half a mile behind us, running dark except her stern lantern. Sefa's rigged her wheel with a line to the boiler whistle." Bram turned his hands over, looked at the calluses, the old burn scar across the left palm that the army had given him and that he still caught himself hiding inside his sleeves. "When she sounds off at the bend, every eye down there goes to her. That's our window. Maybe ninety seconds before they figure out she's empty."

"Maybe," Gideon said.

"Maybe," Bram agreed. "I've run worse odds for worse pay and better lies."

That got him a look — not sharp, not soft either. Gideon had a way of looking at a man that made him feel counted too, weighed and entered into some ledger Bram couldn't read. "You don't have to be topside for this," Gideon said. "If the Trust has your face on file—"

"They don't have my face on anything. That was rather the point of me, once." He said it lightly. It didn't land lightly; he heard it land in the room like a dropped tool. He cleared his throat. "I'll take the bow line. Nils can mind the wheel with Sefa."

Gideon nodded once and went back to the chart, and Bram climbed the ladder into the rain-smelling dark and took his place at the bow rail where the river ran black and wide under a sky the color of a bruise going bad.

* * *

The thing about guilt, Bram had learned, was that it didn't keep a schedule. It came when it liked. Tonight it came standing at the bow with the spray cold on his knuckles, and it brought company.

It brought him Vellacourt.

Not the name — the name he'd buried with the rest of that year, along with two good soldiers and a supply contract that had existed only on paper. But the shape of him. The way a man stands when he knows the convoy he's guarding is carrying nothing but his own signature, and the ambush is coming anyway, and he has already spent the bribe money. Bram had stood that watch. Bram had taken the coin, told himself the convoy would turn back, told himself the road reports were wrong, told himself every lie a man tells when the truth costs more than he's willing to pay. Two names on a memorial wall in a country that had stopped being on anyone's maps, same as Vessany, same as everything else the Crown decided to erase when erasing was cheaper than answering.

He had told himself he'd never again be the man who knew and said nothing.

And here he stood, guarding a chest full of a dead woman's testimony, running a checkpoint for a crew that trusted him, while somewhere behind them Cassian Vole — respectable, patient, well-funded Cassian Vole — was buying up every scrap of Miriam Dunmore's life at auction and hiring guns to meet them at Greyfall Bend. Bram knew how that story ended. He had been the ending of that story once, for other people.

The whistle sounded.

It came up the river from behind them, low and mournful and wrong — a freight barge's voice coming out of an empty hull, and it was beautiful, and Bram's chest hurt hearing it, because old Marta had been Sefa's first salvage and Sefa loved that rusted hulk the way you love a dog that's too old to work, and now she was spending her like a coin. Somewhere astern, a lantern swung. On the toll platform, torches moved. Voices carried flat across the water — "Freight, upbound, no manifest light—" and the scrape of the chain winch beginning to turn.

"Now," Bram said, though nobody could hear him, and the Kestrel answered anyway.

Sefa opened her up the way she did everything — without ceremony, without wasted motion, the engine taking the load like a mule taking a hill. The bow came around. The current took them. And the toll platform slid past on their port side close enough that Bram could smell the brazier smoke and see the swivel gun's crew scrambling, one man shouting, another fumbling at the breech with hands made stupid by cold and surprise, and the chain was rising but not risen, and the gap under it was maybe the height of a man standing on another man's shoulders—

"Duck," Bram said, to the whole boat, to nobody.

The chain passed overhead with a sound like a struck cable, close enough that he felt it in his teeth, and then they were through, and the river opened ahead of them, and behind them the emptiness of old Marta's whistle finally reached the ears of the men on the platform and turned from confusion to fury in the space of three shouted words.

Nils whooped — actually whooped, high and young and ridiculous, from the wheelhouse, and Sefa let the engine sing, and for about forty seconds the Kestrel was the happiest ship on the Sable coast.

Then the searchlight came on.

It swept the water behind them, found old Marta rolling empty in the swell, and held there, bewildered, while the real quarry ran on into the dark. Bram watched it over his shoulder and did the arithmetic he hated doing. Ninety seconds, he'd promised. They'd used sixty getting clear. The swivel gun had a range of eight hundred yards and a crew that was angry now instead of sleepy.

"Get her behind the point," he called forward. "There's a dead slough past the willow bar — deep enough, if the charts are honest."

"The charts are Nils's," Sefa called back, "so they're honest."

They took the point hard, the current grabbing the hull and shoving, and the willow bar went by in a whisper of leaves, and then the slough swallowed them — a channel so narrow the branches combed the deck, so dark that the searchlight swept over them twice and found nothing but trees. In that green-black tunnel, with the engine idling down to a heartbeat, Bram sat on the deck plates with his back against the rail and discovered his hands were shaking.

Not from the running. From the counting. Because he'd counted six men on that platform, and one of them, in the brazier light, just before the chain, had turned toward the river and Bram had seen his face — young, scared, holding a lantern up like a man signaling a train — and the boy had been looking at the Kestrel, not the decoy. Had seen them. Had hesitated, one whole second, before turning away and shouting about the freight barge instead.

One second. A boy on a toll platform had looked straight at them and chosen to look elsewhere, and Bram would spend the rest of this night not knowing whether that was mercy or mistake or something the Trust would make him pay for later.

* * *

They lay in the slough until the small hours, engine cold, lamps dark, mosquitoes working the still air. Gideon sat down next to Bram at some point without being asked, the way Gideon did things — arriving exactly when arriving cost something.

"You saw the gun crew," Gideon said quietly. Not a question.

"One of them saw us first." Bram worked the burn scar with his thumb. "Kid my age when I enlisted. He flagged us and then he didn't. I keep turning it over — whether we owe him or whether he's already paid."

"Both can be true."

"That's what frightens me." He looked out at the black weave of branches. "I've been on the other side of a hesitation like that. I know what it costs the man who doesn't hesitate, and I know what it costs everyone else when he does it too late." His jaw tightened. "Vole will have that platform's logs by morning. He'll know exactly where we slipped him, and he won't send six men next time. He'll send the river itself — papers, injunctions, gunboats with legal right-of-way. You can't outrun a signature, Gideon. I've tried."

Gideon was quiet a long moment. Then: "You could have told us to leave you at the last dock. Before the checkpoint. Nobody would have asked why."

"I know."

"So why are you here?"

Bram laughed — short, cracked, surprised out of him. He hadn't expected the question to hurt. It hurt the way setting a bone hurts: necessary, and proof that the limb was still alive. "Because the last time I kept quiet about what I knew, two men died and I collected the wages," he said. "I'm done collecting. Whatever's in that chest, whatever your mother buried at Greyfall Bend — I'd rather stand on the right side of it this time, even if the right side sinks."

Gideon put a hand briefly on his shoulder — five seconds, no more, and gone again — and it was worth more than any medal Bram had ever declined or earned.

From the wheelhouse, Nils's voice drifted down, drowsy and pleased: "Tollgate's thirty miles behind us. Next one's at Harker's Weir, and nobody's bribed me to care."

Above the trees, through a tear in the cloud, the stars showed for a moment — and in the west, low and heavy, the sky had begun to pile itself into towers, blue-black and silent, building toward something.

Bram counted the seconds between the far-off heat lightning and its thunder, out of habit, out of discipline, out of the soldier's superstition that counting keeps the worst things honest.

Four miles. The storm was four miles out and walking toward them, and somewhere ahead, at Greyfall Bend, Cassian Vole was already waiting with better maps than theirs and worse reasons.

Bram closed his eyes and, for the first time in twenty years, prayed not for deliverance but for one more chance to be counted among the ones who stayed.
