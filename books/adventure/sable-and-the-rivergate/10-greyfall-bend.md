## 10. Greyfall Bend

The river changed color a mile before we saw the ruins.

I was at the wheel when it happened—watching the water go from the brown-green of living current to something older, a slate-grey shot through with veins of copper where the tributaries bled down from the highlands. Sefa called it the deadwater. She'd grown up on these rivers and had a name for everything: the way fog pooled in the bends like breath held in a cupped hand, the way fish went quiet in certain channels as if listening.

"Greyfall's ahead," she said, coming up beside me with two cups of chicory coffee. She handed me one without asking. That was Sefa. She never asked; she simply decided what you needed and provided it, and you learned to be grateful or get out of her engine room.

"How far?"

"Past that ridge." She nodded at a wall of jungle so dense it looked poured rather than grown, green standing up in cliffs along the bank. "The old maps call it a waterfall. There hasn't been a waterfall in sixty years. The quake dropped the whole valley and the river just... moved in. Took the town the way you'd take a chair someone left on a dock."

Nils was already at the bow rail with his charts spread across a plank he'd lashed there for exactly this purpose. He'd been up since before dawn, I knew, because I'd heard him pacing—the runaway cartographer's insomnia, feet going heel-to-toe, heel-to-toe, working through coordinates the way other people worked through prayers. He didn't look up when he spoke.

"The channel narrows to forty meters past the ridge. Then it opens." His finger traced a line on his own hand-drawn chart, because no printed map of this stretch existed anymore—the Trust had let the surveys lapse after Vessany came off the books, as if forgetting a place could kill it twice. "It opens into what used to be the administrative quarter. The archive hall should be—" he paused, recalculating against the journal's bearings, "—thirty feet down. Maybe less if the silt's been shifting."

Thirty feet. I'd worked salvage in thirty feet my whole adult life. It was nothing. It was the shallow end of drowning.

But this time I wasn't diving for cargo. I was diving for my mother's last footsteps, and no depth table accounted for that.

* * *

We anchored in the lee of the ridge while the light was still low and gold, and I stood at the rail and looked at what remained of Vessany.

The guidebooks—and there were none, but Nils had reconstructed one from fragments, from shipping manifests, from a missionary's letter he'd found in a port archive and copied out in his small precise hand—described a city of white stone and green copper roofs, built on terraces above the falls, its archive hall carved into the cliff itself so that the records would survive fire and flood alike. The founders had been proud of that. *What the water cannot burn,* ran the inscription over the door, according to the missionary, *and what fire cannot drown.*

The water had found another way. When the quake came, it hadn't destroyed the archive. It had swallowed it whole, intact, and then the Crown had declared the nation extinct on paper and the drowned city off every chart, and for a hundred years the greatest library on the coast had sat in the dark with nobody left alive who was allowed to remember it existed.

Except the ones who drowned it. Except my mother.

From the deck of the Kestrel, all you could see were hints. A line of masonry breaking the surface like the back of some enormous sleeping animal. The top of a bell tower, tilted fifteen degrees, its bronze bell still hanging—green with age, silent, but hanging, as if the tower refused to finish falling until someone rang it again. Birds had colonized the rooftops that broke the surface in clusters. Their noise was the only sound. It made the place feel less like a ruin than a held breath.

Bram came to stand beside me. He did that more now, since the tollgate. Since he'd run the decoy barge through the checkpoint with the Trust's searchlight sweeping the water and come back to us pale and shaking and forgiven, which was somehow worse for him than punishment would have been. He carried his guilt the way he carried his rifle: close, checked often, ready to be used.

"You don't have to go down first," he said.

"Yeah, I do."

"I know." He watched the bell tower. "I'm saying it anyway. That's what crew's for. Saying the things everybody already knows so nobody has to carry them alone."

I almost laughed. Almost. "When did you get wise?"

"About the same time I got old." He clapped my shoulder once, hard, the way he did instead of embracing anyone, and went forward to check the anchor line for the third time.

* * *

We dove at midday, when the sun stood highest and pushed the furthest down through the grey water.

The four of us went—me, Bram, Sefa, and Nils, who had insisted despite having logged maybe twenty dives in his life, all of them, he admitted, in warm clear harbors. Sefa rigged his lines herself and triple-checked his regulator and told him, with brutal tenderness, "You stay within arm's reach of me or I will personally drag you up by your ears." He nodded. He was too nervous to be offended, and I saw his hands trembling slightly as he pulled on his gloves—not fear, I realized, but the specific terror of a man about to see something he'd only ever known as ink on paper. A map becoming a world.

We went down the anchor line together, four shapes descending through water that went from amber to grey to a deep blue-green dark, and the ruined city rose to meet us.

I've tried, since, to describe it to people who weren't there, and I've failed every time. The problem is that wrecks are chaos—a ship breaks, and broken things look broken. But Greyfall Bend hadn't broken. It had drowned. Streets still ran straight under my fins, cobbled, lined with the stumps of walls and the occasional doorway standing complete, framing darkness. A fountain sat in a plaza, dry for a century, its basin furred with silt and white fan-coral that had taken root in the carved reliefs of its sides. Fish moved through windows. Light fell in shafts through gaps in the collapsed upper stories, and in those shafts the particulate hung suspended like snow that had forgotten how to land, and swimming through them felt like swimming through the memory of weather.

The inscription was still legible over the archive doors. *What the water cannot burn.* The rest was lost to coral, but I put my glove against the stone anyway, just for a moment, just to touch it.

Sefa tapped my arm and pointed.

The archive hall opened below us like a cathedral that had decided, at the last moment, to lie down. The roof had partially fallen—one great slab of it lay angled across the nave, and the shafts of light came through the wound—but the walls stood, and the shelves stood, row upon row of them receding into the gloom, and on the shelves, protected by lead-sealed cases the Vessany archivists had designed against exactly this century, the records waited. Ten thousand documents. A nation's whole memory, filed and shelved and patient.

Nils made a sound into his regulator that might have been a sob. He drifted along the first shelf with his gloved fingers hovering an inch above the case labels, not touching, reading, and even through the mask I could see his face doing something complicated—joy and grief in the same expression, the face of a man finding home in a place he'd never been.

Then Bram's hand closed on my shoulder and turned me, and I saw the camp.

It was tucked into the lee of the fallen roof slab, sheltered from current and silt-fall both—a deliberate choice, a surveyor's choice. Three tent pegs of galvanized iron, still driven into the stone floor. A rusted stove. A coil of rope gone to soft black lace. And rising from the center of the camp, planted upright in a socket chiseled into the archive floor itself, a steel survey marker, the kind Miriam Dunmore had used on every expedition of her career: a rod three feet tall, painted bands long since flaked away, topped with a brass plate stamped with her initials.

*M.D.*

She had chiseled the socket herself. I could see the tool marks, fresh-edged even after five years underwater, because the cold deep water preserved them the way peat preserves bodies. She had stood on this floor, in this drowned dark, and cut a hole in a hundred-year-old nation's archive floor to plant her little flag, and then she had gone upriver and died, and the marker was still standing. Still standing. Everything else in this city had fallen or floated or dissolved, and her cheap expedition-grade survey rod stood perfectly plumb, as if she'd only stepped away to check the next chamber and would be back any minute.

I hung in the water in front of it for a long time. Long enough that Sefa's air gauge must have been screaming at her, because she swam to me and took my gauge and read it and then, instead of tugging me upward, she just stayed there, holding my harness strap, giving me another minute bought with her own tank.

Through the mask, my breath sounded like the tide. I thought: *she touched this.* Her hands had been where my hands were. She had seen the shelves by lamplight, before the water, when this room was dry and the nation it remembered still legally existed. She had walked these rows knowing what was coming—or suspecting it—and she had planted her marker anyway, a small stubborn human claim staked against erasure. Against the Trust. Against Cassian Vole. Against whatever had been closing behind her upriver like a door.

I reached out and touched the brass plate. Under the film of algae, the stamped letters were sharp. My mother's initials, smaller than my palm, colder than the water.

*Nils,* I signaled, and pointed at the marker, and pointed at his slate.

He understood. Of course he did. He swam over and braced himself against the shelf and began, right there at fifty feet, sketching the camp into his chart—the pegs, the stove, the marker, their exact position relative to the fallen slab and the door—because a thing recorded is a thing that can't be taken, and if the Trust ever burned the Kestrel and everything on her, somewhere in some archive Nils kept, my mother's footsteps would survive in ink.

We found her final camp log in a waterproof cylinder strapped to the marker's base. One page, grease pencil, the letters cramped and hurried:

*Day 41. Chest recovered to surface cache. Ledger secured. C. knows we found it. Trust boats at the bend by week's end. If anything happens, the chest goes downriver, and whoever carries it had better be ready to open it in daylight.*

*In daylight.* She'd written it five years ago, and I read it fifty feet down in the dark, and I felt it go through me like a current—the sense, overwhelming and awful and strangely comforting, that my mother had been writing to me. Not to whoever. To me. To the child she'd left ashore with relatives and a locked study, the child who grew up measuring life in miles because years hurt too much. She couldn't have known it would be me. But she'd hoped for somebody like me. Somebody with salvage in their blood and nothing left to lose and enough anger to swim a river with.

*Ready to open it in daylight.*

I pocketed the log page inside my suit, and I gave the marker one last look, and I turned for the ascent line.

That's when Sefa grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise and pointed back down.

She'd gone to the cache site while I said goodbye to the marker—Miriam's note had mentioned a surface cache, and Sefa missed nothing—and she'd found it wedged beneath the fallen roof slab where my mother's crew had hidden it: the chest. Our chest. The lead-lined box with the seal of Vessany that our dredge had torn loose from the riverbed two hundred miles downstream, except—

Except Sefa was running her lamp along its underside, and her face behind the glass had gone very still, and she turned the light so I could see what she was seeing.

There was a seam in the base of the chest. A hairline joint, machined, deliberate, running the full perimeter of the bottom panel. A seam like that meant space behind it. A compartment. A second compartment, hidden in the double bottom, built by someone who had expected the chest to be searched and wanted one part of it to survive the searching.

And set into the seam, small and bright under Sefa's lamp, was a keyhole.

Not a lock shape I recognized. Not a shape I recognized at all—except that some deep animal part of me did recognize it, some memory older than language, and my hand was already rising of its own accord toward the cord around my neck, toward the pendant I'd worn since I was six years old and had never once taken off, the pendant my mother had knelt down and fastened there herself, saying, *keep this close, Gideon, closer than your name.*

The keyhole waited in the dark. The pendant pressed warm against my sternum, warmer than it had any right to be, and above us, faint through a hundred feet of grey water, the drowned bell of Vessany seemed to tremble on its rope.
