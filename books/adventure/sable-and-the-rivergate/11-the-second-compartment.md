## 11. The Second Compartment

Sefa had taken apart engines older than her grandmother and put them back together humming, but she had never once been afraid of a machine until the night she opened the chest.

Not that it was a machine. That was the trouble. It was a box—lead-lined, Vessany-stamped, river-scoured—and boxes did not lie to you the way engines did. An engine told you everything in the sound of its turning. A box kept its mouth shut and let you do the lying for yourself.

She sat cross-legged on the Kestrel's deck plates with lamplight pooled around her knees, the chest between her legs like a patient on a table, and she listened to it the way she listened to anything she meant to open. Tap. Listen. Tap again. The hull of the barge breathed around them—the river's slow pulse through the seams, Bram's boots overhead as he walked his watch, Nils scratching at maps in the aft cabin like a mouse in a wall. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. And underneath all of it, when Sefa pressed her palm flat against the chest's lid, something that was not quite an echo.

A hollowness. Low and wrong, down near the base, where there should have been nothing but lead and old wood.

"You've been holding out on us," she told it.

The chest had already given up its first secret days ago: the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, its final page bearing her mother's name in ink that had outlasted the woman who wrote it. Gideon had carried that ledger around the barge for two days like a hot coal they couldn't set down and couldn't hold still. Now it lay on Sefa's workbench under a cloth, because Gideon had finally surrendered it—"You're better with things than I am right now"—and gone to sit alone in the bow with their back against the rail, watching the dark water slide past Greyfall Bend's drowned rooftops.

The ruins rose all around them. That was the other reason Sefa's hands were not quite steady. They were anchored in the middle of a city that wasn't supposed to exist, in water that held the shapes of streets and steeples forty feet down, and every time the current shifted, the Kestrel's hull groaned like something waking up. She'd grown up on stories about Vessany the way everyone grew up on stories about Vessany—a nation erased so thoroughly that its name survived only as a curse and a cautionary tale. *Don't build what can't be taxed. Don't be what the map forgets.* And here she sat inside the proof that the forgetting had been done by hand.

Tap. Listen.

There it was again. A dead note, half an inch above the true bottom, running the width of the chest like a seam of silence.

She fetched her tools. Not the big ones—the delicate ones, the ones she kept wrapped in flannel because they'd belonged to her father and he'd trusted no one else with them. A feeler gauge thin as a fish scale. A mirror on a jointed stem. A pick set fine enough to clean a watch. She worked by feel mostly, because the light kept sliding off the lead lining in ways that lied, and after an hour of patience that would have made a saint swear, her fingertip found it: a hairline seam in the lead itself, too straight to be corrosion, too deliberate to be accident.

A false bottom. But not a loose one. This one had been *sealed*—soldered, from the look and smell of it, with a heat she could still faintly detect in the metal's temper even after a century underwater. Whoever had built this chest had intended the lower chamber to stay shut until someone very specific came along with very specific hands.

"Of course they did," Sefa muttered. "Nothing in my life is ever just a box."

* * *

She called Gideon over at dawn, because it was their chest, their mother, their ghost, and because Sefa had learned long ago which doors you didn't walk through alone.

Gideon crouched across from her, hair still wet from washing in the river, eyes red-rimmed in the grey light. They looked, Sefa thought, like someone who hadn't slept so much as lost arguments with sleep all night. The ledger sat between them. The chest sat between them. The whole drowned nation sat around them, silent as held breath.

"Feel here." Sefa guided their fingers along the seam. "False bottom. Sealed shut. And before you ask—no, I don't think it wants prying. Lead that thick, soldered like that? You force it, and whatever's inside gets a hundred years of river water through the crack in one bad second."

"So we don't force it."

"We don't force it." She turned the chest on its side and angled her mirror into the gap beneath the rim, working the stem inch by inch. "But sealed chambers are like locked engines. There's always a keyway somewhere. Builders get proud. They want credit for the trick even if nobody's supposed to see it."

The mirror caught lamplight, then shadow, then—there. Near the front face, hidden behind a carved rosette that matched its twin on the opposite corner except for one detail: a pinhole, small and clean, bored straight through into the mechanism.

Sefa sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something complicated.

"It's a lock," she said. "A real one. Not a padlock hasp—those are decorative, most of them, theater for thieves. This is built into the chest itself. Which means whoever made it expected the right person to come looking, and expected the wrong people to waste years trying." She looked up. "It needs a key."

Gideon went very still. It was a particular stillness Sefa had seen on them exactly twice before—once when the seal of Vessany surfaced dripping from the dredge line, and once when the ledger's last page named M. Dunmore, custodian. The stillness of a person feeling the past reach up through the floorboards and take hold of their ankle.

"I don't have a key," Gideon said.

"No," Sefa agreed. "You have that."

She nodded at the cord around their neck, where the pendant hung—the one thing Gideon wore that never came off, not for dives, not for storms, not for the five years of silence since Miriam Dunmore died. Sefa had noticed it the way she noticed everything mechanical: a small object of tarnished silver, shaped like nothing in particular, worn smooth at the edges by decades of a thumb worrying it. She'd assumed it was grief made portable. A keepsake. Everyone on the river carried one kind or another.

Gideon's hand came up and closed around it, an instinct so old it had become invisible.

"This?" Their voice came out strange. "This is just—it was hers. She gave it to me when I was nine. Told me never to lose it, never to sell it, never to let anyone see it. I thought—" A breath. "I thought it was just a mother being a mother. Something to remember her by."

"May I?"

Gideon hesitated. Then, slowly, they worked the cord over their head and set the pendant in Sefa's palm, and Sefa felt the weight of it—more weight than the size promised, dense in the way of good metal, and cold, though it had lived against Gideon's skin for twenty years and should have been warm as blood.

She turned it in the light. The shape resolved: not abstract at all, but a key. A bow worked into a knot pattern she now recognized from the chest's own stamping—the interlaced device of Vessany—and a bit whose teeth were disguised as decorative ridges, worn nearly smooth by Gideon's thumb. Twenty years of anxious fidgeting, and the wearing had never gone deep enough to ruin the cut. The key had waited inside its disguise the way the chamber had waited behind its seal.

"Oh," Sefa said softly. "Oh, you absolute brilliant paranoid woman, Miriam."

"She built the lock around it," Gideon whispered. "Or had it built. Before—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Before the expedition. She gave it to me and sent me away to school and I thought she was just being sentimental. She was *hiding it*. In plain sight. In the one place no thief would ever think to look because it never left her child's neck."

"And the one place," Sefa said gently, "you'd never think to look either."

* * *

They didn't open it right away.

Sefa wanted to—that was the honest truth, and she prided herself on her honesty the way other people prided themselves on their manners. Every craft instinct in her body leaned toward the seam, toward the pinhole, toward the satisfying click of a mechanism answering its key after a century of silence. She had spent her whole life opening things. It was who she was. Engines, locks, the stubborn knots in other people's silences. Opening things was how she loved them.

But Gideon sat there holding the pendant in both fists like a bird they weren't sure was alive, and didn't move, and Sefa found she couldn't either.

Bram appeared at the companionway—he had a talent for materializing when the deck got quiet—and took in the scene without a word. He didn't ask. He lowered himself onto a crate with the careful economy of a man who'd learned that presence was sometimes the only useful thing you could offer, and he stayed.

Nils came next, drawn by the same gravity, clutching a pencil still behind one ear. They hovered at the edge of the lamplight until Gideon said, without looking up, "Sit down, Nils. All of you might as well see this. If it's what I think it is, it isn't mine alone anymore."

So the four of them arranged themselves around the chest in the grey-gold light of morning coming up over Greyfall Bend, and the river muttered against the hull, and somewhere below them lay the archive hall where Miriam Dunmore had walked her last footsteps, and Sefa understood suddenly why Gideon couldn't move.

"It's the last thing she'll ever give you," Sefa said quietly. "Isn't it. Everything else is finished. Said, done, buried. But this—" she touched the chest's lid, "—this is a letter she wrote to a future she didn't believe she'd reach. And once you open it, it's read. There's no unread-ing it."

Gideon laughed, a cracked, wet sound with no humor in it. "Five years I've managed not to think about her properly. Five years of miles instead of years, of keeping the name off my lips and the memory under load, busy enough that it couldn't catch me. And now—" They spread their hands. The pendant glinted between their knuckles. "Now she's everywhere. In the ledger. In the journal. In the goddamn river. And the last door between us is a piece of tin I've been wearing since I was nine years old."

"Then don't open it today," Bram said.

Every head turned. Bram rarely spoke in these moments; he was the crew's ballast, not its voice. But he met Gideon's eyes with the flat, unflinching steadiness he brought to everything, including his own guilt, which Sefa knew sat in him like shrapnel.

"When I came back from the border war," he said, "I had a letter my sergeant wrote me before the last push. Told us all to open it if he didn't. Took me three years to read it. Three years, and when I finally did, I wished I'd taken ten—or none. Because there's no version of it that doesn't hurt. There's only the version where you're ready, and the version where you aren't." He shrugged, massive shoulders rising and falling. "I'm not telling you to wait. I'm telling you the waiting counts for something. Whatever's in there has kept a hundred years. It'll keep another day, if the day does you any good."

"And if the Trust finds us tomorrow?" Nils asked, barely above a whisper. "If Vole's gunboats come up the bend tonight? Then the choice gets made for us, and Gideon never—"

"Nils." Gideon's voice was quiet, but it landed like an anchor. "I know. I've been doing the same arithmetic." They looked down at the pendant a long moment. The morning strengthened around them; the drowned city's broken spires caught the light and threw it back dull as old coins. When Gideon spoke again, their voice had changed—lower, steadier, the voice of someone stepping off a ledge they'd been circling for days.

"My mother spent her whole life being careful for other people. Careful with the artifacts, careful with the sites, careful with the truth—so careful the truth killed her anyway, or the men who couldn't afford it did." Their thumb moved over the pendant's worn teeth, back and forth, the gesture of twenty years. "I spent five years being careful in the other direction. Careful of the name. Careful of the law. Careful of caring. And look where it got me—anchored over her grave with her killers' paymasters closing in." They lifted their head, and Sefa saw the decision arrive in their face like weather. "I'm done being careful. Sefa—give me your hand. Let's see if she trusts me with this."

* * *

It fit.

Of course it fit; it had always fit; it had been fitted, deliberately, lovingly, by a woman who planned her own death's aftermath down to the last tooth of the last lock. But knowing a thing and feeling it are different countries, and when the key slid into the pinhole behind the rosette and turned—with a resistance, then a release, then a deep internal clunk that Sefa felt through her palms more than heard—the whole deck seemed to tilt.

The false bottom didn't lift. It *unlocked*, a section of the lead lining pivoting inward on hidden hinges, exhaling a breath of air that had been sealed since before Gideon learned to write. It smelled of wax and camphor and something mineral, the cold breath of a room no one had entered in a century.

Inside, packed in oiled silk gone amber with age: documents. Dozens of them. Sealed folios stamped with the Vessany device. A smaller strongbox within the chamber, iron-bound, its lock a twin of the outer one. And on top of everything, folded once, a single sheet of heavy paper bearing lines of Miriam Dunmore's handwriting—larger than the ledger's neat script, hurried, written by lamplight by a woman who knew her time was short.

Gideon reached for it and stopped, hand trembling an inch above the paper.

"Sefa," they said, and their voice was nine years old and fifty at once. "What if it tells me to run? What if she wrote it all down and the last instruction is *hide it, keep it, keep yourself safe*—what if the inheritance is just another way of saying *stay invisible*?"

Sefa looked at the chest, at the drowned nation around them, at the crew sitting shoulder to shoulder in the growing light—Bram solid as a piling, Nils wide-eyed and white-knuckled, herself with her father's tools still scattered around her knees like offerings.

"Then we'll have a hard conversation with a dead woman," she said. "But Gideon—she built a lock that needed a key worn on her child's neck. She didn't hide this from the world. She *addressed* it. You don't address a letter to someone you want hiding in the reeds forever."

Gideon closed their eyes. Breathed. Opened them.

And picked up the page.

The paper crackled like ice on a pond. Gideon unfolded it with both hands, and Sefa watched their eyes move down the lines—once, twice—and watched the color leave their face and something harder flood in behind it, something that looked less like grief than like a blade being drawn.

"Gideon?" Nils whispered. "What does it say?"

Gideon read aloud, voice rough, the words carrying across the quiet deck while the river slid past the drowned city and the monsoon clouds stacked themselves black and patient on the southern horizon:

"*If you are reading this, then you found the chest, which means you found the ledger, which means you chose the river over the life I hoped you'd have. Forgive me. There was no one else. What is sealed below this page is proof of what was done to Vessany—not by history, but by men with charters and signatures, among them the man I trusted most aboard my own expedition. Cassian Vole sold my crew to the Trust for a consultancy and a clean reputation, and he will come for this chest the way he came for me. Do not bargain with him. Do not believe him. And do not hide, my love. Hiding is how they win without firing a shot.*"

Silence. Even the river seemed to hold still.

Then, far downstream, thin across the water, came the sound none of them had wanted to hear: the low, deliberate throb of an engine running without lights, coming upriver toward Greyfall Bend.

Bram was on his feet before the echo faded. "That's a gunboat," he said.
