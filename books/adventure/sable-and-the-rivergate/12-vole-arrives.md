## 12. Vole Arrives

Cassian Vole had spent thirty years learning that rivers forgave nothing, and he had built his entire respectable life on the principle of never asking them to.

He stood on the foredeck of the *Charterhouse*, the Trust's gunboat, as it shouldered up the last mile of flooded country toward Greyfall Bend. The water here was the color of steeped tea and old secrets, brown-gold where the sun struck it, black as lacquer in the shadows of the drowned cedars. Half-submerged rooftops broke the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. A church spire leaned out of the flood at an angle of quiet surrender, its bell long since looted, its weathervane still turning in a wind that smelled of rot and rain coming.

Behind him, two more gunboats followed in echelon—the *Ledger* and the *Providence*, each mounting a swivel gun, each crewed with men Cassian had chosen personally from the Trust's river police and paid privately from an account that appeared on no charter document. He had dressed for the occasion the way he dressed for everything: grey linen suit, pressed despite the humidity; a straw boater he would not wear until the business was concluded; his mother's silver pocket watch, which kept perfect time and had never once been right about anything that mattered.

Four days, the barometers said, until the monsoon made landfall. The river would close like a fist. Whatever happened at Greyfall Bend would happen now, or it would happen never.

Cassian found that he was not afraid. He had expected to be. He had rehearsed fear the way an actor rehearses a role, standing before the mirror in his lodgings in Port Sable, watching his own face for the tremor that would betray him. But all he felt was a strange, suspended clarity, as though the whole of his life had been a river running through fog and had finally, in these last miles, come into open country.

Ahead, around the shoulder of a drowned hill, the Kestrel lay at anchor in the lee of what had once been the archive hall's roofline—a black ridge of tile just breaking the surface, furred with forty years of silt and waterweed. The barge looked exactly as he remembered her, which was to say she looked like Miriam: patched, pragmatic, unkillable, flying no colors because she needed none.

And on her deck, small with distance but unmistakable, stood Gideon Dunmore.

Cassian's chest did something complicated and old.

*You have her hands,* he thought, absurdly. *I never saw her hands still, so I never knew what they looked like when they weren't working. Now I know.*

He lifted his field glasses. Through the lenses, the figure resolved: broad-shouldered, weather-browned, hair tied back carelessly, wearing a canvas coat too heavy for the season. And at the throat—Cassian felt his breath go shallow—a small pendant on a leather cord, catching the light.

The key. Worn openly, like a child wearing a saint's medal. Like someone who did not yet know what they carried.

"Signal the other boats," Cassian said quietly. "Form the triangle. No guns shown unless I show mine first. If a single shot is fired before I give the word, every man aboard forfeits his bonus and answers to me personally."

The mate, a slab-faced ex-soldier named Corrigan, hesitated. "Sir, the brief was—"

"The brief," Cassian said, "was written by men who think rivers are ledgers. I am telling you how this will be done." He handed back the glasses. "Run us alongside. Slow. I want them to hear us coming."

* * *

They heard him coming. Of course they did. He watched the Kestrel's deck come alive with the particular choreography of people who had been expecting this hour: the engineer woman—Sefa, the file said, though the file said little else—disappearing below; the big soldier, Bram, taking up a position at the rail with the studied looseness of a man who wanted very much to be underestimated; the young cartographer hovering near the cabin door with a coil of rope held like a weapon he hadn't decided to become.

And Gideon came to the rail and stood there, arms folded, watching the three gunboats fold around the barge like a hand closing.

Cassian stepped to the bow rail of the Charterhouse and raised both hands, empty, palms out—an old expedition gesture, older than the Trust, older than the war. *No weapons. Talk.*

"Gideon Dunmore," he called across the water. His voice carried the way voices carry over still floodwater, flat and intimate, stripped of echo. "My name is Cassian Vole. I expect you know it. Your mother's journals would have told you, if she wrote kindly."

Silence. The river ticked against the hulls. Somewhere in the drowned trees, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"I'm not here for a fight," Cassian went on. "I could have taken this boat twice over between the tollgate and here. I didn't. Ask yourself why, and then ask me, and then decide whether you'd rather hear the answer from me or from the bottom of Greyfall Bend."

He waited. He had learned waiting from Miriam, who could sit motionless over a dig site for six hours while the light changed and the truth surfaced on its own schedule. It was the only part of her he had ever successfully stolen.

Gideon unfolded their arms. "You've got guns on us and a speech prepared," they called back. "That's not a conversation. That's a purchase order."

Despite himself, Cassian smiled. *Oh, she would have loved that line.* "Then let me make it a fair one. May I come aboard? Alone. Unarmed. You can search me yourself—I insist on it. And if you don't like what I have to say, put me back in my boat and I'll sail home and tell the Trust I found nothing but mud."

"And the gunboats?"

"Will hold position. That's not trust, Mr. Dunmore. That's leverage, and we both know it, and pretending otherwise would insult us both."

A pause. Then Gideon turned and spoke briefly to the others—too low for Cassian to catch—and turned back. "Alone. Unarmed. Hands visible the whole time. And Bram will be standing behind you."

"Naturally," Cassian said. "I'd be disappointed in your mother's training otherwise."

* * *

They searched him thoroughly and without apology. Bram did it, moving with a soldier's economy, finding the pocket watch and the fountain pen and the folded letter of authority under the Trust's seal, and nothing else, because there was nothing else. Cassian stood with his arms out and let them do it, and found the indignity strangely cleansing, the way confession is cleansing even when you lie in it.

Then they sat—of all places, at the Kestrel's little galley table, beneath a swinging lamp, with tea that Sefa made without being asked and without smiling. The cabin smelled of engine oil and river damp and, faintly, of tar. Cassian wrapped his hands around the tin cup they gave him because it gave them something to look at besides each other.

Gideon sat across from him. Up close, the resemblance to Miriam was a physical blow—not in the features, which were broader, rougher, more their father's perhaps, but in the way they held stillness. Miriam had listened with her whole body, and so did this one. It was like sitting across from a well.

"You said you'd tell me why you didn't take the boat," Gideon said.

"I said you should ask. I'll answer." Cassian set down the cup. "Because taking it would end the only conversation that has ever mattered to me, and I have waited five years for this conversation, and I am a patient man, but not infinitely one."

"Five years."

"Since your mother died."

"I know how long it's been since my mother died." Gideon's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it. "What I don't know is what you were to her, beyond what the journals say. Expedition partner. Co-signatory. The man who sold the maps to the Trust after she was dead."

"I didn't sell the maps." The correction came out faster than Cassian intended, sharper, and he caught Gideon noticing it, cataloguing it. He made himself lean back. "I licensed them. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole reason I'm sitting in your galley drinking terrible tea instead of having you dragged to Port Sable in irons."

"Enlighten me."

So he did—or the shape of it, the version shaped for this room. He told it slowly, letting the details accumulate like sediment: how he and Miriam had found the first Vessany markers twenty-two years ago, before the Trust existed in its current form, before anyone understood what the drowned nation had been; how the Trust had funded the final expedition not to recover history but to control it; how Miriam had begun, in the last year, to pull away from him, to keep separate notes, to go silent for weeks at a time; how the expedition's return journey had gone wrong in ways the official record buried; how four people had died in the upper reaches, including Miriam, in a flood season that the records called unprecedented and that Cassian had never believed was weather.

"I have spent five years reading that record," he said, "the way you read a dredge snag. Something down there doesn't match the chart. And the thing that doesn't match is wearing my name, or hers, or both."

Gideon's eyes hadn't moved from his face. "You're saying you didn't kill her."

"I'm saying I don't know who did, and that not knowing has eaten me alive in a way I imagine—" he chose the word carefully, watched it land— "*resembles* what not knowing has done to you."

For the first time, something moved behind Gideon's expression. Not softening. Recognition. The awful kinship of two people who had both been left holding a question instead of a person.

"And the compartment," Gideon said quietly.

Cassian's heart turned over. So they had found it. Or suspected it. Either way, denial was dead. "Yes," he said. "The compartment."

* * *

He laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only currency Gideon Dunmore would accept. The pendant was the key—he could see it at their throat, and he named it without reaching for it, which cost him more than they would ever know. The chamber it opened would contain what Miriam had died keeping: the Vessany treasury records, the true charter correspondence, whatever testimony she had assembled in her final months. Enough, in the wrong hands, to hang half the Trust's board. Enough, in the right hands, to do something else entirely.

"Here is my offer," Cassian said. "Not the Trust's. Mine. We open the compartment together—you and I, the two people alive with the best claim to what's inside. We read what she left. All of it, together, no copies made until we've both seen everything. And then we decide, jointly, what the world gets to know. The fraud, the deaths, the money—all of it goes into daylight if that's what she wanted. But *we* aim the light. Not Halloran, not the board, not some customs officer with a rubber stamp and a career to protect. Us. Her partner and her child. The way it should have been from the beginning."

He heard his own voice doing what it did best—building a room out of words, making it warm, making it inevitable. But underneath the craft, for the first time in years, there was something that wasn't craft. He wanted this. God help him, he wanted to sit with this stranger who wore Miriam's stillness and read her last words together, and grieve properly, at last, in company.

"That's reconciliation," Gideon said.

"It's meant to be."

"It's also a bargain. Truth for contents. You get to vet what comes out." Gideon turned the tin cup a quarter-turn on the table. "My mother spent her last year learning that partners can be bought. Why aren't you bought?"

It was a good question. It deserved better than the answer he had prepared.

"Because I'm old," Cassian said, "and tired, and rich enough that money stopped meaning anything around the time your mother stopped writing to me. Because I have spent five years being consulted by the institution that may have killed the only person I ever—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Because I would rather be ruined for the right reason than decorated for the wrong one. And because if I wanted the contents without you, Mr. Dunmore, I would not be sitting here. I would have taken the boat. Think about that. I keep offering you the same evidence, and you keep declining to weigh it."

Bram shifted by the door. Outside, the light was going the color of pewter; the first far-off mutter of the monsoon rolled up the valley like furniture being dragged across a floor above them.

Gideon stood. Walked to the porthole. Stood there a long moment with their back to him, and Cassian watched the cord at their neck, the pendant resting between their shoulder blades like a small dark heart.

"My mother's journal says," Gideon said at last, without turning, "that the last time she trusted her expedition partner, four people went into the upper river and none of them came back. She wrote it the night before she died. She underlined it."

The words went into Cassian like cold water into a boot. He had not known she had written that. He had not known she had thought it. Five years of reconstructing her from fragments, and here was a fragment he had never possessed, and it was aimed at him.

"She wasn't always right," he managed.

"No," Gideon said, turning. Their face in the lamplight was Miriam's face aged twenty years and wrung through grief. "But she was right about people. That was her whole gift. She could read a stranger's bones through their boots." They crossed back to the table and sat, and folded their hands, and looked at him with a patience that was worse than anger. "So here's my counter-offer, Mr. Vole. We open the compartment. Together, like you said. You see everything I see. But the decision about daylight isn't joint, and it isn't yours. It was never yours. She named a custodian in that ledger, and it wasn't you."

Outside, thunder again, closer. Rain began to stipple the river, fine as shot, and through the porthole Cassian watched the three gunboats' riding lights bloom halos in the wet air, and understood, with the clarity of a man watching the water rise over a road he means to travel, that whatever happened next would happen in the next few minutes—

—and that was when the lookout's shout came down from the Charterhouse's mast, thin and cracked across the rain: a fourth boat, unlit, sliding out of the drowned streets below Greyfall Bend, flying no colors at all.
