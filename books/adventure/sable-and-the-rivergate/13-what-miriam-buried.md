## 13. What Miriam Buried

The rain had not yet come, but the river knew it was coming. All morning the water at Greyfall Bend had been behaving strangely—swelling in slow pulses against the drowned walls, breathing like something asleep that dreamed of waking. The air hung thick and green-smelling, and the light came down through the canopy in coins of tarnished gold.

Gideon sat on the Kestrel's deck with the chest open before them and the pendant warm against their sternum, and felt the way a diver feels at the mouth of a wreck: that the next breath changes everything.

Sefa had done her work beautifully. The false bottom she'd found with a sounding hammer and a jeweler's patience now lay lifted away on its hidden brass hinges, set aside on an oilcloth like a piece of surgery. Beneath it, the chamber breathed out its century.

Not treasure. Not coin. Not the glittering hoard the Trust's charter had been written to claim.

Paper.

Reams of it, wrapped in waxed linen gone amber with age, bound with cord that crumbled at a touch. And on top, sealed under a disc of lead stamped with the Vessany crest—the same seal as the chest, the same seal from the sketch Gideon had once traced with a child's finger in their mother's study—a single envelope bearing words in Miriam Dunmore's hand:

*For whoever the river gives this back to.*

Bram stood at the rail with his arms crossed, watching the bend downstream where the channel narrowed toward open water. "We should move first," he said, for the fourth time since dawn. "Read it on the river. Vole's boats will be behind us by nightfall if they're not already."

"Then we'll have read it by then." Gideon's voice came out flatter than they intended.

Nils crouched at the edge of the oilcloth, eyes moving over the documents with a cartographer's hunger. "This is a state archive," he murmured. "Look at the foliation. Registry numbers. This is official paper, Gideon. This is *government*." He said the word the way other people said *wreck* or *reef*—with reverence and a little fear.

"It's bait," Bram said. "It's whatever Vole wants it to be, sitting in a box he's chased across half a country."

"It's my mother's handwriting," Gideon said, and no one argued after that.

Sefa put a hand briefly on Gideon's shoulder—she was not a woman who spent touches freely—and went below to keep the engine warm. Nils retreated to the chart table but did not pretend to work; Gideon could feel his eyes. Even Bram stayed, though he turned his back to the deck and watched the water instead, standing guard against a threat that hadn't yet shown its face.

Gideon broke the lead seal.

* * *

The first document was a census.

That was all it appeared to be at first: a census of the Vessany river provinces, dated ninety-eight years past, in three columns—names, holdings, assessed value. The handwriting was clerical, patient, thorough. Gideon read down the columns of names that no longer existed anywhere on any map: Ostrava-hold. The Weir families of Lower Sennet. The lampwrights' guild of Greyfall itself, population four hundred and eleven.

The third column was what stopped them.

Every holding had been assessed. Every field, every weir, every lampworks, every burial ground—assessed and totaled, and the total carried forward to a second page, and a third, until the wealth of an entire nation had been reduced to a single figure at the bottom of a single sheet, underlined twice.

And beneath it, in different ink, a marginal note in a hand Gideon did not know:

*Crown cannot annex what does not exist. Recommend dissolution precedes acquisition. Treasury to pass to Trust administration pending "custodianship."*

Gideon read it three times. The words didn't change. The river kept breathing against the hull.

"They didn't lose a war," Nils said quietly. He had drifted over despite himself, drawn the way he was always drawn to lines on paper. "There was no war. There was a *filing error*. They wrote Vessany off the maps so they could write it onto a ledger."

"The extinction was administrative," Gideon said. The taste of the words was foul. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out, once, and went silent. "A hundred years ago some clerk decided a nation was a rounding problem."

They unwrapped the next bundle. Treaties of accession, unsigned. Petitions from Vessany's last magistrates, stamped RECEIVED and never answered. A shipping manifest for the treasury itself—forty-one chests of bullion and bond-paper, consigned downriver to Greyfall Bend—and then, chillingly, a duplicate of that manifest with the destination struck through and replaced: *Colonial Salvage Trust, permanent custody.*

The treasury had never been lost. It had been *received*. It was sitting, right now, in a vault in Rivergate, earning interest for the Crown, while schoolchildren learned that Vessany had been a legend, a fable, a name sailors gave to fog.

Gideon's hands were shaking. They noticed it distantly, the way you notice current tugging at your fins—not frightening, just true.

* * *

The testimony was the last thing in the chamber, folded into a leather folio that Gideon knew instantly, horribly well. Their mother had carried it on every expedition. Gideon had seen it sticking out of her pack a hundred times, had once used it as a pillow on a train through the hill country while Miriam sat up all night writing by lamplight.

Inside, pages in Miriam's small, fierce, slanted script. Dated across the final year of her life.

Gideon began to read aloud without deciding to—perhaps because reading it silently made it too easy to stop, and some part of them understood that stopping would be surrender.

*"Third of the Dry Season. Found the archive hall today. Cassian wept when we broke the silt seal—I have never seen him weep, not even for the dead on the Kaverra run. I thought it was joy. I am no longer sure it was. He asked me twice tonight how much the bond-paper alone would fetch. Twice is a habit forming."*

*"Eleventh. The manifests are real. Everything the histories call myth is in this room, catalogued by people who expected to be remembered. I have started making copies. Cassian says copies are paranoia. He has started asking about my copies."*

*"Twentieth. Rell and Osei want to go home. Cassian has convinced them the monsoon makes descent impossible and we should winter at Greyfall. He is lying. I checked the barometers myself. He wants us here because here we can be explained."*

Gideon's voice caught on *explained*. The word sat in the cabin air like a body in still water.

*"Second of the Rains. Osei is dead. The trust men say a footing slip on the weir stairs. Osei had climbed those stairs forty times. Cassian did not come to the burial. He sent wages."*

*"Rell left yesterday, or tried to. Her boat burned at the mooring with everything she owned aboard. She is walking out overland. I have given her my spare papers and told her to forget my name. If you are reading this, Rell, I hope the hills were kind."*

*"I understand now what the expedition was. I was hired to find a grave and I have found a murder that hasn't finished happening. The Trust manufactured a nation's death to steal its treasury, and everyone who could testify is being subtracted one at a time, each subtraction called an accident. I am the last subtraction. I know this the way you know weather."*

*"So I have buried the proof where only a salvager would look—in plain sight, in the water, under a seal they think is superstition. Let the river hold it. Rivers are more honest than archives."*

*"To my child, if it is my child reading this: I did not leave you. I left you *out of it*, which was the only protection I had left to give. I know how that must have looked from the dock. I know five years is a long time to measure silence in. Forgive me the miles. They were all I had."*

Gideon stopped.

The rain had begun—not the monsoon, not yet, but a fat preliminary drizzle ticking on the deck planks—and the light had gone the color of pewter. Nils had both hands pressed flat on the chart table as though steadying himself against weather. From below, the engine idled, patient as a heartbeat.

Gideon read the last page.

*"Final entry. Cassian has offered me a share and a story: we sell the location to the Trust, split the fee, retire rich and silent. He framed it as mercy. He said, 'Miriam, nobody wins this, the only choice is who loses alone.' He is right that nobody wins. He is wrong that silence is survival. Silence is how they won the first time."*

*"If I die at Greyfall, it was Cassian Vole. Write it down. Make it expensive to kill for. That is the only monument I need."*

*—M. Dunmore, custodian, last of the Vessany registry.*

* * *

For a long moment there was nothing in the world but rain-tick and engine-idle and the sound of Gideon's own blood.

They had imagined this moment for five years in a hundred shapes—grief as a wreck you could dive, work, salvage, close. They had thought they wanted answers. They had not understood that answers are cargo, and cargo has weight, and some weight goes straight through the hull of you and keeps going.

Fury came first, and it was enormous and clean, a flood-stage fury that had nothing petty in it. Not rage at Cassian alone—at the whole apparatus of it. The clerk with his marginal note. The stamp that said RECEIVED. The charter, the tollgates, the injunctions, the polite men in good coats who had decided that a nation was a ledger entry and a woman was a line item and a child waiting at a dock was an acceptable cost of doing business. Gideon had spent five years running from the Dunmore name, and the whole time the name had been running from *this*—had died carrying this, had sunk it in the river like a message in a bottle thrown at the future.

The bottle had reached shore. It had reached *them*.

Clarity came after, quieter, colder, better. The fury said *burn it all*. The clarity said something more useful: *this is evidence. Evidence needs witnesses. Witnesses need to live.* Miriam had written it herself: make it expensive to kill for. You didn't make murder expensive by dying gloriously in a flooded ruin with the truth in your fist. You made it expensive by standing somewhere public, somewhere lit, somewhere with press and officers and dockworkers and law, and opening the box where everyone could see.

The Rivergate customs house. The plan they'd already been running. It wasn't recklessness anymore. It was the only shape justice came in.

"Gideon." Nils's voice, careful. "You've been staring at that page for two minutes."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, but you're *decided*, which is better." Nils almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. "What do we do?"

Gideon closed the folio. Wrapped the bundles back in their waxed linen with hands that had stopped shaking, or had shaken so long they'd passed through trembling into steadiness on the far side. Restacked the chamber. Left the lid open—there was no point pretending anymore, and Sefa would want her false bottom back intact.

"We do exactly what we said," Gideon said. "We run for Rivergate. We don't hide this, we don't sell this, we don't trade it. We open it where the whole river can watch." They looked up, and found Bram had turned around at some point and was watching them with an expression Gideon had never seen on him—something older than guilt, something like absolution being offered in advance. "My mother named him, Bram. In ink. Cassian Vole killed her crew and signed my mother's death warrant with a wage packet, and he's coming up this river thinking he's about to buy the last copy of his crime."

"And?" Bram said.

"And he's wrong about what's in the box." Gideon stood, knees stiff, and the pendant swung forward on its cord and hung gleaming in the grey light—the key that had opened nothing until today, that had been a trinket, an orphan's keepsake, and was now the physical key to a nation's stolen memory. "He thinks he's buying treasure. What he's actually sailing toward is a mirror."

Downstream, around the shoulder of the drowned city, a sound arrived that none of them needed instruments to identify: engines. More than one. Deep-throated, government-rough, running without lights in broad grey day because they no longer cared who saw them coming.

Bram was at the rail before the echo faded, spyglass up. "Three boats," he said. "Gun mounts under canvas. Flying no colors." A pause, and then, dry as driftwood: "Vole's not even waiting for dark."

Nils was already pulling charts, fingers flying. "Blind channel off the north weir—if it's still deep enough—"

Sefa came up the companionway wiping her hands, took one look at the water, one at their faces, and said, "How long do I have?"

"However long Bram buys us," Gideon said, and met the old soldier's eyes, and saw him understand what was being asked, and saw him—terribly, wonderfully—begin to nod.

The rain thickened. The first gunboat rounded the bend.
