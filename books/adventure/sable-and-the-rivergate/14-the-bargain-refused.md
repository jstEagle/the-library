## 14. The Bargain Refused

Nils had spent his whole life learning that maps lied.

Not the good ones—never the ones drawn in ink by hands that loved the land—but the official ones, the printed ones, the ones issued by ministries and trusts and men in clean coats who had never stood ankle-deep in the mud they claimed to own. Those maps were arguments. Those maps were weapons dressed as paper. Nils had run away from a cartography office in Vell with three stolen atlases sewn into his coat lining because he could no longer bear to draw lines for people who used them to erase things.

So when Cassian Vole stepped aboard the *Kestrel* under a white flag of parley and began speaking of bargains, Nils did not listen to the words. He watched the man's boots. He watched where he stood on the deck, which way his weight leaned, how his eyes flicked—not to Gideon, not to the chest, but to the gunwale, the mooring line, the channel mouth behind them. Men's bodies told the truth their mouths were hired to bury.

And Cassian Vole's body was telling Nils that there were two gunboats in the mist beyond Greyfall Bend, and that this conversation had never been meant to end in agreement.

* * *

The morning had come up gray and breathless, the flooded ruins of Vessany rising out of the water like the ribs of something that had drowned holding its last secret between its teeth. Mist lay on the submerged archive hall so thickly that the broken bell tower seemed to float free of the earth, a stone finger pointing at nothing. The *Kestrel* sat at anchor in the lee of what had once been a customs quay, her patched hull groaning softly against the old stone, and the chest—the chest with its double bottom and its sealed chamber waiting for the pendant at Gideon's throat—sat lashed amidships under oilcloth like a sleeping animal they were all afraid to wake.

Cassian Vole arrived in a longboat flying no colors at all, which was itself a kind of color. He wore a good coat gone soft at the elbows, the coat of a man who had been respectable long enough to forget he'd ever been anything else, and his hair was silver at the temples in a way that looked arranged rather than earned. He came up the ladder uninvited and unhurried, palms open, smiling at Bram as though they were old comrades, and perhaps they were; Bram's face went the color of river clay and stayed that way.

"Miss Dunmore," Vole said. "Or Mister. Forgive me—I knew your mother when titles still mattered to neither of us."

Gideon stood at the rail with the pendant hidden under their shirt and their hands very still at their sides. "You knew my mother," Gideon said. It wasn't a question. It was a blade laid flat on a table.

"I did." Vole's smile deepened by one careful degree. "That is precisely why I'm here."

Nils stood back by the wheelhouse with Sefa, ostensibly minding the engine controls, actually doing what he always did: reading the water. The Bend was a maze even in dry season—a drowned town whose streets still ran beneath the flood like veins of darker water, whose rooftops broke the surface in a scatter of green-slicked islands, whose channels braided and re-braided through collapsed quarters in patterns no Trust chart had ever bothered to correct because no Trust vessel had ever needed to come here. But Nils had spent four days and nights with Miriam Dunmore's journal open on his knees, copying her route-marks onto his own charts in pencil, and Miriam Dunmore had mapped this place the way a lover maps a body: thoroughly, tenderly, and with an eye for everything the official surveys missed.

Including, Nils hoped, the blind channel.

He hoped it the way you hope about a rope bridge—knowing your whole weight is about to be committed to it.

* * *

The bargain went exactly the way Nils had known it would, which is to say beautifully, and then terribly.

Vole laid it out on the aft deck with the chest between them like a third negotiator. He spoke of partnership. He spoke of legacy. He said the word *Miriam* four times, each time a half-second slower than the last, letting it fall like a coin into a well so they could all listen for the splash. He offered salvage rights, legal absolution, a share of whatever the second compartment held—and, most poisonous of all, he offered the truth.

"Your mother died because she hesitated," Vole said, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost grieving. "I was there. I have carried it eleven years. I can give you every detail of her last day, Miss Dunmore—every detail—if you give me what she would have given me, if she'd had the sense to trust her oldest friend."

Nils saw it land. He saw it land in Gideon's shoulders, in the sudden stillness of their jaw, in the way their hand rose halfway toward the pendant and stopped. Five years of silence, five years of measuring life in river miles instead of years, and here was a man offering to sell them back the last hours of the person they'd loved worst and best.

For one full breath, Nils believed Gideon might take the deal.

Then Gideon laughed. It was not a kind sound. It was short and cracked and it came out of them like something breaking a fever.

"You hesitated," Gideon repeated. "She hesitated. And eleven years later you're standing on my deck telling me hesitation is what killed her—as if that's supposed to make me trust the man who didn't." They shook their head slowly. "My mother wrote a testimony, Mr. Vole. I've read it. She named you."

Something moved behind Vole's eyes—quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under ice. When he spoke again the warmth was gone from his voice, folded away as neatly as a map being put back in its case. "Then you understand your position."

"I understand yours," Gideon said. "It's worse."

"No," Vole said quietly. "It isn't. Because mine has gunboats in it."

* * *

The first shot came from the eastern channel, across the drowned market quarter, and it hit the *Kestrel*'s rail six inches from Bram's hand.

After that the world became noise. Nils had imagined this moment—during the tollgate run, during the dive at the archive hall, during every night since the claim notice—imagined it the way you imagine drowning, uselessly and in vivid detail. But imagination had not included the smell: cordite and wet stone and the green rot of the flooded town churned up by the wake of the longboat as Vole's crew scrambled back down the ladder under covering fire from the mist. Imagination had not included Sefa swearing steadily and creatively in three languages while slapping the engine starter, or Bram hauling the oilcloth off the chest and throwing himself over it with his whole scarred body like a man shielding a child, or Gideon—Gideon moving without hesitation now, all that grief converted instantly to motion, cutting the anchor line with two strokes of a knife.

"Nils!" Gideon shouted across the deck. "Get us out!"

Every eye on the boat turned to him. That was the terrible thing, the thing that made his hands shake on the wheel: they were all looking at him. The runaway cartographer. The boy who'd fled Vell with stolen atlases because he couldn't stand to watch lines on paper become graves. And now the lines were going to decide whether any of them saw evening.

"Where?" Sefa yelled, as the engine caught with a cough and a roar.

Nils closed his eyes for half a second. Half a second was all he could afford. Behind his lids he saw the journal page, Miriam's small precise hand, the little symbols she'd used instead of words: a triangle for a roofbreak, a dotted line for a channel too shallow for gunboats, and beside one particular dotted line, running north off the main bend behind the old granary island, a single annotation he had copied out three times because he hadn't believed it the first two:

*Blind channel. No outlet on Trust charts. I checked twice. It goes through.*

"It goes through," Nils said aloud, to no one, to everyone, and hauled the wheel hard to starboard.

* * *

The gunboats fired again as the *Kestrel* swung, and Nils felt the near miss more than heard it—a flat slap of displaced air along the deckhouse, a splinter stinging his cheek. Ahead the mist hung over the drowned market quarter in a low white ceiling, and somewhere inside it the rooftops waited like teeth.

"Through the market?" Bram's voice cracked upward. "Those channels are—" He stopped. He'd seen the journal too. He knew what Miriam's marks meant: *too shallow for gunboats.* Which meant shallow enough to ground them, if Nils was wrong by a boat-length anywhere.

"If we stay in the Bend we're target practice," Nils said. His voice sounded far away to himself, thin over the engine roar. "Vole's charts end at the survey line. Everything past the granary island is blank on his paper. Blank water is the only cover we have left."

"That's not cover, that's a graveyard with extra steps—"

"Bram." Gideon, at the bow, chest lashed down, knife still in hand, looking back at Nils with an expression Nils would carry for the rest of his life. Not doubt. The opposite of doubt. "Fly it."

So Nils flew it.

He took the *Kestrel* into the drowned market at just under full steam, threading the gap between a submerged chapel wall and the black tip of a bell tower, trusting Miriam's triangle-marks the way he'd never trusted anything drawn by anyone else. The water shoaled visibly—green to brown to the color of steeped tea—and the hull began to sing that low grinding note of silt beneath the keel, and Sefa screamed something about the impeller and Nils screamed back that the impeller could file a complaint later, and then the channel bent hard to port around a rooftop garden gone entirely to mangrove, and the gunfire from astern went suddenly, wonderfully, confused.

Because Vole's gunners could no longer see them. The mangroves closed overhead like a green tunnel, dripping, breathing, alive, and the *Kestrel* plunged into the blind channel with the whole drowned city listening.

* * *

It lasted nine minutes. Nils counted. Nine minutes of the most concentrated terror of his life, delivered in boat-lengths.

Twice the channel narrowed until the mangrove branches scraped both rails at once, and once—once—they passed directly over a street, and Nils knew it was a street because a lamp post rose out of the tea-colored water beside the hull, its glass long gone, its iron arm still crooked in the gesture of lighting the way home for people a century dead. A door frame slid past below them, upright. Someone had painted it blue once. Nils pressed his palm flat against the wheelhouse glass as they crossed above that threshold, some instinct older than cartography making him silent, respectful, a guest.

Miriam had come through here. He understood that suddenly, completely. She hadn't found this channel on a survey. She'd found it the way he was finding it now—with her stomach, with her fear, with the same desperate arithmetic of *they will not follow where the paper ends.* Somewhere in these waters, eleven years ago, she had stood at a wheel or a tiller or the prow of a smaller boat, feeling exactly this. Nils had spent four days deciphering her handwriting. Now, in the dripping green dark, he felt he was finally reading it.

"Depth!" Sefa called.

"Two fathoms. Trust me."

"I don't trust you, I trust the dead woman! Two fathoms is fine!"

Ahead, impossibly, light. The mangrove tunnel opened like a held breath released, and the *Kestrel* slid out into a long narrow lagoon walled by flooded forest, still as a held note, the mist lying on it in unbroken sheets. No gunboats. No sound but their own engine, which Sefa throttled down to a mutter, and then to a whisper, and then to silence.

They drifted. The ripples of their arrival traveled out ahead of them, touched the far wall of trees, and came back changed, and then the lagoon was glass again.

Bram started laughing first—a shaky, disbelieving sound, both hands still flat on the chest as if it might bolt. Then Sefa, sliding down the engine housing to sit on the deck plates, wiping her face with a rag that only spread the grease around. Then Gideon, leaning on the rail, head bowed, shoulders shaking in a way that might have been laughter or might have been the other thing, probably both.

Nils stayed at the wheel. His hands would not stop trembling, so he gripped harder, and stared at the blank water ahead, and thought about blank spaces. All those years in the Vell office, drawing coastlines for men who wanted the world finished, accounted for, owned edge to edge. He had believed the blanks were failures of knowledge. He had run away rather than help fill them in.

But this—this green silence, this impossible channel, this shaking family of his scattered across a deck that should by all rights be driftwood and blood—this had come out of a blank. Out of a dead woman's stubborn little annotation: *I checked twice. It goes through.*

The map hadn't saved them. The mapper had. The person, not the paper.

"Nobody move," Sefa said finally, into the quiet. "Nobody cheer. Sound carries on water like this."

As if summoned by the words, a shot cracked across the lagoon—from ahead, not behind—and struck the water thirty yards off the bow with a flat smack, throwing up a column of spray that stood in the windless air like a small white tree.

Everyone froze. Out of the mist ahead, resolving slowly, came the shape of a third gunboat—one Vole had posted at the far end of the channel, patient as a spider, guarding the one exit Miriam's map had promised them.

Nils looked at the gunboat. Then at his charts. Then, involuntarily, at the sky—at the bruise-colored cloud bank stacking up over the western hills like a debt coming due, the first true monsoon front he'd seen all season, already swelling, already rolling their way.

"We're through the walls," he said quietly, mostly to himself. "But the weather's not done with us. And neither is he."
