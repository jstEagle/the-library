## 15. Monsoon Warning

Sefa had a rule about barometers: never trust one that lies to you gently.

The old aneroid bolted above the Kestrel's engine housing had been lying gently for two days now—a slow, apologetic sag in the needle, the kind of drift you could blame on humidity, on altitude, on a bad night's sleep. Sefa had blamed it on all three. She'd tapped the glass twice with one knuckle, the way her grandmother used to tap the ribs of a stubborn mule, and the needle had shivered and settled back down like it was embarrassed to be caught telling the truth.

By dawn of the third day it stopped being gentle.

She came up from the engine bay at first light with grease to her elbows and found the needle pinned hard against the left stop, quivering there like something trapped. 28.1 inches and falling. She stood very still in the grey half-light of the wheelhouse, listening. The river was wrong. Not rough—wrong. The current had gone slack and oily, the water moving without conviction, as if it were holding its breath. The birds had stopped arguing over the shallows. Even the insects along the bank had gone quiet, and Sefa could not remember the last time she had heard nothing from the reeds at Greyfall Bend.

"Everyone up," she said, and her voice carried further than she meant it to.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse because the wheelhouse was where bad news lived aboard the Kestrel. Gideon arrived first, hair still wet from washing, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth and slung across their chest like a bandolier—they hadn't let it out of arm's reach since Greyfall Bend, since the second compartment, since the night the paper had turned Miriam Dunmore's death into a weapon with edges. Bram filled the doorway behind them, arms crossed, saying nothing, which for Bram was a great deal of saying. Nils perched on the chart table with their knees drawn up, the way they always did when they wanted to be small enough to disappear into a conversation.

Sefa laid two fingers on the barometer's glass.

"It's falling through the floor," she said. "Twenty-eight point one and dropping faster than I've ever seen outside of a storm itself. That's not a squall coming. That's the monsoon making its approach, and the whole coast is the low-pressure trough ahead of landfall."

"How long?" Gideon asked. No hesitation, no softening. They had learned, somewhere between the tollgate and the flooded archive hall, to ask the next question instead of the comfortable one.

Sefa did the arithmetic she'd already done three times, hoping the third time would be kinder than the first two. It wasn't.

"The pilots' almanac says the leading edge comes ashore near Cape Vessany—old Vessany, whatever they call it now—and rides the river valley inland. When it hits, we get forty-eight hours of wind like the sky is trying to peel the water off the earth, then the real rain. The river rises six, eight feet in a day. Current triples. Nothing moves upstream after that, and precious little moves down." She looked around the wheelhouse, at each of them in turn, because this part mattered most. "Landfall in four days. Maybe five if the sea is merciful, and the sea has never once been merciful to me."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the mist sat on the water like a lid.

"So we run downriver," Gideon said. "Now. Today."

"To the Rivergate," Nils said quietly. "Customs house. Before the waterway shuts." They had the route already half-drawn; Sefa could see it in the way their fingers kept twitching toward the charts, a cartographer's hands unable to leave a map alone even when the map was bad news. "Four days is enough if nothing breaks. If nothing hunts us. If the channels hold."

"If," said Bram.

"Yes," said Nils. "If."

* * *

Sefa spent the morning doing what she always did before a hard run: walking the Kestrel stem to stern with a rag in one hand and a mallet in the other, listening to the barge talk.

Every boat talked. The Kestrel talked more than most, being older than any of them and held together by welds Sefa had made herself, by scarred plate and stubbornness. She put her palm flat against the hull plating below the waterline and felt for the hum of stress. She opened every seacock and closed it again. She checked the bilge pumps twice, then a third time, and greased the shaft bearings until her fingers went black. The port engine had developed a tick she didn't like—not a knock yet, just a tick, a small dry voice in the machinery saying *I am tired*. She pulled the injector, cleaned it, reseated it, and the tick faded to almost nothing. Almost.

"You're going to get us downriver," she told the engine, "and then I'm going to tear you apart and find out what's wrong with you. Don't make me do it in the rain."

The engine ticked. Engines don't promise anything. That was why she liked them better than people.

Nils found her in the engine bay an hour later, folding themselves down onto an overturned crate with a chart spread across their knees, weighted at the corners with nuts from her own parts bins. Sefa wiped her hands and looked. The river ran down the page in blue ink, Greyfall Bend at the top, the Rivergate a hundred and forty miles below, and Nils had annotated it in their small, precise hand: channel depths, cutoffs, the blind slough where they'd hidden from Cassian Vole's gunboats, the tollgate where Bram had given himself up and bought them a day with his freedom.

"Bram's still in their holding pen at Marrow Lock," Nils said. "Halloran hasn't transferred him. That's either good or very bad."

"Halloran's the one who started pulling threads," Sefa said. "Bram told us the man asked him questions the Trust wouldn't want asked. Questions about cargo manifests from twenty years ago." She shook her head. "A man like that doesn't transfer a prisoner quickly. A man like that keeps him close and keeps him talking."

"Then we collect him on the way down?"

Gideon's voice from the engine-bay hatch. They stood there with the rain-grey light behind them, and Sefa saw how tired they looked—not the tiredness of sleeplessness but of carrying something heavy for too long, which no amount of rest fixes.

"We collect him on the way down," Gideon repeated. "He gave himself up so we could move. We don't leave him in a Trust cage while the sky comes apart."

"He knew the risk," Sefa said, and immediately hated how it sounded, because it was true and also beside the point. "That's not—I'm not saying leave him. I'm saying the math is ugly. Detour to Marrow Lock costs us six hours, maybe eight against that current. Eight hours is the difference between docking at the Rivergate in sunlight and docking in the teeth of the leading edge."

"Then we'll dock in the teeth of the leading edge," Gideon said.

Sefa looked at them—at this person who had spent years measuring life in river miles precisely so they'd never have to measure it in obligations, who had built loneliness into a craft and worn competence like armor—and she thought: *the chest changed them. Or maybe it just took the lid off.* She'd seen double-bottomed chests before. She'd never seen a double-bottomed person open themselves in front of a customs house full of guns and press, and she suspected none of them had, and that tomorrow's hundred and forty miles were going to be rowed by people who had already decided how the story ended.

"All right," she said. "Marrow Lock. But we take the cutoff past the drowned orchard, not the main channel—it's narrower but it saves the bend, and the bend will be murder once the wind swings south. Nils, can your map carry us through there in low visibility?"

Nils was already redrawing. "It carried us in. It'll carry us out."

* * *

They cast off at noon under a sky the color of wet slate.

Sefa took the wheel herself for the first watch, because she trusted no one else's hands on the levers when the air felt like this. The Kestrel slid away from the drowned ruins of Greyfall Bend—the archive hall's roofline still breaking the surface offshore, black ribs of a building that had swallowed a nation's memory—and pointed her blunt bow downriver, toward home, toward the customs house, toward whatever waited there.

The river had changed character overnight. It moved thick and slow, brown-green, carrying more debris than it should have: branches, whole trees torn loose upstream, a fence post with wire still strung between two lengths of it, turning slowly like something drowning. The current was confused, eddying where it should have run straight, and Sefa read it the way she read engines—by feel, by the tremor in the wheel spokes, by the way the wake folded off the stern.

Behind them, the southern horizon had begun to build.

She watched it whenever the channel allowed. A wall of cloud, blue-black at its base, stacked up in tiers to a height that made her neck ache, and beneath it a smudge of rain so wide it looked like the coastline itself was dissolving. Lightning flickered inside it without sound, silent and constant, like a lamp behind a curtain. Every few minutes the wind gusted out ahead of the storm—warm, dead-smelling, tasting faintly of salt and rot—and the reeds along the banks bowed all together, thousands of them, like a congregation.

"First time I've seen one from the water," Nils said, coming to stand beside her. They had to raise their voice over the engines. "Monsoons. We always got them overland, in the hills. You'd see them coming for days across the plateau."

"And?"

"And we hid." Nils smiled, thin and crooked. "You can hide from weather on land. There's always a cave, a culvert, a cellar. Out here—" they gestured at the enormous flat world of water and sky— "there's nowhere to be except in it."

"There's the Rivergate," Sefa said. "Stone walls. Customs officers. Press, if Gideon's messages reached anyone."

"That's not shelter, that's a stage."

"Same thing, the way Gideon plans it."

Nils laughed despite themselves, and the laugh blew away downwind, and for a while they just watched the storm build.

* * *

The barometer fell another tenth by mid-afternoon, and Sefa stopped checking it hourly and started checking it obsessively, because the rate of fall told her things the needle's position couldn't. A slow fall was weather. A fast fall was danger. A fall like this—twenty-hundredths in four hours, the needle sliding like something greased—was the atmosphere itself collapsing ahead of the storm's circulation, the whole sky leaning inland.

She did the math again at dusk, chalk on the soot-blackened bulkhead beside the engine gauges, because numbers written in chalk felt negotiable in a way numbers in her head didn't.

Hundred and forty miles to the Rivergate. Hundred and thirty-one after the afternoon's run. Six hours lost to the Marrow Lock detour, assuming Bram walked out of that holding pen under his own power and Halloran's conscience held long enough to look the other way—which was a wager on a man none of them fully trusted, placed by a crew that had no better option. Against them: a current that would strengthen hour by hour as the storm's leading edge backed water up the river mouth, wind that would swing south-southeast and then hit them broadside on the lower reaches where the channel widened and there was no lee at all.

For them: the Kestrel, patched and profane and unwilling. Four days. Maybe five.

"Maybe five isn't a plan," she said aloud to the engine room, to the tick in the port engine, to the chalk numbers. "But it's what we've got."

* * *

Night fell early under the storm-light, a bruised purple dark that wasn't really night at all. They ran on through it, one lamp dimmed to a coal at the masthead, Sefa and Gideon splitting the wheel in four-hour watches while Nils navigated by memory and Bram's absence sat in the crew space like a fifth chair nobody moved.

It was during Gideon's midnight watch that the wind arrived.

Not the storm—just its outrider, the first true gusts rolling ahead of the circulation, hitting the river in long pulses that flattened the water and then released it. The Kestrel yawed and steadied, yawed and steadied. Rain came in brief violent flurries, warm as bathwater, each one lasting ninety seconds and dumping what felt like a season, and between the flurries the air steamed.

Sefa climbed to the wheelhouse at the change of watch and found Gideon standing very straight at the wheel, soaked through, eyes fixed south.

"It's beautiful," Gideon said, which was not what Sefa expected.

"It's going to try to kill us."

"I know." Gideon's knuckles were white on the spokes. "My mother chased storms like this. In the journal—Nils showed me. She timed her ascent of the Vessany gorge to the monsoon's first landfall because the floodwater opened passages that were dry stone the rest of the year. She used the thing that could kill her as a tool." A breath. "I used to think that was recklessness. Now I think it was the only honest way to live—to stop waiting for the weather to be kind."

Sefa leaned against the doorframe and studied them: the oilcloth-wrapped ledger, the pendant at their throat that had opened a compartment built to swallow a nation's treasury, the face of someone who had decided, finally, whose child they were.

"Four days," she said. "Marrow Lock at dawn. Rivergate inside the window. And then you stand in front of the whole colonial apparatus and read your dead mother's testimony into the record while the roof tries to come off."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It is insane. It's also the only heading that doesn't end with us sinking quietly somewhere with the truth in our pockets." Sefa pushed off the doorframe and rolled her shoulders, feeling every one of her fifty-one years and grateful for each of them, because they had taught her this: fear is fuel if you feed it to the right machine. "Get some sleep. I'll take her till the lock."

Gideon hesitated at the ladder down, silhouetted against the wheelhouse lamps, and looked back at the southern horizon where the lightning pulsed without end.

"Sefa. If we miss the window—if the river shuts before we reach the gate—"

"Then the evidence drowns with us," Sefa said, "and Vole wins, and the Trust writes the history books. So we won't miss the window." She took the wheel from Gideon's hands, warm from their grip. "Go on. Sleep. I'll wake you when it gets interesting."

Gideon went. The Kestrel ran on down the swelling river, her patched hull shouldering through the confused water, and behind her the wall of cloud climbed higher and higher into the dark, patient as a debt, four days from landfall and closing.

In the engine bay below, the port engine ticked.

Sefa listened to it, and made it a promise anyway.
