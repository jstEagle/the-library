## 16. Bram's Debt

The rain had not yet come, but the air already tasted of it — that green, metallic tang that hung over the Sable in the last days before the monsoon, as if the whole river were holding its breath. Bram Okonfor-Vey stood at the stern rail of the *Kestrel* and watched the water unspool behind them, brown and heavy with silt, carrying the smell of drowned forests and old iron. Somewhere upriver, behind them, Cassian Vole's gunboats were hunting. Somewhere ahead, the Rivergate waited, four days of hard steaming through water that would soon turn murderous.

And somewhere inside Bram, in the place he kept locked the way he'd once kept locked the ammunition stores of a fort he no longer named, there was a debt that had been gathering interest for eleven years.

He had told them the story in pieces, never whole. To Sefa, over the engine hatch, he'd admitted he'd been a soldier. To Nils, on the long nights of charting, that he'd been a bad one. To Gideon, only once, in the dark after the tollgate run, he'd said: *I sold a map once. People died because of where I drew the line.* He had not said whose map. He had not said whose people. He had not said that the man who bought it had been Cassian Vole, younger then, hungrier, with a smile like a knife being shown its sheath.

He was going to say it now. Or he was going to do something better than saying it.

He found Gideon in the wheelhouse, one hand on the wheel, the other holding a chart flat against the frame while the wind tried to take it. Gideon had salt in their hair from the spray and a smudge of engine grease on their jaw they didn't know about, and they stood at the wheel the way Bram imagined their mother had stood over her maps — like a person holding something that mattered and refusing to let the world know it frightened them.

"We need to talk about the patrol station at Weir Nine," Bram said.

Gideon didn't look up. "We go wide of it. Nils found a channel through the reed beds — narrow, but the *Kestrel* draws light enough."

"It'll cost you half a day. You don't have half a day. Sefa says the barometer's falling faster than she likes, and Sefa doesn't dislike things lightly." Bram set his hands on the chart, pinning it, and made himself look at the route Nils had inked — the long cautious arc around the checkpoint, the little penciled note in Nils's careful hand: *shoaling, uncertain, do not trust.* "And if Vole's boats come down behind us while we're picking our way through reeds, we're a duck in a barrel with our feathers on."

"So what's your alternative?"

"Me." He said it plainly, because he'd learned long ago that the only way to say a hard thing was to sand all the drama off it first. "The Trust keeps a patrol cutter at Weir Nine. Two officers, a signal lamp, a wireless set. I know the watch rotation — Trust stations haven't changed their habits since I wore the uniform, and men who've never been shot at always take the same supper at the same hour. I go in. I surrender. I tell them I'm a deserter from their own auxiliary service, that I've been running with a salvage crew, and that the *Kestrel* took on water above Greyfall and broke up in the blind channel. I give them coordinates."

Gideon's hand stilled on the wheel. The river hissed against the hull. "False coordinates."

"Coordinates that will keep their cutter and every gunboat Vole can borrow searching a stretch of dead water for two days. By the time they figure out there's no wreck, you'll be past the last toll light." He paused. "And I'll be in a cell, which is where I've been headed for eleven years anyway. I'm just choosing the door."

"No." Gideon said it fast, the way you say a thing before your mind can catch up to your mouth. Then, slower: "No. Bram. We don't trade people. That's the whole — that's the entire point of this boat. That's the point of everything."

"Then let me tell you what I traded eleven years ago, and you can decide whether I'm a person you're keeping or a debt you're paying." Bram heard his own voice go flat and even, the voice he used for after-action reports, the voice that had kept him sane through things that should have unmade him. "I was twenty-four. Auxiliary Survey Corps, seconded to a private expedition — you know the name on the contract now. Vole. We were mapping the upper Sable for a client who wanted to know what the river had swallowed. There was a village at the bend above Greyfall. Descendants of Vessany folk, still keeping the old ways, still keeping — we thought — nothing but goats and superstition. Vole wanted the area cleared before the survey teams came through. Cleared. He asked me for the patrol routes, because the village had a militia, old men with hunting pieces, and he wanted no trouble with the colonial garrison when his men went in." Bram's jaw ached; he realized he was clenching it. "I gave him the routes. I told myself it was just paper. Paper doesn't kill anyone. I was very good at telling myself things. Fourteen people died in that village, Gideon. Two of them were children. And I drew the line they crossed."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Down on the deck he could hear Sefa singing to the engine, one of her tuneless wordless songs, and Nils arguing with a gull, and the ordinary sounds of a family afloat, and Bram stood inside all of it feeling like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool — visible, cold, and finally, after years of current, not going anywhere.

"Miriam knew," Gideon said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Your mother figured it out before the end. She found the payment record — Vole paid me through a shell company, but he paid me, and your mother could read a ledger like a priest reads scripture. She confronted me at the last camp above Greyfall." He closed his eyes and the camp was there: the tarps snapping in the wind, the smell of woodsmoke and wet canvas, Miriam Dunmore's face in the firelight, not angry — that was the worst of it, never angry, only terribly, terribly clear. "She didn't turn me in. She said, *You'll carry it better if you put it down where someone can see it.* I thought she meant confession. She meant this. She meant the ledger, the testimony, the truth in the second compartment. She left a door open for me, Gideon, and I've been walking toward it for five years without knowing it was there."

"So this is your confession."

"This is my interest payment," Bram said. "The principal I'll never clear. But I can buy you time, and time is the only thing standing between that evidence and the bottom of the river. You said it yourself — the monsoon shuts the waterway, and Vole's injunction will be waiting at the Rivergate. You need every hour. I'm the only one aboard who can steal hours from the Trust, because I know how they think, how they file, how they waste a day being thorough. Let me be thorough at them."

Gideon turned from the wheel at last and looked at him, and Bram made himself hold it. He had faced courts-martial boards with less fear than he felt under that gaze — not because Gideon was harsh, but because they were not. Because they looked at him the way their mother had, with that awful clarity that left no room to hide and no cruelty to hide behind.

"If you do this," Gideon said, "Halloran will process you. Halloran is not Vole. Halloran reads things."

"Then maybe that's not a bug," Bram said. "Maybe that's the second half of the plan. I'm not just carrying false coordinates into that station, Gideon. I'm carrying a story with loose threads in it. Deliberate ones. A date that doesn't sit right. A supply requisition I shouldn't know about. A man who's been running river salvage for a decade doesn't remember the name of the barge he crewed on, but I'll remember it wrong in a way a careful man would catch. If Halloran is the man I think he is — the one who audited the tollgate manifests and got a clerk reassigned for it — then I'm not walking into a cell. I'm walking into his filing cabinet. I'm a discrepancy with legs."

"And if you're wrong about him?"

"Then I'm in a cell, and you're at the Rivergate, and the only person who pays is me." Bram smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth, the one he used to keep people from worrying. "I've been paying for a long time. It's nice, for once, to choose the installment."

* * *

They argued until dusk. Sefa argued loudest — she called him a great stupid anvil of a man and said she'd sooner weld the hatch shut over him than let him walk, and then she went below and came back up with a tin of the spiced nuts he liked and thrust it at his chest without meeting his eyes, which was Sefa's way of surrendering while refusing to admit it. Nils said nothing at all for a long while, and then asked, in a small voice, whether Bram would come back, and Bram said *probably*, and Nils said *probably isn't no*, which was the closest the cartographer could come to *I love you* in front of witnesses. Gideon said yes in the end the way Gideon said everything — by looking at the chart, and the falling barometer, and the arithmetic of hours, and choosing the least terrible number.

They anchored in a backwater below Weir Nine, in the shadow of a dead cottonwood, and Sefa rowed him the last mile in the skiff because she refused to let the last thing he saw from the *Kestrel* be the stern rail. The patrol station sat on a pilings platform where the river narrowed: a corrugated-roof hut, a signal mast, a cutter moored alongside with its engine covers off, and a wireless antenna drooping against the bruised sky. A lamp burned yellow in the hut window. Two figures moved inside. Supper hour. Just as he'd told them. Some things about the Trust never changed, and Bram had built his whole plan on the comfort of that, and it still felt like betrayal of a different kind — that he knew his old masters so well, that their habits lived in him like scars.

"You come back," Sefa said, holding the skiff against the station ladder. She was not asking. She was an engineer; she stated load tolerances. "You come back, or I will come get you, and I will be extremely annoying about it, and I will dismantle their station bolt by bolt."

"Probably," Bram said.

"*Probably*," she snarled, and shoved the tin of nuts into his jacket one more time, as if provisioning him for a voyage, and then she pushed off and rowed into the dark without looking back, because looking back was for people with something left to lose, and she wanted him to believe he had everything left to lose.

He climbed the ladder. He stood on the platform with the river sliding black beneath his boots and the first fat drops of the coming monsoon spotting the corrugated iron above him like slow applause, and he raised his hands, and he called out in the parade-ground voice he hadn't used in eleven years:

"Hello the station! Bram Vey, auxiliary service, deserter, surrendering! I've got information on the *Kestrel* and the salvage chest, and I'll give it to whoever holds the wireless key — but only once, so fetch your senior man!"

The door banged open. Lamplight spilled out. A young officer with a wet towel around his neck stared at him, and behind him an older one — a sergeant with a ledger face — was already reaching for the incident book, and Bram thought: *good, write it down, write all of it down, paper is how you people think, so let me be paper.*

They took his statement in the hut, under the yellow lamp, while the rain found its rhythm outside. He gave them the deserter's confession first — name, service number, the years of drift, the salvage crew, the chest. Then the coordinates: a bend eleven miles above Greyfall, a blind channel, a wreck. He described the *Kestrel* breaking up in loving, bureaucratic detail — hull split on a snag, cargo lost, survivors scattered, two dead. He watched the sergeant write it all down and felt the old familiar sickness of lying well, and then he did the thing he had rehearsed until his tongue ached: he made his mistakes.

He said the chest had come up at a dredge site off Corver's Landing, when every record the Trust held would say Greyfall Bend. He said the crew had run the tollgate on the night of the fourteenth, when the tollgate log — if Halloran had pulled it, and Bram would bet his remaining years that Halloran had pulled it — said the fifteenth. He said he'd last seen Gideon Dunmore wearing a coat he'd described wrong on purpose, a detail too specific and too false, the kind of error that doesn't look like lying, only like a bad memory — unless you were a man who checked things.

The sergeant wrote it all down without looking up. The young officer brought tea that tasted of river water and tinned milk. And somewhere past midnight, when the rain had thickened to a steady drumming and the lamp had burned low, a launch came downriver through the dark with its running lights doused, and a tall figure in a long coat stepped onto the platform, shaking water from his hat, and Bram's heart did not sink — it settled, the way a stone settles, into the place it had been aiming for all along.

Inspector Halloran. Not Vole. Not Vole's men. The Trust's own auditor of discrepancies, come in person, at night, in the rain, for a deserter who knew one detail too many about a dredge site he'd never been shown.

Halloran looked at him for a long moment across the lamplit table, and Bram saw the man's eyes move — the coat, the dates, the dredge site — ticking through them like a man counting coins he suspected were clipped.

"You said Corver's Landing," Halloran said quietly. "The dredge manifest says Greyfall Bend."

Bram let the silence sit. Let it be exactly as long as a tired man's guilty hesitation, and no longer.

"Does it," he said. "Then I misremembered. It's been a long decade, Inspector."

"Yes," Halloran said, and pulled out the chair across from him, and sat down, and did not open the incident book. "It usually is, for men like you. Tell me about the coat again."

And outside, over the black and rising river, the monsoon came down like a verdict, and Bram began — carefully, thread by loose thread — to lie.
