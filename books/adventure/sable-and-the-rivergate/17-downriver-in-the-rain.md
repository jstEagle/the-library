## 17. Downriver in the Rain

The monsoon did not arrive. It *landed* — like something thrown from a great height, like a body dropped from a bridge.

Sefa had given them four days and the sky spent none of them politely. By the second morning the river had gone the color of gunmetal and was rising in the reeds, swallowing the sandbars whole, erasing the channel markers one by one as if the water were tidying up after them, removing all evidence that anyone had ever passed this way before.

Gideon stood at the Kestrel's wheel with rain hammering the canvas awning overhead so hard it sounded like applause — the vast, indifferent applause of a crowd that wanted to see you fail. Downriver lay the Rivergate: two hundred miles of flooded country, drowned snags, and current running hard enough to peel the paint off a hull. Four days of fuel if they were lucky. Less if the river decided otherwise.

"You should lash down," Sefa shouted over the din, hauling herself along the deck rail with both hands, oilskin streaming. "Everything not bolted goes overboard in the first real squall."

"Nothing's going overboard."

"Everything goes overboard eventually." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and cinched a line around the spare propeller crate. "That's just salvage law."

Bram was forward, riding out the pitch with his boots braced wide, one hand on the winch housing, watching the water like a man reading a hostile document. Nils had wedged himself into the wheelhouse corner with the charts spread across his knees, weighting the corners with engine parts because the wind kept trying to take them.

"The flood channel runs wide past Corver's Reach," Nils called. "If we can hold the middle third, we'll make good time. The problem is the middle third is where the old Vessany pilings are."

"Pilings I can dodge," Gideon said.

"Pilings *underwater* you can't see."

"Then we'll feel them and apologize."

Nils almost smiled. Almost. His knuckles were white on the chart edges, and Gideon saw him mouth a count — some private arithmetic of fear he ran through his fingers like prayer beads. The boy had run away from a cartography house where they'd beaten precision into him; now he was navigating a foundering river in a monsoon for people who'd known him three months. Gideon wanted to tell him it would be all right. Instead they said, "Call the turns loud. I can't hear whispers today."

The first day nearly killed them twice.

Once at Corver's Reach, where a half-submerged barge — dead a decade, maybe more — swung on its mooring chains like a hanged man and forced the Kestrel through a gap no wider than her own beam. Gideon took it at full throttle because slowing down meant the current would choose their line for them, and the hull screamed along something beneath the surface, a long grinding kiss of wood on stone that everyone aboard felt in their teeth. Then they were through, and the river opened, and no one spoke for a full minute.

And once at dusk, when the wind came around and hit them broadside, and the Kestrel laid over so far that the deck ran green water, and Gideon — lashed to the wheel by then, both hands and one boot strap — watched the ledger chest slide six feet across the cabin floor until it cracked against the bulkhead like a struck bell.

They'd lashed it after that. Three lines, Sefa's knots, checked hourly. But the sound of it stayed in Gideon's ears all night: wood on wood, sharp and final, like a knock at a door.

*Like a knock at a door,* Gideon thought, and did not let themselves finish the thought.

* * *

They ran through the second night because stopping meant anchoring in floodwater full of traveling debris, and debris at four knots hits harder than most things people shoot at you with. So they stood watches in pairs, and the river roared past the hull, and somewhere behind them — upstream, back toward Greyfall Bend — Cassian Vole was doing whatever Cassian Vole did when a bargain got refused.

Gideon had the midnight-to-dawn watch with Bram. Rain fell straight down in ropes. The world had contracted to the size of the wheelhouse glass, which streamed so badly that the river beyond it looked painted, smeared, unreal.

"I keep thinking about the tollgate," Bram said, without looking over. He'd said little since he'd come back from surrendering himself to the Trust patrol — since he'd walked into their custody with false coordinates folded in his pocket and walked out again two days later, released in the chaos of Halloran's internal audit, thinner and quieter than before. "What I did. Back then. With Miriam's expedition."

"You've told me."

"I've told you the version I could stand." He turned his head. Rain crawled down the glass between them. "There was a night on the upper river when men came to our camp asking questions, and I told them where the survey party had gone. Not the main camp. The survey party. Your mother had sent them ahead to map the narrows." His jaw worked. "I told myself it was to protect the rest of us. That's what I've told myself for five years."

Gideon kept their eyes on the water. "And now?"

"Now I think there's no version where I didn't know exactly what I was doing." He exhaled, long and slow. "When I gave those patrol officers my false coordinates, I wasn't buying you time. I was paying a debt. It doesn't balance. Nothing balances. But it's the first payment I've made in years that didn't make me sick after."

The wheelhouse clock ticked. Somewhere below, Sefa slept in her engine cocoon, arms around the flywheel like a lover.

"Bram," Gideon said. "My mother trusted you enough to put you on her crew. Whatever you did after — she'd have weighed it against everything else. She always did the sums fairly."

"That's the thing that keeps me up," Bram said quietly. "I don't remember what she'd have weighed. Five years is long enough to lose the sound of a person's voice. You start remembering your own edits instead of them." He tapped the glass, where the rain had drawn a long wavering line downriver. "Don't do that. Don't let the edits replace her."

Gideon didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with. The river answered for them both, a long low boom of water striking the hull, patient as a debt collector.

* * *

It happened on the third afternoon, at a place Nils named only as the Widow's Cut — a straightaway where the flood had jumped the banks and turned three miles of farmland into open water, shallow and treacherous, studded with the tops of fence posts and an orchard drowning to its shoulders.

The squall came out of the west with no warning at all. One moment gray light; the next, a wall of rain so dense it erased the horizon, and wind that hit the Kestrel like a shoulder-check from a giant. The barge broached. Gideon felt it happen — felt the stern swing, felt the rudder go soft and useless in aerated water — and heard Sefa screaming from below that the intake was clogged, that the engine was sucking silt, that they were going to be pushed sideways onto the orchard fence line at eight knots.

They might have survived that. What they did not survive was the snag.

It came out of the churned brown water like a spear — a whole tree, root ball first, torn loose from some drowned bank upstream, traveling at the speed of the flood. It struck the Kestrel amidships with a crack Gideon felt in their spine, and the lashings on the cargo deck parted — not all of them, but two of the three holding the ledger chest — and the chest went over the side.

No one shouted. There was no time for shouting. Gideon saw it go — saw the lead-lined box tilt off the deck edge with terrible unhurried grace, like a coffin being lowered — and then they were moving, tearing off their oilskin, kicking free of their boots, and diving off the stern rail into water the temperature of a held breath.

The cold took them first. Then the current took the rest.

River water in flood is not water; it is a moving building. It rolled Gideon under, tumbled them end over end, drove them down into a brown darkness where up and down were opinions rather than facts. Their left hand closed on something — rope, a trailing lashing from the chest — and they held on, and the rope held, and together Gideon and the chest sank.

Fifteen feet. Twenty. The pressure squeezed their ears shut. Below them the riverbed rose up pale and churning, and above them the underside of the flood glimmered like hammered tin, impossibly far away. The air in their lungs was already spent. They knew this with a strange, floating calm — the calm of the body counting its own remaining seconds — and still they kicked, dragging the chest's weight, pulling themselves hand over hand up a rope that led nowhere, toward a surface they could not find.

*You never learned to let go.*

The voice arrived whole, unmistakable, arriving the way memory arrives — not through the ears but through the floor of the skull. Miriam Dunmore's voice, dry and warm and faintly amused, exactly as it had been in the study with the maps pinned everywhere, when Gideon was fourteen and furious about something forgotten now.

*Mum.* Gideon thought it, or said it — bubbles tore away from their mouth, silver, rising wrong, sideways.

*Look at you. Drowning in a river to save a box of paper. You are so much my child it's almost embarrassing.*

*I'm letting go of everything else,* Gideon thought, kicking, arms burning. *Just not this.*

*Why?*

And there it was — the question, the one they'd carried for five years, surfacing now in the brown dark with the last of their breath. *Because you died chasing it and nobody even wrote it down properly. Because the Trust erased a whole country and called it paperwork. Because if I let this go, I let you go, and I'm not ready. I was never ready. I didn't say goodbye. I didn't answer your last letter, Mum. I read it forty times and I never wrote back.*

The current spun them. The rope burned through their grip. And the voice — patient, wry, unbearably fond — said:

*Silly goose. I didn't write to you so you'd write back. I wrote so you'd know where to look. You're not carrying my ghost, Giddy. You're carrying my work. There's a difference, and the difference is that work gets finished.*

*Come back with me,* Gideon thought, and hated how young it sounded. *Come up. Come home.*

A pause. In it, the whole river seemed to hold still — the roar dimming, the cold easing, as though the water itself leaned in to listen.

*Oh, my love. I've been home the whole time. Home was never a place you could lose. It's the part of you that refuses to stop caring about broken things.* A laugh, small and bright, swallowed by the flood. *Now breathe, kick, and stop arguing with rivers. You get that from your father's side.*

The vision broke. Sound crashed back in — the thundering brown world, the tin-bright ceiling of the surface — and Gideon kicked, and kicked, and their head broke water into a deluge of rain, gasping, one arm hooked through the chest's trailing rope.

The Kestrel was fifty yards downstream, wallowing, someone leaning over the rail — Bram, huge against the gray, throwing a line that uncoiled and fell short, coiled again, threw again. The second line landed across Gideon's shoulder. They wrapped it twice around their forearm, hooked the chest's handle, and held on while the river tried to keep them and lost.

Hauling them aboard was ugly and wordless — hands in their collar, knees on deck planking, the chest thumping down beside them like a second rescued body. Gideon lay on their back in the rain and coughed river water and laughed, actually laughed, a raw scraping sound that frightened Nils, who was kneeling over them saying their name over and over.

"I'm fine," Gideon rasped. "Check the chest."

"You nearly *died*—"

"Check. The chest."

Sefa pried it open with shaking hands. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as a sermon: the ledger. Miriam's handwriting. The name *M. Dunmore, custodian* in faded ink.

"She's dry," Sefa reported, and her voice cracked on the second word.

"Good," Gideon said, and closed their eyes, and let the rain fall on their face like a hand.

* * *

They ran the last stretch through the night, engine at full song, Sefa feeding it fuel and prayers in equal measure. The river rose and rose. Twice they passed houses with water at their windowsills and lamplight burning inside — families waiting out the flood in the upper rooms, watching the Kestrel tear past through the rain, and Gideon raised one arm to them each time, some old salvager's courtesy, *we're still afloat, hold on.*

Near dawn the rain changed. It slackened — not ending, but settling into a steady, businesslike fall, the storm's vanguard passing on inland to do worse elsewhere. And out of the gray ahead, materializing like a development in a photograph, came the Rivergate: the customs house on its stone spur, the crane arms, the bonded warehouses, the town climbing the hill behind it all with its lamps still lit against the dark.

And below the customs house, riding at anchor in the lee, three gunboats flying the Colonial Salvage Trust ensign, arranged across the channel like a hand of cards played face-up.

Nils lowered his glasses slowly. "That's not a patrol formation," he said. "That's a reception."

At the rail, Gideon wrung river water from their sleeve and looked at the chest at their feet — at the knock-at-a-door sound it had made sliding across the cabin floor, at the rope burns on their forearm, at the ink-dry name inside.

"They knew we were coming," Bram said flatly.

"Of course they did." Gideon lifted the chest. It weighed nothing now, or everything; there was no longer any difference. "Cassian always did like to be holding the door when I arrived."

Ahead, a launch was putting out from the customs house steps, white flag at its bow, coming to meet them through the rain.
