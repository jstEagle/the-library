## 18. Halloran's Doubt

The rain had not yet reached the capital, but Inspector Aldous Halloran could smell it coming—green and metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth. It came through the shutters of his office on the fourth floor of the Trust's Rivergate headquarters, threading past the brass fittings and the oiled mahogany of a building that had been designed to look like a bank and functioned, most days, like a church. He sat at his desk with the windows cracked against regulation and let the smell in. It was the one honest thing about the place.

Before him lay the intake file for prisoner 4471: Bramwell Ostergaard, surrendered without resistance at the Kilnwater patrol station, three days upriver from Greyfall Bend. The forms were filled out in a neat, blocky hand. Too neat. Halloran had spent nineteen years reading the handwriting of men who wanted something from paper, and this hand wanted nothing at all. It was the writing of a man performing compliance, each letter squared off like a brick laid by someone who had been told exactly where bricks go.

He turned to the personal effects inventory. One knife, folding. One tin whistle, dented. One oilskin packet, contents unlisted.

*Contents unlisted.*

Halloran set down his pen.

In nineteen years he had never once seen *contents unlisted* survive a clerk's review. Every packet was opened, catalogued, weighed. A man's whole life fit into six lines on an intake form, and the clerks were paid to make it seven. But here was a blank field, initialed by Sergeant Pryce—Pryce, who had signed off on eleven other intakes that month with the diligence of a man counting the hours to his pension—and beneath Pryce's initials, in a different ink, a countersignature that did not belong to any patrol schedule Halloran had authorized.

He knew the ink. Trust-issue iron-gall, the cheap kind that went brown as it dried. But the hand—the hand was Vole's. He had watched Cassian Vole sign enough consulting invoices over the past two years to know the way the man made his C's, like little hooks baited for fish. Cassian Vole had countersigned a prisoner intake at a patrol station four hundred miles from anywhere he had any business being, and the form had been filed, stamped, and shelved within a day, which meant someone upstairs had expedited it, which meant someone upstairs had known it was coming.

Halloran rose and went to the window. Below, the Rivergate customs yard spread out under gaslight, cranes standing against the bruised sky like herons in a marsh. Barges rode low at their moorings. Somewhere out there, past the last buoy, the monsoon was assembling itself over the sea, and every man in this building was pretending the weather would be polite.

He thought about the prisoner file again. Not the man—the file. Files did not lie; files only recorded what people wished to have believed. And this file wished very hard.

* * *

The archive room was in the basement, two floors below the water table, kept dry by pumps that groaned all night like animals in pain. Halloran had a key because his position required it and used it rarely, because the basement smelled of mildew and old ambition, and because the records down there had a way of making a man complicit just by proximity. Nineteen years of salvage claims, seizure orders, dredge licenses, toll receipts. The river's entire yield, converted to paper.

He pulled the Kilnwater station ledger first. It took him forty minutes to find the page, and when he found it, he stood very still in the lamplight.

Prisoner 4471 was logged. Arrival time, 21:40. Escorting officer, Pryce. Disposition: transferred to Rivergate, priority escort, per directive.

But the transfer column was wrong. Prisoners transferred to Rivergate went by the morning packet boat, always—it was policy, it was economy, it was the way it had been done since before Halloran wore the uniform. 4471's transfer was marked for the *night* launch, the fast boat, the one reserved for gold shipments and urgent correspondence. And the authorization code beside it—H-9-V—was not a code Halloran recognized, which troubled him more than anything else, because he had helped draft the current cipher book and there were only nine H-series authorizations in existence and he could name every one of them and their holders.

Nine. He counted them twice against the cipher registry. Eight accounted for. The ninth—H-9-V—existed in the registry as issued to *Office of the Director's Consultant*, effective fourteen months ago.

A consultant. Not an officer. Not a warden. A consultant, holding a live authorization code, with power to move prisoners on night launches and leave effect inventories blank.

Halloran closed the ledger and stood in the dark between the shelves, listening to the pumps groan, and felt something shift in his chest that he would later recognize as the beginning of the end of his career—or the beginning of it, depending on how things fell. For nineteen years he had served the Trust the way a good mason serves a wall: believing in the wall, laying his courses straight, asking nothing of the stone except that it hold. He had told himself the wall was load-bearing for civilization—that the river was chaos, and the Trust was order, and order, however administered, was better than the flood.

But walls built on fraud were not walls. They were facades. And he had just found the crack.

* * *

He began pulling threads the way he did everything: quietly, methodically, alone.

The next morning he requested the charter records for the Vessany annexation—a routine historical query, he wrote on the slip, pertaining to a disputed dredge boundary claim. The archivist who fetched the box was a young woman named Ilsa Reyne, sharp-eyed, underpaid, and bored enough to notice what others overlooked. She brought the box herself instead of sending it up.

"You're the third person to ask for these in a year," she said, setting it on his desk. "That's unusual. They're mostly dust."

"Who were the others?"

She shrugged. "Consultant Vole, twice. Once with a writ, once without." A pause, and then, lower, the way people speak when they are testing whether a superior is safe: "The second time, the box came back lighter. Two folios missing. I logged it." She tapped her own temple. "In here. Not in the record."

Halloran looked at her for a long moment. "Why tell me?"

"Because you actually read things," she said. "And because if someone's cutting pages out of the charter records, I'd rather not be the clerk who didn't notice when it finally matters."

After she left, he opened the box. The Vessany annexation papers were thin—suspiciously thin for the legal erasure of a nation. A treaty of cession with signatures he now examined closely for the first time in his career. The signatories' names meant nothing to him, but the ink did. Three different documents, supposedly executed across eight months, all bearing the same batch of iron-gall, the same oxidation browning, the same pen pressure in the flourishes. Documents written in the same week, backdated across a year, conferring upon the Crown a treasury, a river, and a people's right to have never existed.

He had authenticated documents for tribunals. He knew forgery the way a physician knows fever—not by one symptom but by the constellation. This was a constellation blazing.

And beneath the treaty folio, where a second document should have been—an inventory of the Vessany royal archive, referenced in the treaty's own text—there was only a stub, cut close to the binding, and a pencil notation in a dead clerk's hand: *removed for review, [illegible], 11 years prior.*

Removed for review. Never returned. Eleven years.

Halloran sat back. Outside, the first fat drops of the monsoon's outrider squall ticked against the glass, tentative, like fingers testing a lock.

* * *

He worked for two days before he let himself understand what he was doing. He cross-referenced the Kilnwater prisoner against the Kestrel's salvage registration—he had flagged the barge weeks ago when its claim notice went unanswered, a small irritation at the time, a vessel dodging tolls—and found the crew manifest listed a Bramwell Ostergaard, engineer's mate, discharged military, no priors. The same man now sitting in a Trust cell, transferred on a ghost authorization, his effects sealed by a consultant's hook-lettered hand.

Which raised the question Halloran had been circling for two days: why would Cassian Vole want a captured salvager moved fast and quietly? Prisoners were leverage, yes—but this prisoner had been taken *upriver*, near Greyfall Bend, where the Dunmore woman's barge had last been sighted. Vole had gone upriver personally. Vole had come back with a prisoner and a sealed packet and a countersigned lie, and then Vole had filed, that same morning, an injunction request against the Kestrel's cargo—rushed through the customs bench with a speed Halloran had never seen short of bribery or panic.

Panic. That was the word. Everything Vole had done in the past week had the texture of a man running slightly ahead of something. And men running ahead of something did not seal packets unless the packets contained what they feared.

On the third evening, Halloran did the thing he could not take back. He unlocked his own drawer, took out the duplicate key every inspector was issued and forbidden to use, and went down to the evidence vault where the Kilnwater transfer's accompanying parcel had been stored—unlogged, he had confirmed, existing nowhere in the registry except as a weight entry in the launch manifest. Forty pounds, manifest said. The parcel on the shelf weighed closer to sixty by the strain of the rope.

Inside the oilskin: a ledgers' worth of loose documents in a language he did not read, water-stained but legible, stamped with a seal he recognized only from the oldest plates in the charter records—the extinct nation, the erased name, Vessany. And folded among them, in English, in a woman's cramped, confident hand, pages of testimony. He read three lines and stopped breathing.

*I, Miriam Dunmore, being of sound mind and in full possession of the evidence herein, do swear that the Colonial Salvage Trust's charter rests upon forged instruments of cession, and that the following persons did know and profit thereby—*

The first name was Cassian Vole's.

Halloran stood in the vault's electric light with a dead woman's accusation in his hands and heard, distantly, the rain beginning in earnest against the high windows—a sound like applause, or like a crowd gathering. He understood now why the packet had been sealed, why the prisoner had been vanished into a night launch, why the injunction had been rushed. Vole had the evidence. Vole had been carrying the evidence that could hang him, and somewhere between Greyfall Bend and this building, he had chosen to hide it rather than destroy it—which told Halloran something important about Cassian Vole: the man kept exits open. Men who destroyed evidence had decided their course. Men who hid it were still negotiating with the future.

Well. So was Halloran.

He rewrapped the parcel exactly as found, replaced it on its shelf, locked the vault, and walked back up through the building he had served for nineteen years, seeing it plainly for the first time: the marble bought with a forged treaty, the brass polished by stolen wages, the whole edifice humming above a basement full of cut-out pages. His boots sounded loud on the stairs. No one looked up. No one ever looked up; that had always been the point of men like him.

In his office, he took out a fresh sheet of Trust letterhead, considered it, and put it away. Then he took a plain sheet, and on it, in his own careful hand, he wrote a single line:

*To the Crown Commission of Audit, Rivergate Circuit — regarding irregularities in prisoner transfer H-9-V, and instruments of cession cited in enclosed testimony.*

He did not send it. Not yet. The monsoon would close the river in days; after landfall, no dispatches moved, no boats ran, and the Commission's circuit judge would be trapped in the city with nothing to do but hear cases. Vole's injunction would bring the Kestrel and its cargo to the customs house within the week. Whatever happened on that dock would happen in front of officers, press, and witnesses—and Halloran intended to be standing there, in his nineteen years of uniform, with the audit letter in his breast pocket and the vault shelf number memorized.

He sealed the letter anyway, and wrote the address, and set it beneath the prisoner file where he would see it every morning until the day came.

Outside, the rain thickened from applause into roar. Down in the vault, sixty pounds of dead woman's testimony waited on its shelf, patient as water.

Halloran turned off his lamp and stood a moment in the dark, watching lightning walk the river, and permitted himself—for the first time in nineteen years—to hope that the wall was coming down.
