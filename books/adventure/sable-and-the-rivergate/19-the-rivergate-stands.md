## 19. The Rivergate Stands

The rain reached the Rivergate before Cassian Vole did.

He felt it first as a smell — that green, mineral breath the air gives off when a long drought finally breaks its word — and then as a pressure behind his eyes, the old expedition ache that had never quite left him, the one that meant weather was coming with intent. By the time his launch slid beneath the customs house gantries, the first fat drops were striking the river flat as coins, and the dockworkers were running canvas over the cargo cranes with the unhurried urgency of people who had done this every year of their lives and expected to do it every year after.

Cassian stood at the rail in a dry grey coat and let them run.

There was a time — he could still find it if he pressed on the right bruise — when he would have been up there with them, hauling canvas, laughing into the wet, Miriam beside him with her sleeves rolled and her hair plastered to her face, both of them young enough to believe the monsoon was a spectacle rather than an adversary. That man had drowned somewhere upriver, in a season he preferred not to name even privately. What climbed out of that water wore better coats.

"Mr. Vole." The launch's pilot touched his cap. "Customs quay's clear. Inspector Halloran's office sent word you'd have the east chamber."

"Of course they did." Cassian gathered his document case. "Money moves quickly here when it's the right money."

* * *

The Rivergate customs house had been built by people who wanted the river to know its place.

It rose four storeys out of the mud at the mouth of the Sable, all pale stone and iron filigree, its façade carved with the allegories of lawful trade: Justice holding her scales over a barge, Plenty pouring grain from a horn, and above them all, worn nearly smooth by a century of salt wind, the crowned cipher of the Colonial Salvage Trust itself — because the Trust had paid for the building, and the builders had understood whose name deserved the highest stone. Inside, the vaulted hall smelled of ink, pipe smoke, and river damp that no amount of scrubbing ever fully defeated. Clerks moved between desks like patient water finding its level.

Cassian crossed the hall the way he crossed every room now: slowly, so that others had time to notice him arriving.

"Counselor Vole." A junior registrar hurried to meet him, rain-spotted ledger under one arm. "We weren't told to expect—"

"No," Cassian agreed pleasantly. "You weren't."

He set his case on the registrar's desk and opened it with the small ceremony the moment deserved. Papers, in order. He had spent three sleepless nights in the river town of Marrow Quay drafting them, and he had enjoyed the work more than he would ever admit aloud. There was a craft to paperwork that reminded him of salvage: you did not create anything new; you simply raised what had always been resting on the bottom, intact, waiting for someone with the right equipment.

"The Kestrel," he said. "Barge, forty-one feet, patched hull, registry lapsed twice. Owned or operated by one Gideon Dunmore. She is presently upriver carrying cargo seized — or about to be seized — under Trust writ. I require an injunction attaching that cargo the moment she touches a Rivergate berth. Not before. On arrival. To the plank, if we're being precise."

The registrar blinked. "On arrival, sir? Most claims attach at—"

"I'm aware of how most claims work." Cassian smiled, and watched the smile do what his smiles had done for thirty years, which was end conversations. "This one is different. The vessel is running. If the attachment waits for her master to present papers, the cargo will be ashore and scattered through a hundred dockside hands before my clerks finish their tea. Attach on arrival. Every crate, every chest, every coil of rope aboard her. Especially the chest."

"The chest, sir?"

"You'll find it described in schedule B." Lead-lined. Sealed. Bearing the arms of a nation that does not exist. He did not say any of this aloud. Some words were load-bearing, and you did not hand them to strangers. "Initial here. Here. And here, where the court's seal goes."

The registrar initialled where he was told. Outside, the rain thickened against the tall windows until the river beyond became a grey suggestion, and the gas lamps came on early in the hall, each flame shivering in its glass.

Cassian signed last, his own name in the small exact hand that had signed expedition manifests, requisition orders, and — though no living soul but himself knew it — two letters that had ended lives. He looked at the signature for a moment longer than the clerks thought proper.

Miriam Dunmore had once told him his handwriting was too neat for an honest man. She'd said it laughing, over cards, in a tent that smelled of kerosene and wet canvas, twenty years and one lifetime ago. He wondered, sometimes, whether she had known even then. Whether she had written it down somewhere, in that cramped field hand of hers, filed away among the survey notes where a careful reader might someday find it.

It didn't matter now. Careful readers were exactly what he had arranged not to have.

* * *

The injunction took four hours to issue, which Cassian considered a personal triumph; the last one had taken nine days, and that had involved a senator's nephew. Money, properly applied, did not bribe the law. It simply lubricated the joints through which the law already wished to move.

By late afternoon he had taken the east chamber — a high room overlooking the customs quay, furnished with a desk, a stove, and windows fogged with the first real rain — and established himself in it the way a spider establishes itself, without hurry and without apology. His clerk, a narrow young man named Pell who asked no questions and remembered everything, laid out the day's intelligence on the desk in a fan of telegrams.

Cassian read them standing, one finger tracing each line.

*Vessel sighted passing Tarnholt reach, downbound, making poor speed in rising water.*

*Patrol gunboat Wren dispatched to tollgate station. No contact.*

*Inspector Halloran requests clarification of writ scope re: personnel detained at tollgate (one B. Halloway).*

That last one he read twice. Bram Halloway, surrendered himself at the tollgate like a lamb walking into a butcher's cool room, and had fed the patrol a set of coordinates so obviously false that Cassian had almost admired them. Almost. The trouble with men like Bram — big, loyal, guilt-ridden men — was that their sacrifices were legible. You could read a sacrifice the way you read a chart. It pointed directly at whatever the sacrificer loved.

Which meant the Kestrel was coming down the eastern channels, the flooded timber runs, hugging the banks where the current lied. Which meant Gideon Dunmore was cleverer than the mother, or at least more cautious, and Cassian found he respected that, in the detached way one respects weather.

"Pell," he said. "Two things."

"Sir."

"First: wire the Wren. Tell her captain to hold at Greyfall Bend and go no further upriver. The monsoon will do our work for us above the Bend; no vessel that size rides out landfall on open water. Let the river push them to us. We need only be standing here with open hands."

"And second?"

Cassian turned to the window. Below, the customs quay glistened black and silver in the rain, cranes folded like sleeping herons, mooring posts strung with pennants gone dark with water. Beyond the quay the river had begun to rise — he could see it eating the lowest step of the landing stairs, inch by patient inch — and somewhere up that brown immensity, a patched barge with a stubborn, grieving child of Miriam Dunmore at her wheel was running toward him at this very hour, believing herself unseen.

"Second," Cassian said, "find me the newspapermen. The ones who cover shipping and courts. Quietly. Buy them dinner tomorrow, not tonight. Tonight I want them hungry and bored and nowhere near this building."

Pell hesitated. "You don't want press coverage of the seizure, sir?"

"On the contrary. I want it enormously." Cassian smiled at the rain. "I want it on Thursday, after the cargo is in Trust custody and the story is finished. A seizure is only embarrassing while it is still possible to imagine it failing. Once it has succeeded, it becomes history, and history is written by whoever pays the printers. Thursday, Pell. Not before."

"Yes, sir." Pell gathered his telegrams and paused at the door. "If I may — the Dunmore woman. The daughter. The files say she's nobody. A salvager. No standing, no counsel, no family connections willing to claim her. Do you expect trouble from her?"

Cassian considered the question honestly, which was more than he gave most questions.

Miriam's face floated up at him across twenty years — not the face from the photographs, composed and sun-helmeted, but the real one, unguarded, the night before the Greyfall expedition went upriver for the last time. She had sat sharpening a survey pencil with a knife, and she had looked at him across the campfire and said, *You've stopped telling me what you actually think, Cassian. When a partner stops doing that, he's either planning to leave or planning to bury me.* And he had laughed and told her she was being dramatic, and she had laughed too, and neither of them had been laughing by morning.

"I expect," Cassian said at last, "that she is her mother's daughter, which means she will do the one thing I cannot predict, at the one moment I cannot afford it. That is why we are here four days early, with the injunction signed, the quay watched, and the press bought. One does not defeat a Dunmore by improvising. One defeats her by making certain that whatever she does, it changes nothing."

Pell nodded, satisfied, and left.

Alone, Cassian poured himself a measure of the brandy he kept wrapped in oilcloth against inspections, and stood at the window drinking it slowly while the storm arrived in earnest.

* * *

Landfall, when it came, came like a verdict.

It began at dusk as a sound — a vast, low rushing, like the whole sky exhaling — and then the rain arrived sideways, in sheets, hammering the customs house roof with a noise like applause from a hostile audience. The river answered within the hour. Cassian watched it happen from the east chamber with the lamp turned low: the brown water climbing the landing stairs two steps, three, swallowing the low mooring rings, dragging driftwood and a drowned market boat past the quay at the speed of a galloping horse. Lightning walked the far bank in long white strides. The barometers in the hall, he knew, were falling so fast the clerks had begun betting on them.

Any other year, this was the night the river closed. Any other year, every sane vessel on the Sable was tied down double or hauled out entirely, and the river belonged to God and the drowned.

But the Kestrel was coming down it anyway. He was certain of it. The telegrams confirmed it — a sighting at the timber runs, hours ago, riding water no pilot should have attempted — and certainty settled over Cassian like the dry grey coat he wore indoors, warm and weightless and faintly immoral.

They were coming because they had no choice. The evidence would rot, the witnesses would scatter, the season would seal the upper river for months. They had to reach the Rivergate before the waterway shut, and so they were gambling everything — hull, cargo, lives — on a storm-run that would bring them straight down the throat of the one port in a hundred miles where Cassian Vole held paper on everything they carried.

He had spent the evening reviewing it, move by move, the way he had once reviewed expedition plans by lamplight while Miriam argued with him about route lines. The injunction attached on arrival. The Wren held the lower channel. Halloran — that grey, punctual little instrument of a man — believed he was serving the Trust rather than being used by it, and would stand on the quay with his warrant book like a churchwarden at a funeral. The press would arrive Thursday. The cargo would be in Trust warehouses by Wednesday noon. And Gideon Dunmore would step off her battered barge onto Trust soil, into Trust custody, holding a chest that legally belonged to the Crown the moment it broke the surface of Trust waters, and there was nothing — nothing — she could do about it except rage, and grief, and lose.

It was, Cassian reflected, almost elegant. He had not needed to chase her at all. He had simply needed to be standing at the place where she was already going, holding the deed to it.

He raised the brandy glass slightly, to the window, to the storm, to the ghost in the rain.

"You taught me everything I use, Miriam," he said quietly. "Including this. You always said the river gives up nothing for free. Well. Neither do I."

Lightning filled the room white. In its glare the window became a mirror, and Cassian saw himself briefly as the glass saw him: an old man in good clothes, alone in a government room, smiling at a storm. Then the dark came back and the smile remained, and he chose not to examine the difference.

* * *

Near midnight, boots on the stairs — hurrying boots, which at the Rivergate customs house during landfall meant either catastrophe or opportunity, and Cassian had arranged for only one of those to be possible.

Pell burst in soaked to the collar, telegram flimsy crushed in his fist, rain streaming off him onto the polished floor.

"She's been sighted, sir. Downbound off the Narrows — the pilot steamer flagged her an hour ago. Running dark, no lights, riding the flood crest. The Wren's asking for instructions." Pell swallowed. "She'll make the Rivergate by dawn, sir. Maybe sooner if the water keeps rising. She's — Mr. Vole, she's actually going to try it. In this."

"Of course she is." Cassian set down his glass. Something in his chest was singing, some old expedition chord he had not heard in twenty years, and he let it sing, because confidence was a resource like any other and he saw no reason to waste it. "Signal the Wren: hold position at the harbour mouth, take no aggressive action, let her come in. I want the Kestrel to dock under her own power, calm as Sunday, with no idea anything is wrong. Wake the quaymaster. Berth her at number three — the deep-water berth, under the crane, well lit. And have the bailiffs ready below, out of sight, thirty of them. When her gangplank touches stone, the injunction executes. Cargo, chest, and papers, before her crew's boots are dry."

"And the girl, sir? Gideon Dunmore?"

Cassian walked to the window one last time. The storm had torn the clouds open in the east, and through the rent he could see a smear of stars above the black tumbling immensity of the river — and far down that darkness, invisible but utterly certain, a small patched barge driving toward him through the teeth of the monsoon, carrying a dead woman's ledger and a living woman's fury.

"Nothing," Cassian said softly. "Do nothing to the girl. Let her walk off that boat believing she's won the race. Let her stand on my quay and look at the customs house and think, for one full minute, that she made it home." He turned back to Pell, and the lamp caught his face, and his voice was gentle as a closing door. "Then show her the paper. Grief keeps, Pell. Rage keeps. But hope — hope spoils in minutes. Best to break it fresh."

Pell went out. The storm redoubled against the roof, and down on the black quay the great crane at berth three creaked in the wind like something waking.

Cassian returned to his desk, squared the injunction's corners, and began, with his small exact hand, to draft the press release for Thursday.
