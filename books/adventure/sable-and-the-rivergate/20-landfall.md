## 20. Landfall

The river turned against us at Sallow Reach.

For three days it had been a green-brown animal running beside us, and now it stood up on its hind legs and came for the Kestrel like something owed a debt. The rain did not fall so much as arrive sideways, in sheets you could lean against, in ropes thick enough to moor a skiff. Sefa had the engine singing a note I'd never heard her get out of it before—high, strained, defiant—and Bram lashed himself to the wheel housing with a cargo strap because standing unassisted had become a theory rather than a practice.

"Starboard shoal!" Nils screamed from the bow, one arm hooked through the rail, his map case strapped to his chest like a child he refused to put down. "Fifty yards! Maybe less!"

"I see it!" I shouted back, though what I saw was mostly water wearing the disguise of more water.

The Rivergate customs house rose out of the murk an hour later the way a mountain rises out of cloud—first a suggestion, then a certainty, then something too large to deny. Its stone quay ran along the last navigable mile of the Sable, and above it the customs tower climbed six stories into weather that seemed personally offended by its existence. Even through the rain I could see the lights burning in every window, and the crowd gathering under the colonnade despite the storm: uniformed officers of the Trust's enforcement wing, dockworkers in oilskins, and—because Cassian Vole would not have missed this for a fortune, which was precisely what he'd spent—a cluster of men in good coats standing dry beneath the eaves, and among them, unmistakable even at distance, the pale gleam of press lanterns.

He'd brought witnesses. Of course he had. He wanted my surrender to be a spectacle, a lesson written large for every salvager who worked the Sable: *this is what happens*.

"Coming alongside will be fun," Sefa said, appearing at my shoulder, soaked to the skin and grinning like a woman being paid by the hour for her own suffering. "Quay's got a two-foot fender gap and the current's pushing us broadside."

"So we come in angry," I said. "Like everything else we've done."

She laughed, spat rainwater, and went back down to her engine.

I stood at the rail with the chest between my feet.

It had ridden the whole journey lashed to the deck where I could watch it, tarped against the worst of the weather, and even now, even here, with the end of the river in sight and the end of everything else close behind it, I found myself looking down at that lead-lined lid the way you look at a door you've been afraid to open your whole life. The seal of Vessany stared up at me from beneath the wet canvas edge—the sunken crown over the three rivers—and I thought about how many miles I had measured my life in, how many years I had refused to count, and how all of it, every drifting season aboard the Kestrel, every name I hadn't given at every port office, had been a long slow way of walking around this exact moment.

"Gideon." Bram's voice, low, under the wind. He'd come aft without me hearing him, which for a man his size should have been impossible. "Whatever you're planning—it's not too late to do it quiet. There'll be a side ladder. Officers first, paperwork after. We hand them the chest sealed, let Halloran do the rest inside."

Halloran. Four days ago Bram had walked himself into a Trust patrol boat with false coordinates stitched into his confession, and somewhere between that patrol boat and this dock, Inspector Halloran had begun pulling threads we couldn't see. A note had reached us at the last fuel stop, folded inside a barge manifest: *Custody at the Rivergate is theater. Vole holds an injunction. Whatever you mean to do, do it in the open.* No signature. Just a pressed flower of doubt, left where we'd find it.

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm doing it loud."

Bram studied me for a long moment. Rain ran off his jaw. "Miriam would've done it quiet," he said—not cruelly, just as fact, because he'd known her and I hadn't, not at the end. "She'd have slipped it upriver to someone safe and let other people fight."

"Then she taught me wrong on purpose," I said. "Or she learned too late what I'm learning now. Either way—" I put my boot on the chest lid, feeling the cold of the lead through my sole, "—she spent her whole life being careful, and it bought her a flooded archive and a crew of ghosts. I'm done being careful. Careful is just invisible with better manners."

Something moved across Bram's face that might have been grief or relief or both wearing each other's coats. He nodded once, cinched the strap at his shoulder tighter, and went forward to handle lines.

* * *

We hit the quay hard enough to crack a fender.

Sefa brought us in on a surge of flood-current with the engine screaming reverse, and the Kestrel kissed stone with a sound like a knuckle rapping on the world's largest door. Lines went out—Nils threw his with a cartographer's precision, dead center on the bollard—and before the hull had finished settling, Trust officers were swarming the gangway, batons drawn, rain hammering their helmets, a lieutenant shouting words that dissolved into the storm before they could mean anything.

"In the name of the Colonial Salvage Trust, this vessel and all cargo are seized under writ of—"

"—under injunction, yes," I said, stepping onto the gangway with my hands visible and empty. "I heard. I read the notice. It's a very impressive piece of paper."

The lieutenant blinked water off his face. Behind him, on the covered quay, the crowd had gone still in that particular way crowds go still when they smell a story. Press lanterns swung toward us. Cassian Vole stood at the front of the good coats, dry as a ledger, and when our eyes met across forty feet of rain he smiled the smile of a man watching his investment mature.

"Gideon Dunmore," he called, and the name carried—over the officers, over the dockworkers, into the lantern light. He said it loudly enough that everyone understood it mattered. "You're a long way from salvage work. Come inside. Out of the weather. We can settle this like civilized people."

"We can settle it right here," I said.

And then I did the thing that four days of argument, three nights of bad sleep, and twenty-two years of my mother's careful silence had all been building toward.

I turned back to the Kestrel and said, "Sefa. Bring it."

She came up the gangway carrying the chest alone—she'd insisted, and no one argues with Sefa about lifting—and set it down on the wet stones of the quay between the officers and the crowd, between the lanterns and the dark, in the exact center of everything. The tarp came away. The seal of Vessany caught the nearest lantern light and threw it back dull and heavy, like an old coin refusing to shine for anyone new.

"That's Trust property," the lieutenant said, but his voice had lost its footing. "By writ—"

"It's Vessany property," I said. "It always was. Your trust just wrote the paperwork that made a country disappear so nobody would know the difference."

I knelt beside the chest. The rain came down on my neck like a verdict. And I felt, absurdly, my mother's pendant against my sternum—heavy, warm from my skin, the little brass key that had hung there since I was nine years old and never once been asked to explain itself until a double-bottomed chest at Greyfall Bend had explained it for me.

"Gideon." Cassian's voice again, closer now; he'd stepped out from under the eaves, letting the rain touch him for the first time, because he understood theater too and knew when the stage had shifted. It cost him visibly. "Whatever you think you know—whatever she told you—"

"She didn't tell me anything," I said. "That was the whole tragedy. She died keeping your secret so well that her own child spent five years thinking she'd simply chosen the river over me." I looked up at him, and the rain made him blur, and I was glad of it. "So I'm going to let her tell everyone instead."

The second compartment opened the way Sefa and I had practiced a hundred times downriver: pressure here, pivot there, the hidden seam sighing open with a sound like a held breath finally released. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against a century and then five more years, lay the treasury seals of a nation that officially had never existed, the shipping manifests of a colonial trust that had manufactured its extinction, and—on top, placed there by a woman who knew exactly what she was hiding and for whom—a single folded testimony, signed *M. Dunmore*, dated eleven days before the expedition that never came home.

I stood up. My legs were shaking. I let them.

Then I unfolded the pages, turned my back on Cassian Vole entirely, faced the lanterns and the dockworkers and the officers and the rain, and began to read my mother's voice aloud into the storm.

* * *

*"I am Miriam Dunmore, custodian of the Vessany archive, and if you are reading this, then I have failed to bring it home quietly, and I am asking you not to fail louder."*

My voice cracked on the first sentence. I steadied it. The monsoon hammered the customs house roof above us like applause or artillery—I honestly couldn't have told you which—and the crowd pressed closer under the colonnade, dockworkers shoulder to shoulder with officers, none of them moving, all of them listening.

*"The documents in this compartment prove what I could not prove while alive: that the extinction of Vessany was not history but transaction. The nation did not fall. It was purchased. The Colonial Salvage Trust's founding charter rests on a survey that never happened, a treaty that was never signed, and a treasury that was seized from a living people and renamed 'unclaimed assets.' I have seen the original seals. I have held them. They are real, and so was Vessany, whatever your maps say."*

A murmur went through the dockworkers—low, rising, the sound of people recognizing their own ledgers in someone else's crime. I kept reading.

*"My partner, Cassian Vole, knows all of this. He has known since Greyfall Bend. When I refused to help him sell the archive's location to the Trust's founders, he made arrangements of his own. The fever that took Oyelaran. The rockfall that took Petit. The 'bandits' on the return leg who took everyone else. I survived because I was worth more alive as a signature than dead as a witness. I have signed nothing. This is my signature instead."*

I looked up once, briefly, and found Cassian Vole standing in the rain ten feet away, and for the first time since I'd known of him—since the auction, since the gunboats, since the whole long pursuit upstream—he had stopped smiling. His face was doing arithmetic and losing.

*"To whoever finds this: I was a careful woman, and careful was not enough. Be loud. Be loud where they cannot buy the room. Be loud in front of people whose names are small enough to be beneath their notice, because those are the only witnesses left that money hasn't met."*

The rain fell. The roof thundered. Somewhere behind me I heard the lieutenant say, uncertainly, "Sir? Do we—should we stop this?" and heard no one answer him, because there was no honest answer, because stopping a dead woman's testimony in front of forty dockworkers and three newspapers is the kind of act that gets remembered longer than any injunction.

I read to the end. Her final line was short, and I made myself give it the same weight as all the others:

*"If my child ever reads this: I did not choose the river over you. I chose you over the truth, and it nearly buried us both. Come find me anyway. — M.D."*

I lowered the pages. My hands were steady now, which figured—they'd waited twenty-two years for this and weren't about to spoil it trembling.

The quay stayed silent for exactly as long as it takes a crowd to decide what kind of moment it's standing inside.

Then a dockworker—an old woman with rope-scarred hands and a Trust badge pinned crookedly to her oilskin—stepped forward out of the line and said, clear as a bell through the rain: "I loaded those 'unclaimed asset' barges for thirty years. Never knew what the seal meant. Now I do." She turned to face the officers. "So. Which of you is taking her statement?"

And that was the hinge of the whole night, though I didn't know it yet. Because the lieutenant looked at the old woman, and at the press lanterns, and at the chest, and at the testimony still dripping in my hands—and then past all of us, to where a tall figure in a gray inspector's coat had appeared at the top of the customs house steps, holding an umbrella he wasn't using, watching everything with the expression of a man who has just found the discrepancy he's been hunting for months and intends to enjoy it.

Inspector Halloran descended the steps slowly, deliberately, into the rain, and stopped beside the open chest.

"Dunmore," he said. "I believe the Trust owes you a receipt."
