## 22. Home Water

The river had changed its voice.

Gideon noticed it first thing every morning now, standing at the Kestrel's rail with tea going cold in their hands—the way the Sable moved through the season of its own abundance, fat and slow and glittering, carrying the monsoon's leavings out to sea in long brown ribbons. All summer it had been a knife, all autumn a rumor; now it was simply itself again, and so, Gideon thought, were they.

Six weeks since the customs house. Six weeks since the roof had thundered like a drumhead and Gideon's own voice—thin, cracked, unrecognizable to their own ears—had read Miriam Dunmore's testimony aloud into a room full of officers, pressmen, and dockworkers who should have been strangers but who had gone still as congregations. Six weeks since the word *fraud* stopped being a private grief and became a public fact, stamped and docketed and entered into the record of the Crown itself.

The Trust's charter was suspended. Its warehouses stood sealed with yellow paper across their doors, its barges idle at their moorings like sleeping animals. Inspector Halloran had become, improbably, a name people said with something like respect in the riverside taverns—a phrase Gideon overheard more than once: *the man who audited his own masters.* And Cassian Vole was in a cell at the tollgate he had once owned outright, awaiting trial, his respectable gray suits confiscated along with everything else, his face in the broadsheets wearing an expression Gideon recognized from the auction house: surprise that the world had declined to arrange itself around him.

But none of that lived in Gideon's mornings. What lived there was the river, and the smell of pitch smoke from the refit yard, and the sound of Sefa swearing cheerfully at a winch two decks down.

* * *

The Kestrel sat on the slipway at Ferrow's Yard like a patient undergoing surgery, her hull opened along forty feet of planking, her ribs exposed to the sun. She looked mortal like this. She looked old. Gideon had felt a twist of something protective the first time they'd seen her stripped bare—an absurd feeling for a barge cobbled together from three wrecks and a prayer—but Sefa had only laughed.

"She's earned her scars," the engineer had said, running a palm along a seam of silvered oak. "We're not hiding them. We're honoring them. New timber where she needs it, old timber where she wants it. That's how you keep a boat honest."

So the work went on, day after day, in the amber light of the drying season. Nils had drawn the new rigging plan on butcher paper and pinned it inside the wheelhouse, corrections and marginalia creeping up the edges in his small precise hand—little maps within the map, the way his mind worked, always nesting territory inside territory. Bram hauled timber with the yard's crew because, as he put it, sitting still made his hands remember things they shouldn't. And Sefa conducted the whole enterprise like a choir mistress, one arm elbow-deep in the bilge, the other pointing at some hapless apprentice holding a caulking iron wrong.

Gideon worked too. That was the strange grace of it—they had expected, in the weeks after the customs house, to be hollowed out. They had spent five years building a life designed to require nothing of them: no names given, no histories shared, no futures promised. A drifting man could not be sunk. But the chest had opened, and the testimony had been read, and the name *Dunmore* had been spoken aloud in a government building without the sky falling—and what remained afterward was not emptiness. It was weight. Good weight, the kind that ballasts a hull.

The salvage rights had come through on a Tuesday, delivered by a clerk who climbed the yard ladder in shoes far too fine for sawdust and handed over a document heavy with seals. *Salvage and River-Rights, granted to Gideon Dunmore, custodian of the Vessany estate, by order of the Provisional Commission.* Custodian. The word had landed in Gideon's chest like a coin dropped down a well, taking a long time to reach bottom.

Miriam had been custodian. The ledger's last page said so, in her upright, unhurried hand: *M. Dunmore, custodian, against all claimants.* She had carried the burden alone, and it had killed her—not the river, not the monsoon, but the loneliness of the keeping. Gideon had understood that loneliness intimately, had worn it like a second skin for years before knowing where they'd learned it.

They would not carry it the same way.

* * *

On the afternoon the new name-board arrived, the whole crew gathered at the slipway's edge.

Sefa had ordered it in secret—Gideon found this out when the crate came up from the paint shop and Nils, useless at dissembling, went pink to the ears. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a board of seasoned teak, letters carved deep and gilded: **KESTREL**. And beneath, smaller, in letters that caught the light and held it:

**OF THE DUNMORE LINE**

Gideon stood very still.

"It's not presumptuous, is it?" Sefa asked, suddenly uncertain, wiping her hands on a rag that only spread the grease further. "I mean—you fought so hard not to be a Dunmore. But you fought harder to be one, in the end. On your terms. I thought—" She gestured vaguely at the board, at the boat, at the whole shining afternoon. "I thought it belonged here."

Bram said nothing. He had a way of saying nothing that was louder than most men's speeches, and right now his silence was warm as a stove. Nils watched Gideon with the anxious attention he gave to charts in bad weather.

Gideon picked up the board. The wood was smooth, dense, alive under their palms. Their mother's name ran beneath their own like a keel beneath a deck—unseen, structural, bearing everything up.

"She used to say a boat's name is a promise," Gideon said finally. Their voice came out rough. "That you make it to the water, and the water holds you to it." They turned the board toward the crew. "She never told me whose promise I was. I spent half my life assuming it was a debt." A breath. "It's not. It's an inheritance. There's a difference, and I'm only just learning to spell it."

They hung the board themselves, driving the last nail with three blows, and when it swung true and the gilding flared in the low sun, nobody applauded—that wasn't the Kestrel's way—but Sefa pressed her fist briefly against Gideon's shoulder as she passed, and Bram nodded once, slow, like a man closing a ledger he'd kept open too long, and Nils smiled down at his boots as though he'd charted the whole moment in advance and it had come in exactly on bearing.

* * *

That evening they ate on deck, the first meal aboard since the refit began, off planks laid across two sawhorses because the galley was still gutted. The yard's other workers had drifted home; the river ran dark and murmurous beyond the slipway, and the first stars came out over the Sable headlands like rivets in a black hull.

Nils had drawn a map of the evening, in a sense—he'd produced, from somewhere, a bottle of plum brandy labeled in a language no one aboard read, and declared it salvaged, which by the new legal definition of the word it technically was. Bram drank his portion like medicine and then, unexpectedly, like pleasure. Sefa told the story of the winch that had nearly taken her arm in her dredging days, improving it with each telling until the winch had achieved something like sentience and malice. And Gideon sat among them with the brandy warming a line down their sternum and realized they were measuring time wrong.

That was the thing. For years they had counted in miles—*three hundred miles since the last port, forty miles since the last lie, six miles since the last time anyone said my name kindly.* Miles were safe. Miles could be run, escaped, put astern. But sitting there, with grease-stained fingers passing the bottle and lamplight swinging gently on its hook and four voices making a sound that was, unmistakably, *home*, Gideon caught themselves thinking in the old forbidden units. *This is the sixth week. This is the second month. Next spring, when the ice-melt raises the upper reaches, we'll run the Greyfall survey. In three years the Kestrel will be due a full re-plank.*

Years. Futures. Promises made to a horizon instead of away from one.

"You've gone quiet," Nils observed. "You go quiet when you're either planning or grieving. I've catalogued the difference. Your shoulders are wrong for grieving."

"Am I allowed to have secrets?"

"Not aboard," Sefa said serenely. "Crewman's prerogative."

Gideon looked at them—really looked, the way they'd once looked at the drowned archive hall at Greyfall Bend, at the camp markers still standing in the green dark, at the evidence of a life persisted in against all sense. Sefa with her burn-scarred forearms and her laugh like a dropped wrench. Bram, whose guilt had once been a closed compartment and who had paid it out in full at a Trust patrol post, trading his freedom for their head start, and who had come back to them in the spring thaw thinner and lighter both. Nils, seventeen going on ancient, who had run from a cartographer's guild and found, in a stolen journal's cipher, the mother he'd chosen over the family he hadn't.

"I was thinking," Gideon said, "that I know what I want to do with the rights."

Three faces turned.

"The commission granted us salvage along the whole middle reach. The Trust's old grounds—all the wrecks they wrote off as unrecoverable, all the sites they suppressed. There's a century of drowned history down there that they buried under paper." Gideon turned the bottle slowly in their hands. "Vessany didn't vanish. It was erased. And erasure leaves wreckage. Bellies of ships, flooded archives, whole neighborhoods under the silt at Greyfall. Somebody has to bring it up. Carefully. With the names attached."

"The Dunmore Line," Nils said softly, testing it. "Recovery and survey."

"Miriam started it," Gideon said. "Her route, her notes, her markers. We finish the map she died drawing. But we do it—" and here they heard their own voice take on the tone from the customs house, the one that had surprised them by existing, "—in the open. Every find logged publicly. Every claim filed. No more chests in the dark."

Bram grunted, which from Bram was a benediction. Sefa raised the bottle. "To the open river, then. And to hell with the dark."

They drank. Downstream, far off, a steam-whistle sounded—one long note, the signal of a night boat making the bend below Ferrow's, and the sound crossed the water and crossed the slipway and seemed, Gideon thought, to pass straight through the Kestrel's new planking as though the boat were already listening for it, already leaning toward the current, ready.

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their hammocks and the yard lamp had burned down to a red eye, Gideon stayed on deck with the ledger.

They had taken to reading it the way other people read scripture—a page or two, slowly, by lamplight. The Vessany script still resisted them; Nils was teaching himself the grammar from the documents recovered in the second compartment, and each week another passage surrendered. Tonight's page held a shipping manifest, decades old, listing cargo in a hand that looped and flourished: silks, dyes, instruments, books. Books, mostly. A nation that had shipped its treasury in paper.

At the bottom of the page, in the margin, someone had written a line in Vessany characters, and beneath it, in a different ink, a translation added later—Miriam's ink, Miriam's hand:

*What the river takes, the river keeps. What love keeps, the river cannot take.*

Gideon closed the ledger and held it against their chest for a moment, the way you'd hold a child, or a compass.

"Goodnight, Mum," they said aloud, quietly, to the dark water and the stars and the boat beneath them. It was the first time in five years the words had come without salt behind them. Just words. A channel, reopened, running clear.

Below the slipway, the river answered in its new voice—deep, unhurried, going about its ancient business—and the Kestrel tugged gently at her lines, as if impatient, as if certain, as if she already knew the shape of the miles ahead and the years beyond them, and found the distance good.
