## 1. Miles, Not Years

The engine spoke to him before the radio ever did.

Edmund Dunmore had learned its language over seven years and eleven thousand hours—the dry tick of a valve seating cold, the thin whistle in the intake that meant a gasket was thinking about quitting, the deep, contented thrum at two thousand RPM that said *all is well, all is well, all is well*. He trusted that voice more than any he'd heard from a human mouth since the winter of '29. The engine never lied. It only warned, or it failed, and either way it told you exactly where you stood.

He flew the Tuesday run out of Fort Calloway at first light, same as every week. Medicine for the Kettle River mission: quinine, iodine, sulfa in wax-sealed tins, a crate of evaporated milk for the children's ward. Mail bundled in oilcloth. A sack of seed potatoes lashed into the second seat because Mrs. Aldous at the trading post had begged, and because Edmund had long ago stopped pretending he couldn't be moved by begging. The de Havilland took the weight without complaint. She was an old Gipsy Moth converted to a freight nose, patched and re-patched, her fabric skin doped so many times she looked like a quilt. He called her *Wren* because she was small, stubborn, and sang anyway.

Below him the interior unrolled in green and brown and the silver threads of rivers, country so vast it made a man's troubles look like what they were—small, temporary, survivable if you kept moving. That was the arithmetic Edmund lived by. Miles, not years. You didn't count birthdays out here; you counted landings. A good landing was a day earned. A bad one was a lesson paid for in airframe.

He had a routine for everything. Preflight inspection performed in the same order every time, left to right, prop to tail, because ritual was a kind of armor. Fuel measured with a stick, not a gauge, because gauges lied and sticks didn't. Radio checks logged in a ledger with a fountain pen, his handwriting small and square, a bookkeeper's hand on a bush pilot's life. He filed flight plans nobody read and kept schedules nobody enforced, and this was deliberate. A man who was perfectly predictable was a man nobody wondered about. Nobody wondered about Edmund Dunmore. That was the whole architecture of his life, load-bearing and invisible, and he maintained it the way he maintained the Wren—daily, carefully, without sentiment.

Fort Calloway fell away behind him. Ahead, the river bent north toward the divide, and the morning haze burned off the muskeg in slow white ribbons.

His passenger today was a company man.

Edmund had taken him on reluctantly at the strip—a survey clerk named Pell, round-faced, sweating through a wool suit that had no business north of the railhead, clutching a leather dispatch case like it held his mother's ashes. Meridian Consolidated badge pinned to his lapel. Edmund had stared at that badge a moment too long before turning away to check the tie-downs.

"Company business?" he'd asked, flat.

"Geological survey," Pell said, pleased about it. "Upriver. I'm carrying reports back to the district office."

Now, forty minutes into the flight, Pell was talking. They always talked. Passengers treated a cockpit like a confessional, maybe because you couldn't see a pilot's face under the goggles and scarf, maybe because the drone of the engine made silence feel rude. Edmund answered in monosyllables and let the wind take the rest.

"—third season now, actually. Big operation. Crane's got forty men up there." Pell had to lean close to be heard. "Alden Crane? He's famous. Man surveyed half of British Columbia before he was thirty."

The name went through Edmund like a splinter finding old wood.

He adjusted his grip on the stick. Kept his eyes on the horizon, where the mountains were building themselves out of the haze, blue ridges stacked on blue ridges like pages of a book no one had finished reading.

"Surveyor," Edmund said. Not a question. Just placing the word down between them, careful as a man setting down something hot.

"Chief surveyor for the whole division." Pell rummaged in his case, produced a folded company newsletter, glossy paper, absurd this far from any city. "There's a piece about the expedition here somewhere. They're staking the whole upper valley. Meridian's putting serious money into it."

"I don't follow company news."

"Everybody follows company news. Company news is the only news." Pell laughed at his own joke, a wet, satisfied sound. He smoothed the newsletter on his knee. "Look—there's a photograph. That's Crane, there, center. Tall fella with the mustache."

Edmund did not look. He knew exactly what Alden Crane looked like. He had spent seven years knowing it—the precise angle of that jaw, the way the man's hands had rested flat and calm on the witness table while he described, in a clear and reasonable voice, everything he claimed to have seen the night the Fairchild went down with Tom Braddock aboard. *The aircraft was overloaded, Your Honor. I saw the loading myself. Mr. Dunmore ignored the weight limits. I told him so and he waved me off.*

Every word true except the ones that mattered.

"Fine country up there," Pell was saying, oblivious, folding the newsletter away again. "They say the upper valley's got homesteaders all through it. Little farms along the river flats. Must be strange for them, having forty surveyors camped in their backyard."

"Strange," Edmund agreed.

"You know the valley?"

"I fly over it."

Pell nodded as though this were an answer, which it was. Everyone flew over things. That was the arrangement the interior ran on—you passed above people's lives at ninety miles an hour and called it connection. Edmund had been passing above the valley for three years, dropping mail sacks to homesteaders who waved from the strips they'd hacked out of the timber, men and women whose names he knew from manifests and whose faces he knew from the air and whose stories he made a point of not learning. Names were how trouble found you. Faces were how trouble remembered you.

He checked his watch against the river bend below. On schedule. He was always on schedule. Seven years of being precisely where he said he'd be, doing precisely what he'd promised, asking nothing and owing nothing—that was the penance and the payment both. The manslaughter charge had followed him out of the coast cities like a smell he couldn't wash out of his coat; the papers had printed DUNMORE FLIES ON, TRIAL PENDING until the trial itself, and then the verdict, and then nothing, because after a verdict the papers lose interest in a man. But the trade didn't forget. Operators blacklisted him. Insurers refused him. So he'd come inland, where the map went soft and the law went softer, and built himself a route out of medicine runs and mail sacks and the occasional desperate passenger, serving settlements too small to ask questions and too grateful to care.

It was a good life. He told himself that weekly, the way you tell yourself a roof doesn't leak.

* * *

They put down at Kettle River just past ten. The mission strip was a hayfield with ambitions, and Edmund set the Wren down light as a dropped feather, touching on the exact spot he always touched, rolling to the exact fence post he always rolled to. Father Anselm came out across the grass in his shirtsleeves, waving, and a dozen mission kids materialized from the treeline the way kids do, running alongside the plane as it slowed, shouting the way they always shouted, as though a plane were still a miracle even though it came every Tuesday.

"Medicine's in the forward crates," Edmund said, unbuckling. "Milk's marked blue. There's a letter for you under the mail ties."

"You are a faithful man, Mr. Dunmore." Anselm said it easily, the way he said everything, and Edmund felt the word *faithful* pass through him like a draft through a broken window. Faithful to a schedule. That much was true.

"The sulfa's short this month," Edmund said. "Supply's tight on the coast. I'll bring double next week."

"We manage." Anselm was already handing crates down to the older boys. Then he paused, squinting past Edmund's shoulder at the passenger climbing stiffly out of the rear seat, wool suit wrinkled into topography, dispatch case hugged to his chest. "Your man looks like he's seen a ghost."

"He's a company clerk. Probably airsickness."

Pell had gone gray around the mouth during the landing and hadn't recovered. He stood on the grass now, looking at the mission—the whitewashed chapel, the vegetable rows, the children staring—and something in his face shifted, some small civilizing reflex firing at last. "This is where the medicine goes?" he asked. "Actual people?"

"It's where everything goes," Anselm said pleasantly. "Everything goes somewhere."

Pell turned to Edmund, and here it came, Edmund could feel it coming, the question passengers always arrived at eventually, the one that tried to turn a pilot into a person. "You've been flying this route a long time?"

"Seven years."

"That's—" Pell glanced at the mountains, at the mission, at the Wren with her quilted wings catching the sun. "That's a long time to be alone up here."

Edmund buckled the fuel cap. "I'm not alone. I'm flying."

He saw the words land wrong, saw Pell decide he was simple or strange or both, and let him decide it. Simplicity was camouflage. Strange was safe. The dangerous thing—the thing that could get a man found—was being interesting.

But walking back across the grass, Pell said one more thing, almost to himself, almost idly, the way men say the sentences that end up mattering:

"They say Crane's expedition's gone quiet. No radio check-in for nine days now. Company's talking about sending someone up to look." He shrugged, settling his case. "Not my department. I just carry the reports."

Edmund's hand stopped on the cowling.

Nine days.

He knew the schedule. Of course he knew the schedule—he knew every scheduled frequency in the interior the way other men knew the names of racehorses, because a pilot's survival lived in those frequencies, because silence on a scheduled band was the first symptom of every disaster this country had ever produced. Meridian's survey ran check-ins every evening at six, 4,860 kilocycles, a bored clerk's voice reading coordinates into the dusk. Edmund had never once listened. He'd made a discipline of not listening, the way a drunk makes a discipline of walking the far side of the street past the saloon.

Nine days of silence meant lost aerials, dead batteries, weather. Nine days of silence also meant worse, and the worse had a shape he knew intimately: a mountain, a fire, a field of wreckage with a name painted on the tail.

"Quiet," Edmund repeated.

"Rivers flood. Radios break." Pell smiled with the confidence of a man whose salary didn't depend on the answer. "Crane's probably too busy striking gold to file his reports."

He climbed back into the rear seat. Edmund stood a moment longer with his palm flat on the warm engine cowling, feeling the metal tick as it cooled, feeling his own heartbeat do something irregular beneath the routine of it.

Then he closed his eyes, briefly, and put the thought away where he put all the others—in the locked compartment aft of his conscience, the one labeled NOT MY CARGO—and swung up into the cockpit.

* * *

The return leg he flew high, above the haze, the Wren's little engine pulling steady and honest. Below, the valley opened along the river—the homestead valley, the one Pell had mentioned, the one Edmund crossed every fortnight and permitted himself to think of as scenery. He could see the strips from up here, pale scars in the dark timber, four of them, five, each one a family's years of axe-work and burning and drainage, each one a claim staked in sweat rather than paper. Smoke rose thin and domestic from chimneys. Someone had cut a new clearing since last month; the raw stumps caught the light like teeth.

He had landed at the big strip twice. Only twice. The Halloran place, three years ago, with a fevered child aboard, and the father had wrung Edmund's hand at the fence and said, *We won't forget this,* and Edmund had said, *No need to remember it,* and had taken off before the man could offer coffee, before warmth could become acquaintance, acquaintance become history, history become the kind of bond that a man could be summoned by, later, when being summoned would cost something.

That was the discipline. That was the whole religion of it. Miles, not years. Cargo, not causes. Fly over, touch down, lift off, leave no wake.

So why, at twelve thousand feet with the valley sliding away beneath him, did the phrase keep circling back like a hawk riding a thermal?

*Nine days.*

Why did he find himself doing the arithmetic he'd forbidden himself—Meridian's base at the railhead, the relay stations, the distances, the weather window closing as September leaned toward October, the dry season's last weeks when the whole interior turned to tinder and a single spark in the grass could eat a valley in an afternoon?

And why—this was the part that genuinely angered him, sitting there in his own cockpit with his own hands steady on his own controls—why did the name *Alden Crane* sit in his chest like a swallowed coal, still burning after all these miles and all these years?

He had built his exile the way he built an airstrip: cleared it, graded it, packed it down, posted it against trespassers. And in one morning a round-faced clerk with a company pin had walked straight through the fence, trailing newspaper clippings and a name, and now the ground wouldn't pack again. Edmund could feel the old surface cracked under him, hairline fractures radiating out from a single point.

Over the last ridge before Fort Calloway, he made a decision so small he could pretend afterward it wasn't one. He reached over and switched on the radio set—not to transmit, never to transmit, just to listen—and tuned the dial with two fingers to 4,860 kilocycles.

Static. The long, empty hiss of a frequency with no one home.

He listened for a full minute. The static said nothing, patiently, over and over, the way the country itself said nothing, and Edmund Dunmore, who had spent seven years perfecting the art of not hearing, sat in the cold clean roar of the slipstream and heard it anyway.

Then he switched the set off, dropped low over the railhead, and brought the Wren home.

That night, in the shack behind the hangar, he ate beans and bread standing up, washed the plate, wound the clock, and lay down on his cot with the ledger of his life balanced to the penny—flights flown, debts owed none, names known few.

At six o'clock, without deciding to, he was awake, listening to a silence on a frequency where a bored clerk should have been reading coordinates into the dusk.

Nine days now. Ten tomorrow, if nobody answered.

He stared at the ceiling beams until the lamp burned out, and somewhere in the dark he admitted the thing he'd been refusing since the Kettle River strip: that the company would post a notice within the week. That the notice would offer a contract. That the contract would bear a signature he knew.

And that when it did, he would tell himself it was just cargo.

He already knew how well that lie flew.
