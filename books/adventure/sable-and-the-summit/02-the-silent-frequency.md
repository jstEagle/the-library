## 2. The Silent Frequency

The radio at Fetterman's Strip had a habit of dying on Sundays, which Edmund had come to regard as the machine's one honest opinion: that nothing worth saying happened on a Sunday.

This Sunday it worked, and he almost wished it hadn't.

He was in the shack behind his hangar with a tin cup of coffee gone cold an hour ago, feet up on the second chair—the one nobody ever sat in—when the scheduled check-in window opened at nine sharp. The Meridian survey expedition, call sign *Summit Base*, was due to answer the company's weekly hail from somewhere up the Kestrel River drainage, four hundred miles of green nothing north and east of anywhere Edmund cared to be. He wasn't supposed to be listening. He had no contract with Meridian Consolidated, no interest in their business, and seven years of carefully constructed reasons to keep it that way. But the interior was a small country at night, when the frequencies were empty, and a man who lived alone learned to keep one ear on the voices of the world even while telling himself he wanted none of them.

The company operator in Fort Aldous called twice. Then a third time, slower, the way a man knocks on a door he's beginning to suspect is empty.

*Summit Base, Summit Base, this is Meridian Consolidated Operations, Fort Aldous, do you copy? Over.*

Static. The long, grainy static of high pressure sitting over the range like a lid.

Edmund drank his cold coffee and told himself it was none of his affair. Companies lost touch with their expeditions all the time—antennas iced, generators flooded, operators wandered off to fish. The interior ate radios for breakfast. Half the strips he served had transmitters held together with baling wire and prayer, and he'd carried spare valves in his mail bags more than once because some homesteader's wife needed to reach a doctor and the set wouldn't wake up.

But scheduled check-ins weren't homesteader radios. Meridian ran its surveys like a bank runs its vaults. Weekly position reports, weather, personnel status, all of it logged in triplicate down in Fort Aldous by men whose entire careers depended on knowing where every dollar and every body was at all times. Edmund had flown enough freight for enough companies before the fall to know the breed. They didn't miss check-ins. Missing a check-in meant something had gone wrong enough that somebody in a head office was already drafting a memo.

At nine forty the Fort Aldous operator signed off with a flatness that confirmed it: *No contact with Summit Base. Will attempt again 0900 Tuesday. Out.*

Edmund sat in the quiet after the carrier wave died, listening to the tick of the shack cooling, the far-off complaint of a raven over the spruce line. His coffee had a skin on it. He drank it anyway.

It wasn't his affair.

He washed the cup, swept the floor that didn't need sweeping, and went out to the hangar where the *Lark* sat on her wheels in the slanted morning light, and began the pre-flight inspection he'd already done yesterday, because his hands needed something true to hold onto.

*

The trouble with living alone seven years was that there was no one to tell you when you were lying to yourself. You had to catch it alone, usually at two in the morning, staring at the ceiling of a bunkhouse room that smelled of dope and fabric and engine oil, and the lie would be standing there in the dark with its hat in its hands, patient as weather.

The lie this week wore the face of indifference.

He flew his routes. Medicine out to the mission at Two Rivers on Monday, mail and a crate of bootlaces—bootlaces, ordered by name, by size—to the Halvorsens' strip on Wednesday. He kept his altitudes conservative and his landings textbook and his conversations with his passengers short and pleasant and entirely without content, and if his eyes drifted north toward the Kestrel drainage whenever the haze lifted, well. A pilot looks at the horizon. That's what pilots do.

Thursday, coming back from Two Rivers low over the river fog with a half tank and a headache, he caught himself doing the arithmetic without meaning to. Summit Base had last confirmed on schedule eleven days ago. Eleven days. A party of—what had the passenger said, back in chapter one of this whole business, the young geologist he'd ferried to Fort Aldous in June, chattering the whole flight about the big survey going in upriver—nine men? Twelve? Twelve men, eleven days silent, in country where September came early and came mean.

Twelve men was not his affair either. He'd learned the hard way what happened when Edmund Dunmore decided other people's troubles were his to carry. There was a grave outside a town he never flew over anymore that could testify to that, if graves testified.

He landed at Fetterman's, tied down the Lark, and found the telegram waiting under a rock on his doorstep, weighted against the wind the way telegrams always were, as though the words inside might otherwise blow away and spare him.

*

MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED MINING AND DEVELOPMENT STOP OPERATIONS DIVISION STOP RE SURVEY EXPEDITION SUMMIT BASE KESTREL DRAINAGE STOP LAST SCHEDULED CONTACT SEPT 9 STOP COMPANY REQUIRES CHARTER PILOT EXPERIENCED INTERIOR CONDITIONS FOR PROVISIONS FLIGHT DEPARTING FORT ALDOUS OCT 2 STOP RATE NEGOTIABLE PLUS RETAINER STOP REPLY BY WIRE STOP HOLLIS VANE DIRECTOR OF OPERATIONS

He read it three times standing in his own doorway with the wind pulling at his collar, and the first two times meant nothing, and the third time stopped his heart the way a hand on a throttle stops an engine.

Hollis Vane.

Most people would have read that name and seen nothing but a title. Director of Operations. A signature block. But Edmund had spent four days in a courtroom seven years ago learning exactly what certain names looked like when they were written down, and Hollis Vane's had been among them—not on the charge sheet, not on the witness list, but in the gallery, third row, gray suit, taking notes in a leather book with the calm of a man auditing a harvest. Edmund had never learned what he wrote in that book. He'd only known, with the animal certainty of the accused, that whatever it was, it was being written about him, and that the man writing it did not once look away.

Vane hadn't spoken at the trial. He hadn't needed to. He'd been there the way gravity is present at a falling—everywhere in the result, nowhere in the record. And afterward, when the verdict came down and the sentence got suspended into something worse than prison, a fine and a license review and a quiet blacklisting that followed Edmund from company to company until no one would sign him, he'd heard the shape of Vane's name in the pauses of men who were declining to hire him. *We've heard things. Nothing personal, Dunmore. You understand how it is.* He understood. Somebody had written it in a leather book, and the book had made the rounds.

And now the book had turned its pages toward him again, and out of it slid a contract.

He didn't wire a reply that night. He built a fire instead, though it wasn't cold enough to need one, and sat watching it eat birch logs down to orange lace while the argument went around and around in him like a dog circling before lying down.

Refuse it. Of course refuse it. The rate would be good—Meridian paid well, that was the one thing everyone agreed on—and God knew the Lark needed work she couldn't afford; her engine had been eating oil all summer and the fabric on the left wing was going soft at the trailing edge, and a rebuild would cost him most of a year's flying. But the money was the hook, not the reason. The reason was twelve men silent in the mountains, and the reason underneath that, the one he refused to look at straight-on, was that Meridian Consolidated did not send for Edmund Dunmore by accident. Somewhere in Fort Aldous a file existed with his name on it, and someone had gone looking through it, and Hollis Vane—or someone working under Vane's eye—had chosen him. Chosen the disgraced pilot. Chosen the man with no company, no references, no alternatives, who could be paid enough not to ask questions and ruined too thoroughly to ever tell any.

They knew what they were buying when they bought him. That was the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone. Not his skill—they could have any of a dozen younger pilots for less. They were buying his silence, pre-priced by a courtroom, and every mile he flew for them would be a mile further down a road he'd sworn off seven years ago.

So: refuse it. Wire back *regretfully declined*, go back to medicine runs and mail sacks, let the survey be the company's problem and the missing men be the company's dead, if dead they were.

Except.

Except that at two in the morning, staring at the ceiling, the dog finally lay down and Edmund finally admitted what the fire hadn't burned out of him: that twelve men were still twelve men. That somewhere up the Kestrel there might be a camp with a broken generator and a busted ankle and a radio that wouldn't wake, men waiting on a voice that wasn't coming, and the only difference between them and him was luck and distance. That he had spent seven years measuring his life in miles flown precisely so he would never have to measure it in anything else—in debts unpaid, in names unsaid, in the sound a judge's gavel makes when it falls on a man who maybe deserved it and maybe didn't and will never get to know which. Miles were clean. Miles asked nothing of a man except that he keep flying them.

And the telegram lay on his table like a compass needle that had stopped swinging, pointing north, pointing upriver, pointing at the one direction he'd spent seven years not looking.

*

Saturday he flew the mail run to the Halvorsens' and back under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the whole way he argued with a dead man's memory, because that was the other thing about seven years: the dead don't update their positions.

Walter Finch had been his instructor, then his friend, then the man in the right-hand seat the day everything ended, and Walter used to say that a pilot's conscience was just a fuel gauge pointed inward—that it read empty long before the engine quit, and the art of the thing was trusting the gauge over the noise. Edmund had trusted the noise instead, that day. Everyone else's noise. The schedule, the weather-minimizing voice on the phone, his own tired want to be home. And the gauge had been right, and Walter had paid for the lesson, and the court had decided the payment was Edmund's to make forever.

He thought about that sometimes as a debt. More often, lately, he thought about it as a theft: that the crash had taken Walter, and the trial had taken his name, and what was left flying these routes was a man-shaped absence doing careful landings.

The Lark droned north along the river, and below him the valley country unrolled—homestead clearings stitched along the water like buttons on a coat, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the still air, a horse moving through a field, children's laundry frozen stiff on a line. Real places. Real people who waved at his shadow the way they always did, because the mail plane meant the world hadn't forgotten them.

One of those valleys sat under the Kestrel's tributaries. One of those coats of buttons belonged to the country Meridian had sent twelve surveyors into, and now Meridian wanted Edmund Dunmore to fly supplies to the men who'd gone in mapping it.

Supplies. Or whatever else was in the crates he wouldn't be allowed to inspect.

He landed, delivered the mail, drank tea he didn't taste, and flew home with the argument unresolved and the sky lowering behind him like a held breath.

*

Sunday night the wind came around to the north and brought the first real cold with it, rattling the stovepipe, and Edmund sat at his table with the telegram smoothed flat under the lamp and a blank reply form beside it, and understood that he had already decided, and that the decision disgusted him, and that he was going to make it anyway.

Not yes. Not yet. He wasn't signing anything, wasn't flying anywhere for them, wasn't putting his hands back on the wheel of that particular machine. But he was going to wire Fort Aldous and ask questions—rate, cargo manifest, flight plan, insurance, the ordinary armor of a careful man—and asking questions was a door, and doors opened from both sides, and he knew it.

Because here was the truth he'd circled all week and finally had to land on: he could refuse the contract, but he couldn't refuse the silence. Eleven days of it now, soon twelve. It had gotten into him the way cold gets into a cabin—through every crack at once—until he could think of nothing else. Somewhere north of him, past the last button on the last valley, there was a frequency where voices should have been and weren't, and Edmund Dunmore, who had built a whole life out of not listening, could not stop hearing it.

He picked up the pencil. His hand was steady, which surprised him; he'd expected it to shake.

To: Meridian Consolidated, Operations Division, Fort Aldous. Re your wire. Pilot available for consultation re charter terms. Require full cargo manifest, route plan, and aircraft condition report before any commitment. Reply by wire.

Consultation. The word of a man keeping his options open and his conscience closed, dressed up in paperwork. He wrote it out in his small square hand, sealed it, and set it under the rock on the step for the morning plane, and stood a moment in the wind looking north, where the stars over the range were hard and white and indifferent as nail heads.

Behind him in the shack, the radio hissed softly to itself on the empty band, the way it did between voices—a sound like the interior breathing, or like something up there in the dark, very far away, trying and failing to be heard.

He went inside and did not sleep, and in the morning the wire went out, and two days later the answer came back, and it was not the answer he expected.

The terms were generous past all sense. The retainer alone was triple the going rate. And at the bottom of the page, beneath the typed figures, a single handwritten line in ink gone brown with age, in a hand he recognized the way you recognize a scar:

*Dunmore — Some debts can be settled at altitude. — H.V.*
