## 3. A Name He Buried

The rain came in off the coast at dusk, the way it always did, and Edmund Dunmore stood in the open door of his hangar watching it flatten the dust on the strip until the whole world smelled like wet gravel and engine oil. Somewhere behind him the radio crackled with the evening weather broadcast—a woman's voice reading wind speeds and cloud ceilings for a country he had memorized so thoroughly he no longer needed to listen. He listened anyway. It was the closest thing he had to a conversation most nights.

Seven years. He counted them the way other men counted money, in a ledger he kept behind his eyes. Seven years since the hearing. Seven years since a man in a grey suit had stood up in a room that smelled of floor wax and said, *I surveyed that ridge myself, and I can state with professional certainty that the pilot's approach was reckless and below safe clearance.* Seven years since Alden Crane's testimony had gone into the record like a nail into a coffin lid, and Edmund had stopped being Edmund Dunmore, pilot, and become Edmund Dunmore, the man who killed Danny Farrow.

He had a system for the anniversary. He always had. He worked it. There was always work, if you were willing to fly into weather that made other men shake their heads, willing to haul vaccines and mail and the occasional coffin into strips so short they were more suggestion than fact. The interior was full of people who needed things carried and didn't ask questions about who carried them. That was the whole arrangement. That was the deal he'd struck with the world: *I will be useful, and in exchange you will let me be no one.*

The radio crackled again. Not the weather this time.

"—repeat, Dunmore, do you copy? Meridian Consolidated, regarding the survey contract—"

He crossed the hangar in four strides and turned the set down until the voice was an insect trapped under glass. He knew what it wanted. He'd known for three days, since the letter had come in on the mail plane with its heavy cream envelope and its embossed letterhead, and he had read it once standing in this same doorway and then fed it to the stove.

But the voice kept talking under the static, patient as rain, and Edmund found he was standing there with his hand on the volume knob, listening anyway.

*—generous compensation—* *—expedition resupply, two runs, possibly three—* *—signed on behalf of the directorate by A. R. Crane, Field Operations—*

He shut the radio off entirely.

* * *

That night he didn't sleep. That was not unusual either. What was unusual was where his mind went, because for seven years he had kept the crash in a room in his head with the door shut, and he visited it only on the worst nights, and tonight the door stood open before he'd even decided to walk through it.

The Talus River valley, late summer, seven years ago. The light going gold and long across the water. Danny Farrow in the right-hand seat, twenty-three years old, laughing about the girl in the trading post who'd promised to teach him to dance if he ever stayed in one place long enough to learn. The load was light—mail, a crate of nails, a medicine order for the mission. The weather was the kind of clear they wrote songs about in the south, which was why nobody ever suspected the wind.

The wind came off the glacier field at two o'clock in the afternoon like a hand shoving a door. Every pilot on the river knew about the Talus downdrafts. Every pilot respected them. Edmund had flown that valley two hundred times, and he had done what he always did: he'd come in high, wide of the ridge, and let the river current carry him down to the strip.

What he hadn't known—what nobody had known—was that the survey party had strung a cable across the gorge that week. A survey cable, for their instruments, anchored to the ridge on one side and a dead spruce on the other, and unmarked, because Alden Crane's crew had been in a hurry and the regulations about marking aerial obstructions were, in Crane's own later words, "a formality for a valley nobody uses."

Edmund never saw it. He heard it—a sound like a rifle shot, a shriek of parted wire—and then the cable was wrapped around the port wing strut, tearing it half away, and the plane was coming down and there was no strip, no field, nothing under him but the river and the rocks, and Danny was not laughing anymore, Danny was saying *Ed, Ed, Ed* in a voice that got smaller and smaller as the water came up.

Edmund put it down in the only place there was to put it down. He put it down as well as any man could have, with half a wing gone and forty feet of altitude, and the plane hit the shallows and rolled, and when the water stopped coming through the broken windshield and Edmund clawed his way out onto the gravel bar with his arm hanging wrong, Danny Farrow was still strapped in his seat, and the river was very quiet, and the survey cable lay across the wreck like a black snake that had finished eating.

He had told the truth at the hearing. That was the thing he could not stop telling himself, seven years on, in the small hours: *I told the truth, and it did not matter.* He'd described the cable. He'd described the downdraft. He'd said, over and over, that he'd flown the approach the way he'd flown it two hundred times, and that the obstruction was unmarked and unlawful and that any pilot alive would have gone down the same way.

And then Alden Crane had stood up in his grey suit, with his surveyor's calm and his voice like a level being read, and said the cable had been marked. Said he'd seen the warning flags himself, on the ridge, the morning of the flight. Said he could not account for why the pilot had failed to observe them, but that in his professional judgment the approach had been reckless, below safe clearance, and that the death of Daniel Farrow was, tragically but clearly, the result of pilot error.

There had been no flags. Edmund had crawled over every yard of that ridge himself, twice, on a broken arm, looking for them. There had been a stake or two, maybe, long since pulled, or never driven, or driven and never flagged—Crane's crew had been three weeks behind schedule and Crane had not wanted the delay of a formal obstruction report. Edmund knew that. He had known it in his bones. But he couldn't prove it, and Crane could lie with a surveyor's precision, and the hearing had taken four days, and at the end of it a man who had never seen the inside of Edmund's cockpit had read a finding into the record, and Edmund Dunmore had walked out of that room carrying a manslaughter conviction and a suspended license and a name that meant *killer* in every river town from the coast to the divide.

He hadn't fought the appeal. That was the part that shamed him worse than the conviction. He'd been twenty-nine, broke, and hollowed out, and the lawyer had told him the odds, and Edmund had looked at the odds and looked at the money he didn't have and looked at Danny Farrow's mother sitting in the front row with her hands folded like a woman at a church she no longer believed in, and he had said, *I'll go.*

He told himself it was penance. Some nights he almost believed it. Most nights he knew it was just another word for running, and he had been running north ever since, measuring his exile in miles flown, in drums of fuel burned, in strips landed on and lifted off from and never once called home.

* * *

Morning came grey and dripping. Edmund was at the hangar before six, doing the preflight inspection on the old Norseman with a flashlight in his teeth, because work was the only prayer he had and he said it every day.

He was halfway through checking the port landing gear when he heard the truck.

It came up the strip slow, tires hissing on wet gravel, and stopped at the edge of the apron, and a man got out who did not belong to any river town Edmund knew. City coat. City shoes ruined by the mud, which the man clearly resented. He stood at a respectful distance while Edmund finished the inspection, and then he held up a cream envelope the twin of the one in Edmund's stove.

"Mr. Dunmore. My name is Pell. I represent Meridian Consolidated."

"I know what you represent. I got the letter. I burned the letter."

"I'm aware of the letter." Pell smiled the way a man smiles when he has been paid to smile through worse. "Mr. Crane asked me to convey his personal regard, and to extend a revised offer."

Edmund set the flashlight down on the wheel strut, slowly, because his hand wanted to do something faster. "Crane."

"Alden R. Crane, Field Operations Director. He's running the survey personally." Pell said it as though it were a credential, a medal. "The expedition has missed two scheduled check-ins. The company is prepared to pay for a resupply flight at double the original rate, with a completion bonus on delivery. Cash, on the strip, before you take off."

"Double." Edmund picked up the flashlight. "Tell him I said no twice."

"Mr. Crane said you'd say that." Pell produced a second envelope from inside his coat, thinner, and held it out. "He asked me to give you this regardless. He said you'd understand."

Edmund should have refused it. He knew that even as he took it, because his hands had already reached, the way a man's hands reach for a letter from a dead friend's name on the return address. Pell got back in his truck and drove away through the puddles, and Edmund stood alone on the apron with the rain coming down and opened the envelope with his thumbnail.

One page. Crane's handwriting, still narrow and exact, the handwriting of a man who lettered maps for a living:

*Dunmore — I remember the valley. I remember the river. I remember more than you think I do. The offer stands, and it will stand until the expedition is found. Some debts are best paid in person. — A.C.*

Edmund read it three times. The rain dripped off the Norseman's wing and ran down the back of his collar and he did not notice. *I remember more than you think I do.*

What did that mean? That Crane remembered the cable? That he remembered the ridge without flags, the crew three weeks behind, the report that was never filed? That he had stood in that hearing room with all of it folded up behind his surveyor's calm and lied anyway, in complete sentences, with a mother sitting in the front row?

Or did it mean something smaller and crueler—that he remembered Edmund, remembered him as a man who could be handled, who had gone quietly, who had taken the conviction like a dose of medicine and disappeared north, and who could therefore be bought the same way he'd been broken?

Edmund walked to the burn barrel by the hangar door and held Crane's letter over the ash. He held it there a long time. His hand was steady, but something behind his ribs was not, something seven years old and scarred over that had just been pressed hard, and it came up in him not as grief, which he had worn smooth as river stone, but as anger—hot, specific, and entirely new.

Because he realized, standing in the rain with the paper curling, that he was not angry the way a man is angry at an insult. He was angry the way a man is angry at a debt. Seven years he had carried the ledger. Seven years he had paid and paid—his name, his license, his home, every year of his life since—into an account that had been opened on a lie, and the man who had opened it was now sending him perfumed letters asking him to fly supplies into the same mountains, under the same signature, as though none of it had ever happened. As though Edmund were a tool in a shed that Crane could take down whenever a job needed doing.

*Some debts are best paid in person.*

The letter caught at the corner. Edmund watched the flame take it, watched Crane's exact handwriting blister and blacken, and found that he was breathing hard, there in the rain, like a man who had just carried something heavy up a hill.

He went inside and pulled the original offer out of the stove, unburned, because he had never actually lit it—only shoved it in and walked away, and now he understood why. He smoothed it out on the workbench under the lamp. *Resupply flight, Talus River headwaters. Compensation as stated. Signed, A. R. Crane, Field Operations.*

Double the rate. Cash on the strip. And at the end of the flight, a man in a grey suit standing in some company camp, close enough to touch.

Edmund sat down on the crate by the bench and looked at his hands, at the scar across the left palm where the broken windshield glass had opened it seven years ago, and he thought about Danny Farrow's mother, and about the word *in person,* and about how a man could spend seven years burying a name and then feel it start, all at once, clawing its way back up through the dirt.

He wasn't going to answer the offer. He told himself that, firmly, the way you tell yourself you won't look at a wound.

But the Norseman's engine was due for an overhaul he couldn't afford, and the cylinders were eating oil, and the strip at Talus headwaters was three hours' flying time from the last place in the world Alden Crane had ever seen him, and somewhere under the anger, under the shame, under the seven years, a small cold voice that sounded unpleasantly like Alden Crane's was saying: *You'll go. You'll want to see my face when you do. And I know it, and you know it.*

The rain kept falling. Edmund folded the offer into his jacket pocket, over his heart, where a man carries the things he hasn't decided about yet, and turned off the lamp, and stood in the dark of the hangar listening to the river of water coming off the roof, and did not sleep again.
