## 4. Contract of Convenience

The invoice came on a Tuesday, folded inside a parts catalog Edmund hadn't ordered. He read it twice at the kitchen table of his shack above the airfield, coffee going cold beside his elbow, because the numbers didn't make sense the first time and barely made sense the second.

A new cylinder assembly. Valves. A magneto, factory-sealed, not the rebuilt junk the trade houses in Fort Nelson pushed on bush pilots who couldn't afford to ask questions. Gaskets, spark plugs, a propeller hub—enough to tear down the old de Havilland's engine to the crankcase and build it back honest. Enough to keep him flying for another five years, maybe seven, if he treated her right.

Seven years. The number sat there on the page like a man waiting to be recognized.

He set the invoice down and looked out the window at the strip. Morning fog was burning off the willows along the creek, and the Fox—his Fox, the little Moth he'd nursed through six winters of hard country—sat tied down with her cowling off, engine exposed like something surgically opened and left for the weather. He'd been nursing that engine along since spring. A whisper of metal in the oil screen two weeks ago. A vibration at cruise RPM that lived in the stick and wouldn't leave. He knew what it meant. Every pilot in the interior knew what it meant. You could fly on borrowed metal for a while, but the bill always came due over the worst possible terrain, at the worst possible hour, with the worst possible passenger watching your face.

He had maybe forty hours left in her. Maybe less if he pushed.

And Meridian Consolidated was offering him the parts free, plus wages that would buy him outright for a year, to fly one load upriver.

Edmund made himself another coffee he didn't want and told himself he wasn't thinking about it.

* * *

He lasted four days.

On the fifth day he walked into the company agent's office in town—a plank building that smelled of coal oil and carbon paper, with a moose head over the filing cabinets that had been there longer than anyone could remember—and found the same executive from the trial correspondence waiting for him, a man named Halloran, neat as a ledger line, hands folded on the desk like they'd never done anything worse than sign documents.

"Pilot Dunmore." Halloran smiled the way a bank teller smiles. "We're glad you reconsidered."

"I haven't reconsidered anything," Edmund said. "I'm here about terms."

"Of course."

"The parts come first. Before I sign. I inspect them, I install them, and my own ship is airworthy before I carry so much as a nail for you people."

Halloran slid a paper across the desk without looking down at it, the way a man moves a piece across a board he's already won. "Crated and delivered by Friday. And the wage schedule is there, with the bonus clause. Full payment on delivery at the survey camp, upriver."

"And if I don't deliver?"

"Then you don't get the bonus." Halloran's smile didn't move. "But you'll deliver. You strike me as a man who finishes what he starts."

Edmund signed. His signature looked strange to him on the page—smaller than he remembered it, or perhaps just older—and when Halloran countersigned below it with a fountain pen that scratched like a claw, Edmund felt something close, some door he'd kept barred for seven years, swing open an inch and let in a draft.

It's just cargo, he told himself, walking back out into the street. Drums and crates and chain. Fly it up, set it down, fly home. Men haul freight into the interior every day of the week and sleep fine afterward.

He almost believed it. That was the trick of it—he discovered later—that the lie didn't have to be good. It only had to be repeated.

* * *

The parts arrived Friday, exactly as promised, which should have been his first warning. Nothing in the interior arrived on time except trouble.

He spent the weekend tearing the Fox down in the open air, sleeves rolled, hands black to the wrist, and by Sunday night she had a new heart. He ran her up on the tie-downs Monday morning and the engine spoke with a voice he hadn't heard from her in years—clean, even, no whisper of metal, no vibration living in the stick. He taxied her the length of the strip twice just to listen.

She'd never flown better. That was the hook of it, he understood now: they hadn't bought his labor. They'd bought his wings. A man whose ship is dying will sign anything to keep flying, and Meridian knew it the way a fisherman knows the weight on the line before the rod ever bends.

* * *

Loading began Wednesday at dawn.

The freight came out to the field in two trucks under tarpaulins, escorted by a company man named Bragg who chewed tobacco and answered questions with shrugs. Edmund counted the manifest against the load while the hired hands wrestled it aboard.

Fuel drums—twelve of them, full, stenciled MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED in white letters. Crates, long ones, packed with straw, heavy enough that four men grunted moving each one. Coils of new chain, bright galvanized links that caught the early light. Tool chests, rope, canvas bundles, tentage. And at the bottom of the pile, wrapped in sacking and wired shut, three smaller boxes that Bragg put aboard personally, without help, and lashed down where Edmund couldn't reach them without unloading half the aircraft.

"What's in those?" Edmund asked.

"Company property," Bragg said.

"I'm the pilot. Everything on this ship is my responsibility. If I'm carrying dynamite, I want to know where it sits and how it's packed."

"It's not dynamite."

"Then what is it?"

Bragg spat over the wheel of the truck, unhurried, and looked at Edmund with the flat patience of a man being paid by the day. "Company property," he said again. "You got a contract says you haul what we give you. It don't say you get to read it."

Edmund stood there with the manifest in his hand and felt the draft from that opened door move through him again, colder this time. He thought about walking back to the office, tearing the contract in half, eating the loss on the parts. He thought about it honestly, for a full ten seconds, standing in the dew with the sun coming up over the willows.

Then he thought about the oil screen, and the whisper of metal, and forty hours of borrowed time, and he folded the manifest and put it in his jacket pocket.

"Weight and balance is mine," he said. "I'll arrange the load how I want it."

"Fine by me," Bragg said. "Long as it all goes up the river."

* * *

They finished loading by mid-morning. Edmund arranged the drums aft, the long crates amidships, the chain low and centered, and the three wired boxes where he could see them in his mirrors—which was nowhere, because the Fox carried no mirrors, but he placed them anyway, forward of the drums, where his eye would fall on them every time he turned around in the cockpit.

He did his preflight the way he always did it, slow and complete, walking the ship from spinner to tail, and the whole time his hands checked wires and struts and tires his mind kept circling back to the wired boxes like a tongue returning to a broken tooth.

Company property. Not dynamite. Then what?

Chain, he told himself. Chain and tools and fuel. Survey parties need chain for chaining lines, that's the whole trade—chain and transit and stakes. There's nothing sinister in chain. There's nothing sinister in drums of gas or crates of tentage or a man named Halloran signing papers with a fountain pen that scratches.

Except that surveyors don't usually ship their own escort. Except that the last expedition up that river had gone silent as a grave. Except that Halloran's name had been on the letter offering him the contract, and Halloran had been in the courtroom seven years ago, third row, taking notes, and men like Halloran didn't take notes for their health.

He climbed into the cockpit and buckled the harness and sat for a moment with his hand on the throttle, the engine ticking as it warmed, and let himself say the sentence out loud, alone, under the wind noise, because saying it aloud was the only way to hear how thin it was.

"It's just cargo."

The engine answered with its clean new voice. The wired boxes sat behind him in the fuselage, silent as stones.

* * *

He took off into a sky the color of hammered tin and turned north up the river valley, and the Fox climbed like she hadn't climbed since she was young, and for twenty minutes—twenty whole minutes—Edmund Dunmore felt something that might have been happiness, or might have been only the absence of dread, which in the interior passes for the same thing.

The river unspooled below him, brown and muscular, braiding around gravel bars, sliding past muskeg and burn scars and the occasional roof of a trapper's cabin with its thread of woodsmoke standing straight in the still air. He knew this country the way other men knew their own kitchens. He'd measured it in miles for seven years. Miles, not years—that had been the bargain, and he'd kept his side of it faithfully.

At the confluence where the river bent east he passed over the homesteader strip—the last good field, the one with the mowed grass and the wind sock and the cluster of roofs—and he saw, even from eight hundred feet, the surveyor's stakes driven into the meadow at the field's edge, orange-flagged, marching in a straight line toward the water. Somebody had been busy. Somebody with chain and transit and a warrant of some kind, real or invented.

His eye went to the stakes and then, unwillingly, to the wired boxes behind him.

Chaining lines, he told himself. That's all chain is for.

The river narrowed. The hills closed in. The country went from green to grey-green to the iron colours of high ground, and the current below quickened until it shone like poured pewter, and somewhere ahead, beyond the next bend and the next range, was a camp that had stopped answering its radio, and a man named Crane whose testimony had built the walls of Edmund's exile brick by brick, and a contract with a bonus clause payable on delivery.

He flew on. What else was there to do? The parts were installed. The ink was dry. The cargo rode behind him, whatever it was, and the mountains rose ahead, white-shouldered already though it was barely autumn, and the dry season's last weeks were burning down to nothing like a lamp running out of oil.

Somewhere over the divide, the engine note changed—not failing, nothing so merciful as failing, just a hesitation, a stutter, a single missed beat like a heart skipping—and Edmund's hands moved before his fear did, mixture rich, carb heat on, eyes flicking to the gauges, and the engine caught itself again and droned on, steady, steady, as if nothing had happened.

As if nothing had happened. That was the phrase his whole life was built on now, he realized. Seven years of as-if-nothing-had-happened, and here he was hauling company freight into the silence where it had all begun, telling himself the oldest lie in the book one more time.

The wired boxes rode behind him in the dark of the fuselage.

He didn't look back. Looking back, he suspected, was a luxury he'd given up the right to years ago—and besides, the mountains were ahead, and the fuel was finite, and the last good field was falling away behind him into the haze, its orange stakes glinting once in the sun before the ridge took it, and then the river, and then everything else.
