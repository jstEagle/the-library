## 5. The Last Good Field

The strip at Kettle Creek announced itself the way all good bush strips did: by refusing to be obvious.

Edmund saw it first as a seam of paler green stitched through the valley floor, a mowing scar in the wilderness where the homesteaders had cleared spruce and hauled stumps for three summers to make themselves four hundred yards of level ground. From two thousand feet it looked like nothing—a farmer's field, a place where hay grew. That was the point. Meridian's pilots flew over it twice a month and never marked it on their charts, because a strip that wasn't on a chart couldn't be taxed, regulated, or taken away.

He throttled back and let the Waco settle into its descent, running his pre-landing ritual the way he ran everything—by rote, by rhythm, by the small liturgies that kept a man's mind from wandering into country it shouldn't. Flaps set. Mixture rich. Fuel selector checked twice, because checking it once was how men died and checking it twice was how they only felt like fools. The wind sock at the field's north end hung lazy and half-full, a light breeze out of the southwest, quartering tailwind if he landed uphill. He'd land downhill anyway. Uphill meant short. Short meant trees.

The wheels touched at the midpoint with a soft double thump, grass whispering under the fuselage, and the plane rolled out through knee-high timothy toward the cluster of buildings at the field's edge: a log house with a shake roof, a barn built from salvaged crate lumber with MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED still ghosting across its boards in faded paint, a root cellar mound, a smokehouse trailing its sweet resinous smell into the morning air. A dog began barking before he'd cut the engine—a big shaggy thing, all voice and no conviction—and by the time Edmund climbed down onto the grass, stiff from four hours in the seat, there were people coming out to meet him.

That was the other thing about Kettle Creek. Everywhere else in the interior, a landing drew one or two curious faces. Here it drew a dozen, as though the whole valley had been waiting for an excuse.

"Dunmore." Halvard Brandt reached him first, hand extended, weathered face creased into a grin that didn't quite reach his eyes. Brandt was sixty if he was a day, built like the stumps he'd pulled to make this field, and he wore the same canvas coat he'd worn every time Edmund had come through, summer or winter. "Medicine run? Or are you finally lost?"

"Medicine. Mail. And I'm told somebody here owes the company store money, so I brought a bill."

"Tell them we pay in turnips." Brandt laughed, and this time it got as far as his eyes. Then, quieter, as the others came up—the Rasmussen brothers, the Okonkwo family with their two girls hanging back shy behind their mother's skirt, old Marta Lindqvist walking slow with a cane she used more for emphasis than support—"You eat yet?"

"I ate."

"You ate what, jerky and black coffee? Get up to the house. Ingrid made bread yesterday and she'll be insulted if it goes stale without you having any."

Edmund wanted to say no. He always wanted to say no; that was the discipline of his life, the architecture of it. Land, unload, refuel, take off. Four hours' conversation was four hours closer to being known, and being known was a debt he'd spent seven years refusing to incur. But Marta Lindqvist had already taken his arm with a grip that brooked no argument, and the smell of woodsmoke and baking bread was doing something treacherous to the part of him that remembered kitchens, and the girls were staring at the airplane with the naked hunger of children who had never seen anything fly except ravens and their own paper ambitions.

"One cup," he said. "I've got weather coming down the pass by afternoon."

"There's always weather coming down the pass," Marta said. "It's the mountains. They're full of it."

* * *

The Brandt house was one room and a loft, and it held nine people comfortably because comfort, here, was a matter of arithmetic rather than space. Ingrid Brandt set coffee and bread and butter and a crock of honey in front of him and refused to hear a word about it, and Edmund sat at their table among strangers who treated him like a neighbor and felt something in his chest loosen that he hadn't known was clenched.

They talked about ordinary things. The potato crop, which had done well. The school lessons Ingrid taught the valley children three days a week. The new calf that had gotten into the oat bin and eaten itself sick, to the girls' outrage and the boys' delight. Edmund answered when asked and mostly listened, and listening felt like a luxury he'd forgotten how to spend.

It was the younger Rasmussen—Peder, twenty-something, restless in the way of young men in places too small for them—who let the other thing into the room.

"So did you see them?" he asked, leaning forward. "On your way in. The survey crew."

Edmund set down his cup. "What survey crew?"

"The ones camped at the mouth of Alder Creek. Came up three weeks ago on the company barge. Staking lines." Peder glanced at Halvard, and something passed between them, quick and guarded. "Marking boundaries."

"What boundaries?" Edmund asked. "There's nothing here but homesteads."

"That's what we thought," said Halvard.

Ingrid stood and took the coffee pot to the stove, her back to the room, and the silence she left behind her had weight. Edmund looked around the table—at Marta's folded hands, at Mrs. Okonkwo's carefully neutral face, at the way even the dog had gone quiet under the table—and understood that he had walked into the middle of a conversation these people had been having without him for weeks, and that his arrival had been both relief and risk, because he flew for the company. Not on the payroll. But carrying their freight, hauling their mail contracts, tied to them by the thin economics of a man who needed whatever work would have him.

"I'm not company," he said. "If that matters."

"It matters," Marta said flatly. "But it isn't the same as being one of us, either, is it."

It wasn't a question, and he didn't pretend otherwise. He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Tell me about the stakes."

Halvard sighed, a sound like wind moving through standing timber, and told him. Three weeks ago a barge had come upriver with six men and a transit instrument and crates of survey ribbon in colors the valley had never seen—orange and red, bright as wounds. They'd run lines along the creek benches and up the lower slopes, driving stakes every chain, tying ribbon to everything that stood still. When Peder had asked one of them what they were surveying for, the man had smiled and said *mineral reconnaissance, friend, nothing that concerns you*, and had kept right on working. When Halvard had ridden down to look at the stakes himself, he'd found them running straight through the middle of the Okonkwos' cleared pasture and not one of the surveyors could—or would—tell him why.

"They said the maps were company property," Halvard finished. "Said if there was a question about boundaries, it'd be settled at the district office. Three hundred miles south. Where none of us can afford to go and none of us can afford to be away from, this close to freeze-up."

"Meridian doesn't stake mineral claims through pastures for nothing," Edmund said.

"No," Marta agreed. "They don't."

Nobody said the word *gold*. Nobody had to. It sat in the room with them like a fourth person at the table, and Edmund watched these people—who had spent seven years pulling stumps and raising walls and burying two children in the little plot behind the smokehouse, who had made a field out of a forest with their hands—watch each other, and understood that they were afraid in the specific way of people who know exactly what can be taken from them and how long it took to build.

He should have said something reassuring. He was good at saying nothing, better than most, and reassurance was just noise arranged kindly. Instead he heard himself ask: "How many men in the survey party?"

"Six at Alder Creek. There's supposed to be a main camp farther up—" Peder hesitated. "Up past the gorge. Geological outfit. They've got a radio; the barge skipper said they check in with the company office regular as church. Except." He shrugged. "Skipper says they've missed the last two check-ins. Company's not saying much about it. You'd maybe know more than us."

"I don't listen to company frequencies," Edmund said, which was true, and which was also the kind of truth that was mostly a wall built sideways.

Marta snorted. "No. You don't listen to much of anything, from what I can tell. You fly in, you drink your coffee, you count your drums of fuel, and you fly out again like a man rowing past a drowning village because he's got a schedule." She said it without heat, the way she might have remarked on the weather, and it went through Edmund like a splinter finding old wood. "No offense meant, pilot."

"None taken," he lied politely.

* * *

He stayed two hours instead of one. He told himself it was the headwind building in the pass, and partly it was; the barometer falling was real enough, the peaks to the west going soft and gray-edged the way they did before weather. But partly it was the girls, who had worked up courage by degrees until they were standing beside the Waco asking questions about the engine with the gravity of junior engineers, and partly it was Halvard walking him around the strip's perimeter pointing out the soft spots where spring frost heaved the ground, and partly—it was the largest part, though he declined to examine it—it was simply that leaving felt, this once, like an act requiring effort.

He topped off both tanks from his own drums, since Kettle Creek had no fuel cache and never asked him to bring one, and while the pump chattered he noticed Marta Lindqvist standing at the field's edge with something folded in her hands. Waiting. Old women were patient the way rivers were patient; they knew everything arrived eventually or didn't, and either way the waiting was the same.

He finished the fueling, wiped his hands, and walked over.

"My eyes aren't what they were," she said, without preamble, "and my handwriting never was. So I'll read it to you, and you can tell me if it makes sense, and then you'll carry it. If you can."

"Marta—"

"If you can," she repeated. "I'm not a fool. I know what you are, Edmund Dunmore. You're a man who carries things carefully and delivers nothing of himself. Fine. Carry this instead. It weighs less than your conscience does, and it's worth more."

She unfolded the paper. It was written in pencil on the back of a seed catalog page, in a spidery, determined hand, and she read it aloud in a voice that had crossed an ocean and a continent and forty years of hard winters and was not going to be hurried now:

*"To the District Recorder, Land Titles Office, Fort Chimo. From Marta Lindqvist, Lot 14, Kettle Creek Valley. I hereby state for the record that I have lived upon and improved the within-described lands for seven years; that I hold receipt of my filing fee paid in good order; that my improvements include dwelling, barn, clearing of eleven acres under cultivation, and grave of my husband Nils Lindqvist, deceased, whose bones lie in that ground and will not be moved by any company of men living. If any survey has been made contrary to these facts, I request it be shown to me in writing, signed by the hand that made it, and I will answer it in person at your office if I have to walk."*

She folded the paper again along its worn creases. "Nils staked that lot the year before he died. He carried the corner posts up the hill on his back, two miles, because he wouldn't use the horse—he said the horse had earned its rest. He put them in the ground and he said, *there, now we own a piece of the world, and nobody can say different.* And then he went and died on me that winter, the stubborn man, and left me to keep the piece." Her chin came up. "So. Will you carry it?"

Edmund looked at the letter. It was nothing—a pencil note on catalog paper, unsigned by any lawyer, witnessed by no one, addressed to an office three hundred miles and a mountain range away. It was also the most legally dangerous document in the valley, because it was true, and truth filed in the wrong hands has a way of growing teeth.

"I'm flying south eventually," he said. "I can't say when. Weather, cargo, my own schedule—"

"Edmund." She said his name the way she'd said everything else, plainly, without decoration. "I watched you land. You checked your fuel selector twice and you flared that plane onto the grass like you were setting down a sleeping child. A man that careful about machines is careful about promises too. So I'm not asking for soon. I'm asking for yes."

The wind had shifted while they talked. He could feel it on his neck now, out of the west, cooler, carrying the first mineral smell of snow from heights that still looked clear. The barometer in his head—an instrument more reliable than the brass one bolted to the Waco's panel—was reading falling, falling, falling.

"Yes," he said. "I'll carry it."

She tucked the letter into his jacket pocket herself, patting the fabric flat over his chest as though seating it safely home. "Good. Now get in your machine and go, before that weather eats you. And Dunmore—" For a moment something moved behind her eyes, some old grief or old hope, quickly shuttered. "Whatever the company's got those surveyors hunting for up that river—I hope to God it stays lost."

* * *

He took off downhill with the tailwind quartering behind him, the Waco lifting off the grass at the last possible moment before the tree line, climbing in a long shallow spiral over the valley while he trimmed for cruise and set his heading for the gorge.

Below him Kettle Creek arranged itself into the map it really was: the strip, the fields, the fences, the little graveyard with its white pickets, the creek stitching silver through it all. Eleven lots. Eleven families' worth of sweat made visible. He counted them without meaning to—cartographer's habit, pilot's habit, the habit of a man who measured the world in distances and could not stop measuring even now—and then he made himself stop, because counting other people's holdings was the first step toward pricing them, and pricing was the company's trade, not his.

At the mouth of Alder Creek he saw the survey camp: two tents, the transit on its tripod catching the sun, orange ribbon blazing on the brush like signal fires. One of the men looked up at the sound of his engine and raised an arm—not waving, Edmund thought. Sighting. Measuring him against the sky the way they'd measured the valley against their stakes.

Ahead, the gorge opened its dark throat into the high country, and beyond the gorge, two days' flight upstream, the expedition that wasn't answering its radio. The company's doubled offer sat in his memory like a stone he couldn't cough up. The letter pressed against his chest, light as ash and heavy as a headstone.

The engine ran smooth. The weather held—for now. Edmund Dunmore flew on into the mountains, carrying mail, medicine, and one old woman's word, and did not yet know that he was already past the point where any of it could be set down again.
