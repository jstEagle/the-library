## 6. Down in the White

The engine coughed at eleven thousand feet, and Edmund Dunmore's whole body went cold before his mind caught up.

He knew that cough. Every pilot did. It was the sound of a carburetor swallowing ice, or a fuel line starving, or a valve deciding it had worked long enough. Seven years of flying medicine and mail into the interior had taught him to hear trouble before instruments confirmed it, and this trouble had a voice like an old man clearing his throat before he died.

"No," he said aloud, to no one. "Not here."

Here was the spine of the Serrano Range, white peaks running north to south like the vertebrae of some buried animal, the passes between them choked with cloud. Below him there was nothing a man could use: glacier, scree, ridgelines sharp as broken glass. The nearest strip was Harrow's field, ninety miles behind him. Ahead, the expedition camp sat in a river valley he couldn't reach without crossing this wall of stone.

The engine coughed again, caught, ran rough for three seconds—a reprieve, a lie—and then quit altogether.

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Edmund had ever heard.

* * *

There is a moment in every forced landing—every pilot knows it, though few admit it—when the mind simply refuses. When the hands are moving through the checklist and the eyes are reading the ground and some detached part of the self stands off to one side saying *this isn't happening, this is someone else's emergency.* Edmund had been through it twice before, once over flooded lowlands where he'd put down on a cattle road and once when a storm had shoved him into a lake with more luck than skill. Both times the refusal had lasted perhaps four seconds. Then the world came back, hard and total, and there was only the work.

He glided. That was the first law: never stop flying the airplane. Nose down, trade altitude for distance, keep the wings level and the airspeed alive. The Moth—he'd named her *Wren*, after the bird that sang in winter—sank toward the range at a shallow angle, and Edmund's eyes swept the white below with the cold arithmetic of a man counting coins he already knew were short.

Glacier. No.

Rock field. No.

A ridge, and beyond it—there. A plateau. High, maybe nine thousand feet, wedged between two shoulders of the mountain like a shelf someone had forgotten to finish building. Snow-covered, yes, but flat-ish, and flat-ish at nine thousand feet with a dead engine was not a thing you evaluated. It was a thing you took.

He turned for it, banking gently, conserving what the Wren had left. The altimeter unwound. Eight thousand five hundred. Eight thousand. The plateau grew beneath him, resolving from a smear of white into terrain—drifts, a dark seam of exposed rock along its eastern edge, a shallow bowl near the center that might be wind-scoured or might be a crevasse wearing a lid of snow.

Seven thousand feet. The propeller had stopped turning. In the stillness he could hear his own breathing, fast and thin, and the whistle of air over the struts, and underneath both, faintly, absurdly, the tick of the cooling engine like a clock running out.

"Come on," he whispered. "Come on, girl."

Six thousand five hundred above sea level meant perhaps four hundred feet above the plateau itself. He flared too high—the classic error, the error that killed—and the Wren dropped the last few feet like a stone with opinions.

The skis hit snow. The left ski found something solid beneath it—a rock, a frozen hummock—and the airplane slewed sideways with a shriek of tortured metal. Edmund's head met the door frame. The world went white, then black, then white again, and somewhere in the middle of all that whiteness the Wren stopped moving, and so did he.

* * *

He woke to cold.

Not discomfort—cold. The kind that gets into the marrow and starts making decisions. His breath hung in front of his face in a small gray ghost, and when he tried to move, his body answered with a catalogue of complaints: ribs on the right side that lit up like struck matches, a gash above his left ear that had painted half his collar red, a wrist he suspected was sprained if not worse. The windshield was starred with cracks. Through them he could see sky, enormous and pale blue and utterly indifferent.

"Alive," he said. His voice sounded strange in the vastness, swallowed instantly. "Well. There's a thing."

He got the door open—it fought him, jammed in its bent frame, and he had to put his shoulder into it, which cost him a full minute of sitting very still afterward, breathing through the pain in his ribs. Then he was out, standing in snow that came to mid-shin, and the plateau opened around him in every direction.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. The mountains rose on three sides, sunlit and immaculate, their shadows lying across the snow in long violet bars. The air was so clear it seemed to have edges. And the silence—not just the absence of engine noise now but the absence of everything, of birds, of wind, of any sound that meant a human being within a hundred miles—pressed against his eardrums like water.

Edmund stood there for a long moment, swaying, bleeding quietly into his collar, and let himself feel the terror properly. He'd learned that much over seven years: fear denied doesn't leave. It moves in. It pays rent in nightmares. Better to open the door, let it stand in the room, look at it squarely.

He was going to die here. That was the honest shape of it. Nine thousand feet, injured, no radio worth the name, no shelter, days from anywhere, and the dry season's last warmth already draining out of the days. Nights up here would drop below freezing. His survival kit was a first-aid tin, a hatchet, a coil of rope, two chocolate bars, and a canvas tarp he used to cover the engine. Men had died of less in gentler country.

"All right," he said to the terror. "All right."

And then, because standing still was dying slowly and moving was dying slower, he went back to work.

* * *

The radio was the first priority, and the radio was the reason he'd taken Meridian's contract at all—or told himself it was. The Wren carried a transmitter-receiver salvaged from a wrecked mail plane two years back, heavy, cantankerous, and powerful enough to reach Harrow's field on a good day. If it lived, he could call in his position. Someone would come. Not quickly, not cheaply, but eventually, and eventually was the difference between a story and an obituary.

He climbed back into the cabin, wincing, and knelt in the wreckage of the cockpit. The impact had thrown everything forward: charts, the fire extinguisher, a tin cup rolling loose in the footwell. The radio itself sat in its rack behind the seats, and at first glance it looked intact—at second glance, the antenna lead had torn free of its fitting, and the casing near the power terminals was dented inward.

He worked by feel mostly, fingers stiffening already in the cold. Reattached the antenna lead. Checked the battery box strapped under the seat—intact, thank God, the acid hadn't spilled. Found the hand crank, because the radio could be wound by hand in emergencies, thirty seconds of cranking for sixty seconds of transmission. He'd tested it a hundred times on the ground, always with a little private ceremony, always thinking *one day I'll be glad of this crank.*

He cranked. The generator whirred. He keyed the transmit switch and spoke Harrow's frequency into the mouthpiece, voice cracking, then steadying: *Wren, Wren, Dunmore, forced down in the Serranos, position approximately—*

Nothing. Static, thick and dead as wool. He tried the other band. Tried Harrow again. Tried the company frequency at Fort Alder, hating himself for it, hating the word *company* in his own mouth. Static, static, static.

He kept at it for twenty minutes. He checked every connection twice. He pulled the casing panel and looked inside with eyes that were not an electrician's eyes and saw nothing obviously wrong, which was somehow worse than seeing the break. Somewhere in that dented, soldered heart, the machine had decided its working life was over, and machines were entitled to their retirements.

Edmund set the crank down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a glass he wants very badly to throw.

"Fine," he said. "Fine."

Then he laughed—one short, ugly bark that echoed off the ridge and came back sounding like someone else. Fine. The word of men in exactly his situation since the beginning of men. He sat in the wrecked cabin with his bleeding head and his cracked ribs and laughed until it turned into something else, and when it was done he wiped his face with a sleeve that came away red and gray with ash from the crushed instrument panel, and he felt, strangely, better. Emptied out. Swept clean.

Terror, fully felt, transmutes. Nobody tells you that. It burns down to something harder and simpler than hope: the plain animal insistence on continuing.

* * *

He salvaged methodically, because method was a rope to hold onto. From the cabin: the first-aid tin, the hatchet, the rope, the chocolate, the tarp, the charts (useless for navigation now, useful for fire), the logbook, and—after a moment's hesitation—the letter.

Mrs. Calloway's letter. Folded twice, soft as cloth from handling, tucked in his jacket pocket since Harrow's field. *If you can carry it out,* she'd said, pressing it on him with hands like birch bark, and he'd said *if I can,* which had felt safe and noncommittal at the time. Now it was the only piece of mail in the world addressed to somewhere other than where he stood, and holding it was like holding a ticket.

From the engine cowling he unbolted the tool roll. From under the seats he dragged the crate of medical supplies bound for the expedition camp—bandages, sulfa powder, a bottle of iodine tablets. Meridian's property, technically. He decided, with a clarity that surprised him, not to care.

Last, he stripped the Wren herself. This was the part that hurt, though he'd have denied it. He siphoned what fuel remained into a spare can—maybe three gallons, enough for a small stove and a signal fire that would actually light in damp snow. He cut the fabric from the fuselage side in panels: patching material, or a ground sheet, or a sail if it ever came to that. He took the spark plugs, the magneto, the control cables, coiling them like the guts of the machine they were.

When he finished, the Wren sat in the trampled snow with her skin opened and her heart removed, and Edmund stood beside her and put his bare hand flat on the cold engine cowling.

"You got me down," he said. "That's the job. Rest now."

His voice didn't shake. He counted that as a victory and moved on before it could.

* * *

By late afternoon he had a camp: a windbreak of fuselage panels and rock behind a drift, the tarp stretched overhead, his supplies sorted and inventoried. He cleaned the gash on his scalp with melted snow and iodine, taped the ribs as well as tape can hold a man together, splinted the wrist against a tent pole section he'd sawn off. The sulfa he saved. Head wounds bled dramatic and healed stubborn; the real enemy up here wouldn't be the cut but the cold, and after the cold, the arithmetic of food and distance.

Two chocolate bars. Maybe fifteen hundred calories. At nine thousand feet, burning heat just to breathe, a man needed four thousand a day minimum. The math gave him less than a day of full strength, and after that a steep and slippery decline.

So: he had to move. Tomorrow, at first light, before the cold finished organizing its arguments.

The question was *where*, and it was while he stood at the edge of his little camp scanning the terrain for tomorrow's route that he saw it.

Smoke.

At first he took it for cloud—a smudge on the southern horizon, low, where the plateau fell away toward the river country. But clouds moved and changed; this held its shape. A single vertical thread, gray-white against the pale sky, rising from beyond the far shoulder of the range. Woodsmoke. Cooking smoke. The smoke of people who had built a fire in the last hour and expected to build another tomorrow.

He counted on the chart, tracing distances with a stiff finger. The expedition camp lay southeast of his position, down in the river valley—two days' hard walking if the pass was open, three if it wasn't. The smoke sat roughly where the camp should be. Roughly. Charts lied, and so did smoke, and so did hope, but the coincidence was all he had.

"Days away," he said aloud, and heard how the words sounded in the empty air. Days away, on a sprained wrist and cracked ribs, with no food, across country he'd only ever seen from the air, in mountains that ate survey parties whole.

He thought about staying. It was a real thought, honestly weighed: dig in, stretch the supplies, burn the fabric panels a few feet at a time as signals, wait for a search that might or might not come looking in country this size. Waiting was the rational choice. Waiting was what careful men did.

But he had spent seven years waiting. Waiting was the whole architecture of his life—the routine flights, the silent frequencies, the name buried so deep even he sometimes couldn't find it. And here was the thing the crash had cracked open in him, lying in the snow with his blood on the instrument panel: waiting had gotten him exactly here. Down in the white, alone, with a dead radio and a dead airplane and a dead way of living, and a letter in his pocket addressed to people who were probably, right now, watching surveyors stake claims on the land under their boots.

The smoke rose, patient and thin, against the darkening east.

Edmund banked his fire small, laid out the tarp, and sat with the chart on his knees until the light failed, memorizing the route: southeast along the plateau's spine, down through the saddle where the contour lines pinched together, then the long descent into the valley. He memorized it the way he'd once memorized the faces of jurors, because memory was the only luggage that didn't weigh anything.

Before sleep he took out Mrs. Calloway's letter and read it again by the last ember-light. It was short. It said the apples had come in early, and that Thomas had shot his first deer, and that she prayed the Lord would keep the pilots steady-handed. It said nothing about surveyors, nothing about claims, nothing about the nervousness that had hung over the strip like weather. It was a letter written by a woman who still believed the valley would go on being a valley.

He folded it away. He would deliver it. Not because it was likely, and not because it was owed—but because a man who had spent seven years carrying nothing but his own silence could start carrying something else, tonight, on a plateau at the top of the world, and see whether the weight changed him.

The stars came out in their brutal thousands. Edmund pulled the tarp to his chin, set his watch alarm by feel, and slept the thin sleep of the injured and resolved, dreaming of engines that wouldn't start and doors that wouldn't open and, once, briefly, kindly, of a woman with a satchel walking out of the snow toward him—which was nonsense, of course, pure fever-nonsense.

Except that when he woke before dawn, stiff and freezing and alive, and shouldered his pack at the edge of the firelight, he could have sworn the smoke on the southern horizon had doubled in the night.

Two threads now, rising side by side.

Somebody down there was expecting company.
