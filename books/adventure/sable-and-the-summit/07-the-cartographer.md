## 7. The Cartographer

Sable Voss had learned to count her hunger in days the way other people counted money, and by the time she saw the smoke rising off the plateau to the north, she was eleven days past the last of the expedition's flour and eating things she preferred not to name even to herself.

She had been following the river down from the pass country for six of those days, keeping to the timber on the east bank where the ground rose and fell in long folds and a woman walking alone could stay invisible. That was the discipline now. Invisible. She had been the expedition's cartographer for fourteen months, and in all that time she had never once drawn herself onto a map. She had drawn rivers, ridgelines, the braided gravel bars where the water split and rejoined, the faint dotted lines of game trails that might become pack routes if Meridian Consolidated ever got what it wanted. But never herself. A mapmaker who appeared on her own maps was a mapmaker someone could find.

So when the smoke came up thin and gray against the blue of the morning, she did what she had trained herself to do: she stopped, she crouched, and she watched.

The plateau above the river bend was bare rock and old burn, black snags standing like burnt matches across two miles of nothing, and at its center sat an airplane. Not flying—*sitting*, the way a wounded animal sits, one wing canted up toward the sky as though it were still trying to climb. Smoke curled from somewhere near the engine cowling, thinning as she watched, dying down to almost nothing. A crash that had already finished crashing.

She should have turned south. Every instinct she'd sharpened over eleven hungry days said turn south, keep walking, put the wreck behind you, because a plane meant men and men meant Meridian and Meridian meant the thing she had walked nine days out of the mountains to escape. The satchel strap lay against her ribs under her coat, and she pressed her forearm against it without thinking, the way you press a hand to a wound.

But there were no men moving around the wreck. No figures, no voices carrying across the cold air, no second smoke from a cook fire. Just the plane, tilted and silent, and after a while—she couldn't have said how long; time had gone strange since the camp—a man came out from under the wing, moving slowly, one arm held stiff against his side, and stood looking east toward the range with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

He wasn't wearing company colors. That mattered. Meridian's field men wore khaki and brass buttons and carried themselves like they owned the ground they stood on, because as far as they were concerned they did. This man wore a flight jacket gone soft with age and patched at both elbows, and he moved like someone used to being alone, checking his own shadow before trusting it.

Sable stayed in the trees until the light went long and gold, and then a little longer, because patience cost nothing and haste had already cost her everything once. She ate a handful of the lichen she'd scraped off the north faces of rocks—bitter, gritty, better than nothing—and drank from the river, and made herself a promise: she would circle wide, come at the wreck from downwind, and look first. If there was anything about him that smelled of Crane or of Meridian, she would be gone into the timber before he ever knew she'd been there, and she would not stop walking until the pass closed behind her.

She circled wide. It took her most of the afternoon.

* * *

She came out of the rocks on the plateau's western edge as the sun dropped behind the range and the temperature began its evening plunge, and she saw the whole picture at once: the plane with its bent propeller and its belly furrowed through the scree for forty yards, the crates dragged out and stacked with a soldier's neatness, the tarp rigged between wing and fuselage as a lean-to, and the man sitting inside it with the dead radio open in front of him, prodding at its guts with a screwdriver and a patience that looked more like grief than hope.

He heard her before he saw her. She gave him that much—she let a boot scrape stone deliberately, because surprising a man with an injured side and possibly a gun was a way to get shot, and she had no intention of surviving nine days of wilderness only to die of someone else's nerves.

He came out of the lean-to fast for a hurt man, screwdriver still in his fist, and stopped when he saw her, and she watched him take inventory the same way she'd taken hers: the starvation, the cracked lips, the boots worn half through, the canvas satchel slung crosswise under her coat, held the way a woman holds the only thing she owns.

"Hello," he said. His voice was rough, careful. "You alone?"

"I'm asking the questions." Her voice came out flatter than she intended, scraped raw by disuse. She hadn't spoken aloud in four days. "Whose plane?"

"Mine."

"Who do you fly for?"

A pause. She catalogued the pause, measured it, filed it away. "Myself," he said. "I fly mail. Medicine runs. Contract work, when I need parts." He nodded at the stacked crates. "Which is why those are sitting here instead of wherever they were going."

"What's in them?"

"I don't know. Sealed. I was told not to look."

Something in her chest went cold and hard. *Told not to look.* She thought of the forward cache at Kettle Bend, the crates with the stenciled M, the manifest Alden Crane had signed with a flourish and burned the duplicate of, and she took one step back toward the rocks.

He saw it. To his credit—or whatever you wanted to call it—he didn't move to stop her. He just said, quietly, "You're starving. And you've got frost-nip on your left hand. Whatever you're afraid I am, you won't outrun it on an empty stomach."

"I've outrun worse on less."

"I don't doubt it." He set the screwdriver down on a crate, slowly, showing her his empty hands. "There's pemmican in the survival kit. Hardtack. I can't eat much of it myself—ribs are cracked, maybe worse, and my stomach's been sulking since the landing. It'll go bad sitting here." He paused. "I'm Edmund Dunmore."

The name landed on her like weather. She kept her face still, but inside, pieces clicked together with the ugly smoothness of a well-made lock: Dunmore. The pilot. Seven years ago, the crash, the manslaughter charge, the man who'd flown for the companies ever since, ghosting up and down the interior routes. Crane had mentioned him once, late at night over the survey table, with a smile Sable had spent months learning to read. *That one knows what it costs to be on the wrong side of us.*

And here he was, sitting in a wreck full of sealed Meridian crates, telling her he flew for himself.

"Sable Voss," she said, because refusing a name was its own kind of tell, and she was tired of being nobody. "Expedition cartographer. Meridian survey, upper river."

His face changed. Not much—but she'd spent fourteen months reading micro-expressions across survey tables, and she caught it: something going shut behind his eyes, a door closing on a room she couldn't see.

"You're the survivor," he said. "The radio went quiet three weeks ago. They sent me up with supplies."

"They sent you up with supplies," she repeated. "Sealed ones. That you weren't allowed to inspect."

"Yes."

"For a camp that went silent."

"Yes."

"And you didn't wonder?"

He looked at the crates. Then at her. And she saw him decide something—not quickly, not easily, but the way a man decides to step off a ledge, testing his weight on it. "I wondered," he said. "I've been wondering since the day I signed. Wondering hasn't been a habit of mine for seven years. It's expensive."

"It's about to get more so." She looked at the sky, gauging the light, the temperature, the smell of the wind coming off the high snowfields—clean, dry, the smell that meant the season was turning whether anyone was ready or not. "How's your engine?"

"Dead. Thrown a rod, or something worse. I'm not a mechanic good enough to fix it with what's in the kit."

"Radio?"

"Dead too. I've had it apart twice." He almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, tired and self-mocking. "I crashed within sight of where I was supposed to deliver. Isn't that a joke."

"It's not a joke." She crouched, finally, at the edge of his little camp, close enough to talk low, far enough to move. "It's arithmetic. Listen to me, Dunmore, because I'm only going to say this once. There are armed men at the forward camp below the pass. Eleven of them, maybe fewer now. They're led by a man named Alden Crane, and they have the supplies, the rifles, and the authority, and in about two weeks the first snows close the high passes until spring. If you're not south of the range when that happens, you winter here. If I'm not south of the range when that happens, I'm dead—not from the cold. From them."

"Why?" He asked it simply, and she hated him a little for making her say it, and she said it anyway, because a map with nothing on it protects no one.

"Because I'm carrying the only true survey of that valley in existence," she said, "and Crane wants it burned."

* * *

They talked until dark, in the low urgent register of two people deciding whether to trust each other with their lives, which is a different conversation entirely from deciding whether to trust each other.

She told him some of it—the camp splitting, the orders that had come upriver in Crane's own handwriting, the boundary lines that were supposed to be drawn one way and were ordered drawn another, the evidence cache destroyed, the men who'd objected scattered or worse. She did not tell him what was in the satchel. She did not tell him how the camp had ended, the part with the fire and the shouting and the sound a man makes when—she stopped that thought, folded it flat, put it away. Later. If he earned later.

He told her some of it too. She could hear the shape of what he left out, the seven-year-shaped hole in his story, and she recognized the technique because she was using it herself: give enough truth to buy credibility, hold back the coin you can't afford to spend.

What she gave him, finally, was practical. She pulled a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper from her pocket—she always had paper; she'd scratched survey notes on birchbark for eleven days rather than use the last clean sheet—and drew the country between them and the valley, quick and sure, the lines flowing out of her hand like water finding its level.

"There's a pass here," she said, tapping. "Not the one Crane's watching. The old one, the one the trappers used before the company bought the maps and let them lapse. It's higher, it's harder, and it adds two days. But it comes out above the homestead valley, and it's not guarded, because officially it doesn't exist. I know it because I mapped it. On my own time. Because I didn't trust the men paying me."

Dunmore studied the sketch for a long moment. "Two days' hard climbing," he said. "With cracked ribs."

"With cracked ribs, yes. Or you can wait here for Crane's patrols to find you sitting next to eleven crates of their property, and explain to them how you came to be off-course and out of fuel and alive."

"That's not much of a choice."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Most true choices aren't."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she braced for the calculation she'd seen in every Meridian man's eyes, the weighing of what she was worth against what she cost. But what she saw instead was something wearier and more complicated—a man recognizing a fellow exile across a fire neither of them had lit yet.

"The old woman at the strip," he said suddenly. "Before I took off. She gave me a letter to carry out. It's still in my jacket." He touched his chest, as if confirming it was real. "If we go over that pass, I can—" He stopped. Recalculated. "We can take it with us."

"We," Sable said. Not a question. A test.

"We," he said. "If you'll have me. I know I'm not—look, I know what you see when you look at me. A company pilot with a company load. But I've been invisible for seven years, Voss, and I'm starting to think invisible was just a slower way of disappearing. You're the first person in longer than I can say who's given me a reason to walk anywhere."

She should say no. Every hard-won instinct said no: travel alone, trust no one, the satchel stays yours, the knowledge stays yours, because every alliance is a liability and every companion a potential witness for the other side. She had watched what happened to the men at the camp who had trusted each other. She had heard it happen.

But she looked at his hands—steady, competent, the hands of a man who fixed things—and at the dead radio he'd disassembled twice hoping to make it lie differently, and at the letter from a stranger pressed against his heart like a debt, and she thought: *this is either the worst decision of my life or the first good one since the survey went wrong, and I won't know which until we're over the pass.*

"All right," she said. "We leave at first light. You carry the food; I carry the route. And Dunmore—" she waited until his eyes met hers, and made it plain, "—if you're playing me, if any word of this reaches Crane, I will vanish into that country with the satchel and you will never find me or it again. Are we understood?"

"Understood," he said. And then, quieter, almost to himself: "For what it's worth—I believe you. About the valley. God help me, I believed you before you said a word. I think that's why I signed the contract. Some part of me needed to see it for myself."

Sable looked at him a long moment, this broken pilot with his buried name and his dead machines, and felt the first unfamiliar thing she'd felt in weeks: not trust, nothing so generous as trust, but the beginning of a working hypothesis that this man might be worth the risk.

"Eat," she said, handing him the pemmican. "First light comes early, and the pass doesn't care how we feel about it."

Above the plateau, the stars were coming out hard and cold over the range, and somewhere beyond them, two days' march to the south, Alden Crane sat by a fire with armed men and a stolen authority, believing that the truth of the valley was ash in a burned cache and the last witness to it was dead or fled.

By spring, Sable thought, he would learn otherwise. If the snows let them live that long.
