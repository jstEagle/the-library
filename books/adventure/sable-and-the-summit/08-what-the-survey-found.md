## 8. What the Survey Found

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Sable decided to tell him.

She'd been circling the decision all afternoon while they worked—while Edmund splinted a cracked rib of fuselage into a windbreak and she laid out their food like an accountant: nine biscuits, half a tin of bacon fat, a handful of tea leaves gone gray with age. He hadn't asked about the satchel. That was the first thing she'd noticed about him, back when he was still half-conscious in the wreck of his plane and she stood over him with a rock in her hand deciding whether he was one of Crane's men. He didn't ask. Most people asked. Most people couldn't stand a closed door for more than an hour before they had to put their shoulder to it.

Edmund Dunmore sat across the fire with his arm bound and his face lit orange, watching her the way she imagined he watched instruments—a steady read, no panic, no demand. Waiting for the needle to move on its own.

It moved.

"You flew supplies up for Meridian," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the crates in his wreck, the stenciled M.C. on the drums, the manifest folded in his breast pocket when she went through his jacket looking for anything useful. She wasn't ashamed of that. She'd gone through worse for less.

"Contract of convenience," he said. "I needed parts. I told myself it was just cargo."

"Was it?"

He turned his hands over in the firelight, palms up, as if the answer might be written there. "Chain. Drums. Crates I wasn't allowed to open."

Sable nodded slowly. She unslung the satchel from where it had slept against her spine for eleven days—the strap worn into a groove she could feel through two shirts—and set it in her lap without opening it. Her fingers rested on the buckle like a woman resting her hand on a dog's collar, feeling for the tremor before the storm.

"Then you should know what you were carrying," she said. "And what I'm carrying. Because if we're going to walk three days through that pass together, you deserve to know what's in this bag, and why there are men behind us who want it badly enough to burn a valley for it."

Edmund went very still. It was the stillness of a man who had already guessed and been waiting for someone to say it aloud.

* * *

She started where it had started, which was not with gold but with paper.

"We came upriver in June. Fourteen of us. Crane had the contract—geological survey, boundary verification, standard work. I'd mapped for the government before; Meridian hired me because I was cheap and thorough, and because I didn't ask questions." She almost smiled at that. "I've learned to ask questions since.

"The first month was honest work. Traverse lines, barometric readings, sketching the valley from the ridges. The homesteaders were downriver—maybe forty people, six families plus drifters, a schoolteacher, a man who ran a still nobody talked about. They'd been there twenty years. Some of them filed claims back when the land was considered worthless. There's a church now. A mission station with a bell. Children who waved at our boats when we passed."

Her voice did something strange on the word *children*, and she let it do it. Edmund didn't interrupt. He had the particular silence of a man listening with his whole body.

"In August we found the seam."

She opened the satchel then—not all the way, just enough to draw out the top map, rolled in oilcloth, tied with cord. She unrolled it across her knees and turned it so the firelight fell across it. Even upside down, even in bad light, Edmund's breath changed.

"That's the homestead valley," she said. Her finger traced the river, the strip of cleared land, the little crosses that marked buildings. "And this—" the finger moved to a band of hatching beneath the valley floor, following the contour of the earth like a vein under skin— "is quartz reef, carrying free-milling gold, running better than four ounces to the ton over a strike width we measured at eleven feet. It runs under the whole lower valley. Under the fields. Under the church."

Edmund said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with a sound like a long exhale.

"It's one of the richest showings I have ever seen or heard of," Sable said. "Crane knew it the moment the assays came back. I watched him read the numbers, and I watched something go out of his face and something else come in. You know what I mean?"

"I know what you mean," Edmund said quietly.

"He sent a runner downriver with a coded message within the hour. Then he called a meeting of the senior staff and gave us new instructions." She heard her own voice flatten, the way it flattened every time she said this part, as if the words had been sanded smooth by repetition and still cut her every time. "We were to redraw the survey boundaries. Move the southern line north by nine miles. Document the seam as falling outside the homesteaders' claims—outside any existing claim—and inside vacant crown land available for lease. We were to date the field notes retroactively. And we were to sign."

"And you refused."

"I asked him what happened to the homesteaders." She looked into the fire. "That was my mistake. I thought it was a question. It was actually an answer—he just hadn't given it yet."

* * *

The camp had split along a line nobody drew but everybody saw.

Sable described it in pieces, the way memory actually comes: not a story but shards. The cook, Marta, who stopped speaking to anyone after the meeting and started packing her knives. The two young chainmen, Ferro and Dell, who got drunk the night the orders came down and sang until Crane's foreman made them stop. The assistant surveyor, Okafor, who signed first, fast, without reading what he was signing, and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes afterward.

"There were seven of us who wouldn't sign," she said. "Seven out of fourteen. For about a week it felt like strength. Then it started to feel like being counted."

Crane hadn't shouted. That was the thing she kept coming back to, the detail that made it worse instead of better. He'd been reasonable. Patient. He'd taken each of them aside individually, the way a good surgeon works down a list. With some of them it had been money—a bonus, a stake, a quiet transfer to a better posting. With one of them it had been a sick wife and the promise of company doctors. With Okafor it had been nothing at all; he simply broke, the way green wood breaks, all at once and along the grain.

"With me he tried the money first. Then he tried the argument—that the homesteaders would be compensated, that progress was progress, that the gold would build roads and schools whether we drew the line honestly or not, so we might as well be the ones who profited." She shook her head. "And when that failed, he stopped arguing. He just looked at me the way you look at weather. Like something that was going to happen to him regardless."

"What did you do?" Edmund asked.

"For a while? Nothing. That's the part I can't forgive myself for. I copied everything—I made duplicates of the true field notes, the original boundary readings, the assay sheets, the traverse logs. I hid them in the false bottom of my map case. And then I waited, because I kept thinking someone senior would do something, some adult in the room would stand up, and it would all resolve itself without me having to be brave." Her mouth twisted. "Nobody stood up. The ones who wouldn't sign just got quieter and quieter, and Crane got more comfortable, and by September the new maps were drawn and dated and beautiful, and I understood that waiting is a choice too. It has consequences same as anything."

"So you ran."

"I prepared to run. There's a difference, and the difference took too long." She pulled the second document from the satchel—a letter, creased soft from rereading. "Marta found out about my copies. She'd been writing to her sister in the settlement below, warning them something was wrong. She wanted my true maps to go with her letters. So we planned. Marta would take the boat down with the mail run and carry word to the homesteaders that their ground was worth stealing. I'd hold the originals until we could get them to a district registrar—someone outside Meridian's reach."

"And then the camp went wrong."

The phrase hung between them. Sable watched Edmund's face as he said it, and saw that he already knew it was a euphemism, the way *downsized* and *restructured* are euphemisms, the way companies talk about the things they pay to make happen.

"On the ninth of October," she said. "Three days before we were scheduled to break camp anyway. A fire started in the supply tent where the evidence cache was stored—all the original notebooks, the sealed assay samples, the core boxes. Everything that contradicted the new maps. It burned hot and fast and completely, in wet weather, in a camp where fires weren't allowed near that tent." She met his eyes. "You flew the drums and the chain and the crates you weren't allowed to inspect. Ask yourself what was in them."

Edmund's jaw worked. "Kerosene," he said finally. "Or blasting gel. Something that burns."

"Something that burns," she agreed. "Two of the men who wouldn't sign—Ferro and Dell, the young ones—they were on night watch. They saw who went into the tent. By morning they'd changed their minds about everything. Signed the new maps, both of them, hands shaking. One of them cried while he did it. Crane patted him on the shoulder like a father."

"What happened to Marta?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. The fire had sunk low enough that the cold pressed in from all sides, and she fed it a stick without needing to look at her hands.

"Marta left the morning before the fire," she said. "She never reached the settlement. Her boat was found downstream, swamped, ten miles on. No body." She swallowed. "No body isn't death. I know that. I hold onto that. But I also know what kind of river it is, and what kind of men were on it, and I have done the arithmetic every day for eleven days and it always comes out the same."

Edmund looked at her for a long time. "I'm sorry," he said, and it sounded like it cost him something, like the words came up from somewhere deeper than politeness.

"Don't be sorry yet," Sable said. "Be sorry when you hear the rest. Because here's the rest: the fire didn't destroy everything. Crane thinks it did. That's the only reason I'm alive. He believes the only true record burned in that tent, and that I'm a hysterical woman wandering the mountains with nothing but grief. If he believed otherwise, I'd be floating in that river next to Marta." She tapped the satchel. "This holds the complete original survey. Boundaries as actually measured. Assays as actually run. Field notes in my hand, dated, witnessed by Marta before she died—or disappeared, I refuse to say died. It is the only set in existence. It proves the seam lies under forty people's homes, and it proves Meridian's filing maps are forged, and if it reaches a district registrar before Meridian's claim is processed, their whole edifice comes down. The leases, the financing, the board approvals—all of it built on paper I could burn with a match, except I'm going to file it instead."

* * *

Edmund stared into the fire for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"The old woman at the fuel strip," he said. "Halvorsen. She asked me to carry a letter out. She said surveyors had been staking lines on the ridge above her place. She said her neighbors were nervous." He laughed, short and bitter, no humor in it. "I told myself it was routine. Staking happens. Companies measure things. I had drums in my hold and I didn't look inside them, and I told myself that was prudence."

"It was complicity," Sable said. Not cruelly. "Mine was worse. I held the truth for six weeks and called it caution."

He flinched as if she'd struck him, and she watched him absorb it, and she watched him not look away, and she revised her estimate of him upward for the third time since the wreck.

"Why did you come back for me?" he asked suddenly. "At the plane. You could have walked on. You know this country. You had the satchel. Every hour you spent hauling a stranger out of a wreck was an hour of risk."

Sable considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because she'd been asking herself the same thing since dawn.

"Because you crashed flying their cargo and your first instinct, half-conscious, bleeding, was to check whether you'd hit anyone on the way down," she said. "You asked me that before you asked your own name. And because I need a witness. Maps can be stolen. Maps can be forged—Meridian proved that. But a pilot who flew their freight, who saw the crates, who can swear to what was loaded and where—it takes more than one voice to hang a company. Marta was supposed to be my second voice." Her throat tightened. "Now it's you, if you'll take the job. And I should warn you the job description. Three days through the pass ahead of the snows, pursued by men with rifles, to file papers with a clerk who may or may not be bought. And after that, likely as not, a courtroom, or whatever passes for one out here."

The wind came down off the summit ridge again, colder than before, carrying the first mineral smell of coming snow. Sable felt it on the back of her neck like a hand.

Edmund was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "There's something you should know about me too. Why I live out here measuring my life in miles. Seven years ago I—" and he stopped, and started again, and told her about a crash, and a death, and a manslaughter charge, and a surveyor named Alden Crane whose testimony had buried him.

Sable listened without moving. When he finished, she said, "Crane."

"Crane."

She began, very softly, to laugh—not from humor but from something adjacent to it, some grim recognition at the architecture of the world. "Of course it's Crane. Of course the man who lied to convict you is the man who burned the evidence to rob them. He's been building the same building his whole life. Paper walls and perjury for mortar."

"And you still want me as your witness?" Edmund asked. "A convicted man walking in with the company's own pilot's log?"

"I want someone who knows exactly what Crane's testimony costs," Sable said. "Because in three days, someone is going to offer you a lot of money to forget what's in this satchel. Or threaten you. Or both. And I need to know, right now, tonight, before we take another step toward that pass—which way you break."

The fire had burned to coals. Above the black teeth of the ridge, the stars were hard and close, and somewhere beyond them, downriver, men were sleeping beside rifles with her name on a list.

Edmund looked at the satchel in her lap for a long time. Then he looked up.

"Ask me tomorrow," he said. "After I've slept on it. But I'll tell you this much tonight—I've spent seven years being nobody, and I'm starting to remember how much I hated it."

Sable studied him in the coal-light, weighing the words the way she weighed a bearing—against everything else she knew, checking for error. It wasn't a yes. But it wasn't a no, and it wasn't a lie, and after eleven days alone with the dead and the disappeared, it was the most hopeful thing she'd heard.

"Tomorrow, then," she said, and banked the fire, and lay down with the satchel strapped to her body where it had belonged since October the ninth.

She was asleep—or nearly—when Edmund spoke once more from the darkness, so quietly she might have dreamed it:

"Sable. If Crane's men are behind us on the river side of the range... how many rifles are we talking about?"

She lay awake in the cold and answered honestly.

"Enough," she said. "So we'd best be fast."
