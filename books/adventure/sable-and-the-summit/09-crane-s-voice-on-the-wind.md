## 9. Crane's Voice on the Wind

The forward camp announced itself before we saw it. A thread of woodsmoke rose off the ridge ahead, thin and domestic, and beneath it came the sound that stopped me mid-stride: a hammer on iron, ringing out the way men ring when they're building something meant to last.

Sable dropped flat behind a deadfall without being told, and I went down beside her, my ribs screaming where the crash had bruised them purple. Through the trees I could see it—a clearing maybe two hundred yards across, and in it, impossible, absurd, alive: tents pitched in neat rows, crates stacked under tarps, a cook fire burning steady, and men. Eight of them, maybe nine, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been lost.

The missing supplies. The missing expedition. All of it here, arranged like a general's bivouac, three days' walk from a campsite Sable had described as a slaughterhouse.

"That's not a rescue party," she whispered. Her voice was scraped raw from four days of walking. "That's an occupation."

I counted again. Rifles on two of them, slung casual. A man at the riverbank washing pots. Another splitting kindling with the easy rhythm of long habit. Nobody searching. Nobody grieving. Whatever had gone wrong back at the main camp, these men had already decided how the story ended.

"We go around," Sable said.

But then the wind shifted, carrying voices up the slope, and one of them cut through the forest noise like a blade through canvas—clear, measured, faintly amused, the voice of a man accustomed to being listened to.

"—and when the district office opens Monday, gentlemen, we will be standing there with paperwork so clean it squeaks."

I knew that voice.

Seven years is a long time to carry a sound inside you. But some voices you hear only once and remember forever, because everything after them was shaped by what they said. I lay in the deadfall dirt with my heart hammering against my cracked ribs and listened to Alden Crane hold court in the wilderness, and every mile I'd ever flown seemed to collapse into this single point of ground.

"You know him," Sable said. It wasn't a question. She'd been watching my face.

"I know him."

"Who is he?"

I didn't answer. I couldn't yet. Saying the name aloud felt like opening a door I'd nailed shut with my own hands.

Crane stood at the center of the camp near a folding table, and even at this distance he looked exactly as he had in the courtroom: tall without being heavy, silver at the temples in a way that suggested distinction rather than age, dressed in field clothes too clean for the country he was in. He gestured as he spoke, economical, precise, a surveyor's hands describing a world he intended to measure and own. Around him the armed men arranged themselves not like guards but like an audience that happened to be armed.

On the table, spread flat under stones, lay maps. Even from here I could see the colored lines running across them—red and blue, boundaries drawn by hand.

Sable saw them too. I felt her whole body change beside me, the way a bow changes when the string is drawn.

"My maps," she breathed. "That's my work. He's redrawing it."

* * *

We watched for an hour. Long enough to learn the shape of things.

There had been a split, all right—but not the one I'd imagined. Crane hadn't been stranded or besieged. He'd walked away from the main camp with the men loyal to him and the supplies, leaving the rest to starve or scatter, and the "accident" Sable had fled from—the fire, the destroyed cache, the dead—hadn't been chaos at all. It had been policy. I pieced it together from fragments drifting up the slope: a man called Harrow complaining about the cold at the main camp; another saying the homesteaders should have been "handled" years ago; Crane himself, smooth as river stone, redirecting every ugly word into something that sounded almost reasonable.

"Gentlemen. We are not arsonists. We are not thieves." A pause, timed like a man who knew his pauses. "We are the first honest witnesses to a resource this valley has been sitting on blind. Meridian Consolidated intends to develop it legally, peacefully, and profitably—for everyone. The filing goes through Monday. After that, the question of who owns what becomes a question for lawyers, not rifles. I'd hoped we could agree on that."

A murmur of assent. Not unanimous—I caught a sour note in it, a man or two unconvinced—but enough.

And through all of it, Crane's voice kept doing what it had done seven years ago in a courtroom in Fort Nelson: making ruin sound like order. Making cruelty sound like inevitability. I had sat in a courtroom gallery with my hands cuffed and listened to this man describe, in that same reasonable tone, the exact sequence of my crash—altitudes I never flew, decisions I never made, weather I'd reported clear—and the jury had nodded along because he sounded so *sure*. Certainty, I learned that year, is more convincing than truth. It requires no evidence. Only nerve.

"He's going to file Monday," Sable said. "Three days. Those are forged boundary lines—he's moved the seam onto unclaimed company leasehold. If those maps register—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Eleven families live in that valley. Eleven. I ate supper at their tables while I surveyed. The Marchetti place. Old Marta and her goats. They fed me when my rations ran short."

"The letter," I said.

She looked at me.

"Marta's letter. In your satchel?" I'd seen her tuck papers in there nights, guarding them like scripture. "Is hers in there?"

"She gave me three letters to carry out. For her daughters downriver." Sable's eyes were wet and furious at once. "Now there'll be nothing left to send them home to."

Below us, Crane rolled his maps closed with a flourish and clapped his hands once, briskly, a teacher ending a lesson. Men dispersed to chores. And then—because the universe has a sense of humor I've never appreciated—one of the armed men turned toward the treeline to take up a watch post, walking straight along our side of the clearing.

We backed away slow, low, moving from trunk to trunk, and got half a mile through the timber before we spoke again.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow above a frozen creek, no fire, sharing a single blanket against a cold that had teeth in it now. Sable rationed the last of the smoked fish she'd carried out of hell. I chewed mine without tasting it, and she watched me chew, and finally she said:

"You're going to tell me who he is. Because you've gone somewhere else since we saw him, and I need you here."

So I told her. Not all of it—I don't know if I'll ever tell all of it—but the shape of it.

"Seven years ago I was flying charter out of Fort Nelson. Freight, mail, medevac, whatever paid. One October night I flew a doctor out to a mining camp with a man bleeding out from a rock fall. Weather came down faster than forecast. Faster than I've ever seen it come down, before or since. I put us into a lake short of the strip. The doctor died. The patient lived."

"And they charged you."

"They convicted me. Manslaughter. Negligent operation." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it does when I recite it, which is the only way I can say it. "The forecast that night was wrong. The company's own dispatcher pushed the flight because the camp owner was a friend of the board. I filed a protest. Nobody cared. Then a surveyor working the region testified—gave them a flight path, wind readings, a timeline. Said he'd observed my aircraft from a ridge and reconstructed the descent. His numbers proved I'd flown reckless into known weather."

"Alden Crane."

"Alden Crane." The name came out of me like a splinter coming out sideways. "Except his observations were impossible. You can't see a lake approach from that ridge in cloud. I knew it then. Everyone who knew flying knew it. But he sounded certain, and certainty wins. I did eighteen months before a clerical error in my sentence calculation let me out early, and by then I wasn't Edmund Dunmore anymore. I was nobody, flying medicine into places nobody checks names. Seven years of miles instead of a life."

Sable was quiet a long time. The creek muttered under its ice.

"Why would a surveyor lie for Meridian?"

"Because Meridian asked. Because they always ask, and men like Crane always say yes, and the price is whatever the man wants most." I stared into the dark. "Mine cost me everything. His must have paid well."

"You're sure it was bought? No proof?"

"No proof. Just a pilot's knowledge of what's visible from a ridge in cloud, and seven years to turn it over." I laughed, and it came out bitter. "And tonight I find him in the woods running the same play. Forged lines. Bought certainty. Same man, same company, new valley."

Sable pulled the blanket tighter around us both. When she spoke again her voice had changed—softer, careful, the voice of a woman handling something that might detonate.

"Edmund. What do you want to do?"

What did I want to do. Every answer I had was a coward's answer. Go around. Go far around. Fly, if I still had a plane, which I didn't. Be nobody again, further north, new name, new miles.

"I want to sleep," I said, which was true, and also a lie, and she knew it, and let me have it.

* * *

They found us at first light.

Not by tracking—by accident, or by something that wore accident's clothes. Two of Crane's men, cutting wood up the creek drainage, walked into our hollow with axes over their shoulders, and there was no world in which we outran men with rifles waiting back at camp. Sable's hand went into her jacket. I put mine over hers.

"Don't," I said. "Not yet."

They marched us in without roughing us up, which told me orders had been given about exactly that. The camp in morning light looked even more like a claim staked than a camp lived in: survey stakes driven at intervals, transit set up on its tripod, flags snapping in the wind off the peaks. And at the center, pouring coffee from a dented pot with the serenity of a man hosting a garden party, stood Alden Crane.

He turned. He looked at me.

For one full second his face did nothing at all. Then recognition arrived—not surprise, exactly. Something worse. Satisfaction, arriving right on schedule, like a train he'd timetabled years in advance.

"Well," he said. "Edmund Dunmore."

My name in his mouth sounded like a cell door.

"It's Voss now," I heard myself say. "Dunmore's dead."

"Of course he is." Crane handed me the coffee, unasked, warm, deliberate—an act of ownership disguised as hospitality. He studied me the way he'd study a benchmark he'd set years ago and returned to find exactly where he'd left it. "You look well, considering. Better than the last time I saw you. Though I suppose prison agrees with some constitutions."

"I did eighteen months. You did seven years of getting rich. We both served our sentences."

Something flickered behind his eyes—annoyance, quickly filed away. He smiled instead, and turned to Sable, and the temperature of the smile changed entirely. "Miss Voss. The cartographer. We were worried about you. Walking off into the mountains alone like that, after the tragedy at the main camp. Terrible business. Fire spreads fast in dry country."

"There was no fire until your men lit one," Sable said. Her voice was level. Her hands were shaking, and I stepped slightly so my shoulder screened them from the others. "And there was no tragedy until you decided the evidence was worth more burned than read."

"Evidence." Crane sighed, genuinely sorrowful, a performance so polished it deserved its own touring company. "Miss Voss, you're a gifted technician. Truly. Your base maps were the finest I've commissioned in twenty years of fieldwork. Which is precisely why what you did with them afterward—altering boundaries, falsifying seam locations, smuggling documents out of a legitimate scientific operation—it grieves me. It will grieve the court, too, when this becomes a court matter. Forgery is a serious charge against a licensed surveyor."

He let that sit. Around us, the armed men had drifted closer without appearing to move, the way water finds its level.

"But," Crane said, and here his voice changed—dropped, warmed, became the voice from the courtroom, the voice that had once spoken my future aloud to twelve strangers, "I am not a vindictive man. I believe in second chances. Ask anyone." He glanced at me, and the smile widened by one tooth. "Ask Dunmore."

"I wouldn't," I said.

"Because you think I ruined you." He shook his head slowly, sadly, and stepped close enough that only I could hear the next part clearly. "Or because you've spent seven years wondering if you're right."

The words went in like ice water. He watched them land. He'd rehearsed this, I realized. Somewhere between the burned camp and this morning, he had rehearsed me.

"Here is what I know, Edmund." Still quiet. Still just for me. "I know you've spent seven years flying under a dead man's name because your own name is a warrant. I know you're injured, exhausted, and standing in the middle of my operation with no plane, no allies, and no proof of anything except a young woman's word against mine—and my word carries weight in this district. Considerable weight." He sipped his coffee. "And I know that somewhere in that satchel is the original survey record. The true one. The one that, if it surfaces, complicates a great many things for a great many people—including, I should mention, eleven homesteading families who currently enjoy Meridian's goodwill regarding their land titles. Goodwill that could evaporate. Accidents happen in dry country. You've seen."

"You're threatening children's homes."

"I'm describing consequences." He said it gently, the way a doctor describes a prognosis. "But none of that needs to happen. None of it. Because I'm prepared to offer you something no one else in the world can offer, Edmund. Something you've wanted every day for seven years."

He paused. He was good at pauses. He'd used one on the witness stand, right before the sentence that broke my life, letting the jury lean in.

"Recantation," he said. "A signed statement. Sworn, notarized, witnessed. My testimony of seven years ago, withdrawn, with the reasons documented—pressure from the company, altered readings, the whole architecture of it laid bare. Your conviction vacated. Your name restored. Edmund Dunmore, cleared, exonerated, free to be himself again in any town in this territory." He smiled, warm as a stove. "In exchange, Miss Voss surrenders her satchel, withdraws her claims, and takes employment elsewhere with a generous reference. And you two walk out of these mountains together, heroes to each other, debts settled. Everyone gets their life back."

The clearing was very quiet. Wind moved the survey flags. Somewhere a horse stamped.

And I stood there in the ruins of my own caution and felt the offer go through me like current through wire, because God help me, it was everything. Seven years of alias and exile and mail sacks and medicine runs and sleeping in the plane because motels asked questions—seven years measured in miles because years were unbearable—and this man was holding out the deed to all of it, signed and notarized, in exchange for one leather satchel and one young woman's silence.

I thought of Marta's kitchen. Of the letter in the satchel addressed to daughters downriver. Of the red lines on Crane's table, redrawn over other people's lives.

I thought of my name. Just my name. Mine.

"Edmund," Sable said quietly beside me. Not pleading. Just my name—the real one, the buried one—spoken like a hand extended in the dark.

Crane heard it. His eyes moved between us, reading, recalibrating, and I watched the great machine of his certainty adjust its gears.

"You needn't answer now," he said smoothly. "Walk with me to the river. Think. I'll have breakfast sent up. A man shouldn't decide the shape of his whole life on an empty stomach." He turned to signal his cook, and then, pausing, added—lightly, almost kindly, which was the cruelest thing he could have done:

"For what it's worth, Edmund, I always liked you. Even back then. You were the best stick-and-rudder man on that river, and it was a shame how it ended." A beat. "It doesn't have to end that way twice."

He walked off toward the river with his coffee steaming, leaving me standing in his camp with his offer lodged in my chest like shrapnel, and Sable watching me with eyes that had already survived one betrayal and were measuring, honestly, without flinching, whether she was about to survive another.

The morning was cold and bright and full of flags.

Three days until the filing. One satchel between us. And for the first time in seven years, someone was offering me my own name back—and the price was becoming, permanently, the man who'd sold it.
