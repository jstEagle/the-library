## 10. Seven Years of Debt

The fire in the forward camp burned high and clean, fed by split spruce that someone had stacked with a surveyor's precision—each log the same length, each row squared to the last. That was how Edmund knew it was Crane's fire before he ever saw the man. Seven years, and he still remembered the neatness. Alden Crane had never once in his life left anything crooked, including the truth.

They'd given him a tent apart from the others, and a plate of beans and salt pork he couldn't eat, and now they'd given him this: an invitation, delivered by a boy of maybe nineteen with a rifle slung wrong on his shoulder, *Mr. Crane would like a word, sir, if you're awake.*

Edmund was awake. He'd been awake since full dark, lying on a cot that smelled of canvas and kerosene, listening to the wind come down off the icefields and thinking about Sable Voss asleep somewhere on the far side of the camp with her satchel buckled to her wrist like it was part of her body. Thinking about what she'd told him on the glacier flats, in the blue hour before dawn. The gold seam under the homestead valley. The orders to move the boundary lines. The cache of evidence Crane's men had put to the torch.

Thinking about the letter from the old woman at the strip, still folded in his jacket pocket over his heart, going soft at the creases from the heat of him.

He got up because not getting up would only make the not-getting-up louder.

* * *

Crane sat alone by the fire with a tin cup of coffee and a map spread across his knees, weighted at the corners with stones. Up close he was older than Edmund remembered—not gray so much as sanded down, the way driftwood goes, all the soft grain worn out of him until what remained was hard and pale and light for its size. His eyes were the same, though. Quick, gray, and patient as a trapline.

"Dunmore." He didn't stand. He gestured at the flat rock across the fire with the cup. "Sit. You look like a man who's been walking."

"I have been walking."

"Sit anyway."

Edmund sat. The fire was good. He hated how good it was, how his body leaned into the warmth like a dog toward a hand, after days of scree and cold and the wet ache in his side where the harness had bitten him on impact. He kept his hands around his own knees and made himself look at the map instead of the flames.

It was the valley. The homestead valley, drawn in Crane's own square hand, with the river stitched in blue and the strips marked off in parcels like teeth. And across the eastern third of it, in red pencil, a line that did not belong—a line that cut through three homesteads and the mission station's hay meadow and enclosed, neatly, the whole lower slope where the old woman's son had grubbed his first crop out of alder ground two summers ago.

Edmund looked at that line for a long time.

"You've seen it already," Crane said. It wasn't a question. "The girl showed you."

"She showed me the real maps."

"Mm." Crane turned his cup slowly, once, a full rotation, watching the surface of the coffee as if it might tell him something. "And which ones do you believe?"

"The ones that match the ground."

"Sentimental answer." But there was no edge in it. If anything, Crane sounded tired. "I asked Meridian for six weeks and forty men to run this survey properly. They gave me eleven days and nine, half of them boys. You know what happens to a boundary when nobody has time to check it? Whatever the company wants it to be. I drew the honest lines first, Dunmore. I want you to know that. There's a version of this map in my head with the true boundaries on it, and it's been there since June, and no one will ever see it, because the men who sign my paychecks have already decided what the ground contains." He looked up, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "So don't stand there—I mean sit there—and tell me about honesty. Honesty is a luxury item. Like coffee. Like insulin. Like fuel."

"You could refuse," Edmund said. "You could walk out and file the true maps yourself."

"And be sued into the stone age and blacklisted from every survey contract on the coast, and the seam gets claimed anyway by whatever crew they send next, only crueller." Crane shook his head. "No. You stay inside the machine, you can sometimes slow it down. I've slowed it down for four months. Four months those families have had that I bought them. Ask them if they'd rather have my principles or my four months."

"They're burning the valley, Alden."

Something crossed Crane's face then—fast, gone, like a fish turning under clear water. "That wasn't supposed to happen yet."

"That makes it better?"

"It makes it *early*," Crane said sharply, and then he mastered himself, the way he always had, the way he had standing up in that courtroom seven years ago with his testimony folded in his breast pocket like a sermon. He set the cup down on the stone beside him. "But we're not here to talk about fires. We're here to talk about you."

* * *

The wind came off the icefield again and the fire leaned and righted itself. Somewhere across the camp a man laughed at something, short and young, and was shushed.

"Seven years," Crane said. "Do you know what I think about, some nights? Not the crash. Everybody thinks I'd think about the crash. I think about the courtroom. Third day. You in that dock with your collar buttoned wrong—one button off, do you remember? Left side. And me in the witness box with my hand on the book, and the whole time, the whole time, Dunmore, I'm looking at that button and thinking: this man can't even dress himself straight, and they're going to hang a death on him, and I'm going to be the one who ties the knot."

Edmund said nothing. His mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the fire.

"I told them your load manifest was falsified," Crane went on, quietly, evenly, a man reciting coordinates. "I told them you'd flown overweight out of Whitehorse twice that season and I'd warned you. I told them the engine failure was secondary—that you'd have made the field if you hadn't been heavy. None of it was true. The manifest was clean. I checked it myself afterward, which is the part that keeps me up, if you want the truth of it. I checked it, and it was clean, and I testified anyway." He picked up his coffee and drank, and set it down again, precise as everything else he did. "Meridian's counsel wrote most of it. I edited. I'm rather good at editing."

"Why." The word came out of Edmund like something pried loose with tools.

"Because they owned the lease on every job I'd ever want, and they offered me chief of survey at thirty-one, and I was vain, and I was poor, and I told myself the dead man wouldn't care." Crane's voice didn't waver. That was the terrible thing about it; it never wavered. "Hollis. The passenger who died. What was his name to you?"

"He was a schoolteacher going home for Christmas."

"Yes." For one moment—for less than a moment—the sanding showed, the grain underneath, something almost human moving behind the pale wood. Then it closed over. "Well. Now you know. And here is what knowing buys you."

He reached into his coat and brought out an envelope, unsealed, and laid it on the flat rock between them without ceremony, the way a man lays down cards he's already counted.

"An affidavit," Crane said. "Signed, witnessed, dated. My sworn statement that my testimony in the Hollis matter was procured by Meridian Consolidated, that the manifest was genuine, that the engine failure was catastrophic and unavoidable, and that Edmund Dunmore bore no culpability beyond misfortune. It's been in my kit since spring. I wrote it the same week I wrote the honest map." He tapped the envelope once with one finger. "There is a magistrate in Fort Nelson who owes me. It would take ninety days to set aside the finding. Ninety days, Dunmore, and you walk out of this country a free man with your name washed. You could fly anywhere. You could fly *home*."

The fire cracked. A knot exploded in the spruce and threw sparks up into the dark, and Edmund watched them go out one by one, higher than he'd have thought, higher than seemed possible, and he understood that he was not breathing normally and had not been for some time.

Ninety days. He did the arithmetic the way he did fuel arithmetic, automatically, involuntarily: ninety days to spring flying. He'd be cleared by breakup. He could be back on the mail run by the time the ice went out. He could land at the homestead strip and the old woman would know his name and it would be a name worth saying. Seven years of measuring his life in miles, and here was a man offering him years instead.

"What does it cost," Edmund said. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of that.

"Sensible question." Crane nodded as if Edmund had passed something. "Two things. Small things. First: the affidavit stays sealed until the girl's maps are in my possession. All of them. The originals, the tracings, her field notes. She walks out of these mountains empty-handed and goes wherever she likes—Montreal, the moon, I don't care—but the evidence goes into a Meridian safe, and in eighteen months, when the claim's registered and the money's moved, I file my affidavit, the finding gets set aside, and you're free. Nobody's hurt. Nothing's burned that hasn't already burned. The valley changes hands on paper instead of in blood, which, believe me, is the merciful version."

"And the second thing."

Crane looked at him across the fire, and for the first time all night he smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has already won and is being gracious about it.

"The second thing is that you help me convince her. She trusts you—you can see it, it's written on her like a benchmark. She'll listen to you where she won't listen to me or my boys. Tell her the truth, even. Tell her I confessed to you, tell her about the affidavit, show her the envelope. Just tell her the smart play is to trade. Her paper for your freedom. She's a cartographer, Dunmore. Cartographers understand exchange value. Every map is a bargain between what's true and what's useful."

* * *

Edmund looked at the envelope.

It was a plain manila envelope, the kind survey offices used for aerial photographs, and it sat on the rock between them catching the firelight along one edge, and it weighed nothing at all, and it weighed more than the plane he'd brought down on the plateau. He thought about picking it up. His hand actually moved—an inch, two, across his knee—and stopped.

Because he could see it. That was the thing he'd never admit to anyone, the thing he'd carried like a stone in his boot for seven years: he could see it, whole and warm, the life on the other side of that envelope. The magistrate's office in Fort Nelson with its smell of stove oil and paper. The clerk reading Crane's affidavit aloud in a bored voice while Edmund stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands. The word *set aside*, which sounded like weather, like something passing over. Walking out onto the street with the frost coming off the boards in the morning sun and no charge behind his name for the first time since he was twenty-nine years old. Buying a ticket south. Seeing his sister. Sleeping a whole night without hearing the sound the schoolteacher's suitcase had made when it came through the fuselage.

He could see it so clearly it was practically memory.

And Sable Voss was asleep fifty yards away with her wrist through the strap of a satchel full of the only true maps in the world, maps that said three families owned the ground they'd broken their backs on, maps that were the difference between those families keeping their valley and losing it to a red pencil line drawn by a man too hurried to be honest and too well-paid to care.

*Tell her the smart play is to trade.*

She'd trust him. That was the blade of it, the part Crane had sharpened without seeming to touch it. She'd listened to him on the glacier with her chapped lips split and her eyes hollow and she'd believed him, because he was the first human being she'd seen in eleven days who wasn't holding a rifle, and because he'd carried her pack when her shoulder gave out, and because trust is a thing starving people spend too freely.

If he took the envelope, he wouldn't be buying his freedom with paper. He'd be buying it with her.

"You wrote this in spring," Edmund said.

"In April."

"And you've carried it five months."

"Six, now."

"So you could have filed it any time. You could have mailed it from Whitehorse. You could have handed it to the first Mountie you saw." Edmund turned and looked at Crane directly, and let him see that he saw. "But it doesn't work unless the maps are in the safe first. The affidavit isn't a gift, Alden. It's bait. You've been carrying my innocence around in your coat for six months because you knew someday you'd need it to pay for something."

The fire popped. Crane's face did not change, but something behind it did—a recalculation, quick and cold, the way a man re-runs figures when the total comes out wrong.

"You always were slow," Crane said, "but you were never stupid."

"No," Edmund agreed. "That was always the problem, wasn't it. Stupid men testify clean and sleep fine."

He stood up. His legs ached and his side pulled where the bruise had gone hard, and none of it mattered, because standing up felt like the first honest mechanical act he'd performed in seven years. He looked down at the envelope on the rock, at the neat square handwriting visible through the paper, and he found—to his own surprise, genuinely, the surprise of a man finding ice where he expected water—that he did not want it. Or rather, that he wanted it the way a drowning man wants air, hugely, desperately, and was going to refuse it anyway, and that the refusing was the first thing in seven years that had made him feel like Edmund Dunmore instead of like a pilot-shaped absence where a man used to be.

"Keep it," he said. "File it whenever you like. After. File it after the maps are public, after the counter-claim's registered, after the valley's saved. I'll wait ninety days. I've waited seven years; I can wait a season. But I'm not trading her for me. I did that math once already, with a schoolteacher's name in the column, and I've been paying it off ever since, and I am *done*—" and here his voice did break, finally, one crack in the sanded wood, "—I am done letting other people hold the pen on my ledger."

Crane looked up at him for a long moment. The firelight made his face older and younger at once.

"Ninety days," he said softly, "versus a manslaughter sentence. That's the trade you're making. You understand that. She's going to lose, Dunmore. Nine men, rifles, the river at her back, and winter coming down. She loses, and you've tied yourself to a sinking survey, and my affidavit burns with everything else." He rose too, unhurried, and folded the map along its creases with those precise hands. "I'll leave the offer open till dawn. Men change their minds at night. It's why I work nights."

He walked away toward the big tent, and the boy with the wrongly slung rifle fell in behind him, and Edmund stood alone at the fire with his heart going like an engine running too rich.

Then he heard it—faint, from the dark beyond the tents, the small deliberate sound of a buckle being worked loose from a wrist.

And when he turned, Sable Voss was standing at the edge of the firelight with the satchel already on her shoulder, and she had heard every word.
