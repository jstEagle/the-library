## 11. Theft in the Dark

Sable Voss had learned to sleep the way a fox sleeps: shallow, one ear open, body coiled for motion. It was a skill she'd learned young, in her father's survey camps, and refined over eleven days of walking out of hell with a satchel full of truth strapped to her back.

So she heard Crane's men come down the trail at dusk, boots crunching frost, voices pitched low in that particular way men talk when they think they're being quiet. She heard them stop outside the lean-to where Edmund slept. And she heard Alden Crane say, in a voice gone soft as butter:

"He'll come around. He always wanted his name back. Give him until morning."

She lay still in the dark of the supply tent, staring up at canvas gone gray with moonlight, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the moment Edmund Dunmore had walked into Crane's camp wearing the face of a man offered absolution.

Crane had not posted guards on her tent. That was the tell. A man who feared what you carried posts guards; a man who intends to take it from you later lets you rest easy. Crane thought he already owned the night. He thought the satchel would be his by breakfast, along with Edmund's silence and the valley's future, all wrapped in one tidy bargain of redemption.

He was wrong about the satchel. He might even be wrong about Edmund. But Sable had survived this long by never betting another person's soul against her own skin.

She rose without sound.

* * *

The camp sat on a gravel bench above the forward river, three tents and a cook fire burned down to orange eyes, the company plane—Crane's charter, a battered de Havilland—tied down at the edge of the strip with its engine cowled against the cold. The supplies Edmund had flown in were stacked under tarps: drums of fuel, crates of chain and tools, sacks of flour stamped with the Meridian Consolidated diamond.

And there, hanging from a peg inside Crane's own tent, because Alden Crane had never been able to resist holding what he'd stolen, was her satchel.

Getting into Crane's tent was the work of thirty seconds and seven years of hating him. She knew his habits from the expedition—the man slept like a banker, heavy and certain of his security, a lantern turned low, a revolver in its holster hung on the same peg as the satchel because he trusted the holster more than any lock. She lifted both. The revolver she left on the ground outside, dropped into the river shallows where it would rust into uselessness. The satchel she pressed to her chest like a child.

Inside it: fourteen sheets of linen-backed map. The true boundaries of the homestead valley, drawn in her own hand before Crane ordered them falsified. The seam surveys, the assay figures, the correspondence she'd copied by lantern light while the camp tore itself apart around her. Everything Meridian would burn the valley to bury.

The flap of Crane's tent stirred behind her.

She turned with the tent knife—the one she'd taken off the mess table an hour earlier—in her fist, and found herself looking not at Crane but at the boy, Pell, seventeen maybe, one of the two younger crewmen who'd refused to help burn the evidence cache and had been drinking himself stupid ever since. He stood frozen in the cold air, eyes going from her face to the satchel to the knife.

"You're stealing it," he whispered.

"I'm taking back what's mine."

His jaw worked. "They're going to fire the valley," he said. "I heard Crane tell Rourke. Tomorrow night, once the plane's shuttled the last of the men south. They'll start it upwind of the mission so it looks like a homesteader's burn got away." His voice cracked on the last word. He was a boy who had signed on to draw rocks out of mountains and discovered he'd signed on to something else entirely.

"Then you have a choice too," Sable said. "Same as everyone."

"I can't—" He looked toward Crane's sleeping bulk, then at the dark beyond the camp, and something in his face collapsed and reassembled itself into resolve, or the first raw draft of it. "The flats. You'll cross the flats? There's a game trail past the second oxbow. The big man, Rourke, he checks it at first light, but if you're across the river before—"

"Pell." She put the knife away slowly, showing him her empty hand. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." He backed out of the tent like a man retreating from a fire. "Just don't get caught."

* * *

Edmund Dunmore was awake when she came to him. She saw it in the way his breathing changed—not the slow tide of sleep but the shallow rhythm of a man lying very still, listening to his own conscience argue with itself. He'd been doing it for hours. She'd heard him turn over forty times through the canvas wall.

She crouched at the mouth of his lean-to and said nothing. Let the silence do the work.

After a long moment he sighed, and it was the sigh of a man setting down something heavy. "You heard all of it," he said quietly. "This afternoon. What he offered me."

"Every word."

"Then you know I almost said yes." He sat up. In the starlight his face was hollowed out, older than his years, the bruise from the crash gone yellow-green along his jaw. "Seven years, Sable. He stood up in that courtroom and lied me into a grave, and tonight he offered to dig me back out. All I had to do was hand him your maps and look the other way while he burns those people out." His laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "God help me, my hand was actually moving toward the satchel."

"But it stopped."

"It stopped." He rubbed his face. "Because I kept seeing that old woman at the fuel strip. Marta. Asking me to carry her letter out. Because I kept thinking about what it costs to be invisible, and how I told myself for seven years that invisibility was safety, and it turns out it's just guilt with better manners." He looked up at her. "Where are we going?"

"The pass. North and east, up the glacier valley. Three days' hard walking to the summit, then down into the headwaters and the mission station." She shouldered the satchel. "Crane's men check the game trail at first light. We cross the river now, in the dark, or we don't cross at all."

Edmund looked at the sky, reading it the way she read terrain. "Cloud coming in from the west. Wind's dropping. Cold enough tonight that the shallows will have ice at the edges." He reached for his boots. "Your wound's seeping again. Mine's worse. Between us we've got one good pair of legs."

"Then we'd best not waste them standing here."

He dressed in silence, fast and economical, a man whose hands remembered how to move in the dark. At the edge of the lean-to he paused, looking back at the camp—at Crane's tent, at the tarped supplies, at the plane that had brought him here—and Sable watched him take leave of something. Not the camp. Something older than that.

"You could still wake him," she said. "Take his offer. Walk back into that tent and be Edmund Dunmore, cleared man, by Thursday."

"No," Edmund said. "I couldn't. I just hadn't admitted it yet." He picked up the pack she'd prepared for him—flour, tea, the willow bark, the small axe—and settled it on his shoulders. "Let's go steal your maps home."

"They were never stolen. They were mine the whole time."

"Then let's go commit the least criminal theft in history," he said, and for the first time since the crash, since the fever, since Crane's honeyed voice, something that was almost a smile touched his face. "Lead on, cartographer."

* * *

The river flats at midnight were a country of silver bones.

Frost furred every stalk of dead grass; the oxbow channels lay glass-still between bars of gravel, mirroring stars so perfectly that crossing them felt like walking on the sky. The main channel ran shin-deep over clean gravel, singing its cold constant note, and the wind had died completely, which meant every sound they made carried—a boot scuff, the clink of the axe, the wet slap of their trouser legs after the crossing.

Sable went first, testing each step, the satchel slung across her chest where she could feel it beat against her ribs like a second heart. Behind her Edmund moved carefully, favoring his right leg, the wound in his thigh pulled tight and hot beneath the bandage. Twice he stumbled on the slick stones and twice caught himself without a sound, and she understood suddenly that this was a man who had spent seven years practicing silence—who had made an art form of taking up no space at all.

Halfway across the second channel, ice crackled under her boot like a shot.

Both of them froze. The sound rolled away downstream and came back off the far bank, and in the silence after it Sable heard the thing she'd been dreading: a dog. Somewhere back at the camp, a chain rattling, and then a single deep bark, and then another, closer, and a man's voice shouting—not alarmed yet, just annoyed, the way men shout at dogs in the night.

"Rourke," Edmund breathed.

"Move."

They moved. No more caution now, no more fox-silence—the arithmetic had changed, and speed was worth more than stealth. They crashed through the frost grass, splashed the shallows, climbed the near bank with their hands in the freezing mud, and came up onto the trail Pell had promised them just as a flashlight beam swung wild across the flats behind them and found the water, the broken ice, the churned mud of the bank.

"There!" someone shouted. "On the trail! Two of them!"

A rifle cracked. The bullet went somewhere above and behind them with a sound like a whip dying, and the echo came back off the mountain walls three seconds later, flattened and strange.

"Down!" Edmund grabbed her collar and hauled her into the shadow of a spruce blowdown, and they lay in the black needles breathing hard while the flashlight beams converged on the bank below. Two men now, maybe three. Rourke's voice, flat and furious: "Hold fire, hold fire, Crane wants the woman alive, wants the satchel whole—Dunmore too, don't shoot Dunmore—"

*Of course,* Sable thought, pressing her cheek to the frozen ground. *Even now. Even fleeing. Crane's orders protect us both.* Because a dead pilot couldn't recant, and a destroyed satchel proved nothing, and Alden Crane needed them alive and intact until he didn't.

"We can't stay here," Edmund whispered against her ear. "They'll bring the dog around the oxbow. Ten minutes, maybe less."

"The trail forks ahead. Left climbs to the benchland—open ground, they'd see us against the sky. Right goes into the timber and up the glacier valley." She was mapping it in her head as she spoke, laying her memory over the darkness the way she laid tracing paper over a base sheet. "Right is slower. Right is colder. Right is three days of climbing on a bad leg."

"And left?"

"Left is a bullet or a bargain."

Edmund was quiet for exactly one breath. Then: "Right. Always right. I've spent seven years learning what the left fork costs."

They went.

The timber took them in like a closing door. Behind them the flashlights swept the flats, the voices thinned and multiplied—someone running back to wake the camp, someone loosing the dog, someone firing a second shot at nothing, at the dark, at the idea of them—and then the trees thickened and the mountain swallowed the noise and it was only the two of them and the climb, breath smoking, boots finding root-holds in the black.

An hour up, when the camp was a faint orange smudge far below and the dog's voice had faded to memory, Edmund stopped against a boulder and shook so hard she thought at first it was the cold. Then she realized he was laughing—silent, exhausted, disbelieving laughter, his forehead braced on his arm.

"What?" she demanded.

"I flew into that camp to deliver cargo," he said. "That's what I told myself. Just cargo. Just a contract. And instead—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the timber, the stars, the whole enormous indifferent mountain, "—instead I'm a fugitive. Again. Running from the same man, for the opposite reason." He straightened, wincing at his leg. "There's a symmetry there I find either very funny or very damning."

"Both," Sable said. "It's usually both." She checked the satchel straps, touched the linen maps through the canvas, felt the whole shape of the next three days rise up before her like the mountain itself. "Can you make the pace?"

"I can make the pace."

"Then walk, Dunmore. First light comes whether we're ready or not—and behind us, so does Crane."

Above them the glacier valley opened its white throat to the stars, and they began, together, to climb.
