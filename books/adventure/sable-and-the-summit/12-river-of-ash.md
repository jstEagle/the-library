## 12. River of Ash

They ran the river flats with the dawn still gray behind them, and Edmund learned quickly that Sable Voss moved through country the way water moved through it—finding the low line, the soft ground, the path of least resistance that no one else could see because no one else had spent three years drawing this valley into existence on paper.

"Cut left," she said, not looking back. "The gravel bars hold our weight. The mud at the river's edge will print us like a ledger."

He cut left. His ribs screamed where the harness had bitten during the crash, and his left boot was filling again with the blood from the gash in his calf, but he kept pace, because behind them—faint, intermittent, carried downriver on the flat morning air—came the sound of men's voices and a dog.

"They won't follow past the flats," Sable said when they stopped once to breathe among a stand of black spruce. "Crane wants the satchel, but he wants it clean. He can't have a shooting this close to the homesteads. Too many witnesses."

"Witnesses," Edmund repeated. The word tasted strange in his mouth. For seven years he had arranged his life around the absence of witnesses—the empty strips, the radio silence, the miles flown between towns where nobody knew his face. "There are supposed to be forty families down this stretch of river."

Sable looked at him then, and something in her expression made his stomach drop before she said anything at all.

"There were," she said.

* * *

They smelled the smoke an hour before they saw it—not the thin blue thread of a cookfire, which he'd have welcomed like a sacrament after two days of cold rations, but a heavy, oily pall lying along the valley floor like fog that had learned to burn. Sable stopped at the edge of the tree line and put out one arm across his chest, though there was nothing ahead of them but open meadow, and for a moment neither of them moved.

The homestead strip was gone.

Edmund had landed there eleven days ago. He could close his eyes and see it whole: the mowed grass running six hundred feet along the river terrace, the windsock faded to pink, the Rasmussens' barn with its new shingles, the row of cabins set back against the trees with their gardens bright in the late summer light. Old Marta Halvorsen had stood at the edge of the field while he ran up his engine and waved until he banked, and her letter had ridden in his inside jacket pocket ever since, through the engine failure, through the crash, through the plateau, through Crane's camp, through the theft in the dark. He had promised her he'd carry it out if he could.

Now the strip was a scar of black. The grass was burned to the mineral soil in a line so straight it could only have been fired deliberately, lit at the upwind end and walked down its length by men with drip torches. The windsock pole stood alone, charred halfway up, the sock itself reduced to a few threads of melted nylon. Where the Rasmussen barn had been there was a foundation of glowing rubble, still smoking at the edges, the new shingles curled into black commas scattered across the ash. Two of the cabins were standing but scorched; two more were simply absent, as if the earth had decided to forget them.

And the gardens—God, the gardens. Rows of cabbage and potatoes gone to cinder, the bean poles standing like burnt matches, a child's swing hanging from a fire-killed birch, the seat half-burned, swaying slightly in the draft the fire had left behind.

"No accident burns like that," Sable said quietly. "An accidental fire runs with the wind and jumps. This was laid out. Lit in sections. Somebody walked it." She pulled her binoculars from the satchel—her satchel, the one worth two men's lives—and glassed the strip for a long minute. "No bodies. No equipment left behind. They evacuated them first, or drove them out, and then they burned everything that would prove the families had been here. Improvements. Title depends on improvements, out here. A cabin, a cleared field, a working strip—that's your claim. Burn it, wait out the winter, file abandonment."

Edmund stood in the ash and felt something shift in his chest, some load-bearing wall of the numbness he'd built over seven years giving way all at once. He had told himself, loading those drums at Meridian's dock, that he was just cargo, just a pilot, just a man flying supplies to a survey camp. He had told himself the same thing seven years ago, in a different way: that the crash was weather, that the death was fate, that his name on the charge sheet was a technicality he couldn't fight. He had made a religion of *just*, of *only*, of the smallness that kept him safe.

Marta Halvorsen had pressed a letter into his hands and asked him for one small kindness, and he had said yes, and he had meant it, and now he stood in the ashes of her home holding the only thing she owned that hadn't burned.

He took off his jacket. His hands were shaking. The letter was there—soft at the corners now, damp-dried twice, but whole. The envelope was addressed in a careful, old-fashioned hand: *To my son Erik, care of the District Land Office, Fort Chimo.* And beneath it, smaller: *If I do not send another, tell him what is written here is the true account.*

"She knew," Edmund said. "When I was here. She already knew what was coming."

Sable lowered the binoculars. "The surveyors staked claims through her hayfield in June. She went out and pulled up the stakes and stacked them by her gate. That's the kind of woman she is." A pause. "Was."

"Don't say was. Not yet."

"You saw the strip, Dunmore. There's no one here." She turned a slow circle, glassing the tree line, the river, the far bank. "But look—tracks. Wagon tracks, heavy loaded, heading south along the old portage trail. Fresh enough that the edges haven't crumbled. They didn't scatter. They went somewhere."

"Where?"

"The mission station. St. Anne's, on the lake below the pass. It's the only building within three days' walk big enough to hold forty people, and Father Bouchard doesn't turn anyone away." She said it with the flat certainty of a woman reading her own map—which she was, literally, unfolding the true survey from the satchel and tracing the portage with one finger. "Two days' hard walk if we push. Three if your leg gets worse, and it will get worse."

Edmund looked down at his calf. The bandage—Sable had rewrapped it at Crane's camp with a strip of shirt and a smear of pine pitch, which was either medicine or madness—had soaked through again. He could feel the heat in it now, a deep wrong warmth climbing toward the knee. Infection, beginning. He'd seen it take men down in two days flat, back when he flew medevac runs and pretended he wasn't watching.

"Then we push," he said.

* * *

They followed the wagon tracks south, and the valley showed them what the fire had spared, which was somehow worse than the fire itself. A schoolhouse, untouched, its bell rope cut and taken. A well with the rope and bucket removed. Fence posts pulled and stacked in neat piles for hauling—Meridian's men were thorough, methodical, treating the erasure of forty families like a freight manifest. At one homestead a dog had been left behind, chained too short to reach its own water trough, and Edmund knelt in the dirt beside it for a long moment with his hand on its skull before Sable said, gently, "We can't take it where we're going," and he unclipped the chain and the dog trotted off toward the river and did not look back, and he understood that even the animals had somewhere better to be than here.

Near midday they found the first of the abandoned things that told the story in full. A trunk, dragged halfway down the portage trail and left where the wheels had bogged—a family's whole portable life, and they'd chosen speed over it. Inside: christening clothes, a photograph album swollen with damp, a man's good suit wrapped in newspaper dated three weeks ago. On top, weighted under a stone so the wind wouldn't take it, a note in pencil:

*Gone to St. Anne's. If anyone comes looking — the company men came at night. Said the valley was condemned, said there was disease in the water. There is no disease. They gave us till sunrise.*

No signature. No need for one. Every family on the river would have written the same words.

Edmund read it twice and then folded it into his jacket beside Marta's letter, and Sable watched him do it and said nothing, but something passed between them that was better than speech—the understanding that he was no longer carrying cargo. He was carrying evidence. He was carrying testimony. He was becoming, mile by mile, ash heap by ash heap, the witness the valley needed, whether he'd volunteered or not.

* * *

They camped that night in a stand of birch above the trail, no fire, sharing the last of the expedition biscuits Sable had hoarded through her walk out of camp. His leg had begun to throb in time with his pulse, and red streaks had appeared above the bandage, faint but unmistakable, climbing like something written upward.

"You need to lance it properly tonight," Sable said. "Not the pine pitch. A real drain, boiled blade, and then you keep weight off it tomorrow if you can."

"If I keep weight off it tomorrow we don't make the mission."

"Then we don't make the mission with you alive." She said it without cruelty, the way she said everything—as a fact of terrain. "I've navigated alone for nine days, Dunmore. I can navigate alone for three more. But I walked out of that camp because someone has to carry these maps to a place where they mean something, and you—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide to say the true thing instead of the tactical one—"you're the only person I've met who might actually be believed. You flew for them. You signed their contract. You can testify to what Meridian loads in its own planes. A cartographer with stolen maps is a thief. A pilot with maps and a confession and a contract and a burned valley behind him is a case."

He sat with that. Above the birches the stars were coming out hard and cold, the season turning overhead while the valley smoldered below, and he thought about how for seven years he had wanted exactly one thing—to be believed about the crash, about the charge, about the man he actually was—and he had wanted it so badly he'd hidden from everyone who might have granted or refused it. Now belief was being offered to him like a tool, like a role, and it terrified him more than the dog behind them or the fever in his leg, because being believed required saying things out loud, in daylight, to strangers, under his own buried name.

"Marta Halvorsen asked me to carry a letter out," he said finally. "Eleven days ago. Before any of this. Just a letter, to her son at the land office." He touched the jacket pocket. "It names every family on the river. Who held what, who improved what, who was owed what. Forty years of her memory, written down because she didn't trust paper filed by the company clerk." He laughed, short and bitter. "I thought it was a small kindness. An errand."

"And now?"

"Now it's the passenger manifest of a burned valley." He looked at her across the dark. "I'm going to deliver it, Sable. To the district office, to her son, to whatever passes for law out here. And I'm going to stand there under my own name when I do it."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "That's the first time you've said your name like you meant to keep it."

* * *

They came down to the lake at dusk the second day, and the mission station of St. Anne's rose out of the shore mist like something from an older century—log walls, tin roof, a small white chapel with a cross that caught the last light. It should have been a relief. Instead Edmund stopped at the tree line with his heart hammering, because the grounds were covered in people. Dozens of them. Families camped on the lawn in tents of sewn-together tarps, cooking over small fires, children everywhere, horses picketed along the fence—and drawn up on the flat below the chapel, a company floatplane at anchor on the lake, its Meridian markings pale in the fading light, a crew of four men lounging against its floats with rifles laid casually across their knees.

"They're still shuttling them out," Sable breathed. "South. One load at a time. That plane's been running families to Fort Chimo for—" she counted the camps, the faces, her jaw tightening— "days. They're calling it evacuation assistance. Free transport. Compassionate relocation." Her voice cracked on the word. "Every family that boards that plane signs a receipt for 'assisted departure.' That receipt kills their claim."

Edmund watched an old man help his wife toward the floatplane, both of them carrying bundles, both of them moving like people leaving a funeral. Somewhere in that crowd was Erik Halvorsen, or wasn't. Somewhere was the rest of Marta's letter's list, boarding a company aircraft one family at a time, signing away forty years of ground with each landing.

His leg was on fire. The red streaks had reached his knee. Behind them, somewhere north, Crane's men were surely moving. Ahead of them, a company crew with rifles stood between the valley's last families and the truth.

"Sable," he said. "How fast can you walk?"
