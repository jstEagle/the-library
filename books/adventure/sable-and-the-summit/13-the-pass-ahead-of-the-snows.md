## 13. The Pass Ahead of the Snows

Sable Voss had climbed mountains before, but never with a man who was dying by inches and pretending not to notice.

She watched him from ten feet up the slope, where she'd stopped to fix the strap of her pack. Edmund Dunmore climbed the way he flew, she suspected—economically, stubbornly, hiding the cost in his jaw. But the cost was showing now. He favored his left leg on every third step, and when he thought she wasn't looking, he pressed his palm flat against his thigh as if he could hold something inside it.

"Your leg," she said.

"It's fine."

"The streaks reached your knee yesterday. Today they'll reach your hip. After that we stop having this conversation, because you won't be able to have it."

He looked at her then, and she saw him weigh the lie against the truth and choose a third thing: silence. He shouldered past her up the scree without answering, which was its own kind of answer, and Sable followed, counting his steps the way she counted everything else.

The pass ahead of them was a gray notch in the spine of the range, maybe two days' climb if the weather held, four if it didn't. Behind them, somewhere down among the burned homesteads and the river flats, Crane's men were moving. She hadn't seen them since the ridge at dawn, but absence of smoke didn't mean absence of pursuit. It meant patience. Crane had always been patient with other people's lives.

She checked the sky out of habit. High cirrus, feathered and thin, coming in from the northwest like a rumor. Snow weather, or the promise of it. The dry season was spending its last coins.

*Two days,* she told herself. *Maybe three.*

The satchel rode against her back, heavy with paper that weighed more than lead. Every map in it was worth more than the gold seam it described—not to Meridian, who wanted it buried, but to the families below, who needed it read aloud. She'd carried it out of a camp full of dead men and worse men, and she would carry it over this range or be found frozen with it strapped to her spine. There was no version of the future where she set it down.

Edmund stumbled. Caught himself. Kept climbing.

Sable said nothing, and hated herself a little for it.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a hollow below the tree line, a wind scoop between two granite shoulders where a stand of stunted pines broke the worst of the cold. Sable built the fire small—a cook fire, not a signal—and boiled snowmelt for the willow bark she'd been stripping from the trees since noon. Aspirin they had none of. Medicine had gone downriver with the evacuees, along with everything else the valley had owned.

Edmund sat propped against his pack with his trouser leg rolled to mid-thigh, and for once he let her look.

The wound itself was nothing dramatic—a tear from the wreck's torn aluminum, six inches long, closed with the crude stitches she'd put in it beside the plateau. What was dramatic was the country around it: skin tight and shining, hot as a stove lid, red lines climbing toward his groin like ivy on a wall.

"You knew," she said. "In the valley. You knew what this was."

"I've had cuts go bad before."

"Not like this." She pressed two fingers below the swelling and watched his face for the flinch. He gave her the jaw instead, that locked-down pilot's face, but the flinch lived in his eyes. "This is blood poisoning, Dunmore. This is the thing that kills men in tents."

"Then I'll walk faster."

"That's not—" She stopped herself. Arguing with him was like arguing with weather. She unwound the ragged bandage, cleaned the wound with the last of their iodine, and repacked it with moss that was probably useless and certainly better than nothing. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was one of the few things about herself she'd never doubted. "You need a doctor. A real one. Which means we get over this pass, which means you keep that leg moving, which means you eat everything I give you and drink until you're sick of water. Those are the terms."

"And if I don't agree to the terms?"

"Then I take the maps and go, and you can explain to Crane's men why you're sitting alone at ten thousand feet."

It was a bluff and they both knew it. She wouldn't leave him. She'd already decided that somewhere on the scree that afternoon, watching him hide his limp, and she resented the decision the way you resent a stone in your boot—constant, small, undeniable.

Edmund almost smiled. It was the first time she'd seen anything like humor on him, and it changed his whole face, made him briefly someone else, someone younger. "Those are the terms," he agreed.

She fed him half the smoked fish they'd traded for at the mission station and made him drink three cups of willow bark tea, bitter enough to make his eyes water. Then she took out her notebook—the working notebook, not the satchel—and began to plan tomorrow's route by the light of the small fire.

Above the hollow, through the black lattice of the pines, the stars were coming out hard and cold, the way they did at altitude, sharp enough to cut. Sable found Polaris, found Cassiopeia wheeling around it, and felt the old calm settle over her, the cartographer's calm: the world is knowable, the world has lines, follow the lines.

"What are you doing?" Edmund asked.

"Navigating."

"There's no map for this."

"There's always a map." She turned the notebook so he could see: a rough sketch of the range, contour lines drawn from memory, the pass marked with an X, azimuths written in her cramped hand. "I surveyed this country two years ago for the company. Before I understood what the company was. Every ridge, every drainage—I walked it or I drew it from aerials. It's all still in here." She tapped her temple. "Maps don't live on paper. Paper just keeps them honest."

He studied the sketch for a long moment. "You drew all this from memory?"

"Aerial photographs, mostly. Memory fills the gaps." She paused. "My father taught me the memory part. He was a surveyor too, back east. He used to say a map was just a story told with numbers, and every story could be told true or told false. The surveyor chooses which." She looked into the fire. "Crane chose false. That's the whole war, right there. One man choosing to tell it false."

Edmund was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough. "I spent seven years telling my own story false. To everyone. Mostly to myself."

"I know."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because nobody flies medicine into the interior for seven years unless they're running from something. The mail run is where pilots go to disappear." She met his eyes across the fire. "I grew up around men like you. Good ones, running. My father hired them. Half of them died running and the other half stopped running eventually, one way or another."

"And which am I?"

"Undecided." She rolled into her blanket, back to the fire, back to him. "Get some sleep. We move at first light, and you're going to need every hour of it."

* * *

The second day the cirrus thickened, and the mountain began to speak in the language of weather.

Sable heard it first as a change in the wind—a new note under the old one, higher, colder, coming down off the summit icefields the way katabatic winds do, smelling of nothing at all. By midmorning the peaks ahead had pulled cloud caps over their heads like men pulling hats down before a fight. By noon the light had gone pewter and the temperature had dropped ten degrees, and the scree under their boots had begun to squeak with the first grit of rime.

Edmund was worse.

He didn't complain—that seemed to be physically impossible for him—but the signs were all there for anyone who could read terrain, and Sable could read terrain. His pace had dropped by a quarter. He'd stopped talking somewhere above the tree line, conserving himself, and now he navigated by fixing his eyes on her heels and following them the way a tired dog follows. Twice he'd swayed on a narrow ledge and caught himself against the rock, and both times he'd looked at her afterward with an expression of such fierce, furious embarrassment that she'd pretended not to see.

At the midday halt she made him sit while she scouted the next pitch—a chimney of broken rock, thirty feet, the only line through a cliff band that guarded the upper cirque. From the top of it she could see the pass itself for the first time: a saddle of gray snow perhaps four hours beyond, with the far side falling away into blue shadow and, beyond that, the promised country—the long valley of the mission station, the district road, the world where maps could become law.

Four hours. Maybe five, at his pace. Maybe six.

She came down the chimney and found him asleep sitting up, chin on chest, hands loose in his lap. That frightened her more than the leg. Men fighting infection didn't sleep at halts; men losing to it did.

"Dunmore." She crouched and shook his shoulder.

His eyes opened slowly, unfocused, and for a moment he looked straight through her at something else—something behind her, or years behind him. His lips moved. She caught only a fragment, a name or a word, *—the wire—* before his gaze gathered itself and found her face.

"Sable." He said her name carefully, like a man testing ice. "Right. Yes. Moving on."

"We rest here twenty minutes. You eat. Then the chimney."

He ate. She watched him do it, mechanically, chewing with the grim diligence of a man fueling a machine he no longer expected to land well. And she sat there in the cold wind with her notebook open on her knee, drawing contour lines she didn't need to draw, and admitted to herself what she'd been refusing to admit since dawn:

He might not make the pass.

And if he went down on this side of it, she had a choice waiting for her that she did not want: stay with him and lose the season, the snows closing the pass behind them both, or take the satchel and go alone and let a man die on a mountain so that a valley could live. She had watched other people make choices like that. She had sworn, quietly, at nineteen, over her father's grave, that she would never be the kind of surveyor who left things off the map because they were inconvenient.

But the satchel. God, the satchel.

"Stop thinking so loud," Edmund said.

She looked up. He was watching her with those clear gray eyes, and though the fever had put glass in them, there was nothing confused in what they held. "You're wondering whether to carry me or the maps."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. It's the right question. Don't apologize for asking it." He pushed himself upright against the rock, wincing, and got his feet under him. "Here's your answer. If it comes to that, you take the satchel and you go. You don't hesitate, you don't look back, and you don't waste one minute of the head start feeling sorry about it. I've been dead weight before. I know how it ends."

"No."

"Yes."

"No." She stood too, and the wind shoved at both of them, and she planted herself in front of him like a claim stake. "Listen to me, Edmund Dunmore, because I'm only saying this once. Seven years ago somebody testified against you and you decided your name was a sentence you'd serve forever. Fine. That was yours to decide. But I have watched you for nine days now—carrying double loads, walking on that leg, giving away your food, standing up in front of strangers and telling them the truth about yourself when you could have stayed invisible. That isn't a guilty man. That's a witness. And witnesses don't get to quit on a mountainside." She jabbed a finger at the pass, at the gray notch in the white sky. "We both go over. Both. That's the map. Now move."

He stared at her. The wind filled the silence with its high cold note.

Then Edmund Dunmore laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound—and turned, and started up the chimney.

* * *

They gained the cirque as the light failed, and the snow began an hour after dark.

Not the dry-season dusting they might have outrun, but real snow, fat flakes falling straight down through the windless dark, the kind that meant the air mass itself had turned. Sable stood outside the shelter of a boulder field and watched it come, and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading: this much snow tonight, another day of it tomorrow, and the pass would fill. Not impassable for her. Impassable for a septic man on one good leg.

They had tonight. They had tomorrow morning, maybe. After that the mountain would vote.

She got Edmund into the lee of the biggest boulder, built the fire under an overhang where its light wouldn't leak, and force-fed him willow bark and the last of the fish and most of the water. His fever was up—she could feel it radiating off him before she even touched his forehead, and when she did touch it, her hand came away as if from a stove door. The red streaks had crossed his hip. His thigh was swollen taut as a drumhead.

"How bad?" he asked. He'd learned to ask it plainly, at least.

"Bad. Not finished." She wrapped the wound again, hating how little the ritual meant now, and settled beside him with her notebook and her star almanac of memory. Through a break in the clouds to the north, Polaris burned steady above the black teeth of the ridge. She fixed it, fixed the pass bearing, fixed the hours of darkness remaining, and wrote tomorrow's plan in numbers that assumed a man who could barely stand.

"Sable." His voice had gone strange, distant, the voice of a man talking from the far side of a room. "If I start saying things tonight that don't make sense—"

"You'll say them anyway. Fever does that. I'll listen either way."

"It was a woman," he said, to the fire, to no one. "In the other plane. In the crash. She waved at me. Right before." His eyes were open but aimed inward, at some private horizon seven years deep. "Everyone said there was no time. Everyone said nothing could have been done. But she waved, Sable. You don't wave if you've already given up."

Sable went very still.

"She waved," Edmund said again, softer, "and I have been flying away from that wave for seven years."

The snow fell. The fire ticked and shrank. And Sable Voss, who trusted no one, who had walked out of a camp of corpses with a satchel full of true maps and a heart full of locks, reached over and took the burning-hot hand of a stranger she'd known nine days, and held it, and did not let go.

"Sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we climb. Both of us. I told you—that's the map."

His eyes drifted shut. His breathing went ragged, then shallow, then quick and muttering, and the fever took him down into whatever country waited below consciousness, and Sable sat alone with the fire and the snow and the satchel, keeping watch, keeping count, keeping faith.

Above the boulder, through a tearing in the clouds, the stars wheeled on—cold, indifferent, exact. She navigated by them the way she navigated by everything: trusting the lines, doubting the storytellers, choosing, night after night, to tell it true.

Toward midnight, Edmund began to talk in his sleep.
