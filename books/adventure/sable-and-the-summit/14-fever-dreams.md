## 14. Fever Dreams

The fever came for him on the second night of the climb, and it did not come gently.

It came the way weather comes to the interior—first a pressure he couldn't name, a wrongness in the air of his own body, and then all at once, without mercy. One moment he was hauling himself up a shelf of granite with Sable's rope biting into his palm; the next the mountain tilted sideways and the stars began to swim like fish in dark water.

He remembered her voice saying something about his leg. He remembered saying it was fine. He remembered lying.

Then there were no more moments, only miles—not flown this time but fallen through, endless and burning, and somewhere below him a valley full of smoke that smelled like home.

* * *

The old crash came back to him whole, which was how dreams worked, giving him back everything the waking world had rationed away.

The rain over Kettle Narrows. The load shifting in the gusts—crates of drill steel, badly tied by men who didn't care because the cargo wasn't theirs. The engine coughing as she iced up, the stick going heavy in his hands, heavy as a coffin lid. He had been twenty-six years old and he had done everything right, or nearly everything, and nearly was the word that had ruined him. Nearly enough altitude. Nearly enough time. The trees coming up green and patient, like they'd been waiting seven years already even then.

And the sound afterward. That was what no one asked about at the trial—the sound. Not the crash itself, which was over too fast to hear, but the quiet after, rain ticking on bent metal, and him hanging upside down in his harness listening to a man die thirty feet away and knowing, knowing with the whole animal certainty of his body, that if he had been ten minutes earlier, or ten minutes luckier, the man would be alive.

They said his name in the courtroom like it was a thing that could be weighed. *Edmund Dunmore.* Two counts of negligence. One count of manslaughter. The surveyor Alden Crane, neat in a gray suit, testifying that Dunmore had been warned about the weather window, that Dunmore had chosen to fly anyway, that Dunmore's logs showed a pattern of risk-taking. Every word true the way a knife is true. Every word leaving out the company dispatcher who ordered the flight, the company mechanic who signed off an engine Crane knew was failing, the company man who needed the drill steel on site before the season closed and didn't care whose name got burned to get it there.

Seven years. He had measured them in miles since. Miles were honest. Miles didn't ask you who you used to be.

But the fever didn't deal in miles. The fever dealt in years, and it made him count every one.

* * *

He woke once, briefly, to snow-light and Sable's face above him, sharp-boned and filthy, her breath steaming.

"Drink."

Something bitter hit the back of his throat. Willow bark, boiled to tar. He gagged; she held the tin steady with the patience of someone feeding a stubborn animal.

"The leg," she said. "Show me."

"It's fine."

"You said that yesterday. Yesterday you also told me the mountain was a woman named Ruth and that we should ask her for directions." She peeled back the blanket and the wrapping, and he heard her breath change when she saw it—the small, controlled inhale of a person recalculating. The gash from the crash, the one he'd cleaned with whiskey and ignored, had gone hot and angry, the skin around it tight and shining, red lines crawling up toward his knee like slow lightning.

"How long," he managed.

"A day and a half. You walked some of it. Mostly you leaned." She rewrapped it with strips of shirt boiled in snowmelt, her hands quick and unsentimental. "It's infected. If we were anywhere with a doctor, you'd have lost the leg by now, so consider yourself lucky to be lying on a mountain where nobody can take it."

"That's not lucky."

"It's a matter of perspective." She pressed the tin to his mouth again. "Drink. All of it. You're going to burn tonight whether you want to or not, so we might as well starve the infection while you're at it."

He drank. It tasted like punishment. Somewhere behind her, the fire cracked and threw orange light up a wall of black rock, and the snow came down soft and indifferent, and Edmund thought: *this is what the inside of my head looks like,* and then he was gone again.

* * *

This time the dream braided itself, old crash into new, until he couldn't tell the threads apart.

He was in the cockpit of the old de Havilland, but the instruments were wrong—the altimeter read *years*, the needle sweeping backward through seven of them—and out the windshield there was no horizon, only the plateau, white and waiting, and smoke rising from it in a column that bent against the wind into letters. He tried to read them. They spelled his name, then unspelled it, then spelled another name he almost recognized, a name from a courtroom, spoken in a gray suit.

*You could have turned back,* said the dispatcher's voice, from everywhere, from the radio, from under the floorboards. *You always could have turned back.*

"I was told to fly," Edmund said, or dreamed he said.

*Told. Asked. What's the difference when the plane goes down? The dead don't distinguish.*

"They would have fired me."

*Yes. And?*

And there it was, the thing he had never said aloud in seven years, the thing under the exile and the routines and the careful miles: *I was afraid.* Not of dying. Of being nothing. Of losing the one identity he had built after the trial took the first one away. Pilot. Bush flyer. The man who got through. Take that away and there was just Edmund Dunmore, manslaughter, the name people said quietly, the name he had spent seven years trying to leave behind in every town he refueled in, as though a name were a coat you could hang up and walk away from.

The dream laughed at him. Dreams are allowed to be cruel.

Then the cockpit filled with smoke—not engine smoke, homestead smoke, the sweet foul stink of burning spruce and burning barns—and he was standing in the ashes of the strip at Miller's Bend, the old woman's letter in his jacket pocket, the paper scorched at the edges but the words still legible, and the words were changing, rearranging themselves into a list of names, and every name was a family, and every family was looking at him, and none of them said anything, which was worse than accusation. They just waited. As if he owed them something. As if seven years of silence were a debt that interest had been accruing on the whole time.

He woke screaming, or thought he did. Hands were on his shoulders, strong and cold.

* * *

"Edmund." Sable's voice, close, stripped of everything but function. "Edmund. Look at me. Look here."

Snow-light. Her face. The fire rebuilt higher than before, throwing heat across his face. His shirt was soaked through with sweat and she was wringing it out over the flames without ceremony, then draping it back over him damp, which should have been misery and was instead the first relief he'd felt in two days.

"You were calling for someone," she said. "A woman. Then a man. Then you were arguing with both of them."

"My wife left me the year after the trial." His mouth said it before his judgment could stop it. Fever loosens locks. "She said I'd died in the crash too, I just hadn't lain down yet."

Sable fed the fire a branch. Watched it catch. Said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

"What did I say?" he asked. "When I was arguing."

"Doesn't matter."

"Sable."

"You told the man—" She paused, choosing. "You told him you weren't the one who should have paid. And then you told him you'd paid anyway. And then you told him it hadn't worked, paying, that it never works." She looked at him straight. "That last part sounded like the truest thing you've said since I pulled you out of that wreck."

He turned his face toward the rock wall. Shame has a temperature; he was already burning with it.

"Your wound is septic," she went on, relentless, gentle the way a scalpel is gentle. "You know that. Infection doesn't care about your guilt. It will kill you in three days if it turns, and it's turning. So I need you to do the hardest thing you've done on this mountain, harder than the scree, harder than the river flats."

"Walk?"

"Stop punishing yourself long enough to live. Your body is trying to quit because your mind gave it permission. I've seen it before. Men who want to die do, out here, very efficiently." She handed him the willow bark tin again, fresh and boiling. "So decide. Right now, while you're lucid. Are you Edmund Dunmore, the sentence? Or are you the man who flew medicine into places nobody else would go for seven years, and carried an old woman's letter out of a burning valley, and is going to put a set of true maps in front of a district clerk if I have to carry him on my back to do it?"

"The court decided what I am."

"The court decided what you *did*." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose; it cut. "There's a difference, and you've been hiding inside it for seven years. A sentence is something other people serve on you. A name is something you carry. You've been carrying the sentence and calling it your name because carrying a sentence means you don't have to choose anything ever again. It's very convenient, being convicted. Somebody else already decided your story."

"And yours?" The anger came up through the fever, hot and grateful for an exit. "You walked out of a camp that went wrong. You've got a satchel you won't let me touch and a way of talking like you've already read my file. What are you carrying, Voss?"

For a moment he thought she'd leave. She looked at the fire a long time, snow gathering in her hair, and when she spoke it was quiet.

"Three names," she said. "Men who followed Crane because I was too slow getting the maps out, and Crane's men burned the cache with them standing near it. I carry their names the way you carry yours. The difference is I'm not using mine as an excuse to disappear." She stood, brushed the snow off her knees. "Drink that. Sleep if sleep comes. We move at first light, fever or no fever, because the pass closes in four days and I'd rather drag you over it than bury you under it."

* * *

Sleep came, but gentler now, and the dream changed shape.

He was flying again, but low and slow, the way he flew the mail runs in good weather, and the valley below was green instead of burning, and the homesteads stood whole, fences mended, gardens in rows, and the old woman stood at the edge of her strip and raised one hand—not waving exactly, acknowledging—and the letter in his pocket was blank at last, delivered, its work done. And in the seat beside him sat no ghost and no dispatcher, just open air and the hum of a healthy engine, and the altimeter needle rested on a number that meant nothing except how high he was, and that was all it needed to mean.

He woke before dawn to snow tick-ticking on the tarp Sable had rigged, and found her already packing, already watching the eastern ridge where the sky was going the color of a healing bruise.

"Fever's down," she said. It wasn't a question; she'd had her hand on his forehead sometime in the night, apparently, and hadn't woken him to announce it.

"Barely."

"Barely moves a plane, too, if you push it right." She tossed him a strip of dried fish and the boiled-willow tin. "Eat. Drink. Today we make the col before noon or we make nothing."

He ate. He drank. When he stood, the leg held, complaining but holding, and the red lines had faded to pink, and he chose—deliberately, the way she'd told him to—to believe that meant something.

At the edge of camp he paused, looking back down the way they'd come, at the white silence of the plateau where his plane lay folded like a dead bird.

"Sable. Last night. What I said about my wife—"

"Forget it."

"No." He tested the word in his mouth, new and strange. "No. I'm done forgetting things. Let's climb."

Above them, the pass waited, a thin gray notch in the rampart of the range—and beyond the notch, faint on the wind, a smell that did not belong to snow or stone.

Smoke. Fresh smoke, and a lot of it, drifting up from the far side of the mountains like a question neither of them wanted asked aloud.
