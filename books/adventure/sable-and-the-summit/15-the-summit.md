## 15. The Summit

Sable counted the switchbacks the way she counted everything now—in hours, in margins, in the width of the gap between them and the weather.

Fourteen. The trail had given up being a trail two switchbacks ago, becoming instead a suggestion of scree and lichen-crusted stone that her own map, drawn from a surveyor's vantage point three years ago, insisted was passable. She had drawn that line herself, in ink, with a steady hand, and she had written beside it: *Grade acceptable. Snow-free by midsummer.* She had never climbed it. She had drawn it from a ridge across the valley with a telescope and the arrogance of a woman who believed that distance was the same as knowledge.

She was paying for that arrogance now, one switchback at a time, with Edmund Dunmore leaning on her shoulder and the sky coming down to meet them.

"Talk to me," she said. "Tell me what you see."

"Grey," Edmund said. His voice had been scraped thin by four days of fever, and it came out of him now like something dragged over gravel. "Grey rock. Grey sky."

"Look harder."

He lifted his head. She felt the effort of it through his arm, the way a man's whole body participates in the act of looking when the looking is all he has left. "A bird," he said. "High up. Maybe a chough."

"Good." She shifted her grip under his arm and took the next step, and then the next. "A chough means insects. Insects mean the wind is off the glacier, not down the valley. Down-valley wind would be warmer, but it would mean weather behind us. This is better."

"You're lying about the weather."

"I'm not lying about the chough."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed out of him somewhere below the tree line. She had learned to read his sounds over the past four days, the way she'd learned to read the terrain—by attention, by repetition, by the slow accumulation of small data points that added up to a picture. There was the sound he made when the fever climbed, which was silence. There was the sound he made when his leg seized on him, which was a hiss through the teeth, quickly suppressed, because he was the kind of man who apologized for pain. And there was this sound, dry and almost amused, which meant he was still in there somewhere, still flying his own body the way he flew that wrecked plane of his—hands steady, eyes forward, refusing to look at the gauges.

"Rest point," she said, and steered him onto a boulder that the sun had been working on all morning, its south face almost warm.

He sat down the way a collapsed building sits down. She crouched beside him and unwrapped the last of the willow bark, and while he chewed it—bitter, fibrous, the taste of desperation—she looked back the way they'd come.

The first snow was falling behind them.

It wasn't falling on them. Not yet. It hung in the high air to the north like a curtain someone had drawn across the range, a soft grey veil that erased the peaks they'd crossed four days ago, that erased the plateau where the plane sat with its broken back, that erased the whole country they had bled and starved and burned their way out of. It was beautiful. It was also a door closing, and they were on the wrong side of it, and the hinge was this pass, this saddle of broken stone maybe four hundred meters above them, and the door was closing at the speed of falling snow.

She had told Edmund it was three days to the pass. She had told him that four days ago, when he could still walk on his own and she could still pretend her calculations had room in them for error. They had used the three days and then some. The fever had cost them a full day and most of another—Edmund shivering under the one tarp, teeth chattering so hard she'd been afraid he'd crack something, while she boiled willow bark and snowmelt and talked to him in a flat, steady voice about contour lines and magnetic deviation, anything, anything to keep him anchored to the side of the line where people stayed alive.

He had talked back, in the worst hours. That was how she'd learned about the crash seven years ago, the one that wasn't his fault, the one that had been made his. He'd told her about it in fragments, in the third person, as if it had happened to a man he'd read about. *The load shifted. The strip was short. The boy shouldn't have been there.* And then, near dawn, in the first person, barely audible: *I checked everything. I checked everything, and it still wasn't enough.* She hadn't corrected him. There was nothing to correct. She knew what it was to carry a thing like that—the survey had given her her own version of it, a satchel full of true maps that felt, some mornings, heavier than any lie.

"Drink," she said, and held the canteen to his mouth.

He drank. Then he said, quietly, without opening his eyes, "How far?"

"Four hundred meters. Maybe less."

"And the snow?"

"Behind us. For now." She looked at the curtain of weather, measuring it against the wind, against the altitude, against everything she knew and half of what she was guessing. "A day," she said. "Maybe a day and a half. If we were standing here a day from now, we'd be standing here all winter."

He opened his eyes then. They were clearer than they'd been yesterday—still fever-bright at the edges, but focused, and underneath the exhaustion there was something she had first seen in him back at the wreck, when he'd pulled himself out of the shattered cockpit with a bleeding leg and started salvaging the radio before he'd even checked his own wounds. It was the thing she had decided, somewhere on the trail, to bet both their lives on.

"Then we don't stand here," Edmund said.

"No," Sable said. "We don't."

* * *

The last four hundred meters took them three hours.

It should have taken ninety minutes. She had budgeted for ninety minutes, in her head, the way she budgeted everything, and the mountain had gone through her budget like water through a torn map. Edmund's leg—the one the fever had gotten into, the one that had swollen until the boot wouldn't lace—could manage perhaps fifty steps before it demanded a toll, and the toll was always the same: a rock to sit on, a swallow of water, sixty seconds of his jaw working silently against the pain. She stopped counting the tolls after the first hour. Counting them only made the arithmetic worse.

The scree was the worst part. A whole slope of it, loose stone stacked on loose stone, the kind of ground that took a healthy man's patience and gave it back to him as terror. For Edmund it was something else entirely. She went first, testing each hold, calling the good lines up to him—*left of the rib, stay on the grey, don't trust the brown*—and he came after her with the slow, deliberate precision of a man crossing a river on stepping stones, except that the stones were moving and the river was gravity and the far bank was four hundred meters of open air.

At the worst of it, halfway up the scree field, his boot slipped.

She heard it before she saw it—the scrape of leather on stone, the small, wrong sound of a body beginning to disagree with a mountainside. She turned in time to see him go down onto one knee, sliding, arms windmilling, his satchel—their whole shared inventory of nothing—starting to swing him sideways down the slope.

"Edmund."

"I'm—" He caught himself on a stone the size of a skull. Held. Breathed. "I'm here."

"Give me your hand."

"I can—"

"Give me your *hand*, Edmund."

He gave her his hand. His fingers were cold and trembling and they closed around hers with a grip that had nothing gentle in it, nothing polite, the grip of a drowning man, and she braced her boots and leaned back and took his weight without a word, and for a long moment the two of them hung there on the face of the scree, connected, breathing, the wind pulling at their clothes and the snow-curtain hanging in the north like a held breath.

Then he found his feet, and they went on.

She didn't say anything about it afterward. Neither did he. But something had changed in the geometry between them, some final degree of angle, and she felt it in the way he took her arm at the next rest point without being asked, and in the way she offered it.

The saddle, when they finally reached it, was nothing.

That was the thing about summits that no one who hadn't mapped them ever understood. You climbed for days, you bled and starved and burned your way up a wall of stone, and the top of it—the actual top—was a wind-scoured notch between two shoulders of rock, wide enough for six men to stand in, littered with the small bones of some animal that had made it this far and no farther. The wind hit them the moment they came over it, a flat, physical blow that smelled of ice and distance, and Sable turned her back to it and got Edmund down behind the shoulder of rock, out of the worst of it, and only then, only when he was seated and breathing and safe, did she let herself turn around and look south.

She forgot the wind. She forgot the cold, the fever, the four days of willow bark and arithmetic and fear.

The world fell away beneath them.

The range dropped from the saddle in a long, staggering plunge—first scree, then a shelf of dark pine two thousand feet down, then the grey-green roll of the lower forests, and then, opening beyond all of it like a page being turned, the valley.

The homestead valley.

She knew it from above. She had mapped it from above, from the expedition's plane, from the ridge across the water, from every angle except this one, and none of those angles had prepared her for the sight of it from here—from *inside* the world instead of over it. The river ran down its length in a single bright braid, catching the afternoon light and throwing it back up at them like something alive. The fields were the color of old gold—not the gold in Crane's reports, not the gold that had gotten men killed, but the honest gold of cut hay and harvested barley, laid out in irregular patches around the small, stubborn clearings where people had spent years persuading the forest to let them live. She could see the strips. She could see the fences. She could see, tiny with distance, the roofs of the homesteads along the lower river, and the church at the mission station with its small grey spire, and the smoke rising from chimneys in thin, domestic threads that meant *someone is home, someone is cooking, someone is living their ordinary life down there and does not know—*

The smoke.

She went still. There was a thread of smoke that didn't belong. It rose from the middle valley, from the stretch of the river where the Voss survey maps said there was nothing but meadow and the Kovalenko strip, and it was the wrong color—too dark, too straight, too *organized*—and it wasn't one thread. It was three.

"Edmund," she said.

He was already looking. He had seen it before she had—of course he had, he was a pilot, he read smoke the way she read contours—and his face had gone the color of the stone they were sitting on.

"That's the middle valley," he said. "That's—" His voice did something she hadn't heard it do before. It cracked. "The letter. The letter in my jacket is from the woman at the strip. Marta Kovalenko. She asked me to carry it out, and I never—" He stopped. Started again. "Those are her fields."

Sable looked. The three threads of smoke were rising from a cluster of buildings at the river bend, and even at this distance, even through the cold clean air, she could see that the smoke was wrong for cooking and wrong for a chimney and exactly, precisely right for burning.

Then she saw the plane.

It came up the valley from the south, low and steady, a company aircraft with the Meridian Consolidated markings she could not see and did not need to see—she knew the silhouette, knew the drone of it, had spent three weeks at the expedition camp listening to that engine come and go. It flew the length of the visible valley at the altitude of a man on horseback, unhurried, proprietary, the way a landlord walks a property line. At the far end it banked, turned south, and dropped out of sight behind the ridge, and two minutes later it came back again—empty, she'd have sworn, by the way it sat in the air—and turned south again.

Shuttling. It was shuttling men. Or equipment. Or papers. It was moving the pieces into place, whatever the pieces were, and it was doing it in broad daylight, in full confidence, because whoever was flying it believed that no one in the world was left to see.

"Crane's men," Edmund said. "He's moving them down. He's—God. He's already started."

"He started the day the survey found the seam," Sable said. "Everything since has been logistics."

The words came out of her flat and cold, and she heard them as if someone else had said them, someone harder, someone she'd had to become somewhere on the mountain between the wreck and here. But underneath the flatness her heart was going like a hammer, because she was doing the arithmetic again, the only arithmetic that had ever mattered, and the numbers had changed.

The pass would close in a day. That number was fixed. But the valley—the valley was three days' hard descent, two if they pushed, and the mission station was another day beyond that, and somewhere down there, in the smoke and the braided river and the small grey church, were people who had maybe a week, maybe less, before the last piece of Meridian's logistics clicked into place and the valley stopped being theirs.

She turned to Edmund. He was still looking at the smoke, and his hand had gone, without his seeming to notice, to the inside of his jacket, where the letter was—the letter he had promised to carry and hadn't, the letter that had ridden through a crash and a fever and four days of mountain because he would not leave it behind, because he was a man who kept promises even to the dead.

"The snow closes this pass tomorrow," Sable said. "A day later and we'd have been on the wrong side of it until spring. Do you understand what that means?"

He looked at her. The wind pulled at his hair, and his eyes were fever-bright and clear at the same time, and she saw the moment the arithmetic landed in him, the moment the numbers stopped being about survival and started being about everything else.

"It means we were supposed to see this," he said. "Today. Exactly today."

"It means the mountain gave us a day," Sable said. "One day. And we're going to spend every hour of it going down."

She shouldered the satchel—the true maps, the whole weight of the truth, the thing she had carried out of a camp that went wrong and over a mountain that nearly killed them both—and stood up into the wind. Below her the valley lay open in the afternoon light, beautiful and burning, the river bright as a wire, the smoke rising, the plane turning south with its invisible load.

"Three days," she said. "If we're lucky, two. Can you walk?"

Edmund Dunmore looked down at the valley for a long moment. Then he took his hand off the letter in his jacket, and put it on the rock beside him, and pushed himself up onto his feet—slowly, swaying, his bad leg trembling under him—and stood.

"I can walk," he said.

They started down.
