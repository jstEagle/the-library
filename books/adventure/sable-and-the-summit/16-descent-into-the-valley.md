## 16. Descent into the Valley

The mountain let them down by degrees, as if reluctant to give up what it had taken.

For two days they followed the spine of the ridge southward, then cut west where Sable's map said a goat trail became a deer trail became, if you believed hard enough, a man's trail. Edmund's wound had opened twice on the scree fields above, and he'd bound it with strips of his own shirt without telling her how much blood soaked through by morning. The fever from the pass had broken—or retreated, at least, to some low country inside him where it waited—but his legs felt borrowed, and every step down was its own small argument between gravity and pride.

Sable walked ahead of him the whole way, her satchel strapped tight across her chest like something born there. She moved differently now than she had on the plateau, when she'd found him half-dead beside his wrecked plane and looked at him the way one looks at weather—something to be assessed, endured, survived. On the summit she had stood for a long time without speaking, looking down into the valley where the smoke rose in three separate columns, thin and gray and wrong, and Edmund had watched her face do something complicated and then go still.

Now she stopped at the lip of a granite shelf and held up one hand.

"Smoke," she said. "But not that kind."

He came up beside her, breathing hard, and saw what she meant. Below them the valley opened out—the homestead valley, the one whose name he'd never learned until it burned—and along the near river bend stood a cluster of buildings with a bell tower: whitewashed walls gone gray, a tin roof throwing back the afternoon light. From the mission's cookhouse chimney rose a single column of smoke, steady and domestic, the smoke of people making bread or boiling willow bark or simply needing to see something rise.

"That's Father Anselm's station," Sable said. "The survey mapped around it. Crane called it an obstacle." She paused. "It wasn't on the burn list."

Edmund looked at the valley floor below the mission—the black stubble of the homestead strip, the collapsed ribs of barns, a wagon sitting alone in a scorched field with no animal left to pull it. His hand went, without thinking, to his jacket pocket, where the old woman's letter still rode against his chest. He had carried it through the crash, through the pass, through the fever. *If you can,* she'd said. *If you can.*

"They'll be there," he said. "The ones who got out."

"Some of them." Sable's eyes were already moving, reading the ground the way she read everything—as lines to be trusted only after verification. "And some of them will have heard a plane come down over the range two weeks ago and decided anyone walking out of those mountains is either dead or Meridian's."

"I'm both, technically."

She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to humor he'd gotten from her since the river flats, and he took it the way a starving man takes a crust.

They descended the last miles in silence, the trail switchbacking through alder and then pine, the air thickening with resin and river damp until Edmund's lungs remembered what low altitude felt like. Twice Sable stopped him with a hand on his arm and listened to something he couldn't hear. Once, far off through the trees, a dog barked—a real dog, a farm dog—and the sound hit Edmund harder than any of the mountain's terrors had. It was the sound of ordinary life continuing in spite of everything, and it made his throat close.

* * *

The mission resolved itself slowly out of the trees: a fence of peeled poles, a garden gone weedy but alive, a woodpile stacked with the neurotic precision of a man who needed order the way other men needed drink. And people. A dozen of them, maybe more, moving between the buildings with the careful, haunted economy of the recently homeless. A woman drawing water at the well froze when she saw them coming. A man splitting wood set down his axe—not casually, but the way a man sets down an axe when he intends to pick it back up.

Edmund raised both hands, palms out, the universal grammar of the unarmed.

"We're coming in," he called. "We're not armed. We need help, and we've got news worth hearing."

Nobody answered. The woman at the well kept staring. Behind her, doors opened along the mission's long dormitory wing, faces appearing in gaps like animals checking a treeline.

An old man came out of the chapel. He was tall and stooped and wore a cassock gone gray at the seams, and he walked toward them with the unhurried gait of someone who had buried enough people in this valley that two more strangers barely registered. Up close his face was all planes and patience, sun-cracked, with eyes the pale blue of glacier ice.

"You're the pilot," he said.

Not a question. Edmund felt it land in his chest like a dropped wrench.

"How—" he started.

"The wreck on the plateau. Tomas Lindqvist flew over it ten days ago hunting game trails. He said a company plane, nose-down, no movement." The priest's gaze went to Sable, to the satchel, back to Edmund. "Then two weeks later, Meridian men start flying shuttles south out of the valley, heavy-bellied, and the fires start the same week. So." He folded his hands. "You're the pilot. Which side of it are you?"

The question hung there. Around the yard, the mission had gone quiet in the way a room goes quiet before a verdict. Edmund was aware of the axe man drifting closer, of the woman at the well setting down her bucket, of a boy of maybe ten watching from the woodshed with eyes too old for his face.

Here it was, then. The moment he had been walking toward for seven years without knowing it, dressed now in priest's Latin and well water and woodsmoke.

"My name is Edmund Dunmore," he said. "I flew cargo for Meridian Consolidated for seven years. Medicine runs mostly, mail sometimes, and I told myself that made it clean. Two weeks ago they paid me to fly supplies to a survey camp upriver, and I signed because I needed engine parts and because lying to myself is the one skill I've never lost." He pulled the letter from his jacket. The paper was soft as cloth now, sweat-stained, creased into permanence. "Mrs. Halvorsen gave me this at your strip, the day before I went down. She asked me to carry it out if I could. I couldn't. I crashed on the plateau instead, and I've been walking ever since."

He held the letter out. The old man didn't take it.

"I know what you're thinking," Edmund went on, because stopping now would kill him. "That I'm the company's man. That I might be theirs still. That a pilot who flew their freight for seven years doesn't get to walk into your yard and call himself anything but what he is." His voice cracked and he let it. "You're right. All of it. I'm not here to tell you I'm innocent. I'm here because the woman next to me is carrying maps that prove Meridian falsified the survey boundaries to steal this valley, and because Alden Crane told me himself, on a night I should have walked away and didn't, that the testimony that convicted me seven years ago was bought and paid for. I know how their machinery works from the inside. I know the routes, the schedules, the names. And I am asking you"—and here his voice did fail him, and he stood in the mission yard with nothing left, a man stripped down to his one true sentence—"*please listen anyway.*"

Silence. Long enough that Edmund could hear the river, could hear a child crying somewhere inside the dormitory and being hushed.

Then the axe man spoke. "Dunmore." He said the name like he was testing a blade. "There was a Dunmore in the papers once. The crash at Kettle Narrows. Killed a surveyor, didn't he?"

"Yes," Edmund said. "That was me."

"He served time?"

"Seven years of exile counts as time, in my ledger. The courts took four. I ran from the rest."

The man spat, deliberate, into the dirt. "So we're supposed to take the word of a convicted killer who admits he's a liar."

"No," said Sable Voss.

She stepped forward, and Edmund watched the yard recalibrate around her—the way it does around a person who has decided exactly what she is and refuses to spend energy pretending otherwise. She unslung the satchel and held it up.

"You don't have to take his word. You don't have to take mine. These are the original field maps. My hand drew them; my initials are on every sheet. Compare them to whatever Meridian files at the district office and you'll see the boundary moved six miles east—six miles that happen to put every homestead in this valley inside company claim land. They didn't just burn you out. They redrew the map so that legally, none of you ever lived here." Her voice was flat and cold and absolutely certain. "I watched them destroy the evidence cache at the forward camp. I'm the only surveyor left who can prove what the ground actually says. And he"—she nodded at Edmund—"is the only man alive who heard Alden Crane confess to buying testimony and burning camps, and lived to walk out with it. Hate him if you want. Use him anyway."

* * *

Father Anselm fed them first. It was, Edmund thought, the most political act he'd witnessed in years: beans and flatbread and strong tea, served at a long table in the refectory with the survivors of the valley gathered around, because it is very difficult to condemn a man while breaking bread with him, and the priest knew it. The boy from the woodshed sat across from Edmund and stared at him with frank, unblinking curiosity until his mother pulled him away.

Afterward, in the chapel—which smelled of candle wax and pine and, faintly, of smoke that had come in on people's clothes—Father Anselm laid the maps out on a table beneath the window and let the survivors file past them.

Edmund stood apart and watched them look.

He watched a broad-shouldered farmer named Grieves trace the old boundary line with one cracked finger and find his own holding outside it, and say nothing, and go white. He watched Mrs. Lindqvist—the same family name as the pilot who'd spotted his wreck; brothers, it turned out—press her hand flat over the place where her farm had been and leave it there a long time. He watched the axe man, whose name was Corrigan, study the sheets with the suspicious thoroughness of a man who wanted them to be lies and could not make them lie.

"They filed yet?" Corrigan asked finally.

"Not yet," Sable said. "Crane's party is still coming down the mountain. The claim registration happens at the district office. Three days' travel by river, less if you can beg a truck off the logging spur."

"Meridian has trucks."

"Meridian has lawyers," Sable said. "Lawyers travel slower than desperate people."

A thin laugh went around the chapel, quickly smothered, the laugh of people rediscovering that they still owned the equipment for it.

Then an older woman stepped forward—gray-haired, sunburnt, wearing a man's coat two sizes too big—and Edmund recognized her before she turned around. Mrs. Halvorsen. Alive. Her face when she saw him was a battlefield: relief and grief and suspicion fighting for the same ground.

"You carried my letter," she said.

"I tried."

"It doesn't matter now. Everyone it named is standing in this room." She looked at the maps, then at him, and her chin came up with a stubbornness he suspected had built half the farms in the valley. "My husband cleared forty acres out of spruce with a crosscut saw. Forty acres, eleven years. They burned it in an afternoon and wrote on their map like it was empty country." Her voice didn't shake. That was the terrible thing about it. "So. Pilot. You flew for them. Tell us how they think. Tell us what they'll do next, and when, and how to be standing in front of it when it comes."

Every face in the chapel turned to him.

Edmund had flown through storms that erased the horizon, had landed on strips so short the trees combed his wheels, had sat through a trial with a courtroom full of strangers deciding what his life meant. None of it had felt like this. This was heavier than altitude. Because these people weren't asking him to be skilled or lucky or brave. They were asking him to be *useful*—to take the worst thing about himself, the seven complicit years, and turn it into a weapon pointed the other way.

"They think you're finished," he said. "That's their first assumption and their biggest mistake. Crane believes fear does his work for him—that burned-out people scatter, keep their heads down, sign whatever's put in front of them because the alternative is more fire. He'll expect a few of you at the district office, cowed, maybe signing quitclaims. What he won't expect is a counter-claim, filed first, with original survey maps and sworn affidavits and a list of witnesses longer than his legal team's patience." He looked around the room, at the burned-out, the scattered, the leaderless. "He also won't expect me. As far as Meridian knows, I'm dead on a plateau. For the next three days, I intend to stay that way."

Corrigan grunted. It might have been approval. With Corrigan it was impossible to say.

Father Anselm rolled the maps closed with the tenderness of a man handling scripture. "The district office closes for the Sabbath," he said. "Which gives you two days of travel and one day of margin, if the river cooperates. The river rarely cooperates." He looked at Edmund, and behind the glacier-pale eyes was something that might have been pity, or blessing, or simply the professional assessment of a man who had watched many souls arrive at their crossroads. "You'll want the Lindqvists' boat. And you'll want sleep. Both are yours."

* * *

Night came down on the mission soft and cold. Edmund lay on a cot in the dormitory wing, wound freshly dressed by Mrs. Lindqvist, who had scolded him with clinical fury about the state of his bandaging and then, at the door, paused and said quietly, "My brother-in-law saw your wreck. He said whoever walked away from that was either blessed or too mean to die. I'm glad it was the second one." Then she was gone, and he was alone with the sound of the valley breathing.

Sleep wouldn't come. Through the wall he could hear a family whispering, the murmur of children being settled, the small domestic sounds of people rebuilding a life out of ash and stubbornness. Somewhere across the yard a dog barked twice and gave up.

He thought about the plateau. About the wreck, and the radio he'd salvaged and couldn't raise, and the long white silence afterward. Seven years he had chosen silence—chosen it like a coat, worn it until it fit, told himself it was safety when it was really just a smaller and smaller room. And now here he lay in a room full of strangers who knew his name, his crime, his shame, and had asked him to lead them anyway. Not because he'd earned it. Because he was *there*, and willing, and knew the enemy's mind.

Maybe that was all leadership ever was. Being present, and honest, and too mean to die.

In the morning they would take the boat. In three days they would stand in front of a clerk in a district office and put truth on paper faster than money could. And somewhere behind them, coming down out of the mountains with armed men and bought lawyers, Alden Crane was about to discover that the witness he'd purchased seven years ago had finally stopped for sale.

Edmund closed his eyes. For the first time in seven years, the dark felt like rest.

He was nearly asleep when the knock came—soft, urgent, three raps—and Sable's voice through the door, low and tight:

"Dunmore. Get up. There's a plane circling the valley."
