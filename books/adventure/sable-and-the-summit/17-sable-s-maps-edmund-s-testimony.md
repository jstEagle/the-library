## 17. Sable's Maps, Edmund's Testimony

The mission station smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and by the time the lamps were lit, every soul in the valley had crowded into the long room that served as chapel, schoolhouse, and meeting hall. Sable counted them the way she counted everything—by habit, by trade. Forty-one adults, nine children, one blind dog asleep against the stove. She had drawn maps of valleys she'd never walked with less attention than she gave that room.

Edmund sat where they'd put him, near the stove, his bandaged leg stretched out on a crate, his face gray under the lamplight. He looked like a man who had been asked to hold up a roof and was still deciding whether he could. The fever had broken two days ago on the mountain, but it had taken something out of him—or maybe it had put something back in; she hadn't decided which.

Father Anselm, the station's priest, a spare man with ink-stained fingers, cleared the room the way priests do—not by commanding silence but by making everyone ashamed of noise. Then he nodded at Sable, and she understood that Edmund had already spoken for her, or she for him, or perhaps the burned homesteads downriver had done all the speaking anyone needed.

She unrolled the satchel's contents on the long table at the front of the room.

The true maps came out first: the survey sheets, hand-inked, bearing her own drafting marks and the survey seal of Meridian Consolidated. She weighted their corners with stones from the windowsill and let the people look.

"This is the boundary line Meridian filed with the district office last month." Her finger traced the eastern edge of the valley, a clean confident stroke that swung wide of the river flats and took in nothing but scrubland. "This is the line their own surveyors measured." The second line ran close along the water, through the homesteads, through the orchards, through the graves.

The murmur started low, like wind finding a gap under a door.

"The difference between those two lines," Sable said, "is gold. A seam running under your valley floor, roughly east-northeast, dipping below the river terraces. Meridian has known about it since July. The survey I was hired to draw was never meant to be honest. My instructions were to move the boundary east until the seam fell outside company claim—and then the valley would be open land, unclaimed, unwanted. Except it wouldn't be empty. There were people living on it."

A woman near the front—Marta Reyes, whose husband had stayed behind to try to save the mill—said quietly, "The fire."

"The fire was not an accident," Sable said. "I can't prove who struck the match. But I can prove the orders existed to make the valley untenable before the dry season ended. I heard them given. And there is a man who can prove more than that."

She looked at Edmund. He had gone very still, the way men go still when they are about to do something that costs them.

* * *

He told it plainly, without ornament, and Sable thought afterward that this was what made it land—the absence of performance. He stood, though Father Anselm offered him a chair, and he stood because standing was part of the price and he intended to pay all of it.

"My name is Edmund Dunmore," he said. "Seven years ago I flew charter out of Fort Rennick. I carried a load over the Kestrel Range in weather I should have refused, and my engine quit on the far side, and a man named Tomas Weller died when we went down. I was charged with manslaughter—flying a plane I knew wasn't sound, into weather I knew was bad, with a passenger who trusted me."

Sable watched the faces. Some of them had known already—word travels fast in a valley, faster than fire—but knowing and hearing were different things. Hearing a man say *I killed someone* in a room full of people who had just lost everything to other men's decisions—that did something to the air.

"I was convicted on the testimony of a surveyor named Alden Crane," Edmund went on. "He swore he'd warned me about the engine trouble in writing, three days before the flight. His letter was entered into evidence. It convicted me. What I learned eleven days ago, in a camp above the range, is that Crane wrote that letter after the crash—at Meridian's request, for money, because I had been asking questions about cargo I wasn't allowed to inspect, and a dead pilot asking questions posthumously is inconvenient, but a convicted one is discredited forever."

He paused. His hands were flat on the table beside Sable's maps, and she saw they were trembling, and she saw him let them tremble rather than hide it.

"Crane admitted it to me. He offered to recant if I helped him take something from this woman"—a nod at Sable—"and return it to him. I said yes. I sat across a fire from the man who ruined my life, and I nearly shook his hand, because he was offering me seven years back. That's what my word is worth, if you're weighing it. I chose his bargain once. I'm telling you now so you know exactly what kind of witness you're getting."

The silence held for a long moment. Then Marta Reyes stood up.

"You could have kept flying mail," she said. "Nobody made you come down off that mountain."

"No," Edmund agreed. "Nobody did."

"Then sit down, Mr. Dunmore, and tell us how we stop them."

* * *

They worked past midnight, and Sable found herself doing the thing she was made for: making the invisible visible. She pinned sheet after sheet to the mission's plank walls—topography, the seam's projected course, the false filing versus the truth, the chain of custody of evidence she'd carried out of a burning camp in a leather satchel against her ribs for nineteen days.

"Here's the problem," she said, tapping the district map. "Meridian's claim filing goes before the registrar at Corben Falls in—" she calculated, counting days against the calendar nailed by the stove "—nine days. Once it registers, the valley becomes company ground on paper, and paper is the only weapon they need. Eviction notices. Lease cancellations. The Mounties get involved, and the Mounties follow documents, not smoke."

"So we file first," said old Henrik Dahl, whose farm had been the first to burn, whose face still bore the scorch mark from dragging his wife out.

"We file first," Sable confirmed. "A counter-claim, with the true boundaries, the seam survey, and sworn affidavits. If our filing reaches the registrar before theirs is processed, Meridian has to answer it in a hearing—with us in the room. Not in a courtroom in the capital years from now. Here. Now. On ground where forty witnesses have seen the smoke."

"And they'll fight it," someone said from the back.

"They'll fight it with lawyers and lies and probably worse," Sable said. "But a counter-claim forces disclosure. They'll have to produce their original field notes. And their original field notes say what mine say, because I drew half of them." She let that settle. "Crane signed the falsified version. He cannot produce honest notes without hanging himself."

It was Henrik Dahl who asked the question Sable had been waiting for, the one that mattered most: "Three days' travel to Corben Falls, you said. Who carries it?"

The room looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at his leg.

"I fly," he said.

Half the room spoke at once. Father Anselm raised a hand.

"There's the strip at Two Rivers," the priest said slowly. "Burned, but the ground's sound. And there's the company plane—they've been shuttling men south all week. Someone saw it fueling at the lake yesterday."

"Then I borrow their fuel and their strip," Edmund said, "and I fly a cartographer and a satchel of maps to Corben Falls before their own lawyers finish their coffee."

"You can barely walk," Sable said.

"I can sit," he said. "Flying is mostly sitting."

She wanted to argue. She was trained to identify risk, to chart around it, and every instinct she had said that putting a convalescing pilot with a septic wound into a borrowed plane over the range was the worst idea in a season of bad ideas. But she also knew what she'd seen on the mountain: a man who had decided that his name was worth carrying again, and who treated obstacles thereafter as terrain, not verdicts.

"We'll need affidavits tonight," she said instead. "Everyone who saw the fires start, everyone who was threatened, everyone who was offered money to leave. Signatures, dates, places. And I need two hours with Father Anselm's ink and his steadiest pen."

* * *

They wrote until the candles guttered. Sable drafted the counter-claim itself in her drafting hand, each letter a small act of architecture, while Edmund moved along the benches with the affidavit pages, dictating nothing, only listening—letting each homesteader tell their own fire, their own threat, their own loss, and then witnessing the signature.

At some hour past two, Marta Reyes brought them coffee, and Sable found herself sitting shoulder to shoulder with Edmund at the table, surrounded by forty-one testimonies drying in the lamplight.

"You didn't have to tell them about Crane's offer," she said quietly. "About saying yes."

"Yes, I did."

"It cost you."

"It's cheaper than what keeping it would have cost." He turned his cup slowly. "You know what I figured out on the mountain? Shame isn't a weight you carry. It's a debt you keep refinancing. Every year you don't pay it, the interest gets worse. Tonight I paid some of it."

Sable looked at the maps pinned around them, at the seam of gold running like a vein through the lives of everyone sleeping in the mission's outbuildings, and felt the old loneliness of her profession lift slightly—the cartographer's curse of seeing truths no one else will look at.

"When we file," she said, "Crane will come for us. Not lawyers first. Him. He doesn't leave loose ends—he burned a valley to avoid them."

"Then we'd better be quick," Edmund said.

Outside, far to the south, beyond the black line of the trees, a light moved through the dark—low, steady, wrong for a star. A plane, running without lights regulation, heading toward the lake strip.

Sable stood, and Edmund followed her gaze, and neither of them said what both of them were thinking: that Meridian's pilots flew at night too, and that whatever was aboard that plane was coming north.

"Get your satchel," Edmund said. "We leave before dawn."
