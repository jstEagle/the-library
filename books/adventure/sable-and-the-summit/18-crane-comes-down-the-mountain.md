## 18. Crane Comes Down the Mountain

The dogs started it.

They'd been quiet all morning—six of them, half-wild things that belonged to no one and everyone at the mission station, sprawled in the dust by the woodpile like they owned it. Then one of them lifted its head, and a second, and a third, and before Edmund had even registered the sound beneath their growling he heard it himself: boots on gravel. Many boots. The measured cadence of men who wanted to be heard coming.

He was on his feet before he finished thinking about it, and Sable was already at the window of the mission's back room, her body pressed flat against the wall beside the frame, one eye to the gap where the shutter hung crooked. She didn't look at him when she spoke.

"Eight," she said. "Maybe nine. Rifles on four of them."

"Crane?"

"He'll be in the middle. He always walks like the road was built for him." A pause. "There's someone else. Big man, gray coat. I don't know him."

Edmund crossed the room in three strides and looked for himself. Below, past the mission's low fence and the vegetable rows gone ragged with frost-kill, Crane's party came up the track from the river in loose column, packs high on their shoulders, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no. Alden Crane walked at the front as Sable had predicted—not leading so much as occupying the point of the formation, the way a signet ring occupies a finger. He'd shaved. That was the detail that caught in Edmund's chest like a fishhook: seven days into the bush and the man had found the means to shave, because Alden Crane understood, down in whatever passed for his soul, that appearances were ammunition.

Behind him came the armed men, and behind them two more carrying between them a crate Edmund recognized by its stenciling. Meridian Consolidated. He'd loaded its twin onto his own plane eleven days and one lifetime ago.

"They're not sneaking," Sable said. "That's the message. He wants the whole valley to watch him walk in here."

"Then let's give him an audience worth watching."

She turned from the window then, and for a moment they just looked at each other, and Edmund saw her doing the arithmetic he was doing—the same numbers, the same grim totals. Eleven people in the station. Four rifles among them, two of them borrowed from homesteaders who barely knew how to hold them. Sable's satchel with the true maps, hidden since dawn under the floorboards of the chapel, wrapped in oilcloth. And somewhere south, three days' hard travel, a district office where a clerk named Osgood would open his ledger at nine o'clock and write down whatever claim arrived first.

"Whatever happens out there," Sable said quietly, "the maps stay buried. He can have my blood before he has those pages."

"He won't get either." Edmund took his jacket off the peg and worked his arms into it, and felt the letter shift in the inner pocket—the old woman Marta's letter, carried now through fire and mountain, its paper gone soft as cloth from handling. "Stay near the door. If it goes wrong, you go out the back and down to the willows, and you don't stop walking until the pass is behind you."

"It won't go wrong."

"You don't know that."

"No," she agreed, and something moved at the corner of her mouth that wasn't quite a smile. "But I've watched you talk to men who frighten you, Edmund Dunmore. You get quieter. It's the loudest ones who fold first."

* * *

They met him in the yard.

It was Sable's idea—or rather it was what she refused to do that shaped it. She would not hide, and she would not be summoned; if Crane wanted the satchel, he could stand in the open air of the mission yard and ask for it in front of the same people whose valley he intended to burn. So Edmund stood at the head of the mission steps with the morning sun full in his eyes and the homesteaders gathered behind him in a rough crescent—Ruth Calloway with her rifle held wrong but held, old Tomas with his hands jammed in his pockets to keep them still, the Halvorsens' boy perched on the fence rail with the fearless stupidity of fifteen—and he watched Alden Crane come across the yard like a man arriving at his own coronation.

Seven years had silvered him at the temples and thickened him through the chest, but the face was the face from the courtroom, from the deposition room, from every night since that Edmund had tried and failed to forget. The face that had looked at the judge with sorrowful, reasonable eyes and said, *I checked the load manifest myself, Your Honor. Mr. Dunmore overrode me.*

"Edmund." Crane stopped six paces out, hands open at his sides, and shook his head with something that performed regret beautifully. "God, but it's good to see you breathing. When we heard a plane went down on the plateau—"

"You didn't hear anything," Edmund said. "You knew. You flew over the wreck twice looking for it."

Something flickered behind the regret. There and gone, like a fish turning under ice.

"I hoped it wasn't you," Crane said. "I'll say that much."

"And I'll say this much: you're standing on Ruth Calloway's fence line, and her house is ash, so whatever you've come to say, say it to her too."

He felt the yard shift behind him. It was a small thing, saying it—a sentence, nothing more—but he heard the homesteaders take it in, heard the quality of their attention change. They had known him three days as the pilot who'd crashed out of the mountains, the stranger with the fever-scarred side and the careful manners. Now they knew him for something else, and Crane heard them knowing it, and Edmund watched the man recalculate in real time, the way a pilot reads a storm cell building on the horizon.

Crane spread his hands wider. "What I've come to say is simple. Miss Voss is carrying property that belongs to Meridian Consolidated. Survey documents, paid for with company money, produced on company time. She took them when she deserted her post." His voice carried easily—it had been built for courtrooms and boardrooms and it made the mission yard feel suddenly small. "Nobody here needs to be part of what happens next. Hand them over, Miss Voss, and every man walks back down the way he came. There's even a place for you. The company doesn't waste talent, whatever its feelings."

"The company burns valleys," Sable said. Her voice was flat and clear and did not carry as far as Crane's, but it didn't need to; the yard was silent enough that a whisper would have reached the fence. "I stood in the forward camp and listened to you plan it. A campfire 'gone wrong.' Dry season. You had the date picked before the survey was even finished."

"Lies make better stories," Crane said mildly. "That's why courts prefer evidence. And the evidence says those fires started in a homesteader's unattended cook-fire—which, incidentally, is what the insurance assessor will find when he comes through."

"There hasn't been an assessor," said Ruth Calloway.

"There will be."

"There hasn't been a *claim filed* either," she went on, and her voice cracked and steadied itself, and Edmund realized with a jolt that she was not afraid anymore—that somewhere between the burning and this morning she had burned through her own fear and come out the other side into something harder. "You keep talking about filings and assessors like they've already happened. Like it's all just paperwork catching up to what you already decided. Well, mister, I've got forty years of improvements on that land and a deed signed by a land commissioner dead these twenty years, and I'm telling you it isn't decided until somebody writes it down who isn't on your payroll."

For a moment nobody moved. The dogs had gone quiet again, watching from the woodpile with their ears up, and the wind moved through the frost-killed beans in the garden rows with a dry rattle like distant small-arms fire.

Then the big man in the gray coat stepped forward, and Edmund understood his role at last: he was there to end conversations. He unslung his rifle with theatrical slowness and laid it across his forearm, not aimed, merely present, the way a gavel is present.

"Miss Voss," Crane said, and now the warmth was gone from his voice entirely, and what remained was worse than anger—it was patience. "I want to tell you a story about your friend here. About Edmund Dunmore. Because these good people deserve to know exactly whose word they're taking."

"Don't," Edmund said.

"Seven years ago, a plane came apart over the Kettle Range with a passenger aboard. A company geologist. Father of two." Crane turned, addressing the crescent of homesteaders now, his voice dropping into the register of a man sharing grief rather than assigning blame. "The inquiry found the aircraft overloaded beyond every safe margin. Found the pilot had been warned. Found—" and here he paused, and let his eyes find Edmund's, and there it was, the old sorcery, the sorrowful reasonable gaze that had convicted him once already— "found that the last man to check that aircraft swore under oath to its condition, and was overridden."

"You swore to it," Edmund said. "You swore to it and then you sold the oath."

"I swore to what I believed."

"You swore to what Meridian's lawyers wrote for you! You sat across a table from me in the deposition room and lied with your eyes wet, Alden, and I have spent seven years—" His voice broke, and he hated it, hated the crack of it in front of all of them, and then he understood, with a clarity that arrived like weather, that the crack was not weakness. It was the truest thing in the yard. "Seven years flying mail and medicine into places no one else would go, because I couldn't stand to be anywhere a man might look at me and see what you told the world I was."

Silence. Even Crane's hired men had gone still. And in that silence Edmund heard footsteps—two sets of them, at the edge of the party, shifting weight, and he marked it without marking it, the way you mark a change in engine note without yet knowing what it means.

Crane recovered fast. He always had. "Grief makes poor history," he said gently. "The record stands. Which brings us back to the only question that matters today." He extended one hand toward Sable, palm up, an almost courtly gesture. "The documents, Miss Voss. Last time I ask nicely."

Sable didn't move. But Edmund, watching her, saw her glance flick—half a heartbeat, no more—toward the chapel, and he saw Crane catch it too, and he saw the big man in gray begin to turn toward the building, and everything after that happened very quickly and very slowly at once, the way emergencies do.

"That's far enough," said a voice from the fence.

It was one of Crane's own men. The tall one—the one called Brandt, whom Edmund remembered from the forward camp, who had stood guard the night Sable fled and somehow failed to see her go. He had his rifle slung and his hands empty and he was looking not at Crane but past him, down the slope, toward the river flats, where the smoke of the burned homestead strip still rose faintly even now, five days later, a gray smudge against the blue like a bruise that wouldn't fade.

"Brandt." Crane's voice sharpened. "Stand down."

"I flew over it yesterday," Brandt said. He wasn't shouting. That was the terrible thing—he spoke at the volume of a man discussing fence posts. "In the Beaver, with Pell. We flew the whole strip. The Anders place. The Okonkwos'. The schoolhouse." He swallowed. "My sister taught in that schoolhouse, Alden. She got the children out through the creek bottom with her coat over their faces. There's a boy, eight years old, who hasn't spoken since."

"Weeks ago, that was," said the second man—Pell, the younger one, and his voice was not steady at all. "Before any of this. Before we ever came upriver. We were told it was an accident. We were told—Christ, Alden, you *told* us."

"And I'm telling you now—"

"You told us the surveyor's notes proved the valley was company ground." Pell's rifle was in his hands and he seemed to have forgotten he was holding it, or perhaps he was holding it precisely because he'd forgotten, the way a man grips a rail in a rolling sea. "Voss is standing right there. Ask her. Ask her what the notes actually say. Go on. Ask her in front of everyone."

And the yard turned, all of it, the whole gravity of the morning turning like a compass needle finding north, and settled on Sable Voss.

She came down the steps slowly. She unbuckled the satchel—Edmund's stomach dropped, thinking of the floorboards, the oilcloth, and then he saw that this satchel was lighter, older, its canvas faded—and drew out a single rolled sheet, and spread it on the fence rail, and weighted its corners with four stones from the garden bed.

"Forward camp, March survey," she said. "My drafting. Crane's annotations." Her finger traced a boundary line, red ink over black. "This is the seam. It runs under the Calloway improvement and the Halvorsens' and the creek commons—under homes, not wilderness. This line here, in red, is what the company intends to file. Drawn east of the watershed. Legal fiction. I drafted it myself, because I was ordered to, and then I stopped, and that's why the camp split, and that's why there are men with rifles in this yard." She straightened. "Ask me anything. Any grid reference, any benchmark. I'll answer it in front of God and Ruth Calloway and whoever you like."

Crane looked at the map for a long moment. And Edmund, watching him across six paces of trampled grass, saw something he had waited seven years to see and never once believed he would: Alden Crane running out of words.

It lasted three seconds. Maybe four. Then the big man in gray moved—swinging the rifle up off his forearm—and the morning tore open.

He never fired it. He never got the chance. Brandt's hand closed on the barrel and shoved it skyward, and Pell stepped in front of Sable with his own weapon leveled—at the gray man, at the moment, at the whole architecture of the thing—and for one endless breath the yard balanced on its point like a coin spun on edge.

"Enough." Ruth Calloway's voice, thin and iron. She had her rifle up now, held wrong, aimed true enough. "Not one shot fired on this ground. Not one. My husband is buried fifty feet from where you're standing, and no company man dies beside him."

The gray man looked at Brandt's hand on his barrel. Looked at Pell's muzzle. Looked, last of all, at Alden Crane—who did not meet his eyes, who was already stepping backward, already rebuilding the distance, already becoming a man who had never quite ordered what had plainly been ordered.

Brandt let go of the barrel. The gray man lowered the rifle slowly, and something in his face had gone hollow, the look of a man discovering the floor is painted on air.

"I'm done," Brandt said. He unslung his rifle the rest of the way and laid it in the dirt at Ruth Calloway's feet, and Pell laid his down beside it, and the two of them walked around the outside of the crowd like men leaving a church, and did not stop walking until they reached the fence, where they turned and faced the yard and stayed.

Crane stood alone in the center of it. Eight men behind him, minus two, minus the will of most of the rest. He looked older all at once, the way buildings look older when the scaffolding comes down.

"This changes nothing," he said. "The filing goes ahead Thursday. Lawyers don't need rifles, Miss Voss. They need paper, and mine is already on a truck south of the range."

"Then you'd best hope your truck drives faster than a raft," Edmund said.

Crane's eyes came to him, and for a moment—just a moment—the performance dropped away entirely, and what looked out of that face was seven years old and cold as well water. "You were nothing when I found you," Crane said quietly. "I made you a story. I can unmake you one."

"You already did," Edmund said. "Thursday, I get to tell a different one."

Crane held his gaze a moment longer. Then he turned, and whistled up his men, and they went down the track toward the river in their loose column, slower now, the coronation become a retreat, and the dogs barked them all the way to the bend.

* * *

They dug the maps up within the hour.

By lamplight in the mission's back room, while the homesteaders crowded the doorway and the two defectors stood awkwardly outside holding cups of tea no one had asked them to pour, Sable unrolled the true surveys across the table and weighted them with tin cups, and Edmund watched her hands move—steady, precise, the hands of a woman drawing the world as it actually was—and felt something settle in his chest that he had no name for yet.

"Three days to the district office," Sable said, tapping the route with one finger. "Two if we take the river to the falls and beg a truck past it. Osgood's clerk opens the claims book Thursday morning."

"Crane knows we'll come."

"Of course he does. He'll have lawyers and affidavits and probably a sheriff who owes Meridian money." She looked up at him across the lamplight, and her eyes were tired and absolutely lit. "So we'd better be faster, and we'd better be louder, and we'd better bring witnesses he can't buy. How's the side?"

"It'll hold."

"It had better. You're the witness, Edmund. I'm just the paper." She began rolling the maps with quick, economical turns. "Paper needs a voice."

Outside, down by the river, someone was already dragging the raft out of the willows.
