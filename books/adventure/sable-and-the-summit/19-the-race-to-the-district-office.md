## 19. The Race to the District Office

Sable counted the hours the way she'd once counted contour lines—each one a promise of elevation or ruin, depending on where you stood when it ran out.

Three days. That was what the mission station's elder, a broad-shouldered woman named Ilsa Reiner with ash still worked into the creases of her knuckles, had given them. Three days before Meridian's claim filing registered at the district office in Corben Falls, and after that, every map in Sable's satchel became a curiosity instead of a weapon. Paper could be argued with only so long as nobody had already signed the other kind of paper—the legal kind, the kind that turned valleys into assets and families into squatters on their own ground.

They left at first light, four of them: Sable, Edmund, Ilsa's son Tomas, who knew the river like he knew his own pulse, and a homesteader girl of maybe seventeen called Petra who volunteered before anyone asked, her jaw set in a way Sable recognized from mirrors she'd stopped looking into years ago.

The raft was Tomas's idea, and it was a good one, which is why Sable distrusted it immediately. Good ideas on rivers were just drownings that hadn't happened yet.

* * *

The Ash River ran high with snowmelt off the upper passes, brown-green and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways like matchsticks. They launched below the burned homestead strip, where the smell still hung in the air—that sweet-sick char of barn timber and hay and things Sable made herself not name. Edmund said nothing as they pushed off past the blackened posts of the Kessler gate. He'd been saying nothing about the valley since they'd landed at the mission station, but Sable had learned to read his silences the way she read weather, and this one had weight in it. Grief, compacted. The old woman's letter rode in his jacket pocket, and Sable suspected he touched it more often than he admitted.

"Keep her bow into the current," Tomas shouted over the water. "She'll want to swing—we don't let her."

The raft was lashed cottonwood logs with a plank deck and a steering sweep fore and aft, and it handled like a drunk ox, which was exactly what they needed. Speed without finesse. The river did the work; they just aimed it.

Sable sat amidships with the satchel strapped across her chest, oilcloth-wrapped inside a flour sack, and watched the banks unspool. She'd mapped this river once, two years ago, for a company that paid her in promises. Every bend had a name in her head now, and most of those names belonged to people who'd lost something last week. That was the thing about cartography nobody told you when you started: maps outlived their makers' intentions. You drew lines for money and the lines kept drawing themselves long after you'd spent the pay.

Edmund sat at the forward sweep, pale under his tan, working the sweep in long steady strokes. His wound had been dressed at the mission station—Ilsa had cleaned it with boiled water and a sternness that bordered on surgical—but Sable had seen the look of the thing when the bandage came off. Angry red, edges hot, a thread of streaking climbing toward his knee. Infection, walking its slow ladder. He knew it too. She'd caught him looking at his own leg the way a man looks at a clock.

"How far to the takeout?" he asked, not turning around.

"Thirty miles of river if we run it straight," Tomas answered for her. "Then the logging spur crosses at Miller's Bend, and Petra's uncle has a truck."

"And Crane?"

Sable shaded her eyes and looked back upriver. Nothing. Smoke haze, the far ridge, the white noise of water. But she'd stood in Crane's camps. She knew how the man moved—methodical, patient, the way ice moved. He wouldn't chase them down the river. He'd go around. He had horses, he had men, and he had the road.

"He'll be ahead of us," she said. "Not behind. He knows the river won't do him any favors. He'll drive for Corben Falls and file his version first, and have lawyers standing on the courthouse steps when we arrive looking like drowned cats."

"Then we'd better not look like drowned cats," Edmund said, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard in seven years of knowing him by reputation alone—a dry, cracked almost-humor, surfacing like a fish in shallow water. "I own one clean shirt. It's in my plane. On a mountain."

"Noted," Sable said. "We'll lead with the affidavits."

* * *

They ran the river through the morning, and it was the closest thing to flying Sable had ever done with her feet on something solid.

The water took them through canyons where the walls rose sheer and dripping, ferns hanging out of cracks like green fireworks, and the light came down in blades so sharp it seemed you could cut yourself on it. Twice they shot rapids that soaked everyone to the skin and left Petra whooping with a wildness that made Sable think the girl had needed this—needed to feel fast, alive, unburned. Tomas steered with his whole body, leaning into the sweeps, reading the water the way Sable read terrain, and between them they threaded the worst of it.

It was somewhere past noon, in a long flat stretch between canyons, that Edmund went quiet in the wrong way.

Sable noticed it the way she noticed errors on a chart—not the mistake itself at first, but the wrongness of everything around it. His strokes had shortened. His shoulders had dropped. When she crossed to the forward sweep, she found him gray-lipped and sweating despite the cold spray, his hands locked on the sweep handle like it was the only true thing in the world.

"Let me see the leg."

"It's fine."

"It's never been fine. Let me see it."

He resisted for exactly as long as it took him to nearly faint, which wasn't long. She cut the bandage away right there on the moving raft, water slapping beneath them, and the wound looked worse than it had at dawn. Hot, swollen, the skin around it tight and shining, and that thin red ladder now reaching his thigh. Fever heat poured off him like he was a stove.

"You're septic," she said flatly. "Or starting. Either way, you're done rowing."

"I'm done nothing." He tried to stand. The river tilted under him—or he tilted under the river—and Tomas swore and threw an arm across his chest, pinning him gently to the deck.

"Boss," Tomas said, using the word loosely, "you fall in this water, you're gone before we finish saying your name. Sit. Be cargo. Cargo we can deliver."

Something flickered across Edmund's face at that—some old private joke with himself, some memory of drums and crates he hadn't been allowed to inspect—and then he laughed, a short broken sound, and let them lay him against the cargo bundle.

Sable dug through the medical kit Ilsa had pressed on them at the station. Not much. Willow bark, iodine, gauze, a stub of candle. She re-dressed the wound as well as a bouncing raft allowed, packed it with the last of the poultice Ilsa had made from comfrey and something bitter Sable didn't recognize, and forced Edmund to swallow willow bark tea brewed strong enough to strip paint.

"Fever's going to climb," she told him, holding his eyes. "You're going to get confused, maybe. You're going to want to sleep. You don't sleep until we're in that truck. You hear me? You stay with me."

"I stayed with you down a mountain," he murmured. His eyes were too bright. "I can stay with you down a river."

"That's the spirit. Terrible spirit. Keep it."

* * *

Miller's Bend appeared in late afternoon, exactly where her memory said it would, which gave Sable a small fierce satisfaction she badly needed. The logging spur crossed the river on a trestle bridge gone gray with age, and beyond it, tucked in alder scrub, sat a shack and a yard and a truck—an ancient flatbed with a canvas top and tires balder than an egg.

Petra's uncle was a leathery man named Oskar who took one look at the raft, one longer look at Edmund, and asked no questions, which Sable understood was the local currency these days. Questions got people burned out. Silence kept roofs over heads.

"We heard about the fire," was all he said, loading Edmund into the truck bed on a mattress of feed sacks. "Half the valley's sleeping in church basements downriver. You going to fix that?"

"We're going to try," Sable said.

"Hm." Oskar cranked the engine, which caught on the third try with a sound like a rifle shot. "Try hard."

They ran the logging road south as the light went long and copper-colored, the truck bucking over corduroy and washout, canvas snapping, Edmund's fever climbing in the back with every mile. Sable rode beside him with her hand on his forehead like she was taking a bearing, tracking degrees the way she tracked stars. Thirty-eight and change by her guess. Thirty-nine by the time the sun dropped behind the ridgeline and the road plunged into blue dusk.

He talked, in the fever. Not much, and not always to her. Fragments. A runway in the rain. A voice on a witness stand saying *the pilot knew the load was over limit, I saw the manifest myself.* A woman's name—Ruth, maybe, or Ruthie—said twice, softly, like setting something down. Sable listened and said nothing and kept her hand on his forehead, because some bearings you didn't correct. You just held them.

Twice they passed turnoffs where fresh tire ruts cut the mud, wide-tread, heavy-vehicle ruts, heading the same direction they were. Company trucks. Oskar spat out the window the second time.

"They'll have gone ahead," Sable said quietly. "Crane will already be wiring Meridian's office. If his claim registers tonight—"

"Then we drive all night," Petra said from the passenger seat, and there was no argument in her voice at all, just arithmetic.

* * *

Corben Falls announced itself an hour before they reached it: first the sawmill smoke lying flat under the stars, then the glow of the town itself strung along the river like embers shaken from a fire, then the sound—horns, a train shunting somewhere, the mechanical heartbeat of a place that believed in tomorrow.

They rolled in at half past midnight. The district office was a stone building on the square with a lamp burning in its window, and Sable felt her chest loosen half a degree at the sight of it—until Oskar braked hard outside the hotel across the street, where three trucks stood in a line, mud to their windows, wide-treaded and familiar.

Light burned behind the hotel's ground-floor curtains. Voices. And crossing the lobby window, unmistakable even at this distance and this angle, a tall figure with silver at his temples, moving with the unhurried confidence of a man who believes the paperwork is already won.

Alden Crane. Here. Hours ahead of them, rested, shaved, and no doubt already known by name to every clerk in town.

Sable sat very still in the cab, doing the math she hated. The district office opened at eight. Crane would be there at five past, with lawyers, with his version of the survey, with the smooth authority of the company voice. Whatever they carried in the satchel would arrive looking like the desperate forgery of a disgraced cartographer and a fugitive pilot—one of whom, she realized with a cold drop in her stomach, was currently burning at thirty-nine point five degrees and mumbling about a runway in the rain.

"Get him to Mrs. Halloran's boarding house," she said. "Back door. Nobody sees him yet. If Crane learns Edmund Dunmore is in town tonight, he'll have the sheriff waiting for us at the office door instead of a clerk."

"And you?" Petra asked.

Sable lifted the satchel strap over her head and checked the oilcloth wrapping, the affidavits sealed inside, the true maps with their honest lines. Somewhere across the street, behind the lit curtain, Alden Crane was toasting an early victory.

"I'm going to find out what time the clerk gets in," she said, "and whether he takes bribes—or better, whether he hates Meridian Consolidated. Every town this size has one reason to hate them. We just have to find it before eight o'clock."

She stepped down from the truck into the cold, lamplit street, and behind her, faint through the canvas, she heard Edmund's fever-voice say clearly, *I'm coming with you,* and she chose, for the moment, to pretend she hadn't heard.
