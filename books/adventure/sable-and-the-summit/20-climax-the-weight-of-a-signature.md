## 20. Climax: The Weight of a Signature

The district office at Fort Alder smelled of coal smoke, ink, and the particular staleness of buildings where important things happened slowly. Edmund stood in the corridor outside the land registry with his back against the plaster wall, one hand pressed flat over the pocket of his jacket where the old woman's letter had ridden for a thousand miles and now rode no longer—it had been read aloud the night before at the mission station, its names spoken into lamplight until every homesteader in the room knew which of their neighbors were scattered and which were ash.

Three days of travel had cost them nearly everything. The raft through the gorge had nearly swamped twice; the borrowed truck had died twelve miles out and they'd walked the last of it, Edmund's wound weeping through its bandage, Sable carrying the satchel against her chest like an infant. They had arrived at dawn to find the registry already open, a clerk named Prosser—a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the wary eyes of someone who'd been bribed before—unlocking the front doors.

And then they had waited. Because filing a counter-claim required the claimant or his authorized agent to appear, and because the homesteaders' delegation—six of them, led by a broad-shouldered man named Halvorsen whose brother's farm was one of the burned ones—had insisted on arriving together, in daylight, as if legitimacy were something you could wear.

Edmund watched the clock above the clerk's window crawl toward nine. His leg throbbed in time with it. Sable sat beside him on the bench, the satchel between her feet, her hands folded and still, though he could see the tendons standing in her wrists. She hadn't slept more than two hours since the pass. Neither had he. Fever came and went behind his eyes like weather.

"He'll come," she said quietly. "Crane won't let this happen unopposed."

"I know."

"Are you ready for what you have to say?"

Edmund turned the question over. What he had to say was not complicated. It was only enormous. Seven years of silence, and today he would stand in a government office and unsay all of it at once—the crash, the death, the testimony, the bargain Crane had offered him on the mountain with redemption in one hand and a leash in the other. He would name his own crime first. That was the part that mattered, and the part that terrified him. If he stood up there and only accused, he was just another man with grievances. If he confessed first, then accused, he was a witness.

"No," he said. "But I'll do it anyway."

Sable looked at him then, really looked, the way she looked at contour lines when she was deciding whether a slope could be climbed. "That's the whole trick of it," she said. "Nobody's ever ready. You just make sure your feet are pointed the right direction when the ground gives way."

The door at the end of the corridor opened at five past nine, and Alden Crane came through it like weather.

He was clean. That was the first obscenity Edmund registered—after three weeks in the interior, after the burned valley and the river flats and the standoff at the mission station, Crane arrived immaculate. Grey suit pressed sharp enough to draw blood. Two lawyers flanking him, one carrying a leather document case thick as a Bible. Behind them, four men from Meridian's regional office, collars white, faces arranged into expressions of patient grievance, men who had never in their lives been cold.

Crane saw Edmund immediately. Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise, not quite fear. Recalculation. Edmund watched him do the arithmetic: the pilot was here, the cartographer was here, the maps were here, and there were six homesteaders standing in the corridor looking at him the way men look at a wolf that has taken calves.

"Mr. Dunmore." Crane's voice carried the warmth of a stove in a room he owned. "You look terrible. You should be in a hospital."

"You should see the other plane," Edmund said.

A flicker of the old humor passed between them—seven years old, buried under everything—and died there. Crane's lawyers stepped forward and introduced themselves to Prosser through the window, voices smooth as poured concrete. The tall one, a Mr. Calloway, produced documents: Meridian Consolidated's mineral claim, surveyed, staked, witnessed, filed in proper order, covering eleven square miles of the upper valley. The paper was beautiful. The paper was a lie drawn in a fine hand.

"The survey was conducted under license," Calloway said, "by qualified personnel, and the boundaries conform to the provincial grid. We understand certain... irregular parties intend to lodge objections. We trust the registrar will weigh the credibility of the sources accordingly."

*Irregular parties.* Edmund almost laughed. Six families who'd farmed that valley for twenty years, reduced to a phrase you'd use for water in a fuel line.

Prosser accepted the documents without expression and set them on his desk. "Counter-claims will be heard in order of filing," he said. "Is there a party present to file?"

Halvorsen stepped forward. "We are."

"Then you'll need to state the basis of your claim and submit supporting evidence."

This was the moment. Edmund felt it arrive the way he'd once felt engines arrive at the edge of stall—that hollow drop, the whole aircraft hanging on what you did next. Sable lifted the satchel from between her feet and held it, but she didn't move toward the window. She was watching Edmund. So was everyone. Even Crane, who had begun speaking softly to Calloway, stopped and turned.

Edmund pushed off the wall. His leg buckled slightly on the first step and he corrected it, and crossed the corridor, and stood at the clerk's window with the whole room behind him.

"There's evidence," he said. "Maps. The true boundary surveys, done by the expedition's own cartographer, showing that Meridian's filed boundaries were falsified to include homesteaded land and to bury a gold seam the company intends to take by clearing the valley." He nodded at Sable; she came forward and opened the satchel and began laying the sheets on the counter—her drafting hand steady even now, each map squared to the window's edge. "There are affidavits from the homesteaders. There is testimony about the fire."

Calloway smiled the smile of a man reaching for a lever. "Testimony from whom? A pilot with a criminal record? A disgraced employee walking around loose with stolen company property?" He gestured at the maps. "These documents were seized from our expedition. This woman deserted her post. Everything she carries is compromised."

"That's one version," Edmund said.

"It's the *legal* version, Mr.—"

"Dunmore. Edmund Dunmore." He said his own name deliberately, fully, the way you'd set down a heavy load to free both hands. "And since we're establishing credibility, let's establish mine properly. Seven years ago I was convicted of manslaughter following a cargo crash near Kestrel Narrows. One man died. The court found my loading exceeded limits and my judgment failed in bad weather. Both true. I flew overweight because the schedule demanded it and I told myself the weather would hold. A man named Tomas Reyes died because of that arithmetic. I've spent seven years flying medicine into places nobody else would go, and I have never once believed that made up for it, and I am not here to say it does."

The corridor had gone very quiet. Prosser's pen had stopped moving.

"But the conviction rested on testimony," Edmund went on, "and that testimony was bought." He turned and faced Crane across ten feet of polished floor. "You told the inquiry the weight manifest I signed showed figures I'd altered. You said you'd checked the manifests yourself the morning of the flight. You never checked anything. You testified to what Meridian's counsel wrote for you, because the company needed the crash blamed on a pilot instead of on the freight contract that forced the overload, and they paid you for it. A surveyor's fee, they called it. I learned what it was three weeks ago, on a mountain, from your own mouth."

"This is fantasy," Crane said. His voice was still level, but Edmund had flown with him long enough, once, to hear the flutter beneath it. "An emotionally disturbed man repeating the lies of a thief."

"Am I lying, Alden?" Edmund kept his voice low. It carried better that way; he'd learned that over engine noise. "You sat with me by a fire and told me the truth because you thought I'd take your bargain. You offered me a recantation—your sworn statement undoing my conviction—in exchange for helping you contain Miss Voss and her maps. You said, and I'd swear to the words: *the truth is inventory, and inventory gets priced.* Those were your terms. Redemption for sale. I nearly took it." He let that stand. "I want the record to show I nearly took it. Because whatever else happens here, I'm done testifying to things I haven't weighed myself."

He turned back to Prosser. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to receive the evidence. Her maps can be checked against the original grid benchmarks—she'll show your surveyor where every stake was moved and by how much. The homesteaders' occupancy predates every Meridian filing by nineteen years. And the fire—" his voice caught, and he let it, "—the fire that cleared the north farms was set the same night Meridian's advance crew moved into the valley. There are men at the mission station right now who were Crane's own employees until they saw the burns. They're prepared to swear to orders given."

Calloway started to speak and Crane stopped him with a raised finger—one degree of motion, but everyone saw it. Then Crane stepped forward himself, into the space Edmund had opened, and did the thing Edmund had known he would do, the thing that had made him dangerous for twenty years: he changed the subject by changing the frame.

"Gentlemen," Crane said to Prosser and the room at large, "I don't intend to litigate this in a hallway. My client will pursue every available remedy. But I will say this much." He looked at Edmund, and his face did something terrible and honest—it softened. "Edmund is right that we spoke on the mountain. He's right that I offered him assistance with his legal situation. A man learns compassion, working in country like ours. What he's done is twist an act of friendship into conspiracy, because a dying man needs someone to blame besides himself." He spread his hands. "I testified truthfully seven years ago. I grieve that Mr. Dunmore has chosen to carry guilt so long that it's curdled into this."

It was masterful. Edmund felt the room tilt toward Crane, felt pity reorganize itself around the wrong man, and understood in his bones how the world had worked this way forever—how the well-dressed and the composed got to define reality while the bleeding held the maps.

It was Sable who broke it. Not with a shout. She simply pulled the top sheet from her stack, turned it to face the room, and said:

"Benchmark 14. Provincial brass cap, set in 1908, on the ridge above the Halvorsen place. Every survey in this district keys off it. Meridian's filed plat puts their eastern boundary forty-one chains east of Benchmark 14. The cap is still there. Anyone can walk to it in half a day. Their own stake is on the far side of it, inside twenty-year-old fenced pasture." She tapped the sheet. "I didn't seize these documents. I drew them—from the originals, which I also have, in Crane's own expedition seal, including the instruction memo directing the boundary revision. Signed. Dated. In his hand."

She looked at Crane the way she looked at impossible terrain: without hatred, without mercy, simply measuring.

"You taught us that a map is a claim," she said. "You were right. Yours just got redrawn."

Silence. Then Halvorsen said, quietly, "Registrar. We'd like to file."

Prosser looked at the maps, at the affidavits Halvorsen laid beside them, at Edmund's written statement—which he had written out by lantern light two nights before, his fevered hand steadied by Sable's dictation—and lastly at Calloway, who was whispering urgently into Crane's ear.

"If the counter-claim is contested," Prosser said, "it goes to hearing. But filing is filing." He stamped the homesteaders' papers, and the sound of the stamp was small and enormous, like a single rivet driven home in a wing. "Received. Dated. Nine-forty-one this morning."

Calloway recovered fast—he announced an immediate application to quash, cited three statutes, promised injunctions by wire by nightfall. But the machinery had caught. Papers existed now that had not existed at nine-forty. The lie was on file next to the truth, and truth had gotten there with witnesses.

Crane gathered his coat. At the door he paused, and for a moment—only Edmund saw it—the polish dropped away and what looked out was tired, and old, and briefly human.

"You could have had your life back," Crane said. "All you had to do was nothing. Nothing was always so easy for you."

"That's exactly why I couldn't," Edmund said.

Crane held his gaze a moment longer, then was gone, lawyers trailing, the door swinging shut behind them with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

The corridor exhaled. Halvorsen gripped Edmund's shoulder hard enough to hurt. Sable began squaring her maps back into the satchel, and her hands, steady through everything, shook now—only now, only after.

Edmund leaned against the counter and found that his legs wanted very much to stop being relied upon. The fever was back, climbing, patient. Somewhere beyond the walls a truck was starting, and the town went on with its morning, unaware that anything had happened here at all.

"Prosser," Edmund said. The clerk looked up. "There's one more piece of business. Not a filing. A surrender." He straightened, and the motion cost him, and he paid it. "Seven years ago I pled guilty to a crime I committed, surrounded by testimony that was bought. I'd like to know what I owe now that the record's being set right. Where do I go to give myself up?"

Prosser set down his pen slowly. Through the window behind him, the morning light lay across the counter like a clean page.

"Sheriff's office is across the street," he said. "But son—you might want to sit down first. Because there's a wire on my desk that came in an hour ago, and I think it's about you."
