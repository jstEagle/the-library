## 21. What Justice Costs

Sable Voss had spent her whole life learning to read terrain—the way a ridge could lie about its height, the way a river bent to flatter itself on paper—and she had never once been able to read a man's face as well as she could read a contour line. But standing in the district office that morning, watching Edmund Dunmore cross the street toward the sheriff's door with his wound seeping through a borrowed coat and his shoulders square as a surveyor's transit, she didn't need to read anything. She knew what she was looking at. It was the same thing she'd seen in him on the plateau, the first morning, when he'd tried to stand on a broken body because standing was what you did.

He was going to give himself up. He had already given himself up. The walking across the street was just paperwork.

She stayed by the counter, her satchel—her true maps, the only honest lines in three hundred miles of country—clutched against her ribs like a child. Prosser the clerk was still holding out the telegram, his hand trembling slightly, and when Edmund turned back at the door and took it, Sable watched him read whatever the wire said. Watched his face do something complicated. Watched him fold it once, twice, and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he'd carried the old woman's letter through fire and river and pass.

Then he looked at her across the room, and there was no fear in it. There was something almost like apology.

"Wait for me," he said. "Or don't. Either way—" He stopped, and smiled, and it was the first whole smile she'd ever seen on him, unguarded, both sides of his mouth agreeing. "Either way, thank you for finding my wreck."

And then the sheriff's door closed behind him, and Sable Voss stood alone in a government building with a counter-claim filed under her name and nothing left to fight.

It should have felt like victory. It felt like the hour after a storm passes, when the air is too clean and your ears are still ringing and you can't remember what you were so afraid of, only that you were afraid of it for a very long time.

* * *

The wire, she learned later, had come from Meridian Consolidated's head office—or rather, from a lawyer acting for men inside Meridian who had decided, with the speed of rats understanding a ship, that Alden Crane had acted alone. The wire disavowed Crane entirely. It named the falsified boundary surveys, the staked claims, the fire at the homestead strip—all of it, laid out in dry legal language like an autopsy report—as the unauthorized acts of a rogue field director. There would be cooperation with any investigation. There would be compensation for the burned families. There would be, the wire promised, full disclosure.

Full disclosure. Sable had almost laughed when Prosser read it aloud. Seven years of silence bought and paid for, and now disclosure arrived by telegram, an hour after it stopped being dangerous. That was how institutions repented: they waited until repentance cost nothing, and then they repented loudly, and then they sent the bill for the stationery to someone else.

But she kept her mouth shut, because the wire did one thing she couldn't have done herself. It made Crane's arrest clean. When two constables came down from the north camp that afternoon—sent ahead by the defectors, Hollis and Brandt, who had ridden through the night—they took Alden Crane off the mission station porch without a shot fired, without a hand raised, while he stood there in his good coat explaining jurisdiction to them in the voice of a man who had never once been interrupted. They found the oil cans in his supply cache. They found the signed order for the burn, dated three days before the fire, in a hand that matched every fraudulent boundary line Sable had spent six weeks copying into evidence. They found the money ledger, because one of the defectors had thought to bring it down the mountain wrapped in oilcloth, and money ledgers were the one kind of map even lawyers could read.

Crane went into the cell across the corridor from Edmund. Sable saw them pass each other in the booking room, through the window from the street, and she made herself watch, because she believed you owed it to a thing to witness it.

Crane saw Edmund and stopped. For a moment the two of them stood three feet apart, separated by a deputy's arm, and Crane said something—she couldn't hear it, but she could see the shape of it, the easy tilt of the head, the old charm deployed one last time like a man throwing a rope to a drowning friend. Edmund listened all the way through. Then he shook his head, once, slowly, the way you'd decline a drink you'd sworn off, and walked past him into the cell block without looking back.

Crane's face did something she'd never seen a powerful man's face do. It aged ten years in four seconds. Then the door shut on him too, and the street was ordinary again, and somewhere a dog was barking at a wagon.

* * *

That evening she sat in the mission station kitchen with Marta Elling, the old woman whose letter Edmund had carried seven hundred miles in his jacket pocket, through a crash and a fever and a mountain pass, and had never delivered because there was no one left at the strip to deliver it to. The letter had gone soft at the corners from sweat and rain. Marta held it now in both hands, reading it over though she'd written it herself, and crying the quiet way old women cry, without noise, tears just arriving and being dealt with.

"He carried it the whole way," Sable said. "I checked his pockets myself, the night of the fever. I thought it might be money or papers worth stealing." She turned her tin cup on the table. "It was the only thing on him worth anything."

Marta reached over and put her rough hand flat on Sable's wrist. "You'll tell him that," she said. Not a question. "Men need to hear what they're worth while they can still be surprised by it."

"I'll tell him."

"Good." Marta wiped her eyes with her apron, briskly, business concluded. "Because I've known that boy seven years. Flew our medicine in every month, never took a coin off us, never stayed for supper, never let anyone ask him a question longer than a sentence. We all knew there was something behind it. You don't hide that well unless you're hiding something heavy." She folded the letter along its worn creases. "Heavy things get lighter when you set them down in front of witnesses. That's the whole trick of it. Nobody tells you."

Outside, the valley smoke had thinned to a gray smudge on the southern horizon, and the first cold stars were coming out over the range they'd crossed. Somewhere down the road, homesteaders were rebuilding a fence line by lantern light, hammer strokes carrying in the still air like a slow heartbeat. The valley was going to be fought for now—with filings and affidavits instead of fire, which was the whole point of everything they'd done—but tonight it smelled like woodsmoke and cut lumber and, faintly, from the mission garden, the last of the season's sage.

Sable sat with Marta until the lamp burned low, and then she walked down to the jail, because she had promised herself she wouldn't sleep before she saw him, and because—though she'd have denied it under oath—she wanted to see his face one more time before the system got hold of him and started deciding things.

* * *

The deputy on duty was one of the defectors' cousins, a broad young man named Teller who had shaken Edmund's hand that afternoon with visible embarrassment, as if arresting him were a discourtesy he hoped would be overlooked. He let Sable sit in the corridor outside the cells without argument. "Ten minutes," he said. "Visiting hours ended at six, but visiting hours were written by people who never crashed an airplane on purpose."

Through the bars, Edmund sat on the bunk with his back against the stone wall, his wounded leg stretched out, reading—of all things—a Bible some previous occupant had left, though he held it more like a man passing time than a man seeking anything. When he saw her he set it down face-open on the mattress, keeping his place out of habit.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you. I've been working on it." He patted the space beside him on the bunk and she shook her head and dragged the corridor chair up close instead, close enough that the lamplight from behind her fell through the bars and caught his face. The fever was gone from his eyes. That was the thing she noticed first, the thing she'd been checking for since the pass: the brightness was ordinary now, human-bright, not the glassy shine of blood poisoning. His leg was wrapped in clean linen. Someone had fed him. The machinery of consequence had, so far, treated him gently.

"The wire," she said. "What did it say? Really. Prosser summarized it for the room and I want the version you actually read."

Edmund leaned his head back against the stone. "It said Meridian is very sorry. It said Crane acted alone. It said they look forward to assisting justice." He said the words flatly, without heat, and somehow that was worse than anger. "Seven years ago that wire would have worked. Seven years ago I'd have read it and thought: there it is, the machine, it grinds whoever's nearest and it never stops and the best you can do is not be nearest." He looked at his hands. "Now I've seen the machine from the inside twice, and I know it runs on exactly one fuel. People deciding not to say things. That's all. Every gear in it turns on somebody keeping quiet."

"And you're done keeping quiet."

"I'm done keeping quiet." He said it simply, the way you'd state your altitude or your heading. A fact of navigation.

Sable pulled the folded affidavit copy from her jacket—her own handwriting, the true boundaries, the seam measurements, the order for the burn—and held it up between the bars. "This goes to the land board Monday. Your statement goes with it. Hollis and Brandt corroborate. Marta and eleven families will testify about the strip. Prosser says the counter-claim filing is the strongest he's seen in nineteen years at that desk, and Prosser strikes me as a man who doesn't say strong things lightly."

"Prosser," Edmund said, and something warmed in his voice. "He gave me coffee this afternoon. Through the bars. Real coffee." He paused. "Sable. About the trial. About what happens now. They could reopen the manslaughter charge, or they could—"

"They could do whatever they do," she said. "And you'll stand in front of it either way. That's the part I keep trying to make myself understand, because I've spent six weeks watching you and I still find it improbable." She gripped the bars—not dramatically, just to have something solid. "You flew into that valley knowing what the cargo probably was. You walked out of a wreck with a hole in your side. You climbed a pass in September with a stranger who pointed a knife at you the first time you spoke. And none of that was the hard part. The hard part was today, crossing a street that's forty feet wide, and you did it like it was a mail run."

"It wasn't like a mail run." He laughed, low. "My hands were shaking so badly I nearly missed the handle."

"I know. I watched."

"You watched?"

"I watch everything. It's the job." She let go of the bars. "Edmund. Whatever they decide in there—seven years, seven months, whatever it costs—you should know what it bought. Because I've done the arithmetic and I don't think anyone's shown you the sum." She counted it off on her fingers, cartographer's precision. "Eleven families keep their land. A valley keeps its name. A company learns that telegrams can't outrun testimony. Two of Crane's own men learned what their orders were really worth. And one pilot learns that his name fits him after all." She stood, pushing the chair back. "That's what justice costs. That's the receipt. I'd call it cheap."

For a long moment he didn't answer. In the lamplight his eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise, and she understood that this too was part of setting the heavy thing down—that you had to let people see it leave your hands.

"Marta's letter," he said finally. "Tell her I'm sorry I couldn't deliver it."

"She knows. She says you delivered the important part." Sable picked up her satchel. At the end of the corridor, Deputy Teller coughed politely, the universal sound of time expiring. She turned back once at the corner.

"For what it's worth," she said, "when we come down off the summit in spring—I'm mapping the honest lines now, the real ones, the ones that'll hold up. I'll need a pilot who knows every bad field and dead frequency in the interior." She shrugged, deliberately casual, and failed completely. "Seems a waste to let all that experience rot in a cell past its sentence."

Edmund smiled the whole smile again. "Spring," he said. "I'll be counting the days. I'm told I'm good at measuring miles. Might as well measure those."

She walked out into the cold, and behind her, through the small barred window high in the jail wall, she heard him settle back onto the bunk, unhurried, unafraid—a man in custody lighter than any free man she'd ever met—and above the town the stars wheeled on over the pass, and the first flakes of the season's real snow began to fall, soft and early and, for once, falling on nothing that needed burning.
