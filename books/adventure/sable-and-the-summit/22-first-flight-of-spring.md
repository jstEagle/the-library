## 22. First Flight of Spring

The ice went out of the river on a Tuesday, and the mail came back to the interior on a Wednesday.

Edmund knew the date because he'd written it himself, in ink, on the manifest clipped to his kneeboard: *April 14 — Dunmore — Interior Run.* His own name, printed there in the clerk's careful hand at the district office, no longer felt like a stranger's. That was still new enough that he noticed it every time. For seven years he had signed manifests with a version of his name worn smooth as river stone—*E. Dunmore*, sometimes just *Dunmore*, the letters doing their work without meaning anything. Now the whole name sat on the page and looked up at him, and he let it.

The Gull had been rebuilt over the winter in Halvorsen's hangar, mostly by Halvorsen and partly by Edmund, who had discovered that he liked the work more than he'd expected—liked knowing every wire and weld, liked the honesty of an engine that told you exactly what it needed. The old wreck from the plateau was beyond saving; they'd brought it down off the mountain in pieces slung beneath a borrowed floatplane, and the salvage man had paid him almost nothing for it. Edmund hadn't minded. He'd stood in the snow watching them cut the fuselage free of its moorings and thought: *that plane carried me out here, and this one carries me back.* Fair trade.

He ran up the engine at dawn, and it caught on the third blade like it had been waiting for him, and the sound rolled across the frozen mud of the field and came back changed, the way sound does in spring when the world has started listening again.

Sable was already at the fence.

She'd come out from the mission station two days early, ostensibly to help load, actually because she didn't trust anyone else to stow her map cases upright. She wore the same canvas coat she'd crossed the range in, patched now at both elbows where the scree had chewed through, and her satchel rode her shoulder the way it always did—close, angled, one hand resting on the flap without seeming to notice it was there. The satchel had been evidence for four months. It had been photographed, catalogued, argued over by men in good suits, and entered into the record of the district land commission as Exhibit A. When they'd finally released it, Sable had taken it back from the clerk's counter with both hands, the way you take something back that was never really gone but wasn't yours either until that moment.

"Fuel's topped," she said, by way of good morning. "And I checked your tie-downs, because you tie them like a man who expects the plane to leave without him."

"It's a habit," Edmund said. "You spend seven years expecting things to leave without you, it gets into your hands."

She looked at him then—the look she'd learned on the mountain, the one that weighed a sentence before deciding whether to carry it—and something in her face eased. "Not anymore."

"Not anymore."

They loaded together in the grey light: mail sacks, flour, nails, a crate of window glass for the Kestrel place, medicine for the mission, and forty pounds of seed potatoes wrapped in burlap that Mrs. Aldous at the last homestead strip had asked for by name, variety, and soil condition, as though ordering a dress. Sable's map cases went in last, strapped upright against the baggage bulkhead, eleven tubes of rolled linen and paper containing the true lines of the valley—the honest lines, the ones that would now be printed in every survey office south of the range, because in February the commission had ruled in favor of the homesteaders' counter-claim, and Meridian Consolidated's filing had been thrown out entire, and Alden Crane had been led out of the district office in handcuffs while his lawyers were still gathering their papers.

Edmund had watched that too. From the back of the room, standing, the whole time, because sitting felt like hiding and he was done with hiding.

* * *

The trial for the old charge had come first, in January, and it had been smaller and stranger than he'd feared. The manslaughter case from seven years ago had never gone away—it had only ever been postponed by distance and his own cowardice—and when he surrendered himself after the district office, the crown prosecutor, a tired woman named Okafor who had clearly expected a fugitive and gotten instead a man with a documented confession from Crane and a stack of affidavits from homesteaders, had looked at the file for a long time and then looked at him.

"You flew supply runs into the interior for seven years," she said. "Medicine runs. In a plane held together with wire."

"The mail doesn't care what I did," Edmund had said. "It just needs to get there."

The retrial had turned on Crane's recorded admission—that the testimony about the control lines, the weather call, the weight of the cargo, all of it had been shaped, coached, purchased—and on three of Crane's own crew, the defectors from the mission station standoff, who swore to what they'd seen and heard. The jury took a day and a half. The verdict was manslaughter, reduced, with mitigation: negligence under duress, coerced testimony, seven years of self-imposed exile weighed against one night of bad decisions made worse by a company that had spent seven years making sure he stayed gone. Eighteen months, suspended. Two hundred hours of community service, most of which he'd already served before sentencing, flying medicine for the mission station through the worst of the winter because there was no one else and the roads were snowed under.

Judge Wexler, an old man with a voice like gravel settling, had said one thing Edmund kept: *"Mr. Dunmore, the court cannot give you back the years you took from yourself. It can only observe that you appear to have spent them better than most men spend their free ones."*

He'd walked out of the courthouse into January sunlight and Sable had been leaning against the rail, arms crossed, breath steaming, and she hadn't said congratulations or anything else. She'd just fallen into step beside him, and they'd walked to the airfield together, and that had been enough.

* * *

Now the wheels left the mud at 6:40 in the morning, and the interior opened below him like a book he'd read so many times he'd stopped seeing it, except that today he saw it.

That was the difference, he realized, banking north over the river bend. Not the plane, not the papers, not even the name on the manifest. It was that he was looking. For seven years he had flown these miles with his eyes fixed on the horizon ahead—the next field, the next stop, the next mile—because looking down meant seeing the life he wasn't living. Now he tipped the Gull's wing and looked straight down at the water braiding silver through the flats, and it was beautiful, and he let it be beautiful, and nothing terrible happened.

"There," Sable said beside him. She had the door window open despite the cold, a drafting pencil behind her ear, a fresh sheet of mylar on her knee board. She'd talked Halvorsen into installing a small chart table where the second passenger seat used to be, and she worked braced against the gentle shudder of the airframe like a surgeon at sea. "The river's moved again. See the cutoff? Two years ago that was the main channel." Her pencil moved, quick and sure. "Every map of this valley is a lie within five years. That's why they have to keep being drawn."

"Honest lines," Edmund said.

"Somebody's got to draw them." She glanced over, and the wind coming through the window pulled at the loose strand of hair she'd never bothered to pin. "Somebody's got to fly them first."

The first homestead strip came up at 7:15—a cleared meadow along the terrace above the river, mended fences, a new barn roof, and smoke rising straight in the still air from chimneys that had been burned stumps in October. Edmund brought the Gull in low over the trees, waggled the wings twice—the old signal, the one that meant *mail and medicine, all well*—and set down on grass still stiff with frost.

They were waiting for him. That was the other thing that was different.

A dozen people at the edge of the strip, and not the wary, hollow-eyed cluster he remembered from his last fuel stop before everything burned—the families speaking nervously of surveyors, the old woman pressing a letter into his hand. These people were waving. A boy of maybe nine broke from the group and ran the length of the strip alongside the rolling plane, arms pumping, until Edmund cut the engine and the silence rushed in and the boy skidded to a stop by the wheel, breathless, triumphant.

"Mr. Dunmore! Mr. Dunmore, is there glass? Mama says the glass came!"

"Window glass, whole crate of it," Edmund said, swinging down. "Careful with it, though. It's the one thing on this plane more fragile than my reputation."

The boy laughed like that was the funniest thing ever said in the history of the valley, and ran back shouting the news, and Edmund stood in the frost-smelling grass and let the moment go through him like warmth through cold hands.

Mrs. Aldous came up the strip at her own pace, small and straight-backed, seventy if she was a day, wearing a coat that had clearly survived the fire because it had been at a neighbor's house that night. She stopped in front of him and looked him over with the frank appraisal of a woman who had buried two husbands and one valley and had no patience left for nonsense.

"You got thinner," she said. "Mountain food?"

"Mostly willow bark and stubbornness."

"Mm." She handed him a parcel wrapped in cloth, warm from her stove. "Bannock. You'll eat it before the next stop or I'll hear about it." Then, quieter, with her eyes on the scarred hillsides downstream where the burn line still showed black against the new green: "We got the ruling in February. My granddaughter read it out loud twice." Her jaw worked. "I want you to know we know what it cost. What you gave up to stand up there and say it."

"I didn't give anything up," Edmund said, and found that it was true. "I put some things down. There's a difference."

She studied him a long moment, then nodded once, the way you nod at a fence post you've decided will hold. "Sable Voss." She raised her voice slightly. "You'll take tea before you go. Both of you. Don't argue."

"We wouldn't dream of it," Sable said, arriving with a map tube under each arm and frost melting on her collar.

They took tea in the half-built house, sitting on nail kegs around a plank table, and Mrs. Aldous told them which families had come back and which hadn't yet and which weren't coming at all, and Sable marked each homestead on her working sheet with a small confident cross, and Edmund sat with bannock in his hand and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that this was home. Not a place. This. The tea, the kegs, the woman naming her neighbors like a litany, the maps being corrected in real time by a person who cared about the truth of a riverbend more than anyone alive. He had spent seven years measuring his life in miles, and miles, it turned out, were just the space between moments like this one. He'd had it backwards the whole time.

* * *

They flew the rest of the run through the long gold morning.

At the Kestrel place, the window glass came out of the crate unbroken—all thirty panes—and Kestrel himself, a taciturn man missing two fingers to a trapline accident, shook Edmund's hand with the remaining eight and said only, "Heard you on the stand. Radio at the mission rebroadcast it." He looked at the ground. "You said what happened that night. All of it. Even the parts that made you look bad." Another pause. "My brother died in a boating accident I might've prevented. Never told anybody the might've part." He picked up a pane of glass and turned away. "Fly safe, Dunmore."

At the mission, Father Anselm loaded them with letters going south—forty-one of them, the most since before the fire, and he counted them out loud with visible satisfaction, because letters meant people believed in a future worth writing to. "The bishop asks after you," he said. "I tell him you're a penance the Lord assigned to this valley, and the valley got the better end of the bargain."

"He sounds like a wise man, your bishop."

"He sounds like a man who has never eaten Mrs. Aldous's bannock. Take some for the flight."

By early afternoon they were running the high leg north of the pass—the pass itself, white and silent and indifferent, sliding past to the east. Edmund kept his distance from the ridge out of respect and habit, but he didn't avoid looking at it. The plateau where the Gull had come down was hidden behind a shoulder of rock, and he found he didn't need to see it. He'd carried that mountain on his back all winter; it weighed less now, not because it was smaller but because his shoulders were.

Sable worked steadily through the afternoon, correcting, annotating, occasionally asking him to hold a heading or fly a slow circle over a confluence so she could check a detail against memory. Once she laughed—short, surprised—at something the river had done, a brand-new oxbow cutting through an old meadow, and the sound filled the cockpit and stayed there for miles.

"Question," Edmund said, somewhere over the divide.

"Mm."

"When the commission released the satchel. You could have framed those maps. Hung them on a wall. They're the maps that won the valley back. People would have come to look at them."

"They will," Sable said. "There's a wall at the mission. Father Anselm offered."

"But you're here instead. Drawing new ones."

She was quiet for a moment. Below them the river turned south toward country neither of them had mapped yet—upriver past the old expedition camps, past the headwaters, into the blank space on every chart she owned, the space she'd been looking at sideways all winter the way Edmund used to look at the horizon.

"Maps are only true for the day you draw them," she said finally. "The ones in the satchel did their work. They're history now." Her pencil hovered over the blank margin at the top of her sheet, where the known world ended. "I'd rather draw tomorrow's."

Edmund looked at the fuel gauge, at the sun's angle, at the empty country ahead, and did the arithmetic he'd been doing quietly for weeks. The Gull had range for the river run and back with an hour to spare. The season was opening. The mail route ended at the last homestead, and beyond that was everything unmapped.

"Sable," he said. "How do you feel about extending the run?"

She didn't answer right away. She looked out the window at the blank space, and then at him, and the look was the one from the mountain—the weighing look—but lighter now, the way a rope is lighter when two people are carrying it.

"How many days of fuel you carrying?"

"Three, if we camp light."

"And how do you feel about camping light?"

"I've been practicing my whole life," Edmund said. "Turns out I'm good at it."

She uncapped her pencil and drew a single line off the edge of the current sheet, out into the margin, out past everything surveyed, everything claimed, everything burned or bought or lied about—a thin sure line pointing at the unknown, the first stroke of a map that didn't exist yet.

"Then log it," she said. "Day one."

Edmund Dunmore, pilot, witness, homesteader of the air, wrote it in the manifest in his own full name, in ink, and pushed the nose north into the wide clean light, and the miles unspooled ahead of them—not years, not sentences, not debts. Just miles. And for the first time in seven years, every one of them was going somewhere.
