## 1. Fuel, Debt, and Weather

The monsoon was three days out, according to the radio, and two days early, according to the sky. I trusted the sky. The radio had been lying to me about money for years, so it seemed only fair it should start lying about weather.

I was loading the last of the medicine crates into the Otter when I saw Mr. Ferro's car come up the strip road, all chrome and bad intentions, kicking up dust that hung in the still air like a confession nobody wanted to hear.

There is a particular way a man walks when he's carrying a folder full of your failures. Ferro had it down to an art. Shoulders square, chin tucked, the unhurried gait of someone who knows you can't outrun paper. I'd watched him cross that airstrip four times this season, and each time I'd developed a new appreciation for the physics of avoidance.

"Morning, Miss Eriksen," he called, because he liked using my name. Men like Ferro always like using your name. It's how they remind you they know exactly whose throat the paperwork is wrapped around.

"Mr. Ferro." I slid the last crate home and latched it. "You're standing on my runway."

"Lease access road," he said pleasantly. "Technically."

Everything with Ferro was technically. Technically the loan was in default. Technically the arrears were eleven weeks old. Technically my father had signed the original note before I could walk, back when the Eriksen name still opened doors instead of closing them. Technically, technically, technically — the man could build you a whole prison out of technicalities and then charge you rent for the cell.

"I'm mid-flight-prep," I said. "Medical run to Kavu Ridge. Vaccines and insulin, and a woman eight months pregnant who'd rather not deliver on a dirt floor."

"Then I'll be brief." He didn't open the folder. He just rested his hand on it, the way a preacher rests a hand on the Bible. "The bank's called in the note. End of the month."

"The end of the month is also the end of the dry season."

"Yes," he said, with something that in a better man might have been sympathy. "That's not a coincidence."

He left the way he'd come, unhurried, chrome flashing, and I stood on my own airstrip — technically the bank's airstrip — and watched the dust settle over everything I owed.

* * *

Here's what people don't understand about debt: it isn't the money. Money is just arithmetic, and arithmetic has never once kept me up at night. What keeps you up at night is the shape of it. The way it sits on your chest like a passenger who won't deplane. The way it makes every decision feel like it's already been made for you, and you're just walking through the motions of a life someone else financed.

My father understood debt. He died inside it, more or less literally — drowned in the Sable River with a boatload of somebody else's promises, owing everybody and owned by no one, which was the only kind of free he ever managed to be.

I keep his compass. Brass case, glass gone cloudy at the edges, the needle still true after twenty years in a drawer. He carried it on every flight, every run, every fool errand the interior asked of him, and when they shipped his effects back to me in a box that weighed almost nothing, the compass was the only thing I kept. Everything else went to creditors. That's the arithmetic of grief: you learn very quickly which objects are load-bearing.

The compass lives in my breast pocket now, over the left side, where a person keeps things they don't want examined too closely. Before every takeoff I touch it. Not superstition, I tell myself. Calibration. A pilot checks her instruments. Mine just happens to be dead.

* * *

Kavu Ridge was ninety minutes north through air gone soft and heavy, the kind of atmosphere that makes an aircraft feel like it's flying through soup. Below me the country did its annual transformation — green going gold going brown, rivers shrinking back into their channels, exposing all the bones and wrecks and secrets the wet season hides. Every year the land undresses a little more, and every year I look away from one particular stretch of river the way you'd look away from a scar on someone you loved.

The Sable River route. Ninety miles of braided water, sandbars that move overnight, and fog that rises off the current at dawn like the river exhaling. My father flew it forty times a year for a decade. Then he flew it once more, and the river kept him.

So I have a rule. One rule, carved deeper than any regulation in my logbook: never fly the Sable route. Not for cargo, not for cash, not for mercy. There are other ways around the interior. Longer ways. Ways that cost fuel and hours and occasionally clients, but ways that let me sleep, or whatever it is I do at night that resembles sleep the way a photograph resembles a person.

"You're quiet today," said the ridge clinic's nurse, Marta, as we unloaded crates into her arms. She was sixty if she was a day, built like the terrain she worked, and she missed nothing that happened within fifty miles of her, including, apparently, the inside of my head.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a woman counting," she said. "Different animal."

Marta had known my father. Everyone out here past thirty had known my father; he was the kind of man the interior collects and doesn't give back. She looked at me the way she looked at a wound she couldn't stitch — professionally, sadly, from a respectful distance.

"Debt collectors?" she asked.

"One. Polite one. They rotate them out before they get impolite. Union rules, probably."

"And the monsoon?"

"Coming early. This is my last run of the season." I cinched a strap. "After this, we hunker, we patch, we wait for the water to drop."

"You could wait it out here," she offered, the same offer every year. "Kavu's got room."

"Kavu's got mosquitoes with ambitions and a roof that sings. I've got a hangar and a crew waiting on me." I managed something adjacent to a smile. "Besides, if I stay, you'll put me to work."

"I would," she agreed cheerfully. "Deliveries are done, though?"

"Deliveries are done." I said it lightly. Deliveries were not entirely done. There was one more item riding home in the cargo bay, a small sealed box of antibiotics for a fishing camp on the lower Sable — the lower Sable, mind, a good forty miles downstream of the wreck site, practically a different river, practically a different moral universe. I'd told myself it was fine. I'd told myself the rule was about the upper reaches, the fog banks, the place where the channel braids into confusion. Rules need interpretation. That's what makes them survivable.

Marta squeezed my arm once, hard, the way she did instead of saying goodbye, and I walked back to the plane through heat that pressed down like a hand.

* * *

The trouble started on the return leg, because trouble has excellent timing and no respect for fuel gauges.

Weather first. The monsoon cell that was supposed to be three days out had thrown an advance scout across the valley — a wall of cloud sitting on the western hills, purple-black, flickering internally like something thinking bad thoughts. I gave it a wide berth, swung east, burned ten extra minutes of fuel doing it, and told myself the headwind numbers would work out.

They did not work out. Headwinds rarely do; it's their whole personality.

By the time I had the strip in sight, my fuel state had crossed from *planning margin* into *interesting conversation with myself*. And there, parked beside the fuel bowser like it owned the tarmac — which, technically, it did — sat Ferro's chrome car. And next to it, a second vehicle I didn't know, a grey truck with government plates and a man leaning against it eating an orange with tremendous patience.

I brought the Otter down with the smoothness of a woman who refuses to be rattled in front of creditors, taxied to the hangar, and shut down in the sudden ringing silence that follows a long flight. Then I climbed out and stood in the shadow of my own wing, because a wing is as good a place as any to make a stand.

"Miss Eriksen." Ferro again, folder in hand, orange-eating stranger beside him. "Good flight?"

"Uneventful. Which for me is a religious experience." I nodded at the stranger. "And this is?"

"Mr. Oduya. Regional lease administration." The stranger finished his orange segment, wiped his fingers on a handkerchief produced from nowhere, and smiled with the serene cruelty of a man delivering news he'd rehearsed in a mirror. "Miss Eriksen. As of this morning, the leasehold on this strip and hangar has been transferred. Your creditors have elected to exercise their security interest."

I felt it in my sternum before my brain caught up — that cold drop, like turbulence with no warning light. "Transferred to whom?"

"That's being finalized," Oduya said. "But the new holder has indicated interest in continued aviation operations. They're quite keen on the river trade, actually."

The river trade. Something in those words snagged on me, small and sharp, like a fishhook through a sleeve. Creditors don't get keen. Creditors liquidate. Somebody wanted this strip — my strip, my father's strip — for a reason, and the reason had water in it.

"End of the month," Ferro said gently, "you'll need to vacate. Or come to an arrangement with the new holder. Their representative will be arriving shortly to discuss terms."

"Shortly meaning?"

"Soon," said Oduya, folding his handkerchief with terrible neatness. "These days, everyone is coming upriver soon."

They drove off together, two men and a folder, leaving me standing under the wing with the fuel gauge reading empty-ish and the sky going the color of a bruise behind the western hills.

* * *

Bea found me in the hangar an hour later. She didn't ask questions; Bea communicates through tools. She handed me a wrench, pointed at the Otter's number-three cylinder, and we worked in the companionable silence of two women who both knew the fuel bill was going to hurt.

Tomas appeared at dusk, smelling of the river and cheap tobacco, and leaned in the hangar door with the expression of a man carrying news he'd already argued with himself about losing.

"Ferry dock's buzzing," he said. "Big boat came in this afternoon. Chartered out of the capital. Fancy."

"We don't do charters."

"This one's asking for us by name." He picked at a splinter on the doorframe. "Not by your name, Uli. By the company name. Eriksen Air Freight. Your dad's letterhead. Nobody's used that letterhead in years."

The wrench stopped moving in my hand. Outside, the first fat drops of the early monsoon hit the hangar roof, one, two, then a drumroll, the sky making good on its threat ahead of schedule. In my breast pocket, against my heart, my father's compass sat cold and silent, pointing its faithful needle at nothing I wanted to go.

"Tell them we're closed for the season," I said.

"Already did." Tomas grimaced. "Problem is, Uli — the man says he knew your father. Says he's got business on the Sable River, and he's not leaving without talking to you."

The rain came down harder, hammering the roof, filling the hangar with the sound of a river falling out of the sky. I touched the compass through my shirt, an old habit, a calibration against a dead man's heading, and heard myself say the words I would spend the rest of this story regretting:

"Show him in."
