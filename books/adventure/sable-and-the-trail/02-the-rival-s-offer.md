## 2. The Rival's Offer

Dario Vance arrived the way bad weather arrives: you smelled it before you saw it, and by then it was already too late to batten anything down.

I was elbow-deep in the Cessna's port engine cowling when Bea's wrench went quiet on the other side of the hangar. That silence should have been my first warning. Bea does not go quiet for anything less than a snake or a creditor, and we'd already cleared out the snakes this season.

"Uly," she said, in a tone I can only describe as apologetic on behalf of the universe.

I looked up. Dario Vance stood at the mouth of the hangar with the late-afternoon sun behind him, which was a cheap trick, because it meant I had to squint at him and he got to look composed and golden while I did it. He wore a linen shirt that cost more than my annual fuel budget and an expression of wounded sincerity that he practiced, I'm fairly certain, in a mirror.

"Mirabel Oyelaran," he said warmly.

"That's not my name."

"It's the name on your father's old flight registrations." He spread his hands, as if this were a gift he'd brought me. "It's a good name. Strong name. You should use it more."

"I use it exactly as often as it's useful," I said, "which is never, and especially not today. Go away, Dario."

He came in anyway. Of course he came in anyway. Dario Vance has never once respected a boundary that wasn't enforced by physical obstruction, and even then he'd probably try to negotiate.

* * *

Let me back up, because you deserve context, and because telling it takes longer than living it did, which is one of life's small cruelties.

Dario Vance and I grew up two hangars apart at Meridian Strip, children of the same scrappy aviation community, breathing the same kerosene-and-cut-grass air. His father ran cargo contracts up and down the river systems; mine flew medicine and mail wherever the money wasn't, which is to say everywhere that needed it most. We raced paper gliders off the fuel shed roof. We learned to read weather off each other's faces. For about four years, between ages ten and fourteen, Dario Vance was my best friend.

Then his family's business started growing the way some businesses grow — not upward, but outward, like mold — and mine started shrinking under debts nobody would explain to me. By sixteen we were rivals. By nineteen we were enemies, though neither of us had declared it; it was simply understood, the way rain is understood. And by twenty-six, when my father's plane went into the Sable River with the *Kestrel's Promise* salvage job on his manifest and Dario's company holding the contract my father had turned down — well. By then there wasn't a word strong enough, so I stopped using words and started using distance.

Ten years of distance, specifically. Ten years of flying medicine and gossip across the interior under a name that wasn't quite mine, keeping my head down, keeping my compass close, and keeping one rule above all others:

Never fly the Sable River route.

Not around it. Not near it. Not for double pay, not for triple, not if the interior's last clinic burned down and the only alternative was walking. Some people have a line drawn in sand. Mine was drawn in river water, and it runs brown and slow past a bend where a plane went down with my father in it, and no amount of money has ever made me look at it twice.

Until, apparently, today.

* * *

"You're blocking my light," I told him, turning back to the engine. "And my day."

"I'll take five minutes."

"You'll take zero. Bea, how many minutes is he taking?"

"Zero," Bea said loyally, without looking up from the strut she was sanding. Bea's loyalty is a beautiful, blunt instrument. She once threw a fuel broker off the strip for shorting us, and she weighs maybe as much as the fuel drum she threw at him.

Tomas appeared in the hangar doorway behind Dario, arms crossed, filling the frame the way Tomas fills every frame, which is completely. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Between the three of us we formed a fairly convincing wall, and Dario looked at it the way a man looks at a locked door he intends to pick.

"Five minutes," he said again, "and then I'll leave you alone forever. You have my word."

"Your word is worth slightly less than the paper it isn't written on."

"Then hear me out for free." He smiled. God, that smile. It used to make teachers forgive him things. It still worked on people who hadn't known him since he was eleven and stealing their lunch money to fund a "business venture" involving reselling fireworks to younger children. "The *Kestrel's Promise*."

The wrench in my hand went very still.

I want to be clear: I knew what he was doing. I could see the whole architecture of it, the way you can see a con from the inside once you've been raised around them. He'd led with my name, softened me with history, and now he was laying down the one card guaranteed to stop my hands moving. It was manipulation, textbook, and I hated him for it, and it worked anyway, because grief doesn't care how it gets delivered.

"The wreck sits in forty feet of monsoon-swollen water," Dario continued, gentle as a man feeding a dangerous animal, "off the old Sable trail. She went down eleven years ago with her cargo hold sealed. Manifest says personal effects, crew valuables, and a strongbox registered to the owners' consortium. Salvage rights lapsed last month. They're open. First qualified crew to file claims the lot."

"Then go claim it," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "You have boats. You have crews. You have everything except shame."

"My boats are freight barges. I need an aircraft crew for the survey leg — aerial mapping of the channel before the water drops, pinpointing the hull. And there's exactly one pilot in this region who knows that stretch of river well enough to map it blind." He paused, letting it land. "Your father surveyed that trail nine times, Ulyana. His charts are the best in existence. And his daughter—"

"Don't." 

"—is the only person alive who might have absorbed half of what he knew."

I set the wrench down carefully, because throwing it at him would feel wonderful for approximately one second and expensive considerably longer. Then I turned around and gave him the full benefit of my attention, which he visibly enjoyed, which made me regret it immediately.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the number."

"I don't need to hear the number. The answer is no. It's no in a language you don't speak, Dario, so let me translate: get out of my hangar."

"Ulyana—"

"*Get out.*"

Something flickered across his face — hurt, real hurt, which was the worst part, because it meant some part of him genuinely didn't understand. Or genuinely wanted me to believe he didn't. With Dario those were always the same expression.

"The Kestrel's crew died waiting on a rescue that never launched," he said quietly. "Their families have spent eleven years being told their people were thieves who scuttled their own boat. There's cargo down there that might change that story. Doesn't any part of you want to be the one who brings it up?"

For one treacherous heartbeat, yes. One whole heartbeat of imagining it: the hull rising out of the brown water, the truth rising with it, my father's name scrubbed clean of whatever shadow the Sable River had wrapped around it. One heartbeat of wanting.

Then I remembered that Dario Vance had held the salvage contract my father refused, and had said, publicly, at the memorial service, within earshot of my grieving mother, that Erik Oyelaran had been "a fine pilot who let sentiment cost him a fortune." I remembered that Dario's company had bought up my father's debts after the crash, and pursued them against a widow with the tenderness of a dredge. I remembered whose name was on the lease papers that had slowly been strangling our strip for a decade.

Every road in my life, it sometimes seemed, led back through Dario Vance's ledger.

"Here's what I know about you," I said. "You don't do anything for dead crews' families. You do things for Dario. So whatever's in that strongbox, it isn't letters and keepsakes, and whatever you're really after, it isn't justice. Get out of my hangar, Dario. And take your contract with you."

He held my gaze for a long moment. Then, maddeningly, he nodded, as if I'd said something wise instead of something furious, and gathered his coat off the workbench where he'd draped it without permission.

"For the record," he said, "the offer stands until the monsoon closes the upper river. After that, the water drops, the site silts over, and everyone's answer becomes irrelevant." He walked to the hangar door, paused in it, silhouetted against the gold. "It was good to see you, Mirabel. You have his eyes, you know. He looked at rivers the same way you're looking at me right now."

And then he was gone, and I discovered I was gripping the workbench hard enough to whiten my knuckles, and that Bea and Tomas were both watching me with the careful blankness of people deciding whether to say something.

"Don't," I told them.

"We didn't say anything," said Bea.

"You were about to."

"We were," Tomas admitted, "but we've decided to live."

* * *

That evening I sat on the porch of the bunkhouse with my father's compass in my hand and watched the sky do its pre-monsoon rehearsal — towering clouds building in the west, gilded underneath, promising more than they delivered yet. The compass is brass, dented along one edge where it took a fall it never explained, and it doesn't point north anymore. It points about fifteen degrees east of true, stubbornly, permanently, like it decided somewhere along the way that north was a matter of opinion. My father used to say it kept him honest: *a compass with a flaw teaches you to navigate by everything else.*

I'd found it in the wreckage inventory, returned to me in a plastic evidence bag, and I had carried it every day since. I had never opened the case beyond checking the needle. There was a latch on the back, small and stiff, that I had never once touched. Don't ask me why. Some doors you keep closed because you're afraid of what's behind them, and some you keep closed because you're afraid of what opening them will say about you.

Bea came out with two cups of tea, handed me one, and sat down without asking, which is Bea's entire relational philosophy.

"He left something," she said.

My tea stopped halfway to my mouth. "What?"

"When he left. Put an envelope under the wiper on the truck. I figured you'd want to throw it out yourself rather than have me do it for you. Seemed like your kind of ceremony."

I looked at the envelope sitting on the porch rail where she'd placed it. Plain manila. My name — *Ulyana*, not Mirabel — written on it in Dario's neat, self-satisfied handwriting.

"Burn it," I said.

"Could do," Bea agreed, sipping her tea. "Or you could open it, confirm it's garbage, and then burn it. Some of us like closure with documentation."

I hate that she knows me. I picked up the envelope. It was heavier than paper alone should be. Inside was a single sheet, folded once, and something small and square and rigid sliding against my fingers.

I unfolded the sheet first. It was a photocopy of a flight manifest, dated eleven years ago — the date of my father's final flight. The *Kestrel's Promise* salvage survey. Filed by Dario Vance Holdings. And beneath the typed cargo lines, in the margin, in ink that had faded to brown, a handwritten note I recognized instantly, because I had grown up watching that handwriting fill the margins of every chart in our house:

*Declined. Cargo listed does not match hold capacity. Something wrong here. Checking further. — E.O.*

My father's hand. On Dario's copy of Dario's manifest. Which meant Dario had been in possession of my father's notes — his actual last written words — for eleven years, and had never given them to us, and had chosen tonight, now, as leverage, to let me see them.

*Something wrong here. Checking further.*

Checking further. Three days later his plane was in the river.

The anger came up through me slow and total, like floodwater, quiet at the surface and moving everything underneath. Eleven years I had carried a question with no edges — *why was he on that route, why that day, why did he turn back* — and Dario Vance had held a piece of the answer in a filing cabinet the entire time, and had just used it as a door-knocker.

I reached into the envelope and drew out the small square thing.

A photograph. Old stock, sun-faded at the corners. It showed a riverboat at a wooden dock — the *Kestrel's Promise*, I realized, young and white-painted and whole, years before her ending. Crew on deck, laughing, mid-wave. A woman in an apron leaning out of the galley window, grinning at whoever held the camera.

And standing at the dock's edge, cap pushed back, chart tube under one arm, caught mid-laugh at something outside the frame —

My father.

Younger than I ever got to know him. Standing on the *Kestrel's Promise* like he belonged there. Like these people were his people.

On the back, in pencil, in a stranger's writing: *Erik & the crew, last good season.*

I sat on the porch a long time. The tea went cold. The western clouds ate the sun, and the first fat drops of the evening's false start ticked against the tin roof, and Bea sat beside me the whole while saying nothing, which is the thing she's best at besides engines.

Because here is what the photograph did, what it did instantly and irreversibly: it took my rule — *never fly the Sable River route* — and it showed me my father standing on the far side of it, smiling, waving, waiting.

He hadn't been running that route for money. The note said so. *Something wrong here.* He'd been investigating. He'd been *checking further*. And whatever he'd found in three days of checking had put his plane in the water and kept Dario Vance's filing cabinet shut for a decade.

I thought about the number Dario hadn't gotten to quote. I thought about the lease, and the creditors circling, and the fact that our fuel tank was emptier than my patience. I thought about how Dario wanted me on that river, and how the surest way to find out what he actually wanted was to stand exactly where he was pointing and watch what he did next.

Mostly, though, I thought about my father's handwriting, and the sentence it ended on.

*Checking further.*

"Well," I said finally, to Bea, to the rain, to the compass lying heavy in my pocket like a small stubborn heart. "Damn him."

"Yeah," Bea said, and refilled my cup.
