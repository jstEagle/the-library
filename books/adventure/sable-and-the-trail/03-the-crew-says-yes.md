## 3. The Crew Says Yes

I slept badly, which was standard, but this time it wasn't the usual ghosts doing the haunting. It was the photograph. Dario Vance's photograph, sitting on my kitchen table like a scorpion someone had left under a glass so I could watch it.

My father, younger than I'd ever known him, standing on the deck of a boat I'd spent twenty years pretending I didn't recognize. The Kestrel's Promise. And behind him, half-turned toward the camera with that particular squint he got when he thought someone was lying to him — Dario Vance himself, thirty years old and already dressed like a man who expected to be forgiven for things.

I made coffee I didn't drink and stared at it until the light came up gray over the hangar roof. Then I did what I always do when a thing won't leave me alone: I put it in a drawer, told myself I'd deal with it later, and went to work. Later is where I keep everything I can't afford to feel before ten a.m.

The morning had a list of small failures waiting for me. The Cessna's number-three cylinder was running hot again, the med run schedule needed rewriting because the clinic at Puerto Alto had moved their supply day without telling anyone, and there was a letter from the bank in the mail slot that I recognized by its weight — thin envelope, heavy news — and decided could also wait until later.

Bea was already under the Cessna when I got to the hangar, which meant she'd been there since five, which meant something was wrong. Bea only beats the sun when she's avoiding a conversation or chasing a problem. Sometimes both.

"Number three?" I asked.

"Number three," she said, not looking up. "And also your landing gear bushings, and also the fact that you've been flying on a cracked windscreen seal since June because you keep saying you'll fix it after the season."

"I said I'd fix it after the season."

"You say that about everything. You're going to fix your whole life after the season." She rolled out on her creeper and sat up, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being blue sometime last year. "Ulyana. We need to talk."

Those four words from a mechanic are never good. From Bea they're usually catastrophic. I pulled up a crate of engine oil and sat on it, which is the closest thing we have to an office chair.

"Tomas called a meeting," she said. "He's getting the others. He wanted me to tell you first so you didn't hear it sideways."

"Hear what sideways?"

She looked at me the way she looks at an engine that's making a sound it shouldn't. Patient. Braced. "Just — whatever happens in there, remember we voted on things as a crew. That's how we've always done it."

"That's not ominous at all," I said, and she almost smiled, and then didn't.

* * *

Tomas had set out chairs in the mess — actual chairs, arranged in a circle, which told me more than anything he said would have. We don't do circles. We do benches and crates and people leaning in doorways. A circle means formality, and formality means somebody has bad news they've rehearsed.

He'd also made coffee, real coffee, the expensive tin we save for funerals and landings in fog. I took a cup without asking and he let me, which was its own kind of message.

There were six of us: me, Bea, Tomas, young Ines who runs the radio and the books, old Mateo who does our fuel runs when his knees allow, and Rafa, who is nineteen and strong and mostly here because we feed him. Our whole operation, such as it is — one plane, one barge, one strip, and a reputation for getting medicine places nobody else will bother with.

Tomas stood, sat down, stood again, and finally just stayed standing like a man giving testimony.

"The lease," he said. "On the strip."

"What about it?"

"Meridian Holdings bought it. Three weeks ago. Quietly, through a shell company out of the capital." He put a folder on the table between us, and I noticed his hands were steady but his jaw wasn't. "We found out yesterday. The new owners sent notice. Sixty days to vacate."

The room went very quiet. Even the ceiling fan seemed to slow down out of respect.

"Sixty days," I repeated. "To move a hangar, a barge, a fuel cache, and twenty years of—"

"They know what they bought," Tomas said. "That's the point. They didn't buy the land for the land. There's nothing here. No road, no port, no minerals anyone's found. They bought it because we're on it, and because whoever's behind Meridian knows exactly what it costs us to leave."

I heard myself say, "Who's behind Meridian?"

Ines slid a page across the table. Company registration, directors' names, the usual paper trail that isn't really a trail if you know where to look. Two names. One was a lawyer I didn't recognize. The other—

"Dario Vance," I said, reading it aloud mostly so the room could share my disbelief. "You're kidding me."

"We wish," Ines said, dry as a riverbed in March. "He filed through intermediaries. But the signature block is his holding company. Same one that signed your contract draft yesterday."

So that was the shape of it. The rival who'd walked into my kitchen last night with a salvage contract and a photograph hadn't come to negotiate. He'd come to check whether I'd say yes on my own. And while I was sleeping badly and staring at pictures, his lawyers had been quietly buying the ground under my feet.

"He doesn't need my yes," I said. "He owns the strip. He can just evict us."

"He can evict the leaseholder," Tomas corrected. "But the lease has a clause — old one, from when your father negotiated it. Right of first refusal on transfer. If the current holder offers the property for sale, the sitting operator gets first chance to match."

"And we don't have the money to match a purchase price with a shoe string."

"No," Tomas said. "We don't. Not even close. Unless."

I looked around the circle. Everyone was looking at me, except Bea, who was looking at her coffee, and Rafa, who was looking at Bea to see how she felt about it, because Rafa is smart that way.

"Unless," I said, "we take the job."

Nobody said anything, which was everyone saying everything.

* * *

Let me tell you what my father's rule actually was, because I've mentioned it twice now and I've never explained it properly, and today of all days it deserves a full accounting.

It wasn't just "never fly the Sable River route." That was the version I said out loud, the shorthand, the way you compress a wound into a sentence so people stop asking questions. The full rule, the one he gave me the last time I saw him alive, standing on this same strip with his duffel over his shoulder and his compass in his pocket, was simpler and worse:

"Some cargo isn't worth the water it floats on. If a job smells wrong, walk away, even if walking away costs you everything. Especially then."

He said it like a man handing me a map of somewhere he'd already been lost. Four months later the Kestrel's Promise went down in the upper Sable with him aboard, hired — I learned much later, too late, from a lawyer's letter I still keep — as a consultant on a salvage survey. The same kind of job Dario was offering me. The same man signing the contract, though back then Dario was the junior partner and my father was the famous one, the pilot every outfit wanted, the man who could read weather off the skin of the river.

They paid out his insurance to the company, not to us. There were questions about the cargo. Manifest discrepancies. Whispers that the Kestrel had been carrying more than registered, that some of it had gone missing before the wreck, that my father's name was on the survey documents and therefore his name was in the mud with the ship. I was twenty-two. I spent the next decade answering those whispers with silence, which is the worst possible answer, and building a life as far from that river as a plane could take me while still living in the same country, because the medicine work mattered and I couldn't quit that, only the route.

So no. I had not planned to say yes.

* * *

"It's two hundred thousand," Tomas said into the silence. "Dario's advance against the salvage contract. Enough to match the sale price, cover the transfer fees, and keep us flying through the dry season. It clears the strip debt entirely."

"Two hundred thousand," I said. "For sailing up a river I've sworn off for twenty years, to a wreck that killed my father, for a man who now owns the deed to my home and is dangling it in front of me like bait on a hook."

"You said it better than I could," Bea said. "That's why you're the captain."

I looked at her. She was still not looking at me.

"Bea, say what you're thinking."

What she was thinking, it turned out, was this: "If we lose the strip, we don't lose a building. We lose the clinic runs. Puerto Alto, San Fermin, the mission at Alto Verde — none of them have another operator who'll fly the monsoon margins. We are, and I say this with all humility, the entire medical supply chain for about eleven thousand people, and if Meridian takes this ground, that chain breaks inside two months, and Dario Vance knows it, because that's why he bought the ground."

Mateo nodded slowly. "My knees can't relocate a fuel depot," he said. "And neither can anybody's."

Rafa said nothing, but he'd stopped looking at Bea and started looking at me, and his face had the specific nakedness of a nineteen-year-old watching adults decide his future.

Ines spoke last, because Ines always speaks last and always lands hardest. "I ran the numbers three ways," she said. "If we refuse and get evicted, we dissolve. There's no scenario where we restart somewhere else — the equipment sales wouldn't cover relocation, and our contracts with the clinics require a base within range, and there isn't one. If we accept Dario's contract, we survive, and we spend however long it takes hating ourselves. If we accept and something goes wrong—" she paused, delicately, "—then we're in the same place as refusing, plus dead."

"Thank you for the honest risk assessment," I said.

"I'm the bookkeeper. Honesty is the service I provide."

I got up. I walked to the window, because there's a window in the mess that faces the strip, and I needed to look at it. The grass was long at the edges; we'd been meaning to cut it. The windsock hung limp in the dead morning air. My father had graded this strip by hand, or mostly by hand, with a borrowed blade and a stubbornness I've spent my whole life inheriting in pieces. Every meter of it has a story I could tell you, and most of them end with him laughing at something.

Twenty years I've kept his rule. Twenty years of turning down Sable River charters, of eating the lost income, of letting rivals take the routes and the clients and the standing, of explaining to strangers at airstrip bars that no, thank you, we don't go upriver, and swallowing the look they give you — the one that says *ah, one of those pilots, superstitious, damaged, best avoided* — and swallowing it every time because the alternative was to explain, and explaining meant talking about him, and talking about him meant the grief got loose, and grief loose in a cockpit kills people.

And now the choice wasn't mine alone anymore. That was the part that broke me open, quietly, somewhere behind my sternum, while I stood looking at a mowed-dirt runway. When my father made his vow, it cost him. When I kept it, it cost me. But Bea and Tomas and Ines and Mateo and Rafa had never taken that vow. They'd inherited it the way crews inherit their captains' scars — respectfully, at a distance, without ever having been asked. And now the bill for my vow had arrived, addressed to all of them.

Eleven thousand patients. One strip. One barge. One plane. Five people who had followed me into every storm I deemed worth it and never once asked me to justify the ones I refused.

"Ulyana," Tomas said softly, from behind me. "Nobody here thinks less of you if you say no. I want that on the record before you answer. Whatever you decide, we decided it with you. That's the point of the circle."

I turned around. My eyes were doing something I'd rather they didn't in front of the crew, so I fixed them on the middle distance and spoke in my captain's voice, the flat one, the one that means the feeling has been successfully imprisoned pending further review.

"Two conditions," I said. "One: we renegotiate the contract terms. Full advance held in escrow before wheels roll, payment structured so the strip purchase closes regardless of salvage outcome. If Dario wants us on that river, he pays for the privilege upfront, and if he double-crosses us, the money's already ours."

"Reasonable," Ines said instantly. "Second condition?"

"Two: I want it understood, by everyone, forever, that I am doing this for this crew and for the clinics, and not because Dario Vance asked. And that if at any point I judge the job compromised — if it turns into what it was, if it becomes about treasure instead of survival — I pull us out, mid-river, mid-contract, consequences to the money notwithstanding. And you all hold me to that. All of you. Even if I fight you on it. *Especially* if I fight you on it."

Bea finally looked up. "You'll fight us on it," she said. "I give it three days upriver before you start rationalizing."

"Then I'll count on you to be smarter than me by day four."

She grinned. It was a terrible grin, full of foreknowledge. "Always am."

Tomas closed the folder. "Then it's settled? We take the contract, on your conditions?"

Around the circle: nods. Mateo's slow and creaking. Rafa's quick and hungry — he'd never been up the Sable, and youth is constitutionally incapable of fearing a river it hasn't met yet. Ines raised her hand formally, because she's the only one of us who believes procedure is a form of love. Bea nodded with her chin on her fist, already three moves ahead, probably calculating hull reinforcement tonnage.

I nodded last.

"There's one more thing," I said. "Before we sign anything, I'm going to find out what Dario knows about my father's last flight. The photograph wasn't a courtesy. It was a message: *I know what you're hiding.* Well, two can play. If he's been digging through twenty-year-old wreckage records, then he's left a trail, and I'm going to follow it backwards before I follow that river forwards."

"And if what you find makes you want to pull out?" Tomas asked.

"Then condition two activates," I said, "and you all get to watch me eat my own tail in front of a lawyer."

* * *

That evening I sat in the hangar office with the contract draft spread out and Ines across from me with a red pen and a mercenary gleam I found deeply comforting. We rewrote Dario's elegant little document into something with teeth: escrow, milestones, liability caps inverted, a termination clause broad enough to drive the barge through. Ines read boilerplate the way vultures read landscape.

Halfway through, I opened the drawer and took out the photograph again. My father, squinting at a liar. Dario, young and already smiling like ownership.

Under the desk lamp, I noticed something I'd missed. In the background, at the rail, blurred — a woman in a cook's apron, dark hair tied back, one hand resting on the shoulder of a child who couldn't have been more than six. The child was pointing at the water.

I turned the photo over. There was writing on the back, faint pencil, someone else's hand: *Kestrel's Promise, final survey run. Crew manifest attached.*

Attached. But nothing was attached anymore. Someone had removed it.

I sat there a long time, the lamp buzzing, the river running silently through my head in the dark. A cook. A child. People who lived on that boat, whose whole world went down with it, and whose names I had never once thought to wonder about in twenty years of keeping my vow — because my vow had only ever been about my grief, and grief is a selfish tenant.

Tomorrow we'd call Dario. Tomorrow the machinery would start turning, escrow accounts and title transfers and a leaking barge being prepped for a river I'd sworn off. Tonight, I wrote one line in the logbook where I keep things that aren't flight records:

*Going back. Not for him. For them.*

Then I closed the book, and went to tell Bea to start reinforcing the hull, because whatever else was true, monsoon season doesn't care what we're sailing for.
