## 4. Loading the Leaking Boat

There are two kinds of leaks in this world, and I've spent thirty years learning to tell them apart. The first kind you can hear — a hiss, a drip, a slow tick of water finding its way where it shouldn't. The second kind is quieter. It's the sound of a woman standing in her own kitchen at five in the morning, packing a bag she hasn't told anyone about.

I'm the mechanic. My job is the first kind of leak. The second kind belongs to Ulyana, and she thinks I don't notice things.

I notice everything.

* * *

The barge had a name once — *Marta's Grace*, after Tomas's grandmother — but the paint gave up on it years ago, so now she's just "the boat" or, when Bea's feeling honest, "the rust bucket with opinions." She's forty feet of riveted steel that started life hauling fuel drums upriver before I was born, and every plate of her has been patched at least twice, some of them by me, one of them — according to Tomas — by a man who was struck by lightning mid-weld and finished the job anyway.

I believe him. This river attracts that kind of story.

We'd been at it since dawn. The monsoon window Dario's people kept talking about wasn't really a window; it was more like a door left open in a hurricane. Three days, maybe four, between the tail of the last front and the arrival of the long cell sitting out over the coast like a fat gray animal waiting for dinner. Enough time to make the run to the wreck site if nothing went wrong. And nothing ever goes wrong, which is why I carry twice the spare parts any sane mechanic would.

"Bea." Tomas came down the ramp carrying two crates of medical supplies we were running upriver as cover cargo — legitimate freight, real invoices, because even a salvage run looks better on paper if half the hold is honest. "Where do you want these?"

"Away from the water line. If the hull lets go again, I want the bandages dry."

"That's grim."

"That's engineering."

He grinned and hauled them aft, and I went back to the portside seam I'd been fighting since Tuesday. It was an old wound, a weeping crack along a plate that had been patched so many times the welds had welds. I'd ground it back to bare metal, laid a fresh bead, and now I was chipping slag off it while the generator thumped behind me like a second heartbeat.

Ulyana appeared at the top of the ramp around seven, coffee mug in hand, hair still damp from the river tap. She looked at the boat the way she always did — like a pilot assessing whether the thing under her was going to kill her today.

"How's the patient?"

"She'll fly."

"It's a boat, Bea."

"She'll float, then. Same prayer, different altitude."

She almost smiled. That was the thing about Ulyana — the smile never quite landed, just banked away at the last second like a plane changing course. She'd made her decision last night; I'd seen it land on her face over dinner, that particular settling, the way a person looks when they've chosen the lesser grief. But she hadn't said anything about it directly, because Ulyana doesn't do direct when it costs her. She does logistics.

"We'll need to be loaded by tomorrow noon," she said. "Dario's advance clears tonight. Fuel, water, chain, dive gear if we can get it. Tomas is making a list."

"Tomas always makes a list. Half the list is snacks."

"The other half is rope. Between them we're covered."

She stood there a moment longer, watching me work, and I had the distinct feeling she was checking whether I'd noticed anything. Which meant there was something to notice. Which meant my morning had just gotten more interesting.

"Anything else?" I asked, mild as milk.

"No," she said. Too fast. Then, "The charts are in the office if you want the route marked."

And she walked back down the ramp toward the strip, mug swinging, spine straight as a plumb line.

I set down my chipping hammer and looked at the office window across the yard. The curtain was drawn. It was never drawn.

* * *

Here's what I know about Ulyana Eriksen that she'd deny under oath: she grieves in secret and calls it privacy. Her father has been dead eleven years, and in eleven years I have never once heard her say his name out loud. There's a compass she carries — brass case, dented, older than both of us — that lives in her flight jacket pocket, and I've watched her take it out at odd moments and hold it without opening it, like a letter she can't bring herself to read. I asked her about it once, years ago, early days, when I still thought honesty was a strategy that worked on people. She told me it was broken.

It isn't broken. I've held it. The needle swings true.

So when I saw her, mid-morning, crossing the yard with a canvas roll tucked under her arm the way you carry something you don't want to have a shape, I didn't say a word. I just watched her slip into the office, watched the curtain stay drawn for twenty minutes, watched her come out with the roll gone and her jacket pocket riding heavier than it should.

Charts. Old ones. His.

I went back to welding. Some cargoes you don't declare.

* * *

By afternoon the boat looked less like a corpse and more like a patient prepped for surgery. We'd lashed the deck cargo low and center — weight down, weight amidships, because the upper Sable doesn't forgive top-heavy boats, and neither do the rapids below the Kestrel Narrows. Tomas had rigged new canvas over the hold. I'd replaced the raw-water impeller, serviced the bilge pumps — both of them, plus the backup, plus the bucket Tomas insisted on calling "the tertiary system" — and re-packed the stuffing box until the shaft log dripped exactly once every eight seconds instead of once every three.

Progress, in riverboat terms.

Fuel was the ugly math. We could only afford to fill the tanks to eighty percent — Dario's advance covered the fuel, barely, but the monsoon window meant no refuel stops, and the side-channel detour around the Narrows added hours we hadn't budgeted. Eighty percent meant margin for nothing. I wrote the number in grease pencil on the engine housing where I keep numbers I don't like: 80%. Underneath it, after a moment, I added: *and pray.*

Tomas found me there, elbow-deep in the bilge.

"Dario's man called again," he said. "Wants a passenger manifest by tonight. Their insurance, apparently."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him we'd have one by tonight." He scratched his jaw. "Which reminds me. There's a woman at the gate."

"A woman at the gate."

"Says she wants passage upriver. Paying. Cash." He paused. "Says she knows the trail."

Everybody says they know the trail. Half the people who say it have never been past the second bend, and the other half are lying about why they're going. "Did you tell her we're not a ferry?"

"I did. She said she wasn't asking for a ferry. She said—" he squinted, remembering, "—she said 'I'm asking for the boat that's going to the Kestrel.'"

The chipping hammer went very still in my hand.

Nobody outside this crew knew our destination yet except Dario's people, and Dario's people communicated via satellite messages and lawyers, not by showing up at the gate with cash and a straight face. I climbed out of the bilge, wiped my hands on a rag that was cleaner than my hands, and went up the ramp to see for myself.

* * *

She was standing by the fuel drums with a pack at her feet and her arms folded, taking in the boat with an expression I recognized because I wear it myself: the professional assessment of a vessel's flaws, conducted silently and without mercy. Young-ish. Late twenties, maybe. Dark hair cut short and practical, sun-browned skin, hands that looked like they'd done work — rope work, oar work, something with lines. She wore a canvas jacket that had been rained on many times and dried badly, and she stood the way river people stand, weight settled, ready for the ground to move.

"You're the mechanic," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the mechanic."

"Bea. Right? The tall one mentioned it." She nodded toward Tomas, who had the decency to look unrepentant. "Sable."

"Just Sable?"

"Just Sable." Something flickered across her face when she said it — quick, gone, like a fish turning under the surface. A name worn smooth from use, or a name put on recently, or both. "I want passage to the upper Sable. Past the Narrows."

"That's a working boat, not a taxi."

"I know. I'm offering to work. Rope, galley, bilge — whatever your mechanic doesn't want to do." She glanced at my hands, at the grease to the wrist. "Though from the look of it, that list might be short."

Flattery. Points for accuracy, though.

"Why up there?" I asked. "Monsoon season. Nobody runs the upper Sable in monsoon season except idiots and us, and we're only doing it because we were paid to stop being sensible."

She looked at me for a long moment, weighing something. I've watched Ulyana do that same arithmetic — how much truth can this stranger carry — and I know all its faces. Sable came down on the side of almost nothing.

"My mother lived up there," she said. "On the river. There's things of hers I need to see with my own eyes. Things that got left." A beat. "That's true, and it's not all of it. Take it or leave it."

Honesty, rationed. Another face I knew.

Here's the part I should probably admit: I liked her immediately, and I trusted her not at all, and those two facts sat together in my chest as comfortably as old friends. River people are like that. You can love someone and check their knots at the same time.

"Passage is four hundred, cash, paid before we cast off," I said. "You sleep on deck under canvas. You eat what we eat. You pull your weight or you swim home. And if the captain says jump, you ask how high on the way up."

"Fair terms." She dug a fold of bills out of her jacket — actual bills, counted already, which meant she'd expected the price, which meant she'd done her homework on us, which I filed away in the drawer where I keep things that bother me. "One condition of mine. Nobody goes through my pack, and I don't ask what's in yours."

"Deal," said Tomas, appearing beside us with the timing of a man who smells money.

"It's not my boat," I said. "Captain decides."

"Then let's go find the captain."

* * *

We found Ulyana in the office, and I'll give Sable this much — she didn't flinch when Ulyana looked up from the manifest with that flat pilot stare that has ended negotiations in rooms from here to the coast. She just set her pack down, put the cash on the desk, and said, "Four hundred. Passage past the Narrows. I work for my berth."

Ulyana looked at the money. Then at Sable. Then, briefly, at me — and I gave her the smallest shrug I own, the one that means *your call, and also I have opinions I'm saving.*

"Who told you our route?" Ulyana asked.

"Nobody told me. I asked at the fuel dock who was running the upper trail this window. They laughed. Then someone said the Eriksen crew was loading a barge, and here I am." She tilted her head. "Was I wrong to come?"

Ulyana was quiet long enough that the generator's thump through the wall became very loud. I watched her do it — the whole calculus, the debt, the window, the extra pair of competent hands versus the stranger who knew too much about where we were going. I watched her land, the way she lands everything: hard, fast, and slightly short of the runway she wanted.

"Deck watch, midnight to four," Ulyana said. "You're mine during those hours. Cash stays on the desk until we cast off."

Sable put out her hand. Ulyana took it after a pause that lasted exactly one heartbeat too long to be casual.

"Pleasure," Sable said.

"We'll see," said Ulyana.

* * *

Evening came down over the strip the way it does before a front — copper light, dead air, the birds going quiet all at once like someone shut a door. We worked until we couldn't see, then worked by lamp until the loading was done: crates lashed, drums secured, dive gear stowed in the dry locker, Tomas's snack list fully realized. The boat sat heavy on her lines, riding low and ready, and I stood on the ramp looking at her with the specific pride and dread of a mechanic sending a patched hull into weather it was never built for.

Ulyana came out to stand beside me. For a while neither of us said anything. Downriver, the first far-off grumble of thunder rolled along the horizon like furniture being moved in a room above us.

"Eighty percent fuel," I said finally. "Fresh welds on the port seam. Bilge pumps good. Bucket operational."

"So she'll float."

"She'll try."

Ulyana huffed — almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "You saw the charts."

Not a question either. I kept my eyes on the water. "I see everything. It's my best quality and nobody appreciates it."

"He'd have hated this," she said. "The route. The job. All of it." Her hand went to her jacket pocket, touched the shape there, dropped. "But he'd have wanted the boat to hold."

"The boat will hold," I said. "I built most of her twice."

She nodded, and squeezed my shoulder once — Ulyana for *thank you*, delivered at pressure — and went down the ramp toward the office to finish the manifest.

I stayed a minute longer. Thunder again, closer. The river sliding past black and patient, carrying everything downstream eventually, the way it does.

New variable, I thought. The woman called Sable, with her counted-out cash and her rationed truths and her hands that knew rope. Strangers who show up knowing your destination aren't passengers. They're questions wearing boots.

Tomorrow we cast off at first light, into the door left open in the hurricane, with eighty percent fuel, a leaking hull, a captain carrying her father's secrets in her pocket —

— and somewhere downriver, in the dark, the first rain of the long cell hit the water, so far away it was only a sound like static, like a radio tuning itself toward us.
