## 5. First Rapids

The Sable River does not begin so much as it seeps — out of marshland and old logging cuts and a hundred nameless creeks that all decide, somewhere north of the last fuel depot, to become one wide brown argument heading downhill. I have flown over it for eleven years and refused to land on it for nine of those, which is the kind of arithmetic that keeps a person honest about her own cowardice.

Now here I was, on it, in it, of it, standing at the wheel of the *Marta June* while Bea's patched-together engine knocked underneath my feet like a polite but persistent creditor.

"Port side coming up," Tomas called from the bow, where he stood with a pole he mostly used for pointing at things dramatically. "Shoal line. Maybe."

"Maybe," I repeated.

"It's either a shoal or a very committed log."

"On this river those are the same animal."

I eased us left. The *Marta June* is forty feet of riveted stubbornness that Bea inherited from an uncle who won her in a card game and immediately regretted it, and she handles like a filing cabinet with opinions. But she floats, which after the week we'd had felt like showing off.

Behind me, on the cargo deck, our paying passenger sat on a crate of diesel filters with her boots off and her feet bare, reading the river the way other people read newspapers.

Sable. One word, no surname offered, and when Tomas had asked where she was headed she'd said "upriver" with the serene confidence of someone who considered geography a formality. She'd paid cash — actual paper money, which in this economy was either charming or alarming — and she'd climbed aboard at dawn without luggage beyond a canvas roll and a tin cup that hung from her belt like a piece of jewelry.

She was maybe thirty, sun-brown, with forearms that suggested she'd done real work and hands that suggested she'd done some of it with tools I didn't want to think about. Her hair was going gray at the temples early, the way hair does on people who live outdoors and don't apologize for it.

And she watched water. That was the thing. Most passengers watch scenery. Sable watched the current — tracked it, actually, her eyes moving upstream along the seams between fast water and slow, reading eddies the way I read cloud bases. Twice now she'd said something quiet before we hit rough water, not loud enough to be advice, just early enough to be suspicious.

The first time, I'd told myself it was coincidence. The second time, I'd started keeping score.

* * *

By midmorning we reached the first set of rapids proper — nothing lethal, just the river narrowing through a shelf of black rock and getting ideas above its station. The channel braided into three threads around midstream islands, and the middle one looked smoothest and was therefore, by the ancient law of rivers, lying.

"Tomas," I called. "Which one did the chart say?"

"The chart says the chart is from 1987."

"Helpful."

Bea came up from the engine hatch wiping her hands on a rag that had once been a shirt and would one day be a rag again, completing its journey. "She'll take the left thread if you keep her bow into the standing wave," she said. "Right thread's got a sweeper across it. I can hear it."

"You can *hear* it?"

"I can hear everything. It's a curse." She squinted at the water. "Also there's somebody standing on the left bank who seems to want to tell us something."

There was. A figure in a rain slicker despite the dry morning, waving one arm in long deliberate sweeps — the universal semaphore for *no*, or possibly *yes*, depending on which school of river signaling had trained you.

"That's a warning," Sable said, from behind us. Not loudly. Just present, the way weather is present.

I turned. "You know this stretch?"

"No." She pulled her boots back on, unhurried. "But I know what a drop looks like from the bank. He's marking the safe line with his arm. See — he stops sweeping when your bow points left of the island. Starts again when you drift right. He's drawing you a picture."

I looked. She was right. The man's arm went still every time we swung toward the left-hand channel and resumed its frantic erasing whenever we wandered. Somebody downstream had spent their morning doing unpaid air-traffic control for strangers, and I made a mental note to leave him something from the galley.

"Left thread," I said. "Bow into the wave. Bea, keep her honest."

"She's never been honest a day in her life, but I'll encourage her."

We took it. The *Marta June* climbed the standing wave with a shudder that rattled my teeth, wallowed, dug in, and shot through into the slack water below while the river slapped the hull like applause from someone who resents you. Tomas whooped. Bea allowed herself the ghost of a smile, which for Bea was a standing ovation.

And Sable — Sable hadn't braced. Hadn't grabbed anything. Hadn't even shifted her weight. She'd ridden the whole thing loose-kneed and easy, absorbing the boat's motion like she was part of its ballast, watching the rocks go by with an expression I could only describe as homesick.

That expression bothered me more than the competence did. Competence you can explain. Homesickness aimed at white water is a different animal entirely.

* * *

We stopped past noon at a place called Halfway Bar — a gravel spit with a leaning lean-to, a fire ring, and a hand-painted sign reading NO SERVICES that someone had thoughtfully amended with NO NOTHING. Tomas got a line in the water because Tomas considers fishing less a hobby than a constitutional right, and Bea disappeared under the engine hatch again, muttering the litany of small failures that passes, in her, for prayer.

I put the kettle on the camp stove and found Sable already gathering firewood. Of course she was. She moved around a campsite like she'd been born in one, stacking wood in the tepee pattern, finding dry tinder under wet bark with the unerring instinct of someone who'd been hungry outdoors before and hadn't enjoyed it.

"Coffee?" I asked, because I am capable of civility even during an interrogation.

"Please." She sat down on the gravel across the fire ring from me, close enough to talk, far enough to be polite. Or far enough to make a point about how little space she needed. Hard to say.

"You've done this before," I said. "River work."

"Some."

"What kind?"

Her eyes came up and met mine, and there was nothing evasive in them, which was somehow worse. Evasive people you can work with. "Ferry runs, mostly. Freight. A season pushing logs, which I don't recommend." She turned her cup in her hands. "And I grew up on a boat."

"Which boat?"

The pause was small. A half-second, no more. But I fly instruments for a living, and I can feel a needle twitch the way other people feel rain coming.

"Various," she said.

"Growing up on various boats is a hard childhood."

"It had its moments." She drank her coffee and looked at the river instead of me, and I let it go, because pressing too hard too early is how you lose people, and I wanted — I told myself I only wanted — information.

Tomas saved us both by arriving with a fish that was technically legal and morally embarrassing, and lunch became a group project. Bea emerged smelling of diesel and triumph, announced that the number-two cylinder was "complaining but not yet filing charges," and ate two fish. Conversation drifted the way it does — fuel prices, the monsoon reports crackling in from the coast, whether the lean-to would survive another season or had achieved a kind of architectural dignity in its ruin.

It was Sable who steered it back.

"The Kestrel's Promise," she said, conversationally, cracking a fish bone between her fingers. "You're the crew Vance hired for that, aren't you. Word travels."

"Word travels," I agreed, in a tone designed to travel nowhere further.

"Heard the wreck's up past Widow's Bend. Thirty-one years down." She said it lightly, the way you'd discuss the weather. "Big salvage. What's he paying you to bring up?"

"Contract terms are contract terms."

"Mm." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it did absolutely nothing to soften her eyes. "Fair enough. I only ask because everyone on this river has a Kestrel story, and I'm collecting them. Like stamps."

"Everyone has a story about everything up here," Bea said. "Half of them are true."

"Which half is yours?" Sable asked me.

I fed the fire a stick instead of answering, which was an answer, and she had the grace to accept it as one. But I noticed — filed away, tagged, cross-referenced — that she hadn't asked about the payment. Everyone else in my life, from Dario Vance to the tax office, led with the payment. She'd led with the story. People who lead with stories usually already know how it ends.

* * *

The afternoon ran long and gold, the river widening into slow braided channels where herons stalked the shallows with the patience of debt collectors. I let Tomas run the wheel for a stretch — good practice for him, and it gave me an excuse to stand at the rail doing nothing, which is a skill I maintain rigorously.

That was when I saw her, aft by the cargo deck, with my charts.

Not hiding it, exactly. She'd taken the folded river survey from the chart box under the wheelhouse bench — the box I'd left latched, which meant she'd opened it, which meant she'd gone looking — and she stood with it open against the rail, one finger tracing the river's line north. Tracing it steadily, unhurriedly, until the finger stopped.

Stopped at a bend marked in faded pencil. Not the printed soundings — the annotation. My father's handwriting, small and slanted, the numbers he'd penciled beside a bend thirty-one years ago: depth readings, a bearing, a note in shorthand I'd never learned to read. The coordinates of the Kestrel's grave, copied from his last flight plan onto a working chart, because he was the kind of man who wrote important things where they'd get used and then died before he could explain them.

Sable's finger rested on that bend and did not move.

I crossed the deck slower than my pulse wanted to. "Good chart," I said.

She didn't startle. I'll give her that — most people caught red-handed at least perform surprise. She just folded the chart along its creases, neat as a letter, and held it out to me handle-first like a tool being returned.

"It is," she said. "Older edition than mine."

"Yours." I took it. "You have charts of this river."

"I have charts of most rivers." She met my eyes, and whatever I'd expected to find there — guilt, deflection, the quick slide of a caught liar — wasn't there. What was there was tiredness, and something underneath the tiredness that looked a lot like a person deciding something. "Ulyana Eriksen," she said. "You fly medicine up and down this country and nobody can find a bad word to say about you except the people who owe you money. You hate Dario Vance, you hated this job, and you took it anyway, which means somebody's holding something over your crew. And you're flying your father's route notes to a wreck you swore off nine years ago." She tipped her head toward the chart in my hand. "So before you decide what I am — ask yourself why a woman who grew up on a riverboat would pay cash to ride straight into the exact bend where the Kestrel's Promise went down."

The engine knocked. The herons kept stalking. Somewhere behind us Tomas laughed at his own joke, unaware that the world had tilted four degrees.

"My father's route notes," I said carefully, "are none of your business."

"They're the only reason any of us are going to find that wreck," she said. "Your father knew where the manifest hold wasn't. Which makes him the most interesting man who ever drowned on this river, and I have been waiting nine years to meet whoever inherited his questions." She picked up her tin cup and slung it back on her belt, and for the first time since the airstrip she looked, briefly, exactly as tired as I felt. "My mother was the cook on the Kestrel. Her name was Odile. And I'd like to know what happened to her ship, Captain, because the version everyone tells isn't the version I lived."

The river carried us north. I stood there holding a chart with my father's handwriting on it, looking at a stranger who had just handed me a grief to match my own, and I did not believe her.

But I wanted to. God help me, I wanted to — and wanting is the most dangerous instrument on any flight deck, because it reads steady right up until it doesn't.

"We'll talk tonight," I said. "After dark. No crew."

"After dark," she agreed, and walked forward to help Tomas with the lines, moving through the boat like she'd always belonged on it.

I unfolded the chart and looked at my father's pencil marks, and for the first time in nine years I wondered — really wondered — whether the vow I'd built my whole life around had been protecting the truth, or burying it.

Ahead, the sky over the northern hills had gone the particular bruised color that Bea calls *weather with intentions*.
