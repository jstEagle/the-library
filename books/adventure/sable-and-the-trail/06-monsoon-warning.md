## 6. Monsoon Warning

I heard the weather before I saw it, which is the wrong order for things to happen on a river.

Tomas hears storms in his teeth first — that's what Bea says, anyway, and Bea is usually right about machines and usually wrong about me. But that evening I was up on the barge roof re-lashing the fuel drums when my jaw started aching like a bad molar, and I looked north and there it was: a wall of cloud sitting on the horizon like something that had been waiting for us specifically. Not the pretty kind of storm cloud, all towers and drama. The flat kind. The kind that means business conducted over days rather than hours.

The radio crackled before I'd finished climbing down.

"—monsoon cell forming over the Khota highlands, tracking southeast, upper Sable corridor expect sustained heavy precipitation seventy-two to ninety-six hours—" The voice belonged to some regional forecaster with the cheerful weariness of a man who says this every year and is never listened to. "—advise all river traffic to seek shelter or divert—"

I stood there with my hand still on the antenna guy-wire and did the arithmetic every river man does and hates doing. Distance to the wreck site: two days at our pace if nothing went wrong. Duration of the cell: three to four days minimum, probably more once it parked itself over the gorge. Fuel remaining after yesterday's leak scare: enough for one good run or two bad ones.

And behind me, through the cabin window, Ulyana Eriksen reading her father's charts by lamplight like a woman studying scripture she didn't believe in anymore but couldn't put down.

* * *

We held the council of war at dinner, which is to say we sat around the little table in the cabin eating Bea's rice-and-something while the lamp swung gently overhead and nobody looked at anybody else.

The satellite terminal had printed out Dario Vance's latest message an hour earlier, and it lay in the middle of the table like a dead thing. I'd read it twice. It hadn't gotten better with repetition.

**PROGRESS? WINDOW CLOSING. REMINDER: CONTRACT STIPULATES DELIVERY OF POSITION CONFIRMATION WITHIN 5 DAYS. NON-COMPLIANCE VOIDS ADVANCE AND INCURS RECOVERY COSTS. V**

"Recovery costs," Bea said, around a mouthful of rice. "He's going to recover costs from us. With what army?"

"With lawyers," Ulyana said quietly. "Lawyers are worse than armies. Armies you can see coming."

She was different since the night camp — not softer exactly, because Ulyana soft would be like iron softening, but more present. Before, she'd been flying this boat from somewhere slightly above her own body, a woman piloting herself by instruments. Now she was in the room. She'd even laughed once yesterday, briefly, at something Tomas said about catfish, and the sound had startled all three of us like a shot.

But tonight she wasn't laughing. She spread the river chart flat over Dario's message, weighting the corners with cups, and drew the line with her finger. Here: our anchorage, a bend full of drowned timber. Here: the gorge narrows. Here, another day up: the Kestrel's last known position, marked in her father's handwriting, small and sure.

"The cell hits the upper river tomorrow night," she said. "If we're past the gorge before then, we can shelter near the wreck site itself — there's high ground, old quarry cuttings. We sit out the worst of it and work the wreck after."

"And if we're not past the gorge?" I asked, because someone had to.

"Then we spend four days tied to trees watching the water rise, and Dario voids the contract, and the strip goes under, and we go back to owing people who aren't as polite as he pretends to be." She said it flat, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse.

Bea pushed her bowl away. "Or we turn back now. Beat the cell downstream, refund the advance, eat the loss. Live to fly another season."

Nobody answered that right away, because turning back meant the lease, and the lease meant the creditors, and the creditors were the whole reason we were floating up a cursed river toward a wreck that had already killed one Eriksen.

It fell to me, as it sometimes does, to say the thing everyone's thinking. "How much does the advance actually cover?"

Ulyana's jaw tightened. "The lease buyout plus interest. Barely. If we finish the job."

"If we don't finish the job?"

"Then it's a loan. And loans are how this started."

The lamp swung. Somewhere outside, the first far-off grumble of thunder rolled down the valley like furniture being moved upstairs by giants.

* * *

I took the first watch, because I always do — Tomas sleeps like a dropped anchor and Bea sleeps like a woman rationing sleep against a future shortage, but neither of them trusts the river at night, and I grew up on water that wanted to kill me, so night rivers feel honest to me. They tell you exactly what they are.

What the river told me that night was: hurry.

The water had come up half a meter since morning. Debris rode the current in the dark — branches, a whole tree once, spinning slowly like something drowning. The monsoon was pushing floodwater down from the highlands ahead of the rain itself, the way a crowd pushes ahead of the doors opening. Every stick of wood in the river was a potential hull wound. I steered us wide around everything and thought about my grandmother, who used to say the river gives you three warnings and then it takes payment.

The river had given us the forecast. The rising water. And now, at midnight, the third warning arrived in the form of the satellite terminal chirping inside the cabin.

I let it chirp four times before curiosity beat superstition. The message printed slow, letter by letter, like it resented being sent:

**POSITION NOW. COORDINATES. NOT TOMORROW. V**

Coordinates. He wanted coordinates — not salvage confirmation, not cargo manifest verification, just the location. I carried the printout back to the stern and read it again under the running light, and the more I read it the less it looked like a client's impatience and the more it looked like a man asking for a map.

Because here's the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I'd been chewing on since the ridge two days ago: clients don't need coordinates. Clients need results. A man who wants coordinates wants them so he doesn't need *you* anymore.

I woke Ulyana. She came up on deck barefoot, hair unbound, and read the message without a word. Then she read it again. Then she folded it very carefully, along the existing creases, the way you fold something you intend to keep as evidence.

"He's never asked for position before," she said. "Every other job, he asks for manifests, condition reports, chain-of-custody forms. This time it's just 'where.'"

"You think he's got another boat."

"I think I've known Dario Vance for twenty years and he has never once spent money he could steal instead." She looked north, where the lightning was starting to flicker behind the cloud wall, silent heat-lightning, the storm showing its cards face-down. "We keep moving. We just don't tell him where we are."

"That voids the contract."

"That contract was void the moment he asked for coordinates instead of cargo." She handed the printout back. "Get some sleep, Tomas. Tomorrow we run the gorge."

* * *

The gorge, for those who haven't had the pleasure, is eleven kilometers of river squeezed between walls of black stone, where the water moves fast enough to have opinions and the rocks move not at all, which is the fundamental problem. In dry season it's a technical run — doable with a good pilot and a good engine and moderate luck. In rising water it becomes something else entirely. The rapids stack up. The eddies turn into whirlpools with ambitions. The whole river stops being a road and starts being an argument.

We ran it at dawn, because Ulyana wanted maximum daylight and Bea wanted to run the engine hard while it was still cool, and because waiting felt worse than going.

I won't pretend I was calm. I worked the bow line through the worst of it, hauling us off a mid-channel fang that appeared out of standing waves like a whale surfacing, and at one point the water hit us sideways with a ton or so of flood-current and shoved us twenty meters downstream in about two seconds, and I heard Bea below decks saying something to the engine in a tone I've only otherwise heard people use with dying relatives.

But we came through. Eleven kilometers later the walls opened out, the current slackened into the long flooded valley above, and the three of us stood on deck breathing like we'd all been underwater personally.

Behind us, the sky over the gorge had gone the color of a bruise.

Ahead, the rain began.

* * *

It started gently, insultingly gently, a fine warm drizzle that made everything smell green, and Bea actually laughed and said maybe the forecasters were wrong, and I remember thinking: they're never wrong downward. Forecasts miss the size of a storm, never the fact of it.

By noon it wasn't drizzle. By two o'clock it was the kind of rain that doesn't fall so much as arrive — a vertical ocean, a white roaring curtain that erased the far bank and turned the world down to the size of our deck. We anchored in the lee of a bluff, engines idling against the current, and held a second council, this one shorter.

"We're through the gorge," Ulyana said, shouting over the drumming on the cabin roof. "Wreck site's half a day. We push now, in this, or we anchor here and hope the anchors hold four days of rising water."

"Pushing in this kills us," Bea said. "Debris in flood water, zero visibility, the channel markers are all underwater—"

"Anchoring here kills us slower."

That was the whole debate, really. Fast death or slow death, with a third option nobody said aloud: turn back through the gorge in flood conditions, which wasn't an option at all unless you were tired of living.

Sable spoke for the first time. She'd been quiet through all of it, sitting by the window with her mother's journal wrapped in oilcloth in her lap, watching the rain with an expression I couldn't read — not fear, not excitement. Recognition, maybe. Like the storm was a language she'd learned as a child.

"There's a side channel," she said. "Two kilometers up. My mother described it — the crew used to shelter there during monsoon weeks, waiting out the runs. Deep water, high banks, overhanging rock. The Kestrel herself wintered there once." She looked at Ulyana. "If we're going to be trapped, we can be trapped somewhere with walls."

"And you know this from a journal?" Bea asked, in the voice she uses for things she doesn't believe, which is most things.

"I know it from a childhood," Sable said. "I told you. I grew up on that boat. I've been to that anchorage. I was six." Her hands went flat on the journal. "The river hasn't moved that much in twenty years. Rivers are slow about everything except killing."

Ulyana looked at each of us in turn. I watched her do the thing she does — weighing, deciding, taking the weight of the decision onto herself the way her father must have, the way that gets people killed or saves them depending on the day.

"Vote," she said. "All in favor of pushing to the side channel, say so. All in favor of anchoring here, say so. Nobody abstains. If we do this, we do it together."

"Aye," Bea said, surprising no one, because Bea votes for motion the way some men vote for violence — instinctively, and then worries after.

"Aye," I said. Anchoring in open flood water with debris coming downriver felt like lying down in traffic.

Sable raised her hand without a word.

Ulyana nodded slowly. Then she reached across the table and picked up the satellite terminal, and did something I'll remember the rest of my life. She opened the casing, found the transmit board, and pulled it — clean, deliberate, one smooth motion, like a nurse drawing a splinter.

"What are you doing?" Bea yelped. "That's company property!"

"It was company property until the company started asking for maps instead of cargo." Ulyana set the board on the table, a small dead rectangle of circuitry. "Receive-only from here on. He can shout at us all he likes. We can't answer, and more importantly, we can't be tracked by reply-ping. If he's got a second boat out there, let him find us the hard way."

The rain hammered. The lamp swung. Four women on a leaking barge in the throat of a monsoon, having just cut their own phone line, and I want to report that someone objected, that someone said wait, that someone pointed out we might need rescue.

Nobody did. That's the part I keep returning to. Nobody did, because somewhere in the last week we had stopped being a crew under contract and become something else — a family, maybe, or a conspiracy, or just four people who had decided the same lie was true: that together was a place, and we'd rather drown in it than be safe apart.

"Cast off," Ulyana said.

* * *

I took the bow through the side-channel approach, because the entrance was a slot between two drowned trees and I'm the only one aboard who reads water fast enough to call it. Rain in my eyes, river shoving at the hull, Bea's engine answering every demand like it had something personal to prove, and Sable beside me calling depths from memory — "sandbar, port, ten meters — snag, starboard, shallow, swing wide" — her voice steady as a metronome, a child's map spoken in a grown woman's voice.

We slid into the anchorage at dusk, if dusk can be said to exist during a monsoon; the light simply failed, surrendered, gave up pretending. Overhanging rock closed above us like a roof. High clay banks rose on both sides. The current out in the main channel roared past our sheltered pocket like a freight train on the next track, and inside our little harbor the water barely rippled.

Bea killed the engine and the silence was enormous.

We tied off to the old iron mooring rings still set into the rock — rings the Kestrel's crew had driven a lifetime ago, rusted orange, exactly where Sable said they'd be. I put my hand on one and felt something I don't have a word for. Twenty years ago, a cook on a riverboat drove these rings for her ship, and her daughter just led us to them through a monsoon from memory.

Ulyana stood at the rail looking out at the roaring dark, compass in her hand, thumb resting on the case lid the way it always does — not opening it, not yet, but closer than I'd ever seen her come.

"Tomas," she said, without turning around. "When the water drops. When we make our run at the wreck." A pause, filled with rain-sound. "I'm diving first."

I didn't argue. Some sentences you hear and you know the argument ended years ago, before any of us met, in a flooded hold or a burning sky or wherever her father made his choice.

That night the storm hit the upper river in earnest, and the world outside our rock roof became a single continuous sound, and the mooring rings sang in their sockets like tuning forks.

And in the middle of the night, I woke to a light on the water.

Far out in the main channel, past the curtain of rain, a white running light was moving upstream. Slow. Deliberate. Against the current, which no sane captain would attempt in flood conditions — unless the captain wasn't navigating by current at all, unless he knew exactly where he was going and exactly who was already there.

I counted seconds between the light and the next lightning flash, doing range math I didn't want to be right about.

Two kilometers. Maybe less.

I climbed down into the cabin to wake Ulyana, and found her already awake, sitting up in her bunk with the compass case open in her lap for the first time since her father died, staring at what was inside it.
