## 7. What Sable Knows

I have been lying to these people for six days, and the strange thing is how easy it's gotten.

Not the big lie. The big lie I've been carrying since I was nine years old, and it sits in my chest like ballast, familiar as my own heartbeat. I mean the small ones. The ones I tell Ulyana Eriksen over the galley stove while she pretends not to watch me. That I've never been up this stretch of river before. That I don't know what the Kestrel's Promise looked like before the river took her. That I paid my passage in cash because I'm careless with money, when the truth is I've been saving that money for eleven years, in a coffee tin with a rusted lid, for exactly this trip.

Tonight the rain has pinned us to a mudbank camp under a tarp that Bea swears is waterproof in the way that a sieve is waterproof. Tomas is on watch, or pretending to be, and Ulyana is cleaning her compass with a rag soft as an old wound. She does that a lot. Cleans things that don't need cleaning. It's what grief looks like when it's been folded up small enough to fit in a shirt pocket.

I decide, somewhere between the second cup of terrible coffee and the third, that it's time.

"You're going to ask me eventually," I say. "So I'd rather you asked me while the rain's loud enough that you can pretend you didn't hear the answer."

Ulyana doesn't look up from the compass. "That's a strange sentence, Sable."

"I'm a strange passenger."

"You're an expensive passenger." She winds the rag around the compass case, tucks it away. "Expensive passengers don't get to be cryptic. It's in the contract."

"There is no contract. Tomas wrote my name on a scrap of tarpaper."

"And that's binding," she says, and for the first time all evening there's something almost like warmth under the dryness. Almost like a door left open a crack. I've been watching this woman for six days, cataloguing her the way she probably catalogues weather fronts, and I've learned that her warmth is like the sun on this river — it comes out sideways, when you're not looking for it, and it's gone before you can point at it.

I take a breath. Here it goes. Eleven years of coffee-tin patience, and it comes out as one sentence, in the rain, to a woman who has every reason to hate what I'm about to say.

"I grew up on the Kestrel's Promise."

* * *

The compass rag goes still.

Ulyana looks at me the way you look at a river gauge when the water's rising faster than the numbers should allow. Recalculating. I can see her running the arithmetic — my age, the dates, the route, the questions I've been asking about the wreck since the first rapids. Every casual thing I've said this week rearranging itself behind her eyes into a different shape.

"My mother was the cook," I go on, because now that I've started, the words come up like water finding a crack. "Mirela Sable. I took her name when she died. Everyone on that boat called her Sabli, and I was little Sable before I was anything else, so it seemed simpler than explaining."

"Explaining what?" Her voice is careful. Level. But her hand hasn't moved from the compass case, and I notice she's holding it the way some people hold a crucifix.

"That I'm not a passenger. I never was. I hired on to this trip because the Kestrel's Promise is the only home I ever had, and it's been sitting in forty feet of river for eleven years, and nobody — *nobody* — has ever gone back for what's inside her."

The rain fills the silence for a while. Somewhere out in the dark, the river is doing what this river does, which is getting bigger and more opinionated by the hour.

Ulyana sets the compass down on the crate between us. Deliberately. Like she's putting a knife on a table instead of using it.

"Your mother was aboard," she says, "the night she went down."

"Yes."

"And you think there's something in the wreck worth diving for. In monsoon season. In a hull that's been rotting since before you were old enough to vote."

"I *know* there is." I lean forward. This is the part I've rehearsed in a hundred motel rooms and a dozen river towns, the part I've never said out loud to anyone, and now that it's coming, it comes out faster than I planned. "Everyone thinks the Kestrel went down with a strongbox of payroll and company papers, and everyone thinks the crew stole them, and everyone's been wrong for eleven years. The crew didn't steal anything. The crew was *cheated*. And the proof of it is in that hold, in letters, in the captain's own hand, and I am going to get it out if it's the last thing I do on this earth."

I expect her to argue. I've watched her argue with Bea about engine oil like it's a court case. I've watched her argue with the river itself, which is a losing proposition if ever there was one.

Instead she says, very quietly: "Who told you the crew was cheated?"

And that — that's the question I wasn't ready for. Because there's only one answer, and the answer is going to hurt her, and some treacherous part of me wants it to hurt her, and a better part of me is ashamed of that part.

"My mother," I say. "In a journal she kept hidden in the galley. In a coffee tin. She hid things in coffee tins. I come by it honestly."

Something flickers across Ulyana's face. Not anger. Not yet. Something more like a man hearing a floorboard creak in a house he thought he knew every inch of.

* * *

Here is what I know about Ulyana Eriksen, gathered in six days of watching and one lifetime of listening to river people talk:

I know her father piloted the rescue run the night the Kestrel went down, and turned back. I know the river people still argue about it in bars — that he was a coward, that he was ordered back, that the weather was a wall of water no machine could fly through. I know Ulyana has built her whole life on the far side of that argument, flying medicine and mail on every route except this one, as if the river could be starved of her by avoidance.

I know she carries a compass that belonged to him and cleans it like a rosary.

And I know — because my mother wrote it down, in a hand that got shakier as the pages went on — that the story the river people argue about is wrong in a way nobody has guessed. Not wrong about whether he turned back. He did. Wrong about why.

But that's not tonight's confession. Tonight's confession is mine.

"My mother cooked for thirty-one crew and passengers on a boat the width of a road," I say. "She could feed an army out of a galley the size of your barge head. She knew everybody's secrets, because people tell cooks things they'd never tell captains. And in her last year on the Kestrel, she started writing down what she knew, because she could feel the boat going wrong. Not the hull. The *company*."

"The owners," Ulyana says. It's not a question. She's a pilot; she thinks in ownership structures the way I think in currents.

"The Meridian Line. They were carrying the crew's deferred wages and pension papers downriver — months of it, a fortune to people like us, nothing to them. And my mother figured out they weren't carrying it to deliver it. They were carrying it to *lose* it. They'd insured the cargo. They'd insured it for more than it was worth, and the plan — her words, not mine — was that the river would do the stealing so the company wouldn't have to."

I watch her absorb that. She's good at absorbing; I'll give her that. Most people flinch or argue. Ulyana Eriksen just goes very still, the way the air goes still before a cell wall comes through.

"You're telling me the wreck was arranged."

"I'm telling you the *scuttling* was arranged. The storm was real. The storm was just convenient. My mother said the captain argued with a Meridian man at the last port — she served them dinner and heard every word through the galley hatch. The captain said he'd write it all down. Every payment, every order, every name. He said the crew would be paid if he had to haunt the river to do it." My voice does something I don't authorize. It cracks. "He had four days to get those letters somewhere safe. He had three. The river took the boat on the second night."

Ulyana is quiet for a long time. The tarp drips its slow, dishonest drip.

"If the letters exist," she says at last, "they'd be in the strongbox. The one every salvage man on this river has dreamed about for eleven years. The one Dario Vance has spent a small fortune trying to find." Her eyes come up to mine, and they're not warm, but they're not cold either. They're *measuring*. "The one your passage money bought you a berth on a boat heading toward."

"I never said I was innocent."

"No. You said you were strange. Which I notice is not the same thing."

* * *

She stands. Paces the little mud island of dry ground like a woman circling a decision, and I let her, because I've said my piece and the rest is hers. This is the part I can't control, and I hate it, and I've known since the coffee tin that it would come to this: at some point I'd have to hand the truth to a stranger and trust her to hold it.

"You should know something else," I say to her back. "Since we're doing this."

She stops. Doesn't turn.

"The night the Kestrel went down, my mother made it to the roof deck. She was in the water for six hours before the boat broke up under her. She wrote about it — the last pages of the journal, after. She wrote about seeing a plane." Now my voice is the one that's careful, level, holding itself together with rivets and prayer. "A light in the storm, low over the water, coming toward the wreck. And then turning away."

The rain gets very loud, the way rain does when the world wants to give you an excuse not to say the next thing.

"I've spent eleven years hating that pilot," I say. "And then I signed onto this boat, and I watched you fly that girl with the fever through a cell wall that would've grounded any sane pilot on this river, and I thought — that's not a coward's daughter. That's not even a coward's *river*. So before you decide what I am, you should know I got on this boat half to find those letters, and half to find out what kind of man turns away from a light like mine."

Ulyana turns around.

Her face in the lamplight is doing something complicated, something with a lot of moving parts, and I realize I've just done to her what the river does to a hull — found the seam and put pressure on it. I almost take it back. I don't. My mother waited six hours in the water. I can stand in the rain for one.

"He didn't turn away," Ulyana says.

The words come out flat. Not defensive. Flat, like a fact she's carried so long it's worn smooth.

"He turned back. He turned back *twice*. The second time he got within a mile of the wreck and the wind sheared his wings and he nearly bought it in the water alongside them. He flew that river for a year after, looking for survivors, looking for — anything. And then he stopped flying it, and then he stopped talking about it, and then he died with a compass and a set of charts and a look on his face I was too young to read." She picks up the compass. Holds it in her closed fist like she could crush truth out of it. "So don't stand there and tell me about lights in storms, Sable. I was the one who waited up for him."

The tarp drips. The river breathes. And I understand, all at once, that we have been circling the same wreck from opposite banks for eleven years — she from the air, me from the water — and neither of us ever knew the other was out there.

"He tried," I say. It comes out small. "My mother wrote that the light came back. Twice. She wrote it so I wouldn't grow up hating a dead man." I laugh, and it's not a funny laugh. "I did it anyway. Out of habit."

"Out of grief," Ulyana says. "Habit's just grief with a schedule."

And that's when it happens — the thing I came up this river for and didn't have a name for until this exact moment. Not the letters. Not the strongbox. This: two women on a mudbank in the rain, both orphans of the same night, both carrying the same wreck in different pockets, looking at each other across a crate of terrible coffee and recognizing the cargo.

"Your mother's journal," Ulyana says slowly. "The captain's letters. If they're both in that hold—"

"Then the crew gets their names back. Their pensions. Their *graves*, Ulyana — half of them are buried under 'thief' on paper, and their kids grew up with it the way I grew up with the tin."

"And if Dario Vance finds them first?"

The question hangs there, and I watch her answer it herself before I can. I watch her do the arithmetic she's been refusing to do since the contract: what Dario wants, what Dario pays, what Dario would do with letters that prove his own industry drowned thirty-one people on paper to save a payout.

"He'd burn them," she says.

"He'd burn them," I agree.

She sits back down across from me. Puts the compass on the crate between us, face up, like a card played to the table. For a long moment neither of us says anything, and the rain hammers the tarp, and somewhere downriver the water keeps rising the way it always does, patient as a debt collector.

"First light," Ulyana says finally, "you're going to show me that journal. Every page. And then you and I are going to figure out what my father's charts have to do with your mother's coffee tin, because I am starting to suspect—" she stops. Starts again, quieter. "I am starting to suspect they were working on the same thing."

The thought lands in my chest like a struck match.

Because my mother's journal has a page I never understood. The last page. Four words in a shaking hand, written after the water, after the rescue that half-came: *The pilot knows. God help him.*

I look at the compass on the crate — at the worn brass case Ulyana's father carried into that storm and out of it and to his grave — and I feel the whole river shift under us, current changing, the trip turning into something neither of us signed up for.

"Ulyana," I say. "I think I know what your father left in that wreck. And I don't think it was the money."

Her hand closes over the compass case.

Above us, through the torn corner of the tarp, the clouds are coming down the valley like a second river — the big one, the long monsoon cell, the one Tomas has been listening to on the radio all night with his face getting grayer by the hour.

We have maybe a day before it closes the upper river for good.

And somewhere behind us in the dark, I'd swear I can hear the low throb of an engine that isn't ours.
