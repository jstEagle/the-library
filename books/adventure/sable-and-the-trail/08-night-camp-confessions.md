## 8. Night Camp Confessions

The rain came down like it had a grudge, and we made camp under a shelf of rock that Bea swore was stable and Tomas swore was going to kill us all in our sleep. I trusted Bea's opinion on engines and Tomas's opinion on nothing, so I set my bedroll where the drips wouldn't find me and told myself the night would pass the way most nights did: badly, but quietly.

Sable had other plans.

She'd been watching me since the rapids—those flat grey eyes tracking me across the fire like I was a chart she was trying to memorize—and when Tomas turned in early and Bea started her nightly argument with the outboard carburetor, Sable moved her tin cup closer to mine and said, "You knew my mother."

I didn't answer right away. That was a mistake, because silence is a confession with better manners.

"Marisol Vance," she said. "Cook aboard the *Kestrel's Promise*. Eleven years. She had a scar on her left hand from a canning accident and she used to sing to the river when she thought nobody was listening."

"I don't—" I started, and stopped, because lying to this woman felt like lying to my father's ghost while he watched over my shoulder. "I never met her. But I know the name. Everyone who flew that route knew the crew manifest by heart. It was required reading for anyone stupid enough to take the contract."

"You took it," Sable said.

"My father took it." I fed a stick into the fire and watched the bark curl. "I just inherited the not-flying of it."

Bea looked up from her carburetor, sensing weather of a different kind, and wisely went back to work. Some people have a gift for knowing when a conversation is about to become a wound. Bea is one of them.

Sable turned her cup slowly in her hands. "Tell me about the last flight."

"There's nothing to tell. The official record says he turned back at Marker Nine because of weather."

"The official record also says the *Kestrel* sank because her crew overloaded her," Sable said, "and that they abandoned passengers to save cargo. My mother drowned waiting on a sandbar two miles downstream from the wreck, Ulyana. She wasn't dead yet when your father turned around. The inquiry established that much before it established everything else wrong."

The rain filled the pause for me, which was kind of it.

"I was eight," Sable continued, quieter now, the way people get quiet when they've carried something too long and finally set it down. "I wasn't on the boat. I was staying with my aunt in the delta town. My mother wrote me a letter the week before she died. It's the only letter I kept. She said the owners were moving something heavy in the false hold and paying the crew half what they'd promised, and if the season went well she'd come home and we'd buy a house with a roof that didn't leak." A short, bitter laugh. "She got the roof. Just not the way either of us wanted."

"Sable—"

"I'm not telling you this to hurt you." She looked up, and there was no accusation in her face, which somehow made it worse. Accusation I could have handled. Accusation has edges; you can grip it. "I'm telling you because you've been flying away from this river for fifteen years and calling it a rule instead of a grief, and I've been walking toward it for fifteen years and calling it a search instead of one. We're the same person, Ulyana. We just picked opposite directions."

That landed somewhere I hadn't armored.

Because here's the thing about my rule—the famous, ironclad, never-fly-the-Sable-Route rule that Dario had dangled in front of me like bait on a hook: it was never about the money. Anyone who believed it was about the money had never watched a man pack a compass into a case he never opened again.

My father came back from that last flight as a man returned from a country that no longer existed. He flew fine afterward—technically flawless, the instructors used his incident report as a teaching document—but he never flew *light* again. There was weight in him that no cargo manifest accounted for. He drank his coffee black and cold. He stopped singing. And once, only once, when I was fourteen and pushing every boundary I could find, he grabbed my wrist hard enough to bruise and said, "You don't understand what turning back costs," and then let go like my arm had burned him, and never spoke of it again.

I built a whole life on top of that sentence. I became a pilot who never turned back—which sounds noble until you realize it means I became a pilot who never forgave anyone else for doing it, including him. Especially him.

"He should have gone through," I said to the fire. "The weather was bad but flyable. He'd flown worse. He told the inquiry the cell closed faster than forecast, and maybe that was true, but I've read that report forty times and I can hear the lie in it—not a big lie, just the small comfortable kind a tired man tells so he can sleep. He turned back because he was scared, and eleven people waited on a sandbar for a rescue that was ninety minutes away, and he chose his own skin."

"You don't know that," Sable said.

"I know it the way you know a shape in the dark. Not by looking at it. By living around it long enough that its outline wears into you."

She was silent for a moment. Then: "My mother waited ninety minutes. The inquiry timeline says the water rose four inches in that time. Four inches, Ulyana. She was standing on a sandbar with nine other people and the river took its time about it, and your father was already home eating dinner when it happened."

I had done that arithmetic myself, more nights than I'd admit. Ninety minutes. Four inches. A dinner getting cold. There are numbers that live in a person like parasites, and those were mine.

"So yes," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "You want to know why I run up debts I can't pay and take medical flights nobody else wants and keep a crew of two misfits paid above market rate? It's not generosity. It's penance. I've been trying to crash-proof my conscience for fifteen years and it doesn't work, because guilt isn't a debt, Sable. Debts get paid. Guilt just accrues interest."

Bea's hands had gone still on the carburetor. She didn't look up, but she wasn't working anymore, and we both knew it.

Sable reached into her jacket and pulled out an oilcloth packet, unwrapped it with the care of someone handling a relic, and set a photograph on the rock between us. A woman with Sable's grey eyes and a cook's forearms, squinting into sun on the deck of a riverboat, laughing at someone off-frame.

"She laughed like that the day she left," Sable said. "I remember because I was annoyed at her for something stupid—I don't even remember what—and I wouldn't smile back." She wrapped the photo up again. "So when I say I understand unfinished sentences, believe me."

The rain hammered on. Somewhere upslope, water found a new channel and began its patient argument with the ground.

"Why come to me?" I asked. "Why not go to the wreck alone? You clearly know the river. You clearly know more than you've said."

"Because the hold needs divers, fuel, and a boat, and I have none of those things." She smiled, and it was almost warm. "And because I think you need to see it more than I do. You've spent fifteen years treating that wreck like a grave you desecrated by existing near it. Graves deserve visitors, Ulyana. Even guilty ones. *Especially* guilty ones."

"That's a terrible sales pitch."

"It's the only honest one I've got."

We sat with that. The fire popped. Bea, deciding the moment had passed some threshold of survivability, muttered something about gaskets and retreated to the boat.

"Sable," I said finally. "If we find what you're looking for—if the letters exist, if the crew really were cheated—what happens to my father in that story?"

She considered it honestly, which I appreciated more than kindness. "I don't know," she said. "Maybe he was a coward. Maybe he was lied to about the weather. Maybe he turned back because someone paid him to, or because he saw something in the water that scared him worse than the storm. I've imagined every version. In some of them I hate him. In one of them—" she hesitated— "in one of them he tried to come back later, alone, and that's why he never talked about it. That's the version I like best. It's probably not true. But it keeps me company."

I thought of the compass case in my bag. Sealed since the funeral. I had promised myself, somewhere over the years, that I'd open it when I was ready, and I had defined *ready* so carefully that it had never arrived.

"In the version I like best," I heard myself say, "he wasn't running from the river. He was running toward something in it, and he ran out of runway."

Sable looked at me for a long moment. "Then maybe we're both about to find out which version is true."

She offered her hand across the fire. Grey eyes, cook's daughter, fifteen years of grief folded into a stranger who'd talked her way onto my boat with a story I now suspected was only half invented.

I shook it. Her grip was strong and her palm was calloused in the places a river leaves its signature.

"Truce?" she said.

"Alliance," I corrected. "Truces expire. Alliances have terms."

"Fine. Terms." She leaned back against the rock. "Nobody lies about the past anymore. Starting tonight."

"Starting tonight," I agreed, and the strange thing was how light I felt having confessed the worst of it—as though the guilt had been ballast all along, and saying it aloud was the first bale I'd ever thrown overboard.

I slept better than I had any right to.

Which is probably why I didn't hear Bea shaking Tomas awake at dawn, or see the expression on her face as she pointed upstream—at the flat grey line of a second boat, riding low in the water, holding position two bends back like it had been there all night.

* * *

Morning found us already moving. Nobody said the word *shadow*, but everybody thought it.
