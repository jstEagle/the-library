## 9. The Hull Gives Way

I've been turning wrenches since I was nine years old, and in all that time I've learned exactly one universal truth: engines don't lie. People lie. Contracts lie. Weather reports lie with a straight face and a government seal. But an engine tells you what it is, every time, if you're willing to listen.

The barge's engine had been telling me for two days that it was tired. Not broken—tired, the way a mule gets tired, still pulling but letting you know about it. I'd been listening back, hand flat on the housing during my checks, feeling for the shudder that meant something worse than fatigue. So far it was just fatigue. I could live with fatigue. Fatigue and I were old friends.

What I couldn't live with was the water level in the bilge, which is why I was down in the engine bay at dawn on the fourth day out, flashlight clamped in my teeth, watching a puddle that had no business existing do its slow, patient work of becoming a lake.

"Uly," I called up through the deck plates. "You want to come see something or do you want to keep pretending we're fine?"

Her boots appeared at the top of the ladder. She climbed down with the particular economy of motion she used when she already suspected the answer to a question and didn't want to waste energy being surprised by it. She crouched beside me, looked at the puddle, and said nothing for a long moment.

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that I noticed yesterday and told myself it was condensation. Slow enough that I'm not lying to you when I say we have days."

"You told yourself it was condensation."

"I tell myself a lot of things. It's how I stay cheerful."

She put her palm flat against the hull plating, the way I'd taught Tomas to check for vibration, and held it there. Outside, the river ran high and brown and fast, swollen three meters above its dry-season bed, carrying whole trees past us like it was clearing out a junk drawer. The monsoon cell was still north of us—Tomas had gotten the forecast off the radio before the static got bad—but "north" on this river meant "eventually here."

"We beach it tonight," Ulyana said. "Find a side-channel, run it aground somewhere shallow, and I'll strip the plating while we've got light."

"I can patch from inside. Slower, but we don't lose the current."

"No." She said it without heat, which from Ulyana meant she'd already argued with herself and lost. "If the hull lets go mid-channel in flood water, we don't get to choose where we stop. We choose now, while we still can."

That was the thing about Ulyana Eriksen that people who only knew her reputation never understood. They thought she was cautious because she was afraid. She wasn't afraid of anything except the past, and she didn't fly away from the past so much as she flew *around* it, the way you route around a storm cell—wasting fuel, wasting hours, but alive. Her caution wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. She just did the math faster than most people and accepted the answers without complaint.

"All right," I said. "But if we ground her hard, I want to be the one driving. You fly, you don't drive. No offense."

"Some taken."

"None meant."

* * *

We found the side-channel an hour before dark, which sounds like good luck until you understand that finding a side-channel on a flooded river is like finding a knife in a drawer full of knives—you're looking for one specific blade and everything feels similar until it's too late. Tomas spotted it first from the bow, a seam of slower water threading off the main channel behind a drowned stand of bamboo. Sable stood next to him, and I watched her watch the water, and I watched her say something quiet to Ulyana that made Ulyana's jaw tighten and then loosen in a way I'd learned to read as agreement.

Sable had been aboard four days and had already done more than earn her passage. She'd spliced a fuel line on day two that I would have sworn couldn't be spliced. She knew the river—not chart-knowledge, not pilot-knowledge, but the kind of knowledge you get from growing up on a boat, where the water is a language you dream in. When she said the channel behind the bamboo would hold depth, I believed her, mostly because she said it the way I talk about engines: no performance, no sales pitch, just reporting what was true.

I took the wheel. Ulyana stood at my shoulder doing what she does, which is reading water the way other people read weather—the boils and seams and pressure lines of a river in flood, all of it translated into a voice in your ear saying *left, ease, hold, now*. We came off the main channel slow, into the slack water behind the bamboo, and I brought us in bow-first toward the mud shelf on the channel's far side with maybe half the grace I wanted and all of the care I had.

The keel touched. It was gentle, as groundings go—a kiss, not a collision. I killed the engine and let the silence ring, and for about ninety seconds I thought we'd gotten away with something.

Then the river reminded me who was in charge.

It came out of the main channel without any warning at all—one of those flood-borne logs, black and huge, riding low, the kind of debris that doesn't show on any sonar worth the name. I heard it more than saw it: a deep wooden groan against our stern quarter, transmitted through the hull into the deck plates and up through my boots into my teeth. Not a hit. A *shove*. The log caught us amidships as it swung in the eddy line, and the whole barge rolled—maybe ten degrees, maybe fifteen—and then the log was gone downstream, unhurried, anonymous, having ruined our evening without ever introducing itself.

"Tomas!" Ulyana was already moving. "Sound the bilge!"

Tomas dropped the hatch and I heard him drop the sounding rod, and I heard the rod hit water, and I heard the sound his breath made after that, which is a sound I have heard maybe four times in my life and never once enjoyed.

"Bea. How bad?" Ulyana again, calm, terrible, wonderful.

"Get me down there."

The engine bay lights showed me what the dawn puddle had become. The log hadn't opened us wide—it had flexed the hull, and the flexing had found the seam I'd been babying for two days and torn it along its whole length, a split maybe a meter long, weeping brown water in a sheet rather than a trickle. The bilge pump was running—I could hear it laboring—and losing.

"Ulyana!" I shouted up. "We're taking water faster than the pump! I need the second pump rigged and I need it now!"

Boots on the ladder. Sable came down first, which surprised me, followed by Tomas hauling the backup pump, followed last by Ulyana, who took one look at the split and did the thing she always does in a crisis, which is get quieter.

"Options," she said.

"Pump and pray, we might keep ahead of it till morning. Or we drive her onto the mud shelf properly, hard aground, and I patch her dry."

"If we drive her on, can we get her off again?"

"With the water dropping? Eventually. With the water rising?" I shook my head. "Once she settles into that mud, she's there till the dry season."

Ulyana looked at the water coming through the split. Then she looked at Sable, who was standing hip-deep already in the flooding bilge, holding the backup pump's intake clear of debris, and who said, without looking up: "The shelf holds her. I've seen boats winter on that mud. And the channel behind us will silt in within the week—we'd be trapped anyway if we stayed afloat."

"So we're choosing between stuck-now and stuck-later," Tomas said.

"I prefer stuck-now," Sable said. "Stuck-later comes with sinking."

There it was. The whole argument in six words. Ulyana closed her eyes for one second—her whole internal committee convening and voting, I've seen her do it in less—and then opened them.

"Bea. Put her on the shelf."

"Aye, Captain."

"I'm not the captain."

"You are tonight."

* * *

Driving a leaking barge onto a mud shelf in rising water, at dusk, in monsoon country, is not a maneuver they teach anywhere, and if they did teach it, the first lesson would be "don't." But the physics are simple enough: you need way on to make the mud, you need the mud to take the way off gently, and you need the engine to survive both. What you don't control is the river, and the river had spent the whole day establishing its position on the matter.

I gave her everything the old girl had left—which wasn't much, the engine complaining the entire time like a relative asked to help move furniture—and swung us off the shelf's edge and drove her nose-first into the mud with about as much tenderness as a bar fight. The impact threw Tomas against the rail. The hull groaned from stem to stern, a long descending note like a whale giving up on something, and then we stopped, and the river kept going, sliding past us on both sides, indifferent.

Hard aground. Bow buried to the second strake. Dead in the water, except the water was dead in us.

I went straight back to the engine bay, because that's where I go when the world ends; it's the one room where endings are fixable. The split had slowed once we grounded—the hull wasn't flexing anymore, which meant the tear wasn't working wider—but the bilge was knee-deep and climbing until the pumps caught up and turned the tide. By full dark we had it stable: two pumps holding against the seep, the split exposed above the waterline where the bow-up attitude actually helped us, and me lying on my back under the framing with a lamp, a hammer, and a length of oakum, doing the oldest repair in the history of boats.

"Talk to me," Ulyana said from the ladder. She'd been shuttling supplies all evening, tireless, and she had that look she gets when she's counting things in her head and doesn't like the total.

"It'll hold. It won't be pretty and it won't be permanent, but it'll hold."

"And the fuel?"

Ah. The fuel. Here's the part I'd been putting off telling her, because telling her made it real. When we grounded, the impact had stressed the fuel lines, and one of the fittings on the port tank had let go—not a gush, but a steady seep into the bilge, where it had been happily mixing with the river water for the better part of an hour before I found it. Diesel floats. That's the joke of it. All that fuel, floating on top of the floodwater in our own belly, useless, unrecoverable, slowly skimming itself into the pump intakes.

"Half," I said. "Maybe a bit less. The port tank's contaminated and the starboard took a knock. I can save the starboard, clean. The rest is gone."

She absorbed that. I watched her absorb it, watched the number land somewhere behind her eyes and get filed with all the other numbers she carries—the debt, the lease, the distance home, the distance to the wreck site, the distance Dario Vance's money buys versus the distance her father's memory forbids.

"Half is enough," she said finally. "If we're careful."

"Careful is my middle name."

"Your middle name is Gertrude."

"Which is why I go by Bea."

She almost smiled. Almost. On Ulyana that's a parade.

Then Tomas's voice came down the hatch, and it had a wrongness in it that stopped both of us cold. "Bea? Ulyana? You should come see this. The antenna."

The radio antenna. Our mast antenna had taken the grounding badly—it had been marginal since day one, a splice I'd made in a hurry at the last fuel stop—and sometime in the chaos it had parted. Tomas had the broken end in his hands when we came up on deck, and the break was clean, the cable snapped back inside its jacket, the connector sheared off at the base. Beyond repair with what we had aboard. Beyond repair, period, until we reached a town with a chandlery, and the nearest town with a chandlery was two hundred kilometers behind us and on the wrong side of a closing monsoon.

"So we're deaf," Tomas said. He said it simply, the way he says most hard things, which is one of the reasons I love the big fool.

"We're deaf," Ulyana confirmed.

"And blind," Sable added quietly, from the bow. She was looking upstream, at the wall of cloud sitting over the upper river, lit from inside by lightning with no sound reaching us yet. "That front will be here tomorrow night. Maybe the night after."

Nobody said the obvious thing, which was that Dario's messages had been getting shorter and colder, and that the last one had used the word "abandon" in a sentence that also contained the word "salvage," and that without the radio we wouldn't hear whatever he sent next, and that some part of Ulyana—only some part, a small part, a part she'd deny—had seemed almost relieved when the antenna broke.

I went back below. There's always work. That's the mercy of being the mechanic: grief and fear are luxuries for people whose hands are idle. I cleaned the port tank's fitting, flushed what I could, re-torqued everything that could be torqued, and lay back down under the framing to finish the oakum, driving it into the split strand by strand in the lamplight while the river breathed against the hull a meter from my ear.

Around midnight, Sable came down without being called. She didn't ask what I was doing. She just knelt at the edge of the lamplight, picked up the mallet I'd set aside, and started tapping the oakum home on the far end of the split, working in mirror to me, matching my rhythm without ever discussing it. She'd done this before. Not this exact repair—this exact repair nobody has done, because this exact repair is mine—but this *kind* of repair, the patient kind, the kind where the boat talks to you and you answer.

"My mother caulked like that," she said, after a long while. "She said you don't fight the seam. You convince it."

"She sounds like she was smarter than me."

"She was smarter than everyone. That was the problem." Tap. Tap. "Bea. When we reach the Kestrel—if we reach her—there are letters in the strongbox. Letters that prove what was done to the crew. To my mother. I need you to know that's why I'm here. Not the cargo. The truth."

I sat back on my heels and looked at her across the lamplit bilge, this stranger who'd patched our fuel line and matched my rhythm and named her dead in the first hour of knowing me, and I thought about Ulyana up on deck keeping her lonely watch, carrying her father's compass like a stone in her pocket, and I thought: well. Here it is. Whatever this crew was before tonight, it's something else now.

"The truth'll do," I said. "Truth floats. I've seen it."

Tap. Tap. Went the mallet, convincing the seam.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, the front closer, the air thick enough to chew. I did my rounds—engine, pumps, patch, tanks—and found the numbers survivable. Patch holding. Starboard tank clean and intact. Bilge manageable. Half our fuel gone, our ears gone, our options narrowed to a single thread, but the barge was ours and she was going to float, barely, stubbornly, the way the best ones do.

Ulyana was at the bow with her father's compass open in her palm, staring upstream at the storm-light, and Sable stood beside her, and neither of them spoke, and I decided some conversations weren't mine to overhear.

Instead I found Tomas coiling rope on the aft deck, and I sat down next to him, and we watched the river carry the world past us—trees, drums, a whole uprooted island of reeds sailing by like a slow green ship.

"Half the fuel," he said.

"Half the fuel."

"No radio."

"No radio."

"Dario's going to think we died."

"Dario's going to think whatever's cheapest for Dario to think." I bumped his shoulder with mine. "We didn't die. We're stuck on a mud shelf with a patched hull, half a tank, and a passenger who knows this river better than the river does. Honestly? I've launched worse expeditions with better equipment."

He laughed—that big easy laugh of his, the one that makes the whole boat feel bigger—and for a moment the fear loosened its grip.

Then Sable's voice came from the bow, sharp and certain, cutting through the gray morning like a blade through wet canvas:

"Ulyana. You need to see the ridge line. Now."

And the tone of her voice—the tone, not the words—was what sent us both to our feet, because it wasn't fear and it wasn't wonder.

It was recognition.
