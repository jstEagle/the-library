## 10. Stranded Together

I want it on the record that I never once said we were going to die.

Bea said it, twice, in the technical sense — "we're going to die if this patch doesn't hold" — and Ulyana said it in the philosophical sense, staring at the flooded side-channel like it had personally insulted her, muttering something about rivers and fathers and the general unfairness of the universe. But me, I kept my mouth shut and my hands busy, which is what deckhands are for. You don't hire a man for his optimism. You hire him for his forearms and his ability to hold a plank against a hull while water tries to take it away from him.

The barge sat wedged in the side-channel where Bea had beached her after the collision, canted maybe ten degrees to port, half her fuel gone downriver with the antenna and most of my dignity gone with them. The monsoon cell hung over us like a landlord who'd decided to move in permanently. Rain came in sheets, then in curtains, then in that fine malicious drizzle that gets inside everything you own and stays there until you die or move somewhere sensible.

We'd been stranded two days. It felt like eleven.

* * *

The morning of the third day started with Bea throwing a wrench at the engine housing, which was her way of saying good morning.

"It's not seating," she announced, to no one, to everyone, to God. "The plate's warped. I can't get a seal on a warped plate. I need a machine shop, a dry dock, and about six hours without rain, and I have been offered none of these things."

"Options?" Ulyana asked. She was standing under the tarp we'd rigged over the aft deck, arms crossed, hair tied back in that severe way she did when she was thinking instead of feeling. She'd been doing a lot of severe hair lately.

"Option one, we wait for the water to drop and haul her out on the bank and fix her properly. Option two, we keep patching and pray." Bea wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime yesterday. "Option three is not an option because it involves swimming."

"Then we wait," Ulyana said.

"We can't wait forever," Sable said from the bow, where she'd spent most of the morning doing something quiet and competent with rope. She had a way of appearing at the edge of conversations like she'd been there all along, which, knowing her, she probably had. "The river drops fast after a cell passes. If we're not moving when it does, we're aground until next season."

"Thank you for the weather report," Bea said, with the particular sweetness she reserved for people who were right.

Sable didn't rise to it. She never rose to anything. She just coiled her rope and looked at Bea with those flat gray eyes, and Bea went back to swearing at the engine, and I went back to holding things, which was my whole job description these days.

Here's what nobody had said out loud yet: we were stuck between two clocks. The first clock was the river, which would drop and strand us. The second clock was Dario Vance, whose satellite messages had been arriving with the regularity and warmth of collection notices. And the third clock — the one nobody talked about — was whatever Sable knew about the Kestrel's Promise that she still hadn't told us, ticking away behind her face like a second heart.

* * *

The message came through at midday, on the shortwave, crackling through static like a ghost with a business degree.

Ulyana read it twice. Then she read it aloud, because apparently she'd decided we should all suffer equally.

*"Progress update required. If salvage cannot commence within seventy-two hours, abandon operation and return. Advance will be considered forfeit. Further costs will be recovered per contract terms, Section Nine."*

"Section Nine," Bea repeated. "He put a clawback clause in. He put a *clawback clause* in a contract he gave to a woman he's known since she was stealing his lunch money."

"He didn't give it to me," Ulyana said quietly. "He gave it to the strip. There's a difference." She folded the paper along its existing creases, which were getting soft from handling. "Seventy-two hours. That's generous of him. That's practically a gift."

"That's a trap," Bea said. "That's a man telling us exactly how long we have before he stops pretending this is a partnership."

I'd been listening from the rail, and I'll admit my contribution to the strategic discussion was mostly silence, but even I could do that math. Seventy-two hours meant Dario expected us to fail. Or worse — Dario needed us to fail slowly enough that he could arrive at the wreck himself, with our charts, our route, and our names attached to whatever went wrong.

"Tomas," Ulyana said. "You've got the best eyes. Get up on the ridge tonight if the rain breaks. Look downstream."

"For what?"

"You'll know it when you see it."

That was the thing about Ulyana Eriksen. She never told you what she suspected. She just handed you a pair of binoculars and let your stomach figure it out on its own.

* * *

The rain broke around dusk, the way it does — all at once, like someone pulling a plug — and the world came out steaming and dripping and briefly, treacherously beautiful. I climbed the ridge behind camp while the light lasted, and I looked downstream, and I saw it.

A boat. Small, running dark, tucked into the tree line maybe four kilometers back the way we'd come. Not moving. Just sitting there, patient as a spider.

I watched it until the dark ate it, and then I climbed down and told Ulyana, and she nodded like I'd confirmed a bill she'd already received.

"Dario's second boat," she said. "Or his first boat, and everything since has been theater." She turned the compass over in her hands — her father's compass, the brass one she carried everywhere and never opened, thumb worrying the lid like a worry stone. "He's not waiting for our seventy-two hours. He's waiting for us to find the wreck *for* him."

"So what do we do?"

"What we always do." She almost smiled. It didn't quite land, but it tried. "We finish the job better than the man paying for it expects."

* * *

Here is what I know about Sable, three weeks into knowing her: she eats less than anyone I've ever shared a boat with, she sleeps in shifts so short they barely qualify as sleeping, and she can tie a bowline faster than Bea can untie one, which Bea takes as a personal affront.

Here is what I learned that night: she can also patch a hull.

It happened around two in the morning. I was on watch, sitting under the tarp with a thermos of coffee that had achieved a flavor profile best described as *regret*, when I heard sounds from below decks. Not alarming sounds. Working sounds. The specific rhythm of somebody doing a job they'd done a thousand times.

I found her in the flooded bilge up to her thighs in cold river water, headlamp strapped on, working a sheet of canvas and sealant compound against the split seam with a patience I have only otherwise seen in monks and cats.

"You're supposed to be asleep," I said.

"So are you."

"I'm on watch."

"Then watch," she said, and kept working.

So I watched. And I'm telling you, I've seen Bea work seams, and Bea is the finest mechanic on this stretch of river and would fight anyone who said otherwise, including Bea. What Sable did was different. She wasn't fighting the leak. She was *negotiating* with it. She'd read the flex of the hull the way Ulyana reads weather — not where the water was coming in, but where it *wanted* to go — and she set her patch accordingly, braced it against the frame, weighted it with sandbags we'd brought for ballast, and worked the sealant into the seam in layers, letting each one cure before the next.

"How do you know how to do that?" I finally asked.

She didn't look up. "Riverboats leak. I grew up on one."

"The Kestrel."

A pause, long enough to count as an answer. "Every kid aboard learned hull work by age eight. My mother said a boat is just a promise you make to the water every single day. Miss a day, and the water collects."

Her mother. The cook. The woman who died waiting on a rescue that never came. I didn't say any of that. Some doors you don't knock on at two in the morning in a flooded bilge.

Instead I said, "Does Bea know you can do this?"

"She knows I coil rope well. That's been enough for her."

"She's going to be insufferable about being wrong."

"No," Sable said, and there was something almost warm in it, buried deep, like a coin at the bottom of a well. "She's going to pretend she taught me. Let her. It costs nothing."

By dawn the seep had slowed to a trickle, and by the time Bea came down with her coffee and her opinions, the bilge pump was cycling once an hour instead of once every twenty minutes. Bea stood at the top of the ladder and stared down at the patch for a long time.

"Who did this?"

"Sable did," I said.

Another long stare. Then Bea climbed down, ran her thumb along the seam edge, checked the bracing, tested the weight distribution, and delivered her verdict with the gravity of a judge sentencing a friend:

"Huh."

From Bea, that was a parade with confetti.

* * *

Tempers flared that afternoon, as tempers do when you mix exhaustion, rain, and a deadline. It started small — Bea and Ulyana arguing about whether to attempt the run on the patched hull if the river held high another day — and escalated the way arguments do when everyone's real fear is standing in the room wearing a gray coat and saying nothing.

"Seventy-two hours," Bea snapped. "And now we know there's a boat behind us. So we're racing a clock and a knife, and you want to debate *sealant curing times*—"

"I want us alive," Ulyana shot back. "My father raced this river to beat a schedule. You know how that ended."

The words landed harder than she meant them to. I saw it hit her even as she said it — saw her flinch at her own voice. The camp went quiet except for the rain.

Sable stood up from where she'd been mending netting. She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; raising a voice, I think, seemed to her like a luxury from a previous life.

"Your father didn't turn back because of a schedule," she said. "And he didn't turn back because he was a coward. When he tells you why — and he will, because he wrote it down, men like him always write it down — you're going to wish you'd never doubted him. All of you." Her eyes moved across us, Bea, me, settling last and longest on Ulyana. "Until then, argue about sealant. It's honest, at least."

Then she picked up her netting and sat back down, and the argument died of embarrassment.

Ulyana stared at her for a long moment. "You keep saying things like that. Like you're carrying a letter you haven't opened."

"I am," Sable said simply. "I'm just deciding who deserves to read it first."

* * *

That night I took second watch again, and around midnight Sable appeared beside me with two cups of tea she'd made from some river herb that tasted like a rumor of mint. We sat under the tarp and listened to the water move in the dark.

"She's coming around," I said. "Ulyana. She doesn't trust easy, but when she trusts, it's load-bearing."

"I know." Sable blew on her tea. "I've been watching her for years, Tomas. Before I ever met her."

I turned to look at her. "That's either romantic or terrifying, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"My mother waited on that bank for three days," Sable said, to the river more than to me. "The Kestrel's crew. They all waited. One boat passed close enough to see their signals, and it turned away, and I've spent my whole life being angry about it. And then I found out whose boat it was." She sipped her tea. "And then I found out *why* it turned. Those are two different discoveries, and they don't fit in the same person. I'm still working on it."

"And which discovery brought you onto our barge?"

She was quiet for a while. The rain ticked against the tarp. Somewhere out in the dark, the river moved with the slow muscular patience of a thing that always wins eventually.

"When I boarded," she said finally, "I came for answers. Journal pages, testimony, proof — paper things. Things I could use." She looked over at me, and in the dim lantern light her face did something complicated. "Somewhere between the first rapids and the bilge, it stopped being about the papers. Now it's about the people holding the other end of the rope. Your mechanic pretends she hates me but re-rigged my hammock so it wouldn't sag. Your captain carries a dead man's compass like it owes her an apology. And you—" she gestured at me with her cup, "—you hold things. That's not nothing, Tomas. Holding things is the whole job."

"That's what I keep telling people."

"Tomorrow," she said, "when the water's right, we should talk about moving. And Ulyana should open her father's compass case. Because whatever's in there, my mother's journal says he drew it himself — and I think it's time both our families stopped keeping secrets in boxes."

She finished her tea, wished me a watch that was uneventful, and disappeared into the dark of the boat, quiet as she'd arrived.

I sat there a long time, turning it over. *Compass case.* I thought about how many times I'd seen Ulyana's thumb on that brass lid, worrying it like a scar. I thought about the boat sitting dark in the trees downstream, patient as a spider. I thought about seventy-two hours, of which maybe forty remained.

Then I woke Ulyana, because some watches are too heavy to stand alone, and told her what Sable had said.

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket and took out the compass, and held it in both hands, and looked at it the way you look at a door you've been afraid of your whole life.

"First light," she said.
