## 11. Dario's Second Boat

The ridge found me before I was ready for what it would show me.

I'd climbed it for the practical reason, which is the only kind of reason I trust anymore: the radio antenna was gone with half our fuel in the flooded channel below, and if I wanted line of sight to the southern horizon—where weather either forgives you or doesn't—I needed elevation. Bea had patched the hull with resin and a prayer and a piece of Tomas's spare shirt, and the barge sat low in the side-channel like a dog that knows it's been bad. Somebody had to look at the sky. The sky owed us nothing, but I've learned you check anyway.

So I climbed. Forty minutes of mud that tried to keep my boots as souvenirs, through trees hung so heavy with wet that they dripped even between rains, up a spine of granite slick as a liar's promise. And when I came out of the treeline onto the bare crown of it, the clouds had torn open just enough to give me the river—a long brown ribbon stitched through green, running high and fast with the monsoon's appetite—and everything on it.

Including them.

* * *

I want to say I knew immediately. I want to tell you my stomach dropped and every hair on my arms stood up and some ancestral instinct whispered *Dario Vance* into my ear like a warning from beyond. That would be tidy. That would be dramatic.

What actually happened is that I stood there for a full minute thinking, *huh, another boat*, the way you might notice another car on a road you thought was empty. It was maybe two kilometers downstream of our position, holding station against the current near the eastern bank where the water ran slower, tucked under the overhang of the forest like something hiding. A river cruiser, steel-hulled, cabin amidships, an awning aft. Not a local craft. Local craft don't have radar masts. Local craft don't idle in floodwater burning diesel they can't easily replace.

Then it moved—not toward us, not away, just sideways, adjusting its position the way a man adjusts his chair when he's trying not to look like he's watching someone. And I felt the cold arrive, not from the wind but from somewhere behind my ribs, spreading outward with the patience of spilled water.

Because I knew that boat.

Not this exact boat. But that *kind*. Dario Vance had three of them over the years—fast, gray, deliberately anonymous, the kind of vessel that shows up in satellite imagery as a smudge and in port records as somebody else's name. I'd watched one of them leave his marina in Kaltero the summer I was nineteen, back when Dario and I were still friendly enough to wave at each other across the water, back before I understood that everything he built was designed to be deniable. He used to joke about it. "Plausible deniability, Ulyana," he'd said, tapping the hull of the first one, "is just honesty with better planning."

I'd laughed then. Nineteen-year-olds laugh at anything that sounds clever.

I wasn't laughing now. Now I was counting.

One boat shadowing us at two kilometers, holding position, moving only when we moved. Which meant it had been there long enough to learn our rhythm. Which meant it had been there since—at minimum—yesterday, while we were beached and bleeding fuel into a flooded channel, while Bea was elbow-deep in the bilge, while Sable was patching seams with a competence none of us had any right to expect from a paying passenger. While we were blind, deaf, antenna-less, and grateful for the help.

It had been watching us the whole time. And we hadn't seen it because we were too busy surviving.

* * *

Here's the thing about betrayal: it almost never arrives as a surprise. It arrives as a confirmation. Some part of you—the part you've been feeding excuses to, the part that keeps saying *he wouldn't, not after everything, not after your father*—that part goes quiet all at once, and what's left is just arithmetic.

Let me do the arithmetic for you, because I did it standing on that ridge with rain starting again, fat drops ticking against the granite around me like a slow applause.

Item one: Dario Vance hires a crew he has spent fifteen years refusing to hire, for a job he could have given to any of a dozen salvage outfits closer to the coast. Why me? Because I'm good? Flattering, but no. Because my father flew the Sable River route. Because my father died on it. Because somewhere in my possession—somewhere I hadn't even looked, not really, not in fifteen years—there were charts. His charts. The ones I kept in the compass case I never opened because opening it meant admitting he was dead, and I have never once in my life admitted anything I could avoid admitting.

Item two: the contract itself. I'd read it four times on the flight up, looking for the trap, and found nothing, and told myself that meant there was no trap. What it actually meant was that the trap wasn't *in* the contract. The contract paid for salvage of cargo from the wrecked riverboat *Kestrel's Promise*, coordinates approximate, conditions hazardous, payment due on delivery of manifest goods. Standard. Generous, even. Too generous—I'd noted that too, and filed it under *Dario being Dario* instead of under *Dario needing me specifically*.

But here's what the contract didn't specify: it didn't say how we'd find the wreck. It gave coordinates so vague they were nearly decorative—"upper Sable reach, vicinity of the old channel markers"—and it assumed, without ever stating, that we'd manage. That *I'd* manage. That Eriksen blood would do what Eriksen blood does.

He wasn't hiring a salvage crew. He was hiring a compass needle. He was hiring the daughter because the daughter was the map.

Item three: the satellite messages. They'd started polite and turned demanding with a speed that should have embarrassed him. *Status. Progress. Estimated arrival.* Then, after the hull went: *If delayed past the window, abandon salvage and return. Payment forfeit.* I'd read that message aloud to the crew in the rain and Bea had said, "Forfeit? After what we've spent getting here?" and Tomas had said, "He's testing whether we'll quit," and I'd said, "He's impatient," and all three of us had been wrong in the same direction. He didn't care whether we quit. Quitting was fine. Quitting was *acceptable*. Because if we quit, he still had the second boat, and the second boat had been following our smoke, our noise, our wake, our choices, learning everything we learned, needing us for nothing except to do the dangerous part first.

We weren't employees. We were mine canaries. We were the people sent ahead to find out which parts of the river kill you.

And item four—the item that made me sit down on the wet granite because my legs had opinions about holding me—was my father. My father's charts. If Dario wanted those coordinates, and Dario knew about my father's final flight—which he'd proven, that day in my office, by sliding that photograph across my desk, my father's plane on the strip at Kaltero, loaded, fueled, photographed hours before he went into the river—then Dario had known for fifteen years roughly where the *Kestrel's Promise* lay. Roughly. Within a few kilometers. Within enough water that searching blind would take a season and cost more than the salvage was worth.

Unless someone narrowed it for him. Unless someone with the charts and the blood and the stubbornness made the search cheap.

That's what the contract was. Not a job. A sonar ping. He threw me at the river the way you throw a stone down a well to hear how deep it goes, and every mile we'd traveled, every rapid we'd run, every camp we'd struck—he'd been reading the echo.

* * *

The rain picked up. I stayed on the ridge anyway, because I'm a fool, or thorough, and those are frequently the same thing, and I watched the gray boat for the better part of an hour while the light went from silver to pewter to nothing much at all.

They were good. I'll grant them that. Whoever was handling it held station in floodwater with a delicacy I respected professionally and hated personally. Twice, when our barge shifted in the side-channel and sent up a smear of woodsmoke, the boat adjusted—never approaching, never retreating, just *rebalancing*, the way a card player keeps their stack neat without looking at it. There was a figure at the rail once, briefly, in a yellow rain shell. Too far to see a face. I found myself hoping it was Dario himself, because I wanted someone specific to hate, and hating an anonymous shape in the rain felt like punching fog.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years I'd carried the story the way I carried the compass case: sealed, unexamined, heavier than it looked. The story was this: my father took a job he shouldn't have, on a route he'd sworn off, for money he needed, and the river killed him, and that was the whole of it—tragedy, no villain, just water. I'd built my entire life on that foundation. Never fly the Sable route. Never take salvage work. Never owe anyone. Three rules, load-bearing, all of them mortared with the assumption that the disaster was random. That nobody had *done* anything.

But Dario had known about the final flight. Had a photograph of it. Had waited fifteen years and then hired me with a precision that now looked less like sentiment and more like a man cashing an old investment. You don't sit on information that long unless it's appreciating. You don't photograph a man's last departure unless you already know why it matters.

Which raised a question I didn't want to ask on that ridge, alone, in the rain, with the cold spread all the way through me now: what else had Dario known? What had my father been carrying? What was on that plane besides charts?

And underneath that question, quieter, worse: if Dario had spent fifteen years circling this secret, had my father known he was circling? Had he flown that last flight *because* of Dario—or away from him?

I pressed my palm flat against the granite. Cold stone, cold rain, cold arithmetic. Somewhere below me, my crew was patching a boat with shirt fabric and hope, and one of them was a stranger who asked pointed questions about the *Kestrel* and studied wreck coordinates when she thought no one was watching, and I realized I couldn't even be sure anymore who was pinging whom.

Everyone on this river was using everyone else as an instrument. The only difference was that most of us hadn't read the manual.

* * *

I climbed down slower than I climbed up, which is the correct way to descend both ridges and realizations.

By the time I reached the side-channel the light was nearly gone, and the barge was a warm smudge of lamplight and woodsmoke in the dripping dark. Bea was on deck, greased to the elbows, arguing with a fitting. Tomas had a pot going—rice and the last of the dried fish, smelling like civilization's distant cousin. And Sable sat apart, as she always did, at the edge of the lamplight, mending something, her hands busy and her eyes elsewhere.

Four of us. One leaking boat. One gray shadow two kilometers downstream that none of them knew about yet.

Bea looked up first. "Well? Sky?"

"Sky's got another front in it. Twelve hours, maybe less." I set down the water bottle I'd carried up and hadn't drunk from. My voice came out level, which surprised me; apparently the outside of me hadn't gotten the memo about the inside. "Also, we're not alone on this river."

Tomas's spoon stopped mid-stir. Bea straightened slowly, wiping her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands, which took effort.

"Talk," Bea said.

"River cruiser. Steel hull, radar mast, holding station downstream since at least yesterday. Gray. Moving only when we move." I let that land. "It's one of Dario's."

The silence had texture. Rain on canvas. River against hull. Bea's rag stopping mid-wipe.

"You're sure," Tomas said, not making it a question so much as giving me room to make it one.

"I know his boats. I know his habits. And I know that everything about this trip makes sense now, which is how you know you've been played—when the pieces finally fit and the picture isn't the one you were promised." I looked at each of them in turn, because they deserved my face while I said the rest, not the fire. "He didn't hire us to salvage the *Kestrel*. He hired us to find it. My father's charts, my father's route, my father's daughter—cheap sonar. We run the risks, we mark the way, and when we get close enough, his second boat collects. The contract was bait. We were bait."

Bea swore, comprehensively, in two languages. Tomas set the spoon down with the exaggerated gentleness of a man managing his own temper like live cargo.

"The messages," he said. "Abandon if delayed. Forfeit payment."

"Cover. If we turn back, he loses nothing. If we go on, we clear the hard water ahead of his people. Either way, he wins." I laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and short, like a cough. "He even paid the advance generously. Goodwill. Can't have the instrument breaking down before the measurement's done."

Sable hadn't moved. She sat with the mending in her lap, forgotten, and she was looking at me with an expression I couldn't file—something between recognition and grief, as if I'd confirmed something she'd suspected longer than I had.

"Your father's charts," she said quietly. "You brought them."

It wasn't a question. I hadn't told her. I hadn't told anyone. Bea knew I'd packed something extra; she'd never asked what.

"Yes," I said.

"Then he doesn't need the second boat," Sable said. "He needs you to open them. Everything Dario wants is in whatever you're still carrying."

And there it was—the thing I'd been circling on the ridge without landing on. The compass case. Sealed fifteen years. I'd packed it out of habit, out of superstition, out of the same refusal-to-look that had governed my whole adult life, and I'd told myself it was a talisman, a keepsake, a dead man's luck. But Dario had counted on exactly that. On Eriksen blood carrying Eriksen ghosts. On me bringing the one thing I'd sworn never to use.

"He's not tracking the barge," I said slowly. "He's tracking the route. Every choice I make is a data point. And the moment I steer us anywhere that looks deliberate—anywhere the vague coordinates don't explain—that's when he knows we've found it, and that's when his boat stops pretending to be scenery."

"Then we don't steer deliberate," Bea said.

"We already have," Sable said. "Since the side-channel. Since you beached us here." She nodded downstream, toward the dark where the gray boat held its patient station. "This channel isn't on any chart except one. Your father's. You chose it by instinct, and a man who's spent fifteen years studying that instinct just watched you pick it out of a flood."

The rain thickened, drumming on the canopy, and the lamp guttered and steadied. I stood there with my crew looking at me—Bea furious and ready, Tomas calm the way deep water is calm, Sable watching me with those eyes that knew too much—and I felt the weight of the compass case in my pack like a second heartbeat.

Dario wanted what my father left. Fine. So did I—finally, actually, after fifteen years of leaving it sealed. The difference was that I intended to open it first, understand it first, and decide what it *meant* before a man in a gray boat decided what it was worth.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we run north. Loudly. Smoke high, engine hot, straight up the main channel like we're following the contract coordinates to the letter." I looked at Sable. "And tomorrow night, when we're anchored, you and I are going to talk about the *Kestrel's Promise*—everything you know, everything you haven't said, and why a passenger knows my father's channels better than I do."

Something flickered across her face. Not fear. Relief, maybe. Or readiness.

"And the compass case?" Tomas asked.

I put my hand on my pack, on the hard little rectangle I'd carried across fifteen years and half a country without ever once opening it.

"Tomorrow night," I said. "After the talk. It's time I found out what my father was really doing on that river—and who else knew."

Downstream, unseen in the dark, the gray boat held its station, patient as debt.
