## 12. The Sworn-Off Job

I have been carrying my mother's journal for eleven years, and in all that time I have never once opened it sober, dry, or willing. Tonight I am two of the three.

The rain has settled into that long monsoon patience, the kind that doesn't fall so much as *insist*, drumming on the tarp over our camp like a creditor at a door. Bea's fire hisses under an oilcloth lean-to. Tomas is asleep sitting up, which is a talent of his. And Ulyana sits across from me with her hands wrapped around a tin mug she isn't drinking from, watching the water the way people watch a door they're afraid will open.

I've watched her for days now. The way she reads the river like weather. The way she flinches every time someone says *Kestrel's Promise* out loud, like the name is a splinter working deeper. She thinks I'm here for the salvage. Everyone does. Dario Vance certainly does — he thinks I'm his little insurance policy, planted aboard to make sure his charts get used and his cargo gets lifted.

He's half right. I did come for what's in the Kestrel. Just not what he thinks.

My mother's journal sits in the oilskin packet against my ribs, where it has ridden through six years of rivers and three years of pretending I didn't have it. It's wrapped in waxed cloth and tied with fishing line, because when I found it I was seventeen and had nothing else to wrap it with. My mother sewed the packet herself. She always said paper needed armor on this river, same as people.

"You keep looking at me like you're about to ask for something," Ulyana says. Her voice is flat with tiredness, but there's a wry edge under it. She's been generous enough not to use it all day.

"I'm about to give you something," I say. "You might wish I'd asked instead."

She sets down the mug. That's the thing about Ulyana Eriksen — she can smell a hard conversation coming from farther off than anyone can smell rain.

* * *

Here is what I know, and how I know it.

My mother, Adaeze Sable — yes, that's where the name comes from; I never had much patience for surnames after — cooked aboard the Kestrel's Promise for nine seasons. Nine seasons of rice and river fish and crew who treated her like the ship's spine. She kept accounts in a ledger and everything else in a journal she hid inside a false bottom of her spice chest, because she said a cook sees everything and a cook who writes it down becomes dangerous.

The Kestrel ran cargo and passengers on the upper Sable trail for a shipping concern called Meridian & Locke. You won't find them anymore. Companies like that don't die; they just change their letterhead until the name stops meaning anything.

In her last season, my mother wrote that the Kestrel took on a sealed consignment at the delta — crates stenciled M&L, manifest listed as machine parts, insured for more than the boat was worth. The captain argued about the weight distribution. The owners' man aboard insisted. My mother, who could read a loaded deck the way other people read a menu, wrote that the crates were packed wrong on purpose. Heavy high. Ballast light. A ship rigged to handle badly in rough water.

She thought she was imagining it. Then she found the second manifest.

It was tucked into the purser's wastebasket, carbon copy, the kind of document nobody burns carefully enough. It listed the same crates twice — once as cargo, once as *loss*. Pre-dated. Notarized. The owners had already filed the claim before the boat ever left the delta. The Kestrel's Promise wasn't carrying treasure. It was carrying its own funeral paperwork.

If the boat went down in the seasonal floods — and boats did, everyone knew boats did, it was practically a business model — Meridian & Locke would collect the insurance, and the crew's wages, which were held back against "delivery," would drown with it. Every soul aboard was worth more dead than paid.

My mother wrote one line that night that I have never managed to forget:

*They have built us a grave and called it freight.*

* * *

"So the story everyone tells," I say to Ulyana, "is that the Kestrel went down heavy with stolen goods, and the crew were thieves running from the law, and the wreck's been sitting there ever since full of somebody's contraband waiting for a brave fool with a barge."

"That's the story," Ulyana says slowly. "That's the story my father—" She stops. Recalibrates. "That's the story Dario told us."

"Your father flew the last supply run to the Kestrel before she sank." I watch her face when I say it. I've watched a lot of faces hear things they weren't ready for, and hers does something complicated — grief and anger fighting over the same ground. "Everyone downstream says he turned back. That he abandoned them."

"He turned back," she says. Quiet. Like a confession she's made so many times it's worn smooth. "The storm was closing and he turned back and the Kestrel went down that night and he never flew again. He died still owing them. Still owing *her*." Her eyes come up to mine. "Her. Your mother."

"My mother waited on the upper deck," I say. "According to the journal, she helped get passengers into the water first. She gave her place in the skiff to a child. She wrote right up until the water reached the deck, because she wanted whoever found it to know the truth." I untie the fishing line. My hands are steadier than I expected them to be. "She trusted your father to be the one who came back. And when no plane came, she stopped trusting anyone. Except paper. Paper doesn't turn around."

Ulyana has gone very still. Stillness is her native language, I'm learning, but this is a different dialect of it.

"Read it," I say, and pass her the packet.

* * *

She reads by lamplight while the rain keeps its rhythm and Tomas snores gently, a man with a clear conscience or excellent survival instincts. I watch Ulyana's thumb move down the pages, watch her stop at the manifest entry, at the weight notes, at the line about the grave called freight. Watch her arrive, page by page, at the part I've been carrying toward her since I talked my way onto this barge.

Because here's the rest of it. The part I only understood myself three nights ago, lying awake listening to the hull tick.

The journal's final entries aren't about the sinking. They're about your father.

He came back.

Not that night — the storm had him pinned at the strip, that part of the story is true. But two days later, when the floodwater was still high and the wreck was fresh, he came back. He hired a diver out of his own pocket. My mother's last entry describes him standing on the bank in the rain, arguing with the diver about depth and current, refusing to leave. The entry ends mid-sentence — the ink smeared, the page warped — and below it, in different handwriting, cramped and hurried, someone else finished the thought:

*Eriksen says the hold was emptied before she went down. Says the crates were moved aft and the seals broken. Says if the owners scuttled her, the proof is what's missing, not what's aboard.*

That handwriting is my father's. The diver. The man who taught me to swim in this river and then drowned in it two seasons later, chasing the same truth.

So your father didn't abandon the Kestrel. He spent everything he had trying to prove she was murdered. And the world — Dario included, Dario most of all — buried that version and kept the convenient one, because a coward's story lets everyone sleep.

"Say it," I say, when she finally looks up. "Out loud. I want to hear someone say it besides me."

"The cargo was never stolen," Ulyana says. Her voice cracks on *stolen* and she pushes through it. "There was no theft. There was a scuttling. The owners sank their own ship to cheat their own crew, and my father figured it out, and everyone spent twenty years calling him a deserter for it." She laughs, and it's an awful sound, wet and furious. "Dario sent us up this river to rob a grave based on a lie. And my father—" 

She stops. Presses the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"And my father died trying to tell the truth," she finishes, "and I've spent my whole life being ashamed of him."

"Not ashamed," I say. "Running. There's a difference. I'd know — I've done both."

* * *

We sit with it awhile. The rain fills the silence the way water fills a hull — gradually, then all at once.

Tomas wakes up somewhere in there, listens for exactly as long as it takes to understand, and says, "So the inheritance Dario wants — the strongbox, the sealed hold —"

"Is evidence," Bea says from the shadows of the lean-to. She's been awake the whole time; I'd bet the barge on it. "Or it's nothing. Either way, it's not what Dario thinks he's buying."

"It's worse than evidence," I say. "It's my mother's testimony, the second manifest if it survived, maybe the owners' correspondence. Enough to prove Meridian & Locke murdered a ship. Enough to clear every name on that crew list — including both our fathers'." I look at Ulyana. "And enough to hang Dario Vance out to dry, because he's spent years trading on the theft story, buying debts, buying leases, positioning himself to 'recover' cargo that was never legally anyone's but the drowned."

Ulyana is quiet for a long moment. When she speaks again, the wryness is back, but it's load-bearing now, holding something up.

"You realize what this means," she says. "Every rule I've lived by. Never fly the Sable route. Never touch the Kestrel job. Never take Dario's money. I built my whole life around a story that wasn't true."

"Stories are ballast," I say. "You pack them wrong and they'll capsize you just the same."

That almost gets a smile out of her. Almost.

Then her head comes up, sharp, the pilot reading weather that hasn't arrived yet. "Sable. If Dario believes the strongbox holds gold — and he does, he's staked everything on it — then he doesn't need us anymore once we've located the hold. We're not a salvage crew to him. We're a map with legs."

"Now you're catching up," I say.

"And if we find the box first—"

"Then we'd better hope the river's on our side," I say, "because his boat won't be far behind. My father tracked Meridian & Locke's survey launches for two seasons. I grew up knowing the sound of their engines." I nod upstream, into the dark and the rain. "And I've been hearing one all evening."

Nobody moves for a moment. Then Bea kills the lamp, and we sit together in the wet dark, four people and a journal, listening to the rain — and underneath it, faint and patient, the low thrum of an engine that has no business being there.
