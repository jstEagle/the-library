## 13. Charts in the Compass Case

I hadn't opened my father's compass case in eleven years.

That's not strictly true. I'd opened it plenty of times—opened it to check the compass, opened it to oil the hinge, opened it once at three in the morning in a hotel room in Puerto Verde when I couldn't sleep and needed to hold something that had outlived him. But I'd never opened it *all the way*. There was a bottom tray beneath the compass well, a shallow drawer that slid forward if you pressed the brass catch just so, and I had never once pressed it. Not because I didn't know it was there. Because I did.

Some doors you leave shut because you're afraid of what's behind them. Others you leave shut because you already know what's behind them, and you're afraid of what knowing will make you do.

For eleven years I'd told myself it was the first kind. It was easier that way. Cowardice with better branding.

Now I sat on the floor of the barge's aft cabin, rain hammering the deck above like an impatient creditor, Sable's mother's journal open beside me, and there was no version of the story left where I got to keep pretending.

*The cargo was scuttled,* the journal said, in a cook's careful hand. *Not stolen. Sunk on purpose by the men who owned it, so they could collect the insurance and cheat the crew of their shares. The pilot knows. He has proof.*

The pilot. My father.

I turned the compass case over in my hands. Brass, dented at one corner where it had hit the cockpit frame—the dent I'd always assumed came from the crash itself. The leather strap was cracked and dark with age, and the clasp was the same kind of clasp as the drawer catch, which meant either my father had been a man of consistent taste or the case had been built to keep its secrets behind the same stubborn little mechanism.

"Ulyana?" Bea's voice through the bulkhead. "You want tea? Tomas found actual tea. Don't ask where."

"In a minute," I said, which is what you say when you need someone to go away without making it a whole conversation. Her boots moved off down the corridor, and I was alone with the rain and the brass and eleven years of not pressing the catch.

I pressed the catch.

The drawer slid out with a sound like a held breath being released—a soft wooden whisper, no resistance at all, as if it had been waiting. Inside lay a folded chart, oilcloth-wrapped, and beneath it a slim bundle of papers tied with waxed twine, and beneath *that*, wedged into the corner, a small tin of mints that had long since achieved fossilization.

He'd packed for a trip. That was the detail that got me first, before anything else. The mints. He'd expected to come back. You don't pack mints for a flight you don't plan to survive.

My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, because I've done emergency landings with steadier hands than this. I unwrapped the oilcloth.

It was a river chart of the Sable trail—the route, the forbidden route, the one I'd built a career and a personality around never flying. His chart. His handwriting all over it, dense and precise, the handwriting of a man who annotated things the way other people prayed. Depth soundings corrected in pencil. Hazards marked with his own shorthand. And running along the channel like a thread pulled tight through fabric, a route line in red ink that did not follow the main channel at all.

It cut away from the Kestrel's wreck site—or rather, it cut away from where the wreck site was *supposed* to be, the coordinates Dario had put in his contract, the coordinates every salvage man and insurance adjuster had been squinting at for a decade. My father's red line swung wide, up a side channel, past a bend he'd marked with three exclamation points and the word *SHOALS — TRUST THE EAST BANK*, and terminated at a point maybe two kilometers downstream of the official wreck.

At a point labeled, in his small square capitals: **TRUE HOLD.**

I sat very still. Rain on the roof. River muttering against the hull. Somewhere forward, Tomas arguing cheerfully with a bilge pump that was winning.

Because here's what I had carried for eleven years, the thing I'd never said out loud even to Bea, especially not to Bea: I believed my father had run. That when the Kestrel went down, when the storm closed in and people were drowning, my father had turned back. Had abandoned the rescue. Had flown home with his tail between his legs and lived another two years on whatever he could earn hauling medicine and shame across the interior until the river took him too. That was the story the town told. That was the story Dario's photo had implied. That was the story I'd swallowed whole because the alternative—that everyone was wrong, that I alone knew something worse—was unbearable.

And here was a chart proving he hadn't fled the job at all. He'd been *navigating* it. Planning it. Working it out in red ink with the patience of a man who knew exactly what he was looking for and exactly why nobody else would ever find it.

I untied the waxed twine on the paper bundle. Letters. Copies—carbon copies, the old smudged kind—of correspondence between the Kestrel's owners and their insurers, dated weeks before the sinking. I read three pages standing up and had to sit back down for the rest. Cargo manifests that didn't match the hold inventories. A payment to a salvage firm *before* the disaster, for services rendered after it. And one memo, initialled by the owner himself, discussing the "write-down of crew obligations upon total loss," which is what a certain kind of man writes when he means *drown their pensions and call it weather*.

My father hadn't just known. He'd documented. He'd been building a case, copying papers, plotting routes—and then he'd died before he could finish, and the whole thing had gone into a brass box under a compass, and I had spent a decade flying circles around it.

"You absolute mule," I said to him. Affectionately. Mostly.

Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. The thing underneath the thing. He hadn't hidden this from the world. He'd hidden it from *me*. From his daughter who couldn't keep her mouth shut, who argued with creditors at airstrips, who would have marched into the owners' office at nineteen years old waving carbons and gotten herself thrown in a river. He'd put it where only someone who loved him enough to grieve slowly would ever find it. A lock keyed to time.

Sable's knock came soft, two knuckles, the way she does everything—precise, unhurried, like she's giving the door the option. "Bea says tea's ready. Also she says if you're crying she'll pretend she asked nothing."

"I'm not crying."

"Good. She also says the pump's winning."

I looked down at the chart spread across my knees, my father's red thread running through the drowned country, and I laughed—one short, cracked sound that surprised us both, judging by the pause outside the door. "Come in, Sable."

She came in, ducking under the low lintel, hair damp, eyes going immediately—not to me—to the papers. She sat down across from me cross-legged, the way she sits everywhere, like furniture is optional, and waited. That's a skill most people never learn. Waiting without filling the silence with themselves.

"My father wasn't running," I said. "From any of it. Not the job, not the wreck, not—" My voice did something embarrassing on the last part. "He was finishing it. The whole salvage. Alone. He found the true hold, or he worked out where it had to be, and he was going to bring the proof out and hang the owners with their own memos." I tapped the chart. "This isn't a flight plan. It's a testimony."

Sable leaned forward and studied the route line for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched the terminal point, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave marker. "Two kilometers downstream," she said. "The side channel past the shoals." Her jaw tightened. "I used to swim there. When the water was low. Mama told us to stay clear of it because of the current, but there was a sandbar—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "There was a shape under the water we weren't supposed to ask about. The crew called it the iron box. We thought it was engine parts."

"It's not engine parts."

"No," she said. "I don't suppose it is."

We sat with that. Rain. River. The tin of fossilized mints sitting in the open drawer like a small dead joke my father had left for whoever finally came looking—which, I realized, was exactly what it was. He'd known someone would come eventually. He'd just gambled, correctly as it turned out, that it wouldn't be anyone who'd sell the find to the first man with a contract and a smile.

"Dario's expecting the manifest hold," I said. "The coordinates in his contract. The official wreck site. Whatever's there, it's what the owners wanted found—scrap, ballast, maybe a decoy strongbox if they were thorough. Enough to make a show of it." I felt something shift in my chest, some load-bearing wall coming down quietly. "He doesn't have this. He can't have this. This route exists nowhere but my father's head and this chart, and my father's head is—" I gestured vaguely at myself. "Inherited."

"So we don't go where the contract says."

"We go where the compass says." I picked up the compass itself, the needle swinging lazily, settling north with the calm of an instrument that had outlasted everything asked of it. "Eleven years I thought this thing pointed at a coward. Turns out it points at a witness."

Sable smiled—not her usual half-smile, the guarded one, but the full one, rare as dry season. "Your father and my mother," she said, "were working the same truth from opposite ends of the boat. Cook's journal, pilot's chart. It took both of us to read it."

"And Dario thinks he hired the key to the front door."

"He did," Sable said. "He just didn't notice the house has a back entrance."

* * *

I called a council of war in the galley, which is generous language for four people crammed around a table meant for two, drinking Bea's tea, which she makes strong enough to strip varnish.

I laid the chart flat and let them look. I watched Bea's face do the arithmetic—she's a mechanic, she reads diagrams the way priests read scripture—and watched her arrive at the same place I had. "He was finishing the job," she said quietly. "All this time we thought—"

"I know what we thought."

Tomas, to his credit, said nothing sentimental. He put one enormous finger on the shoal marking and said, "East bank. In flood season the east bank scours deeper. Your papa knew his water." Then, because he's Tomas: "Also, we should take the mints."

"They're eleven years old."

"Mints don't expire. They achieve character."

"The route adds six hours," Bea said, tapping the side channel. "Maybe eight, if the shoals are as ugly as he says. Waters start dropping in—" she counted on her fingers, lips moving— "four days. Maybe five. After that, this channel becomes a gravel parking lot and we walk home."

"And Dario's second boat," Sable said, and the temperature in the galley dropped ten degrees, "is somewhere behind us, following our wake and wondering why we haven't stopped at the coordinates yet."

Right. Him. The man whose contract I'd signed, whose advance had saved the strip, whose satellite messages had escalated from *courteous* to *demanding* to, this morning, *if you are delayed, abandon salvage and return, we will reassess*—which, translated from Dario, meant *you've served your purpose as a map, please come home so I can stop pretending you matter.* He wanted us to fail slowly enough to locate the wreck and quickly enough not to reach it. We were, at this moment, ruining his schedule by succeeding.

"Here's what we do," I said, and heard my father's cadence come out of my mouth, which should have frightened me and instead felt like putting on a coat that fit. "We log a false position tonight. Radio's patched enough for a burst transmission—we tell Dario we've reached the manifest site, that it's silted over, that we need two more days to dig. That keeps him patient and keeps his boat sitting on the wrong spot of river."

Bea grinned the grin she gets when a repair goes better than planned. "Lying to a rich man with paperwork. My favorite genre."

"Meanwhile we run the east-bank channel at first light, quiet, no lights, and we put divers on the true hold while he's watching mud." I rolled the chart, and my hands were steady now, fully steady, the way they get in bad weather when the work finally starts. "Then we bring up the proof, we bring up whatever else is in that hold, and we let the river decide what Dario Vance gets to keep."

"Which is nothing," Tomas said happily.

"Which is nothing," I agreed.

Sable was quiet a moment longer, looking at the chart where it lay coiled on the table, at the red thread hidden inside it. "She'd have liked this," she said. "Mama. The cook outfoxing the owners, twenty years late." She looked up at me. "Your father waited eleven years for someone to open that drawer. Mine waited twenty for someone to read her journal. We're late, Ulyana. But we're here."

"We're here," I said.

Outside, the rain had thinned to a steady whisper, and upstream the sky had that bruised greenish light that means the monsoon is reloading rather than leaving. Four days of water, maybe five. A channel that would be a gravel parking lot after that. A rival's boat somewhere in the murk behind us, and a strongbox two kilometers from where the whole world thought it was, waiting under a sandbar shaped like an answer.

I wound my father's watch—I mean his compass, I always mix them up, they were the same object to him—and set it on the chart, needle north, steady as a promise.

First light, we run the east bank.

And God help anyone standing between us and the true hold, because I am done inheriting grief. I'm collecting the estate.
