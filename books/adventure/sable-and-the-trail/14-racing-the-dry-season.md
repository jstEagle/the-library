## 14. Racing the Dry Season

The river dropped four inches overnight. I know because I measured it, because I'm the kind of woman who measures things when she's scared, and I have been scared since roughly the moment the hull opened up like a tin of sardines nobody asked for.

Four inches doesn't sound like much. Four inches is the difference between the barge floating over the sandbar at Marker Nine and the barge becoming a permanent monument to my poor life choices, with a plaque that says *Here lies Bea's engine room, drowned by optimism.*

So we ran.

* * *

You want to know what a boat sounds like when it's held together by prayer and epoxy? It sounds like Tomas humming. He hums when he's terrified, which is most of the time, but he's been humming in a lower key since Ulyana opened her father's compass case and found those charts. Hope changes a man's pitch. Fear keeps the rhythm.

I was in the engine room at dawn, which is where I live now, apparently. My whole world had contracted down to a space the size of a coffin with better lighting, kneeling on a wet deck plate with my hand flat against the patch Sable had sewn into our hull. That patch is the single most humiliating thing in my professional life, and I include the time I set my own eyebrows on fire in front of a fuel inspector.

Here's what happened: I spent two days on that leak with marine epoxy, canvas, and every trick I learned from a man in Puerto Viejo who charged me for lessons in beer. My patch was beautiful. Textbook. And it seeped.

Sable looked at it for about nine seconds, said "the seam breathes when you torque it cold," and re-did the joint with waxed twine, pine pitch, and a strip of rubber off an old boot. Then she sat back on her heels and waited for the water to argue with her.

The water lost.

I don't know where she learned it. River kid, boat cook's daughter, grew up living aboard the Kestrel's Promise — I suppose you learn hulls the way farm kids learn weather. But there's a particular flavor of humiliation in watching someone fix your engine room with a boot, and I've been chewing that flavor for three days now, and it has started to taste like respect, which is worse.

"Patch is holding," I called up through the deck hatch. "Holding is a generous word. It's *acquiescing.*"

"Acquiesce faster," Ulyana's voice came back, dry as gunpowder. She was at the wheel, because of course she was. The woman sleeps standing up these days. I've seen her do it — eyes open, hands on the spokes, breathing slow, and if you didn't know her you'd think she was awake. Maybe she is. Maybe sleep is just another debt she's decided not to pay until the estate settles.

We were running the east bank channel at first light exactly like she'd promised the compass, and I'll say this for Ulyana Eriksen: when she finally points herself at something, she doesn't so much travel as arrive, continuously, at increasing speed.

* * *

Let me tell you about the dry season, because everyone thinks monsoons are the enemy out here, and they're wrong. The monsoon is loud and honest. It hits you like a drunk uncle at a wedding — unpleasant, but you see it coming. The dry season is the quiet one that ruins you. The water falls. The channels braid apart into threads too thin to float. Sandbars rise overnight like the river growing bones. Every year the upper Sable swallows boats that stayed one day too long, and every year the salvage men come collect them in December, and every year the owners swear they'd have made it.

We had maybe five days of floatable water left in the east channel. Dario Vance's second boat had been shadowing us for two days before Ulyana spotted it from the ridge, and God only knew how much lead time they had or how deep their draft ran. If their boat drew less water than ours — and it would, because rich men buy shallow boats the way they buy shallow friends — then every hour we dawdled was an hour they gained.

So: momentum. Momentum was the whole strategy. A leaking barge, a crew of four, half our fuel gone to the river, no radio antenna, and a plan that consisted entirely of *go fast and don't think about it.*

I've flown with Ulyana six years. This is, statistically speaking, her best plan ever.

* * *

Tomas came down mid-morning with the fuel gauge report, which he delivered the way doctors deliver bad news to family. "Sixty-two percent of what we'd need for the round trip," he said. "If the patch holds and the current cooperates."

"The current never cooperates," I said. "The current is a professional adversary."

"So we're going anyway."

"We're going anyway."

He nodded and went back up, and I heard him start humming again, low and steady, and I thought: this is what my father meant when he talked about crews. Not the payroll. Not the licenses. He meant the specific miracle of people who hear the odds out loud and nod and go back to work. You can't buy that. Believe me, I've checked the prices.

* * *

The east bank channel is a liar. It looks wide from the ridge — a broad brown ribbon sliding between flooded forest — but the floodwater lies over old fields, and under the surface are fences, tractor axles, a church bell somebody never got around to rescuing, and one memorable year, according to Tomas, an entire piano. You run it by reading the trees. Where the current combs the branches all one direction, the water's moving; where the leaves hang still and slick with gray mud above the waterline, that's your depth gauge. Mud line at knee height means four feet under your keel. Mud line at chest height means turn around and write your will.

Ulyana read trees like she reads weather, which is to say like she was personally offended by both. She'd stand at the wheel with her father's compass open on the chart box beside her, needle north, and call depths to Tomas on the bow pole, and Tomas would sound the bottom and call back numbers, and I would be below listening to all of it through the deck plates like a submarine captain who'd been demoted to plumbing.

At Marker Nine — the sandbar I'd measured against, my personal nemesis — the channel pinched hard. I felt it before anyone said anything: the engine note changed, the vibration went from *working* to *arguing*, and the patch behind my hand gave a long creak like an old floor.

"Bea?" Ulyana called down.

"She's fine," I lied, and then, because lying to Ulyana is like lying to a customs officer, "She's fine-ish. Keep her straight. Don't let Tomas lean on the same rail twice."

Up top, I heard Sable's voice for the first time that morning. She'd been quiet since the charts came out of the compass case — not withdrawn, exactly. Focused. Like a woman walking toward a door she'd been locked out of for twenty years.

"Port," she said. "There's a cut past the dead fig tree. My mother used to take the supply skiff through it when the main channel silted. It's narrow but it's deep — the current scours it every flood."

"You know a shortcut through a channel you haven't run since you were a child," Ulyana said. Not a question. An audit.

"I know it the way you know a scar," Sable said. "It doesn't move."

A pause up there. Then Ulyana: "Tomas. Port past the fig tree."

And I swear the engine and I both held our breath.

* * *

The cut was everything she said and twice as ugly. Walls of flooded cane on both sides, close enough that the cane dragged along the hull with a sound like fingernails, and the current through it ran hard enough to comb the water into long gray ropes. I lay on my back under the engine with one hand on the patch and one on the throttle linkage, ready to feather it if she surged, and I listened to Sable call the turns from the bow in a voice that never once wavered.

"Ease left. Now hard right — there's a fence post under you, feel it? No? Good, that means you missed it. Straighten. Straighten. Straight—"

Scrape. A long, singing scrape along the starboard side, and Tomas yelped, and I closed my eyes and listened to my hull with my whole soul.

Then it passed. Open water. The main channel again, wider here, running fast toward the high country.

"Depth under the keel never dropped below seven feet," Sable reported, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before. Not triumph. Relief. Like she'd asked the river for a favor on her mother's account and the river had paid up.

Ulyana was quiet a long moment. Then: "Your mother ran this route."

"Every season. Twelve years."

"Then she was the best pilot on this river," Ulyana said, "and I'm sorry I never knew that."

Nobody said anything after that. Down in my coffin-sized engine room, I tightened a fitting that didn't need tightening, because some silences aren't empty. They're load-bearing.

* * *

We made the bend below Coffin Rapids — yes, that's its real name, no, I don't want to discuss it — by late afternoon, and that's where the day tried to kill us properly.

The dry season drop had done something vicious at the bend: the current, retreating, had piled a debris raft against the outside bank, whole trees woven together with silt into a floating dam the size of a tennis court, and the entire river was funneling through a gap on the far side maybe forty feet wide. Forty feet of churning, standing-wave water between us and the next reach.

Any other day, we anchor and wait for morning light. We didn't have any other days.

"Fuel says we run it now or walk home," I called up.

"Dario says his boat draws eighteen inches," Ulyana said. "Ours draws thirty. If he runs this gap tonight, we wake up tomorrow watching him dive our wreck." A pause. "Sable?"

Sable studied the gap for a while. I could hear the water from down below, a sound like continuous applause from an audience that wanted us dead.

"Run it on the left third," she said finally. "The waves look taller there but they're standing on rock. Right side is soft bottom — you broach on soft bottom in current like that, you roll. Left side, if you hit, you hit stone and stop, and stopping is survivable."

"That's the worst safety briefing I've ever heard," I told the deck plates.

"It's the only one on offer," Ulyana said. "Tomas, lash everything. Bea—"

"Already lashing myself," I said. "Go."

* * *

I won't describe the next four minutes in detail, partly because detail would require adjectives I don't own, and partly because I experienced most of it from inside the engine room with my arms wrapped around the engine block like it was a large, expensive relative.

What I remember: the roar getting personal. The bow lifting. The world tilting past forty-five degrees while loose wrenches rained around my head like iron hail. The patch giving one enormous groan — a groan I felt in my teeth, in my sternum, in whatever organ it is that processes regret — and holding. Holding. Water slapping across the deck plates above my head, green daylight strobing through the hatch, Tomas's humming rising into something close to opera.

Then the stern swung, caught the current fair, and we shot out the far side into calm water so suddenly that my stomach arrived several seconds after the rest of me.

Silence, except the engine ticking and the river being smug behind us.

"Bea," Ulyana called down. Her voice was level, but I've known her six years, and level-for-Ulyana after a rapid like that meant her hands were shaking on the spokes where nobody could see. "Report."

"Hull holds. Patch holds. Engine holds." I took inventory of my limbs, found them all present and complaining. "I hold. Barely. Ask me again in an hour."

"Chart says two more hours to the wreck anchorage," she said. "Waters drop tonight. We dive at first light or not at all."

"Not at all is not on the menu," said Sable, from the bow, and there was steel in it now, the kind you only get from waiting twenty years. "Not for me."

"No," Ulyana agreed quietly. "It isn't."

* * *

We anchored at dusk in a cut beneath a bluff, engines cold, running lights off, because a lit boat on this stretch at night might as well send up flares spelling *salvage me*. I did a final walkthrough of the patch with a lamp pressed to my forehead like a demented miner, and it held, and I put my palm flat against it one last time — Sable's boot-rubber and pine pitch and waxed twine, holding back the whole patient weight of the Sable River — and I thought about names.

That's the thing nobody tells you about boats. They keep names the way rivers keep bodies. The Kestrel's Promise has been down there in the dark for years, and somewhere in her flooded corridors is a strongbox, and somewhere near that strongbox is whatever my captain's father left behind when he chose lives over loot, and tomorrow morning four people who have no business being a crew are going to swim into a grave to collect it.

I should be terrified. I am, a little. But mostly — and I checked twice, because it seemed unprofessional — I'm hopeful. Hope with teeth, Tomas calls it. Hope that bites.

Above me, on deck, I heard the low murmur of voices. Ulyana and Sable, heads together over the chart, planning the dive in the blue lamplight. Two women from two wrecks, comparing scars.

Tomorrow we dive. Tonight, somewhere downriver, a shallow-draft boat with a rich man's name on her transom is running the same falling water we ran, and every mile she gains is a mile we'll have to answer for.

First light. Masks on. Hold your breath.

The estate opens at dawn.
