## 15. The Kestrel's Grave

I have a theory about rivers. It's not a good theory—Tomas Okafor's theories rarely are, according to Bea, who has heard most of them and rated them accordingly—but it goes like this: every river keeps one place it refuses to give back. A bend where the current runs wrong, a pool that swallows anchors whole, a stretch of water that takes something from everyone who passes through and never says thank you.

The lower Sable had been polite enough for three days. Almost apologetic. But as we came up on the last reach before the old wreck site, I felt the river change its mind about us.

The light was going. That was the first problem. Dusk on the upper Sable doesn't arrive so much as descend—the jungle closes in on both banks until the sky is a strip of bruised copper overhead, and then even that goes out like someone pinching a wick. Ulyana stood at the bow of the barge with her father's compass open in her palm, and she didn't say anything for a long time, which from Ulyana is practically a speech.

"There," she said finally, and pointed her chin at the far bank.

I saw it before I understood what I was seeing. A shape in the water that wasn't water—a long dark spine breaking the surface at an angle, furred with moss and hanging vines, half-swallowed by a stand of dead trees that had grown up around it in the years since. The river had moved around it the way a body adjusts around an old injury. It didn't look like a boat anymore. It looked like something the river had been digesting for eleven years and still hadn't finished.

The *Kestrel's Promise*.

Nobody said anything for a while. Bea killed the engine to a bare idle, and the silence that came down over us was the heavy kind, the kind you can hear your own pulse in. Even the rain held off, as if the monsoon itself wanted to watch.

"That's her," Sable said quietly.

She was standing beside me at the rail, and I'll be honest—I'd been watching her all day without quite admitting it to myself. She hadn't spoken more than twenty words since dawn. She'd spent the whole run upriver at the rail or at the wheel, reading the water the way Bea reads engines: not looking at it so much as listening to it. And now here was the thing she'd been steering toward for—what? Eleven years? Her whole life, maybe—and she looked at it the way you look at a grave. Which, I suppose, is exactly what it was.

"You okay?" I asked, because somebody had to, and because I'm the one who asks.

"No," she said. Then, after a moment: "But yes."

I decided that was the truest answer anyone had given on this boat in weeks, including me.

* * *

We anchored in the lee of a gravel bar maybe two hundred meters downstream, because nobody—not Bea, not Ulyana, certainly not me—wanted to sleep tied up next to the thing. Call it superstition if you like. I call it respect. You don't park your truck on top of somebody's headstone just because the ground is flat.

Bea got the anchor set on the third try, swearing at it in two languages, and then we gathered under the tarp in the stern with the single lantern hissing between us, and Ulyana laid out the situation the way she lays out everything: flat, fast, and with no room left in it for feelings.

"Here's where we are," she said. "Waters drop in four days, maybe five. After that, this channel becomes a mud track and we're walking home. So we dive tomorrow morning, first light, when the current's lowest and there's light to work by."

"And tonight?" Tomas asked, meaning me, meaning the question I already knew the answer to.

"Tonight we look at what we're diving into."

She unrolled her father's charts across the crate lid, weighted the corners with four mugs of Bea's coffee, which Bea guarded jealously, and there it was—the *Kestrel's Promise* drawn in faded ink, annotated in a hand I recognized from eleven years of watching Ulyana pretend she hadn't inherited it. The wreck lay on its port side, wedged against the old channel wall, decks awash. The manifest hold—the one Dario's contract described, the one full of "salvageable cargo"—was marked with a neat X.

And then, in smaller letters, almost hidden in the margin, another mark. Another hold. And a line of handwriting that made Ulyana go very still when she read it aloud:

*"Not the manifest hold. They'll look there. Go deeper."*

"Go deeper," Bea repeated. "Into a flooded wreck that's been rotting for a decade. Sure. Easy."

"It gets better," Ulyana said, and her voice had gone dry in the way it does when she's holding something down hard. "The interior corridors are flooded to within a meter of the overheads in places. Visibility inside will be zero—silt, eleven years of it. No natural light past the first compartment. Current moving through the hull whenever the river breathes." She looked up. "And it's a grave. There may be—there were crew who didn't get out."

Nobody said anything. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, the river moved against the wreck with a sound like slow breathing.

"So," Bea said, into the silence, with the delicacy of a sledgehammer, "who's going in?"

And that's when Sable stood up.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because I don't think any of us understood, right up until that moment, what we had sitting at our own table eating our rice and complaining about Bea's coffee like a regular person.

Sable stood up, and she reached into her bag—the same battered canvas bag she'd carried aboard as a paying passenger a lifetime ago, back when we were strangers—and she took out a roll of paper soft with age, tied with string. She untied it on the crate lid, right on top of Ulyana's father's charts, and smoothed it flat.

It was a drawing. A child's drawing, in colored pencil, the kind of thing a kid makes on the back of a supply list. But it was a floor plan. Decks, corridors, compartments, labeled in a careful childish hand: *Mama's galley. Engine room (LOUD). The big hold. Papa's cabin.* Every corridor, every hatch, every ladder, drawn from memory by a girl who had lived aboard the *Kestrel's Promise* until the river took it.

"I was eight," Sable said. "I could walk that ship blindfolded. I did, sometimes, for games." Her finger traced the corridors, sure and unhesitating, no pauses, no searching. "This hasn't changed. The hull's settled, the silt's come in, but the bones are the bones. I know where every hatch is. I know which ladders will still hold and which ones are rust and lies." She looked up, and the lantern caught her face, and I saw that she wasn't afraid—or rather, that she was afraid of exactly one thing, and it wasn't the water. "You can't dive this blind. Nobody could. But I can take you through it like it's my own house. Because it was."

"Sable." Ulyana's voice was careful. "Inside that wreck, if something goes wrong—if you get turned around, snagged, pinned—"

"I know what happens inside a flooded wreck," Sable said. "My mother is still in one."

The words landed on all of us at once. Bea stopped chewing. I forgot to breathe, which as a professional diver I'm told is discouraged.

"She went back for the letters," Sable went on, quieter. "The night it went down. She told me to get in the skiff and stay there, and she went back below for the strongbox, and the deck shifted, and—" She stopped. Started again. "I've had eleven years to learn everything about how people die inside ships. I'm not planning to join her. But I'm the only person alive who can guide you through those corridors, and I am *going*, Ulyana. You can vote me off the boat if you want. I'll swim."

There was a long silence. Out in the dark, the river breathed against the wreck.

"We do it by the book," Ulyana said at last, and her voice had changed—it had gone from captain-flat to something else, something that sounded almost like it was coming from behind glass. "Lines on every diver. Bea on surface tender. Thirty-minute bottom time, hard stop, no heroics. And Sable—" She hesitated, and I watched her wrestle with whatever she was going to say next, and lose. "Sable, you go in first. You know the way. But the moment you say turn back, we turn back. All of us. No arguments."

Sable nodded once. And then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, tired, real smile, the first one I'd seen on her since the storm. "My mother used to say the *Kestrel* would give herself back someday," she said. "To somebody who asked nicely."

"Did she say what asking nicely involved?" Bea asked.

"Diving into a black flooded hole," Sable said. "So. Roughly this."

* * *

I couldn't sleep, which is standard procedure for me the night before a dive, so I ended up on the stern deck with the last of the coffee, watching the wreck sit in the dark. The moon was behind cloud, but there was enough light to see the shape of it—that long broken spine, the vines trailing off it into the water like hair. Every few minutes the current pushed a wave of debris against the hull, and the sound carried across the still water: a low wooden groan, deep and patient, like something turning over in its sleep.

Ulyana found me there around midnight. She didn't ask why I was awake; she just sat down on the crate beside me and looked at the wreck too.

"My dad used to say the river doesn't keep things," she said, after a while. "He said that's the lie people tell themselves. Rivers only borrow. Everything comes back eventually—driftwood, boats, bodies, the truth. Just not always in one piece, and not always when you want it."

"He sounds like he'd have liked Sable," I said.

"Yeah." Ulyana turned her father's compass over in her hands, the brass case catching the faint light. "That's the thing that's been sitting wrong with me, Tomas. Eleven years I thought he died running from this job. Fleeing. Taking the money and drowning in it." She shook her head slowly. "And now I find his chart, and it says *go deeper*, and there's a second hold, and I think—he wasn't running. He was finishing it. He went back for the same reason Sable's mother did. For the truth. And the river kept him too."

I didn't have anything clever to say to that, which for me is genuinely unprecedented, so I just nodded and handed her the coffee.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we ask nicely."

Out in the dark, the *Kestrel's Promise* groaned again, long and low, and I swear on my mother's grave it sounded less like a warning than like an answer.

Then Bea's voice came sharp from the wheelhouse, cutting across the water: "Tomas! Get up here—you need to see the far bank!"

I was moving before I finished standing. Because there, on the downstream side of the wreck, where an hour ago there had been nothing but jungle shadow—there was a light. Small. Moving. A lantern, sweeping slow and deliberate along the waterline, right beside the grave.

Somebody else was asking.
