## 16. Into the Hold

The Kestrel's Promise sat in the water like a drowned animal that hadn't finished dying.

From the surface she was all shoulder and shadow—hull canted hard to port, superstructure leaning into the current as if listening for something downstream. Forty years of flood seasons had combed silt through her ribs and hung the trees with her paint. Tomas cut the engine a hundred meters out, and in the sudden quiet I could hear the river working at her, patient as a debt collector.

"There," Sable said.

She stood at the bow with one hand on the rail and the other shading her eyes, and for a moment she wasn't the stranger who'd talked her way aboard with a duffel bag and a lie about visiting an aunt. She was a girl pointing at a house.

"The galley door. We used to come out that way after supper and sit on the rail. Mama said if I fell in she'd let the river keep me, because I'd be too heavy to haul out twice." Her voice did something complicated on the last word. "It's still there. The door."

"Half of everything's still there," Bea muttered from the engine hatch. She'd spent two days coaxing our patched hull upriver and it showed in her face—the gray around the mouth, the hands that kept checking tools that were already checked. "That's the problem."

I brought us alongside along the leeward side, where the current curled back on itself in a slow eddy, and we tied off to what had once been a mooring bollard and was now a knuckle of rusted iron wearing forty years of moss like a glove. The water here was tea-dark and opaque. You couldn't see your own hand at elbow depth.

Diving conditions, Tomas had said at dusk, with the particular cheerfulness he used for terrible news. Lethal, he'd meant. Zero visibility below two meters. Debris everywhere. A wreck that had been settling, groaning, and rearranging itself since before any of us were born.

And yet here we were, four people rigging dive lights on a barge deck while the river breathed against the hull.

* * *

Sable went first.

Of course she did. She'd been memorizing these corridors since she was six years old, back when they were hallways and not drowning architecture, and no argument from me—and I made several, each one more reasonable than the last—was going to stop her. She went down the old galley stairwell on a line Bea anchored to the deck winch, and for eleven minutes the only sign of her was the glow of her lamp moving behind the portholes like a firefly trapped in a bottle.

When she surfaced, she spat her regulator out and grinned, and it was the most frightening thing I'd seen all week.

"It's all still furnished," she said. "Tables. Bunks. There's a piano in the saloon, or what's left of one. It's like—" She stopped, recalibrated. "It's like everyone just left."

"They did leave," Bea said quietly. "That's the whole point."

"No." Sable pulled herself onto the ladder, water sheeting off her. "They didn't *leave*. They were put off. There's a difference, and the difference is down there, and I want it found tonight, while there's still light enough above for you to pull us out if something goes wrong."

She looked at me when she said *us*, and I understood that the *us* was me and her, specifically, because she'd decided—somewhere between the night camp confessions and the hull giving way—that whatever the Kestrel held belonged to both our dead, and she wasn't letting me stand on the deck being brave in a useless way.

So I suited up.

* * *

I want to say something noble about descending into my father's grave. The truth is less flattering: I was cold, I was scared, and I was furious at Dario Vance with a fury so clean and total it felt almost restful, the way pain is restful once it stops pretending to be anything else.

The water took us at the galley door. Below two meters the world became brown murk pierced by lamplight, and my own hands turned into pale suggestions. Sable swam ahead of me with a confidence that bordered on the supernatural, hooking her line through doorframes, pausing at junctions to touch things—a rail, a bulkhead, a brass fitting worn smooth by decades of thumbs—as though greeting them.

*Galley,* she signed, tapping a rusted stove. *Corridor. Crew country.*

We moved through the ship's working heart, past cabins where bunks still hung from their chains, and every meter of it I could feel my father's ghost getting denser, the way air does before a storm. He'd flown this river. He'd stood in these corridors. And then he'd turned back, and a woman named Adaeze—Sable's mother—had died waiting for a rescue that never came, and I had spent twenty years building my entire life around the shape of that failure.

Never fly the Sable River route. That was the rule. That was the vow. That was the load-bearing wall of who I was.

Sable stopped at a hatch.

Below us, according to the charts I'd finally opened in my father's compass case three nights ago, lay the true hold—not the manifest hold Dario's salvage schematics drooled over, but a smaller compartment aft, sealed separately, accessible only through a maintenance trunking that appeared on no drawing but my father's. His annotations were in his cramped pilot's hand: *NOT ON MANIFEST. CREW'S OWN. SEALED FROM INSIDE.*

Sealed from inside. I'd read those words a hundred times since, trying to make them mean something other than what they obviously meant.

Sable looked back at me, lamp light catching the edge of her jaw, and pointed down, and asked with her eyebrows whether I was ready.

I wasn't. I went anyway.

* * *

The trunking was narrow, silted at the bottom, and bent twice like a broken finger. We went through it single file, lamps sweeping walls furred with sediment, and then the space opened and my light found metal that wasn't rust-colored.

A strongbox. Knee-high, riveted corners, sitting upright on the hold floor as if someone had set it down carefully and stepped back. Not scattered. Not buried. *Placed.*

My heart was doing something unprofessional. Sable hovered beside me, and even through the mask I could see her breathing change.

There was writing on the lid.

Not engraving. Paint—long dried to a ghost of itself, but legible under the lamp, in a hand I knew better than my own, because I'd watched it fill out flight logs and grocery lists and birthday cards for the first ten years of my life:

**DO NOT OPEN UNTIL THE RIVER GIVES THEM BACK.**

Beneath it, smaller:

**E. ERIKSEN — FOR THE CREW OF THE KESTREL'S PROMISE — ALL 23 — NOTHING TAKEN**

All twenty-three. The full crew manifest. The number that had never come home.

And beneath that, wedged under the box's hasp as if the lid had been closed on it deliberately, a folded oilskin packet, swollen with decades of river but intact, tied with wire.

Sable's hands rose slowly to cover her regulator. Through the mask her eyes were enormous.

I reached out and touched the painted letters. My gloves were thick; I barely felt the metal. But I felt it anyway, somewhere deeper than skin.

He came here. During the disaster—during the flood, the panic, the night the Kestrel went down—he came *here*, into the dark, into the true hold, and he found the crew's sealed box, the evidence of what the owners had done to them, the proof that the cargo they'd been branded thieves for was scuttled deliberately to cheat them. And he had it in his hands. He could have taken it. Could have flown it out, sold it, used it, saved himself the ruin and the running and the daughter who grew up ashamed of his name.

Instead he'd written on the lid. Instead he'd left it sealed. Because taking it meant leaving someone behind—because the boat was going down and there were lives in the balance and only so much room in the sky—and my father, the coward, the deserter, the man whose name I'd been hiding from creditors under three aliases, had chosen the twenty-three over the truth, trusting the river to keep it until the river gave it back.

Until now. Until his daughter floated in the black water above it with a stranger whose mother waited on the bank.

Grief is a strange cargo. It doesn't lighten when you finally understand it. It just changes shape—from lead to ballast, from the thing that drags you down to the thing that keeps you steady in weather. I hung there in the flooded hold of the Kestrel's Promise and cried into my mask, which is a miserable experience and I recommend it to no one, and Sable put her hand on my shoulder and left it there, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the paint on the box hadn't already said better.

* * *

Getting the packet out was easy. Getting the box out was impossible—it weighed more than dignity allowed, and the trunking wouldn't take it, and Bea would have our hides if we tried to shift forty kilos of iron through a collapsing wreck on empty lungs. So we did what my father had done. We left it sealed. We photographed the lid with the waterproof camera, we logged the position, and we took only the oilskin packet, wired shut, cradled in Sable's arms like an infant on the ascent.

We broke the surface into dusk and rain-smell, and Bea hauled us over the gunwale, and Tomas took one look at our faces and stopped asking questions, which is the finest quality a deckhand can have.

The packet opened on the chart table under lamplight, dripping. Inside: documents. Pay ledgers. A contract of carriage with the freight terms blacked out and re-inked. Letters—dozens of them, crew letters, claims letters, a petition signed by all twenty-three names demanding wages the owners swore had never been owed. And at the bottom, a sworn statement in a lawyer's dry hand confirming what Sable's mother's journal had told us: the cargo was scuttled. Deliberately. The theft was the owners'. The thieves were the victims.

Sable read the names aloud, one by one, all twenty-three, and her voice held until the eleventh, which was her mother's, and then it didn't, and none of us pretended not to notice, and Bea put the kettle on because that is Bea's answer to everything and it is always correct.

"We have to get this authenticated," I said finally. "Every page. And then—" I looked at the ledger, at the petition, at the box still sealed in the dark below us with its painted promise. "Then nobody owns this. It goes to the families. All of it."

"And Dario?" Tomas asked.

As if summoned—which, knowing Dario, he probably considered himself to be—the radio crackled. Static, then his voice, silk over gravel, tight in a way I'd never heard it before:

*"Ulyana. Position report. Now. My people are half a day out and I'd hate for there to be a misunderstanding about who holds salvage rights."*

Bea looked at me. Tomas looked at me. Sable looked at the oilskin packet, then at me, and her expression was the one I imagined her mother wore when the plane turned back on the horizon.

I picked up the handset.

"Dario," I said sweetly. "About those salvage rights. You're going to want to sit down."
