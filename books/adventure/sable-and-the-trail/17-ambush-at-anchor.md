## 17. Ambush at Anchor

I should have heard them coming. That's the part that will keep me up for the rest of my life, however long the river allows it to be: I grew up on that water, I knew every sound it made, and I still didn't hear the second boat until its searchlight was already crawling across our deck like a finger looking for something to steal.

We had anchored in the lee of a drowned cottonwood, maybe two kilometers short of the Kestrel's grave. Ulyana wanted to make the wreck site before full dark; Bea argued the channel was too shallow to risk with the hull patch sweating under pressure; and Tomas, who votes last because he says his vote weighs more, sided with Bea. So we dropped anchor in the shadow of a tree that had been dead longer than any of us had been alive, ate cold rice and beans off the same plates we'd been eating off for nine days, and let ourselves believe, for one whole hour, that the worst of it was behind us.

The strongbox sat in the middle of the deck under an oilskin tarp, water still weeping out of its seams from the dive. We'd gotten it open just enough to confirm what my mother's journal promised—papers, sealed in oilcloth bundles, letters and ledgers and a pension roll with forty names written in a hand I recognized as my mother's—and then Ulyana had called a halt. "Not out here," she'd said. "Not until we're somewhere with walls." She'd lashed it shut again with the tenderness other people reserve for sleeping children.

I remember watching her do that and thinking: this is what it looks like when someone stops running.

Then the light came.

* * *

It swept the deck once, slow, deliberate, the way you'd read a page. It paused on the strongbox. It paused on me.

"Kill the lamp," Ulyana said quietly, but there was nowhere to kill it to. The searchlight found their generator hum a half-second later, and then the shape of Dario Vance's boat resolving out of the rain-dark upstream—a river cruiser, big, low, with a bow ramp and six men standing on it like they'd been arranged by someone with a sense of theater.

Dario stood at the front. He wasn't wearing a slicker. Of course he wasn't. Men like Dario don't get wet; they get *arrived*.

"Sable," he called across the water, warm as a bank manager. "And Ulyana Eriksen. And the mechanic and the boy. You've all been very busy."

"You followed us," Ulyana said. Not a question. Her voice had gone flat in the way I'd learned meant she was doing arithmetic she hated.

"I *hired* you," Dario said. "There's a difference. A subtle one, I'll grant you, but I'm told subtlety is wasted on this crew."

Bea moved first—not toward the box, toward the winch, and I understood her plan a heartbeat before anyone else did: beach the barge, run her aground in the shallows, make them come to us on foot through the flooded timber. It was a good plan. It was a better plan than anything I came up with in the next ten minutes, which were the worst ten minutes of my life, and I include the night my mother died in that ranking, which is saying something, because that night I set a personal record.

Dario's men simply started shooting.

Not at us—at the winch. Two rounds and the cable parted with a sound like a snapped bone, and Bea's plan died at birth. Then a third round took out our running lamp, and the fourth went into the air over Tomas's head when he made the mistake of standing up straight, and after that nobody stood up straight anymore.

"Nobody needs to be a hero," Dario said. "I've read about heroes. They die in chapters."

He had a way of talking that made violence sound like paperwork. His men came across on a skiff, four of them, boots wet, rifles slung casual, and they did not hurry, because hurrying is for people who are afraid of losing. One of them—the big one with the burned forearm, the one they called Cutter—stepped onto our deck, looked at the oilskin tarp, and peeled it back like he was unwrapping a gift he'd bought himself.

"There she is," Dario said softly, from the cruiser. "Forty years of interest."

"It's not yours," I said. I hadn't planned to say it. My mouth just went ahead without filing the paperwork. "None of it ever was. Your family scuttled the Kestrel. My mother's journal proves it. The cargo was written off, the crew was cheated, and your grandfather signed the order."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across Dario's face that wasn't amusement. Something older and uglier, the look of a man hearing a debt read aloud in church.

"The journal," he repeated. "Where is the journal?"

"On its way to people who'll publish it," I lied, beautifully, instantly, the way you learn to lie when lying is all that stands between you and the truth getting used against you. "Three copies. Four, actually. I lost count."

He smiled again, and I understood that I had just made everything worse, and that he knew I was lying, and that none of it mattered because he had the guns and the boat and the box, and I had a mother's handwriting and a hole in my chest where forty years of justice used to be.

Cutter hauled the strongbox up like it weighed nothing. Maybe it didn't. Maybe grief has no mass. They passed it across to the skiff, and then across to the cruiser, and I watched Dario put his own hand on it, flat, proprietary, the way you'd rest a palm on a horse you'd just won in a crooked bet.

"Ulyana," he said, and now his voice changed, dropped the warmth entirely, and what was underneath was worse. "Your father had this same box in his hands once. Did you know that? Thirty years ago. He was carrying it to shore when the storm turned, and he put it down to pull a drowning man out of the water, and by the time he got back to it—" He shrugged, elegantly. "Well. Rivers."

"That's not what happened," Ulyana said. Her voice cracked exactly once, in the middle, like ice giving way.

"No?" Dario tilted his head. "You weren't there. Neither was I. But my grandfather kept records too, Miss Eriksen, and records are such funny things. Yours say he abandoned the salvage. Mine say he stole it. The truth is probably that he drowned trying to be decent, which is the most expensive thing a man can be." He gestured, and his men began withdrawing to the skiff. "Here's what happens now. The storm front hits within the hour—you can feel it, even you two river-rats can feel it. When it hits, this anchorage becomes a killing floor. Our boat rides it out at the bend, three kilometers downstream, where the channel's deep. Yours doesn't. You're welcome to try for the wreck site—it's got high ground, if the Kestrel's bones haven't finally rotted through. Or you're welcome to stay here and drown politely."

"And if we follow you?" Bea asked from behind the winch wreckage, her voice like gravel in a tin cup.

"Then my men will shoot at you again, and I'll have to pay them overtime, and I resent that almost as much as I'll resent what happens next." Dario settled into the cruiser's wheelhouse like a man settling into a verdict. "Go home, Eriksen. Take the loss. Some inheritances are just lessons with extra steps."

They pulled away. The searchlight shrank. And the rain began.

* * *

Not monsoon rain—I'd lived through the monsoon, I knew its moods, its long rolling patience. This was different. This was the last front, the season's closing argument, and it came down the valley like something with intent. The wind arrived first, flattening the flooded timber around us, and then the rain hit sideways, and the temperature dropped five degrees in a minute, and Tomas got the barge's remaining lamp lit and lashed down while Bea screamed instructions at him over the noise.

"We can't ride it out here," Bea shouted. "Open water, dead tree snagging the anchor line—first gust surge puts us on the timber and we're done!"

"So we go to the wreck," Ulyana said.

I looked at her. She was soaked through, hair plastered to her skull, holding the wheel with both hands, and she was looking upstream at the black wall where the Kestrel lay half-drowned, and there was something in her face I'd only seen once before—the night she told me about turning back, about her father, about the debt that wasn't money. It was the face of a woman who had decided that the river could take whatever it wanted, but it was going to have to take it from her personally.

"In this?" Bea said.

"In this. High ground, you said so yourself. If the superstructure's still above water, we tie off inside the bones and we live. If it isn't—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. We all did the math.

Tomas was already hauling in the anchor, coughing rain, hands bleeding from the cable. I went to the bow and became eyes, because that was the one job left that I was actually good at: reading the channel in the dark, calling the water, finding the deep thread between the drowned trees while the lightning strobed the world white every few seconds like a bad photographer.

"Starboard! Starboard, hard!" I screamed it more than once. The barge groaned, wallowed, answered. Somewhere behind us I heard Bea keeping the engine alive with what sounded like prayer and profanity in equal measure, and somewhere ahead, growing, the Kestrel's grave.

We hit the shoal coming in. Of course we did. The whole starboard side lifted, scraped, slammed back down, and the patch Bea had sweated over for two days tore loose with a shriek, and suddenly we were taking water again, real water, fast water, and Bea was screaming for Tomas to get the pump running while Ulyana drove us the last hundred meters on pure fury.

And then the Kestrel rose out of the dark.

I hadn't seen her since I was seven years old. She was smaller than memory and larger than grief—half-submerged, listing hard to port, her superstructure leaning out of the floodwater like a drowning woman's arm. Rust had eaten her down to suggestion. But her main deck house still stood above the waterline, and her old mooring deck—my mother's deck, where she'd hung laundry and sung off-key and told me the river was just a road that moved—was still there, still solid, still waiting.

"Tie off inside the list!" Ulyana shouted. "Get us against the deck house!"

We came alongside in a chaos of fenders and rope and Bea swearing at the engine, and we got two lines over, and then three, and then the barge was snugged up against the Kestrel's flank like a child pressed against a mother's back, and we climbed aboard my mother's ship for the second time in my life and the first time in terror.

The deck house was half-flooded, roofed against most of the rain, alive with the sound of the storm hammering the river flat outside. We got the pump going. We got dry-ish. We got alive, which at that point felt like an achievement worth framing.

And then, in the lamplight, Tomas said the thing none of us wanted said:

"They have the box. They have the letters. Forty names, and it's sitting in Dario Vance's wheelhouse three kilometers downstream, and we're tied to a rotting corpse in the middle of a killing storm."

Nobody answered him. The wind answered instead, a long shriek through the Kestrel's broken windows, and I thought about my mother singing in this room, and about how she died waiting for a rescue that turned back, and about how her daughter had just watched the proof of her murder get carried away in a rich man's boat.

"Not everything," I said.

They all looked at me.

"Dario thinks the box is the inheritance. Papers, pensions, proof—that's what he took, and that's what he'll burn or bury or lock in a lawyer's drawer forever." I was shaking, and I let myself shake, because hiding it would have cost more strength than I had. "But my mother didn't hide everything in the strongbox. She hid things in *places*. She was a cook. Cooks don't trust safes. Cooks trust kitchens."

Ulyana's head came up slowly. In the lamplight, with the storm screaming outside and the Kestrel groaning around us like a ship remembering how she died, she looked at me with an expression I couldn't name yet—but I'd see it again, later, and understand it. It was the look of a woman realizing the river wasn't finished with either of us.

"The galley," she said.

"The galley," I said. "Below decks. Where the flooding never reached the upper cabinets, because my mother was smarter than the river."

Lightning whitewashed the world. Through the broken windows, downstream, past the rain, I could just make out the far-off glow of Dario Vance's riding lights at the bend—safe, smug, and holding forty years of stolen proof.

The storm was going to be the worst of the season tonight. Which meant that tomorrow, or whatever passed for tomorrow in this drowned cathedral, Dario's men would be cold, wet, miserable, and certain they'd already won.

I put my hand flat against the Kestrel's rusted bulkhead, the way you'd touch a grave, or a door.

"Hold on, Mama," I whispered. "I'm coming below."
