## 18. Storm Fight

The monsoon's worst hour arrived the way the worst hours always do: without announcement, without mercy, and with a certain theatrical flair that suggested the sky had been rehearsing.

I had flown through weather like this. Not in this — no pilot with a functioning survival instinct flies *in* this — but through it, threading cells that meteorologists described with words like "anomalous" and "please reconsider." And here was the thing about flying through weather that wants to kill you: you stop thinking of it as an enemy. An enemy you can hate. Weather you can only read. You read it the way you'd read a river, which is to say the way you'd read a liar — watching for the tells, the seams, the places where the surface says one thing and the current says another.

I stood at the wheel of our patched, exhausted barge and read.

The rain came sideways now, in ropes thick as my wrist. The side-channel where we'd beached after the hull gave way had become a brown torrent overnight, and beyond its mouth the Sable River itself had stopped pretending to be a river. It was a sea with opinions. Somewhere out in that chaos rode Dario Vance's boat, carrying my father's compass case, Sable's mother's strongbox, and enough armed men to make the arithmetic very simple.

They had taken everything at anchor two hours ago. They had left us alive because dead witnesses are inconvenient and drowning us would have been free anyway, courtesy of the storm. Dario had even smiled when he said it. "The river will do the paperwork," he told me, pressing the strongbox under his arm like a man collecting a prize at a raffle.

I had watched his boat wallow away into the rain and felt something I hadn't felt since my father's funeral. Not grief. Grief is soft. This was something with edges.

"Ulyana." Bea appeared beside me, soaked through her oilskins, hair plastered flat, shouting over the wind out of habit rather than necessity because at some point in the last hour the wind had gotten loud enough that shouting felt redundant. "Tell me we're not doing what your face says we're doing."

"What does my face say?"

"It says you're going to fly a boat."

"I'm going to *pilot* a boat. There's a difference. Boats have more inertia and fewer instruments."

"They also have a hull we patched with resin and prayer."

"Then we'll add a third ingredient." I looked back toward the stern, where Tomas was lashing down the last of anything not already lashed, and where Sable stood at the rail staring into the rain with an expression I was beginning to know by heart. It was the expression of someone reading a letter from the dead.

"Sable," I called. She turned. Rain ran off her chin. "You've been on Dario's boat. You saw how she sits in the water."

"Heavy aft," she said immediately. "He loaded the salvage gear wrong. He's riding stern-low, and he doesn't know what that means in a following sea."

"And?"

"And he's heading for the narrows above Kestrel Bend because it's the only sheltered water on this stretch, and he thinks the storm gives him cover." Her eyes came up to mine, and there it was — the thing underneath the grief, the thing that had gotten her aboard my boat under a false name in the first place. Fury. The patient kind. The kind that has waited twenty years and can wait three more hours if it must. "He thinks the storm is his."

"The storm isn't anyone's," I said. "That's the whole point of storms. But he's flying blind in conditions he doesn't understand, and I do. Bea — engines?"

Bea closed her eyes as though consulting a deity she didn't believe in. "Port engine is held together with spite. Starboard is actually fine. Rudder response is... present."

"Present is all I need. Tomas!"

Tomas came forward hand over hand along the rail, a big man made bigger by rain gear, wearing the face of a man who has done the math on his life expectancy and found the sum insulting. "Captain."

"We're going after him."

A pause. Rain drummed the deck like applause from a hostile audience. Then Tomas grinned — that slow, crooked grin that had gotten him thrown out of at least two river towns — and said, "Finally. I was worried you'd make us sit here being sensible."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about ramming another vessel in a monsoon: the hard part isn't courage. Courage is cheap. Courage is what you feel in the first thirty seconds, before your body understands what you've volunteered it for. The hard part is *reading* — because the river in flood is not one current but forty, braided and fighting each other, and every one of them is trying to write your ending for you.

I had spent fifteen years reading air. A thermal, a downdraft, a wind shear hiding behind a ridge line — they all leave signatures, if you've trained yourself to see them. Water leaves them too. Where two currents shear against each other, the surface goes glassy-smooth in a long seam, like a scar. Where water piles over a submerged bar, it stands up in a hump that looks like calm and means violence. And where the current runs backward along an outside bend — the eddy that every river pilot fears and every smart one uses — the rain itself falls differently, pocked and stuttering, hitting water that's moving against it.

I steered by the rain. It sounds mad. It worked.

"Two points to starboard," I said, and Bea fed the starboard engine, and the barge swung her blunt nose out of the eddy and into the main current, and the river took us like a hand taking a card it intends to play.

The first ten minutes were the worst of my life, and I include the time I landed a medical flight on a road while the patient's family waved lanterns and the road turned out to have a truck on it. We surfed standing waves. We took green water over the bow that knocked Tomas off his feet and sent a crate of Bea's tools skating across the deck like frightened animals. Twice the hull groaned in a register I did not want to hear from a patched hull, and both times Bea put her palm flat against the deck plating like a doctor palpating a patient and then gave me a nod that meant *she holds.*

"She holds," Bea shouted, unprompted, the third time. "But she's judging us. Both of us."

"Good. Let her judge. Judgment keeps a hull honest."

Ahead, through a tear in the rain, I saw him. Dario's boat — a proper steel-hulled river cruiser, twice our tonnage, riding exactly as Sable had predicted: stern-heavy, sluggish, wallowing in the troughs like a drunk trying to remember where he lived. He'd reached the mouth of the narrows and slowed, hugging the sheltered water inside the bend, confident as a man who believes the universe has finally started paying him what he's owed.

Dario Vance had been owed things his whole life. It was, I reflected, the central tragedy of him. Some people inherit money. Dario inherited entitlement, and he'd spent forty years trying to cash it.

"He'll have a watch posted," Sable said at my shoulder. She had come forward without my noticing, which was becoming a habit I'd have to decide whether to forgive. "Bow, probably. One man. The rest will be below or under cover."

"One man I can handle. What I can't handle is him seeing us early and making the narrows. In there, he knows the water and I don't, and then this becomes his fight instead of mine."

"So don't give him time to see you."

"That's the plan."

"That's not a plan, that's a wish with a schedule."

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in the teeth of a storm, and heard Sable make a small startled sound beside me, as if she'd caught a glimpse of whatever my father had seen in me once, before the river took him and I locked that person in a box with the compass. "Sable. When you were a kid on the Kestrel, did your mother ever tell you what your name means?"

"It's not my real name."

"No. But it's the one you chose, and chosen names count double. Sable. Black water. The color a river turns when it's deep enough and fast enough that light can't get a grip on it." I pointed the wheel where I wanted the bow to go. "That's what we're going to be for the next four minutes. Light won't get a grip on us either."

* * *

The approach was the flying part. That's the only way I can describe it, and I've tried, lying awake at night since, to find better words, and there aren't any. You commit. You trim. You read the seams in the current and put your hull on them the way you'd put a wing on a thermal's edge — close enough to use it, never so close that it owns you. The rain fell so hard it became a texture, a grain, and I flew the barge through that grain with both hands loose on the wheel, loose because tight hands die in weather, tight hands fight the medium instead of joining it.

My father taught me that. Loose hands. He taught me on the Sable River route, in fact, years ago, in a smaller boat, in weather half this bad, laughing at me while I white-knuckled the tiller. *You're not wrestling it, Uli. You're dancing with something that doesn't know the steps yet.* He'd been dead eleven years and he was still teaching me, and I hated how much that helped, and I loved it too, and there wasn't time for either feeling so I put them both in the same pocket and flew.

Dario's watchman saw us with maybe ninety seconds to spare. I know because that's how long it took for the shout, the scramble, the single lantern swinging wildly on the other boat's bow — and by then the geometry was already settled, because I'd picked my line not for stealth but for the current. The eddy along the bend ran backward, upstream, right along Dario's sheltered water. I brought the barge in on the seam between his slack water and the main current, and the river did the rest, swinging our heavy patched nose around his stern-light quarter like a door closing.

"Brace!" I roared, and Bea reversed the port engine to soften the hit, and we struck Dario's boat just forward of his stern wheel with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat.

Not a killing blow. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this, if anyone does. I angled it, I throttled it, I hit him the way you hit a man in a bar fight — hard enough to end the argument, not the man. His stern slewed. His crew went down in a tangle of tarps and cargo netting. Our rails kissed and ground, and in the grinding gap between the two hulls, in the rain and the roar, I threw the first line myself.

"Board!" Tomas was already moving, a coil of rope over his shoulder, and Bea had a fire axe for reasons she declined to explain, and Sable — Sable went over the gap before any of us, landing on Dario's deck light as an accusation.

* * *

What happened next took four minutes and will take longer than four minutes to finish telling, but here is the shape of it.

Dario's men were river thugs, not fighters; the storm had scared the aggression out of them, and Tomas's size plus Bea's axe plus the sheer deranged momentum of our arrival settled most of the question before it was asked. Two of them sat down in the scuppers and stayed there, hands up, looking almost relieved. The watchman decided his career lay elsewhere. That left the wheelhouse, and Dario, and the strongbox lashed to the wheelhouse rail with a cargo strap, because Dario trusted nothing — not the weather, not his men, and certainly not the river he'd spent his whole life underestimating.

I came through the wheelhouse door with the rain behind me and found him braced against the chart table, older-looking than he'd been a week ago, as if the storm had been sanding him down. He had a pistol in his hand. Of course he did. Dario always had a document for every occasion, and a pistol was just a document with a faster signature.

"Ulyana." He tried for regret and got somewhere near indigestion. "This is theft. That cargo is contractually—"

"The contract died when your second boat started shadowing us at Devil's Elbow," I said. "You used me to find the wreck. My father's charts, my father's route, my father's grave — and you were going to let the river do the paperwork. Your words, Dario. I've had two hours to appreciate them."

"You're a pilot, not a salvager. You were never going to survive this business. I was *offering* you a graceful exit."

"You were offering yourself my inheritance and calling it generosity." I took one step closer. The pistol tracked me, and I watched his hand, not his eyes — hands tell the truth, eyes audition. "Put it down. Not because I'm brave. Because look at your own deck. Look at the water coming over your transom. You're riding stern-heavy in a rising river in the worst cell of the season, and every minute you spend pointing that gun at me is a minute neither of us is bailing."

Something flickered in his face — the arithmetic landing, the dawning awareness that the storm had not been his cover but his sentence. And in that flicker, Sable moved.

She came through the doorway on Dario's blind side, soaked, small, unstoppable, holding her mother's journal open in both hands, pages rippling in the wet wind. She didn't grab for the gun. She didn't need to. She held the book up between them like a lamp.

"Read it," she said. Her voice didn't shake. Twenty years of waiting had burned all the shaking out of it. "Page forty-one. My mother's handwriting. She wrote down what the owners ordered the night before the Kestrel went down — the scuttling order, the names, the amounts. The cargo was never stolen, Dario. It was *sunk on purpose*, to cheat the crew of their pay, and your father signed the order, and your family has spent two decades calling honest drowned people thieves." She stepped closer, journal out, rain spotting the ink of words written by a woman who had died waiting for a rescue that profit had canceled. "And my mother wrote down something else. She wrote that the pilot who turned back that night — Eriksen — turned back because he'd found the truth and was going upriver to prove it. He wasn't fleeing the job, Dario. He was finishing it. Your family's fortune is built on a lie, and this book is the receipt, and the river kept it safe until tonight."

The pistol sagged. Just a few degrees. Just enough.

I could tell you I felt triumph. I didn't. I felt the storm lean on the wheelhouse, and I heard the water slopping over Dario's transom with new urgency, and I understood with terrible clarity that the fight was over and the emergency was not. Dario's boat was dying under him, stern-first, exactly as Sable had read it, and the narrows above Kestrel Bend were about to collect their toll.

"Dario," I said, and for the first time in our entire acquaintance I used the voice I used with frightened passengers. "The gun. Now. Because in about six minutes, you and I and everyone on this deck are going to be swimming, and I'm the only one here who can read this water well enough to get any of us out of it. Help me save your crew, and I'll let the courts have you instead of the river. It's a better offer than you deserve. It's the only one you're getting."

He looked at the gun. He looked at the journal. He looked, last of all, past us both, through the streaming wheelhouse glass, at the black furious water standing up in humps over the bar downstream — water that had stopped being a river and become a verdict.

The pistol clattered onto the chart table.

And that was when Tomas's voice came down from the bow, raw with a note I had never heard him use, cutting through the rain like a flare:

"Ulyana! The strongbox strap — it's gone — the box went over the side —"

I turned to the glass. Downstream, where the current tore past the bar, something small and iron-dark vanished into the seam between two currents, into the black water that light can't grip, and the river closed over it the way a ledger closes over a debt.

Sable made a sound beside me that I will hear for the rest of my life.

"Hold on to something!" I shouted, grabbing the wheel as the storm shoved the whole world sideways. "All of you — hold on! Nothing's finished yet!"
