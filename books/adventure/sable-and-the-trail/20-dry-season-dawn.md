## 20. Dry Season Dawn

I woke to the sound of the river leaving.

That's not a thing most people know exists, but anyone who's spent a monsoon on the Sable will tell you: a river in flood has a voice like a crowd, and when it starts to go, it goes the way a crowd does after the last train — thinning, muttering, embarrassed about all that shouting. I lay on my bunk in the engine room with my ear against the hull plating and listened to the waterline drop an inch, then two, then enough that I could hear Tomas snoring on deck for the first time in nine days without the rain eating him alive.

Then I got up, because that's what I do. Things break. I fix them. The universe has never once seen fit to explain why this arrangement should be mine, but here we are.

The barge was a crime scene. There's no other word for it. We'd beached her in that side-channel with half her fuel gone and her port hull opened up like a tin of sardines somebody lost a fight with, and everything we'd done since had been triage. My patch job on the breach was held together with epoxy, two steel plates, a section of fuel drum, and — I want this on the record — forty-one of Ulyana's medical supply straps, which she agreed to sacrifice with the expression of a woman donating a kidney. The welds were ugly. The seams wept. But she floated, and after the week we'd had, floating felt like a personality trait worth celebrating.

I made coffee on the little spirit stove and went out on deck to take inventory of the morning.

The sky was doing something obscene. After nine days of monsoon gray — a gray so total you forgot other colors were legal — the east had gone the color of a peach left too long in the sun, and the clouds were tearing apart into long ragged banners, and underneath it all the river ran brown and fast but *lower*, visibly lower, the drowned trees along the banks lifting their heads out of the water one by one like swimmers surfacing.

Tomas was asleep in the pilothouse chair with his boots on the rail and his hat over his face. Sable sat on the roof of the wheelhouse with her knees drawn up, watching the water go down with an expression I couldn't read, mostly because I'd stopped trying around day four. She wasn't a puzzle anymore. She was just a person who'd grown up on a boat that was now a grave downstream, and some mornings a person like that needs to sit on a roof and not be read.

Ulyana came out of the cabin last, hair wet from washing in the rain barrel, carrying the repaired radio handset like it might bite her. It had taken us three days and most of my dignity to rebuild the antenna from wire salvaged off Dario's abandoned skiff, and the first thing it gave us was the confession.

You could hear it again if you wanted. The station at Fort Meridian had recorded it, replayed it twice on the morning band while the whole interior listened. Dario Vance, soaked and hollow-eyed on our aft deck, telling a radio operator four hundred miles away that he'd scuttled nothing himself, no — but that he'd known. That his father had known. That the Kestrel's Promise was sunk on purpose by her owners in '61 to cheat twenty-two crew members out of wages and shares, and that the "stolen cargo" story was invented before the water even closed over the decks, and that Ulyana Eriksen's father had found out and been ruined for it, run off the river, hounded into debt and silence until the river finished what the owners started.

He said it flat. No performance. That was the worst part — Dario Vance, who could sell a drowning man a life vest at markup, didn't perform a single syllable. He just stood there with the rain coming off his chin and told the truth like a man paying a bill he'd been dodging for thirty years.

And then the operator read back the confirmation: the debt claim against Eriksen Air & River, secured against the airstrip lease, was void pending review. Fraudulent instrument. Both names cleared — the father who supposedly stole, and the daughter who supposedly inherited the stealing.

Ulyana hadn't said much since. She'd thanked me for the coffee yesterday, which is how I knew she was either very happy or very close to falling apart, and with Ulyana those have always been the same weather system.

"Bea." She held up the handset. "Meridian. They're asking if we need anything."

"Tell them a new hull," I said. "And a haircut. And for someone to explain to me why every time this crew leaves port, something structural suffers."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she keyed the handset and asked instead about water levels on the lower trail, and channel markers, and whether the Meridian dock crane was working, because ours wouldn't lift anything heavier than a bad mood for a month.

* * *

We got underway at noon, which by river standards is practically dawn, and by our standards was a miracle requiring three arguments and a prayer to the starter motor.

The trail was open. That was the thing none of us quite trusted. For eleven days the upper Sable had been a single churning brown throat, and now it was a river again — wide, lazy, littered with debris and deadfall and one entire floating outhouse that Tomas insisted on saluting as we passed. The channel markers were mostly gone or moved; the ones that survived leaned at angles suggesting they'd seen things. So we went slow, Sable standing at the bow calling depths from memory, because it turned out the girl had the whole trail mapped behind her eyes from childhood runs on the Kestrel, and there was something in the way she called the turns — *sandbar shifting left, give it a boat-length wide* — that sounded less like navigation and more like greeting old neighbors.

I spent the afternoon below, doing the honest work. Pumped bilges. Repacked the shaft seal that had been screaming at me since the rapids. Re-torqued every bolt on my patch and added three more plates where the weeping had gotten philosophical. My hands hurt. My back hurt. There was a bruise on my ribs from the night the hull gave way that had turned a color not found in nature, and every time I laughed it reminded me I shouldn't.

But here's the thing I kept coming back to, elbow-deep in grease: the leak was holding. My ugly, blasphemous, strap-and-drum patch was *holding*. Nine days ago I'd welded it in rising water with the river trying to eat us, and it had held through the worst storm front in a decade, and now it was going to hold us home. There's a feeling in that. People think fixing things is a small life. It isn't. When everything else fails — contracts, fathers, rivers, men like Dario Vance — the bolt you torqued properly still holds. I've built my entire character on that principle and I regret nothing.

Tomas stuck his head down the hatch around midafternoon. "Radio says the lease office reopened the strip file. Provisional. Pending the review."

"Provisional," I repeated. "Meaning?"

"Meaning nobody's evicting us this week."

"That's the best sentence anyone's said to me since the monsoon, and I include the confession in that."

He grinned and disappeared, and I heard him and Sable arguing cheerfully up top about whether the Kestrel's old pilot had really once backed her through Horseshoe Bend out of spite or whether that was a story that improved with each retelling. From the sound of it, Sable was winning, which meant the story was true.

* * *

Evening came down gold and enormous. We tied up at a bend where the bank was high and dry for the first time in weeks, and Ulyana built a fire out of driftwood while Tomas fished with the patience of a man who'd learned it from a grandmother and the skill of a man who'd inherited none of it.

I sat on a fuel drum with my coffee and watched Ulyana feed the fire. She'd been quiet all day, but it was a different quiet than the one she'd carried out of Meridian at the start of the season. That quiet had been a wall. This one was more like... a settled thing. A woman sitting still because she can, not because she's hiding.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm inspecting. You've got a crack in your composure and I want to know if it's structural."

That got a real laugh out of her, short and surprised, and then she looked at me across the fire with an expression I'd only seen on her once before — the night she told us about the creditors buying the lease, when she thought she was about to lose everything and was deciding how to say it.

"My father used to fix things," she said. "Before the flying. Before any of it. He said a repair done right outlives the reason you made it."

"He sounds like a wise man."

"He sounds like you." She poked the fire. "The compass case. I finally opened it. There were charts inside — his route to the real hold. He wasn't running from the job, Bea. He was finishing it. He reached the strongbox during the flood and sealed it shut and left it, because there were people drowning and he chose them." Her voice caught on the last word, just barely, and smoothed itself out. "Thirty years everyone said he abandoned that boat. He was the only one who stayed."

I didn't say anything clever. I'm capable of it, occasionally, but there are moments where cleverness is just noise, and this was one. I passed her the coffee instead, and she took it, and we watched the fire eat the driftwood down to coals while upstream the river ran clean and quiet toward morning.

"The debt's void," I said eventually. "The name's clear. What happens to you now? A woman with no debts and no enemies. Genuinely don't know what you'll do with yourself. Might get boring."

"Boring," she said, "would be a luxury I intend to abuse thoroughly." But she was smiling at the fire, and the smile had weight to it, the kind that stays.

Sable came and sat with us later, after Tomas finally caught — I am not exaggerating — one fish, which he presented to the group like a trophy head. She didn't talk much. She just sat close to the fire, closer than she'd sat to anyone in two weeks, and when Ulyana shifted to make room, Sable let her, and that small ordinary thing seemed to mean more to both of them than the whole broadcasted confession combined.

Above us the stars came out hard and cold, the way they only do after rain scrubs the sky, and somewhere south the Kestrel's Promise sat in her flooded grave with her secrets finally told, and I thought: well. Some wrecks you raise. Some you just make peace with.

* * *

We made the lower reaches by the third day, and that's where the river showed us what the flood had cost everyone else.

A ferry aground on a bar, its crew waving us down, grateful for fuel and a working pump. A village dock rebuilt overnight out of whatever the water had given back. Kids swimming in the shallows around a half-sunk canoe, shrieking like the monsoon had been a gift. Word traveled ahead of us the way it always does on the river — faster than any boat — and at the second dock, an old woman pressed a bag of oranges into Ulyana's hands and said, "For your father's girl," and Ulyana stood there holding fruit like it was a medal, unable to speak, while Tomas pretended very hard to check a mooring line.

By the time the Meridian bluffs rose out of the haze on the fifth morning, the barge was limping in earnest — the patch had developed opinions, the port engine was running on stubbornness alone, and I had a list of repairs as long as my arm and twice as expensive. None of it mattered. The whole strip crew was on the dock waiting, and Bea — fine, fine, people call me that too, it's a long story involving a naming accident and my own stubbornness — anyway, the whole town was there, and when we bumped the tires they cheered like we'd brought back treasure.

In a way we had. Just not the kind that clinks.

The letters from the strongbox — the ones Sable's mother had hidden, the pension records, the wage ledgers proving what the owners did — were already with the surveyors in Meridian, authenticated and moving through channels that actually had power. The crew pensions would be restored. The descendants would be paid. And the airstrip lease, the fraudulent debt dissolved, was ours again — ours meaning Ulyana's, meaning the crew's, meaning, if you believed the way she'd started saying it lately, *everyone who'd earned a bunk*.

I stood at the rail while the dock lines came aboard, and I watched Ulyana step onto solid ground for the first time in a month, and I watched her stop. Not collapse. Not celebrate. Just stop, and look back at the barge — at the patched hull, the scarred deck, the whole battered honest wreck of a vessel — and then at the people on it, and something in her face unclenched that I suspect had been clenched since she was nineteen years old.

Tomas elbowed me. "She's going to cry."

"She absolutely is not."

"She's going to—"

"If you say it louder, you'll cause it, and then I'll have to hurt you, and my ribs are in no condition."

But she didn't cry. She laughed — one short, disbelieving bark of it — and turned to Sable, who was standing beside her with her mother's journal under her arm, and said something too quiet for the dock to hear. Whatever it was, Sable nodded like a woman accepting a job offer.

Which, I'd learn by sundown, was exactly what it was.

Because that evening, while I was already elbow-deep in the port engine with the whole strip's worth of broken machinery stacked around me waiting its turn, Ulyana climbed down into the engine room, sat on an oil drum like she owned it — which, legally speaking, she now did — and said:

"So. The barge needs a new name."

"It has a name."

"It has a name nobody chose and everybody forgets. Sable and I were talking. Two families' names. Both boats. Both crews." She paused, watching me expect resistance. "We're repainting her."

I wiped my hands slowly, giving the moment the gravity it deserved. "You want me to approve a paint scheme."

"I want you to approve a paint scheme."

"Fine. But I pick the colors, and if anyone suggests beige I'm scuttling her myself."

Ulyana grinned — the full one, the rare one, the one that had apparently come home with us from upriver — and headed for the ladder. Halfway up she stopped, and looked back down at me, and there was something new in her face. Something not yet settled.

"Oh," she said, almost casually, too casually. "One more thing. The surveyors found a second strongbox bolted under the Kestrel's engine room floor. Sealed. Stamped with my father's initials."

The engine room went very quiet. Even the bilge pump seemed to hold its breath.

"And?" I said.

"And it opens tomorrow," Ulyana Oyelaran said, and climbed out into the light.
