## 21. Inheritance

The letters came home in a plastic evidence bag, which struck me as the river's idea of a joke. Everything my father died for, everything Sable's mother hid in a galley wall for thirty years, everything Dario Vance broke his own boat and his own name trying to steal—and it arrived at the airstrip looking like someone's confiscated groceries.

Bea carried it up from the barge like it was a sleeping child. Tomas walked behind her with the repaired radio antenna over his shoulder like a fishing rod, because he couldn't stand to come back empty-handed and that was the only thing left worth carrying. And Sable—Sable stood on the dock for a long moment before she stepped off, looking up the trail behind us as if she expected the Kestrel's Promise to surface one more time and ask if anyone had seen her mother.

The monsoon had gone out with the tide of it, the way storms do when they've finished their business with you. The sky over the strip was doing that washed-blue thing it does after weeks of drowning, so bright it felt rude.

"Eleven days," Bea said, setting the bag on my desk with a click that sounded far too final for plastic. "We were out there eleven days."

"I counted," I said. "I was there."

"You know what I mean."

I did. Eleven days is nothing in the ledger of a life and everything in the ledger of a boat. She'd earned the right to say it twice.

* * *

The authentication took six weeks, because bureaucracies are the only force in nature more patient than water.

The letters came out of the strongbox dry—not a miracle, just good tin and better luck—and they were exactly what Sable's mother's journal said they'd be: wage ledgers, signed contracts, and a letter of protest from the Kestrel's crew, dated nine days before she went down, accusing the owners of loading her heavy with ballast disguised as cargo and planning to scuttle her on the lower bend for the insurance. Nine signatures. Nine families who'd been told for three decades that their people were thieves who'd stolen their own wages and drowned running.

The pension board's man came out to the strip in a government jeep that bottomed out twice on our road, and he sat at my desk—the same desk where Dario once spread his contract out like a winning hand—and he read every page twice through his little spectacles.

"These are authenticated?" he asked.

"They're notarized by the river," I said. "It doesn't lie. It just keeps things."

He didn't laugh. Government men never do until the third meeting, and we weren't there yet.

What he did do, eventually, after the archives confirmed the signatures and the insurance records surfaced and some clerk in the capital found the original scuttling order filed under the wrong year by a man who had presumably been paid to misfile it—that clerk, whoever he was, deserves a statue—was sign the restoration order. Back pensions for all nine crews' families, indexed to thirty years of interest. The lease on our strip, which the creditors had bought with money Dario fronted them as part of his elegant little trap, voided and returned. And a formal clearing of the names of every soul who'd sailed the Kestrel's Promise, including one Aksel Eriksen, pilot, who had reached the wreck first during the disaster and chosen to pull swimmers out of the water instead of diving for the strongbox, and who had spent his last years being called a coward and a thief by people who'd never been wet past the ankles.

I read that paragraph four times. Then I folded it into my father's compass case, next to his charts, and closed the lid, and didn't cry until later, alone, in the hangar, where nobody could make it mean anything.

* * *

The creditors' man came by on a Tuesday, because creditors always come on Tuesdays; I think it's in their training manual, between *smile insincerely* and *ruin breakfast*.

He was a small neat person named Oduya with a clipboard and the expression of a man who'd been sent to collect a debt from a flood. He looked at the void order, then at me, then at the void order again, as if one of us might blink.

"The debt was secured against this property," he said.

"The debt was fraudulently induced," I said. "Dario Vance advanced the money to buy your paper specifically so you could squeeze me onto that river. There's a sworn confession on tape, a government investigation, and a warrant with his name on it. Your security interest was fruit of a poisoned tree, Mr. Oduya. The tree's been cut down. Go home."

He looked at Bea, who was standing in the hangar doorway holding a wrench in a way that suggested she'd been waiting years for a legal pretext. He looked at Tomas, who smiled the smile of a man who'd survived eleven days on a leaking barge with a torn antenna and considered this a light afternoon.

Oduya gathered his clipboard with dignity. "You'll be hearing from our office."

"We don't have a radio anymore," Tomas said helpfully. "Well. We do. But it only receives confessions."

I'll give him this—he almost laughed on the way out. Almost. First crack in the armor. By the third visit, they're putty.

* * *

Here's the part I keep turning over, though. The part I didn't expect.

When the pension board finished, there was a residual. Salvage law is strange and old and mostly written by people who assumed the salvager would be a gentleman with a yacht, but the short version is: the crew who brought up the letters were entitled to a finder's share of the recovered estate. Not the gold—there never was gold, or rather there was, once, and it went down with the owners' second boat when the insurance investigators started asking questions, which is a story Sable tells better than I do—but the estate, the settled value, the restored funds. A percentage. It came to a number with enough zeros behind it that Bea whistled when she saw it, and Bea once rebuilt an engine during a hailstorm without swearing, so her whistles are calibrated instruments.

There was a form. There's always a form. One signature, and the airstrip would have new hangars, a second plane, a fuel farm that didn't leak, and I'd never have to fly medicine runs in a machine held together by prayer and Bea's opinions ever again.

I sat with the pen for a long time.

Sable watched me from across the office, pretending to read a supply manifest upside down, which fooled neither of us.

"You're going to sign it," she said finally.

"I'm deciding whether to sign it."

"You decided three minutes ago. You've just been staring at the pen like it owes you money." She set the manifest down. "Sign it, Ulyana."

"It's not mine." I said it and heard how it sounded—like a vow, like a wall, like my father's voice coming out of my mouth wearing my accent. "None of it. The letters weren't ours. The truth wasn't ours. We just carried it home. You don't get paid for carrying the truth. That's not how it works."

"That's exactly how it works," Sable said. "Carrying things is the whole job. Ask any pilot." She came around the desk and turned the form to face me. "But fine. Don't sign it for yourself."

I looked at her.

"Sign it as trustee," she said. "Found-family trust. You, me, Bea, Tomas. Four names. The money goes into keeping the strip alive, keeping the flights cheap for the villages, keeping a berth open for anybody the river spits out who needs one. You don't take a cent. Nobody takes a cent. But you sign, because if you don't, the number sits in a government account earning two percent and dying of loneliness, and that would be a worse grave than the one we just dug Dario in."

I stared at her. "How long have you been thinking about this?"

"Since the hull split," she said. "You patched a boat with tarpaulin and stubbornness for people you'd known a week. I figured out what kind of trust you run pretty early." She tapped the line. "Four names. Or five, if you count the compass."

"The compass doesn't sign."

"The compass has been signing your decisions for twenty years. Give it a line."

So I signed. Ulyana Eriksen, trustee. And beneath it, in handwriting that got steadier as it went: Beatrice Okonkwo, Tomás Ferreira, Mara Sable Voss. Bea signed with a grease pencil because she refused to stop working long enough to find a pen. Tomas signed and then drew a small boat under his name, because he is Tomas.

Nobody signed for themselves. That was the whole point, and it took a stranger three weeks to explain it to me, which either says something about strangers or something about me, and I've decided not to investigate which.

* * *

The night the papers were filed, we did what this crew does instead of celebrating properly: we worked. Bea torqued down the barge's patch plates for the last time and pronounced her "sound-ish," which from Bea is a knighthood. Tomas repainted the name on the bow—two names now, side by side, ERIKSEN LINE and, underneath in smaller letters, KESTREL'S PROMISE, because Sable had asked and none of us could think of a reason the river should forget.

I flew the evening med run to Halvorsen's landing with the window down and the air smelling like the end of a war. Old man Halvorsen met me at the dock with his insulin and his gossip, in that order, and told me the capital papers were calling Dario Vance's collapse "the salvage scandal of the decade," which pleased him enormously because he'd never trusted a man with a boat too clean.

"He'll do time?" Halvorsen asked.

"Some. Fraud, conspiracy, the scuttling order has his uncle's signature all over it and Dario's fingerprints on the payments. Enough." I shrugged. "Mostly he'll do worse. He'll be famous for losing."

Halvorsen chuckled and paid me in eggs and news, which is the local currency, and I took it, because a woman has to eat.

On the flight back, the sun going down orange behind the ridge where I'd once spotted Dario's second boat, I took my hand off the stick for a moment and let the compass case sit open on my knee. My father's needle swung steady north-northeast, toward the trail, toward the bend where the Kestrel lay quiet under her thirty years of silt, holding nothing now but the shape of everything she'd cost and given.

"You'd have hated the paperwork," I told him. "You'd have loved the ending."

The needle didn't answer. They never do. But it didn't waver either, and from him, that's agreement.

* * *

Sable was waiting on the dock when I tied down, sitting on a crate with her mother's journal on her knees, reading it the way other people pray.

"There's a letter in here," she said, without looking up, "we haven't opened yet."

My stomach did something complicated. "From the strongbox?"

"No. From the journal. Tucked in the back cover. Mama's writing, addressed to—" she turned the page around so I could see the inscription on the envelope, yellowed and soft at the corners, sealed these thirty years. "It's addressed to A. Eriksen."

The river went quiet. The whole strip went quiet. Even Bea's wrenching stopped, which I will swear to on any document you like.

"She wrote to him," Sable said. "After. After the wreck, after everyone said he turned back, after she waited on that bank. She wrote him a letter, and she never sent it, and I don't know why. Maybe she was angry. Maybe she was afraid. Maybe she was waiting for him to come explain himself." Sable looked up at me, and her eyes were doing the thing they do—steady on the surface, whole rivers underneath. "He can't read it now. But you can. Or we can. Together. If you want."

I looked at the envelope. At my father's name in a dead woman's careful hand. At the girl who grew up on a riverboat and a grudge and had somehow become the other half of my crew, my partner, my family—the inheritance I never knew I was owed.

"Tomorrow," I said, and my voice wasn't entirely steady. "At dawn. On the dock. Both of us."

Sable nodded once and put the journal away, and above us the first stars came out over the trail, and somewhere downriver the Kestrel kept her silence, and the letter waited, the way the river waits—for morning, and for the truth it still had left to tell.
