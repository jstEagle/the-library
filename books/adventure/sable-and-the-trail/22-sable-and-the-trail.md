## 22. Sable and the Trail

*Sable*

The barge came home on a Tuesday, which felt right, because Tuesdays were the day my mother used to say nothing good ever happened, and I wanted to prove her wrong one more time.

She'd have liked the sight of it, I think. The old girl limping down the last bend of the Sable with her patched hull showing like a scar she wasn't ashamed of, Tomas standing at the rail waving both arms at nobody in particular, Bea at the tiller with the expression of a woman who has personally defeated several rivers and intends to mention it at every opportunity for the rest of her natural life.

And Ulyana at the bow. Watching the water. Not flinching from it.

I'd watched her not-flinch for three days now, since we'd cleared the upper gorge and the river had gone wide and slow and forgiving, the way it does when it's decided to let you go. Every mile of it she'd stood there with that compass of her father's open in her palm, and every mile the needle had held steady, and every mile something in her shoulders had come down another notch, like a knot being worked loose by patient hands.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm admiring," I corrected. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Admiring is practically a compliment."

"Uh-huh." She turned, and the light caught her face, and I saw what three weeks on the river had done to it — burned off the wariness, or most of it, the way sun burns off morning fog. What was left underneath was younger than I'd expected. Or maybe just less tired. "You're going to be insufferable now, aren't you."

"Now?" Bea called from the tiller. "Now? Where you been, Eriksen?"

Tomas laughed so hard he nearly dropped the mooring lines he was coiling, which would have been a fitting end to the whole voyage — survived monsoon, rapids, ambush, and a sinking, then killed by a rope. He saved himself at the last second, mostly because Bea threw a cleat at him.

That's the thing about coming home that nobody warns you about: it isn't quiet. I'd spent so long imagining homecoming as some kind of stillness — the story ending, the credits rolling, everybody finally getting to stop — that the actual noise of it took me by surprise. The strip was half-ruined, the hangar roof sagged where the creditors' men had stripped the good tin, and the lease papers were a mess of legal language that would take a lawyer months to untangle even with Dario Vance's recorded confession doing the heavy lifting. And everybody was talking at once. Bea was already listing repairs. Tomas was already inventing reasons why the repairs required him to supervise rather than perform them. A couple of the strip families had come down to the bank to watch us dock, and they were shouting questions across the water, and somebody's dog was losing its mind on the shoreline, and Ulyana stood in the middle of all of it with her hands in her pockets and the strangest look on her face.

"What?" I asked her.

"Nothing." She shook her head slowly. "I keep waiting for it to feel like running. Coming back here. It always has before. And it just—" She gestured at the chaos, the dog, the shouting, the sagging hangar. "It doesn't."

"That's because you're not running anymore," I said. "You're arriving. Different direction entirely."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she'd started looking at me somewhere around the night camp — like I was a chart she was still learning to read but no longer suspected of being forged.

"So," she said. "You've got nowhere else to be."

It wasn't quite a question. It also wasn't quite an offer. Ulyana Eriksen did everything sideways; it was part of her charm, if you squinted.

"I've got exactly nowhere else to be," I said. "And a mother's journal that belongs in your father's file cabinet next to his charts, and a memory of the Kestrel's corridors that's apparently worth money now, and no boat, no family, and one change of clothes that smells like river."

"We could use a cook," Tomas offered. "Bea burns water."

"I do *not*—"

"The rice incident," Tomas said solemnly. "We swore never to speak of it, and yet here we are."

I laughed, and Ulyana laughed, and the dog on the shore barked as if it had been following the conversation and wanted to register its opinion, and that was how it was settled — not with a contract, not with a signature, but with laughter and burnt rice and a dog. Which, my mother would have told you, was the only way anything worth having ever got settled on the river.

* * *

*Ulyana*

My father kept his important things in a tin ammunition box under the floorboard of his bunk, and I'd hated that box my whole childhood, because it meant he expected to leave. Pilots who expect to stay keep their things out where they can see them. Pilots who expect to go pack them away where the next person won't find them.

I opened the box for the first time in eleven years on our third evening home, with Sable sitting cross-legged on the floor beside me and the lamp turned low, because some archaeology you want witnesses for and some you don't, and this was somehow both.

Inside: his spare logbook. A photograph of my mother I'd forgotten existed. A bundle of letters wrapped in oilcloth — the ones we'd salvaged from the strongbox, the ones that proved the Kestrel's crew had been cheated out of wages and pensions by men who'd scuttled their own ship to avoid paying, the letters that had already begun the slow work of clearing two names instead of blackening them. And beneath all of it, flat and worn soft at the corners, a folded page in his handwriting.

A route. His route. The one he'd flown the day he died — except now, with Sable's mother's journal open beside it, I could read it properly for the first time. He hadn't been fleeing the job. He'd been flying the supply run that might have reached the wreck in time, the run the owners had cancelled and lied about, and when he'd realized he couldn't make it through the storm, he'd turned back rather than die uselessly and leave me alone. The journal confirmed it. Two people, on opposite sides of the same disaster, each carrying half the truth for eleven years, each believing the worst about themselves.

"He was finishing it," Sable said quietly. "All along. He just ran out of river."

"Yeah." My voice came out rougher than I intended. I cleared my throat. "He was better at finishing things than I am. Took me eleven years and a flood."

"Took you eleven years and a flood," she agreed, "and here we are anyway. I think he'd count that as on schedule."

We put the letters in the strip's safe the next morning, and the lawyer came the week after, and the pension claims went in with both names attached — Eriksen and Oyelaran, side by side on the paperwork, which made Sable cry in a way she pretended was allergies and which fooled absolutely no one, least of all the dog.

The debt collectors took longer. Fraudulent instruments, the lawyer called them, purchased in bad faith from a man now facing charges, and when the letter came voiding the last of it, Bea nailed it to the hangar wall upside down, which she said was traditional, and which she had certainly just invented, and which I let stand because some traditions deserve to exist even when they're brand new.

* * *

*Sable*

We painted the barge on a Saturday, because Bea declared that hull work waited for no one and aesthetics could ride along for free.

The old name — *Meridian*, Ulyana's father's boat, named for a line you cross and can't uncross — was still legible under the grime, and Tomas wanted to sand it off entirely and start clean. Ulyana surprised all of us.

"Leave it," she said. "Add to it."

So we did. Bea mixed paint in three dented buckets while the morning heated up, and Tomas did the high work because he was the only one who didn't complain about ladders, and I did the lettering because my mother had taught me a steady hand — years of painting menu boards in the Kestrel's galley, chalk and later paint, *today's special* in letters tall enough to read across a dining room.

*MERIDIAN*, in the old white, weathered and proud. And beneath it, fresh and blue and slightly crooked because Tomas sneezed at the wrong moment:

*& OYELARAN.*

Two names. One line. Crossed together.

Ulyana stood on the bank looking at it for a long time, arms folded, head tilted, the way she looked at weather she hadn't finished deciding about.

"It's crooked," she said finally.

"It's *character*," Bea said.

"It's crooked," Ulyana agreed, "and it stays." And she picked up the brush and added a small compass rose at the stern, freehand, in gold, and her hand didn't shake once.

* * *

*Ulyana*

The medical flight was scheduled for the first Thursday of the dry season, and I stood in front of the flight board for a full minute before anyone noticed what I was doing.

The board listed the routes. Coastal run. Highland circuit. And there, at the bottom, written in my own handwriting from a year ago and boxed in red marker like a warning label: *SABLE RIVER ROUTE — NO.*

"Sable," I said.

"Mm?" She was inventorying the medicine crates, ticking items off with a pencil held in her teeth between words.

"I'm taking the river route tomorrow. The full run. All six stops."

The pencil stopped moving. She looked up at me, and I watched her decide not to make it a moment, which was the single kindest thing anyone had ever done for me.

"Good," she said. "Mrs. Okonkwo at the third landing will feed you until you beg for mercy. Pace yourself."

"I'm asking if you want to come."

"I know you're asking if I want to come." She went back to her list, but the corner of her mouth was doing something. "I'm letting you ask properly. You'll get better at it."

"I will push you in the river."

"You'll try."

She came, of course. We both knew she'd come. That was the thing about us by then — the asking and the answering had become a formality, like filing a flight plan. You did it because the rules required it, not because there was any real doubt about the destination.

The morning of the flight I took my father's compass out of its case and set it on the instrument panel where it had always belonged, and I flew the trail low and slow the way he would have, reading the water, reading the wind, the needle steady as a heartbeat under my thumb. At the second landing a kid chased the plane along the grass waving both arms, and at the fourth I delivered vaccines and gossip in equal measure, and at the fifth Mrs. Okonkwo fed me until I begged for mercy, exactly as forecast, while Sable watched with unconcealed delight and ate a second helping purely out of spite.

On the way home, with the evening going gold over the gorge, Sable leaned over from the passenger seat and tapped the compass.

"Steady," she observed.

"Steady," I agreed.

"You know," she said, settling back, watching the river unspool below us — *our* river now, in the way that a thing becomes yours by surviving it — "my mother used to say the Kestrel's Promise was cursed. That the river took it and everything after it was just fallout." She was quiet a moment. "I don't think that anymore. I think the promise just took its time arriving."

Below us the water ran broad and bright toward home, and the strip lights were coming on one by one, and Bea would have rice on — actual edible rice, she'd practiced, we'd all suffered through the practice batches — and Tomas was probably up on the hangar roof fixing the antenna badly, and the dog would lose its mind when the engine sounded, and none of it was quiet, and none of it was stillness, and all of it was mine.

Not inherited. Not owed. Mine the way a riverbed belongs to the river: because it stayed, and because the water chose it.

I brought us in smooth, into the last of the light, and the whole ridiculous loud loving lot of them came out to meet the plane, and I sat in the cockpit one extra second with my hand on the compass before I climbed down to them.

Homecoming, it turns out, isn't a place. It's a heading.

And for the first time in my life, mine pointed true.
