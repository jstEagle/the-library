## 1. The Blacklist

The freight was pig iron, and the mule was named Duchess, and between the two of them they had opinions about Silas Dunmore that he did not care to hear.

"Come on," he said, hauling on the lead rope. The dock at Harrow's Bend was a plank road slick with river mud, and the Kestrel ran brown and fast below it, carrying spring melt down from country Silas had sworn, in writing and out loud, never to enter again. "You've carried worse. You carried me last Tuesday."

Duchess turned her head and regarded him with one wet black eye, an eye that had seen through every lie Silas had told in the four months since he'd arrived in town broke as a church mouse. She did not believe him now either.

The foreman, a squat man called Ottery with a whistle on a lanyard and a voice like gravel in a bucket, cupped his hands and bellowed across the dock. "Dunmore! If that animal's still standing there when I count to ten, you're off the roster. And the roster's all there is."

There it was, the whole of Silas Dunmore's life, laid out by a man with a whistle: the roster was all there is.

He got the mule moving. It took nothing but patience and the particular shame of a man who had once guided parties over the Vane Glacier for paying clients who wore boots worth more than his boat, and who now hauled pig iron up a plank ramp for eleven coppers a day, minus the coppers Ottery skimmed for "dock maintenance." Nobody had ever explained what the dock maintenance consisted of. The dock had a hole in it you could lose a boot in, and Silas had, twice.

The morning went the way mornings went. Load, haul, unload, swear at the mule, get sworn at by Ottery, watch the river. By noon his shoulders had settled into their familiar ache and his mind had done what it always did when his hands were busy — gone upstream, against his will, into the high country where the Kestrel narrowed into white water and the mountains stood up around it like a jury.

Four months ago he'd come down out of those mountains with two dead clients and a reputation in ashes. The inquiry had been very thorough about everything except the truth. They'd asked about his route choice, his weather judgment, his rope work, and whether he'd been drinking — he hadn't, but the question told him what they'd already decided. The Aldervane Company had needed someone to blame for the Vane Glacier, and Silas Dunmore, mountain guide, son of nobody, man without family or money or friends in the right rooms, had been available. *Blacklisted from all concession territory,* the notice said, which meant every river, every range, every pass worth guiding anyone through. He could still walk in the low woods with fishermen if he liked. He could carry pig iron.

He could not go home to the mountains.

*Home.* He caught himself using the word and spat over the side of the dock, which earned him a look from Ottery and a sympathetic flick of the ears from Duchess.

The trouble with disgrace, Silas had learned, was not the hunger. Hunger was simple; you solved it with work or you didn't. The trouble was that the mountains didn't know about the blacklist. Every clear morning the peaks above the valley caught the first light and burned gold, and they looked exactly as beautiful as they had the day the inquiry read out its findings, and something in Silas's chest turned toward them like a compass needle, faithful and stupid, pointing at the thing that had ruined him because it was also the only thing he'd ever loved.

"You're staring at the water again," said a voice behind him. "Ottery says staring is half a copper."

Silas turned. Wren Okafor leaned on the rail of her tea stall at the head of the dock, a small round woman with grey braids and the unbothered air of someone who had buried two husbands and outlived every debt collector in three counties. Her stall sold tea, fried dough, gossip, and — unofficially — credit, which made her the most powerful institution in Harrow's Bend.

"I'm watching for hazards," Silas said. "Professional habit."

"On a dock." She poured him a cup without asking and held it out. "That'll be nothing, since you've got nothing. Drink. You look like a man whose luck is fixing to change, and I like to be present for those."

"My luck changed already. That's how I ended up owing you for four months of tea."

"And paying it back at a copper a week, regular as rain." Wren nodded with real approval. "Honest broke is rare these days. Most men who owe me money have the decency to skip town so I can enjoy being angry at them."

Silas drank the tea. It was terrible, over-steeped and bitter, and he loved it the way you love anything that has been given to you freely in a place where you expected contempt. Harrow's Bend had taken some getting used to that way. A border town full of boatmen, smugglers, freight handlers, and people running from various versions of their pasts, it operated on a simple principle: whatever you'd done, you'd done it somewhere else, and here you were judged by whether your rope held and whether you paid your debts. By that measure Silas was doing middling well. His rope held. His debts were current. His disgrace was nobody's business but his own, though everyone knew it anyway, because news traveled upriver faster than boats did.

It was Wren who heard things first, always. So when she set down her pot and went quiet in the particular way that meant a thought was loading itself behind her eyes, Silas felt the day shift under him before she said a word.

"You saw the Bureau boat this morning," she said. "Grey hull, official pennant, came through the narrows at dawn going like the devil owed it money."

"I saw it. Government boats don't stop here unless they're lost."

"It stopped." Wren folded her arms. "At the telegraph office. And the operator's boy came running past my stall twenty minutes ago heading for the Company house, and that boy runs slow for everything except news. So." She looked at Silas with an expression he distrusted immediately, because it was gentle. "Word from up the Kestrel. From the survey country."

The cup was warm in his hands. The river went on below them, brown and loud, coming down out of the exact country Wren was talking about, and Silas found he had stopped breathing in the ordinary way.

"The Aldervane survey," he said. His voice came out flat. "The mapping party. Vey's people."

"The same. Six weeks upriver, no word back, and now the Bureau's sending boats instead of letters." Wren shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "Could be nothing. Radios fail. Rivers flood. Men get lost."

"Men don't get lost on the lower Kestrel. Not survey men, not with their instruments." Silas set the cup down carefully, because his hands wanted to do something else with it. "Who's leading them?"

"That cartographer. The famous one. Oduya."

Emmanuel Oduya. Silas had met him once, years ago, at a Bureau function he'd attended because a client dragged him — a small precise man with ink-stained fingers who had asked Silas more questions about the upper Kestrel in one evening than the entire Bureau had asked in a decade. Had listened, too. Had written things down. *A good map needs a man who's walked the ground,* Oduya had said, and shaken Silas's hand like he meant it, and Silas had gone home that night feeling, briefly, like a professional instead of a curiosity.

Now Oduya was up there, six weeks silent, in the country that had eaten Silas's career whole.

"They'll want guides," Wren said. "They'll want them today. And every licensed guide within two hundred miles is going to say no, because the upper Kestrel's inside concession territory and the Company pays better than the Bureau does and nobody crosses the Aldervane Company on purpose."

"Except a man with nothing to lose."

"I wasn't going to say it like that."

"You were absolutely going to say it like that." Silas looked up the river, where the grey boat's wake had long since faded, and felt the old pull open in his chest like a door he'd nailed shut. Bitter pride said: *let them drown in their own paperwork. Let the Bureau that read out its findings in a cold room send somebody else.* Something older and less reasonable said: *Oduya shook your hand. Somebody's up there waiting on a rope that holds.*

"Dunmore!" Ottery's voice cracked across the dock. "You're paid through noon! Either load the next sledge or clear my dock!"

Silas looked at the sledge, at the pig iron, at the rest of his life arranged in neat humiliating rows. Then he looked at Duchess, who was watching him with that eye that missed nothing.

"He's thinking," Wren observed. "Watch this. This is better than the fights at the Anchor."

"I'm not thinking," Silas said. "I'm remembering that I swore an oath. In writing. Never to enter concession territory again."

"And oaths sworn to men who lied about you are just words you gave to thieves." Wren picked up her pot. "I don't tell you what to do. I just keep the tea hot and the accounts straight, and I note for the record that a man can be blacklisted from a valley. There's no list anywhere that keeps a valley from needing him."

Silas stood there while the river talked and the gulls argued and Ottery counted to ten in a voice that carried to the far shore. At ten, Ottery declared him off the roster, fired him with tremendous satisfaction, and told Duchess she was the hardest worker on the dock, which was true.

And Silas Dunmore, unemployed again, disgraced again, free in the way that only ruined men are free, picked up his coat and started walking toward the telegraph office — because the Bureau boat would put in at the Company dock until dusk, and because a man ought to at least hear the offer before he refused it.

He was halfway there when he admitted to himself, with the honesty he usually saved for 3 a.m., that he wasn't going to refuse it.

* * *

The Cartographic Bureau's launch sat against the Company pier like a cat at a dog show: grey paint, brass fittings, two crewmen on the gangway who looked at Silas's coat and decided in advance he was nobody. A clerk met him at the top of the ramp, a young man with a clipboard and the harried expression of someone who had spent all day telling people no.

"If you're here about the survey position, the positions are filled."

"I'm here about the route."

"We have qualified personnel for—"

"For the lower Kestrel, sure." Silas leaned on the rail, unhurried. "Ask your qualified personnel who's run the Sable Narrows at spring melt. Ask them who's portaged the falls above Quarry Bend. Ask them who knows which side of the valley floods and which side kills you slower. Then ask them why none of them will take this contract, and you'll be right back out here talking to me."

The clerk opened his mouth, closed it, and then, visibly deciding that his afternoon could not get worse, jerked his thumb toward the deck cabin. "She's in the aft cabin. Ilsa Vey. And sir — she's had a long day."

"So have the people upriver," Silas said, and went in.

The cabin was cramped and smelled of lamp oil and wet charts. Ilsa Vey stood over a table covered in maps weighted with brass instruments, and she did not look up for a long moment — long enough for Silas to study her. Fifty or so, silver at the temples, sleeves rolled, a woman built out of competence the way other buildings were built out of stone. When she finally raised her head, her eyes went over him the way his own eyes went over a mountain face: reading the lines, noting the weaknesses, calculating what would hold.

"Silas Dunmore," she said.

"You know me."

"I read the inquiry transcript twice. All four hundred pages." She said it without apology. "I also read the weather records for the Vane Glacier for that week, which the inquiry did not, because the inquiry was conducted by men who wanted it finished by Friday. Your route was sound. Your judgment was sound. You were ruined for reasons that had nothing to do with mountaineering, and we both know it, and neither of us can afford to say so out loud yet." She slid a chart across the table. "Sit down, Mr. Dunmore. I have six weeks of silence from a party led by the best cartographer alive, and a list of licensed guides who have all suddenly remembered urgent business elsewhere. Tell me what you see."

It was a test, and a good one, and Silas respected her for it even as something in him bristled. He bent over the chart. The Kestrel ran up the page like a vein, and there — marked in the careful faded ink of an older survey — lay the Narrows, the falls, the high valley, the glacier country beyond. The route that ended his career. The route he could draw from memory with his eyes shut, having dreamed it badly for four years.

"Six weeks is too long to be radio silence," he said. "Even allowing for equipment failure. They'd have sent a runner down to the forward camp. There's always a runner." He tapped the map. "Unless the forward camp isn't answering either. Is it?"

Vey's silence answered for her.

"When did anyone last confirm contact?"

"Nineteen days ago. A routine transmission. Since then, nothing." She laid a second sheet beside the first — a supply manifest, a crew list. Six names. Silas read them and felt the paper go heavy under his fingers. Emmanuel Oduya. Five others. "The Bureau is sending me up with a relief party as far as the boundary post. Beyond that, I need a guide who actually knows the upper river. The Company's enforcers patrol the concession, and they will not welcome a government vessel, so we move quietly and we move fast. Double pay. Full pardon of record upon completion — the blacklist struck, formally, publicly."

There it was. The thing dangled on the hook, gleaming. Four years of shame, erased for the price of one trip up a river that had already taken everything he had once.

"No contract ever saved anybody," Silas said quietly. "I've signed contracts before. Paper doesn't hold a rope."

"No," Vey agreed. "But it can hold a name. Yours is currently held underwater by men who enjoy the view." She pushed a pen across the table. "I won't pretend this is rescue, Mr. Dunmore. Nineteen days of silence means I'm likely asking you to help me recover bodies and burn company secrets. But Oduya is up there, and I am going after him, and I would very much rather do it with the one man in this territory who knows that river better than the river knows itself."

Silas looked at the pen. He thought of the inquiry room, the cold voices, the findings read aloud while he stood straight-backed and said nothing because saying anything would only prove how little they understood. He thought of Duchess, and Wren's terrible tea, and the roster that was all there is.

He thought of Oduya's hand, warm and brief, and the voice saying *a good map needs a man who's walked the ground.*

He picked up the pen.

"One condition," Silas said. "When we come back down — and I say *when*, because I don't plan on teaching your Bureau how to write obituaries — the record gets corrected. All of it. The Vane Glacier, the findings, the works. Publicly."

"Agreed."

"And I pick the route. Nobody overrides me on the water. Not you, not the Bureau, not God."

A flicker of something that might have been humor crossed Vey's face. "God doesn't have jurisdiction above the boundary post. Sign."

Silas signed. The pen scratched, the ink dried, and the contract sat on the table looking exactly as much like salvation as any piece of paper ever had, which is to say: not at all, and completely.

Outside, the light was going amber on the water, and somewhere up that brown loud river, six weeks into silence, a good man might still be waiting on a rope that holds.

Silas rolled the chart, tucked it under his arm, and went to find out how fast he could buy a boat.
