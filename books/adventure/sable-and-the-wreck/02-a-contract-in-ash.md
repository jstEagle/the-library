## 2. A Contract in Ash

The freight was salt pork and survey stakes, which struck Silas Dunmore as a joke the river itself might have written. He hauled it up the gangplank of the *Merry Adela* at first light, his shoulders doing the work his pride refused to acknowledge, and tried not to look at the Kestrel Road where it climbed out of Harrow's Bend toward the country that had ruined him.

He failed to not look. He always failed.

Six years. Six years since the Vane Glacier had eaten two of his clients and spat his name back downriver with the word *reckless* attached to it like a fishhook. Six years of hauling freight, breaking ice off water troughs, and drinking just enough at the Tin Lantern to remember why he'd stopped drinking more. The blacklist wasn't a document. It was a weather. It followed him into every hiring hall in three counties, and every foreman's face did the same small arithmetic when he walked in: strong back, bad name, next.

"Mind the corner, Dunmore," called the stevedore, a boy of maybe nineteen who didn't know he was talking to the man who'd once been paid simply to know things about mountains. "You'll crack a crate."

Silas set the crate down gently, out of spite, and straightened his back one vertebra at a time. That was when he saw her.

She stood at the foot of the gangplank in a traveling coat too fine for Harrow's Bend and boots too practical for the coat, as though she'd dressed for two different cities and split the difference. Gray hair cut short and unapologetic. A leather document case held against her ribs the way other people held a child. She was watching him with the particular patience of someone who had already decided how this conversation would go and was merely waiting for him to catch up.

"You're Dunmore," she said. Not a question.

"I'm busy."

"You're hauling pork." She glanced at the crate. "I'm told you used to be the best river-and-ridge man north of the capital. I'm also told you're the only one willing to say the word *Kestrel* without flinching, though flinching, I gather, is optional equipment for you."

Silas looked at her properly then. She had the kind of face that made foremen nervous — the kind that did its own arithmetic. "Who's telling you things?"

"The Cartographic Bureau tells me things. It's my job to be told things." She extended a gloved hand. "Ilsa Vey. Deputy Director of Expeditions, which is a grand title meaning I sign the papers and take the blame."

He didn't take the hand. She let it hang there a moment, then folded it away without offense, which he noted, and resented noting.

"I know what happened on the Kestrel," she said. "The Aldervane survey went silent eleven days ago. Nine people. My senior cartographer among them — Edric Oduya."

Something moved behind Silas's ribs, old and unwelcome. Oduya. He'd never met the man, but he knew the name from Bureau publications, the neat confident maps with the neat confident marginalia. A man who wrote jokes in his map margins, if rumor was true. Men who wrote jokes in margins were usually good in the field. Usually.

"That's a Bureau problem," Silas said. "I haul freight."

"The Bureau has hired three guides since the silence began. All three resigned within a day of reading the route. One left town. One left his wife, briefly, until she talked him back." Vey's mouth did something that was almost humor. "You are, apparently, the fourth option and the last one. Which makes you either the worst guide alive or the only honest one."

"Could be both."

"It could." She stepped closer, out of the traffic of stevedores, and lowered her voice as though the river might repeat what she said next. "I don't want a rescue party, Mr. Dunmore. Rescue parties need hope, and hope is heavy and eats the provisions. What I want is proof. Someone goes up the Kestrel, finds out what happened to nine of my people, and comes back with evidence I can put in front of the Concessions Office before their permit window closes. Nineteen days, and then the valley seals for ten years. Whatever is up there gets buried under paperwork for a decade unless I have something worth digging through the paperwork for."

Silas set down the second crate more slowly than the first. "Evidence of what?"

"If I knew, I wouldn't need you." But her eyes flicked, just once, upstream, and Silas had spent enough years reading faces in bad weather to know what that flick meant. She suspected. She suspected something specific, and she wasn't spending it yet.

"Why me?" he said. "Truly. There are guides in the capital with clean names and better gear."

"There are. And every one of them will turn back at the Sable Narrows or the first sign of trouble, because turning back has never cost them anything." She met his eyes. "You, Mr. Dunmore, have already lost everything there is to lose on that river. You're the only man I can hire whose fear won't outrun his contract."

It was the most insulting compliment he'd ever received, and the worst part was that it was accurate. He wiped his hands on his trousers, mostly so he'd have something to do that wasn't signing anything.

"What's the pay?"

"Double your freight rate. Triple, if you bring back proof."

"And the record?"

Vey reached into the document case and drew out a single sheet, heavy paper, Bureau watermark, the kind of paper that cost more per page than Silas earned in a week. She held it out. "Full pardon of the Vane Glacier finding. Blacklist struck. Your license restored, countersigned by the Surveyor-General himself. You lead this expedition home with evidence, and you walk back into this world a free man."

The paper sat between them in the gray morning light, and Silas found he couldn't quite touch it. Not yet. His hands had gone strange and far away, the way they did before rockfall, before the ice groaned, before all the moments when his body understood danger faster than his mind agreed to.

*No contract ever saved you,* said the voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like his own father. *Contracts are what men write after they've decided what you're worth. And they always decide wrong.*

On the Vane Glacier there had been a contract too. A beautiful one, full of guarantees: weather windows, insurance bonds, a clause about reasonable risk. The glacier had not read it. When the cornice came down and took Aldous Wren and the young photographer with it, the contract had turned out to be a piece of paper, and pieces of paper do not dig. The inquiry had read the same paper very carefully afterward and concluded that a reckless guide had exceeded the terms. Nobody asked whether the terms themselves were a lie told in comfortable offices by men who would never feel wind.

"You want me to go up a river that ended my life," Silas said, "for an institution that helped end it, carrying a promise from the same kind of office that broke the last promise."

"Yes," said Ilsa Vey, without a flicker of apology. "That is precisely what I want. I appreciate that you can see it clearly. Most men need it dressed up."

And there it was — the thing he hated most about her already, and the thing he trusted most. She wasn't lying to him. Everyone else in his ruin had lied to him: the inquiry, the sponsors, even kindly old Foreman Gutch at the freight yard who kept promising next season, next route, next chance. Vey stood in the pork-smelling morning and offered him a poisoned bargain with the poison labeled.

"How many crew?" he asked, and heard, with distant disgust, that he had already decided.

"Small. Fast. A cartographer — Oduya's daughter is insisting, and I've stopped pretending I can stop her—"

"No."

"She's better with instruments than half my senior staff, and she knows her father's field conventions better than anyone living. If there's a cache, a coded bearing, anything Edric left behind, she'll read it and you won't."

"No," Silas said again, but the word had gone soft in his mouth and they both heard it.

"She's twenty-three and stubborn as a jammed lock," Vey continued, merciless. "There's also a former constable, Bram Hale, who requested assignment and whom I have no reason to refuse. Good shot, better liar, and he says he knows the Kestrel country."

"Nobody knows the Kestrel country. That's rather the point of it."

"Then you'll have company in your ignorance." She finally pressed the contract into his hands, and the paper was heavier than any crate on the *Merry Adela*. "Nineteen days, Mr. Dunmore. The permit expires and the valley closes, and whatever killed my people gets to keep its secrets behind a wall of law for ten years. I don't believe in coincidences of timing. Somebody wanted that valley empty of witnesses, and somebody wants a decade of quiet to finish whatever they started."

Silas looked down at the contract. His own name was already printed in the space for expedition leader, in careful clerk's handwriting, as though the outcome had been decided before he was asked. That should have frightened him. It did frighten him. But underneath the fear was the other thing, the thing he'd been hauling around for six years like ballast: the Kestrel country was unfinished business with teeth, and he had never in his life walked away from unfinished business, even when walking away was the entire content of wisdom.

If he signed, he went back. Back past the Narrows, back past the falls, back into the green dark where the river came out of the ground cold and angry and the ridgelines ran like the backs of sleeping animals. Back to being the man everyone had decided he wasn't anymore.

If he didn't sign, he hauled pork until his shoulders gave out, and then he drank at the Tin Lantern and listened to boatmen talk about a river he knew better than any of them, and died slowly in a town that had already forgotten him.

"Wary hope curdling into knowledge" — no, that was just hope curdling, and he'd drink it anyway, because the alternative was ash.

"One condition," Silas said.

Vey raised an eyebrow. "You're in no position for conditions."

"Nobody's ever been in a position for my conditions. That's why they work." He tapped the pardon clause. "This covers me. Make it cover whoever crews with me. Anyone who goes up that river under my name comes back clean, or nobody goes."

For the first time, Vey hesitated. He watched her do arithmetic of her own — political arithmetic, the kind that got deputy directors promoted or quietly retired. Then she nodded once, sharp. "Done. I'll amend it tonight."

"Then I'll sign tonight. After I see the amendment. I've been burned by gentlemen's agreements in rooms warmer than this one."

A ghost of respect crossed her face. "Oduya's daughter was right about you."

"What did she say?"

"That you'd haggle. Her father haggled too. Right up until the day he stopped needing money." Something passed over Vey's composure then, quick as a fish under ice — grief, real grief, banked but burning. For a moment she was not a deputy director but a woman who had sent nine people up a river and hadn't slept since. "Bring them home if you can, Mr. Dunmore. Bring proof home regardless. I'd rather have justice than comfort, but I'd sell a great deal to have both."

She turned and walked up the Kestrel Road toward the Bureau waystation, her boots striking the frozen mud like a clock counting down, and Silas stood alone on the dock with a contract in his hands and the *Merry Adela*'s mate yelling at him about pork.

* * *

He signed it that evening in the back room of the Tin Lantern, because the waystation was too official and he wanted witnesses who were the wrong kind. Old Marda the landlady witnessed it, and so did a fur trader named Petch who was drunk enough to agree to anything and sober enough, remarkably, to spell his own name.

"Kestrel River," Marda said, reading upside down as he signed. She'd pulled his splinters and fed his fevers for six years; she had license. "You hate that river."

"I do."

"You going because of the pardon?"

Silas blotted the ink and looked at the signature a long moment — his own name, the first time it had appeared on anything but a debt notice in six years. Somewhere above the treeline, nine people were dead or worse, and a girl with her father's eyes was packing instruments, and a constable who'd walked away from something was sharpening whatever constables sharpen.

"No," he said, and rolled the contract, and felt the wary hope inside him go cool and hard, like a stone he could carry. "But it'll do until something better comes along."

Outside, the wind had come around to the northwest, smelling of ice off the high country, and the first flakes of the season came down through the lamplight of Harrow's Bend like ash from a fire nobody had reported.

Three days, he figured. Three days to provision, to count rifles, to meet this crew of strangers and decide how much of the truth to tell them. He buttoned his coat and stepped into the snow, and behind him on the tavern wall his signature dried in the lamplight, small and black and binding.

Upstream, the Kestrel waited with its mouth open.
