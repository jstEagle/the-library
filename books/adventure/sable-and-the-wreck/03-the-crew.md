## 3. The Crew

Maren Oduya had been thrown out of better offices than Ilsa Vey's, but never by a kinder hand.

"You can't come," Vey said for the third time, guiding her toward the door of the Bureau annex with the gentle firmness of a woman who had spent thirty years steering stubborn men away from cliffs. "The expedition is contracted personnel only. Insurance, liability—"

"My father is missing on that river."

"Which is precisely why you cannot come."

"Which is precisely," Maren said, planting her heels on the worn floorboards, "why nobody else will care enough to look properly."

Vey sighed and released her arm. She was a tall woman gone slightly stooped, as though she'd spent decades folding herself into doorways built for smaller ambitions, and her office smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of paper that had been argued over too long. Maps hung everywhere — the good kind, hand-drawn, the linework confident even where the country itself was guesswork.

One map hung apart from the others, in a frame that had cost something once. The Kestrel headwaters, surveyed eleven years ago. *E. Oduya, Principal Cartographer* inked in the corner in her father's small, exact hand.

Maren had grown up tracing that river before she could write her own name.

"I knew your father," Vey said quietly. "Everyone at the Bureau did. He was the best of us." She looked at Maren with something that was not quite pity, because pity would have been an insult to them both. "That's why I've hired Silas Dunmore. Not because he's likable. Because he's the only guide alive who knows that stretch of water, and the only one desperate enough to take the contract. Desperation and competence are a rare pairing. I need both."

"I'm not asking you to like me either."

"Maren—"

"He taught me the river." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, a voice she didn't recognize as hers. "Every rapid, every portage, every camp from the mouth to the Narrows. I've been drawing maps since I was nine years old. I can read water better than any man you'll hire to pole a barge. And you're going to send four strangers up there who don't know which side of the current eats boats."

Vey studied her for a long moment. Outside the window, Harrow's Bend went about its morning: freight carts rattling over the bridge, gulls arguing over the fish market, the Kestrel sliding past brown and patient, already halfway to being far away.

"The boat leaves tomorrow at first light," Vey said finally. "From the coal dock, not the main quay. If you happen to be standing on it when the lines come off, that will be an accident of scheduling I am powerless to prevent."

It was, Maren would think later, the closest thing to kindness the Bureau ever showed her.

* * *

The coal dock at dawn smelled of tar and old smoke, and the boat waiting there was an insult to the word *expedition*.

She'd expected something with a name painted proudly on the bow. What she got was a flat-bottomed river scow about forty feet long, deck stained dark with years of cargo, a shallow cabin box aft, and a mast stepped amidships carrying a sail that looked borrowed from a smaller, more optimistic vessel. Crates were stacked along the gunwales. A winch sat rusting at the bow like a beached crustacean.

Silas Dunmore stood in the middle of it all checking off a manifest, and up close he was younger than his reputation and older than his face — somewhere past forty, with grey coming in early at the temples and hands that looked carved for rope rather than pen work. He glanced up when her boots hit the gangplank, and his expression performed a small, efficient collapse.

"No," he said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're the cartographer's daughter. Vey mentioned you'd try this." He went back to his manifest. "Gangplank's behind you."

"I'm the best navigator you're going to get."

"You're a grieving girl playing at adventure, and grief makes bad ballast." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse — the tone of a man stating the weather. "I've carried passengers up that river. Passengers are weight. Weight is what kills you in the Sable Narrows."

"Then consider me crew." She came down onto the deck proper, and she saw him register it — the way she moved without grabbing the rail, how she'd already noted the trim of the boat sitting low at the stern. "Cook, chart-hand, oar, whatever you're short of. You're short of everything. I counted three rifles in that crate and a man your size doesn't run a river alone."

Something flickered across his face — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a familiar animal in unfamiliar country.

"Who taught you to count rifles?"

"My father. He said a surveyor who can't count guns deserves the survey he gets."

Dunmore set down the manifest. For a moment he just looked at her, and she had the uncomfortable sense of being measured against some internal standard she couldn't see — weighed, tested, found provisional.

"Rapids at Dusk," he said abruptly. "The Sable Narrows. Tell me what you do."

"You scout from the right bank first. Always, no matter how late the light, because the left bank has a blind bend below the drop and you can't see the recovery eddy until you're committed. You line the heavy cargo down the shore if the water's above the third marker stone. And you tie down anything you'd cry about losing, then you tie it again, because you *will* lose half of it anyway."

A long silence. Somewhere behind her, gulls screamed over the fish market.

"Third marker stone was washed out two springs ago," Dunmore said. "There's a deadfall there now. But the rest is right." He picked the manifest back up, and she understood she'd just been given something — not welcome, but a foothold. "You eat last, you sleep in the cabin corner nearest the bilge, and if I say jump current you jump current without asking why. Deal?"

"Deal."

"And Oduya." He didn't look up from the page. "I'm sorry about your father. That's not a lie to make you work harder. It's just true."

Maren nodded, throat suddenly tight, and went to find out where the galley was, because a boat runs on food and she intended to run this one.

* * *

Bram Hale arrived an hour later, and he arrived the way weather arrives — without announcement, taking up more space than his body required.

He was a big man, big through the shoulders and chest, with a constable's walk even out of uniform: weight balanced, eyes doing constant quiet inventory of every rooftop and alley mouth. His coat was good quality cut down to look ordinary, and his left hand kept drifting to his hip as though reaching for equipment that wasn't there anymore.

"Dunmore," he said, stepping aboard. The gangplank didn't so much creak as surrender.

"Hale." Silas didn't offer a hand and neither did Bram; they clasped forearms instead, brief and hard, men who'd done business before or around each other. "You came."

"You said you needed someone who could handle trouble."

"I said I needed an extra pair of hands."

Bram smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Same thing, up that river."

Maren watched them from the galley hatch, ostensibly scrubbing pots, actually listening. There was history between these two, and not the friendly kind — the careful kind, the kind where both parties remembered the same events differently and had agreed to stop discussing it.

"Bram Hale," she said, coming out with her hand extended. "Maren Oduya. Navigator."

His handshake was gentle for such an enormous man, which told her something. So did the way his gaze snagged on her face when she gave her surname — a flicker, quickly buried, of something that might have been recognition or might have been calculation.

"Oduya," he repeated. "Your father led the Aldervane survey."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, and turned to help Silas shift crates, and Maren filed the moment away with the precision her father had taught her. *Everything goes on the chart,* he used to say. *Even the things that don't fit yet. Especially those.*

By midday they had the boat loaded, and Maren had learned three things about their fourth member, who had still not appeared.

First: Silas Dunmore lied fluently and constantly, and most of his lies were small and practical. He told the harbormaster they were a licensed fur-trading outfit. He told the customs clerk their cargo was survey instruments and trade goods, when anyone with eyes could see the rifle crate held six barrels, not three. He told Maren the dried meat was venison, and it was absolutely not venison.

Second: Bram Hale watched the docks the way other men watched a fire — constantly, hungrily, waiting for it to spread. Twice Maren caught him scanning the crowd on the quay and once, when a pair of men in matching grey coats paused at the harbor mouth, his whole body changed, the way a dog's does before it decides about a stranger. The grey coats moved on. Hale unclenched by degrees.

Third: nobody talked about why they were really going. Silas had his blacklist and his double pay. Bram had whatever lived behind his eyes when the grey coats walked past. And she had her father, whose absence sat in her chest like a stone she'd learned to carry. Three strangers, three secrets, one boat.

It should have frightened her. Instead, perversely, it steadied her. Honest company would have required honesty, and none of them had any to spare.

* * *

The stowaway problem announced itself the next morning, forty minutes after the lines came off, in the form of a sneeze from inside the cargo.

They were an hour upriver, the banks of Harrow's Bend finally shrinking behind them into haze, when the sound came from beneath the tarpaulin lashed over the midships crates — a small, explosive, unmistakable sneeze, followed by a silence so profound and guilty that Maren nearly laughed.

Silas stopped poling. Bram's hand went to his hip out of pure habit.

"That," Silas said, "was not a fish."

He crossed the deck in three strides and hauled back the tarpaulin, revealing a nest of disturbed sacking and, curled into it like a comma, a girl. Fourteen, maybe fifteen — underfed in the way of children who'd been fed *by* institutions rather than *for*, with sharp elbows, cropped hair growing out unevenly, and eyes that went immediately to the exits. Which, on a boat, meant the water.

"Well," the girl said, with the doomed dignity of someone who has rehearsed this speech and knows it won't survive contact. "Before you say anything, I paid for my passage."

"With what?" Silas asked.

"Work. I bailed the bilge twice during the night. Your boat leaks, by the way. Aft, starboard side, about a handspan above the waterline. I stuffed it with rag but you'll want real pitch."

There was a pause while everyone digested this.

"She's right," Maren said, kneeling to check. "She's right, it's patched, badly, but patched."

Silas looked at the girl. The girl looked at Silas with the flat, unblinking patience of someone who had been turned away from every door in Harrow's Bend and had stopped expecting anything different. Behind her, Maren saw Bram watching the exchange with an odd stillness — not amusement, but something older and sadder, the look of a man recognizing a familiar story.

"What's your name?" Maren asked.

"Tess."

"Tess. Where are your people?"

"Dead, mostly. The rest are the workhouse, and I'd sooner be dead than go back, which is why I'm on your boat, so let's not make it awkward."

Silas pinched the bridge of his nose. "We turn around. We put her ashore at the ferry landing—"

"The ferry landing's a day downstream and the current's against us," Maren said. "You said it yourself yesterday — we lose a full day, maybe two, and every hour matters if anyone upriver is alive."

"We can't take a child into—"

"A child who bails bilges and patches hulls and apparently keeps watch through the night without waking anyone." Maren stood. "You want to talk about weight killing us in the Narrows? Fine. She weighs less than the anchor chain. And unlike the anchor chain, she pulls her own load."

Tess's chin came up a fraction. It was, Maren thought, the smallest possible flag raised over the smallest possible territory: *I am here, I am useful, do not send me back.*

Silas looked at the girl for a long, long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — Maren couldn't read it, then or later, but she suspected it was memory. Some other small face, some other river, some other time he'd made the safe choice and hated it.

"One week," he said finally. "First settlement we pass, you're off my boat, and I pay your fare wherever you want to go. That's the deal. Take it or swim."

"Deal," Tess said instantly, in the tone of someone who had absolutely no intention of honoring it and knew he knew.

Bram snorted. Even Silas's mouth twitched, briefly, before he killed it and went back to his pole.

Maren helped the girl stow her nest of sacking properly, and Tess — settling in with the efficiency of someone who'd been living rough a long time — looked up at her and said, quietly, so the men wouldn't hear:

"You argued for me. Why?"

Because I know what it is to be unwanted on a boat, Maren almost said. Because my father always took on the ones nobody else would hire. Because somebody on this river has to start saying yes.

"Because you were right about the leak," she said instead.

Tess considered this, nodded once, satisfied, and closed her eyes. Within minutes she was asleep — truly asleep, deep and total, the way only the exhausted and the very young can manage on a stranger's deck in open country.

Maren stood at the rail and watched the Kestrel carry them north. The banks narrowed by degrees. The hills came down closer to the water, wearing darker trees, and somewhere ahead — days ahead, weeks maybe — her father's river waited with whatever it had taken from him.

Behind her, Silas counted rifles again, quietly, and told Bram another smooth lie about fur-trading licenses, and Bram answered with a lie of his own about why a former constable wanted river work, and the lies stacked up between them like sandbags, holding back nothing, holding up everything.

Trust, Maren thought, watching the water slide past. Nobody aboard had earned any yet.

But the current only ran one way, and they were all in the same boat.

Two days later, at dawn, Tess sneezed again — and this time, it wasn't from the cold.
