## 4. Stowaway

The crate smelled of tar and dried herring, and Tess had been breathing it for the better part of a day.

She had calculated everything before climbing in — that was the thing people never understood about her, that she *calculated*. She'd watched the loading at Harrow's Bend from behind a stack of salt barrels for two full hours. She'd noted which crates went aboard last (the ones nobody would open until camp), which sat against the hull where the engine's warmth would seep through the boards on cold nights, and which crewman had the habit of napping after lunch (the big one, the ex-constable with the face like a closed door). She'd even brought provisions: half a loaf of black bread, a twist of salt pork, three apples she'd paid for honestly, thank you very much, with two days of scrubbing floors at the Ferryman's Rest.

What she had not calculated was the herring.

Or rather, she'd calculated the herring's presence but underestimated its persistence. The smell got into her hair, her clothes, the cracks of her knuckles. By midday she was breathing through her mouth in slow, disciplined sips, like a diver, and by late afternoon she had begun composing, in her head, the argument she would make when they found her.

Because they would find her. She knew that too. Nobody hid successfully forever; the trick was to be found *useful*.

Her plan went as follows: stay hidden until the boat was a full day upriver, past the point where any sensible person would turn back just to be rid of one small girl. Then emerge, present herself calmly, and lay out her case. She could cook. She could mend. She could read and figure faster than most grown men, a fact the workhouse matron had punished her for twice because it made the other children restless. She was fourteen, small enough to fit in a crate, and therefore — this was the crown of her argument — ideally suited for crawling into places boats needed inspecting.

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, an excellent plan, right up until the moment the crate lid came off and she found herself looking up into the grey eyes of Silas Dunmore, who did not look surprised, or angry, or anything else useful, but simply tired, the way a man looks at weather he has been predicting all week.

"Well," said Tess, from inside the herring. "Good afternoon."

* * *

They made her sit on the cargo deck in the drizzle while the whole crew assembled around her like a tribunal, which she thought was theatrical of them, given there were only four people aboard including herself.

Silas stood closest, arms folded, rain dripping off his beard. Maren Oduya hovered at his shoulder with her father's field case strapped across her chest like a shield — she hadn't taken it off since Harrow's Bend, Tess had noticed through the knothole, not once, not even to sleep, if the shape of her bedroll meant anything. Bram Hale leaned against the rail with the unhurried stillness of a man who had spent years watching people lie for a living. And at the tiller, half-turned, was Quill — Ilsa Vey's man, quiet as furniture, whose eyes moved over Tess the way a clerk's eyes move over inventory.

"So," said Silas. "How long."

"Since Harrow's Bend."

"Through the first set of rapids."

"Yes."

"Alone. In a crate. Through rapids." He said each word separately, like he was stacking stones.

"It was a very well-packed crate," said Tess. "Whoever loaded your cargo knows their business."

Bram made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Silas shot him a look, then turned back to Tess, and she watched him do the arithmetic every adult did when they looked at her: too small to be dangerous, too mouthy to be harmless, what do we do with this.

"Where are your parents?"

"Dead. My mother when I was nine. My father I never met, or possibly met and didn't notice, depending on how many men pass through a river town in a year." She said it flatly, because pity was a currency she refused to accept at a bad exchange rate. "I was in the Marlow Street Workhouse since. I left Tuesday."

"You *ran*."

"I *left*," Tess corrected. "Running implies being chased. Nobody chases you out of Marlow Street. That's rather the whole horror of it. You can walk out the door and the world just closes behind you like water." She paused, aware she was getting ahead of her notes. "Also the matron sold the older girls' ration tickets to the mill foremen, if you want a second charge, but I expect that's outside your jurisdiction."

That landed. She saw it land — saw Maren's chin come up and Bram's stillness sharpen into attention. Only Quill's expression stayed smooth, and something about that smoothness made Tess decide, privately and immediately, not to turn her back on him.

"The workhouse will have posted a recovery notice," Silas said. "There'll be a reward. There's always a reward."

"There is," Tess agreed. "Eleven shillings. I've seen the notices. They get my age wrong, incidentally. They say twelve. Eleven shillings for a twelve-year-old is insulting even by workhouse standards."

"And what exactly were you planning? Stow away to the middle of nowhere, pop out of a box, and ask us to keep you?"

"I was planning to stow away to the middle of nowhere, pop out of a box, and demonstrate my value so thoroughly that keeping me would seem like your idea." She lifted her chin. "I'm adaptable. Plans change."

Maren laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly smothered, but Tess counted it. First vote.

Silas didn't laugh. He crouched down to her level, forearms on his knees, and studied her with those grey river-stone eyes, and Tess held the look because looking away was surrender and she had never once surrendered to anyone over the height of her own eyes.

"Here's what happens now," he said quietly. "We're a day upriver. Current's against us, but not enough against us. We put in at the next landing, and you're on the morning coach back to Harrow's Bend, and I write ahead to the Bureau so there's someone respectable waiting at the coach house. That's the whole of it. This river already has a body count, girl, and I'm not adding yours to it."

"That's it?" Tess said. "You don't even want to hear my qualifications?"

"I know your qualifications. You're fourteen and you smell like herring."

"*Everyone* smells like herring on this boat—"

"Tess." He said her name — she hadn't told them her name; she realized with a small internal wince that she must have talked in her sleep, which was a tactical failure she'd have to live with — and his voice dropped into something that wasn't unkind, which was somehow worse than if it had been. "There are men behind us on this river willing to kill a survey crew over papers. I've seen what they leave. If I let you stay, and something happens to you, that's mine to carry. I've got enough of that kind of weight. Do you understand?"

And here was the moment, Tess understood later, where a smarter child would have cried, or gone quiet, or done any of the things that made adults feel large and merciful. But Tess had spent five years learning that softness was bait, and so instead she heard herself say:

"You're counting bodies that haven't happened yet. That's not caution, that's superstition. Meanwhile there are four real bodies upstream — my father's map partner among them, probably — and you're going to spend a day rowing backwards to deliver me to a coach house so you can feel responsible." She stood up, all four feet eleven inches of her, rain running off her chin. "Keep me aboard. Feed me half rations, I don't care. Put me to work. And if we reach the next landing and you still want me gone, I'll go, and I won't fight you, and I'll even say thank you. But give me until the landing. That's fair trade, Mr. Dunmore. You strike me as a man who respects fair trade."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked on the cargo boards. Somewhere upstream a bird screamed its idiot scream.

"She's got you there," Maren said. "Current's against us anyway. Turning around costs us a full day, maybe more with the light going. Every hour matters if—" She stopped herself, but everyone heard the rest: *if my father's alive.*

"I'm not deciding this by committee," Silas growled.

"No," Bram said mildly, from the rail. "But the constable in me wants it noted that delivering a runaway to a coach house without her consent is technically abduction, and I've written that report before, and it reads badly."

"You walked away from the constabulary."

"Which is why I remember the paperwork so fondly."

Silas looked at the sky as though petitioning it directly, got no answer, and looked back down at Tess. Something shifted in his face — not softening, exactly. More like a man finding a crack in a wall he'd believed solid, and not yet knowing whether to fill it or worry at it.

"Half rations," he said finally. "You sleep where I can see you. You touch nothing that floats, burns, or shoots without asking. And the first time you become more trouble than you're worth—"

"You'll put me ashore," Tess finished. "Yes. Agreed. Thank you." And she stuck out her hand, because her mother had taught her that a deal sealed with a handshake was harder to wriggle out of than one sealed with words, and she wanted this one nailed down.

Silas stared at the hand like it might be armed. Then, with the expression of a man signing a contract he intended to contest later, he shook it.

Her hand disappeared entirely into his. She filed that away too: *he is bigger than he looks, and careful with it.* Useful information. Everything was useful information.

* * *

They gave her the corner behind the engine housing, which was warm and smelled of oil instead of herring, and by evening she had repaid the first installment of her value by producing, from the bottom of her bundle, a needle and three lengths of thread and mending the tear in Maren's oilskin sleeve that had been letting rain down her wrist all day.

"How did you—" Maren began.

"I watched you flex your hand at supper. You kept shaking the left cuff." Tess bit the thread. "People leak information constantly. It's mostly a matter of watching."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. "My father used to say that. About maps, though. He said a river tells you everything if you watch where it doesn't go." Her voice caught slightly on *father*, and she busied herself with the mended sleeve, turning the cuff over twice.

Tess, who had spent five years reading adults the way other children read adventure serials, recognized grief when it walked into a room wearing practical clothing. She also recognized that mentioning it would be the fastest way to lose this woman's trust, and so instead she asked, "Was he good? Your father? At the mapping?"

"The best." No hesitation. "He used to bring home draft surveys and pin them to the kitchen wall, and I'd find the mistakes. He stopped letting me find mistakes when I was eleven because I found one in a published chart and wrote to the Bureau about it." A ghost of a smile. "They corrected it. He framed the correction letter."

Tess felt something in her chest shift — the specific ache of hearing about a family from the inside, like pressing your face to a lit window in winter. She had a mother once, and a kitchen once, and a wall once, though theirs had held a calendar and a pressed flower instead of charts. She said none of this. Instead she said, "Then we'll find him."

Maren went still. "You don't know that he needs finding."

"I know the crew's missing and you haven't taken that case off since Harrow's Bend," Tess said. "So either it's sentiment, or it's because whatever's in it is the reason he's missing. Either way — we'll find him."

Across the deck, she felt Silas watching them. Pretending to check a mooring line, actually listening, the way careful men listened. She let him listen. Let him hear that the stowaway was not dead weight but ballast of a kind.

* * *

She woke once in the night, as she always woke, at some animal hour when the workhouse dreams came — the bell, the rows of beds, the matron's keys. But this time when she woke there was no bell. There was the river talking to itself under the hull, and the engine ticking as it cooled, and above her the sky doing that enormous spill of stars that never happened over towns because towns burned their own skies down.

And there was a blanket over her that hadn't been there when she fell asleep. Wool, army-surplus, smelling faintly of pipe smoke.

She sat up. Silas was at the stern, awake, smoking, looking upstream into the dark with the fixed attention of a man expecting the dark to look back.

Tess pulled the blanket to her chin and lay back down.

*Superstition,* she thought, with something dangerously close to fondness. *He counts bodies that haven't happened yet. But he gives blankets to ones that have.*

She was asleep again before she could decide what that made him.

* * *

Morning came grey and cold, with fog lying on the water like spilled milk. Tess was up first — of course she was up first; she'd been up first for five years, because at Marlow Street the last one out of bed washed everyone's bowls — and she had porridge going on the little stove before anyone else stirred, using less meal than the recipe allowed and more salt than seemed wise, which was the trick to making thin porridge taste deliberate.

Bram appeared first, drawn by the smell like a large polite animal. He accepted a bowl, tasted it, and raised his eyebrows.

"You've fed men before," he said.

"Workhouses run on feeding crowds cheaply. It's the one skill they teach properly." She ladled him a second helping without being asked, because Bram Hale struck her as a man who had not been handed things without asking in a long time, and that was a debt worth starting early.

One by one the others came. Maren, blinking, wrapping both hands around her bowl. Quill, who ate standing, watching the banks. Last, Silas, who looked at the pot, then at Tess, then at the pot again with deep suspicion, as though breakfast might be a second stowaway.

"It's not poisoned," Tess said. "If I wanted to poison anyone I'd have started with Quill, no offense."

"None taken," said Quill, in a voice like a drawer closing.

Silas took a bowl. He ate. He did not say thank you, but he had seconds, and Tess had learned long ago that seconds *were* the thank-you, in the language of practical men.

They cast off into the fog with the current pulling them north toward the Sable Narrows, and Tess stationed herself amidships with a boat hook she'd been granted conditional custody of, watching the banks slide past — reeds, stone, pine, the occasional ruin of an old logging camp sinking back into the green.

It was near midday when she saw it: a thread of smoke rising off the eastern bank, thin and straight, too disciplined to be a squatter's fire.

She didn't shout. She touched Bram's arm and pointed, and watched him go from relaxed to absolutely motionless in the space of a breath, and then heard him say, quietly, "Silas."

Silas came forward. Followed the line of Bram's arm. His jaw tightened.

"Camp's supposed to be abandoned," Maren said, coming up beside them, her father's case clutched to her chest. "Forward camp. Everyone moved upriver weeks ago."

"Fires don't stay lit on their own," Bram said.

"No," Silas agreed. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice. "They don't."

The boat slid on through the fog toward the smoke, and Tess gripped her boat hook and thought: *Day one. I said give me until the landing.*

She had a feeling the landing was going to be a long way off.
