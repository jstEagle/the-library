## 5. First Blood on the Water

Bram Hale had spent eleven years as a constable learning to read a room, and the first thing he read about the Kestrel River was that it did not want them there.

It said so in a hundred small ways. The current shouldered the boat like a bored bouncer. The fog came off the water in the mornings thick enough to chew. Even the birds seemed to have opinions — some kind of gray jay that followed them for an hour upstream, screaming what Bram could only assume were insults in a language he was fortunate not to speak.

"You could ask it to be quieter," Tess suggested from her perch on the cargo crates. She'd been officially promoted from stowaway to deckhand two days ago, mostly because Silas had run out of arguments and partly because she'd already learned three knots and was insufferable about it.

"I could," Bram agreed. "And then it would follow us for two hours."

He stood at the bow with the pole while Silas worked the stern oar, and Maren crouched over her charts in the middle of the boat like a small, serious lighthouse of a girl. The river narrowed ahead into a corridor of black spruce, and somewhere beyond that bend — if the charts were honest, and Maren assured him her father's charts were the only honest thing on this river — lay the forward camp.

Camp Aldervane. The last place anyone had heard from the survey party. Nineteen days ago, by the Bureau's reckoning. A radio check-in, routine, cheerful even: *All well, making good time, source valley by month's end.*

Then nothing.

Bram had been on enough missing-person calls to know the shape of silence. Most silences meant nothing at all — a dead battery, a missed appointment, a man who'd decided his wife's sister's house was preferable to his own. But some silences had weight. This one had weight. He'd felt it since Harrow's Bend, sitting across from Ilsa Vey while she slid the contract across the table, watching the way she didn't quite look at the map when she talked about where the expedition had last reported from.

He hadn't told the others why he'd really signed on. He'd told Silas it was the pay, which was true the way a bone is true — technically, but missing most of what mattered. The truth was he'd trained half the enforcement officers the mining companies used out here, back before he'd understood what they were for, and he'd walked away from the constabulary rather than keep pretending otherwise. Walking away was easy. It was the staying away that had turned out to be the hard part.

The boat ground against gravel, and Silas called the landing.

* * *

The camp sat on a shelf of flat ground above the east bank, exactly where the chart said it would be, and from fifty yards out Bram knew.

He didn't know how he knew. That was the maddening part. Nothing looked wrong yet — just canvas and timber and a flagpole with no flag, the ordinary furniture of a survey camp. But something was wrong with the *stillness*. Living camps were never still. There was always smoke shifting, a line snapping in the wind, somebody's laundry arguing with the breeze. This camp was holding its breath.

"Wait here," Silas said, and started up the bank alone.

Bram followed him anyway. So did Maren, and then Tess, because apparently the word "wait" had stopped meaning anything the moment they'd fished her out of a crate. Silas gave them all a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces, but he didn't order them back, and Bram understood why. Whatever was up there, nobody wanted to be the one who waited below without knowing.

The first thing was the door. The supply hut's door hung off one hinge, torn outward, not inward — opened fast, from inside or by someone in a hurry, the wood around the latch splintered in long pale gouges.

The second thing was the radios.

There were two of them, the expedition's primary set and its backup, and someone had destroyed both with a thoroughness that made Bram's neck prickle. Not smashed in anger — anger is sloppy, leaves parts scattered, moves on. These had been *worked*. The casings pried open, the valves crushed individually under a boot heel, the wiring pulled out and cut into short lengths too short to splice. The battery acid had been poured into the fire pit.

"That's not rage," Bram heard himself say. "That's procedure."

Silas glanced at him. "Meaning?"

"Meaning whoever did this wanted to make sure nobody downstream ever heard another word from this camp, and they took their time doing it." He crouched by the fire pit, touched the ash, rubbed it between his fingers. Cold, but not old-cold. "And they burned something else in here besides batteries. Paper, I think. A lot of it."

Maren had gone very still by the mess tent. "My father kept duplicates," she said, to no one in particular. "He always kept duplicates. He used to say paper was the only instrument you couldn't recalibrate." Her voice was steady. Her hands, folding and unfolding the edge of her coat, were not.

Tess had found the third thing.

She was standing at the edge of the clearing, near the treeline, and she wasn't moving, and she wasn't talking, which for Tess was its own kind of alarm. Bram crossed to her and followed her gaze down.

The grave was shallow. Whoever dug it had done the job quickly and without ceremony — a rough rectangle maybe six feet by two, the spoil heaped carelessly to one side, already softening with rain. A single survey stake stood at the head, the brass marker plate still bright, as though whoever drove it had taken a strange, spiteful pride in using the dead man's own equipment to mark him.

Bram knelt. The body beneath the thin layer of stones and forest duff had been dead long enough to stop being a person and start being evidence, which was a transition he'd witnessed too many times and never gotten comfortable with. He checked what he could check without disturbing more than necessary. No wounds visible above the collar. The boots were gone — taken, not lost. The pockets had been searched; the jacket lining hung out like a tongue.

"Male," he said quietly. "Middle-aged. Been here — I'd guess ten days, give or take, depending on the weather we've had." He looked up at Silas. "That puts it after the last radio call. After 'all well.'"

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somewhere across the river, the gray jay screamed something that no longer felt funny.

"Can you tell who?" Maren asked. Her voice was careful, flattened, the voice of someone carrying something fragile across ice.

Bram looked. The face was past knowing, but the hands weren't — the hands still had their calluses in the right places, the deep grooved ones across the palm base that came from decades of instruments and rope and pencil stubs. And on the ring finger, caught in the shrinking flesh, a band of pale skin where a ring had recently been removed.

"He wore a ring until recently," Bram said. "Somebody took it." He hesitated, then reached out and turned the wrist gently, looking at the forearm. "And there — see the tattoo? Anchor, with a date underneath. Navy man. Your father have any sailors with him?"

Maren exhaled, and it shook only a little. "Mr. Corben. Boatswain. Twenty years in the coastal service before my father hired him." She pressed her knuckles to her mouth, lowered them. "He taught me to box the compass when I was eight. He said I had a natural feel for it, which was a lie, but a kind one."

"I'm sorry," Bram said, and meant it, and hated how small the words were.

"Which means," Silas said, in the low, level voice Bram was beginning to understand was how the man handled everything too large to handle any other way, "that the others are either scattered, taken, or buried somewhere we haven't found yet. And it means whoever did this has been gone—" 

"—hours," Bram finished. He'd been circling the camp's perimeter in his head the whole time, cataloguing, and now he let it out. "Look at this." He pointed to the mud at the clearing's edge, where the ground dipped toward the water. Boot prints. Several sets, overlapping, heading north along the bank. "Rain day before yesterday would have softened all this. These are crisp. The edges haven't slumped. Four men, maybe five, heavy loads — see how deep the prints are, and the drag marks between them? They were carrying gear out. And here—" he moved to the supply hut doorway, "—they took the good stuff. Rifles are gone. Food stores stripped. But the theodolite's still in its case, untouched, and the tents are slashed but left standing."

Tess frowned. "Why leave the expensive thing and take the cheap things?"

"Because the expensive thing proves nothing," Bram said. "A theodolite measures angles. Nobody hangs a man for angles. But rifles feed a patrol, food feeds a patrol, and papers—" he nodded at the fire pit, at the ash that had been documents, "—papers convict people. They burned every scrap of writing in this camp and took the guns. That's not thieves. Thieves don't destroy the ledger."

Silas had picked up the survey stake from the grave and was turning it over slowly, reading the stamped numbers on the brass. His jaw worked once. "These are Corben's stakes. Marked C-1 through C-40. He was setting the baseline for the source valley survey." He set it down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you put down something you'd like to throw. "They killed him with his own work."

"Or near enough," Bram said. He straightened, scanned the treeline again, and felt the hair rise on his arms — not fear exactly, more the professional certainty that the woods were watching them back. "Here's the arithmetic I can't get away from. Ten days dead, but these prints are fresh. Fresh means hours. Which means the men who did this came back. Why would they come back to a camp they'd already emptied?"

"Maybe they left something behind they needed," Maren said.

"Or maybe," Silas said, "they're coming back again."

* * *

They worked the rest of the daylight, because Bram insisted and because work was better than standing in a murdered man's camp letting your imagination do the surveying. Bram taught Tess how to walk a grid without trampling sign, and she turned out to have an eye for it — she found a brass button in the grass by the mess tent, and a broken pencil, and finally, wedged under the flagpole's base stone, a scrap of oilcloth wrapped around something the size of a cigarette case.

Inside was a strip of paper, water-stained but legible. Survey figures. Elevations, bearings, a string of numbers marching toward the source valley — and at the bottom, in a cramped hand that Maren identified instantly as her father's, four words:

*If found — go quiet.*

"Go quiet," Tess repeated. "What does that mean, go quiet?"

"It means he knew," Maren said. Her thumb moved over the paper like it might feel a pulse. "He knew they were being watched, and he hid this instead of broadcasting it, because the radio was already compromised. He was telling whoever found it — telling *us*, somehow, he couldn't have known it'd be us — don't transmit. Don't signal. They're listening."

Bram felt the words land in his chest like a dropped anchor. Because he'd been thinking it for two days and hadn't said it, and now a dead cartographer had said it for him: *the radio was compromised.* Which meant the Bureau's cheerful reports of silence might not have been silence at all. Which meant someone had been reading this expedition's mail from the day it launched.

He looked at Silas, and Silas was already looking at him, and neither of them said it aloud, because Tess and Maren were standing right there and some knowledge was a load you didn't hand to people who couldn't put it down.

"We burn nothing else tonight," Silas said instead. "No fire after full dark. No lamps. We sleep cold and we post watches, two hours each, and tomorrow we're on the water before first light." He looked around the ruined camp, at the torn tents and the empty flagpole and the stake-marked grave. "We'll bury Mr. Corben properly on the way back down. He'd understand the delay. Sailors always do."

They made camp two hundred yards downstream, in a hollow of alders with the river at their backs, and Bram took first watch because he knew he wouldn't sleep anyway.

The night came down cold and enormous. Stars in ragged sheets through the cloud gaps. The river muttering to itself. Twice he heard movement in the dark and brought the rifle up, and twice it resolved into nothing — a deer, probably, or wind, or his own eleven years of night shifts playing tricks.

On the third time, it wasn't nothing.

Far upstream, around the bend where the spruce swallowed the river, a light blinked. Once. Twice. Three times. Then darkness, long enough to count to ten. Then twice more.

Not a lantern — lanterns glow, they don't blink. Not lightning — the sky was clear as a bell. It was deliberate. Rhythmic. A message, passed from somewhere upriver to somewhere else, in a code Bram didn't know but recognized absolutely, the way you recognize a language you don't speak.

He counted the interval. Waited. It came again — same pattern, same patience.

Behind him, in the hollow, four people slept the dreamless sleep of the exhausted, and Bram sat with the rifle across his knees and did the coldest arithmetic of his life. The camp had been visited within hours of their arrival. The prints went north, upriver. The light was upriver. Everything, always, upriver — toward the source, toward the valley, toward whatever it was that men with rifles and procedures and no names were willing to kill a boatswain and burn a ledger to protect.

Rescue, he thought. That was the word on the contract. Rescue implied there was still something to rescue, and maybe there was — Maren's father had hidden his figures like a man planning to survive, and planning to survive was half of surviving.

But Bram watched the light pulse its patient, patient pattern against the dark, and he understood that the word on the contract had changed. Somewhere between the splintered door and the stake in the grave, this had stopped being a rescue.

This was a reckoning now. And they were outnumbered, outgunned, and being counted.

The light blinked three times, and went out, and did not come back — and somehow that was worse.
