## 6. Rapids at Dusk

The Sable Narrows announced itself an hour before they reached it, the way bad news always does — first as a rumor in the water, a low mutter under the hull that Silas felt through his boot soles before anyone else heard anything at all. Then the banks began to close in, granite walls leaning together like conspirators, and the river went from brown to white to a color he had no name for except *trouble*.

He'd run the Narrows twice before. The first time, at twenty-six, with a full crew and full daylight and a man named Corrick shouting the channels from the bow, it had been the finest hour of his life. The second time was the Vane Glacier year, and he did not let himself think about that one.

"Silas." Maren stood at the chart case, her father's river notes spread across her knees. "There's a line here. 'Narrows runnable at dusk only if desperate.' He underlined it."

"He would," Silas said. "Underlining is how your father apologizes."

"To whom?"

"To whoever comes after him."

Bram came forward from the stern, where he'd spent the afternoon pretending not to watch the treeline. Watching the treeline was his actual occupation; the pretending was for Tess's benefit. "How desperate are we?"

Silas looked at the sky. The sun had already gone behind the western ridge, leaving them a half hour of grey-gold light and then a long blue dusk, and then dark. Behind them, somewhere downriver, a patrol boat with concession colors sat at anchor or moved or waited — he didn't know which, and not knowing was its own kind of current.

"We're desperate," he said.

"I was afraid you'd say that." Bram rolled his sleeves. "I've never been in rapids."

"You're about to be in rapids."

"That's what I meant by afraid."

Tess appeared at his elbow the way she always did, silent as smoke, having heard everything and admitted to none of it. She'd been quiet since the grave at the forward camp. Not frightened-quiet — Silas knew frightened-quiet from his years hauling greenhorns up rivers — but *counting*-quiet. She was doing sums in her head, and he suspected none of the totals were cheerful.

"Where do you want me?" she asked.

That was new. Two days ago she'd been cargo. Now she was asking for a station like she had a right to one, and the worst part — the part Silas refused to examine — was that she did.

"Bail bucket," he said. "Center of the boat. When I shout *low*, you get flat on the floorboards and hold onto the ring bolt. When I don't shout anything, bail."

"And when do I get to do something interesting?"

"When you've earned interesting." He caught himself almost smiling and turned it into a scowl, out of professional habit. "Interesting on a river is just another word for drowning with scenery."

* * *

They lined the boat through the first quarter mile, walking her along the rocks on bow and stern lines while the water thundered beside them, because Silas was many things — disgraced, broke, blacklisted, and lying to three people about at least two of those — but he was not stupid enough to run the upper Narrows loaded. It cost them forty minutes they didn't have. The light went from gold to pewter while they hauled, and Maren worked the lines without being told how, and Bram discovered that his constable's shoulders were good for something besides standing in doorways looking disapproving.

"You've done this," Silas said, watching her take up slack.

"Mining camps. My father believed maps should be walked." She hauled, braced, hauled again. "He believed most things should be walked, actually. Chairs were a failing."

"He sounds like a man I'd have liked."

"He sounds like a man who's alive," Maren said, in a voice that closed the subject like a door, "and we're going to go collect him."

Silas let it lie. There were things you said on a river and things you didn't, and grief was usually both.

At the last eddy above the true drop, he called them in. The Narrows proper ran half a mile between walls no wider than a wagon road, and the guidebooks — the honest ones, the ones that hadn't been burned with the rest of his reputation — rated it a strong four in high water. This was not high water. This was late-season water, which meant more rock showing its teeth and less forgiveness in the holes between them.

"Rules," he said. "One: nobody stands. Two: nobody argues with me mid-rapid. Three—" he looked at Tess, who was holding the bail bucket like a shield, "—if anyone goes in the water, feet up, back downstream, and you swim for the eddies, not the shore. The shore will kill you politely. The eddies are rude but they'll keep you."

"What if we argue with you *before* the rapid?" Bram asked.

"That's allowed. Encouraged, even. Once we're in it, I'm the only opinion on this boat."

"A dictatorship, then."

"The mildest kind. I'll even let you vote afterward."

Maren snorted — actually snorted, the first sound he'd heard from her that wasn't a question or a wound — and something in the boat loosened by a degree. Silas took the sweep oar. The light was going blue-grey at the edges now, the kind of light where white water stops being white and starts being a rumor.

"On my count," he said. "Three. Two. One."

* * *

The Narrows at dusk was less a river than an argument the mountains were having with the sea, conducted at volume, and their little boat was a footnote being shouted over.

For the first hundred yards it went well, which Silas distrusted immediately. Good openings were how rivers lied to you. He read the water ahead — the tongue of smooth current threading left of the first fang of rock, the lateral wave he'd need to punch through, the hole beyond it that looked like a sleeping dog and was not — and he called the strokes, and the crew, to their enormous credit, gave him exactly what he asked for. Maren on the bow line calls. Bram rowing like a man who'd once been trained for something violent and had never entirely stopped being able to do it. Tess bailing with the grim rhythm of someone paying off a debt.

Then the river changed its mind.

It happened the way it always happens: all at once, and too late to matter. A pour-over he'd marked on memory as a minor chute had shifted — spring flood had moved stone, the way spring flood does, patient and criminal — and the channel he'd planned folded shut like a book. The water that should have carried them left turned instead and drove them right, toward a seam of chaos where two currents met and stood up on end.

"Hard left!" Silas roared, and put his whole back into the sweep, and the boat *almost* obeyed.

Almost is a word rivers love.

The bow clipped the standing wave at an angle no boat enjoys, and the world became vertical noise. They took the next fifty yards sideways. Something heavy in the cargo — the spare tent, half their dry rations, he'd know soon enough what the river's tax amounted to — tore loose from its lashing and went over the side without ceremony. Then the crate with the radio spares. Then, with a sound like a drawer opening, the lashing on the starboard gunwale parted altogether, and the boat wallowed, and Tess — flat on the floorboards as instructed, hand on the ring bolt as instructed — found that the floorboards themselves had decided to leave.

She didn't scream. That was the thing Silas would remember afterward, the thing that kept him honest for years: she didn't scream. She simply wasn't there anymore, the way a coin isn't there when you reach into the pocket, and the river took her the way it takes everything, instantly and without malice, which is worse than malice because malice can be reasoned with.

"Tess!" Maren's voice, cutting through the roar like wire.

Silas had the sweep, the boat, three other lives, and roughly four seconds. Every instinct he owned — every instinct that had gotten him blacklisted, every instinct the Vane Glacier had ground down to a nub and then salted — screamed at him to hold his station. You don't leave the helm. That was the first law, older than any contract: the helmsman holds.

He held. God help him, he held, because the alternative was five people in the water instead of one, and the arithmetic of drowning doesn't care how brave you want to be.

What happened next happened in the space between two heartbeats. Maren dropped the bow line, coiled it once around her forearm — a throw he'd have taught differently, a throw that shouldn't work, too much rope, too short a window — and threw it anyway, upstream of where Tess's head had gone under, leading the target the way you lead a duck. And the river, for once in its long career of cruelty, cooperated. The coil opened clean. The loop settled over Tess's shoulder and caught under her arm, and Maren went down onto her belly on the wet boards with her boots hooked under the thwart, and Bram dropped his oar and grabbed her belt, and for three full seconds — three seconds that lasted approximately a geological age — the whole enterprise hung on a girl's grip and another girl's stubbornness and an ex-constable's refusal to be a bystander ever again.

Then they had her. Half-drowned, coughing the Kestrel out of her lungs, nine years old in every way that mattered despite being fourteen, but aboard. Alive.

The rest of the Narrows, insulted at being upstaged, spat them out into the lower pool like a pit boss ejecting a cheat.

* * *

They made camp on the gravel bar below the drop, in the last violet light, and took inventory of what the river had collected as toll. Half the dry food, gone. The tent, gone. The radio spares — which had been decorative anyway, given the state of the radios at the forward camp — gone. One oar, recovered, bent into an opinion. Silas added it up and arrived at a number that meant they ate thin and slept cold until they reached resupply or rescue or ruin, whichever came first.

Tess sat apart on a drift log, wringing out her shirt, saying nothing. Her hair had come loose from its braid and hung in ropes. She was shivering in the way that's mostly temperature and partly everything else.

Maren built the fire. Bram rigged a windbreak from the bent oar and a tarp. And Silas stood at the edge of the firelight with his hands in his pockets, rehearsing sentences and discarding them, because there was a speech in him — there were several — and every one of them was wrong.

*I should have jumped.* No. If he'd jumped, they'd be fishing two bodies out of the lower pool instead of drying one child's socks.

*You did well.* Too easy. Cheap as a tip.

*This is why stowaways stay home.* Cruel, and a lie, and they both would have known it was a lie.

In the end he said nothing, which was its own sentence, and went and sat down beside her on the log with a length of rope off the wrecked lashing.

Tess looked at the rope. Looked at him. Said nothing, in the fluent teenage dialect of *I'm fine, stop looking at me.*

Silas tied a bowline. Slowly, exaggeratedly, the way you show a greenhorn. "River takes the loose things," he said. "Boats, crates, people. Everything that isn't tied down, eventually. So." He handed her the rope. "We tie you down."

"It slipped," she said. Her voice was steady but her hands weren't, and she knew he could see that, and she hated it, and he pretended not to, which was the kindest available option. "The bolt. My hand slipped off the bolt."

"No," Silas said. "The floorboard came off the frame. Your hand did exactly what it was supposed to do. Which is why tomorrow it learns something better to do." He nodded at the rope. "Bowline. The king of knots. Won't slip, won't jam, comes apart after the worst day of its life like nothing happened. Go on."

She tied it. Wrong, naturally — a granny knot wearing a bowline's clothes — and he untied it without comment and put it back in her hands.

"Again."

"Again?"

"Everything worth knowing is *again*. Again until your hands know it without you. Hands are smarter than heads in an emergency. Heads panic. Hands remember." He watched her fingers find the rabbit hole and the tree, clumsy, concentrating. "Where'd you learn to be so quiet? On a boat, I mean. You move like ballast."

"My mother was a deckhand on the lake freighters. Before." Before the workhouse, before whatever had come between, before the word *before* became the whole geography of her life. "She said a quiet person hears the river change its mind before loud people do."

"She was right." He adjusted the girl's grip with two fingers, no more. "Your throw today," he said to Maren, across the fire, because some things could only be said sideways, to the flames, to nobody in particular. "Upstream lead. Most grown men can't make that throw sober on a calm morning."

"Most grown men haven't spent their lives around people who fall in things," Maren said. "My father fell in things constantly. Rivers, crevasses, political arguments. Someone had to develop a skill."

Bram laughed — a real laugh, rusty, surprised out of him — and the sound sat down among them like a fourth chair at a table that had been set for three.

* * *

Later, when the fire had settled to coals and Bram had taken first watch with the rifle across his knees and his eyes on the dark water, Silas lay awake listening to the river run. It sounded different at night. Bigger. Older. A thing that had been saying the same sentence for ten thousand years and would still be saying it when every name on this expedition was weather.

Tess was asleep — actually asleep, finally, the shivering gone — with the practice rope still looped loosely around one wrist like a bracelet. She'd tied forty bowlines before her eyes gave out. The last six had been perfect. He'd counted.

*We tie you down,* he'd told her, and heard what he'd actually said a moment too late, the way you always do. Nobody had tied him down in eleven years. That was the whole of the blacklist, really, boiled to its essence: a man so determined never to lose anyone again that he'd arranged to belong nowhere, and called the arrangement freedom.

Maren stirred in her blanket across the fire. "Silas."

"Hm."

"In the rapid. You had the sweep. You could have come after her." Her voice was low, level, neither accusation nor absolution. Just cartography — mapping the fact of it.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Because the Vane Glacier, he thought. Because I jumped once, and the boat I left went into the ice without a helmsman, and the men in it trusted me, and trust is a debt the dead collect forever. Because holding the helm is the whole religion of my disgrace and I have never once been forgiven for either keeping it or breaking it.

"Because the boat was the rescue," he said. "All of it. Everyone in it. She'd tell you the same, if you asked her."

"I'm not going to ask her. I'm asking you."

He was quiet a while. The coals ticked. Somewhere upstream, the Narrows kept arguing with the sea.

"Ask me again someday," he said finally, "when I've got a better answer. I'm working on one. It's slow going."

Maren considered this, the way she considered contour lines — seriously, completely, looking for what was hidden underneath. "Fair," she said, and rolled over, and was asleep within minutes, because people who walk their maps sleep well.

Silas stayed awake a while longer, watching the fire, and it was near dawn when he noticed the thing that pulled him upright with his heart going like the Narrows.

Tess, asleep, wrist roped, breathing slow — and beneath her blanket, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs where she'd hidden it since the forward camp, the corner of a waterproof document case. Survey-stamped. Bureau issue.

Her father's cache, he thought, sitting very still in the grey light. She'd found something. She'd been carrying it since the camp with the fresh grave, quiet as ballast, saying nothing, waiting — for what? For trust, maybe. For a bowline tied forty times until the hands learned it.

Or maybe she'd simply been waiting for someone to ask.

The sun came up over the eastern ridge and lit the river gold, and Silas Dunmore looked at the sleeping girl with the survey case against her ribs, and understood that the next conversation on this boat was going to change everything — again.
