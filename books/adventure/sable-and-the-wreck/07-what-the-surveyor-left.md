## 7. What the Surveyor Left

The cache was exactly where it shouldn't have been.

That was the first thing Maren noticed about it, standing hip-deep in the alder thicket while the morning fog burned off the Kestrel in long gray ribbons. Her father had taught her that a cache went somewhere *boring*. Under a rock shelf. In the root ball of a dead tree. Somewhere a tired man's eye would slide past without catching. What it did not do was sit in the most conspicuous hollow on the riverbank, marked by a cairn of stones stacked with the particular neatness of a man who alphabetized his bootlaces.

"He wanted it found," she said.

"By us," Silas said. "Or by anyone."

"By *us*." She was already wading in. Bram hung back at the treeline with the rifle, doing his quiet constable thing, which mostly consisted of looking at the forest as though it owed him money. Tess crouched at the edge of the alders, pretending not to be excited and failing completely. The girl had developed a habit over the past two days of orbiting whatever Maren did, close enough to help, far enough to pretend she wasn't hovering. It should have been irritating. It was, instead, the only reason Maren hadn't come apart entirely since the Narrows.

The cairn came apart in her hands stone by stone. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with pitch, lay a tin box the size of a bread loaf. Her hands knew the wrapping before her mind caught up — the double fold at the corners, the pitch smeared in two parallel strokes. Her father's habit. He'd said once, laughing, that if he were ever murdered, the killer would be identified by his own tidy knots.

She hadn't laughed at that memory in four days. She didn't laugh now. But she also didn't drop the box, which felt like its own kind of victory.

"Maren." Silas's voice, careful. "You want a minute?"

"I want to open it before my hands decide otherwise."

Inside the tin: a brass compass she recognized, its bezel dented from the time Aldous Oduya had dropped it off a cliff in the Corries and then spent an entire evening apologizing to it. A roll of survey plates in waxed paper. And the field journal, bound in green canvas, its spine cracked in the familiar way, its pages swollen slightly with damp despite every precaution — because her father had never met a waterproof container he fully trusted, and so had written everything twice, in pencil and then in ink, like a man arguing with the future.

Maren sat down right there in the wet grass and opened it.

* * *

The early entries were almost funny, which was somehow the worst part.

*Day 3. Mosquitoes have organized. They have ranks now. I believe I saw a sergeant.*

*Day 5. Ferran insists the fish downstream of camp are inedible due to "mineral taste." Odile has eaten four. One of us is wrong and I decline to adjudicate.*

Odile Ferran. Maren filed the name away. The expedition roster Ilsa Vey had given them listed eleven men; she'd memorized it the way she'd once memorized tide tables, as a discipline against grief. Ferran, O., assistant surveyor. Twenty-six years old. There had been a photograph in the Bureau file, a narrow-faced young woman squinting into sun, and Maren had looked at it longer than she'd meant to.

She read aloud sometimes without deciding to. Tess crept closer for the mosquito entry and made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost a sob, and Maren reached out without looking and squeezed her wrist, and that was that; the girl stayed, cross-legged in the grass, close enough that their shoulders touched.

Then the entries changed.

*Day 9. Ran the lower gorge. Readings anomalous. Iron, copper — and something in the pan that Vey's preliminary reports never mentioned. Sending samples forward with Kessler.*

*Day 11. Assays back. God help me.*

Her father had underlined it twice, and beneath it, in his small, relentless hand, the numbers. Maren read them three times because they refused to be real. Ore body estimates along the source valley, running figures that would have bought Harrow's Bend outright and paved it in silver. Not a claim. Not a strike. A *province*, buried under the ice at the top of the Kestrel, sitting inside concession land that was supposed to be surveyed for a cartographic bureau and nothing else.

"No wonder," Silas said quietly. He'd drifted near without her noticing, arms folded, reading upside down the way guides learn to. "No wonder they went silent."

"They didn't go silent," Maren said. "They were silenced. There's a difference, and I intend to make everyone in the capital understand it."

*Day 12,* the journal went on. *Vey's advance man arrived unannounced. Polite. Too polite. Asked to see our assay copies "for Bureau records." Told him the Bureau would receive the full report at term's end, as contracted. He smiled like a man being patient with a child who hasn't yet understood the joke.*

*Day 13. Made duplicate plates. Hid originals. Location known only to myself and F. If you are reading this and I am not the one who cached it, then either F. is alive or someone very determined is not.*

Maren stopped. Read it again. *Known only to myself and F.*

"She knows where the plates are," Tess whispered.

"If she's alive," Bram said from the treeline, not turning around. His voice was flat in the way she'd learned meant he was keeping himself flat on purpose, like ballast. "If."

Maren turned pages with fingers that had gone strangely steady. Grief, she was discovering, was not the drowning thing she'd expected. It was more like cold water — shocking at first, then simply present, then something the body learned to work inside of. Every page in her father's handwriting was a small resurrection and a small funeral at once. She could hear his voice in it. She could hear him *underlining*.

*Day 14. They know we know.*

The last entry. No day after it. No closing line, no tidy knot. Just those five words, pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the grooves on the reverse side with her thumb, the way you'd read a letter written by someone desperate not to be overheard.

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, in the pencil draft his ink had covered:

*If Aldous Oduya does not come home, tell Maren the river was worth it. Tell her I saw the source. Tell her—*

The sentence stopped. Not faded. Stopped, mid-line, the pencil stroke trailing off as though the hand holding it had been called away, or had decided there wasn't time, or — she made herself think it — had heard something in the dark outside the tent.

Tell her what? She sat with the unfinished sentence the way you sit with a door left open in winter. There was no version of finishing it that didn't hurt. There was no version of leaving it alone that didn't hurt worse.

Tess put her hand flat on the journal page, below the broken sentence, as if to hold the words down. "He was going to," the girl said. "Whatever it was. He was going to tell you. People don't write things like that unless they mean to finish them."

"You can't know that."

"I know about unfinished sentences." Tess said it simply, without drama, the way she said most true things, and Maren understood suddenly that this fourteen-year-old had a whole archive of letters from a mother nobody had ever told her about, and that she carried them the way soldiers carry shrapnel — not visibly, but always.

Maren closed the journal. Held it against her chest. The tin, the compass, the plates: evidence, all of it, but it took her a moment to remember that word, *evidence*, because what she was actually holding was her father's voice, and grief was sharpening itself into something with an edge, and the edge had a direction, and the direction was upstream.

* * *

They broke camp within the hour. Silas ran the boat through a stretch of shallow braids with the particular economy of a man who'd done it angry before, and Maren sat in the bow with the journal wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, reading it again in the intervals between rapids, because once was not going to be enough and twice probably wouldn't either.

Bram took the middle seat and, somewhere past the second bend, said, without preamble: "Vey sent an advance man. Day twelve. That means the company knew what your father had found before the expedition ended."

"Yes."

"And Vey hired us to find out what happened to them."

"Yes."

"So either she doesn't know what her own sponsors were doing—" He let it hang.

"Or she does," Maren finished, "and we're carrying exactly the proof she needs us to find, straight to whoever wants it found." She turned a page. "My father trusted the Bureau his whole life. He used to say maps were the honest profession, because the land can't lie even when people do."

"And now?"

"Now I think the land is the only witness left that can't be bought." She looked up at him. "Which is why they killed eleven men to keep it quiet."

"Ten," Bram said. "Eleven minus one. Your father's note says Ferran might still be alive."

Maren had been carefully not doing that arithmetic. Now she did it all at once, and it landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. *Known only to myself and F.* If Odile Ferran had run, or hidden, or been taken rather than killed — then somewhere ahead of them, upriver, in country that had already swallowed ten good people, there was a twenty-six-year-old assistant surveyor who knew where the original plates were cached, and who was either the most important living witness in the territory or already dead in a ditch.

"We find her," Maren said.

Silas, at the tiller, didn't turn around. "We find the truth. If she's part of it, we find her too."

"That's a guide's answer."

"It's the only kind I've got left."

Tess, wedged amidships among the remaining crates — half their supplies were at the bottom of the Sable Narrows, and the girl had taken the loss personally, as though the river had robbed her specifically — spoke up. "The journal says Day Fourteen was the last entry. How far upriver is that?"

Maren checked the bearings her father had logged against the map sheets Bram had spread across a crate. "Two days' hard poling. Maybe three, with the current against us and half our food at the bottom of a rapid."

"Then we're two days behind whatever happened," Tess said, "and somebody else is maybe hours behind *us*." She nodded upstream, and Maren followed her gaze.

Smoke.

Not cookfire smoke — that rose thin and domestic, a single gray thread. This was heavy, dark, industrial, hanging over the valley ahead in a smear that no survey camp had any business making. Machinery, Maren thought, and then thought: *or a bonfire of things somebody needed destroyed.* Papers. Instruments. Evidence.

Silas brought the boat alongside a gravel bar and held it there with the pole, studying the horizon the way other men studied faces. Whatever he saw, he didn't share it. He just said, in the mild tone he used when the news was worst:

"Rations are short, the current's against us, and there's smoke where there shouldn't be smoke. So. Last chance to swim home."

Nobody moved.

"Didn't think so," Silas said, and pushed off.

Maren opened the journal one more time as the bank slid past, and read the last line again — *they know we know* — and then wrote, beneath it, in her own hand, in ink, the way her father would have:

*Day 16. We know too. We're coming.*
