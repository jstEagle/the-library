## 8. The Company Boat

Bram Hale had spent eleven years learning to read fog, and the fog on the Kestrel that morning was lying to him.

It lay in long banks along the water, white and soft as wool, and through the gaps the river showed itself in pieces — a heron standing on one leg like a clerk waiting to be paid, a gravel bar, the green wall of the far bank sliding past. It was beautiful. It was also the kind of morning a man could put a rifle through a boat hull from two hundred yards and never be seen doing it.

Bram sat in the stern with the tiller line in one hand and his tea going cold in the other, and he did not like the morning at all.

"You're making that face again," Tess said. She was perched on the cargo crates with her knees drawn up, mending a tear in her sleeve with stitches that were, frankly, better than his. "The one where you look like the tea insulted you."

"The tea *did* insult me," Bram said. "It's the same tea we've had for three days. It's insulting me on principle now."

"Drink it anyway."

He drank it anyway. That was the arrangement with Tess: she needled, he complied, and neither of them acknowledged that this was the closest thing either of them had to a routine. The girl had a way of anchoring a boat. He suspected she didn't know it, and he suspected Silas Dunmore knew it perfectly well and would rather be shot than say it out loud.

Ahead, Maren stood in the bow with her father's field journal open against her chest, reading the river the way her father had taught her, presumably — looking for the bend where the survey party had turned off the main channel. Silas crouched beside her, one hand on the gunwale, saying little, pointing occasionally. The two of them had developed a shorthand in the last two days that Bram found quietly impressive and quietly worrying. People who communicated in shorthand were people who'd stopped saying the important things out loud.

The fog closed around them like a held breath.

And then, through it — a sound.

Bram set down the tea.

It was nothing, at first. An engine note so faint it might have been his own boat, reflected off a cliff face. But his own engine had a knock in it, a tired, honest knock, like an old man clearing his throat. This sound had no knock. This sound was smooth. Expensive smooth. The kind of smooth that came out of a company boathouse and not a border-town salvage yard.

"Silas," Bram said, quietly.

Silas turned. He'd heard it too — Bram could see it in the set of his shoulders before he saw it in his face. That was the thing about Dunmore that the blacklist had never managed to erase: the man heard rivers the way Maren read them. Instinct first, explanation after.

"Kill the engine," Silas said. "Tess, down behind the crates. Maren —"

But Maren was already flat in the bow, journal pressed under her, and Bram was already shipping the oars, and the boat slid on its own momentum into a fog bank thick enough to chew.

They drifted. The engine note came again — closer now, off the port side, upstream of them and closing. Smooth. Patient. Whoever was running that boat wasn't hunting. They were *herding*. There was a difference, and Bram knew the difference the way you know a scar: he'd helped write the manual on it.

Because here was the thing Bram Hale had not told them. Not on the dock at Harrow's Bend, not through the rapids, not around the fire at night when Tess asked her careful questions and he gave her his careful, partial answers. He had told them he was an ex-constable, which was true. He had told them he'd walked away from it, which was also true, in the way that "the ship sank" is true of a voyage.

What he had not told them was *which* constabulary, and *why*, and what exactly a man trained to stop exactly this kind of thing was doing arriving at the exact moment this kind of thing needed stopping.

The fog thinned for a heartbeat.

Through the gap, perhaps a hundred yards off the port beam, a shape resolved: long, low, painted a grey so deliberate it wasn't camouflage so much as a statement — *we do not need to hide from you*. Along its bow ran a painted band of green and white, and above the band, a flag. Concession colors. The Aldervane Charter Company, or whatever name it was trading under this decade; the colors stayed the same even when the letterhead changed. Bram had seen those colors on a dozen river stations, and he had stood under them in a constable's coat, and he had saluted them.

On the patrol boat's deck, three figures. One at the helm. One forward with binoculars. One amidships, leaning on the rail, utterly relaxed, a rifle slung across his back like it was a guitar at a party.

Bram knew that lean.

His stomach did something complicated and old.

The fog closed again. The engine note faded upstream, unhurried, and then — this was the part that turned Bram's blood to well-water — it stopped. Not faded. *Stopped*. Somewhere ahead of them in the white, the patrol boat had shut down its engine and was waiting.

"They know where we are," Maren whispered. She'd come aft without a sound, journal clutched to her chest. "Why aren't they — why don't they just come for us?"

"Because coming for us is work," Bram said. "And work leaves marks. They don't need marks yet. They need us to know they're here." He heard his own voice go flat and official, the constable's voice, the one he'd sworn off, and he hated it and used it anyway. "It's called shadowing. You stay just outside reach. You let the quarry feel the leash. Most people turn back. Turning back is free for them."

"And if we don't turn back?"

"Then eventually they stop shadowing."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Tess's head appeared above the crates, and Silas didn't tell her to get down, which from Silas was practically a hug.

"That flag," Maren said. "You looked at it like you'd seen a ghost."

"I looked at it like I'd seen a *uniform*," Bram said. And then, because the fog was sitting on them and the engine was silent ahead and there was no good time left to say it, he heard himself add: "There's something I should have told you at the dock."

Silas turned around fully for the first time. He didn't say *go on*. He just turned, and waited, and somehow that was worse.

"I wasn't border constabulary," Bram said. "I was Charter Police. Concession enforcement. Eleven years. The last four of them, I trained the river units. Patrol tactics, boarding procedures, how to shadow a boat through fog." He nodded at the white wall around them. "That boat ahead is running my curriculum. The man leaning on the rail — I can't see his face, but I know the lean. That's Doss. Ferren Doss. I taught him that lean. I told him a relaxed posture made a subject underestimate you. I gave him *that*, like a gift."

Tess had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Working-still. Bram was beginning to recognize the difference, and it unnerved him more than the patrol boat did.

"Why did you leave?" Maren asked. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — *curious*. She had her father's way of asking a question like she already had half the answer and just wanted to see if you'd match your half to hers.

Bram looked at his cold tea. He looked at the fog. He took the long way around, the way he'd been taking it for four years.

"There was a station on the Marrow Fork. Upstream of a village called Tessel. The Company wanted the fork surveyed — officially. Unofficially, they wanted the village moved, and the survey was going to find whatever it needed to find to make the moving legal. I wrote a report saying the survey readings had been altered. My commanding officer thanked me, commended my thoroughness, and filed the report somewhere that reports go to die. Six months later the village was gone. Compensation was paid. On paper, it was all very clean." He turned the cup in his hands. "I resigned the week after. Not because I thought resigning would fix anything. Because I couldn't stand under those colors one more day without being sick on my boots."

"And the Kestrel?" Silas said. His voice was careful in the way of a man walking on river ice. "Why this river? Why this boat? Don't tell me it was the pay. I'm not paying enough for a man to lie to me this well."

Bram almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a cough.

"The Kestrel concession was in my last portfolio. The renewal filings crossed my desk. I saw the boundaries, the expiry dates, the — the *shape* of the thing. The way the maps had been drawn so that the source valley sat just outside every jurisdiction that could inspect it." He looked up. "When the survey went silent, and Ilsa Vey started hiring, I knew two things. I knew the Company did it. And I knew that if anyone was going to be able to recognize the men they sent after you — know their names, their habits, their *training* — it was going to be me."

The fog held them. Somewhere ahead, water moved over stones, and the silence where the engine had been sat on the river like a hand.

"So you came to be useful," Tess said.

"I came to be *sorry*," Bram said. "Which is a worse reason to get on a boat, if you're asking. Useful gets people home. Sorry gets people killed, usually the wrong people." He set the cup down. "I should have told you at the dock. Silas had a right to know he was shipping with a man the Company trained. If Doss sees my face —"

"Doss will see your face," Silas said, "when I decide he sees it. Not before." He crouched, and Bram watched the guide do the arithmetic he'd been doing since the engine note first reached them: current, drift, the bend ahead, the fog's lifespan. "They've shut down ahead of us, which means they think we'll come to them, or turn. We're going to do neither. There's a back channel on the east side — braided water, gravel, too shallow for their hull. It costs us two hours and most of our dignity."

"You've run it?" Maren asked.

"I got blacklisted for less," Silas said, and there it was — the ghost of the grin, the one that had probably gotten him into half his trouble. "Bram. Your curriculum. If they've posted a man on the east bank to watch the shallows —"

"Then he'll be at the *mouth* of the channel, because that's where I would have told him to stand," Bram said. "And he'll be watching the water, not the treeline, because I told him boats come down rivers." He paused. "I also told him that anyone smart enough to use the shallows would come in above the mouth and walk the bank first. So if you want my professional advice, as the man who wrote the book we're currently cheating from: send Tess up the bank."

Every head turned to Tess.

Tess looked, if anything, pleased. "I knew the sewing would come in handy."

* * *

The back channel was every bit as undignified as promised. They poled through water barely a hand deep in places, the hull grinding on gravel with a sound like teeth being set on edge, while Tess moved along the eastern bank ahead of them — low, quick, absurdly quiet, a girl-shaped shadow among the alders. Twice she stopped and held up a hand, and twice they waited, hearts in throats, while she watched something they couldn't see and decided it was nothing. The second time, she came back to the bank's edge and held up three fingers, then pointed downstream, then made a gesture that Bram eventually decoded as *a heron, you idiots, a heron*, and Maren had to put her face against the gunwale to keep from laughing out loud and giving them all away.

They came out of the braids below the patrol boat's position, and Bram got one clean look back through a parting in the fog.

The patrol boat sat in the main channel, engine off, drifting at anchor. Doss was still at the rail. And now Bram could see his face — older than he remembered, harder around the mouth, but the same. The same man he'd stood in front of a classroom of river constables and taught to read wakes, to estimate cargo weight by waterline, to lean on a rail like the day was long and the quarry was already caught.

Doss was looking downstream. Watching the water. Exactly where Bram had taught him to look.

Bram raised two fingers off the gunwale — not a wave, nothing that would carry meaning at distance, just a small, private, bitter salute — and then the fog took the boat back.

They made six miles before the fog burned off, and the sun came down on the river like a benediction, and for an hour it was almost possible to believe they'd slipped the leash.

Then Tess, up at the bow on watch, said, "There's smoke. Ahead. On the east bank."

Bram came forward. Thin and grey, a cookfire's worth, rising from the trees above the water — and beside it, on the shingle, pulled up and covered with brush, a long grey hull.

The patrol boat had gone to ground ahead of them. Not behind. Ahead.

"They're not herding us anymore," Maren said quietly, beside him.

"No," Bram said. His mouth was dry. He knew what came after shadowing; he'd written that page too. "Now they're setting the trap. And there's only one way through that isn't the river."

Silas had come forward, and he was looking up — up at the white shoulder of the gorge, where the trees gave out and the portage trail climbed toward the falls, the route every freight crew used and every freight crew hated.

"Portage," Silas said. "Around the falls, over the shoulder, back to the water below. Two days of carrying everything we own on our backs, through country where a man with a rifle owns the high ground." He glanced at Bram, and something passed between them that wasn't quite comfort and wasn't quite apology. "Your book have a chapter on that?"

"No," Bram said. "That chapter's ours to write."

Above the falls, faint on the wind, a bird called — and Bram, who had spent eleven years learning every sound that country could make, could not decide whether it was a heron, or a man imitating one very well.

Tess was already counting the crates, lips moving, working out what four backs could carry and what they'd have to burn.

"Then we'd best decide what we can't live without," she said, "and what we can."
