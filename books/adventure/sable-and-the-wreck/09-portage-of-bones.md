## 9. Portage of Bones

Tess had learned three things about boats in the four days since she'd been dragged out of a cargo crate and handed a bilge sponge instead of a ticket back to Harrow's Bend.

One: boats were heavier than they looked. Two: everyone on this crew lied about how tired they were. Three: Silas Dunmore counted things in his sleep — she'd heard him doing it through the canvas the second night, low numbers over and over like a prayer that had lost its religion.

She was about to learn a fourth thing, which was that rivers had opinions about all of them.

The waterfall announced itself long before they saw it — a sound like the sky tearing along a seam, growing louder until Maren stopped rowing and just looked at Silas, and Silas looked at the water, and nobody said anything because there was nothing to say that the noise wasn't already saying.

"Portage," Silas said finally, steering for the left bank. "Half a mile around, maybe more."

"Portage," Bram repeated, in the voice of a man who had once believed his knees were decorative.

Tess kept her mouth shut, which was her specialty. She'd gotten very good at it in the workhouse — shut mouth, open eyes, small hands busy with something useful so nobody remembered you existed until they needed something carried. It was a survival strategy, and like most survival strategies, it looked from the outside like nothing at all.

The bank was black rock and old flood debris, and hauling the boat up it took everything the four of them had. They rigged ropes through the thwarts, Silas calling the pulls in a flat river-voice that never rose no matter how bad things got, and Tess learned that this was how he managed fear — by giving it a job to do. She approved. She did the same thing with hers, except her jobs were smaller: hold this line, wedge that crate, don't cry, don't be sick, don't think about the sound the waterfall was making, which was starting to remind her of something she didn't want reminded of.

They got the boat onto the portage trail — barely a trail, more a suggestion the land made grudgingly — and began the long staggered walk, Silas and Bram under the hull's ribs, Maren with the supply packs, Tess carrying what she could carry, which she had decided would always be slightly more than anyone expected.

That was when she smelled it.

Not the waterfall. Not the wet stone and rotting leaves. Something sweeter and wrong underneath, the way a room smells wrong before you know why.

She stopped. The others kept walking, boots slipping on moss, and then Maren noticed Tess standing still and came back, and then Maren smelled it too, and Maren's face did a thing that told Tess everything before any words did.

"Tess." Maren's voice was careful. "Maybe wait here—"

"I've seen dead people before," Tess said. It came out flatter than she meant it to, the way her words sometimes went flat when she actually cared about them. "Two winters ago. Fever took half our ward. I helped lay them out."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Tess's face seemed to settle something, because she didn't argue. She just said, "Stay behind me anyway," and Tess thought: *that's fair*, and fell into step.

* * *

They were off the trail, down among the flood-tumbled boulders near the water's edge, where the spray hung in the air like weather that couldn't commit to being rain.

Tess saw the first one before she understood she was seeing him.

He was sitting against a boulder as if resting, which was the trick her eyes played for about three seconds before her mind caught up and corrected the picture. He wore a survey coat, green wool, Bureau buttons. There was a hole above his collarbone and another in his forehead, close together, tidy, deliberate. His instruments case lay beside him, opened, and someone had taken the time to smash everything inside it — the theodolite beaten into brass petals, the lenses ground to powder under a boot heel. Not robbed. *Erased.*

The second man was ten feet farther on, face-down, arms out toward the water as if he'd been crawling. Same wounds. Same careful destruction scattered around him: a chronometer crushed, a field notebook torn into pieces fine as confetti and weighted down with stones so the river would finish the job.

Nobody spoke. The waterfall filled the silence, roaring its one enormous syllable over and over.

Bram crouched by the sitting man and did constable things without seeming to decide to — tilting his head, checking the ground, reading the story written in bootprints and scuff marks. "Kneeling," he said quietly. "Both of them kneeling when it happened. Close range. And then—" he nodded at the shattered instruments, "—they made sure of the rest."

"They weren't robbed," Maren said. Her voice had gone thin. She was staring at the survey coat, at the name tape stitched inside the collar. Tess watched her read it and watched her lips move around the name without sound. Someone from her father's expedition. Someone whose handwriting might have been in the margins of her childhood maps.

"No," Silas said. "Robbers take the good brass and go. This is tidying up." He stood looking downstream, toward the source, toward whatever waited up there, and Tess saw his jaw work once. "Somebody wants the valley to stay empty on paper."

Tess didn't say anything. She was looking at the man's hands.

They were workhouse hands. She knew workhouse hands the way other children knew their mothers' faces — the cracked knuckles, the nails cut short and ragged, the burn scar across the left palm from a boiler or a press. This man had been poor before he'd been a surveyor. He'd climbed out of somewhere like the place she'd run from, using his head instead of his back, and somebody had knelt him down on a riverbank and put two bullets in him and smashed his beautiful instruments because of what those instruments had seen.

Something in Tess's chest went very quiet and very hard, like water freezing in a crack.

"We should bury them," she said.

Everyone turned. Even the waterfall seemed to pause, though of course it didn't; waterfalls never paused for anything, which was maybe why she liked them.

"It'll take hours," Bram said gently. "And the ground here is all rock—"

"There's gravel by the high-water line." She'd already seen it; she saw everything, that was her whole job in life. "We can stack stones. My brother and I buried a dog once. It holds if you stack them right."

Silas looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then he set down his end of the boat rope and said, "Stack them right, then," and started gathering stones, and that was that.

* * *

It took the rest of the day, and it was the hardest work Tess had ever done, harder than the rapids, harder than the crates she'd hidden among, harder even than the years of pretending not to mind things.

They laid the men side by side above the high-water mark, facing upstream, because Silas said rivermen faced upstream and Tess decided that was a rule worth keeping. Maren found a pencil stub in the dead man's pocket — the only instrument the killers hadn't bothered with, too small to matter — and she held it in her fist the whole time like it was burning her and warming her at once. Bram dug with an entrenching tool and swore softly at the rocks and apologized to no one in particular. Silas worked in silence, moving stones with the economy of a man who had buried people before and hated that he was good at it.

Tess stacked the cairn.

She found she was good at it. You couldn't rush stones; they told you where they wanted to go if you listened with your hands. Flat on flat, heart-stone in the middle, each layer a little smaller, until the pile stood waist-high and solid and wouldn't fall in a hundred floods. She worked methodically, choosing each rock, testing its weight, setting it true. Her eyes stung but she didn't let anything fall. Crying was allowed — she'd decided that much, after the fever winter, after she'd seen the matron cry herself — but crying while working was wasteful, and waste was the one sin the workhouse had taught her to despise.

"You're steady," Bram said at one point, quietly, passing her a stone. "Steadier than me."

"Somebody has to be," Tess said. "You're all busy being sad."

Bram laughed — a surprised, cracked sort of laugh — and then looked ashamed of laughing, and Tess shook her head. "No. Laughing's fine. Laughing's how you keep the sad from eating you. We laughed in the ward too. The matron said it was disrespectful and we did it anyway, quieter."

Bram looked at her the way adults sometimes did, like she was a locked drawer they hadn't known the cabinet contained. He didn't say anything else. He just handed her bigger stones after that.

When the cairn was done, Maren stood over it a long time with the pencil stub in her hand. Then she wedged it upright between two stones at the top, a tiny wooden marker, and said, "His name was Aldous Wren. He taught me to draw contour lines when I was nine. He used to say a map was a promise you made to strangers." Her voice held. Barely. "I'm going to keep his promise."

Tess reached up and touched the cairn once, flat-palmed, the way you'd touch a horse to tell it *easy now*. Then she turned and went back to the boat, because there was still a boat to carry and a waterfall to get around and nineteen days — no, eighteen now — ticking away somewhere above the treeline, and grief that stopped working was just another way of lying down.

* * *

They finished the portage at dusk, relaunching below the falls into water gone the color of pewter. Nobody talked much. The day had wrung them out and wrung something else out of them too — some last comfortable idea that this was an adventure, that the worst thing ahead was rapids and bad rations.

Tess sat in the bow, because Silas had started putting her there, saying she had the best eyes, which was true, and also, she suspected, because he'd figured out that she watched the banks the way a watch-dog watches a road, and he'd decided to make use of it rather than waste it.

The camp that night was cold food and no fire, Silas's call, after the graves. They ate hardtack and dried fish in the dark, and Tess chewed and listened to the river and felt the new hard thing in her chest settle into place like a keel.

"So," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to start feeling sorry for itself. "Whoever killed them is between us and the source. Probably lots of somebodies. With guns."

"Thank you, Tess," Silas said dryly, "for that cheerful summary."

"Someone had to say it. You were all doing the thing where you pretend the next day will be better." She broke her hardtack precisely in half and ate one half. "In the workhouse we called that Tuesday thinking. Every night you think tomorrow won't be Tuesday. Every morning it's Tuesday."

"And what do you call today?" Maren asked.

Tess considered. "Today was a Tuesday," she said. "But we stacked the stones right. So tomorrow we're still floating."

There was a pause, and then Bram snorted into his fish, and even Silas made a sound that in a louder man would have been a laugh, and Maren reached over in the dark and squeezed Tess's shoulder once, brief and hard, like sealing a letter.

Later, wrapped in her blanket, Tess heard the adults talking low by the boat. She was supposed to be asleep. She was excellent at supposed-to-be.

"—two shots each, kneeling, and then the instruments," Bram was murmuring. "That's not panic. That's procedure. Somebody wrote this down before it happened."

"Then whoever wrote it knows exactly who's coming upriver," Silas said. "And what they're carrying."

A silence. The river went on saying its one word.

"Then we'd best be worth writing down," Maren said.

Tess stared up at the stars through the gap in the trees and pressed her tongue against the new hard place in her chest, the way you test a healing wound, checking it will hold.

It would hold. She'd stacked it herself.

And far upstream, faint through the trees, so faint she almost doubted it — but she never doubted her own ears, that was the whole point of her — she heard something that was not the waterfall, not the river, not wind.

Metal. Rhythmic. Like machinery, chewing slowly through stone.

She lay still and listened until she was sure, and then she sat up and said, into the dark, in the flattest voice she owned:

"Silas. You're going to want to hear this."
