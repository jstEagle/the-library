## 10. Turn Back or Go Up

The fire was small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and Silas built it that way on purpose. Twelve days of permit left, and he was crouched under an overhang of river stone like a man cooking supper in a confessional.

He'd laid it all out on the flat rock between them: the map, the journal pages Maren had copied before Quill ever set foot aboard — thank whatever gods watched over fools for her suspicion — the stub of pencil he'd been chewing since noon. The others sat around it the way people sit around a doctor who hasn't spoken yet.

"Say it plain," Bram said. He was cleaning his rifle with the methodical patience of a man who'd rather be doing anything else. "You've had that look since the portage. Like a man doing sums he doesn't want to finish."

Silas spat out the pencil. "Fine. Plain, then." He tapped the map. "We're here. The boundary post is four days downstream if we run light and the weather holds. The source valley is six days up, maybe seven, counting the falls country. The permits expire in twelve days. After that, Aldervane's concession lapses, the valley seals for ten years, and everything in it — every cage, every guard, every piece of ore they pulled out of the ground — goes dark until the next round of bribes gets signed."

"And the plates," Maren said quietly.

"And the plates," Silas agreed. "Her father's originals. The readings. Whatever proof exists that this was murder and not misadventure. If we come down that river without them, Ilsa Vey gets a boat full of grieving relatives and a story about bandits, and the company gets its decade of quiet."

Tess, curled against a crate with her knees drawn up, raised her hand like she was still in school. It was such an absurdly polite gesture in the middle of all this that Silas almost smiled.

"You don't have to raise your hand, Tess."

"I know. It's habit." She lowered it halfway, then thought better and put it back up. "What happens to Odile? And the others in the cages?"

And there it was. The question he'd been circling all day like a crow over a carcass.

"If we go down," Silas said, "the mine keeps running right up to permit's end. Then they seal it. My guess is they don't leave witnesses behind when they do."

Nobody said anything for a while. The river talked to itself around the bend below, patient as ever, as if it hadn't already eaten half their supplies and nearly eaten Tess besides.

* * *

He'd spent the morning doing what he did best, which was arithmetic nobody wanted done. It was a blacklisted guide's particular talent: knowing exactly how much food, how much daylight, how much luck a stretch of water would cost you, and then adding the margin for error that separated a hard trip from a fatal one.

The math came out ugly both directions.

Downstream: four days to the post, safe-ish water, and a life. Also silence. Also ten years of whatever happened behind that boundary happening without anyone to say otherwise. Also Odile Ferran's face, which he hadn't even seen yet and somehow couldn't stop seeing — a woman alive enough to talk, according to the notes Maren had taken from her father's journal, which meant a woman the company would very much prefer stopped being either.

Upstream: seven days through forbidden country, past enforcers who knew the river better than they did, toward a valley where the mining company had already murdered at least five people and shown no signs of losing its appetite. Carrying less food than the trip deserved because the Sable Narrows had taken their share off the ledger.

"Twelve days," he said now, to the fire more than to them. "Seven up, four down, if nothing goes wrong. Something always goes wrong. So really it's eleven days of work in a twelve-day window, and the twelfth day is the one where we find out what kind of liars we are."

"That's not math," Tess said. "That's a eulogy with numbers in it."

Bram snorted. Even Maren's mouth twitched, though she kept her eyes on the map.

"It's honest math," Silas said. "I promised myself I'd give you that much. On the Vane Glacier I gave my clients pretty math. Rounded down the risks, rounded up the margins. Six people believed me." He let that sit. It was an old wound and he'd learned to stop picking at it, but they needed to see it. They were about to bet their lives on his judgment, and they deserved to know the judgment had a criminal record. "So. Questions. Objections. Tess, put your hand down, you can just talk."

She put it down. Then: "If we vote no, do you turn back anyway?"

The question landed harder than any enforcer's bullet could have. He looked at her — fourteen years old, workhouse-thin, saved from drowning by Maren's grip on her collar three days ago — and understood that she wasn't asking out of fear. She was asking because she wanted to know if the vote was real, or if it was theater, and Silas Dunmore had sat through enough theater in his life to hate the idea of putting on more.

"The vote's real," he said. "If it goes against me, I'll grumble the whole way downstream, but I'll steer us there. That's the deal. Majority rules, and I've been a minority opinion before. Usually about my own decisions."

* * *

Maren spoke first, which surprised him. He'd expected Bram.

"My father went up that river believing the survey mattered," she said. Her voice was steady in the way that cost something; he'd heard that steadiness before, in widows and in soldiers, and it never fooled him. "They killed him for a map. If I go home without finishing it, I'm telling them the map mattered more than the man, and I don't believe that. But I also can't tell him — wherever he is — that I quit at the last fork because the arithmetic scared me." She looked up. "I'm voting up. Not because I'm brave. Because going down feels like leaving him twice."

Silas nodded slowly. He didn't offer comfort. Comfort would have been an insult, and she'd have known it.

Bram racked the bolt of his rifle and set it across his knees. For a long moment the ex-constable studied the fire like it owed him money.

"I trained half the men on that patrol boat," he said. "Fed them, drilled them, taught them how to shoot straight and lie straighter. Every one of them thinks I died or deserted. Walking away didn't wash anything off me — I just got good at pretending it had." He turned the rifle over. "Going down means I stay washed-out. Going up means I finally aim at something worth aiming at. Even if I miss." A pause, and then, drier: "Also, I've read your steering, Dunmore. Downstream we're dead men anyway once they catch us in open water. Upstream at least we get to pick our ground."

"That's the first flattering thing anyone's said about my steering," Silas said, "and it contained the phrase 'dead men,' so I'm going to need a minute."

That got a laugh out of Tess — short, surprised, like it had escaped custody — and he counted it a victory. Three days ago the girl had flinched at loud noises. Now she was making jokes at funerals. He wasn't sure whether to be proud or worried, so he settled on both, which seemed to be the family business lately.

"Tess?" he asked. "You don't get a formal vote. You're fourteen and technically contraband. But I want to hear it anyway."

She uncurled and came forward to the rock, studying the map upside down, which she'd apparently decided was her preferred orientation.

"They locked me in a workhouse for two years for being poor," she said. "Those people in the cages didn't even do anything wrong. They just got found." She looked up at him, and her eyes were older than her years in the specific way that made his chest hurt. "If we run, I'll run with you. But I'll know what we ran from. I already know what that's like. I'd rather not add to the collection."

Silas looked at the fire for a long moment.

"Well," he said. "That settles the moral question and ruins my evening entirely."

* * *

They voted anyway, because he'd promised, and because rituals matter even — especially — when everyone knows the outcome. Four hands into the firelight, one after another, like a slow tide coming in.

"Up, then," Silas said. "God help us, up."

He started assigning loads before the word finished leaving his mouth, because the fastest cure for doubt is work. Maren took the journal copies and the bearings. Bram took ammunition inventory and pronounced it, in the tone of a man reading a diagnosis, "adequate for optimism, insufficient for confidence." Tess claimed the food crates and began repacking them with a ruthless efficiency that suggested the workhouse had taught her things no school could.

Silas stood apart for a moment, looking upstream.

The Kestrel narrowed ahead into the gorge country, and beyond the gorge, past the falls they'd have to portage, lay the source valley — the one that had ended his career, the one the maps marked with a surveyor's polite fiction, *unverified*, the one where a glacial cathedral held a river in its fist and, apparently, a fortune in ore under its floorboards. He'd sworn never to go back. He'd said it aloud, more than once, usually after drinking, which is where most vows live and die.

"You're doing the face again," Tess said beside him. He hadn't heard her come up. She moved quietly when she wanted to; he was starting a list of things she did when she wanted to, and the list was getting long.

"What face?"

"The one where you're arguing with a river." She squinted upstream. "Is it winning?"

"Rivers always win eventually. The trick is making them pay for it." He glanced down at her. "First lesson of gorge water: you don't fight the current, you negotiate with it. You find where it's lazy and you make your move there."

"Like Quill did."

The name dropped between them like a stone into still water. Silas felt his jaw tighten. Quill — Vey's man, planted among them from the first day, smiling and competent and signaling their position to the patrol boat with a lamp while they slept. Gone now into the forest with the journal pages proving the ore claim, and every hour he gained was an hour the enforcers gained.

"Nothing like Quill did," Silas said. "Quill sold people. We're going to spend ourselves instead. It's a better trade. You get to keep your soul, even if you spend everything else."

Tess considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, then nodded once, satisfied, and went back to her crates.

* * *

They broke camp at gray dawn, and the river had changed overnight the way rivers do — colder air rolling down from the heights, the water gone from brown to a green so dark it looked solid. Above the last stands of pine the gorge walls closed in, and the current quickened like something waking up hungry.

Silas took the sweep himself. Behind him, the crew did their jobs without being told: Maren reading the water ahead and calling the lines, Bram watching the banks with the rifle across his lap, Tess bailing with the grim rhythm of someone who had learned in three days that bailing was life.

At the mouth of the gorge he paused, letting the bow swing, and looked back once. Downstream lay the boundary post, safety, sense, the whole sensible world. Somewhere down there, too, was a patrol boat full of men Bram had trained, closing the distance at their leisure.

"Last chance to object," he said. "Once we're in the gorge, the river decides a lot of things for us."

"Steer, Dunmore," Bram said.

Silas grinned — the first real grin in days, crooked and unwelcome on his face, like a guest showing up at the wrong house — and put the bow upstream.

The gorge took them the way it takes everything: without malice and without mercy. But for the first time since Harrow's Bend, since the contract in ash, since the grave with the survey stake, Silas Dunmore felt something that wasn't dread. It took him a mile of white water to name it.

Purpose. Ugly, borrowed, probably doomed purpose — but his.

Then the first rapid rose up ahead, taller than it had any right to be, and somewhere above them on the canyon rim, faint but unmistakable, a rifle shot cracked off the stone and echoed down the gorge like a knock at the door.

"They found us," Maren called.

"No," Silas said, hauling the sweep hard over, finding the lazy water, making his move. "They found the gorge. There's a difference. Paddle."
