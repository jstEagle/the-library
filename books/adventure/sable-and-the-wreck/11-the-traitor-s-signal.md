## 11. The Traitor's Signal

Bram Hale had spent eleven years as a constable learning to read people the way Silas read water — the small eddies, the undertow beneath a calm surface. And for six days aboard the *Kestrel Maid*, he had been watching Quill and telling himself he was wrong.

He wasn't wrong. He was just late.

It began, as most of his bad nights did, with insomnia. The crew had made camp on a gravel shelf below a bluff where the river bent north, and Bram had taken the second watch because he'd stopped sleeping properly years ago and saw no reason to pretend otherwise. Tess was asleep in a nest of tarps by the fire ring, one boot off, her hat over her face like a small tent. Maren had gone down hard after dinner, still holding her father's journal against her chest the way other people held prayer books.

Silas sat up across the coals, cleaning the rifle that never seemed to need cleaning.

"You should sleep," Bram said.

"I'll sleep when we're past the Narrows." Silas worked an oiled rag along the barrel. "You hear something?"

"No."

"Then why'd you say it like you heard something?"

Bram didn't answer, because the honest answer was that he'd been hearing something for days without being able to name it. Quill's habits, mostly. The man was Ilsa Vey's clerk, sent along as "bureau observer," and he performed observation beautifully — always sketching, always noting, always pleasant in the flat way of a man reading lines off a card. But there were small things. He walked the perimeter at odd hours and called it stretching his legs. He asked Tess questions about the cargo manifest that a man who'd packed the manifest himself shouldn't need to ask. And twice now, when the patrol boat's lantern had shown through the fog downstream, Quill had been the first to spot it — quick, helpful, *there*.

A good constable learned early that the helpful ones were the ones to watch.

"I'm going to walk the bluff," Bram said, standing. "Stretch my legs."

Silas looked at him for a long moment. The firelight did things to his face that made him look older and more tired than he ever admitted to being. "Take the whistle."

"I don't need the whistle."

"Nobody needs the whistle until they need it. Take it."

Bram took it.

* * *

The bluff path was a goat track threaded through pine, and Bram climbed it slowly, letting his boots find the quiet places between stones. Below, the river ran black and silver under a half-moon, and the fog lay on it in long ropes, drifting upstream against all sense, which was the kind of thing this valley did just to remind you it made its own rules.

He reached the top and stopped.

There was a gap in the trees on the far side of the bluff — a natural window looking downriver — and through it, maybe two hundred yards along the ridge, a light blinked.

Three short flashes. A pause. Three again.

Then darkness, and then, faint as breath, the sound of oars — no engine, muffled oars — and a lantern answering from the water, low behind the fog bank. The patrol boat. Sitting dark, sitting quiet, exactly where a boat would sit if it wanted to receive a message rather than deliver one.

Bram stood very still in the cold and felt eleven years of constabulary training settle over him like a coat he'd sworn he'd burned.

Three-and-three. He knew the code. Everyone who'd served the border districts knew it — it was in the first-year manual, right next to the section on how to spot a smuggler's false stern. *Position confirmed. Approach at your discretion.* It was the signal enforcers used when a target was fixed and waiting.

Which meant someone on this ridge was fixing them.

He moved along the bluff track with his heart going slow and heavy, the way it did before arrests, and came around a stand of jack pine into a clearing of moonlit grass — and there was Quill, standing with his back to Bram, a shuttered hand-lamp in his fist, sending three more flashes out over the water with the calm precision of a man posting letters.

Bram had imagined this moment, in the last day or so, in the way you imagine a tooth going bad. He'd run the list of suspects the way he'd once run lists of poachers and payroll thieves. Silas — no; Silas lied about supplies but not about direction, and a man running from his own disgrace doesn't invite the thing that made it. Maren — no; grief like hers didn't bend toward treachery, it bent straight through stone. Tess — the thought was so absurd it almost made him laugh even now.

Quill. Bureau observer. Ilsa Vey's own appointment.

Planted from the start.

"Well," Bram said.

Quill didn't jump. That was the worst part — he simply closed the lamp's shutter with a soft click and turned around wearing the same mild, helpful face he wore at breakfast, as if they'd met on a stairwell.

"Constable Hale," he said. "Or is it Mister Hale? I can never remember what you are these days."

"Neither can I." Bram kept his voice level. His hand found the whistle in his pocket and didn't blow it yet. "How long?"

"How long what?"

"Don't do that. Don't make me work for it. I've hauled a boat around a waterfall with you. I've shared tea with you. Give me the courtesy of a straight answer." He stepped forward into the open. "How long have you been reporting our position?"

Quill considered him. Then, with a small sigh, like a man setting down a heavy case, he let the helpfulness drop away, and underneath it was someone older and harder and entirely unembarrassed.

"Since Harrow's Bend," he said. "Before, actually. I wrote the first report while you were still arguing over rifles."

"The manifest."

"You noticed. I wondered." Quill smiled faintly. "You were better at the job than they gave you credit for, Hale. That's why Vey wanted you on this crew. A former constable lends legitimacy. And a disgraced one—" he spread his free hand apologetically "—lends deniability. If it goes wrong, the record will show Silas Dunmore led a rescue of criminals and cranks. Nobody will look twice at the clerk."

"And if it goes right?"

"If it goes right, the survey plates and the journal pages end up in company hands, the concession gets quietly renewed on amended terms, and everyone goes home rich except the dead." Quill said it the way he'd recite shipping weights. "You've seen the ore readings by now. You know what's up that valley. Men have gone to war over less, and here you four are rowing toward it in a leaky boat with a child aboard."

"She's fourteen."

"She's a complication. So are you." Quill tilted his head toward the river. "Here's what happens now, and I say it as a friend, because I've genuinely come to like you, which is an occupational hazard I should guard against better. In an hour, that boat puts men ashore at the bend below camp. They take the documents. They take the witness material — the girl's father's effects, anything Odile-adjacent, though she's ahead of us now. And the four of you get written up as lost to the river, which, given the Narrows you insist on running, will surprise no one."

"And if we object?"

"Then the write-up becomes true early."

Bram breathed the cold air in through his nose. Somewhere below, faintly, he could hear Silas moving around the fire — the particular scrape of a kettle being set on, the domestic sounds of a man keeping watch by pretending to make tea. Tess would be awake soon; she woke at every sound and pretended she didn't. Maren slept like the drowned but only until dawn.

He thought about blowing the whistle. He thought about it very carefully, the way you think about a bridge before you put weight on it.

If he blew it, Quill ran, and they lost the element of surprise but kept the man? No — Quill wouldn't be caught; men like Quill were never caught at the moment of discovery, they were caught weeks later in a different jurisdiction when it no longer mattered. If he blew it, Quill ran *now*, into forest he'd clearly already mapped, and the patrol boat below would know within minutes that its signal had been interrupted, and would either withdraw or come up shooting in the dark.

And there was one more thing. One thing Quill didn't know Bram knew.

"You're carrying the pages," Bram said.

Quill's smile flickered — just barely, just at the corner. "Am I?"

"Maren re-cached the journal two nights ago. Split it. The readings went into the spare chart tube, wrapped in oilskin, in the boat's ballast. What's in your satchel is her decoy — the old coastal surveys, worthless." He let that land. "You've been guarding paper we wanted you to steal."

For the first time, Quill looked genuinely uncertain, and Bram watched him do the arithmetic — watched him weigh whether to believe it, whether to gamble, whether to cut losses. It lasted perhaps three seconds. Then Quill laughed softly, and it was almost admiring.

"The cartographer's daughter," he said. "Vey underestimated her. She won't forgive herself." He shifted his weight, casual as a man adjusting his stance on a train platform, and Bram saw the coiling begin under it. "Well. Then I'll want the real pages after all."

"Quill—"

"Give my regards to the girl. Tell her her father's bearings were excellent. We'd have never found the source cache without them."

That landed like a hook under Bram's ribs — *they already know where the plates are* — and in the half-second it took him to absorb it, Quill moved. Not away — *through*. He came forward fast, slammed the hand-lamp into Bram's chest, and was past him and into the pines before Bram finished stumbling, and by the time Bram got his feet under him and the whistle to his lips, there was only crashing brush going downhill, away from camp, away from the boat, toward the game trail he must have scouted days ago.

Bram blew the whistle anyway. Three sharp notes, the old constable's alarm, and heard Silas answer from below with a shout and the crack of a boot heel on stone.

* * *

By the time Bram reached camp, the fire was kicked apart and Silas had Tess behind him with one arm and the rifle in the other hand, and Maren was up and wild-eyed with the journal's empty oilskin wrap in her fists.

"He's gone?" Silas said.

"Gone. Downhill, east fork of the trail. He had ten minutes' start by the time I got clear." Bram bent, hands on knees, getting his breath back. "I found him signaling the patrol boat. Three-and-three. They're sitting dark below the bend with muffled oars. They were coming ashore within the hour."

Maren made a sound that wasn't quite a word.

"He told me the journal in his satchel was yours," Bram went on, straightening, meeting her eyes because she deserved to hear it from him and not discover it later. "I told him it was a decoy. It isn't. He has the readings. The ore claim, the source bearings — everything."

Tess spoke from behind Silas, her voice small and steady in the way that had stopped surprising any of them. "So he knows where Papa hid the plates."

"He knows where the plates are," Bram said. "Whether he can reach them with the enforcers is another question. But yes. He has the map to them."

Silas lowered the rifle slowly. He looked at the dead fire, at the empty wrap in Maren's hands, at the black line of the forest where a helpful man with a card-reading voice had vanished, and Bram watched him arrive at the same ugly conclusion the whole camp was arriving at together: that the enemy hadn't followed them upriver. The enemy had signed the manifest.

"He was Vey's from the beginning," Maren said. Her voice was terrible in its calm. "She sent her own man to make sure we either succeeded for her or failed for the company. Either way she wins."

"Either way she wins," Bram agreed. "Unless the plates reach the capital in hands she doesn't control. Which means we have maybe—" he calculated the current, the portage, the fog "—two days on him. Less if he travels light, and he does."

Silas slung the rifle. When he turned around, his face had gone the way it went in the Narrows — stripped down, all business, the bitter humor burned off it.

"Then we stop mourning the paperwork and go get it back," he said. "Tess — ballast tube, check the seal. Maren — everything your father taught you about reading country off a bearing, because we're navigating by memory now. Bram." He looked at him, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "You had him in front of you. Talk fast on the trail. Did he favor a leg? Old injury? Anything?"

Bram thought of Quill stepping lightly over the gravel at the portage, of the easy way he'd shouldered his share of the boat. "Nothing. He's fit. He's better than fit. Whoever trained him trained him well."

Something crossed Silas's face — a question he decided not to ask, or saved for later.

They broke camp in the dark, working without words now, four people who six days ago had been strangers and were now a unit with a hole torn in the middle of it. As Bram loaded the last of the gear, he looked back once at the ridge where the lamp had blinked, and at the fog-bound river where the patrol boat waited, patient as a debt.

Two days' head start. The plates. The pass beyond.

And somewhere ahead of them, walking away through the pines with a stolen map in his satchel, the man they'd shared tea with.
