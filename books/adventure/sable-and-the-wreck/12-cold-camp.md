## 12. Cold Camp

The rain began an hour after Quill vanished into the trees, as if the sky had been waiting for permission to make things worse.

Maren found what shelter there was to find — a shallow overhang beneath a shelf of granite where the river bent away from the trail — and by the time she'd spread the groundsheet, it was already soaked through. Everything was soaked through. The tarp had gone overboard in the Narrows along with half their food, and what remained of their dry clothing existed now only as a theoretical category.

Silas stood at the edge of the overhang with his back to them, watching the grey curtain of rain where a lamp signal had flashed two nights ago. He hadn't said ten words since they'd buried the pages Quill stole in memory and started counting what was left.

Which was: one rifle with eleven rounds. One boat, patched twice. Maren's father's bearings, copied out in her own hand before the originals went into Quill's coat pocket. And four people who no longer had any reason to trust each other, except that they were all still here.

"We camp," Silas said finally. "Rain's set in. Nobody moves fast in this, and nobody thinks straight either."

"Speak for yourself," Tess said.

"I am. Constantly."

Bram huffed something that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't gotten so expensive lately.

* * *

The fire was Bram's doing, and it was less a fire than an argument he was having with wet wood. He crouched over it for the better part of an hour, feeding it slivers of birch bark shaved fine as paper, shielding the flame with his body like a man protecting a secret. When it finally caught — small, sullen, hissing every time the drips came through the rock above — nobody cheered, but everybody moved closer.

They ate cold rice softened in river water and heated, eventually, in the one tin pot that had survived. Tess doled it out with the gravity of a quartermaster, giving herself the smallest portion and pretending not to notice when Silas dumped half of his back into her bowl without comment.

"You need it," she said.

"I need you upright tomorrow. Eat."

"That's not how portions work."

"It's exactly how portions work on my boat."

Maren watched them argue and felt something in her chest loosen by a single degree. It was the first ordinary thing that had happened since the lamp blinked on the water. Ordinary things were load-bearing. Her father used to say that — that expeditions didn't break at the rapids or the crevasse fields, they broke at dinner, when there was nothing to do but be people together.

She looked around the circle. Silas with his hood down, grey-streaked hair plastered flat. Bram still crouched at the fire's edge, one hand bandaged from the portage, his constable's posture gone soft with exhaustion. Tess cross-legged, spooning rice with fierce concentration, fourteen years old and older than all of them in the ways that counted.

"Well," Maren said, before she could talk herself out of it. "Since we're going to die of boredom before we die of anything else—"

"Don't say die," Tess said.

"—since we're stuck here," Maren amended, "someone should probably explain why an ex-constable is shooting at the men he used to train."

The fire popped. Rain drummed the granite. Bram looked into the flames for a long moment, and Maren thought he wasn't going to answer.

Then he shifted, wincing, and sat back against the stone.

* * *

"Harrow district, twelve years," Bram said. "You know what a constable does up there? Mostly it's drunks and stolen chickens. Once a year somebody falls through river ice and you pull out what's left. Good work, mostly. Honest." He turned his bandaged hand over, examining it. "Then the concessions came. Company towns. Company law. The Kestrel Valley Company bought the policing contract for three districts, and suddenly I'm not keeping peace, I'm keeping *quiet*. Evictions. Strikebreakers. Men who complained about wages getting visited at night by other men, and me told to write it up as accidents."

He was quiet a moment.

"There was a woman. Odile Ferran's sister, as it turns out — though I didn't know that then. She organized the dock workers at Aldervane Landing. Company asked me to find a charge that would hold her through the shipping season. I refused. They found another constable who didn't." His jaw worked. "So I resigned. Loudly. In writing. Which is why I know the men on that patrol boat — I trained half of them, and the other half I arrested. And it's why I signed onto this expedition, if anyone was wondering. I owed the valley a debt. Now I owe it a second one."

Nobody spoke. Tess reached over and put another piece of birch bark on the fire, which was its own kind of comment.

"And you?" Bram said, looking at Maren. "Fair's fair."

Maren wrapped her hands around her knees. "My father led surveys for twenty years. He used to take me along in summer — I could read contour lines before I could read words. He believed maps were promises. That if you drew a place truly enough, you made it harder to lie about." She smiled despite herself. "Very earnest man. Terrible cook. Believed in the Bureau the way some men believe in church."

"And then?"

"And then the Kestrel contract came, and the Bureau gave it to him because he was the best, and he took it because he was the best, and—" Her voice caught; she let it, because hiding it would cost more. "I got his last letter six weeks ago. He wrote that the readings were extraordinary. That the source valley was worth more than the whole rest of the survey combined. And that he'd requested a Bureau escort for the return trip because 'some of the hired men answer to someone else.'"

The rain filled the silence.

"When the expedition went silent, the Bureau offered condolences and a filing fee. A *filing fee*. Like he was a lost parcel." She stared into the fire. "I forced my way onto this boat because someone has to bring him home, and because whoever killed him is going to keep killing until someone makes it expensive. I can't make it expensive with grief. But I can with a map."

Tess had stopped eating. She was looking at Maren with an expression Maren couldn't read — something between sympathy and recognition.

* * *

It was Silas, unexpectedly, who broke the quiet.

"The Vane Glacier," he said, to the fire rather than to any of them. "Since apparently we're confessing."

Maren went very still. Everyone knew the outline of the Vane Glacier story — it was the reason his name was mud in every guide office from Harrow's Bend to the capital. Nobody knew the shape of it.

"Eleven clients," Silas said. "A guided crossing, high season, textbook route. I'd run it nine times. Tenth time, the weather turned early. Forecast said three days of clear; we got six hours." He fed a stick to the fire with unnecessary precision. "There was a shortcut. Lower route, below the icefall. Faster. I took it because the clients were slow and tired and I wanted to beat the front in. My mistake wasn't the shortcut. The shortcut was sound. My mistake was that when the serac came down and cut the route behind us, I went back for the last two stragglers instead of getting the nine ahead of me to shelter first."

He paused.

"The nine waited in the open. The avalanche line came through while I was digging the two out. Four of the nine died waiting for me to come back and lead them. The two I dug out lived." He looked up, and his eyes were flat and old. "The inquiry called it a command failure. Abandoning your primary duty of care. They weren't wrong. A guide's job isn't heroics — it's triage. You save the many by moving them, even when your heart's screaming at you to go back. I knew that. I've always known that. On the Vane Glacier I let the two faces in the snow matter more than the nine behind me, and four people paid for my arithmetic."

"So that's why you left Tess on the boat at the Narrows," Maren said quietly. "When she went over. You stayed with the boat."

"Tess went over because I was running water I should have portaged at that light," Silas said. "One error at a time. You don't compound them." He shook his head. "And then Maren went in after her, which was either the bravest thing I've seen or the stupidest, and I've decided it doesn't matter, because it worked, and I've spent enough of my life grading decisions by whether they worked."

"What changed?" Tess asked. She'd pulled her knees up under her chin. "Between the glacier and now?"

Silas was silent so long Maren thought he wouldn't answer. The fire hissed. Somewhere upslope, water ticked down through the trees.

"Bram told me to leave him at the pass, when it comes to that," Silas said at last. "I can see it coming. Wounded man, narrow ground, one rifle — he's already composing the speech. And everyone expects me to refuse, because that's the Vane Glacier story, right? The guide who goes back." He turned his hands toward the heat. "But leaving him might be the thing that saves the rest. And I've had nineteen years to learn the difference between courage and vanity, and most days I can even tell them apart." He glanced at Bram. "So when the time comes, I'll hate it. And I'll do it. And that's the whole confession."

Bram met his eyes across the fire and nodded once, the way soldiers nod.

* * *

"My turn," Tess said.

"You don't have to," Maren started.

"You all did it. It's like cards." She set down her bowl. "St. Verity's Workhouse, since I was seven. Mother died of the lung fever, father unknown, standard tragedy, next orphan please. It wasn't the worst place. There was bread most days and Sister Anselm taught me letters, real ones, not just prayers." She picked at a thread on her sleeve. "But when you turn thirteen they apprentice you out, and mine was a dye works on the tannery road. Twelve-hour shifts, the fumes, girls losing fingers to the mixing gears because the guards wouldn't stop the machines during a shift. Ever. Stopping cost money."

"How'd you get out?" Bram asked.

"There was a girl, Petra. Sixteen. She figured the schedule of the night porter down to the minute — she was like you, Maren, she saw patterns everywhere. We went over the wall on a Tuesday." Tess's voice went careful. "Petra twisted her ankle on the drop. Couldn't run. She told me to go, and I did, because she told me to, and I've thought about that every single day since. Whether going was the brave thing or the coward thing. Whether she'd want me to have gone back."

The rain came down harder, rattling on the rocks above.

"So when I stowed away on this boat," Tess said, "and then fell in the Narrows, and Maren came in after me — I understood it. Finally. What Petra did. Sometimes the person who goes back is the one who decides what kind of person everybody gets to be." She shrugged, embarrassed, fourteen again for just a moment. "Anyway. That's my hand. Fold or match."

Silas reached over and, with great ceremony, dropped a piece of dried fish into her bowl.

"That's not how portions work," Tess said, but her voice cracked on it.

"It's exactly how portions work on my boat."

* * *

The fire burned down to coals and they fed it carefully, eking it, because none of them wanted to be the one to suggest sleep. Something had happened around those coals that Maren didn't have a cartographer's word for. A survey term almost fit — triangulation. Three known points fixing the position of an unknown one. Except here it was four points, and what they fixed was each other.

"So we're agreed," Bram said eventually. "Quill took the ore-claim pages. He didn't get the bearings — Maren memorized those — and he didn't get the plates, because nobody knows where those are except Odile Ferran, if she's alive, and her father's cache."

"He'll tell the company everything else, though," Maren said. "Our numbers, our route, our boat. They'll stop chasing us blind and start herding us."

"Then we don't travel like a crew anymore," Silas said. "We travel like weather. Scattered, unpredictable, impossible to forecast." He looked around the circle, and for the first time since Harrow's Bend, Maren saw something in his face that wasn't grim arithmetic. "We're two days from the tree line. Three from the source valley, if my reading of her father's bearings is right." A nod to Maren. "After tonight, I'd follow those bearings myself."

Maren felt her throat tighten. "He'd have liked you," she managed. "He collected stubborn people."

"He'd have hated me," Silas said. "I'm exactly the kind of guide whose shortcuts kill people." But he said it lightly, and it landed lightly, and that was new too.

Tess yawned enormously, ruined the solemnity, and was ordered to sleep. She went willingly, curling against the warm stone with the boat cloak over her, and within minutes her breathing went long and even — a workhouse kid's trick, sleeping fast and deep whenever safety offered, because it might not offer twice.

Bram took first watch without being asked, settling at the mouth of the overhang with the rifle across his knees, facing the dark and the rain.

Maren lay beside Tess and listened to the fire tick and the river run somewhere below in the black. Her father had been dead for weeks, but tonight was the first night she'd truly believed she could finish what he started. Not because she was strong enough — she'd known she was strong enough since the cache. Because she wasn't alone in it anymore. Grief carried solo breaks a person. Grief shared becomes ballast.

She was nearly asleep when Silas, rolling into his blanket, said quietly:

"Maren. Your father's journal entry. 'They know we know.' Did he write anything else that night? Anything about the mine, the guards, the layout?"

She opened her eyes. "Why?"

"Because Quill took the pages about the ore," Silas said, "which means the company now knows exactly how much this valley is worth to them. Worth dying for. Worth killing for." He stared at the coals. "Worth fortifying. If I were running that mine, I'd double the watch tonight, and I'd start moving prisoners deeper underground where no rescue reaches them. Every hour we spend being honest with each other is an hour Odile Ferran spends one day closer to disappearing for good."

Maren sat up slowly, sleep gone.

"How fast can we move in this rain?" she asked.

Silas smiled without any warmth at all. "Faster than they'll believe."
