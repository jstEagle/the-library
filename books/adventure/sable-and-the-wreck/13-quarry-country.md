## 13. Quarry Country

The treeline gave up on them the way old men give up arguments — gradually, then all at once. One hour the slopes were thick with black spruce leaning over the river like gossips; the next, the trees thinned to wind-bent shrubs, and then to nothing at all, just stone and lichen and a sky so wide it made Silas feel like an insect that had wandered onto a dinner table.

He stopped where the last stunted pine clung to a crack in the rock and let the others catch up.

"From here we walk," he said. "The boat stays hidden. If anyone asks, we're Bureau surveyors doing boundary work."

"Anyone asks," Tess repeated, "meaning the men with rifles."

"That's the general hope, yes."

Maren crouched to check her father's journal one more time, though Silas had noticed she no longer needed to. She'd memorized the bearings days ago. She kept opening the book anyway, the way you'd touch a locket. "Two miles past this ridge," she said. "The valley opens up along a fault line. Papa marked it as 'unremarkable geology.'" Her mouth went thin. "He was lying in his own field notes. He must have known someone would read them."

"He was protecting the location," Bram said.

"He was protecting *us*," Maren said, "and it didn't work."

Silas said nothing. There was nothing useful to say, and he had learned — mostly through years of saying the wrong thing — that silence was often the kindest tool on a mountain. He adjusted his pack straps and started up the switchback trail, if you could call it a trail. It was more of a suggestion the rock made about where feet might go.

Tess fell into step beside him. She'd been doing that more lately, matching his pace without being asked, and he'd stopped pretending not to notice. She carried her share of the load without complaint and asked questions only when the questions mattered, which put her ahead of half the grown men Silas had ever guided.

"How far above the treeline before the air gets mean?" she asked.

"You're not feeling it yet?"

"I'm pretending I'm not."

"Then another mile," Silas said. "Breathe through your nose. Small steps. And if your lips start tingling, tell me — don't be brave about it. Bravery at altitude kills faster than cowardice."

"Noted." She paused. "You're in a good mood today."

"I'm always in a good mood."

"Bram says you're in a good mood when you're scared."

Silas glanced back over his shoulder. Bram was fifty yards down the slope, walking sweep with his rifle slung, scanning their backtrail with the patience of a man who expected the world to disappoint him and was rarely proved wrong. "Bram talks too much for an ex-constable."

"He says ex-constables talk exactly as much as they're paid to, which is now nothing, so everything after that is free."

Despite himself — despite the ridge ahead and what the ridge might hold — Silas felt his mouth twitch. The girl had a way of doing that. Of finding the loose thread of humor in a bad day and pulling until the whole grim sweater unraveled a little. He suspected she'd learned it in the workhouse, the way other children learned piano.

They crested the ridge an hour later, in the flat grey light of early afternoon, and Silas dropped to his belly behind a shelf of granite before his brain fully caught up with his eyes.

"*Down,*" he breathed, and the others flattened beside him.

Below them, the fault line valley opened like a wound.

It should have been beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. The valley was a long green trough cradled between shoulders of snow-streaked rock, with a stream running silver down its spine — the Kestrel's true source, or close enough to it, water born from ice fields higher up and pooled in terraces before it found its courage and became a river. In another life, Silas would have stood on this ridge and called it the finest campsite in three hundred miles.

Instead, the valley floor was crawling with machinery.

A tram line ran from a dark adit halfway up the north wall down to a processing shed the size of a chapel. A generator hut coughed diesel smoke into the clean air. Ore carts sat on rails like patient beetles. Tarps and timber framed three long buildings near the stream, and there — Silas's stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like a man making sure he'd seen right — there were fences around two of them. High fences. Wire-topped fences.

And figures moving inside them. Slow figures. Figures that moved the way people move when moving is all they have left.

"Cages," Tess whispered. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone very young and very flat at the same time, and Silas recognized the sound. He'd heard it from miners' wives and drowned men's brothers. It was the voice people found when the world showed them something worse than they'd prepared for.

"Mines need workers," Bram said quietly. "Remote mines need *cheap* workers. Workers who can't leave." He didn't look away from the valley. His knuckles were white on the rifle stock. "Debt labor. Or worse. This far from any magistrate, 'worse' is the cheaper option."

"How many guards?" Maren asked, all business, because Maren did grief the way she did maps — by getting the details exactly right.

Silas counted. "Eight visible. Two at the adit, two on the tram platform, one at the generator, two walking the fence line, one asleep — or dead — in that chair by the shed door. There'll be more inside. Assume twenty, maybe thirty, and assume they're fed and rested and we are neither."

"And us?" Tess said. "What do we assume about us?"

"We assume we're four people with two rifles and a great deal of luck already spent."

Maren's jaw tightened. "Then why are we still lying here?"

"Because of them." Silas nodded toward the nearer cage building. Through the wire, in the grey light, he could make out faces now. Thin faces. Filthy faces. But faces he knew.

Knew.

"Oh God," Maren whispered. "That's — third bunk from the end. That's *Perrin*. Papa's assistant. Perrin Voss. He ate dinner with us the night before they shipped out. He told me the expedition would make my father famous."

Silas looked where she pointed and saw him: a big man gone gaunt, hair wild, hands wrapped in filthy rags, sitting against the fence post with the particular stillness of someone conserving strength for nothing. Alive. Perrin Voss was alive.

And beside him, hunched under a shared blanket, two more figures whose build and bearing Silas recognized from the expedition photographs Ilsa Vey had shown him in Harrow's Bend. Three survivors. Out of fourteen.

"The rest?" Tess asked softly, though her voice said she already knew.

"The mine happened to them," Bram said. "Or the mine's owners did."

* * *

They spent the afternoon watching, because Silas refused to do anything else. Watching was the blacklisted guide's whole religion: watch the light, watch the patterns, watch the guard rotations until you could set a clock by them. The enforcers changed shift at six-hour intervals. The fence walkers overlapped badly on the south side, where the stream culvert passed under the wire. The generator ran loud enough to cover voices within fifty yards of it.

And every evening, just before dusk, a bell rang, and the workers were marched from the tram yard back into the cage buildings, counted twice, and locked in.

"They're mining the vein my father found," Maren said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, folded around the journal, were not. "All those readings. All those pages of numbers he thought were going to change cartography. They weren't readings to him anymore. They were a shopping list."

"We don't know that the company directors knew—"

"Don't." Maren turned on him, and Silas saw something in her face he hadn't seen before — not tears, not rage, but both fused into something harder than either. "Ilsa Vey sent us upriver with a contract and a prayer. Quill was planted in our crew. The patrol boat followed us like a dog on a leash. Somebody at the top has known about this mine since before my father ever launched, and I will not soften it because the truth is heavy. My father is dead because of a *shopping list*, Silas."

Silas held her gaze. "Understood."

"Is it?"

"It is," he said, "because ten years ago I watched a company do the same arithmetic on a glacier. Ice doesn't leave bodies, though. It just closes over them. These people—" he nodded at the cages, "—left theirs where everyone can see. That's either arrogance or confidence. Both mean they never expected witnesses to survive."

"Then let's disappoint them," Tess said.

There was a silence. Then Bram snorted — actually snorted — and had to turn it into a cough, and even Maren's hard face cracked a degree, and Silas thought: *there it is again.* Four starving, freezing, hunted people on a mountainside, and the smallest one of them handing out courage like it came free with supper.

* * *

Full dark came late this far north, and they used every minute of the twilight. Silas led them down the south slope in a wide arc, avoiding the tram line, following the stream's noise to mask their own. Bram carried the rifle. Maren carried the journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. Tess carried the food bag, because she was smallest and quickest, and because — though nobody said it aloud — if shooting started, the adults wanted her nearest the escape route.

The plan was not much of a plan. Silas had led enough expeditions to know the difference between a plan and a wish wearing a plan's clothes. The wish was: get close to the fence, confirm the survivors, learn what they could, get out unseen. The plan's clothing was: the generator's noise, the culvert, the guard rotation gap.

The culvert was exactly where the watching promised. Iron-barred, rusted at the base, wide enough for a thin person. Silas measured Tess with his eyes and hated himself for measuring.

"No," Tess said, before he opened his mouth.

"It's forty feet of crawl space."

"I know how long forty feet is. I've crawled longer for less reason." She was already unshouldering the food bag. "Give me the bag. If I'm caught, I'm a runaway workhouse girl looking for scraps. Nobody shoots a scavenger. They'll just throw me in a cage, and look—" she gestured at the wire, "—there's room."

"Tess—"

"Silas." She looked up at him, and in the blue half-dark her eyes were older than her years and steadier than his own. "You've been teaching me knots for two weeks. You don't teach knots to someone you're planning to leave behind. So don't start now."

Silas discovered there was an argument in his throat, fully formed, and that it died there without ever reaching the air. He handed her the bag.

"Forty feet," he said. "Count the bars. If anyone comes near the culvert, you stop breathing and you wait. We're at the fence gap in twenty minutes whether you're through or not."

"Yes, sir," she said, with just enough irony to keep it from being obedience, and slipped into the dark like water finding a crack.

* * *

She was through in eleven minutes.

Silas counted every one of them, crouched in the shadow of the generator hut with Bram and Maren, listening to the diesel engine hammer and his heart hammer louder. Then Tess was simply there, materializing beside them with the empty food bag and a look on her face that Silas would remember for the rest of his life.

"There's a woman," she whispered. "In the second cage building. She grabbed me through the wire before I even got close. She said — she made me repeat it twice — she said, *tell them Odile Ferran saw everything. Tell them I know where the plates are.*"

Maren made a sound like she'd been struck.

"She's one of the survey team," Tess went on, rapid and quiet. "She said three of them are alive, but the other two won't last the week. She said the foreman keeps asking her about her father's notes and she keeps telling him she buried them, and he believes her because she's *good* at being believed. She said—" Tess faltered for the first time, and Silas watched the girl absorb something and file it away in whatever iron drawer she kept such things. "She said they shot the others at the waterfall. Lined them up. She heard it from the next tent."

Nobody spoke. Above them, the mountains held their snow and their silence, indifferent as accounting books.

"Plates," Maren finally whispered. "The original photographic plates. Papa cached them. If Odile knows where—"

"Then she's worth more than the journal," Silas said. "And she's worth more than us, because she's the witness. Documents can be disputed. A living voice that saw the massacre cannot." He looked at the cage building, at the faint shapes beyond the wire, at Perrin Voss sitting his endless vigil against the post. "We can't take them tonight. Eight guards minimum, unknown numbers inside, and we'd be dragging three starved people over open ground at night. It would be a slaughter — ours."

"So we do nothing?" Maren's voice cracked on it.

"We do the hardest thing," Silas said. "We come back tomorrow night with a real plan instead of a wish. And tonight—" he turned to Tess, "—you go back through that culvert with every scrap of food we can spare and every word of comfort we can carry, and you tell Odile Ferran that help is coming. Can you do that?"

Tess was already re-packing the bag. "I told her I'd come back. I keep promises."

"I know," Silas said. "That's why it's you."

She paused at the culvert's mouth and glanced back, and for one second the irony dropped off her face entirely. "Silas. The foreman. The one who believes her. He had a ledger on his desk — I saw it through the window. Shipment schedules. There's a shipment leaving in two days. The healthy ones go first."

The words landed in Silas's chest like stones dropped down a well.

Two days. Maybe less. After that, the survivors would be scattered to some deeper hole in the company's empire, and the witness would be gone, and the plates would be a rumor in a dead man's journal.

Above the treeline, under the indifferent stars, Silas Dunmore looked at the cages and felt the last of the guide's caution burn away, leaving something older and simpler underneath.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "We take them all."
