## 14. Witness

Tess had learned a long time ago that the best way to be somewhere was to be nobody.

In the workhouse it had been an art form. You kept your shoulders rounded and your eyes down and your hands busy with some small, unimportant task — mending a sock that didn't need mending, sorting buttons by a color nobody could see in that light anyway — and the wardens' gazes slid right off you like rain off oilcloth. The girls who got noticed were the ones who wanted things. Tess had wanted plenty. She'd just gotten very good at wanting it quietly.

Up here, above the treeline, in the shadow of the company's illegal mine, being nobody had turned out to be a survival skill of a different order entirely.

The camp sat in a bowl of gray rock below the glacier's shoulder, all corrugated tin and canvas and the constant grinding cough of machinery that should not have existed this far into sealed country. There were maybe thirty men — guards, mostly, big men in company gray with rifles slung casual as fishing rods — and then there were the others. The ones in cages.

Tess had seen the cages on the first night, from the rocks where Silas had them lie flat while he counted sentries through his spyglass. Not cages like for animals, exactly. Cages like for something the company wanted to keep but didn't want to look at. Welded bar, half-buried in the scree, low roofs so nobody could stand up straight. She'd counted eleven figures inside the nearest one before Silas's hand closed on her collar and drew her back down into the dark.

"Eleven," she'd whispered.

"Nine," Silas said. "Two are dead and the cold hasn't taken them off their feet yet." He'd said it without cruelty, the way you'd correct someone's arithmetic, and somehow that had been worse than if he'd shouted.

That had been two nights ago. Since then they'd hidden in a fold of the mountain above the camp — a crease in the rock with a trickle of meltwater and just enough room for four people who had all recently stopped liking each other's elbows — and waited, because Silas waited the way stone waits, and Bram, feverish and gray-faced with his bandaged shoulder, waited because he had no choice, and Maren waited because she'd made a study of Silas and copied him faithfully.

But Tess had never been any good at waiting. Waiting was what happened to you when you couldn't think of anything better.

So she'd thought of something better.

* * *

"You're not going down there." Maren said it the way she said most things, flat and certain, like reading coordinates off a chart. "Absolutely not."

"It's broad daylight," Tess said. "That's the whole point."

They were crouched at the mouth of the crease, looking down at the camp through the screen of boulders. The midday shift change was the one hour when the guards got sloppy — Silas had timed it across two days, muttering numbers under his breath like prayers — and the mess tent opened, and the smell of stew rolled up the bowl of rock strong enough to make even Bram's stomach complain, which was saying something, because Bram complained about nothing except his own conscience.

"Silas," Maren said.

Silas didn't answer right away. He was watching the camp, jaw working, and Tess knew that look. It was the look he got whenever somebody suggested doing the thing his whole body was screaming against. He'd worn it at the Narrows. He'd worn it when they voted to come up instead of turning back. He wore it now, and Tess watched him lose the argument with himself in real time.

"Three guards on the cage line at shift change," he said finally. "Fourth goes to eat. The dogs—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a dog. Big yellow brute, chained by the cook tent. It'll smell you."

"I've been smelling your cooking for two weeks," Tess said. "I'm immune to fear."

Bram laughed from the back of the crease, then winced and pressed his hand to his shoulder. "She's got you there, Dunmore."

"This isn't funny."

"No," Bram agreed, and his voice went quiet and level, constable-voice, the one he used when he was about to say something true. "It isn't. But she's right, and you know it. We can't take that camp with three rifles and my arm hanging off. What we can do is find out if Odile Ferran is alive, and if she is, get to her before we try anything stupid. Somebody has to talk to her first. Somebody small."

"Somebody invisible," Tess said.

Silas looked at her for a long moment. In the flat gray light his face was all weathered lines and reluctance, and Tess thought — not for the first time — that he looked like a man perpetually braced for the world to hand him something broken.

"If anyone shouts," he said, "you run for the water channel and you don't stop until you're back here. If they catch you, you're a workhouse runaway looking for food. You've never seen us. You don't know our names. Can you do that?"

"I once convinced three wardens I was a boy named Pip for eight months," Tess said. "I can do it."

Something moved at the corner of Silas's mouth. It wasn't quite a smile. With Silas it never quite was. But it was adjacent to one, and Tess had learned to bank those the way other people banked coins.

Maren pressed a bundle into her hands: hardtack wrapped in cloth, a strip of dried fish, a twist of salt pork, and — this was the part that made Tess's throat go tight — a square of the chocolate Maren had been hoarding since Harrow's Bend like a dragon with a grudge.

"That's yours," Tess said.

"My father used to say you don't visit someone empty-handed," Maren said. Her voice did the thing it did when she talked about her father — went steady on top and hollow underneath. "Take it."

* * *

Going down was easier than Tess had expected, which should have frightened her more than it did.

The trick, she'd found, was to move like you belonged. The guards belonged here; the laborers belonged here; therefore anything small and quick and purposeful, head down, belonged too. She carried an empty pannier she'd found abandoned by the ore chute, and she walked bent under its imaginary weight, and twice guards looked straight through her, exactly the way the wardens used to.

The dog was the problem. It lay chained by the cook tent, a huge yellow thing with ribs showing and a chain scarred into the dirt around its stake, and it raised its head when she passed and gave a single low woof that stopped her heart.

Tess did the only sensible thing. She set down the pannier, scratched its ears, and fed it half the dried fish.

The dog decided, with the easy pragmatism of every creature Tess had ever bribed, that she was fine.

The cages sat along the eastern wall of the camp, in the lee of the slag heap, close enough to the machinery that the noise covered everything. Three guards, like Silas said, but two of them had drifted together near the far end to share a pipe, backs half-turned, and the third was young and bored and watching the mountains instead of the mud.

Tess came at the nearest cage from behind the slag heap, low and quiet, and put her eye to the bars.

The smell hit her first. Then the dark. Then the shapes.

Nine people, in a space built for four. They lay packed together on the frozen ground like spoons in a drawer, sharing warmth the way the starving share everything, automatically, without shame. Most of them didn't stir. One man rocked gently back and forth, humming. Near the front, propped against the bars where a thread of daylight came through, a woman lifted her head.

She was forty-something, maybe, though it was hard to tell — the kind of face hunger redrafts until age stops meaning much. Cheekbones like a cliff face. Eyes that took Tess in, all of her, in one slow pass: the size of her, the pannier, the direction she'd come from, the cut of her coat. Survey-issue boots, two sizes too big. Tess saw the woman notice the boots and file it away.

"You're either very lost," the woman said. Her voice was cracked and dry but steady underneath, like a rope that looks frayed and holds anyway, "or very brave. In my experience those are usually the same person."

"I'm looking for Odile Ferran," Tess whispered.

The woman's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a door opening a crack in a house that had been shut up for winter. "You found her. Who sent you?"

"A survey party. Upriver. My— Maren Oduya. Aldous Oduya was her father."

For the first time, Odile Ferran moved like her body cost her something. She shifted forward, and her hand came through the bars and gripped Tess's wrist — not hard, but with the grip of someone who has decided that letting go is not among today's options. Her fingers were ice.

"Aldous Oduya," she said. "He's dead. They killed him second-to-last. He went last of the officers, if you understand me. He made them work for it." Her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "You tell his daughter that. Don't soften it. She'll want it true."

"I'll tell her," Tess said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. She cleared her throat and tried again. "What happened? All of it. Maren needs all of it."

Odile looked past Tess, toward the two guards and their pipe, toward the bored young one watching the peaks, and when she spoke again she spoke fast and low, a recitation, something she'd clearly rehearsed against the dark for weeks.

"There were nine of us left after the portage. Quill — your Quill, the one who smiles too much — he'd gone ahead 'to scout,' and the patrol boat came that same evening, and I understood then that we hadn't been followed. We'd been delivered. They marched us up here. Mr. Vey's men — the director himself came up, did you know that? A fat man in a good coat, standing in the snow like it offended him personally. He asked Aldous for the plates. The original plates, the source readings, the full survey. Aldous told him they were already out of the valley." Her mouth twisted. "It was a good lie. It bought us six days."

"And then?"

"And then the fat man stopped asking." Odile's hand tightened on Tess's wrist. "Listen to me, girl, because I don't know how many times I get to say this. The plates exist. Aldous cached them — not at the source itself, he was too clever for that, he knew they'd tear the source apart — but at the cairn below the twin falls, the glacial valley, half a day east of here. Under the largest stone, the one with the surveyor's mark chiseled on the north face. He told me the bearings the night before they—" She stopped. Steadied. "The night before. I've held them in my head for nineteen days. Every number. If I die, they die with me, so you will remember that I told you, and you will tell the Oduya girl, and between the three of us that map gets out of this valley if we have to carry it out in pieces."

"The cairn below the twin falls," Tess repeated. "Largest stone. Mark on the north face."

"North face," Odile confirmed. "And there's a second thing. The laborers in these cages — most are press-ganged rivermen, but three of us were survey. Me, and Doss, and little Anselm, and Doss is dead and Anselm is—" she glanced back at the rocking man, the humming man, and her voice gentled, "—Anselm is somewhere else now, wherever he goes. So it's me. I'm the witness. Whatever your people are planning, whatever foolhardy scheme the Dunmore man has cooked up — and I know it's the Dunmore man, don't look surprised, that route up the Kestrel has his fingerprints all over it — it has to include me. Alive. Testimony is worth more than paper. Paper burns. I don't."

Tess unwrapped the bundle and passed it through the bars piece by piece. Hardtack. Salt pork. The chocolate last, and Odile stared at it, and for a moment her composure wobbled like a candle in a draft.

"He'd have liked that," she said. "Aldous. He had a sweet tooth he pretended was a scientific instrument."

Around them the camp ground on — the machinery coughing, the guards laughing at something by the mess tent, the yellow dog gnawing its fish bone with tremendous satisfaction. Ordinary sounds. Horribly ordinary, laid over all this.

"I have to go," Tess whispered. "Before the shift changes back."

"Yes." Odile let go of her wrist, finger by finger, deliberately, like untying a knot. Then she caught Tess's chin through the bars and tilted her face up, and her eyes were fierce and bright and terribly tired. "Girl. What's your name?"

"Tess."

"Tess." She said it like she was setting it somewhere safe. "You came down alone, into a company camp, to a stranger in a cage, because someone needed feeding and someone needed telling. That's not small. Whatever they've told you you are — workhouse, nobody, whatever — that's not what you are. You're the one who comes back. Do you understand?"

Tess's eyes were stinging and she hated it and she nodded anyway.

"I'll come back," she said. "I promise. Before we do anything — anything — I'll come back for you first."

"I know," Odile Ferran said, and smiled, and it transformed her ruined face into something you could imagine young, once, laughing on a riverbank. "Now run, little witness. And watch the dog doesn't follow you — he's a traitor to any hand that feeds him, and I mean that as a compliment to you both."

* * *

Tess made the rocks, the crease, the fold of the mountain, and collapsed into it breathing like a bellows, and three faces swung toward her — Silas's guarded, Maren's hungry, Bram's grim over his sling.

"Well?" Maren said.

"She's alive." Tess got her breath back in pieces. "She's alive, and she's been holding my father's cache location in her head for nineteen days, and she says to tell you he died fighting and made them work for it, and—" she reached out and gripped Maren's sleeve, "—and she says testimony is worth more than paper, and she's coming out of that cage with us or not at all."

Maren made a sound Tess had never heard from her before — half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled — and turned away to blink at the mountains, and Silas put his hand briefly on the back of Tess's neck, heavy and warm, and said nothing at all, which from Silas was practically a speech.

But it was Bram whose face had changed the most. He was staring down at the camp, at the thin smoke rising from the cook tent, and his jaw was set in the shape of a decision.

"Dunmore," he said quietly. "We've got a problem. While she was down there — the patrol boat just came round the point. And Quill's standing on the deck."

Silas went very still.

"Not coming in?" he said.

"No," Bram said. "Signaling. And Dunmore — the guards are signaling back."
