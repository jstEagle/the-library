## 15. The Source

The river stopped being a river two miles before it actually stopped.

Maren noticed it first in the sound — the Kestrel's constant chatter thinning out, going quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it. Then in the water itself, which turned from brown to green to a color she had no name for, something between glass and sky, so clear that she could see fifty feet down to cobbles that looked close enough to touch.

"It's coming straight off the ice," Silas said from the stern. He'd gone still in the way he did when the country started talking to him. "No silt. No sediment. That water hasn't touched dirt since before your grandfather was born."

"Then we're close," Maren said.

Her father's bearings sat open on her knee, the field journal wrapped in oilcloth against the spray that no longer existed. The last pages were all numbers and arrows and one sentence, underlined twice, that she had read so many times she could recite it in her sleep: *The source lies at the head of the valley where the walls stand like pillars of a church. Follow the west wall to the fallen pillar. The plates are beneath the third stone.*

A cathedral. Her father had thought in cathedrals. He'd been raised by a chapel-keeper in the Fennish lowlands and had never quite shaken the habit of seeing God's architecture in geology.

Tess stood in the bow with the boathook, though there was nothing left to hook. "It looks like the inside of a jewel," she said. "If a jewel were cold."

"Everything here is cold," Bram said. He was checking his rifle for the fourth time that morning, which Maren had learned was not anxiety but liturgy. "Cold keeps things honest."

"You would say that," Tess said. "You're from the north."

"I'm from everywhere. That's what being a constable is." A pause. "Was."

The valley opened around them like a held breath.

Maren had seen mountains her whole life — had drawn them, measured them, loved them in the way of someone who understood them as lines on paper before she ever understood them as rock. But nothing had prepared her for this. The walls rose sheer on either side, granite streaked with quartz that caught the morning light and threw it back in veins of silver, and at the head of the valley the glacier came down between two peaks like a frozen waterfall the size of a town. The ice was blue. Not white-blue, not winter-blue, but deep glacial blue, the blue of deep water and deep time, and where the sun struck it, light came *through* it, so that the whole face seemed lit from within.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Even Silas, who Maren suspected had opinions about everything, simply stood at the tiller with his mouth slightly open, looking like a man who had walked into a church mid-sermon and forgotten why he'd come.

"Well," Tess said finally. "That's showing off."

Maren laughed — actually laughed, a real one, the first in days — and the sound went up the valley and came back changed, flattened and stretched by all that stone.

"The west wall," she said, finding her father's handwriting again. "He says there's a fallen pillar. A column of rock that broke off and came down."

"There." Silas pointed. Partway up the western wall, a spur of granite had sheared away sometime in the last century; the scar was raw and pale, and below it a monolith the size of a house lay half-buried in scree, tilted like a headstone for a giant. "Your father had an eye."

"He had a hundred eyes," Maren said quietly. "That was the problem. He saw too much, and he wrote all of it down, and he trusted the wrong people to read it."

Silas didn't answer, but he brought the boat alongside a gravel shelf with the particular grace he reserved for landings that mattered, and they got out onto ground that crunched like sugar.

* * *

They cached the boat under brush and climbed.

The scree was treacherous — every step sent a small avalanche rattling downslope, announcing them to the entire valley — so Silas set them along the base of the wall where older rubble had settled into something like stability. Tess went first because she was smallest and lightest and because she had appointed herself scout somewhere around the Portage of Bones and nobody had managed to un-appoint her. Bram took the rear with the rifle slung across his back, walking backward as often as forward, watching the way they'd come.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Tess said, hopping a gap between stones. "The workhouse matron used to say God sees everything. I used to think that was just a threat. Like, don't steal the bread, God's watching. But standing here—" she gestured at the whole impossible valley— "I think maybe she meant it literally. Maybe this is where He does His paperwork."

"That's blasphemy," Bram said.

"It's theology," Tess said. "There's a difference. Blasphemy is when you're wrong about it on purpose."

Maren smiled and kept climbing, and let herself, for a few minutes, feel something dangerously close to happiness. It was absurd. They were fugitives in a sealed concession with enforcers somewhere behind them and a wounded witness waiting in a cage below the treeline, and she was grinning at a fourteen-year-old's jokes on a pile of loose rock. But her father's bearings were good. They were *here*. Whatever happened next, Elias Oduya's last work had led true, and that felt like a kind of conversation with him — like following his voice through country he'd walked alone and afraid, and arriving where he'd asked her to arrive.

*Here, Mare. Look what I found. Now tell everyone.*

The fallen pillar was bigger up close than it had looked from the water. Up close it wasn't a pillar at all but a whole section of cliff face, sheared clean and dropped, its upper surface tilted toward the sky at a lazy angle. Lichen had gotten a start on it, orange and gray, decades of patient work.

"Third stone," Maren said. She counted along the pillar's broken edge, where slabs had split away and settled. First. Second. The third was a flat slab the size of a door, wedged against the main mass, its underside shadowed.

She knelt. Her hands were shaking. She hated that they were shaking, and told them to stop, and they didn't, so she let Bram do the lifting while she watched.

The slab came free with a grinding groan. Beneath it: a cavity in the rubble, dry, dark, lined with the same oilcloth her father had used on his journals.

Inside were the plates.

Twelve of them, zinc, each one etched with survey lines and elevation figures and annotations in her father's cramped, beautiful hand. The original plates — not copies, not notes, the actual engraved evidence, the thing that could not be dismissed as a forgery or lost in a fire, because here it was, cold and heavy and real. Maren lifted the top one and read the figures and felt the floor of the world shift under her. The ore body wasn't a seam. It was a *lode*, a fat silver artery running the length of the source valley, worth more than the entire annual budget of the province, and her father had mapped every inch of it knowing what it would cost him.

"He knew," she said. Her voice came out strange. "He knew exactly what he'd found, and he hid the plates anyway instead of selling them. He could have named his price. Any company in the world. And he buried them under a rock and wrote *they know we know* in his journal like a man leaving a note for whoever came after."

"Maybe he hoped it'd be you," Tess said softly.

"Maybe he hoped it wouldn't be anyone."

Silas crouched beside her and turned one of the plates toward the light, reading the etched lines with a surveyor's eye. "These are admissible. These are better than testimony — testimony can be bought, plates can't. Vey gets these to the Bureau archive and the concession dies in committee." He looked up, and for once there was nothing guarded in his face. "You did it, Maren. Your father did it. We're carrying it out."

And that — of course, *of course* — was when the shots came.

* * *

The first bullet hit the pillar above their heads with a crack like the mountain snapping its fingers, and stone spalled down in a hot rain.

"DOWN!" Bram was already moving, hauling Tess behind the slab by the collar of her coat, and Maren went flat against the cold rock with the plates crushed under her chest and her heart trying to climb out through her throat.

She counted shots. Three, spaced, deliberate — not a volley, a *herding* pattern, driving them away from the open scree and toward—

"Toward the wall," Silas breathed beside her. He'd gone very calm, the way he did, his voice dropping into that flat guide's register that meant the situation had passed beyond panic into arithmetic. "They want us pinned against the cliff. There'll be men on the rim above us."

"How many?"

"If they're smart? Six. If they're stupid, four, and we might shoot our way out." He risked a look, ducked back as another round sparked off granite a hand's width from his skull. "They're smart."

Bram had found a crease in the pillar and was shooting back, unhurried, each report rolling up the valley and returning doubled. "Rim, eleven o'clock, two shooters," he called. "Second position, the scree fan, at least three more. They've been sitting on this cache since yesterday — they knew your father's bearings too, or they followed us."

"Quill," Maren said, and the name tasted like iron.

"Quill sold them the route," Silas agreed. "But they can't have the plates. Maren — listen to me. The oilcloth bundle. Can you run with it?"

"I can run with it."

"Then we're going to do the ugly thing. Straight down the scree, all of us at once, fast and loud, and Bram covers until—" He stopped. Everyone heard him stop. Everyone heard the sentence he refused to finish.

"No," Bram said.

"Bram—"

"I've done the math you're not saying out loud, Dunmore, and I'm better at it than you are." Bram reloaded without looking away from the rim, his hands steady, his voice almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Somebody holds this position or nobody leaves the valley. You're the only one who knows the way downriver in the dark. Maren's the only one who can authenticate those plates. Tess is—" he glanced at her, and something old and tired moved across his face— "Tess is the reason any of us are still human. So it's me. It was always going to be me. I trained half the men on that rim, I know their habits, and I have been looking for a way to be useful since the day I resigned. This is it. Let me have it."

"You don't get to *have* it," Tess said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "That's not how crews work. We voted to come up. We vote to go down. All of us."

"Tess—"

"Shut up, Bram." She was crying and furious about it. "Shut up and shoot."

Another burst from the rim drove them flatter. Maren pressed the plate bundle tight and made herself think, made herself be her father's daughter, the girl who solved problems with a pencil and a ruler while other people shouted. The scree fan ran down-slope toward the glacier's meltwater channel — open ground, deadly, but fast if you didn't stop. The wall side was cover but a dead end. Between the two positions, a shallow gully cut diagonally across the slope, invisible from above except—

"Silas." She grabbed his sleeve. "The gully. From the rim it reads as part of the scree shading. If we go down it low, they'll lose sight of us at the bend. It's thirty yards of blind ground."

Silas looked at the gully, then at her, and something flickered across his face that she'd learn later was pride. "That'll do," he said. "Bram. Thirty seconds of loud. Then you fall back to the pillar and make them pay for every yard. We'll signal from the boat."

"And then you come down that gully right behind us," Tess said, jabbing a finger at him, "or I will come back up this mountain and drag you, and I am *very* motivated and *very* hard to kill, ask anyone at the workhouse."

Bram smiled — actually smiled, crooked and warm, the first full one Maren had ever seen from him. "Yes, ma'am."

"On three," Silas said. "One—"

Bram stood up out of cover and opened fire, and the world became noise.

Maren ran. She ran hunched with the plates hugged to her chest, boots skating on loose stone, Tess's breath ragged beside her and Silas a stride behind calling the line of the gully, and bullets chased them across the slope, kicking up little gray ghosts in the scree. She slid into the gully, rolled, came up running, and for eleven glorious seconds there was only rock above her and her own heartbeat and the plates safe against her ribs.

Then the shooting changed.

It changed the way weather changes — a new note in it, lower, closer, and Bram's rifle answering from a different place entirely, farther right, higher up. He'd shifted. He'd shifted to draw them off the gully, which meant he'd left the pillar, which meant—

"Keep moving!" Silas roared, shoving her shoulder. "Don't you dare stop!"

"But Bram—"

"*Move!*"

They came out of the gully's mouth onto the lower scree and Maren looked back, one look, half a second, the kind of look you can never take back.

Bram was at the crest of the ridge above the pillar, standing in the open like a man addressing a jury, working his bolt with terrible calm. Even at this distance she could see the shape of him — solid, deliberate, immovable — and then he staggered, and fired again anyway, and staggered again, and went down to one knee, and kept shooting.

"Bram!" Tess screamed, and lunged back upslope, and Silas caught her around the waist and carried her, actually carried her, down the scree while she beat at his arms.

The last thing Maren saw before the boulders swallowed the ridge was Bram's rifle flash one more time from behind the fallen pillar — still firing, still holding, doing the thing he had chosen the way he did everything, thoroughly and without complaint.

Then the meltwater channel was under their feet, and the boat was ahead, and Silas was shoving them aboard with the plates bundled into Maren's coat and his face gray as the granite.

"Get us out," he said hoarsely, taking the tiller. "Get us out and get them to the capital, and I swear to you—" His voice failed. He tried again. "I'm coming back for him. Do you hear me? Whatever it costs. I'm coming back."

The current took the boat. Behind them, high on the western wall, the shooting went on — slower now, spaced far apart, each shot rolling down the cathedral valley like a bell tolling — and then, between one toll and the next, it stopped.

Maren held the plates and stared up at the silence where her friend had been, and the glacier light poured down blue and indifferent over all of it.
