## 16. Holding the Pass

Bram Hale had been shot twice in his life before this morning, and both times he'd had the good manners to do it standing up, facing the right direction, with witnesses present to file reports about it afterward. This third time was different. This third time he was lying on his back behind a granite boulder at the mouth of a pass that didn't have a name on any map worth trusting, watching his own blood soak into the gravel in a way that struck him as untidy and faintly embarrassing, like spilling soup at a formal dinner.

The bullet had gone through the meat of his left shoulder — through, not into, which the old constabulary instructors would have called *lucky* and which Bram called *a matter of interpretation*. It hurt the way a river hurts when you're under it: everywhere at once, and no single place you could point to and say *there, that's the problem*.

The problem was actually quite easy to point to. The problem was coming up the trail behind them in numbers.

He heard them before he saw them — boots on scree, the particular careful rhythm of men who knew they were hunting and didn't mind taking their time about it. Six, maybe seven. The patrol boat crowd, plus whatever reinforcements the mine had coughed up once word went upstream that four trespassers were loose in the source valley with a wounded witness and a satchel of survey plates that could hang every director of the Aldervane Concession from the same gallows beam.

Bram rolled onto his knees, which was a process involving several words he hadn't used since leaving the constabulary, and got his back against the boulder. The rifle lay across his lap where Silas had set it down twenty minutes ago, along with two full bandoliers and a look that Bram was still trying to decode.

* * *

It had started, as most bad ideas did, with arithmetic.

They'd come down out of the glacial cathedral with the plates safe in Maren's oilskin wrap and Odile Ferran leaning on Tess's shoulder, walking on a leg that had been broken and badly set and then made to march anyway. Odile weighed nothing. She complained about nothing. She had told Tess, in a voice like dry paper, exactly which direction the enforcers would come from, because she'd watched them move for three months from inside a cage and she'd had nothing else to do but learn.

She'd been right. They'd been maybe an hour ahead of the pursuit when the valley narrowed into the pass — a slot between two walls of stone where the old survey trail pinched down to the width of a cart, maybe less. A natural chokepoint. The kind of ground a man who'd spent eleven years in the constabulary looked at and thought one thought:

*One rifle here is worth ten anywhere else.*

Silas had seen it too. Bram watched him see it — watched him stop, look up at the walls, look down the trail behind them, and do the same arithmetic. And then Silas had done something worse than the arithmetic. He'd looked at Bram's shoulder, at the blood coming through the field dressing Maren had tied, and Bram had watched him arrive at the answer neither of them wanted to say out loud.

"Don't," Bram said.

"You haven't heard me say anything."

"You're going to say it with your face, then. You're doing it now. That's your *responsible* face. I hate your responsible face."

"Everybody hates my responsible face." Silas crouched, set the rifle down on a flat stone, and began checking the action with the unhurried competence of a man loading a weapon he intended someone else to fire. "Maren can't stay. She's the only one who can read her father's bearings if we have to cut cross-country. Tess—"

"Tess stays with Odile. Obviously."

"—and I'm the only one who knows the river well enough to run it downstream at night, which is what we're going to be doing, because the boat's waiting below the falls and those plates don't mean a thing unless they reach the capital." He snapped the bandolier closed. "Which leaves you."

"I'm shot."

"You're shot *through*. Maren says the shoulder's clean. You'll lose some blood, you'll be useless with your left arm for a month, and none of that matters, because all you have to do is sit behind that rock and pull a trigger with your good hand until we're through the narrows." Silas finally looked up. "Six hours. Maybe eight. Then you come down the far side and follow the river to the boundary post."

"And if they bring more than six hours' worth of men?"

Silas didn't answer that. He didn't need to. They both knew what the answer was, and they both knew that Bram had already done the sum himself, somewhere back on the portage, before anyone said a word about passes or rifles. Bram had spent thirty years being the man who stayed behind. Barricades. Doorways. The last one out of burning buildings, back when he'd worn a uniform and believed the uniform meant something. He had walked away from the constabulary because the constabulary had stopped meaning it, not because he had.

And he had watched Silas Dunmore fail this exact test once before — on the Vane Glacier, five years ago, when Silas had gone back for a man who was already dead and lost two more getting there, and come down off the ice carrying nothing but frostbite and a reputation that followed him into every hiring hall on the frontier. Bram hadn't been there. But everyone on this river knew the story, and he'd watched Silas flinch from it for two hundred miles.

So Bram sat down behind the boulder, took the rifle, and said, "Go on, then. Before I change my mind and make this sentimental."

* * *

The goodbyes were the worst part, and Bram got through them by treating each one like paperwork.

Maren came first. She knelt, checked his dressing with her father's brisk economy, tightened it half a turn, and then did something he didn't expect: she pressed her father's field journal into his hands.

"If I don't—" she started.

"You will," Bram said. "I've read enough endings to know a live one when I'm in it."

"That's not how stories work."

"No," he admitted. "But it's how I'm choosing to read this one." He tapped the journal. "Take the pages that matter and leave me the rest. If I'm stuck here a while, I'll want something better than counting rocks."

She almost smiled. "There's a chapter on contour intervals that'll put you to sleep fast enough."

"Heaven." She squeezed his good hand once, hard, and went.

Tess came next, and Bram braced himself, because Tess did not do paperwork. Tess did feelings, at full volume, at fourteen, with the total disregard for dignity that only the young and the stubborn could manage. She stood over him with her jaw set and her eyes wet and furious about being wet, and she said, "You come down that mountain. You hear me? I've buried enough people. I'm not doing yours."

"Wouldn't dream of it. Who'd teach you to shoot straight? Maren holds her breath when she fires. It's agonizing to watch."

"It's called *control*, Bram."

"It's called missing low and left." He fished in his coat pocket and put the little brass compass in her hand — the one he'd carried since his first day in uniform, the one thing he'd kept when he'd turned in the rest. "North is that way. Everything else is negotiable. Go."

She went, but she went backwards, watching him, until the trail bent and took her.

Odile paused beside him without being asked. She looked at the trail below, at the enforcers who would come up it, and then at Bram, and she said quietly, "I held a door once. In the mine. When they came for the man in the next cage." Her voice didn't waver. "He lived. I did not enjoy it."

"Nobody enjoys it," Bram said. "That's rather the point of it."

She nodded, as if he'd passed some examination, and let Tess steer her away.

That left Silas.

Silas stood there with his pack on and the whole downstream world waiting on him, and for a long moment he said nothing at all, which from Silas Dunmore was practically a speech. Then he crouched so they were eye to eye, and Bram saw that the man was fighting it — fighting the story, the glacier, the ghost of the man he'd failed to leave behind and the men he'd failed by not leaving him.

"On the Vane," Silas said finally, "I went back because I couldn't stand the arithmetic. Three lives for one. I told myself love doesn't do sums." His mouth twisted. "Love does sums, Bram. Somebody has to. It's the coldest kind of caring there is, and I'm asking you to let me do it."

"I know." Bram shifted the rifle against his good shoulder. "You're doing it right now. Look at you. Hating every second. It's beautiful."

"It's not beautiful."

"It's a little beautiful."

"Bram."

"Go run your river, Silas." And then, because the moment demanded it and because Bram Hale had never once let a solemn moment escape unimproved: "And if you capsize the boat, I will haunt you. I'll be very thorough about it. Every rapid, every campsite, humming."

Silas huffed — half laugh, half surrender — gripped Bram's neck once with a hand that shook slightly, and went.

Bram listened to their footsteps fade up the trail: Silas's long stride, Maren's quick light ones, Tess's deliberate pace matching itself to Odile's shuffle. Four sets of feet getting smaller. He counted them out of habit, the way he'd once counted prisoners into cells and constables out of doors, and when the last one faded he was alone in the pass with a rifle, two bandoliers, a dead man's journal, and a view of the trail that any marksman would have wept over.

He checked the rifle. Checked it again. Settled his back against the granite, which hurt, and found the position that hurt least, which still hurt quite a lot.

Then he opened the journal to the chapter on contour intervals, and waited.

* * *

They came an hour after noon.

He heard them first — the same careful boot-rhythm, closer now, seven men moving in a loose line with the confidence of hunters who hadn't yet met anything that hunted back. Bram eased his head around the boulder's edge and picked them out among the pines: dark coats, concession colors, rifles carried low and ready. In front walked a big man with a grey beard whom Bram recognized from the patrol boat — Voss, the one who'd once been his student, back when Bram taught marksmanship and procedure to young men who mostly wanted to shoot things legally.

Voss stopped at the mouth of the pass. He looked up at the walls. He looked down the slot of the trail. And Bram felt a flicker of professional pride despite everything, because Voss hesitated. He knew the ground was wrong. He'd learned that much, at least, from somebody.

"Survey party!" Voss called, his voice flat against the stone. "You're done running. Come out with the documents and nobody gets hurt."

Bram considered his options. There was the honest reply, the tactical reply, and the reply that a tired, bleeding ex-constable with a dry throat and a grudge might actually give.

He raised his voice and called back, cheerfully, "You're standing in my office, son. Take your boots off the desk."

A pause. Then, warily: "Hale?"

"The very same. How's your trigger discipline these days? Still jerking it like you're swatting flies?"

Voss said something low to the men behind him. Bram used the time to shift his aim to the gap between two pines where the trail narrowed tightest — the spot he'd chosen an hour ago, measured against his breathing, against the drop of his injured arm, against everything the constabulary had ever taught him and everything it had failed to teach him.

"They said you quit," Voss called.

"I did. This is my retirement hobby."

"Hale. Last chance. Walk out."

"Here's my counteroffer," Bram said, and sighted on the pine gap, and let his breath out slow. "You turn around, go back to whichever director sent you, and tell him the pass is held. Tell him it's held by one man with one rifle and a great deal of free time, and tell him that every hour he spends digging me out is an hour that boat gets further downstream. Tell him the arithmetic's changed."

"And if we don't?"

Bram fired.

The man who stepped into the gap went down with a cry, alive — Bram had put it through his thigh, deliberately, because the constabulary had taught him that too, before it taught him anything else: *you shoot to stop, not to kill, and the day you forget which is the day you stop being worth the badge.* The line scattered for cover. Rifles cracked back at the boulder, at the walls, at shadows, spending ammunition with the panicked generosity of men who'd never been shot at from ground they couldn't read.

Bram worked the bolt. Loaded. Breathed. Found the next gap.

Below him, miles down through the stone, a river ran toward the capital carrying four people and a satchel of truth, and every minute he kept seven men pinned in this slot was a minute bought with the only currency he had left.

He settled in. He opened the journal across his knee to mark his page. And when Voss's voice came again — angrier now, closer, promising things — Bram Hale smiled at the trail, wiped the blood from his good hand, and prepared to hold his office for as long as the paperwork allowed.

Somewhere past the second hour, he ran out of contour intervals to read, and the shooting started in earnest.
