## 17. Descent Under Guns

The plates rode in a canvas satchel strapped across Silas's chest, and every time the boat slammed down into a trough he felt them knock against his sternum like a second heartbeat. Glass and emulsion. Her father's last work. Maren had wrapped each one in oilcloth and then in her own scarf, and she sat behind him now with her arms around the bundle as if it were a sleeping child.

"Tell me again," she shouted over the water.

"The Sable Narrows run easier at night," Silas called back. "You can't see what's coming, so you stop flinching early."

"That's not comforting!"

"It's not meant to be."

Behind Maren, Odile Ferran lay wedged between the cargo crates with a blanket to her chin and a rifle across her lap that she was too weak to lift but refused to surrender. Three days in a cage, six weeks before that hauling ore in the quarry, and still she had insisted on holding something. "I've been helpless long enough," she'd said when Silas tried to take it back. He hadn't argued. There were people you argued with and people you let keep their rifles, and he was learning which was which.

Tess crouched at the stern with the steering sweep, fourteen years old and steady as a cleat. She'd braided her hair back tight for the run, workhouse-style, and she kept her eyes on his shoulders the way he'd taught her — read the boat through the helmsman, don't look at the water, the water lies.

* * *

They had put in below the source valley two hours after full dark, on a shingle beach that Silas knew only from a survey map twenty years out of date, and the map had been right, which felt like a small miracle or an old debt being quietly repaid. Behind them, up at the pass, Bram held the narrows with a wounded shoulder and thirty rounds and instructions Silas had hated giving: *hold until dawn, then come down the goat track if you can, stay if you can't.* Bram had grinned through the blood on his face and said, "Go on, Dunmore. I've been wanting some quiet time with my thoughts for years."

Silas had left him. Again. The Vane Glacier had taught him that leaving a man behind was the one sin the mountains never forgave, and here he was committing it a second time, except this time it wasn't cowardice or panic — it was arithmetic. One man at a choke point could stop twenty. Four people on open water could stop no one.

"You did the right thing," Odile said now, reading him across the dark water the way she seemed to read everything.

"I did the necessary thing," Silas said. "Those aren't the same animal."

"No," she agreed. "But they're usually found in the same pasture."

The river took them then, and there was no more talk.

He ran the Narrows by feel and by memory of a kind — not memory of this river, which he'd never run at night, but memory of every bad water he'd ever shot in the dark with stolen goods aboard and the law's lanterns swinging along the bank. That had been another life, the one that got him blacklisted: running contraband zinc through the Kestrel headwaters for a man named Corb, fast runs and faster lies, until the night the ice took the Vane Glacier party and the whole country decided that Silas Dunmore's name was a synonym for *dead men*. He had spent eleven years paying for that reputation. Tonight, for the first time, he spent it like money instead of carrying it like a stone.

"Rock, left channel!" Tess called, because she'd learned to hear the difference in the current's voice — smooth water sings, broken water stutters.

"I see it."

"You do not see anything, it's black as a boot."

"That's why I'm listening to you." And God help him, he meant it. The girl had been aboard nine days and already she read water better than men he'd crewed with for seasons. Somewhere below the fear and the cold spray there was a warm coal in his chest that he recognized, eventually, with some embarrassment, as pride.

* * *

The first lantern appeared on the east bank an hour into the run.

It didn't move like a searchlight. It moved like a man walking, keeping pace, and that was worse. A searchlight sweeps and hopes; a walking lantern knows.

"They're tracking us along the shore," Maren said.

"Company trail follows the east bank the whole way down," Silas said. "Old pack route from the first concession surveys. They'll leapfrog ahead and try to pin us against the White Stair rapids."

"Can we outrun them?"

"On flat water, yes. On the Stair, nobody outruns anybody. You either thread it or you die in it, and either way it takes about ninety seconds."

"Then we should arrive tired and they should arrive fresh," Odile said from her nest of crates. "That seems like poor arithmetic."

"It gets better," Silas said. "There's a second lantern. West bank."

Maren twisted to look. Far off across the black water, small as a struck match, a light bobbed among the trees. "Two parties?"

"One party, split. Or—" he didn't finish it, because finishing it meant saying *Quill*, and saying Quill's name out loud felt like inviting him.

But Quill came anyway. Quill always came; that was the whole architecture of the man. Ilsa Vey had planted him in their crew from Harrow's Bend, smiling and competent and helpful with the knots, and he'd run at the first confrontation with the journal pages proving the ore claim tucked inside his coat, and everything since — the ambush at the source, the guards waiting at the mine road, Bram's shoulder opened by a bullet that had been aimed at Silas — traced back to those soft footsteps going into the trees.

"He'll be on the water," Silas said finally. "He won't trust the banks twice. He'll have commandeered something."

"What does a man like that commandeer?" Tess asked.

"A boat," Silas said grimly. "There's always a boat."

* * *

They portaged the White Stair in the small hours, hauling the boat up a deer trail on rollers cut from deadfall, all four of them — Odile leaning on Maren, refusing again to be carried, swearing at the terrain in three languages with genuine fluency. Silas went ahead to scout the drop and found what he'd feared: the company had gotten there first. Not enforcers — just evidence of them. A cold fire ring. Boot prints. A chalk mark on the rock at the head of the chute, an arrow pointing downstream. *They know the route. They're setting the net below.*

So he did the thing that had ended his respectable life, the trick old Corb used to call *dunmoring it*, half admiring and half disgusted: he ran the Stair anyway, at night, with a wounded woman and a girl and a sack of irreplaceable glass, but he ran it backwards from the book. Where the guidebooks said hug the west wall, he hugged the east. Where the safe line threaded between the two mid-channel teeth, he went hard over the left tooth itself, letting the hull kiss it and grind, praying to whatever god keeps accounts for fools that the crack he heard was wood giving and not the plates.

The boat came through the bottom pool shipping half its weight in water and listing like a drunk leaving church.

Tess bailed. Maren checked the plates with shaking hands and reported, voice cracking on the second word, "All twelve. All twelve, all dry." Odile laughed — a rusty, disused sound, like a gate opening after winter — and said something in her own language that needed no translation.

And that was when the second boat came out of the dark behind them.

* * *

It was a company skiff, narrow and fast, riding low with four men and a lantern hooded to a slit. Silas heard it before he saw it — the particular hush of a hull that had been greased for silence — and by the time he got the sweep braced it was fifty yards back and closing, using the same water they were, riding their wake like a wolf on a flank.

"Lamp," he said, and Tess unshuttered theirs for one second, one deliberate heartbeat, giving the pursuers a target worth shooting at while Silas counted the shapes behind the glare. Four. One at the tiller with a familiar set to his shoulders, that patient, economical way of moving that Silas had spent nine days mistakenly calling competence.

Quill.

"Down!" he roared, and the first shot cracked across the water and went somewhere into the night, and the second one took a splinter off the gunwale a hand's width from Maren's ear.

Odile fired back — propped on her crate, braced against the roll, one-armed, wild, magnificent. She hit nothing, but she made the skiff's bowman duck, and ducking cost them speed, and speed was the whole war.

"They're gaining," Tess said, bailing and steering and lying flat by turns, doing three jobs at fourteen that grown men couldn't do one.

"Then we give them water they don't know." Silas's mind had gone somewhere cold and clear, the place he lived during the worst hours, the place the blacklist had built. Below them, maybe two miles, the Kestrel dropped through the Cutbanks — a stretch where the river tore loose from its canyon walls and braided into six channels around gravel islands that moved every spring flood. No map alive showed the true channel. No map alive *could*; he knew because he'd once paid a drowned man's widow to draw it from her husband's notes, and then burned the drawing, because a channel like that was worth more than gold to a smuggler and worth a hanging to anyone who caught him with it.

Every blacklisted trick of his disgrace. Well. Here was the ledger coming due.

"Hold everything," he told his crew. "Whatever happens, hold everything."

* * *

The Cutbanks announced themselves as a change in the river's voice — the single deep note of the canyon opening into a ragged chord, water splitting and muttering, gravel hissing. In the dark it was simply nothing, a wide black absence where the banks fell away. To any pursuer it would look like a choice of deaths.

To Silas it looked like home.

"Third channel from the west," he murmured, more prayer than order. "Past the snag that looks like a kneeling man. Then hard left where you smell the willows."

"You *smell* the willows," Tess repeated.

"Willows grow where the slow water goes. Slow water means deep. Deep means alive." He glanced back at her. "Your father ever tell you the river has a grammar?"

"I didn't have a father."

"No," Silas said. "Well. It does. Third channel. Go."

She went. The boat leapt sideways into the braid and the world dissolved into noise — water tearing over gravel bars, channels colliding, the kneeling-man snag sliding past close enough to touch, black branches like a drowned giant's fingers. Twice the hull touched bottom and slewed, and twice Silas leaned the sweep against the current's shoulder and walked the boat off it, feeling the channel through the wood the way a blind man reads a face. Behind them the lantern-slit flared, swung, hesitated — he could feel Quill doing the arithmetic on the water, choosing a channel, choosing wrong.

The skiff's crash, when it came, was very loud and then very quiet.

They came out of the braid into the long single throat of the lower river, and Silas called the lamp shuttered and the paddles quiet, and they drifted, breathing steam, listening. From upstream, faint through the canyon, voices. Shouting. Not the shout of men who had found a body — the sharp, angry, organized shouting of men whose boat was holed and whose quarry was gone.

"Did they drown?" Tess asked. There was no satisfaction in it. There never was, with Tess; she asked about death the way she asked about weather.

"Some," Silas said. "Cutbanks kills swimmers even in daylight. But not him."

"How do you know?"

"Because men like Quill are the cockroaches of the—"

He stopped. Because the words were barely out of his mouth when the shape rose out of the dark astern — a man clinging to a broken plank, having ridden the braid himself, alone, having chosen the *right* channel after all, or been carried down it by the same luck that had kept him alive through every treachery he'd ever committed. Quill's face was gray with cold, his coat gone, the journal pages surely lost to the river, and he was reaching for their gunwale with the terrible confidence of a man who believed no one would actually do it.

"Pull me up," Quill gasped. "Dunmore. Pull me up. I can fix this. Ilsa will pardon the whole crew, I swear it, I can make this go away—"

Silas looked at him. Eleven years of disgrace stood in the way, and the old arithmetic — *one man at a choke point, four on open water* — and the newer, warmer ledger: Bram bleeding at the pass, Odile's three weeks in a cage, Maren's father face-down in shallows with a survey stake driven in beside him like a fence post for a grave.

It was Maren who stood.

She didn't say anything. That was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing he would tell at fires for years: she didn't say a word. She simply took up the boathook — the humble iron-tipped thing they'd used to drag the boat off gravel bars for nine days — and she reached past Quill's grasping hand, and she hooked him, not by the arm or the collar but by the belt, with a fisherman's economy, and she lifted him bodily off the plank and held him above the water, turning slowly, like a specimen.

"Maren," Quill said, and his confidence flickered. "Maren, your father wouldn't want—"

"My father," Maren said, very evenly, "would have pulled you out. That's the difference between us, and it's the reason you're going in the river instead of under it." She swung. The boathook released at the top of the arc, and Quill went into the black water with a slap that echoed off the canyon walls.

For a moment they all watched the dark where he'd gone. Then the current took a shape — swimming, strong, angling for the west bank and the company trail beyond it — and the shape diminished, and was gone.

"He'll bring them down on us by dawn," Silas said quietly.

"Then we'd best be worth chasing," Maren said, and sat back down, and put her arms around the plates.

* * *

They ran the rest of the night in shifts, Silas and Tess trading the sweep while Maren slept sitting up with the satchel and Odile kept her watch with the rifle across her knees, eyes open, missing nothing. Toward the gray hour before dawn the canyon walls lowered and softened into hills, and the river widened and slowed, and the air began to smell of farms and woodsmoke and the ordinary world.

Silas handed the sweep to Tess and sat down heavily beside Odile, and found, to his mild horror, that he was smiling.

"You look like a man remembering something good," Odile observed.

"I'm remembering being good at something bad," Silas said. "Twenty years I've been ashamed of what I knew. Turns out it was just knowledge. It depends entirely on what you point it at."

"And tonight you pointed it at justice."

"Tonight I pointed it downstream," Silas said. "Justice is Ilsa Vey's problem now. We're just the delivery."

Tess, at the sweep, spoke without turning around. "Boundary post is four hours. Maybe five."

"I know."

"And the concession ends at dawn."

"I know that too."

Tess was quiet a moment, reading the water, reading him. "Bram's still up there," she said. Not a question. Just a fact laid on the deck between them, plain as a compass needle.

Silas looked back upriver, at the country they'd bled through, at the pass somewhere in the graying distance where a wounded ex-constable was spending bullets like a miser spends his last coin. The boundary post waited downstream with its seal and its ten-year lock, and the capital waited beyond it with warrants and newspapers and a witness who could hang a company, and every sensible mile pointed down.

"Yes," Silas said. "He is."

The river carried them toward the dawn, and Silas Dunmore began, silently, to plan the climb back up.
