## 18. Permit's End

Maren had imagined the boundary post a hundred times during the descent, and in every imagining it was smaller than this.

In her head it had been a stick with a tin plate, something a clerk would drive into the mud and forget. What stood at the river's edge instead was a timber frame the height of two men, squared and braced, painted in the concession's colors — deep green over white — with a copper plate bolted to the crossbeam that read, in letters meant to be read from a moving boat: *ALDERVANE CONCESSION — LIMIT OF SURVEY — BY ORDER OF THE BUREAU OF LANDS AND MINES.* Below that, smaller, almost apologetic: *Permit expires at first light, the 31st of Harvestmonth.*

The river ran gray and quick past it, carrying them the last few hundred yards, and Maren found she couldn't look away from the plate. It was the most official thing she had ever seen, and it was also, she understood now, a door closing.

Behind them, somewhere back in the dark they had come down through, Bram Hale was lying in a narrow pass with a rifle and a wound that had soaked two shirts and a blanket, buying them the hours they'd needed. Ahead of them, past the post, the river widened into country where the law still meant something, or at least pretended to.

"Hour before dawn," Silas said quietly. He stood at the stern with the sweep oar, and in the last of the starlight his face was just a shape with a voice attached. "We make the boundary with time to spare."

"Time to spare," Tess repeated, from the bow, where she had spent the whole night's run with Odile Ferran's head cradled against her shoulder. The survey survivor had drifted in and out of consciousness for two days, waking in fragments — the mine, the cages, the massacre, the names of the dead, all of it poured out in a voice like dry paper. "That's a funny way to say it."

"It's an accurate way to say it," Silas said.

The plates were wrapped in oilcloth and lashed under Maren's knees. Her father's original survey plates, the ones he had hidden under a cairn in a glacial cathedral of a valley, the ones worth more than the mine that had killed him. She had carried them down the mountain herself, and her shoulders still ached from it, and she would have carried them twice as far. They were the whole case. Odile was the witness. The plates were the proof. Between the two of them, the Aldervane Company was going to burn.

If they got past that post before first light.

* * *

They grounded the boat on the shingle a stone's throw below the post, and for a while nobody moved. The eastern sky had gone the color of dirty pewter, with a thin band of orange sitting on the hills like a coin balanced on its edge. Maren got out first, legs unsteady, and helped Tess lift Odile down. The woman weighed almost nothing. That frightened Maren more than the enforcers had.

Silas beached the boat and stood looking upriver for a long moment. Then he looked at the post. Then he did something Maren had learned to dread over the past three weeks: he took off his hat.

"Silas," she said.

"We're through," he said. "That's the thing. We're *through*. Odile gets to a doctor, the plates get to Ilsa Vey, the testimony gets written down and witnessed and stamped, and in a month there are warrants. That's the job. That's the whole job." He turned the hat in his hands. "Bram's not part of the job."

"Bram is part of *us*," Tess said. It came out of her flat and certain, the way she said most things now. Somewhere between the workhouse and the portage of bones, the girl had burned off every ounce of hesitation she owned. "Don't you dare stand there and do arithmetic."

"I'm not doing arithmetic." Silas put the hat back on. "I'm doing what I should've done on the Vane Glacier, and nobody stopped me, and four people died because of it. I stood at the bottom of that icefall and I did the sums and the sums said *turn back*, and I turned back, and I have spent eleven years being right and hating it." He looked at Maren. "You were there when I told the story. At the cold camp. You remember what I said."

*The sums said turn back, and I turned back, and the mountain kept the difference.*

"I remember," Maren said.

"Well." He gestured upriver, back toward the dark wall of the valley, toward the pass where a wounded ex-constable was holding a rifle on whatever came for them. "The sums say turn back. The permit expires in — " he squinted east — "call it fifty minutes. After that, this whole valley is sealed. Ten years. No survey, no rescue, no law. If I go in now, I can be at the pass in three hours if the trail's kind, four if it isn't. If I go in and I'm not out by first light — " he stopped, and Maren watched him decide not to finish the sentence, and loved him for it, and hated him for it.

"Then you're sealed in for a decade," she finished for him.

"Then I'm sealed in for a decade." He said it the way a man reads his own sentence. "Ten years. I'd come out at fifty-one. If I come out." He looked down at Odile, unconscious against Tess's shoulder, and at the oilcloth bundle under Maren's arm. "You three don't need me for the rest. The river below the post is easy water. Any ferryman can run it. The hard part's done."

"You absolute *mule*," Tess said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and Maren saw that the girl was crying without making any fuss about it, the way she did everything. "You taught me knots. You said — you said a crew is just people who decide to be one. You don't get to decide to not be one. That's not how it works."

"Tess — "

"No. *No.*" She shifted Odile gently against a driftwood log and stood up, small and furious in the gray light. "Bram stayed in that pass because you asked him to. Because you *promised* him you'd come back. I heard you. I was right there. You put your hand on his shoulder and you said *I'll be back for you before the light*, and if you walk through that boundary post and keep walking, then every word you've ever said to any of us is just — is just rope you hand people before you drop them."

Silas flinched. Actually flinched, a small involuntary movement, like a man touched with a live coal.

Maren stepped between them, mostly because someone had to, and partly because she had spent the whole descent doing the same sums Silas was doing and arriving at the same answer and hating herself for it, and she wanted to say her piece before her courage ran out.

"Here's what I see," she said. "I see a man who spent eleven years believing he was a coward because he made the safe choice once, under impossible conditions, and four people died anyway. I see that the safe choice is standing right in front of him again, wearing a different mountain, and he's about to make the other one this time." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I'm not saying it's wise. I'm saying I understand it. And I'm saying — " her voice did something embarrassing, and she let it — "my father went up that river knowing what he knew, because the truth mattered more than coming home. I've spent three weeks being angry at him for it. I'm done being angry. If you go back in after Bram, you're doing what he did. You're choosing a person over a probability. And I think — I *think* — that's the only thing that was ever going to scrub the Vane Glacier off you."

The band of orange on the hills had widened. Somewhere upslope a bird started up, one idiotic cheerful note repeated, and Maren wanted to strangle it.

Silas looked at each of them in turn. Then at the post. Then upriver.

"Odile first," he said. "We put her and the plates on the water with you. I walk you to the post, I see you across the line, and *then* I go. That's not negotiable. If I'm going to be a fool, I'll be a fool in the right order."

* * *

They rigged a litter from a sweep oar and the boat's canvas, and carried Odile the last stretch to the boundary post, Tess walking beside her the whole way with one hand resting on the woman's arm as if steadiness could be transferred by touch. The copper plate caught the first real light as they reached it, and flared, and Maren thought: *there it is. The exact minute.* She had spent her life around maps and instruments and the precise notation of where things were, and here was the purest coordinate she had ever stood on — the line between the sealed country and the open one, the line between the law and the story they would have to tell instead of it.

Silas shook each of their hands like a man closing accounts. With Tess he did something more — put both hands on her shoulders, looked at her for a long moment, and said, "Apprentice guide. First of my career. Don't let it go to your head." Tess laughed wetly and punched him in the arm, hard enough that he rocked.

With Maren he said nothing at all. He took the oilcloth bundle from under her arm, weighed it, and handed it back. "Your father's work," he said. "It was always going to be you who carried it out. I just carried you far enough."

Then he turned and looked at the pass road, and Maren saw the whole decision land on him at once — saw it the way you see weather come over a ridge, all at once and from everywhere. Eleven years of blacklist. Eleven years of being the man who turned back. And now a sealed valley, a decade of exile if he missed the light, a wounded friend, and a body that had taken everything three rivers and two mountains could give.

"Silas," she said. "The light."

"I know." He didn't move. "Funny thing," he said, to nobody, to the river, maybe to Bram, "I spent eleven years thinking the mountain was punishing me. And it wasn't. It was just waiting to see what I'd do next time."

He stepped over the line.

Backward, facing them, so that the boundary post was at his back and the sealed valley ahead of him, and he touched two fingers to his hat brim in a little salute that Maren would remember for the rest of her life, and then he turned and went up the pass road at a guide's pace — that long, ground-eating, infuriatingly economical stride — and the gray light took him in pieces, legs, back, shoulders, hat, and then the trail bent around a spur and he was gone, and the valley was sealed, and the bird on the hillside was still saying its one stupid note.

Tess was crying openly now, holding Odile's hand. Maren put her arms around them both and looked up at the pass road until her eyes blurred.

"Come on," she said finally, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "We've got a witness to deliver, plates to unseal, and a company to burn down. He'd never forgive us if we did it half-heartedly."

They pushed the boat out into the current. Behind them the boundary post stood in the new sun, and above it, faint as a scratch on glass, Maren thought she could see — though she would never swear to it, not under oath, not to anyone — a figure on the spur of the pass, small and upright, one hand raised.

Then the river took the bend, and took the post, and took the valley, and there was only the water ahead, running fast and clean toward the capital, toward Ilsa Vey's marble offices, toward warrants and testimony and the end of the Aldervane Company.

Toward the ten years they would have to survive first — and the man on the far side of them.

Maren dug in her oar.

"Hold on, Bram," she said to the hills. "We're coming back with the law. And then he's coming back for you."

The current, which owed nobody anything, carried them on.
