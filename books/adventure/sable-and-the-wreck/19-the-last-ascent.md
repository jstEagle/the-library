## 19. The Last Ascent

The boundary post was a stick of painted timber and a lie.

Silas stood at it in the grey before dawn, watching the concession seal go up — literally up, a paper notice nailed to the post by some clerk's clerk in the capital, its ink already bleeding in the river damp. *By order of the Aldervane Concession Company, all passage beyond this point is forbidden until such time as—* The rest had washed to illegibility years ago, apparently. Even the company couldn't be bothered to keep its own fences mended.

Behind him, downstream, Maren was arguing with the current of the morning itself. He could hear her without hearing her: *You can't go back in there. The permits are dead. The valley is sealed. Bram knew what he was choosing.*

All true. And none of it mattered, because Silas Dunmore had spent eleven days learning that truth was a thing you carried, not a thing that carried you.

"You're going to do it regardless," Maren said, arriving at his elbow with her father's journal strapped to her chest like armor. "Aren't you."

"Looks that way."

"You'll die in there. Or worse — you'll be *in there* when they reopen it in ten years, and they'll arrest you at the gate like a mushroom someone forgot to pick."

"That's oddly specific."

"I've had time to think about it." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, he could see her hating it, jaw set against the wobble. "Silas. We got them out. Odile, the plates, all of it. It's done. Let it be done."

He looked at the post. At the washed-out notice. At the country beyond it, where the Kestrel went from a river to a rumor and the mountains stood up out of the mist like old men refusing to answer questions.

On the Vane Glacier, nine years ago, he had turned around. That was the whole story of his disgrace, told in one gesture: he had looked at the storm coming down the col, looked at Hendricks with his broken ankle, done the arithmetic every guide is taught first — *one life is not worth five* — and turned around. And the arithmetic had been correct. The inquiry said so. The blacklist said so. Every night since then had said so, quietly, in the voice of a man who knows the sums are right and hates the answer anyway.

Bram Hale had stayed at the pass with a rifle and one working arm so that three people and a witness could live. Bram Hale was still there. Probably.

Probably was not arithmetic. Probably was the thing Silas had spent nine years learning never to bet a man's life on, and here he was, about to bet the only currency he had left.

"Maren," he said. "Take the boat down. Get Odile and the plates to the boundary commission at Fennick's Landing. Don't wait for me past noon tomorrow. If I'm not there, I'm not coming, and you'll know what to do with what we gave you."

"And if I refuse?"

"You won't. You're your father's daughter. You know which promises matter." He shouldered the pack he'd repacked in the dark — rope, the good axe, morphine tablets, four days of food compressed into two. "And take Tess's hat off the crate before it blows into the water. She'll want it when she wakes up."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something he hadn't expected: she hugged him, quick and fierce, around the middle, face pressed into his coat, gone again before he could decide whether to return it.

"For the record," she said, stepping back, eyes bright and furious, "I think this is stupid."

"Noted for the record."

"And if you die, I'm putting it in the book. My book. The map notes. *Guide Silas Dunmore, died of stubbornness, elevation approximately 2,100 meters.*"

"Fair enough." He almost smiled. "Make sure you spell my name right."

Then he turned and walked upstream, into the sealed country, and did not look back — because looking back was how the Vane Glacier had started, and he was done starting things that way.

* * *

The pass where Bram had made his stand was six hours' hard climbing above the boundary, most of it through country that had no business being climbed by anyone, least of all a fifty-one-year-old man with bad knees and a guilty conscience.

Silas climbed it the way he'd learned to climb everything: badly, then correctly. The blacklisted routes were burned into him deeper than the official ones ever were. Any Bureau-trained guide would take the east spur — visible, graded, marked with cairns some optimistic surveyor had stacked a decade back. The east spur was also a shooting gallery, three hundred meters of open scree under the ridge where the enforcers had their camp, and Silas had watched enough muzzle flash over the last two days to have opinions about open ground.

So he took the chimney instead. The Vane Chimney, the old-timers called it, though the old-timers were mostly dead or discredited, and Silas was both. A crack in the mountain's shoulder wide enough for a determined body and too narrow for a comfortable mind, wet with snowmelt, choked with loose stone that announced every move to the entire watershed. He climbed it slowly, deliberately, placing each handhold like a man writing an apology letter one word at a time.

Halfway up, wedged in a niche, he found a tin cup. Enamel, chipped, with a survey stake driven beside it and a strip of cloth tied on — red, faded to pink.

Bram's marker. Left sometime in the last day and a half, meaning: *I came this far. Meaning:* I am or was, somewhere above.

Silas sat in the niche with the cup in his hands and let himself feel, for exactly thirty seconds, the thing he'd been holding off since dawn. Then he put the cup in his pack — evidence, or relic, or both — and kept climbing.

The wilderness forgave nothing. It never had. It didn't forgive the Vane Glacier, didn't forgive Hendricks, wouldn't forgive him now if he slipped. But standing in that crack in the mountain, muscles burning, lungs full of cold iron air, Silas realized something that felt less like wisdom than like simple fatigue: he had stopped asking it to.

For nine years he'd treated every mountain like a tribunal. Every route a cross-examination. Every storm a verdict being read out. He'd climbed defensively, apologetically, waiting for the range to bring up the old charges. And the range hadn't cared. The mountains had never once mentioned Hendricks. Only Silas had. The wilderness wasn't holding a grudge. It was just being a wilderness — indifferent, magnificent, and scrupulously fair in the way that weather is fair, which is to say: it rained on everyone.

He stopped asking. He just climbed.

* * *

He found the pass at dusk, and found it littered with brass.

Spent cartridges, dozens of them, arranged in a rough arc behind a low wall of stone that Bram had built — or reinforced; some of the stones were older, moss-grown, maybe left by surveyors or smugglers or the mountain itself. Beyond the wall, the ground fell away toward the mine road, and scattered down the slope were the small dark details that Silas's eye catalogued before his stomach could object: dropped kit, a torn strap, dark stains gone brown with weather, and further down, mercifully distant, shapes he chose not to count.

No sign of Bram.

"Bram!" His voice cracked off the rock walls and came back thinner. "Hale! Constable!"

Nothing. Wind. A bird somewhere, complaining about the weather on principle.

He climbed over the wall and saw the blood trail immediately — drag marks, deliberate, heading back and up, away from the pass, toward a shallow overhang fifty meters along the ridge. Whoever had dragged themselves had done it in stages. Rest. Drag. Rest. Drag. The kind of progress that costs everything per meter.

Silas followed it with his heart in his throat doing the thing hearts do when they're trying to climb ahead of the body.

The overhang was deep enough to crawl into, floored with dry pine needles, and in it, curled on his side with his ruined arm bound in a sling made from a shirt sleeve and his rifle across his knees like a child's toy he refused to surrender even in sleep — Bram Hale.

Alive. Breathing. Breathing was the important part, and he was doing it, shallow but steady, his face grey as the granite and crusted with two days of weather.

"Bram."

One eye opened. It took a moment to focus, and when it did, it focused with the particular flat clarity of a man who has been shot, has held a pass alone for two days, and has decided that surprise is a luxury.

"Dunmore." Voice like gravel in a bucket. "You're late."

"Traffic on the east spur."

"Enforcers?"

"Pulled back to the mine last night. Odile's testimony will do the rest." Silas knelt, already working — pulse, pupils, the wound dressing stiff with old blood but not fresh, thank God, not fresh. "How bad?"

"Shoulder went through and through. Arm works about as well as a government inquiry." Bram tried to sit up and failed with tremendous dignity. "Legs are fine. Mostly. There's a rib situation."

"Can you walk?"

"I can walk." A pause. The flat eye held his. "Can't climb. Tried the chimney yesterday after the shooting stopped. Got twenty feet up and my arm quit. Came back down." Another pause, longer, and something moved under the flatness — the thing Bram had been carrying since the constabulary, since before, maybe since always. "Figured if nobody came, I'd stay here and make them come to me. Waste their time. Buy yours."

Silas sat back on his heels. Above them the sky was going the color of a bruise healing, and somewhere below, faintly, he could hear the mine machinery turning over, patient as digestion.

"Well," he said, and found his voice had gone rough. "Somebody came."

"Somebody always does." Bram closed the eye. "Took you long enough to be somebody."

* * *

They slept four hours, because neither of them could sleep more and both of them needed some. Silas fed Bram morphine and broth from the tinned supplies he'd hauled up the chimney on his back, and while Bram drifted, Silas studied the far side of the pass by moonlight and did the thing only he could do.

There was no way down the north face. Not officially. The Bureau maps showed a blank — *impassable cliffs, no viable descent* — and the Bureau maps were right, in the way that official things are right: technically accurate and completely useless to anyone in actual trouble.

But Silas had been in trouble on this range before the blacklist, during the blacklist, and once memorably because of the blacklist, and trouble teaches you things maps don't. Nine years ago, running from a warrant that turned out to be a clerical error but felt real enough at the time, he had crossed this very massif by a route the maps denied existed: a long diagonal shelf system on the north face, hidden under cornices half the year, invisible from below, connected by short scrambles and one gully that required a rope and a strong opinion about gravity. The locals called it the Ladder. Nobody local used it anymore. Nobody local, these days, was allowed near the mountain at all.

It would take two days. With a wounded man who couldn't use one arm, three. Maybe four.

Silas looked at Bram sleeping, at the rifle across his knees, at the chipped enamel cup sitting between them like a third companion, and began coiling rope.

* * *

Dawn of the second day found them on the Ladder, and the Ladder was everything the maps promised and less.

The shelves were real — broad enough in places to walk abreast, in places barely wider than Bram's boots — and they traversed the face in long, lazy switchbacks, like a drunkard's signature across the cliff. The cornices overhead groaned in the morning warmth, shedding occasional lumps of snow that fell past them with soft, insulting ease. Below, eight hundred meters of nothing waited with the patience of geology.

Bram walked. That was the miracle Silas kept not remarking on. One arm strapped tight, ribs wrapped in the last of the bandage, face white as the cornice snow — and walking, meter after meter, hour after hour, with the grim mechanical persistence of a man who had once held a pass alone for two days and considered this merely the next assignment.

"Tell me something," Bram said, on the fourth shelf, when the sun had cleared the ridge and the climbing had settled into rhythm. "The Vane Glacier. The story everyone tells. Is it true?"

Silas placed his boots carefully on a ledge furred with lichen. "Which version?"

"The one where you abandoned a man to save four."

"It's the true one."

"Hm." Bram negotiated a step-down, hissing through his teeth. "I held this pass because I did the arithmetic. Three lives moving fast beat one life standing slow. Same sum you did." He glanced sideways. "Difference is, you came back for me. Nobody came back for Hendricks."

"They couldn't have. Storm had the col for six days. Coming back would've killed the other four and not saved him." Silas heard his own voice reciting the inquiry findings, the ones he'd repeated ten thousand times to ten thousand mirrors. "I know the math."

"So why nine years of penance?"

Silas stopped. Below them the valley opened up — the Kestrel a silver thread now, going home without them, the smoke of Harrow's Bend a smudge on the horizon's imagination. He looked at it for a while.

"Because the math was right," he said finally, "and it didn't help. Knowing the sums are correct doesn't make the answer hurt less. I thought if I punished myself long enough, the numbers would change." He started walking again. "They don't. They're just numbers. The only thing that changes them is what you do next."

Bram was quiet for three shelves. Then: "That's the first sensible thing anyone's said to me since I resigned."

"Gentle humor and hard-won wisdom. It's on my card."

"Your card says *blacklisted guide, will work for food.*"

"That's the old card."

* * *

The gully came on the afternoon of the third day, and it was as bad as memory and slightly worse.

Forty meters of steep ice-smeared rock, one good anchor at the top, and a wounded ex-constable to lower down it with one functioning arm between them. Silas rigged the anchor twice, tested it with his full weight plus the pack plus a generous margin for divine skepticism, and then lowered Bram in slow, grinding increments while Bram narrated his own descent in the flat voice of a man filing a report.

"Ten meters. Still alive. Fifteen. Regretting the rib comment. Twenty. If I die here, tell Maren her map notes need better margins—"

"If you die here I'm leaving you, and I mean that sincerely."

"—thirty. Ground. Barely. Tell her anyway."

Silas followed down, retrieved the rope with a sling trick that had gotten him banned from two climbing clubs, and they stood together at the bottom of the Ladder looking at green country for the first time in three days. Real forest. Soft ground. Water that ran downhill without malice.

Bram sat down heavily on a log and laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. "We're going to make it."

"Don't say that. Mountains listen."

"Mountains don't care, Dunmore. That's the whole point of them." He tipped his head back against the log, exhausted beyond posture, and grinned up at the canopy. "That's the *whole point* of them."

Silas looked at him — this man who'd walked away from the law and walked back into gunfire for strangers, who'd held a pass with one arm and cracked jokes down a gully — and felt the last cold knot of nine years come loose somewhere behind his sternum. Not forgiven. The wilderness didn't forgive. But the account was settled, or close enough to settled that a man could stop auditing it.

"Come on, Constable," he said, hauling Bram up by his good arm. "Fennick's Landing by tomorrow night. Maren will have written your eulogy. You'll want to edit it."

"She'd have spelled my name wrong on purpose."

"Almost certainly."

They started down through the trees, two battered men and a rifle, following water toward people. And a mile on, rounding a bend in the game trail, they came out onto a bluff above the river — and Silas stopped dead.

Below, moored at a gravel bar where no boat had any business being, flying concession colors at half-mast as if the company itself had begun to mourn, lay a patrol boat. And on its deck, arguing with a subordinate in a voice that carried clear across the water despite the distance, stood a lean figure Silas had last seen going overboard into the Sable Narrows.

Quill. Alive. Downstream of them. Between them and Fennick's Landing.

Bram followed his gaze, and the grin drained slowly off his face. "Tell me that's not who I think it is."

Silas watched the patrol boat cast off, turning its bow downriver — toward the boundary, toward Maren, toward Odile and the plates and everything they'd bled for.

"It's him," Silas said. "And we're three days behind him."
