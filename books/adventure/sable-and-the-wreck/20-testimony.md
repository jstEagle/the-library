## 20. Testimony

The capital smelled of coal smoke and cut flowers, which Odile Ferran decided was the most dishonest thing about it. A city that perfumed its own filth.

She had been in the city four days, and in that time she had been fed better than she had eaten in two years, slept in a bed softer than any she'd known since the survey, and been coached — patiently, relentlessly, and by three separate lawyers who all wore the same expression of expensive concern — on exactly what she was permitted to say tomorrow.

"You will address the tribunal as 'Honored Commissioners,'" said the youngest of them, a round man named Pellwick who reminded her of a pigeon that had learned to read. "You will describe only what you personally witnessed. You will not speculate as to motive, corporate structure, or—"

"Or names," Odile said.

Pellwick's smile flickered. "The Bureau's counsel feels it would be more effective to let the documentary evidence speak to individuals. Your testimony establishes the *conditions*. The plates establish the *ore*. The permits establish the *timeline*. No one person needs to be—"

"Mr. Aldervane was shot in the back of the head while kneeling," Odile said. "He was kneeling because they told him to kneel. I knelt next to him. The man who gave the order was called Dross, and he reported to a director named Corvane Helt, whose signature is on the requisition for the enforcer detachment, and if you think I survived nineteen days in a cage so that I could stand up in front of your tribunal and say *conditions were suboptimal*, then you have wasted your pigeon feed, Mr. Pellwick."

The room went quiet. Somewhere below, in the Bureau's marble atrium, a clerk laughed at something, the sound rising through the floor like a bubble through water.

Pellwick cleared his throat. "I'll inform the Commissioners' office of your... approach."

"Do that," Odile said. "And bring me tea. The real kind, not whatever this is." She lifted the cup and peered into it. "This tastes like it was brewed over a grudge."

* * *

They kept her in a guest suite on the fourth floor, which she understood was a courtesy and also a cage with better upholstery. She was not supposed to leave without an escort. She was not supposed to speak to the press. She was, above all, not supposed to speak to *them* — the four who had pulled her out of the quarry country, who were lodged somewhere across the city in rooms the Bureau had also paid for, and whom Odile had seen exactly once, across a courtyard, before the lawyers descended like weather.

So when a knock came at her door that evening — three quick raps, then one slow one, a rhythm she did not recognize but which carried, unmistakably, the confidence of someone who had already decided the door would open — she opened it.

Tess stood in the corridor holding a paper sack and wearing the expression of a girl who had talked her way past three checkpoints and intended to talk her way past a fourth if necessary.

"They've got a boy on the stairs," Tess said, "but he's fifteen and bored, and I told him I was delivering laundry. Nobody questions the laundry girl." She held up the sack. "Bram sent sweets. He says you're owed interest. He wouldn't explain on what."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. In the quarry, this child had come to the bars of her cage in the dark, small hands passing bread through wire, and had promised — in a whisper, with the guards forty paces off — *I'm coming back for you.* Odile had believed her, which was itself remarkable, because Odile had stopped believing anyone months before that.

"You climbed out of a workhouse," Odile said, "crossed half a wilderness, got shot at on two rivers, and now you're committing crimes against a government building. For sweets?"

"For Bram's interest," Tess said solemnly. Then the grin broke through. "Also I wanted to see you. Everyone keeps asking me questions and none of them are the right ones."

"Come in before the laundry girl gets arrested."

They sat by the window, where the light was going amber over the rooftops, and split the sweets — candied ginger, which Odile ate with the reverence of a woman reacquainting herself with civilization — and Tess talked. About Silas, who paced his room like a caged animal and had already tried to leave twice and been caught once. About Maren, who spent every evening redrawing her father's map from memory, refining it, arguing with the dead man's bearings until they confessed their secrets. About Bram, who was walking again, slowly, with a stick he pretended not to need, and who had taken to sitting in the Bureau's public reading room reading law books "like a man planning to sue God."

"And Quill?" Odile asked quietly.

"Overboard," Tess said. "Maren put him there. He grabbed a branch downstream, last anyone saw. Silas says men like Quill always grab a branch." She shrugged. "Silas says a lot of things."

"He does," Odile agreed. "I watched him hold a pass against six rifles with a pistol and a bad attitude. He talks like a man apologizing for existing and fights like he's never once doubted himself. It's a strange combination."

"It's why we keep him," Tess said.

Then, because Tess was fourteen and honest in the way only children and the recently starved can be: "Are you scared? About tomorrow?"

Odile considered lying, found she didn't want to, and said, "Terrified. Not of the commissioners. Of the moment after. You spend long enough surviving and it becomes the whole of you. Tomorrow it stops being survival and starts being *testimony*, and testimony has to be true in a particular way, in a room full of people being paid to find it inconvenient." She turned the ginger tin in her fingers. "In the cage, I promised myself that if I ever got out, I'd say the names. All of them. Out loud, slowly, so no one could pretend they hadn't heard."

"Good," said Tess, with the flat finality of youth. "Say them loud. Old people never turn their hearing aids down for anything important."

Odile laughed — really laughed, the first time since the Kestrel — and the sound startled them both.

* * *

The tribunal chamber had been a ballroom once, before the Republic decided pageantry was vulgar and converted it to jurisprudence, keeping the chandeliers and adding microphones. Seven commissioners sat behind a curved bench of black walnut. Below them, counsel tables; beyond those, a gallery packed with journalists, clerks, and — Odile spotted her immediately — Ilsa Vey, seated in the second row with the stillness of a woman who had rehearsed not fidgeting.

Odile took the stand at nine o'clock precisely. Her hands were steady. She noted this with detached surprise, the way one notes good weather.

For two hours, they led her through it. The survey camp on the lower Kestrel. The arrival of the patrol boat flying concession colors. The separation of the party — the skilled ones marched upstream toward the mine workings, the rest made to kneel on the gravel bar. She described the sound the rifles made, which was smaller than people expected. She described the cage at the quarry, the shifts, the cold, the guard named Dross who liked to talk, because men who like to talk eventually tell you everything — the tonnage figures, the shipping schedule, the name of the man in the capital who signed the requisitions.

"Corvane Helt," she said, and let it sit in the air like a struck bell.

Commissioner Ardle, a dry woman with spectacles like coins, leaned forward. "Witness, these are grave accusations. You understand that the documents supporting them — the plates, the journal pages recovered from the source cache — are still under Bureau seal pending authentication?"

"I understand," Odile said, "that the Bureau has had four days alone with them."

A rustle went through the gallery. At the counsel table, Pellwick winced with his whole body.

"The Bureau," Ardle said carefully, "has acted in good faith throughout this matter. It was the Bureau's expedition that discovered the crime. It was the Bureau that commissioned the rescue."

"It was the Bureau," Odile agreed pleasantly, "that hired a guide it had blacklisted, staffed his crew with a plant reporting to Director Vey's office, and scheduled the rescue so that it concluded eleven days before the concession expired — leaving, by my arithmetic, barely enough time to reach the valley and almost none to return with evidence. If that is good faith, Commissioner, I'd hate to see the bad."

The silence had texture now. In the second row, Ilsa Vey had gone very still, the stillness of a chess player realizing the board has changed.

* * *

They broke at midday, and Odile ate lunch in a side chamber under the eye of two clerks, and at one-fifteen Ilsa Vey walked in as though the room had been booked for her, which possibly it had.

"Miss Ferran." Vey set a leather folder on the table and did not sit. Up close she was smaller than her reputation, gray at the temples, with eyes that did arithmetic constantly. "I wanted to speak with you before the afternoon session. As a courtesy."

"A courtesy," Odile said. "Those seem popular today."

"What you endured is beyond defending. What you survived is extraordinary. And what you intend to do this afternoon — I'm asking you to consider whether it serves the victims." Vey opened the folder. Inside were photographs: the plates, the journal, laid out on Bureau tables. "The evidence is secure. It will convict. Helt, Dross, the whole apparatus — the plates don't lie, and neither do you. But the manner of their recovery..." She paused, choosing words the way a surgeon chooses instruments. "The guide's methods were irregular. Unlicensed travel in sealed territory. An armed confrontation at the source. There are commissioners on that bench who will seize on any irregularity to muddy the water, to make this about procedure instead of murder. If your testimony entangles the rescue party in legal jeopardy, the scandal swallows the crime. The directors walk. Is that what Aldervane died for?"

It was well done. Odile gave her that. Every word true, every implication false, the whole structure balanced like a house of cards in a draftless room.

"You want me to testify about the mine and stay silent about the mountain," Odile said.

"I want justice for thirty-one murdered people."

"No," Odile said. "You want a clean verdict. Those aren't the same thing, and you know it, because you built the crew yourself. You chose Dunmore because a blacklisted man is deniable. You planted Quill because you needed eyes. You timed the window because you needed the plates more than you needed the people carrying them. I lay in a cage for nineteen days listening to guards gossip, Miss Vey. Do you know what prisoners do? They memorize. Everything. Names, dates, tonnage, and which officials in the capital were receiving ore samples marked as agricultural survey material." She leaned forward. "Your signature was on two of the transmittal slips. I saw it described twice, in detail, by a man who hated you for cutting his bonus."

Something moved behind Vey's eyes — not fear, exactly. Recalculation.

"So here is my courtesy," Odile went on. "When I take that stand this afternoon, I will say the names of the men who murdered my colleagues. I will say the name of the director who armed them. And when the commissioners ask how the evidence reached the capital — and they will ask, because Commissioner Ardle has the look of a woman who reads footnotes — I will tell them about a disgraced guide who went back into hell for strangers, about a constable who took a bullet at the pass, about a cartographer's daughter who navigated a river that killed better men than either of them, and about a fourteen-year-old girl who kept a promise made through cage wire." She stood. "You may try to bury Silas Dunmore, Miss Vey. But you should know that I spent nineteen days learning how to be unburiable, and I am an excellent teacher."

Vey gathered her folder with unhurried hands. At the door she paused. "You'd throw away a clean conviction for sentiment."

"I'd trade a clean conviction for a true one," Odile said. "Sentiment is just truth with the temperature left in."

The door closed. One of the clerks, a young man with ink-stained fingers, was staring at her with naked admiration. "Ma'am," he said, "that was — I mean — the stenographer is going to need a new ribbon."

"Send her my regards," Odile said, and finished her tea, which was still terrible, but which she drank anyway, because tomorrow she might be in a cell and good habits mattered.

* * *

The afternoon session ran four hours. Odile gave them every name, every date, every tonnage figure, and when Ardle asked the question — *how did the evidence reach this capital?* — she answered it fully, warmly, and at length, and watched Silas Dunmore's name enter the official record spoken by a witness rather than whispered by a file.

The warrants came down at dusk. Corvane Helt was arrested boarding a night steamer with two trunks and a forged passport. Two more directors surrendered by morning. Dross, captured in the quarry country by constables sent upriver, was talking already; men who like to talk always did.

Outside the tribunal steps, the press was a wall of noise, and through it Odile saw them: Maren with her father's map rolled under her arm like a scepter; Bram leaning on his stick and pretending otherwise; Tess waving both arms as though Odile might miss her, which was impossible; and Silas, at the edge of the group, hat in hand, looking profoundly uncomfortable with daylight and attention and hope.

Odile came down the steps toward them, and the first thing she said, loud enough for the nearest reporters to hear, was:

"Gentlemen — the man you want is Silas Dunmore. Get his name right."

And Silas, mortified, muttered something about needing a drink, and Tess laughed, and somewhere across the square Ilsa Vey watched them go — four rescuers and a witness, walking together into the ordinary evening — and began, very carefully, to recalculate.

But that was her problem now. Odile had testified. The rest, she suspected, would be names cleared — and she intended to be there when they were.
