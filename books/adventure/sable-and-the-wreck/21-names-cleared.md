## 21. Names Cleared

The notice came on a Tuesday, pinned to the board outside the Harrow's Bend assay office between a reward poster for a stolen mule and an advertisement for Dr. Pellam's Invigorating Tonic, which cured everything from gout to grief and had, in Tess's professional opinion, cured neither.

She read it three times to be sure.

*By order of the Cartographic Bureau and the Crown Commission of Concessions: the name Silas Dunmore is hereby struck from the Register of Disbarred Guides, all privileges restored, effective this date.*

Below it, in smaller type: *The Bureau further acknowledges the service rendered by Maren Oduya, Bramwell Hale, and one T. [surname unrecorded], in the recovery of evidence pertaining to the Kestrel concession.*

*T. Surname Unrecorded.*

"They couldn't even spell it," she said to the mule poster. The mule looked sympathetic. "I have a surname. It's Fenwick. I hate it, but I *have* it."

"You could take a new one," said a voice behind her, and there was Maren, arms full of paper tubes, spectacles fogged from the cold, looking like a heron that had decided on a career in cartography. "Half the great surveyors did. Half the great surveyors made them up entirely."

"Did your father?"

"No, but he once spelled his own name wrong on a chart and we've been the Oduyas ever instead of the Oduyars." She shifted the tubes. "Come on. He's at the Boar's Rest pretending he isn't nervous."

"Who?"

"Silas."

Tess fell into step beside her. "Silas doesn't get nervous."

"Silas has been re-reading the same page of the permit for an hour," Maren said. "Bram counted. Eleven times. He's nervous the way glaciers are nervous — you can't see it moving, but the whole valley knows."

* * *

The Boar's Rest smelled of woodsmoke and spilled ale and, faintly, of the river, which clung to some people the way perfume clung to others. Silas Dunmore sat at the corner table with his back to the wall — he would sit with his back to the wall until the day he died, Tess suspected, possibly even after — and a document spread before him that he was studying with the expression of a man who expected it to bite.

Bram Hale sat across from him, one arm still in a sling, his shoulder healing crooked and refusing, on doctor's orders, to do anything dramatic about it. He raised his mug when they came in. "Eleven," he said.

"Twelve," said Silas, without looking up.

"It's real," Tess announced, dropping into the fourth chair. "It's on the board. Your name's off the blacklist. You're a person again."

"I was always a person."

"You were a *cautionary tale*," Bram said. "There's a difference. Cautionary tales don't get permits."

Silas finally set the paper down. He rubbed his jaw, which needed shaving, and looked at each of them in turn — the look he gave before running bad water, the one that weighed everything and promised nothing — and then, unexpectedly, he smiled. It was a small smile, lopsided, rusty at the hinges, like a gate opened for the first time in years. But it reached his eyes, and Tess thought: *there he is.* That was the face she'd caught glimpses of around cold campfires, the man under the disgrace, the one who taught knots by lamplight and pretended not to notice when you got them right.

"Well," he said. "That's that, then."

"That's that," Maren agreed, and began unrolling paper tubes across the table until the landlord came over and, seeing what they were, simply turned the sign on the door to CLOSED and went back to his tap. He'd been paid in advance. Everyone in Harrow's Bend had been paid in advance, more or less, by the scandal.

What Maren unrolled was the map.

Tess had seen drafts of it for weeks — in the Bureau's borrowed drafting room, under lamplight, Maren's pen scratching past midnight while Tess sat on a stool sharpening pencils and learning, without admitting she was learning, how a coastline became a line. But the finished thing, printed, inked, titled, was another animal altogether.

*THE KESTREL RIVER AND SOURCE VALLEY,* it read. *Surveyed by Edmund Oduya and party. Completed and published by Maren Oduya.*

And beneath the title block, in letters no smaller than any other credit on the sheet:

*With acknowledgment to Silas Dunmore, guide; Bramwell Hale, escort; and Tess Fenwick, of the crew.*

Not *stowaway*. Not *surname unrecorded*. *Of the crew.*

Tess stared at her own name until the letters swam. Fourteen months ago she'd been a number in a workhouse ledger, fed porridge twice a day and addressed as "girl" by people who couldn't be bothered with the second syllable. Now she was typeset. Inked. Permanent. Somewhere in a Bureau archive, in a hundred chart cases, in every schoolroom that would ever hang this map, her name would outlive everyone at this table.

"It's good," Silas said quietly. He touched the source valley with one blunt finger, the glacial cathedral none of them would see again for ten years, sealed now behind law instead of guns. "He'd have been proud. Both of you."

Maren's chin did the thing it did — the small stubborn lift — and she said, "The ore readings are in appendix two, cross-referenced to the testimony. Odile checked every figure twice." Odile Ferran was in the capital still, giving depositions, dismantling the Aldervane company plank by plank with the flat, patient voice of a woman who had watched men die and intended to watch the responsible ones answer for it. Warrants had gone out in the spring. Two directors had already surrendered; the third had surrendered a great deal of money to a very fast ship, and the papers were enjoying themselves immensely.

"And the Bureau?" Bram asked. "They're happy to have their scandal back with a bow on it?"

"The Bureau," Maren said, "is delighted. The Bureau would like very much for everyone to stop asking why its own permits were used to hide a mine. Ilsa Vey sent congratulations." She said the name the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd recovered from.

"She also," Tess said, "sent me a letter." She'd been carrying it for two days, working up to it, and now seemed as good a moment as any, because if she didn't say it aloud soon it would curdle inside her like milk left on a stove. She put the envelope on the map, dead center, over the source valley.

Silas read the direction upside down. His eyebrows did something complicated.

*Miss T. Fenwick. Offer of admission, Cartographic Bureau Training School, full scholarship, by recommendation of I. Vey.*

"A school," Bram said. "In the capital."

"With a uniform," Tess said. "And a dormitory. And a stipend. And classes in spherical trigonometry, which I'm told is like regular trigonometry but worse." She took a breath. "It's everything I ever wanted. When I was in the workhouse I used to dream about exactly this. A bed that was mine. Books nobody could take away. People calling me *Miss Fenwick* like I was somebody."

The table was quiet. On the map, the Kestrel ran blue and innocent from source to mouth.

"So take it," Silas said.

And there it was — the thing she'd known he'd say, the thing that made her want to throw the letter at his head.

"I *did* want it," she said. "That's the point. That's what I keep trying to explain to everybody, including me. I wanted it when wanting it was the only thing I had. But that was—" She gestured vaguely at herself, at all of it. "That was the old me. The workhouse me. The me who thought a warm room was the top of the world." She pushed the letter aside and put her finger where hers and Maren's names sat together in the title block. "Then I went up a river that should have killed me four separate ways, and I held a dying woman's hand through a cage door, and I learned that the top of the world isn't a warm room. It's people who come back for you. Every single time. Even when it costs them."

"Tess," Maren started gently.

"Let me finish, I've rehearsed this." She turned to Silas. "You're going up another river. You told the Bureau you'd take the Ashvane survey — the one nobody else will touch, because of course it's you, of course it's the impossible one. They need a crew. And I am done being carried in crates. I want to apprentice. Properly. Knots and water-reading and stars and all of it, starting at the bottom, starting with the bilge if that's what the bottom is." Her voice wobbled and she hated it and pushed through anyway. "The school will still be there in ten years. So will the workhouse, probably, because the world's slow. But you're only going to run the Ashvane once, and I want to be on the boat when you do."

Silas looked at her for a long time. Outside, someone's dog barked at the mail cart; inside, the fire popped; and Tess sat in the silence and let him weigh her, because being weighed by Silas Dunmore had stopped being frightening somewhere on the Kestrel and become, instead, a kind of honor.

"You'd be giving up a sure thing," he said at last. "A bed. A stipend. Miss Fenwick."

"I've slept rough on granite," she said. "I've eaten hardtack with weevils in it and pretended the weevils were seasoning. I can survive missing a stipend." Then, because honesty was the house rule now, the rule they'd built around that rainbound fire when they'd traded their worst days like currency: "I'm scared of it. Choosing this. It feels like being ungrateful, and it feels like being foolish, and both of those feelings are lying, but they're loud."

"Loud liars usually are," said Bram. He leaned forward, sling and all. "For what it's worth — and I spent nine years wearing a constable's coat, so I know institutions — the Bureau didn't find you. We found you. The Bureau just wrote down where we'd already gone. Take the school and you belong to them. Take the boat and you belong to us. Only one of those options comes with family."

"He means himself," Maren said. "He's terrible at saying it directly."

"I am *extremely* direct."

"You cried at my father's memorial."

"That was dust. There was chalk dust. From the— it was a dusty hall."

Silas huffed something that was almost a laugh, and then he did the thing he almost never did: he reached across the table and put his big scarred hand flat on the map, next to Tess's smaller one, so that for a moment their fingers nearly touched across the blue thread of the river.

"Apprentice," he said. "Proper terms. Wages start small. Chores start large. You'll stand night watches alone and you'll hate them, and you'll do the charts, and if you slack I'll say so, and if you shine I'll likely forget to mention it because I'm bad at that part. Ask Maren."

"He once told me my draftsmanship was 'adequate,'" Maren said. "I framed the memory out of spite."

"But," Silas went on, and his voice dropped into the register he saved for rapids and promises, the one that meant every word had been tested against current first, "you'll never be cargo again. Not on my boat. Crew or nothing. That's the whole contract."

Tess realized her eyes were doing something embarrassing and blinked furiously at the ceiling beams. "Crew," she managed.

"Crew," said Maren, and stuck out her hand to shake on it, formal as a treaty, and then ruined the formality by pulling Tess half across the table into a hug that smelled of ink and river water.

"Bilge duty starts tomorrow," Silas said into the embrace.

"You're ruining the moment."

"The moment wasn't paying its way."

* * *

They celebrated, in the way of people who had learned celebration the hard way: modestly, and with exits mapped. Bram bought the first round and was bought three in return by strangers who'd read the newspapers and wanted to shake the hand of the man who'd held the pass, and he bore it with the long-suffering grace of a man secretly pleased. Maren stood on a chair and read the title block aloud to the room, and the Boar's Rest — half of whom had refused Silas work for six years — drank to *Dunmore's crew*, and Silas sat through it staring into his mug like a man watching weather come in.

Later, when the lamps burned low and Bram had been walked home by Maren (he insisted he didn't need walking; he needed walking), Tess found Silas out on the inn's back step, smoking one of the pipefuls he allowed himself on historic occasions, looking up at the hills.

"Can't sleep either?" she asked.

"Sleeping's fine. Thinking's the problem." He moved over on the step. She sat. For a while neither spoke, which was its own kind of conversation, the kind they'd gotten fluent in.

"The Ashvane's worse than the Kestrel," he said eventually. "Nobody's mining it, far as anyone knows. It's just mean. Cold water, bad portages, and a gorge section nobody's ever mapped. If you come, you come because you want the work, not because you're proving anything. Proving things gets people killed. I've seen it."

"I know. I watched you almost do it."

His pipe paused halfway to his mouth. "Cheek."

"Learned from the best." She hugged her knees. "Silas? Why'd you say yes? To taking me on. Really."

He considered the question honestly, which was more than most adults would have done, smoke curling up into the dark.

"Because on the Narrows," he said, "when you went over the side, Maren went in after you without hesitating a heartbeat. And I stood at the sweep and I knew — knew the way you know a rock under black water — that if it had been me in that water, ten years ago, on the Vane Glacier, nobody would have come in after me. Nobody did." He knocked the pipe out against the heel of his boot. "You lot came in after each other the whole way up that river. I just want to keep sailing with people who do that. It's rare. Rarer than ore."

Tess swallowed. "That's the nicest thing you've ever said."

"Don't tell anyone. I'll deny it."

"There are witnesses."

"Bram saw chalk dust too, did he?"

She laughed, and he almost smiled, and above the rooftops the stars came out thick over the northern hills — over the Ashvane country, cold and unmapped and waiting.

* * *

Three mornings later, at grey light, they loaded the boat.

It was a new boat, Bureau-stamped and properly permitted, which Silas regarded with deep suspicion, as though paperwork might spring a leak. Maren stowed her father's spare instruments — the originals were in the capital, entered into evidence, but Edmund Oduya had owned duplicates of everything, being a man who planned for rivers to eat his luggage. Bram appeared at the dock with his sling off, his arm stiff, and a duffel he dared anyone to comment on. Nobody commented. Some things were obvious.

Tess came last, carrying one canvas bag containing every object she owned in the world, which fit comfortably and always would, and she stopped at the gangplank and looked back once at Harrow's Bend — at the assay office board where a stolen-mule poster flapped in the wind, at the workhouse chimney standing grey and indifferent on the hill, at the town that had been the whole width of her world and was now, thank God, only a place she'd survived.

"You coming, apprentice?" Silas called. "Or does the dock pay better?"

She stepped aboard. The current took them gently, then less gently, and by the first bend the town was gone and the river had them, and Tess took up her post at the bow with a sounding pole she was still learning to love.

Ahead, where the valley narrowed and the water quickened, the first rapid of the Ashvane waited — white water threading through black rock, uncharted, unnamed, humming its low warning note that she felt through the hull and up through her boots and into her chest, where it settled in beside everything else the river had taught her.

"Name it when we've run it," Maren said beside her, pencil ready over a blank chart. "That's the rule. Nothing gets a name till it's been survived."

Tess grinned, spat ceremonially over the side the way Silas had taught her, and gripped the pole.

"Then it hasn't got a name yet," she said. "But it's about to."
