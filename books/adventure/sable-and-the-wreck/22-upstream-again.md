## 22. Upstream Again

The river was called the Merrow, and it was nobody's favorite.

It came down out of the northern ranges in a hurry, all gravel bars and standing waves, the kind of water that forgave nothing and apologized for less. Silas Dunmore had read the survey notes three times, marked the dangerous reaches in pencil, and then — out of old habit — marked them again in ink, because pencil was for men who expected to be wrong.

He stood at the stern of the *Kestrel's Answer* — Maren had named her, and he'd lost the argument about it before it started — and watched Harrow's Bend fall away behind them.

The town looked smaller from the water. It always did. A year ago it had been the whole world: the dock where he'd hauled freight with a bad back and a worse reputation, the Lamplighter where nobody bought him drinks, the Bureau office where Ilsa Vey had once slid a contract across a table like a knife. Now it was just a scatter of rooftops and smoke, already shrinking, already ordinary.

He looked back at it exactly once. That was the rule he'd made for himself, and he was a man who kept his rules, because a man who didn't keep rules on a river ended up keeping company with the drowned.

*That's enough of that,* he told the town, silently. *You had your chance to be rid of me. You didn't take it.*

The blacklist was gone. Struck through in the Bureau's registry in ink that, Maren had informed him with some satisfaction, matched the ink of the original entry exactly. She had a cartographer's feelings about ink.

"Skipper." Tess's voice came down from the bow, where she'd appointed herself lookout, a role she had invented, argued for, and defended so fiercely that Silas had simply surrendered the way he surrendered most things to Tess these days. "Gravel bar, portside, maybe forty yards. And Bram's doing the thing with his face again."

"What thing with his face?" Bram Hale called from midships, where he was ostensibly checking the lashings on the cargo and actually napping in the sun with his rifle within arm's reach, as always.

"The thing where you pretend you're working," Tess said. "Your face does it very convincingly. You should teach a class."

"I was a constable for eleven years," Bram said, without opening his eyes. "I was paid to have a convincing face."

"You were paid to have a convincing face and you still quit," Tess said. "That's a terrible advertisement."

Silas let the banter wash over him the way he let the river wash over the hull — checking its weight, its current, its direction. A year ago, the sound of voices like this behind him would have meant strangers sizing each other up, trust not yet earned. Now it meant the opposite of that. Now it meant *crew*.

The word had taken him a long time to say, even inside his own head. He'd lost crews before. The Vane Glacier had taken six, and the Kestrel had nearly taken four more, and there had been a stretch of last winter — Bram still walking with a cane, the scar on his shoulder pulling tight in the cold, all of them crowded into Maren's rented rooms above the map shop — when Silas had lain awake doing the arithmetic he always did: *what did this cost, what did it cost, what did it cost.*

And for the first time in his life the arithmetic had come out level.

Not cheap. Never cheap. Bram had paid in blood, Odile in years of her life she'd never get back, Maren in a father, Tess in a childhood that had been stolen long before the Kestrel and only partly returned. But *level*. Paid in full. He'd stopped asking the wilderness to forgive him, and the wilderness, in its indifferent way, had stopped punishing him for asking.

The Merrow's first rapid came up an hour past noon, announced not by sound but by the water's color — a sudden milkiness where the current chewed the gravel shallows — and then by sound, a long low mutter like a crowd deciding whether to be a mob.

Silas took the wheel himself. He always took the first rapid of a river himself. It was a superstition, and he'd earned his superstitions the way he'd earned his scars.

"Lines off the bow," he called. "Tess, watch the right channel — if you see green water going the wrong way, sing out."

"Green water going the wrong way," Tess repeated. "Skipper, if I see water doing anything wrong, you'll hear about it for a week."

"That," Silas said, easing the boat toward the left channel, "is why you're lookout."

The rapid was honest, at least. No hidden rocks, no back-eddies waiting to eat a hull — just a quarter mile of big, confused water, the river shrugging off the mountains it had carried on its back all spring. Silas read it the way Maren read maps, top to bottom, and threaded the *Kestrel's Answer* through the standing waves with the boat complaining underneath him and the spray coming aboard cold as a handshake from a stranger.

At the bottom of it, in the long calm eddy below, he looked back.

Once.

Harrow's Bend was still visible, a smudge of smoke against the green. Somewhere in it was the Lamplighter, where the barkeep now bought *him* drinks. Somewhere in it was the Bureau office, where his license hung framed on the wall — Maren had insisted on the frame; Bram had insisted on the largest frame available; Tess had insisted on hanging it crooked, "for humility," and they'd compromised on slightly crooked.

Somewhere in it was a dock where a broken man had once hauled freight and tried not to think about a river.

He looked at all of it for the length of one long breath. Then he turned upstream.

"Skipper." Maren had come aft without him noticing, a survey notebook in one hand and a pencil behind her ear — her father's habit, adopted so seamlessly that sometimes Silas forgot it hadn't always been hers. She nodded at the water ahead. "The notes put the next bad water at the confluence, maybe two days up. After that the mapping's blank for forty miles. Nobody's charted it since Oduya the elder, and his notes are—" she touched the notebook, the one that had come out of a waterproof cache on the Kestrel, the one she'd transcribed and published and defended before a Bureau tribunal with a composure that had made three directors resign rather than face her cross-examination, "—sparse."

"Sparse," Silas said. "Your father called the Sable Narrows 'a mild inconvenience.'"

"He was an optimist. I take after him." She looked at him sideways. "You're doing the thing with your face now."

"I don't have a thing with my face."

"You absolutely do. It's the face you make when you're deciding whether to tell us something. Tess has a name for it. Several names. One of them is unrepeatable in front of Bram."

"Nothing's unrepeatable in front of Bram. Bram was a constable."

"That's what worries me," Maren said, and went forward to take her soundings, leaving Silas alone with the river and the thing with his face.

Because there *was* something. There always was, on the first day, and he'd learned — slowly, clumsily, the way he learned everything that mattered — that keeping it inside was the old way, the blacklist way, the way that had nearly killed them all on the Kestrel. A crew that didn't know what its skipper knew was just four people in a boat.

He called them together at the evening camp, on a gravel bar that sloped down to the water like the palm of a hand. Bram had the fire going with the efficiency of a man who'd spent a wet winter learning to do it one-handed while the other shoulder healed. Tess had the fish lines out, because Tess had discovered on the descent of the Kestrel that she could catch fish when nobody else could, and had never once let anyone forget it. Maren had the maps spread on a crate, weighted with stones.

Silas stood where they could all see him, and told them the truth.

"The Bureau's notes on the upper Merrow are thin," he said. "Thinner than they let on when they issued the license. There's a reach past the confluence the old trappers call the Weeping Stair — a waterfall in steps, no portage recorded, and the one trapper who described it described it from downstream, which tells you everything." He let that sit. "The concession on this river is clean — I checked the permits myself, twice — but the survey that mapped the lower reaches was done by Aldervane's old firm. The same one that faked the Kestrel charts."

The fire cracked. Somewhere up the dark valley, a bird made a sound like a question.

"So we might find nothing," Silas said. "Or we might find another valley full of somebody's secrets. I wanted you to know before we were past the point where turning back is easy. It's your license too. All four names are on it."

He'd put all four names on it. That had been a fight with the Bureau, and he'd won it, and it had cost him more than the frame had.

Bram spoke first, from beside the fire, in the tone of a man discussing the weather. "I didn't come this far to map the easy part."

"I came for the fish," Tess said. "Also the part where we nearly die and then don't. That's my favorite part."

"I came," Maren said quietly, "because forty miles of blank map is forty miles of my father waiting to be finished. And because the last time we went up a river together, we came out with the truth. I've gotten greedy for that feeling."

Silas looked at them — the ex-constable with the crooked-healing shoulder, the girl who'd been a stowaway and was now the best hand on his boat, the cartographer who'd turned grief into a weapon and then into a map — and felt the arithmetic settle again, level and quiet.

"Then we go up," he said.

* * *

They ran the Merrow for six days, and the river behaved the way good rivers do, which is to say it tried to kill them in new and inventive ways and was thwarted each time by a crew that had learned its trade on worse water.

Tess read the shallows like print. Maren charted as they went, her pencil moving in strokes that Silas was beginning to recognize as her father's, though her own hand was steadier now. Bram stood watch on the bad reaches with the rifle slung and his eyes working the banks, an old habit he'd stopped apologizing for. And Silas steered, and taught — knots to Tess in the evenings, reading water to Maren, the proper way to fall asleep sitting up to Bram, who claimed he'd known it all along and was only humoring him.

On the seventh day they heard the Weeping Stair before they saw it: a sound like the whole valley sighing, layered and long.

They pulled to the bank below it and made camp in the blue hour, and Silas climbed the bluff alone to look at what the morning would ask of them.

The waterfall came down in five great steps, white water stitching the dark rock, and there was no portage — no trail, no cut brush, nothing. Forty miles of blank map above it, and the only road was the one they'd have to make.

He stood there a while, the spray drifting over him, and did not look back downstream. There was nothing down there that needed him.

Behind him, boots on the scree. Tess, scrambling up the bluff with the fearlessness of the young and the sure-footedness he'd drilled into her until she out-climbed him. She came and stood at his shoulder and looked at the Stair, and whistled, low.

"That's a lot of waterfall, Skipper."

"It is."

"Portage route?"

"We make one. Tomorrow. You, me, and the machetes." He glanced at her. "Nervous?"

Tess considered it honestly, the way she considered everything — a year ago she'd been a girl who lied about her age and hid in cargo crates, and now she was a girl who looked at a five-step waterfall and did arithmetic instead of flinching.

"A little," she said. "Good nervous, though. The kind where you know it matters." She looked up at him, and there was something in her face of the woman she was going to be, and something of the child she'd been, and both of them were grinning. "Maren says there might be a lake above the Stair that nobody's ever mapped. An actual blank lake. She's been talking about it for two days straight. Bram's threatening to sleep in the boat."

"He'd have to wake up first."

They stood together a moment longer, the four of them below in the firelight — Maren bent over her charts, Bram pretending not to nap — and Silas Dunmore, blacklisted man, freight hauler, survivor of the Vane Glacier and the Kestrel and his own worst company, felt something in his chest that had no name he knew, and didn't need one.

"Come on," he said. "Morning's going to come whether we're ready or not. Might as well be ready."

They climbed down to the fire together, and above them the Weeping Stair went on weeping, and above *that*, forty miles of unmapped river waited in the dark like a page that hadn't been written on yet.

Silas slept well. In the morning, they went up.
