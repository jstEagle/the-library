## 1. The Silence at Kilometer Forty

The rain came up the valley the way it always did in the wet season — not falling so much as arriving, a gray wall of it walking upstream against the current of the Vell, swallowing the far bank first and then the near one, until Ivo Thornbury's whole world was the width of his relay hut and the sound of water on a tin roof like gravel poured over a drum.

He had learned to love that sound, or to pretend he had. Twenty years is long enough to make a lie feel like furniture.

Inside the hut, the radio equipment hummed its small patient hum: the transmitter with its amber dial-light, the receiver tuned to the expedition band, the backup generator sulking in the lean-to because the primary had flooded again that morning and Ivo had spent two hours waist-deep in the culvert with a wrench, cursing in three languages, feeling every one of his fifty-eight years arrive at once through his knees. The contract said *maintain relay integrity, Kilometer Forty station, Vell River corridor, duration ninety days.* It did not say anything about culverts, or about the particular loneliness of being the only fixed point between the lowland towns and whatever waited upriver past the gorge.

It did not say anything about the Cartwright expedition, either, though everyone knew that was why the trust wanted the line kept open. Ninety days of silence from Kilometer Forty and the whole enterprise went deaf.

Ivo poured himself tea from the dented kettle and sat down at the set with the ease of long habit. Log first. He wrote in the ledger with a carpenter's pencil, block letters gone slightly ragged since the tremor started in his left hand two winters ago:

*Day 41. Rain heavy since dawn. Primary gen down 0600, restored 0815. Traffic: none. Signal check scheduled 1900.*

Traffic: none. That was the trouble with relay work, he thought. You were paid for other people's voices. Forty-one days of them, mostly — the Cartwright party checked in twice daily, morning and evening, their signal bouncing off the repeater mast above the falls and landing in Ivo's hut clean as a coin dropped in a dish. He had come to know them by their voices alone, the way you come to know neighbors through a wall.

There was Cartwright himself, the expedition leader, whose transmissions were models of bureaucratic calm — *position confirmed, all personnel accounted for, river gauge reading normal* — delivered in a voice like a man reading his own obituary and finding it acceptable. There was the ethnographer, Dr. Osei, who always signed off with a word or two in a language Ivo didn't recognize but which made something in his chest tighten anyway, some old guilt stirring like a fish under ice. There was the photographer, Lenn, young and breathless, forever reporting light conditions as though the sky were a personal acquaintance. And there was the security chief, Dekker, whose voice Ivo disliked on principle — too smooth, too controlled, a man who pronounced every word as if it had been ironed.

Forty-one days of voices. He could have drawn a map of them.

He drank his tea and watched the rain and did not think about the gorge, twenty kilometers further up, where the river narrowed to a throat and the maps in the government archive went politely blank. He was very good at not thinking about the gorge. He'd had practice.

At seven minutes past seven in the evening, the receiver crackled.

Ivo set down his cup. Evening check-in. He reached for the log pencil out of habit, then paused, because something was wrong with the sound before any words resolved — a wrongness in the texture of the static itself, a stutter in it, like a heartbeat skipping. He leaned forward and turned the gain up with two fingers, careful, the way you approach a bird you don't want to startle.

*—Kilometer Forty, do you copy, Kilometer—*

Cartwright's voice. But stripped of its calm. The bureaucratic varnish was gone and underneath it was something raw, and Ivo felt the hair rise on his forearms before his mind had even finished translating, the body knowing fear's accent before the ear does.

*—repeat, we have a situation at the vault site, I say again, situation at—* A burst of static tore the sentence open. When the voice came back it was lower, faster, half-swallowed. *—Dekker has sealed the lower camp, he's got men at the boats, we can't— Amara says they're going to burn the—*

Another tear of static, longer this time. Ivo's hand hovered over the transmit key. Protocol said the relay station did not intervene; protocol said he acknowledged, logged, and passed traffic upriver or down. His finger rested on the key anyway, trembling slightly, an old instinct waking up in him like a dog too old for hunting still lifting its head at a gunshot.

*—listen to me, whoever's on that relay—* Now it wasn't Cartwright. The voice was younger, ragged, close to the microphone as if the speaker were cupping his hands around it, hiding. Lenn, the photographer. *—if anyone hears this, they took the porters' boats, they took everything, we're going up toward the falls, do you understand, we're going UP—* A sound behind the voice, distant but distinct even through the static: a shot, flat and small, the way gunfire sounds when it's real and not a film. Then shouting, several voices tangled together, and someone — a woman, he thought, though he couldn't be sure — crying out a word in that language he didn't know.

Then nothing. Not silence exactly — the carrier wave held for another few seconds, breathing, alive, a room where people had just been — and then it collapsed into ordinary static, the indifferent hiss of a frequency with no one on it.

Ivo sat very still.

The rain went on hammering the roof. The amber dial-light pulsed. Somewhere under the lean-to the backup generator chugged its idiot rhythm, unaware that anything in the world had changed.

He keyed the microphone. "Kilometer Forty to Cartwright expedition." His own voice sounded strange to him, rusty, too loud in the little hut. "Kilometer Forty calling. I copy your last transmission. Say again your position. Say again your situation." He released the key and listened.

Static.

"Kilometer Forty to Cartwright. Come back."

Static, and the rain, and the generator.

He tried for an hour. He tried on the emergency band and the secondary band and the old survey frequencies he wasn't supposed to remember, the ones from twenty years ago, and on one of those — only on one — he caught something, just once, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well: a voice, or the ghost of a voice, saying what might have been *—under the water—* before it dissolved.

Under the water. Under the waterfall. Or nothing at all. A man's ears will make words out of weather if he wants them badly enough.

* * *

He did not sleep. He told himself this was procedure: a lost-contact event required continuous monitoring, hourly attempts on all bands, documentation of every anomaly. Procedure was a comfort. Procedure was a wall you could put your shoulder against while the thing behind it scratched.

By midnight the rain had moved off downstream, taking its gray wall with it, and the clouds shredded enough to let down a few indifferent stars. Ivo stood outside the hut with his coat collar up and looked upriver, where the Vell ran black and swollen between banks he couldn't see, toward the gorge he couldn't see, toward the falls he had seen once, twenty years ago, in circumstances the tribunal records described in careful neutral language: *survey activities conducted without full disclosure of intended use of cartographic materials.*

Without full disclosure. As if the villages had been erased by a clerical error. As if the maps he drew hadn't been used to draw lines through living country, lines that became roads, roads that became evictions, evictions that became a people with no name on any chart. He had signed his name to those surveys. Ivo Thornbury, field cartographer, competent, thorough, recommended for commendation. Then the burning started, and he had run — not to stop it, not to testify, just run, downriver and away, and the blacklist had followed him like a smell.

And now, twenty years later, the river had sent him a voice cut off mid-word, and the word, he was almost certain, had been *burn.*

He went back inside and pulled the contract folder from the drawer beneath the bunk. Trust letterhead, the Cartwright Geographic Trust, and there in the list of sponsors, printed small and respectable: *with additional support from the Vane Foundation for Historical Preservation.* Vane. He'd heard the name whispered at the trading posts, attached always to acquisitions, to archives, to things bought and quietly shelved. He'd thought nothing of it forty-one days ago. Names on letterhead were weather to him now.

He read the whole contract twice, looking for the clause that would tell him what he was allowed to do, and found none, because there wasn't one, because contracts are written for situations that have already been imagined, and no one had imagined this.

At 3 a.m., on the secondary band, the carrier wave flickered again. Ivo was across the hut before it finished flickering, headphones clamped on, heart going like a boy's.

This time there were no voices. Just a rhythm in the static — deliberate, spaced, unmistakable to a man who'd spent his life learning languages made of marks and lines. Three short. Three long. Three short.

SOS, tapped out weakly, from somewhere upriver past the gorge.

Then it stopped, and did not come again, and Ivo Thornbury sat in the amber light with his hands flat on the desk, and understood that the choice he had spent twenty years avoiding had finally come up the river to find him.

Protocol said: log the anomaly, notify the trust office downriver, wait for instructions.

Procedure was a wall. But walls, he knew better than most men, are just lines on a map, and lines are drawn by somebody, and somebody can decide to draw a different one.

He opened the ledger and wrote, in his ragged block letters:

*Day 41, continued. Contact lost with Cartwright expedition, approx. 1910 hrs. Final transmission incomplete. Distress signal received 0300 hrs, origin estimated beyond gorge, Kilometer Sixty or above. Requesting immediate guidance from trust office.*

He looked at the words a long time. Then, beneath them, smaller, in the space where a responsible man would leave the page blank for the office's reply, he added:

*Am proceeding upriver at first light.*

He blew out the lamp. Outside, the Vell ran black and fast toward the gorge, carrying the rain down from the high country, carrying everything down except, apparently, him.

* * *

First light came late and reluctant, the sky the color of dishwater, the river running high with silt the brown of strong tea. Ivo loaded the skiff by lamplight: fuel, the spare radio set, rope, rations, the medical kit he'd never once needed in four seasons on this stretch. Last of all, from the bottom of his footlocker, wrapped in oilcloth, a battered leather case he had not opened in twenty years — his old field notebooks, the ones from the gorge survey, the ones he should have burned and hadn't, because some part of him had known even then that a day would come when the only honest map of that country would be the one in a disgraced man's handwriting.

He pushed off from the stake at Kilometer Forty as the mist lifted off the water, and the current took the bow and swung it upstream, and Ivo Thornbury — blacklisted, half-retired, fifty-eight years old with a tremor in his left hand — bent to the oars against the Vell, rowing toward the place with no name, toward the silence at Kilometer Forty and everything waiting beyond it.

Behind him, the relay hut shrank into the mist, its antenna a thin dark stitch holding shut a seam in the world.

Ahead, faint through the fog, the river made the sound of water falling from a great height, and did not stop making it, all the way to the horizon.
