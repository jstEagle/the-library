## 2. A Daughter with Better Maps

The telegram arrived at the Cartwright Trust offices at eleven minutes past four on a Tuesday, and by half past four everyone in the drafting room had read it twice and said nothing once.

SENDER: VELL RIVER RELAY STATION, KM 40. CARTWRIGHT EXPEDITION OFF AIR 61 HOURS. LAST TRANSMISSION GARBLED. REQUESTING INSTRUCTION.

Sebastian Thornbury stood at her table with a ruling pen still wet in her hand, a line half-drawn across the survey sheet of the lower Vell basin — a line that now seemed suddenly, obscenely trivial, the way all careful work looks trivial the moment the world remembers it is not made of paper. The ink bled a small black star into the vellum where her hand had stopped moving.

Sixty-one hours. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Sixty-one hours of silence on a river where silence was itself a kind of message. The Cartwright expedition had gone up the Vell with thirty-one souls, four tons of supplies, a radio schedule of twice daily contact, and — she knew this because she had drawn the supply manifests herself, in her small neat hand, in the corner office they gave to junior cartographers with unpronounceable surnames and no family name worth mentioning — a security contingent whose payroll she had been told never to itemize.

"Thornbury." Mr. Halloway, the chief cartographer, stood in the doorway with the telegram in his fist as though it were a bird he'd caught. "You know the upper Vell."

"I've drawn it," she said.

"I mean *know* it. Been there."

"No one who's drawn it has been there," she said. "That's rather the point of drawing it."

Someone near the window made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough. Halloway's jaw worked. He was a fair man, mostly, in the way institutions are fair — evenly, indifferently, like weather. "There's a rescue party forming. Volunteers. It'll be led by someone with river experience, and God knows where we'll find—"

"I'll go," Sebastian said.

It came out of her before she had decided anything, the way a stone comes out of a sling — already flying, already aimed, the decision living entirely in the wrist. She heard herself say it again, more quietly, the way you repeat your own name in the dark to be sure of it. "I'll volunteer. I know the route notes better than anyone in this building. Better than whoever drew them."

Halloway looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him arrive at the thought she had been refusing to arrive at herself for twenty years: that the route notes he meant were signed *I. Thornbury*, and that the girl standing at the drafting table with ink on her fingers was the daughter of the man whose name had been struck from every professional register in three provinces.

"You're sure," he said. Not a question. A door held open.

"I'm sure," said Sebastian, and went back to her line, because if she looked up she would have to let him see what was in her face, and it was not grief, whatever anyone might have called it. It was something harder and less forgivable.

* * *

She did not go home that evening. Home was a rented room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and river damp, a room with one window facing away from the water, chosen deliberately, though she'd have denied the deliberateness if asked. Instead she stayed in the archive.

The Cartwright Trust kept its records in the basement of the old customs house, in long iron drawers that breathed cold air when you pulled them, like the building exhaling everything it would rather forget. Sebastian signed the access ledger at seven o'clock. The clerk on duty — a stooped man named Pell who had been old when she was born — raised his eyebrows at the hour but asked nothing. Archives after dark were not forbidden; they were merely never wanted, which amounted to the same thing.

Route notes, upper Vell corridor, 1904–1906. Drawer forty-one. She knew the drawer number the way other people knew their mother's birthday.

The folder came out heavy, tied with government twine gone soft and grey as hair. Inside: forty-one sheets of survey paper, field notebooks, pencil tracings, a set of triangulation tables annotated in a hand she had spent two decades learning to hate — a strong, slanted, impatient hand, letters leaning forward like men walking into wind.

Her father's hand.

She untied the twine and laid the first sheet flat under the green-shaded lamp, and told herself she was doing this for the expedition, for Dr. Osei and the photographer Lenn and the porters whose names appeared only as cargo weights in the manifests, for Cartwright himself with his silver beard and his voice like a benevolent foghorn. She told herself she was doing it for the thirty-one souls. She believed it about sixty percent, which she supposed was a respectable margin of error for any map.

* * *

The notes were good.

That was the unbearable thing, the thing she had come down into the dark prepared to dispute and could not. They were *good*. Ivo Thornbury had read that river the way scholars read scripture — every rapid annotated with its temper, every bend described not just in bearing and distance but in character. *The narrows below Kessel Rock lie. The channel looks east; it runs west. Trust the sound of the water, not the eye.* And beneath, in smaller script, almost apologetic: *Confirmed twice. Do not confirm a third time.*

He had been funny, once. That was worse than anything.

Sebastian turned the pages and let the lamp hum and built the river in her head, reach by reach, the way she built every map: first the skeleton of bearings, then the muscle of contour, then the skin of detail — the alder thickets, the shingle bars, the fog holes where cold air pooled at dawn like spilled milk. Kilometer by kilometer she walked the upper Vell in the lamplight, following a man's handwriting through country she had never seen, hating how well he wrote, hating how much she trusted him, hating most of all that trust, which arrived in her chest fully formed and unwelcome, like a bird flying into a closed room.

Because here was the thing the drafting room didn't discuss, the thing that lived under her career like a fault line under a city: when Sebastian Thornbury had finished her certification — top of her cohort, first honors in projection theory, a thesis on indigenous survey methods that two examiners called brilliant and one called *politically inadvisable* — three mapping houses had withdrawn offers within a single week. No reason given. One clerk, younger and less careful than the rest, had let it slip over a drink: *Thornbury. Any relation? Ah. Right.* The name operated at a distance of twenty years and four hundred miles, quiet as radiation. Her father had not merely disgraced himself. He had salted the ground behind him, and she had spent her whole adult life trying to grow something in it.

And now his maps were the best thing in the building.

She sat back. The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere above her, the customs house settled and ticked, and out on the river the fog was coming up the harbor the way it always did, erasing the world in slow white strokes, and thirty-one people were somewhere up inside that whiteness, or had ceased to be anywhere at all.

*They took documents,* she remembered suddenly — a detail from the garbled final transmission, a fragment the relay operator had transcribed before the signal dissolved: *—found it, the room, they're taking the docu—* and then static, and then nothing. Everyone in the office had assumed the sentence ended in disaster. Sebastian, turning page after page of her father's field notes, found herself assuming it ended in theft. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

* * *

Toward midnight she stopped reading his notes and started correcting them.

Not from arrogance — or not only. Twenty years of hydrological surveys lay between his last ascent and tonight. The river had moved; rivers do. The great cutbank below the second gorge had migrated a full chain eastward according to the 1911 survey. The portage trail around Widow's Falls had been rerouted when the trading company built its new landing. She took fresh tracing paper and overlaid it on his sheets, and in her own hand — upright, precise, a hand that leaned on nothing — she marked every correction, every update, every place where the world had disagreed with Ivo Thornbury and won.

It should have felt like victory. It felt like conversation.

*Your river has changed,* she wrote in the margins, in effect. *Here, and here, and here. You are twenty years out of date. So am I. So is everyone.*

By two in the morning she had assembled a working packet: his original route notes, her overlays, a compiled distance-and-time table estimating the rescue party's progress against the expedition's last known position at kilometer one hundred twelve. At their best pace — and rescue parties never traveled at their best pace — they would need nine days to reach the last transmission point. Nine days during which anything could happen to anyone, and probably would.

She was squaring the corners of the packet when she saw it: tucked inside the back cover of the third field notebook, a loose sheet she hadn't noticed, thinner than the others, older. A sketch, done fast, in the same slanted hand — but softer, the lines less certain. A woman's face, seen in profile, looking out a window. Below it, in pencil so light it was almost a wish:

*S. asleep. Tell her when she's older.*

Sebastian put the sheet down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

She had been told, eventually. She had been ten years old, standing in a neighbor's kitchen while an aunt explained in a lowered voice that her father would not be coming back, that there had been an inquiry, that names had been crossed off lists. She had understood almost none of it and all of it at once, the way children understand catastrophes — completely and without context. What she had carried out of that kitchen was not the scandal. It was the arithmetic of abandonment: he left, therefore I was leavable. Everything since — the scholarships pursued out of spite, the certifications earned like weapons, the name *Thornbury* worn openly when she could have hidden it, precisely because hiding it would have conceded something — everything had been built on that single subtraction.

And now here was his handwriting again, saying *tell her when she's older*, and she was older, and he had never told her anything, and the sheet trembled very slightly in the lamplight because her hand was trembling, and she hated that too, catalogued it, filed it away with all the other things she refused to feel until a more convenient season.

*Tell me now,* she thought at the dead man's handwriting, at the living man presumably still alive somewhere south of the river towns, drinking his disgrace in some dockside room. *Tell me why. Tell me what you found up there that was worth my whole life.*

Then she folded the sketch away, squared the packet, and got back to work, because the work at least answered when spoken to.

* * *

Dawn found the fog lifting off the harbor in rags, and Sebastian climbing the stairs from the archive with red eyes, ink-stained fingers, and a packet under her arm that weighed less than a rifle and more than most rifles ever fired.

Pell the clerk was lighting the stove. He looked at her, then at the ledger, then at the packet, and something moved across his old face — recognition, maybe, or memory. "Thornbury," he said. "Like the route notes."

"Better than the route notes," Sebastian said.

He nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something he'd suspected for twenty years. "Drawer forty-one gets pulled twice in one lifetime," he said. "That's more than most drawers get."

Outside, the city was waking into pale gold light, gulls arguing over the fish market, the river running brown and patient toward the sea. Somewhere upstream, past the rapids and the gorges and the territory the porters wouldn't name, the Cartwright expedition's silence continued, vast and specific, a blank space on the map of the world exactly the size of thirty-one lives.

Sebastian stood on the customs house steps and looked north, the way she had looked north her entire childhood from a different window in a different town, waiting for a figure on the road.

This time she wasn't going to wait. This time she was going up the river herself, with his maps corrected in her own hand, and she would find what the silence was hiding — the survivors, or the bodies, or the truth, or all three — and she would bring it home if she had to carry it on her back.

A boy was running along the quay with a bundle of morning papers, shouting the headline before he could read it: *CARTWRIGHT TRUST NAMES RESCUE LEADER —*

Sebastian bought a paper from him with coins still warm from her pocket, unfolded it against the wind, and read the name printed under the announcement in bold black type.

The paper rattled in her hands. The gulls screamed. Upstream, the fog was burning off the water mile by mile, revealing the river all the way to the horizon — and standing at the center of that horizon, in ink, in print, in fact, was the one name in the world she had sailed four hundred miles and twenty years to escape.

*Ivo Thornbury to lead Vell River rescue.*
