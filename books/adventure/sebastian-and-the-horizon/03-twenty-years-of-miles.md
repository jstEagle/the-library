## 3. Twenty Years of Miles

The muster yard behind the Vell River Company's dock office smelled of tar and wet rope and the particular rot of a town that lived off other people's departures. Ivo Thornbury arrived an hour early because he had nowhere else to be, and stood at the edge of the cobbles with his duffel over one shoulder, watching the rescue boat take on cargo.

She was called the *Meridian Bell*, a shallow-draft sternwheeler with a patched paddle housing and a crane arm for hauling supplies over the shallows. Not the boat he would have chosen. But the river above Kilometer Forty didn't care what you chose; it only cared whether you could read it.

He counted the crew as they assembled. A pilot named Osgood, heavy through the shoulders, hands like knotted rope — Ivo remembered him from the old days, before the blacklist, when Osgood still ran freight as far as the gorge. Two riggers, young, loud, already arguing about who'd get the forward bunk. A company man in a gray coat whose boots were too clean for the river, which meant he was either a clerk or something worse. And a woman in her fifties with a field medic's satchel and the unhurried eyes of someone who had seen enough blood to stop being impressed by it. Dr. Ferro, the manifest said. Good. Someone should know how to keep people alive past the point where the river stopped helping.

Six souls, plus himself. Seven going up into country that had swallowed eleven and gone quiet.

Ivo set down his duffel and began doing what he always did before a journey: reading the water. The Vell ran high with snowmelt, brown-green and muscular, carrying whole trees on its back like driftwood offerings. Fog sat on the far bank in long white ropes. Somewhere upriver, past the trading post, past the gorge with no name that no map bothered naming, the Cartwright expedition had spoken its last word into a radio and been answered by static.

He was measuring the current against a mooring piling — counting the seconds between eddies, the way you did to learn a river's temper — when the dockside gate opened and a figure came through it carrying a map case.

He knew the walk before he knew the face. He knew the walk from twenty years ago, from a girl chasing gulls on a shingle beach, from a teenager stomping ahead of him up a trail because she refused to be carried and refused to be left behind and refused, most of all, to look back. The walk hadn't changed. Only everything around it had.

Sebastian Thornbury crossed the cobbles in a surveyor's coat with the trust's brass buttons, a junior cartographer's insignia at the collar, and stopped three meters away — close enough to speak, far enough to run.

"You're not on my manifest," she said.

"Your manifest," Ivo said. His voice came out lower than he intended, scraped by disuse and river wind.

"The rescue party. Signed by the Cartwright Trust. Guide, one, experienced, *available*." She said the last word like she was spitting out a pit. "They must have been desperate."

"They were."

"And you said yes."

"I did."

She looked at him then, fully, and Ivo felt the look go through him like a sounding line dropped into black water. Twenty-two years old now. Her mother's jaw, her mother's stubborn set of the mouth, and his own gray eyes looking back at him out of her face, which was somehow the cruelest thing about it — that every time she looked at him she had to see him too.

"They told me they'd hired a local pilot," she said. "They told me the guide was someone from the upper settlements. Nobody said your name. I checked the roster twice."

"Maybe they were afraid you'd refuse to come."

"Maybe they were right."

* * *

Sebastian had spent the night before with his route notes spread across her bunk like evidence at a trial.

The trust had them archived — Ivo Thornbury's original survey journals from the Vell headwaters expedition, twenty years old, catalogued under a restricted seal that she'd had to sign three forms to break. Everyone in the cartography division knew the name. It was a cautionary tale told to first-year draftsmen: *this is what happens when a guide falsifies terrain*. Except Sebastian had read the actual notes now, all four hundred pages of them, and there was nothing falsified in them. There was nothing in them at all except the most precise hand-drawn soundings she had ever seen, contour lines laid down with a patience that bordered on devotion, marginalia in a small cramped script noting things no one else thought to note — where the fog pooled, where the porters sang, where the water changed taste.

Her father had been a better mapper than anyone currently employed by the trust, and the trust had erased him, and she had built her entire career on the erasure without ever once asking what lay underneath it.

She hated him for that. She hated him more neatly, more efficiently, than she had ever managed in person, because the notes gave her so much material. Every beautiful page was another count in the indictment. And then she had packed the notes in oilcloth, because she was not a fool, and because whatever else he was, he was the only person alive who had taken a boat past Kilometer Forty and brought it back.

Now he stood on the dock in a canvas jacket gone soft with age, grayer than the photographs, thinner, with a scar she didn't remember cutting down through his left eyebrow, and he was looking at her the way he used to look at weather coming in — assessing, bracing, saying nothing.

"Twenty years," she said. "You could have written."

"I wrote."

"Once. When I was twelve. A forwarding address and two lines about the weather." She had memorized both lines. She despised herself for having memorized them. *"Rivers here run clear until the rains. Hope you're well."* Hope you're well. As if she were a distant cousin. As if he were asking after her health the way you asked after a neighbor's.

"It was more than you sent," he said quietly.

"I was twelve."

"When I left, you were two."

And there it was — the thing they never said, dragged up into the light between crates of hardtack and coils of rope, in front of the riggers who had gone suddenly silent and the doctor who was watching with clinical interest and the gray-coated company man who was watching with a different kind entirely.

"You chose the river," Sebastian said. "Everyone says it politely — the work took him, the mountains took him, poor Odile raising the child alone. But it was a choice. You signed the contract. You boarded the boat. You knew the survey was going into country nobody comes back from unchanged, and you went anyway, and when it went wrong you didn't come home. You let them make you a ghost rather than come home and explain yourself."

"Explain." He laughed, or something near it, without any humor in it. "You want the explanation? Here it is. I mapped that gorge for men who used my maps to move villages. Whole villages, Sebastian. Families that fed me, that taught me the crossings, that sang their children to sleep in houses I put coordinates on. By the time I understood what the maps were for, the ink was dry and the trucks were rolling. So yes. I stayed away. Because the last thing those people needed was the man who drew their destruction coming back to be forgiven."

"That's not an explanation. That's a eulogy. For them, fine — mourn them. But I wasn't a village. I was your daughter, and I stayed, and you never—"

"You stayed," he said, "because your mother kept you. Because she was strong enough to raise you alone and proud enough never to send me begging letters, and I thanked God for her every single day of those twenty years. Don't stand on this dock and tell me about staying. Staying was the easy half."

The words landed harder than he meant them to. He saw it hit her — saw her chin lift, the way it did when she was hurt and converting the hurt to armor in real time.

"The easy half," she repeated.

"Sebastian—"

"No. Say it again. Tell me which half was easy. Waking up in a house where your chair was empty. Watching Mother sell her piano to pay the tribunal fines for crimes you committed. Being fifteen years old and hearing my surname whispered in the drafting hall like a disease." Her voice didn't rise; that was the terrible part. It got quieter and sharper, like wire. "You think staying was easy? Staying was the *hard* half. Leaving is one decision made once. Staying is deciding every single day."

Osgood cleared his throat from the gangway. "We cast off in forty minutes," he said to no one in particular, to the river, to the fog. "Weather won't wait and neither will the dead."

Neither of them moved.

* * *

Ivo watched his daughter turn away and start checking the cargo manifests against the loaded crates, because that was what she did instead of weeping — she worked. He recognized that too. He'd invented it.

He picked up his duffel and followed her down the line at a distance, cataloguing what she'd become. She stowed the map cases high and dry without being told. She re-rigged a crate sling that the rigger had tied wrong, and she did it with a quickness that made the young man flush and say nothing. She had his hands. That was the joke the world had played on him: he had given her nothing but the hands, and the hands were the best part.

Dr. Ferro appeared at his elbow, unwrapping a strip of dried meat with the calm of a woman boarding a ferry and not a boat bound for a killing ground. "Thornbury," she said. "That's the daughter?"

"Yes."

"She signed on to spite you or to save you?"

"Does it matter?"

Ferro chewed, considering the river. "On the water it matters enormously. Spite keeps a person moving. Saving keeps a person careful. You'll want to know which one you've got before the rapids, not after." She walked off toward the bow, leaving him with the observation lodged in him like a splinter.

The gray-coated company man approached next, introducing himself as Mr. Halloway, representing the interests of the expedition's financier, and Ivo shook the offered hand and noted that Halloway's palm was dry and that his eyes went, twice, to the map cases Sebastian had stowed, and once, longer, to Ivo himself. Memorizing faces. Men who memorized faces were usually planning to report on them, or replace them.

"Mr. Thornbury," Halloway said pleasantly. "Your reputation precedes you."

"My reputation is why you couldn't find anyone else," Ivo said. "Let's not dress it up."

Halloway smiled the way lawyers smile and drifted aft, and Ivo stood alone amid the cargo and felt the old dread settle over him, familiar as an old coat. Not fear of the river. Fear of the arithmetic: seven people going up, and somewhere above the fog, eleven who had stopped answering, and hired men — he was nearly certain now, from the cache reports and the cut telegraph line — hired men moving faster than they were.

* * *

Sebastian found the last crate unlisted and unlabeled, lashed near the stern, and pried one corner of its tarp loose with her thumb. Rope. Climbing rope, hundred-meter coils, carabiners, pitons — mountain gear, on a riverboat. She looked up and found her father watching her from ten feet away, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"There's no climbing on the Vell above Forty," she said. "It's all water and gorge floor. Your own notes say so."

"My notes are twenty years old," Ivo said. "And the men who wrote the border treaty wanted everyone believing there was only one way into that gorge. There isn't. There never was." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes — the same expression she'd seen in the archive photographs of him, young and sunburned, standing at the rail of a different boat. "That's why they need me. And it may be why somebody needs you to stay exactly on the charts."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," he said, "that if I'm wrong about the rope, you'll want to have copied your maps onto waterproof paper before we pass the trading post. Tonight. Before anyone sees you do it."

He turned and went forward to argue with Osgood about the paddle housing, and Sebastian stood in the shadow of the stacked crates with her heart going hard and strange, hating him, needing him, unable to separate the two currents any longer than she could separate the Vell from its mud.

Above the fog, upriver, the gorge waited with its stolen name. And somewhere inside it, she was beginning to understand, waited the answer to a question larger than *why didn't you come back* — a question about what else the maps had been hiding, all these years, in plain sight.
