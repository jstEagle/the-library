## 4. Upriver

The fog came off the Vell before dawn, the way it always did in the lower reaches — a slow white exhalation off water that had traveled a thousand miles and remembered all of them. Sebastian stood at the bow of the *Meridian's Mercy* with her chart case strapped across her chest like a bandolier and watched the river erase itself.

She could not see the far bank. She could not see the near bank, really — only the suggestion of it, dark vegetation dissolving into mist, and somewhere above it all a sky the color of unbleached linen. The engine thudded beneath her feet, patient as a heartbeat. Somewhere behind her, on the aft deck, her father was already awake. She knew it without turning around. She had heard him come up the gangway at four in the morning, boots quiet but not quiet enough, and she had lain in her bunk cataloguing every sound he made the way she used to when she was nine years old and he was leaving for another contract: the creak of the third stair, the pause at her door that lasted two seconds too long or not long enough, the door of his room closing soft as an apology.

Twenty years later, nothing about him had changed except the distance.

"Miss Thornbury." It was Okonjo, the pilot, a broad man with hands like mooring rope and a voice pitched to carry over rapids. He nodded at the fog ahead. "Your father says we hold here until first light burns it off."

"My father," Sebastian said, "is working from charts that are twenty years out of date."

Okonjo's eyebrows rose, just slightly. Not offense — interest. "And yours aren't?"

Sebastian unlatched her case and drew out the sheet she'd spent half the night redrawing by lamplight: the lower Vell, from Kilometer Forty to the gorge, corrected against the Cartwright trust's most recent hydrographic surveys, against the trading-post soundings she'd bought for three days' wages, against her own memory of every account ever written about this river. She spread it on the chart table beneath the deck lamp. The ink was still faintly tacky.

"The channel shifted after the flood year," she said. "The old survey has the deep water hugging the eastern bank below the second bend. It doesn't anymore. There's a gravel bar there now that sits maybe forty centimeters under the surface at low water. If we follow his line, we'll open our hull like a letter."

Okonjo studied the chart for a long moment. His finger traced the new channel she'd drawn, the careful dotted corrections, the small precise annotations in her cramped cartographer's hand. *Bar, shifting. Sound again in dry season.* *Deadfall reported 2019, likely gone; verify.* *Village of Tsembe relocated — see note.*

"You made this last night?"

"I couldn't sleep."

He looked up at her then, and something passed between them that she would think about later, much later — the look of a man deciding to trust a stranger's map with his boat and everyone aboard it. "I'll tell your father we're holding until light," he said. "For the fog. Not for his charts."

* * *

The fog burned off slowly, reluctantly, the way ice leaves a window. By seven they were moving, and Sebastian learned what it meant that Ivo Thornbury read the river.

He stood at the pilot's rail with one hand resting on the wood, not gripping it, just touching, and he called the water the way other men called birds. To her untrained eye the Vell was a brown-green ribbon of noise and confusion, standing waves and boils and long smooth tongues of current that all looked identical. To him it was text. He read the V-lines of the riffles and named the depth beneath them. He saw a seam of darker water sliding along the bank and said, "Eddy fence — stay outside it or you'll fight it all morning." He watched a single leaf ride the current past the bow and adjusted Okonjo's course by two degrees without explaining why, and Okonjo, who had piloted this river for eleven years, adjusted.

"He's not even looking at the chart," murmured Reyes, the expedition medic, beside her. She was a compact woman with sun-cracked lips and a habit of speaking in a low register, as if everything might be overheard by the river itself. "Is he? He's just — looking."

"He grew up on water like this," Sebastian said. It came out flatter than she intended. "Before the maps. Before any of it."

"And you didn't?"

"I grew up on the maps." She heard how it sounded and didn't take it back. It was true, and its truth had a shape she'd been carrying since she was old enough to understand that her father's absence had a paper trail: contracts, expeditions, a name blackened so thoroughly that no survey office in three provinces would hire anyone who shared it. She had grown up on maps because maps were where he lived when he wasn't home, and if she could master the thing that took him away, then — what? Then what, little Sebastian? She still didn't know. Maybe there had never been a *then*. Maybe she'd simply become excellent at the weapon closest to hand.

Ahead, her father raised one hand — flat, fingers together — and Okonjo eased the throttle back so smoothly the wake died mid-ripple.

"What is it?" Reyes asked.

Ivo didn't answer immediately. He tilted his head, listening, and Sebastian realized he was reading with his ears now. Beneath the engine's thud, beneath the river's broad constant roar, there was something else — a thinner sound, a hiss laid over the deeper noise, like a whisper inside a shout.

"Shoal water over rock," Ivo said finally. "Long bar, probably. Chart says—" he glanced down at the chart tube at his feet, the old one, the trust's official issue, "—chart says clear channel here."

"It's wrong," Sebastian said.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to say it loudly. She said it anyway.

Her father looked at her across the deck, through the spray and the morning glare, and for a moment neither of them moved. Twenty years of miles stood in the space between them. Then he said, "Show me," and it wasn't a challenge and it wasn't a surrender. It was the tone of a man asking a colleague. That was somehow worse than contempt. Contempt she had rehearsed for.

She brought her chart to the rail and held it flat against the wood while the wind tried to peel it away. Her finger found the bend, the shoal, the corrected channel threading east of it. "Flood year silted the western approach. The bar runs diagonal — you can't see it because the standing wave masks it, but you can hear it. That's your hiss. The safe line is thirty meters further east than your chart shows, and you want to enter the bend wide and cut late, not early."

Ivo studied the sheet. He read fast — she remembered that, the way his eyes used to move down a page like a man drinking — and then he read it again, slower, and she watched him find the annotations, the soundings, the little marginal notes in her handwriting that were, she realized too late, in places almost identical in phrasing to notes she'd seen in his old route journals. She had absorbed his style the way a child absorbs a language. She hated that he would notice.

He noticed. Something crossed his face — brief, quickly mastered, gone — and then he turned to Okonjo. "Wide entry, late cut, thirty meters east of the book line. Miss Thornbury calls the distance."

"Aye," said Okonjo, and there was no irony in it at all.

* * *

They ran the bend at mid-morning, and the bar was exactly where she said it was.

You couldn't see it — she'd been right about that too — but when they came through on the corrected line, close enough that Sebastian could count the rings on a fallen tree on the eastern bank, the hissing whisper slid past on their port side, hidden under its mask of broken water, and Okonjo exhaled through his nose, a short satisfied grunt, and said something in his own language that needed no translation.

After that, things changed on the *Meridian's Mercy*, quietly, the way currents change — no announcement, just a new set. The crew began bringing her their questions. Reyes wanted to know where the reliable landing points were for a medical evacuation, if it came to that. The engineer, a taciturn man named Brandt, wanted to know which side of the gorge mouth offered sheltered anchorage. Even Dekker — the security man, broad and bland and always pleasant, always watching — asked her, with a smile, whether she thought the fog would hold past the second day.

"Fog's a river thing," she said. "It burns off earlier every mile you climb. Ask my father. He's the one who taught me that."

Dekker's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Oh, I intend to ask him plenty," he said, and moved off along the rail, and Sebastian stood a moment longer than she should have, watching him go, filing the moment away in the drawer where she kept things that didn't fit yet.

* * *

They camped that night on a shingle spit below the second gorge approach, and the river talked to itself in the dark, the way big rivers do — a low continuous argument, endless, unresolved. The crew ate beans and smoked fish around a driftwood fire, and someone produced a battered guitar with three working strings, and the music was thin and good.

Sebastian sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, correcting tomorrow's stretch of chart by lamp. This was her ritual and her armor: work, always work, work as a place to stand when there was nowhere else. She had the waterproof sheets pinned to a board across her knees and her pen moving in small sure strokes, and she was so deep in it that she didn't hear her father approach until his shadow fell across the page.

She didn't look up. "The eastern channel narrows to twelve meters at kilometer sixty-one," she said. "If you have advice, write it down. I don't take dictation after dark."

A pause. When he spoke, his voice was low, pitched under the guitar and the river. "Your sounding marks. You use the old convention. Dot-dash for verified, dot-dot for estimated. Nobody's taught that in fifteen years. They use color coding now."

"I know they do." Her pen didn't stop. "Color fades on waterproof stock. Ink doesn't. Your way survives the river better."

Another silence, longer. Out on the water, something large surfaced and sank — a fish, a log, a rumor. When Ivo spoke again, each word seemed to cost him freight. "Who taught you the convention?"

"You did." Now she looked up. Firelight moved on his face, and she made herself study it the way she'd study terrain: the gray in his beard she'd never seen arrive, the scar over his eyebrow she'd forgotten, the eyes that were hers, exactly hers, and always had been, which felt like theft. "There's a box in Mama's attic. Your route journals, the early ones. From before. You drew your own conventions in the front of the first volume, like a legend. Like a key." She looked back down at her chart. "I taught myself out of your books, Father. Everything I know about rivers, I learned from a man who wasn't there. So if my charts are good, you can thank yourself. And if they're better than yours, that's just twenty years of practice you missed."

The words landed. She watched them land. She had carried them upriver like ballast and she'd told herself she wouldn't fire them, and she had fired them, and now the smoke was between them and she couldn't take it back and discovered she didn't want to.

Ivo Thornbury stood very still. The fire cracked. Down the beach, the guitar stumbled through a chorus nobody quite knew.

"Yes," he said at last, quietly. "They're better than mine." He crouched, slowly, joints stiff with years and cold, and rested one hand on the shingle, and did not touch her chart, did not reach for her pen, occupied none of her territory. He only pointed, with a finger that trembled slightly — cold, or age, or something else — at the stretch of river she was drawing. "This bend here. In the journals, the ones you have — I marked it as a hazard. It isn't anymore. The flood cut a new channel through the point and made it the safest water on the whole upper reach." A breath. "I need you to know that I know the world changed while I was gone. I'm not going to pretend my map is still true." He rose, unhurried, an old man's economy of motion. "Goodnight, Sebastian."

He walked back toward the fire, and she sat with her pen poised over the virgin channel he had described, and she did not draw it, not for a long time. Because drawing it would mean accepting the correction. Accepting the correction would mean admitting he had read her work closely, carefully, generously — that he had done tonight what she had done all day: followed the other's lines, hunting for errors, finding instead a hand eerily like his own.

*Competence as a love language,* she thought bitterly. *In this family we don't say it. We sound it. We chart it. We correct each other's depths.*

She drew the new channel. It took her four tries to make the lines steady.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, into fog again, thinner now, burning faster. The country was changing as they climbed — the banks steepening, the trees giving way to pine and then to bare gray rock, the river gathering itself, growing louder, more urgent, as if it too knew what waited upstream and was hurrying toward it or away from it.

It was near noon, running the long straight below the gorge mouth, that the first wrongness arrived.

Not seen — heard. Or rather, not heard: a silence where sound should be. The porters' chatter from the aft deck stopped all at once, mid-sentence, like a radio cut. Sebastian looked up from her charts and saw the six hired men standing motionless along the rail, all facing forward, all looking at the same point in the fog ahead, and none of them speaking, and their faces wore an expression she had never seen on hired men before.

It was fear. Old fear. Practiced fear — the kind that comes from having been afraid of this exact thing before.

Ahead, the fog was beginning to tear apart in the wind of the gorge, and through the gap she could see the walls rising, black rock streaked white with bird lime, and the river narrowing between them with a sound like a crowd building to a roar. And on the near wall, barely visible, weathered nearly to nothing, there was a mark — a painted sign, ancient, deliberate, in a script she didn't recognize.

One of the porters began, softly, to pray.

At the pilot's rail, her father had gone rigid, one hand flat on the wood, staring at the mark on the cliff face as though it were a face he knew — as though it had spoken his name.

"We're here," she heard him say, to no one, to the river, to twenty years ago. "God help us. We're here."
