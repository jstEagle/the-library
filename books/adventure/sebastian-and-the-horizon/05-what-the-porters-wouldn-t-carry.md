## 5. What the Porters Wouldn't Carry

The trading post at Sarn's Landing was less a settlement than an argument the river had won. A dozen buildings leaned against the current's appetite on stilts of blackened ironwood, their floors warped into gentle waves, as if the whole town were perpetually bracing for a flood that had already come and gone and would come again. Drying racks hung with fish like silver coins strung on wire. A dog with one ear watched them from beneath a porch with the weary authority of a customs officer.

Ivo felt the place before he understood it — a pressure behind the sternum, the old animal sense that had kept him alive in mountains where maps went honest and men did not. Twenty years since he'd stood on this stretch of the Vell. The river had changed its course in places; the landing had not.

"Two hours," Cartwright had said before the silence took him, and so they made two hours: repack the loads, buy what could be bought, let the crew eat something hot. Dekker organized the crates with the crisp efficiency of a man who liked inventories more than people. Sebastian was already at the postmaster's counter, unrolling her charts, asking questions in the patient, precise way she asked everything — as though the world owed her answers and she intended to collect.

Ivo went looking for porters.

He found them where he expected, in the shade behind the fuel store: six men and two women sitting on overturned crates, playing cards with a deck soft as cloth from handling. They were river people, broad-shouldered and quiet, with the deep calm of those who lived by water that could kill them any afternoon and had chosen to stay anyway. Their headman was a heavyset man called Ossai, silver at his temples, hands like knotted rope.

Ivo laid out the terms simply. Upstream past the gorge, ten days' carry, good wages, hazard pay doubled. He'd done this speech a hundred times in a hundred languages of gesture and coin. He knew how it landed before it finished landing.

Ossai listened all the way through, which was courtesy. Then he shook his head once, slow, like a door closing.

"No past the gorge."

"The pay is doubled."

"I heard you." Ossai turned a card over in his thick fingers — the six of something, worn nearly blank. "Pay don't weigh anything there."

One of the younger men said something low in the river tongue. The others didn't laugh. That was the thing Ivo noticed, the thing that raised the hair along his arms: nobody laughed. In every trading post on every river he'd worked, porter crews joked about hazard the way sailors joked about drowning — lightly, constantly, to keep the fear moving through them instead of pooling. These people held their fear still, the way you hold a full cup crossing a room.

"It's a rescue," Ivo said. "People are alive up there. Maybe."

Ossai looked at him for a long moment. His eyes were the color of the river in flood season — brown carrying green, depth pretending to be mud.

"You're the one who asks," he said. Not a question. "You're not the one who goes."

"We go too."

"You go past the gorge." Ossai set the card down face-up, blank side showing. "We don't. You want to know why, ask your own government. Ask them what name they gave that country." He paused. "They didn't give it one. That's the answer."

* * *

By midday the refusal had spread through the landing like dye through water. The second crew Ivo approached wouldn't even discuss terms; a woman selling cassava told him, not unkindly, that he should buy a better map, because the one he was using clearly ended where it shouldn't. Even the postmaster — a dry, administrative man who Ivo suspected would forward mail into a war zone for the correct fee — grew evasive when Sebastian asked him to log their route past Kilometer Sixty.

"There's no kilometer sixty," the postmaster said.

Sebastian laid her chart on his counter. "There is. Surveyed 1971. Here's the benchmark."

"That survey was withdrawn."

"Withdrawn," Sebastian repeated, tasting the word. "Not corrected. Withdrawn."

The postmaster's pen hovered. He was a small man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular courage of clerks everywhere, which is to say almost none and exactly enough. "Young woman," he said quietly, not looking up, "in thirty years at this window I have processed manifests for eleven expeditions past the gorge. Seven came back short. The government sent no inquiries after any of them. You do the arithmetic of what that means, and then you decide whether my stamp goes on your paper." He stamped it anyway — but only up to Kilometer Fifty-Five, and he met her eyes when he did it, and Ivo, watching from the doorway, understood that this was the bravest act the man would perform that year, maybe that decade.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the water in long rags. Somewhere upstream, past the bend, past the rapids they hadn't run yet, the gorge waited with its missing name.

Dekker found Ivo at the rail. "We've got a problem. Nobody local will carry past Fifty-Five. We can hire boatmen to the mouth of the gorge, but after that we're hauling everything ourselves — four tons, Thornbury. Four tons on our backs."

"We'll take less."

"Cartwright's manifest says—"

"Cartwright isn't here to ask." Ivo heard his own voice come out flat and hard, and saw Dekker register it, file it away. There was something in the security chief's face when he spoke of the manifest — a smoothness, a readiness — that Ivo had been trying not to look at directly for three days, the way you don't look at the sun or at a card player's eyes. "We cut to essentials. Medical, comms, food for twelve days. The rest stays locked at the landing under the postmaster's seal."

Dekker smiled. It reached his eyes. That was somehow worse. "You're the guide."

* * *

They found replacements by accident, which was to say by Sebastian.

She'd spent the afternoon doing what she always did in strange towns — drawing. Not the official charts, but her own quick field sketches: the stilt houses, the fish racks, the angle of light on the water, faces. She drew people the way other people greeted them, as a way of making contact. By late afternoon half the children of Sarn's Landing had gathered to watch over her shoulder and critique her proportions, and through the children she'd reached the adults, and through the adults she'd found the three men who would go.

They were outsiders themselves, she explained to Ivo that evening on the porch of the guest house, where the river muttered below like a sleeper arguing with a dream. A brothers pair from two days' walk inland — Yawo and Bem — who worked the timber camps and thought the gorge stories were "town talk," and a third man, older, who had simply appeared at the edge of the group that morning and said, in accented trade-language, "I will carry past the gorge," and named a wage without blinking.

"What's his name?" Ivo asked.

"He calls himself Toma. But Ivo—" She hesitated, and in the lamplight Ivo saw his daughter's face do the thing it always did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed: her chin came up a quarter inch, armor going on. "He knows you. Or he knows *of* you. When I showed the children a sketch of the team, he looked at yours longest. And tonight he asked me — not the others, me — whether you were the same Thornbury. The Vell survey. Twenty years ago."

The lamp flame stood very still. Outside, the river kept its argument going.

"What did you tell him?"

"I said I didn't know what he meant." Her jaw tightened. "Which was a lie, because I've read your file, Papa. All of it. The tribunal record, the survey contracts, the displacement orders your contour lines got used for. I read it when I was nineteen, the night before my certification exam, because I wanted to know exactly what I was refusing to become." She said it without heat now, and that was the worst version of it — the anger burned down to coals, structural, load-bearing. "So yes. I lied to a stranger to protect your name. Write that down somewhere. It may be the last time."

She left the porch. Ivo sat with the lamp and the river and the word *Toma*, turning it over the way Ossai had turned the blank card, and found nothing written on either side.

* * *

He recognized him at dawn, on the landing, in the gray light before the sun cleared the ridge — recognized him the way you recognize a scar on your own body.

Toma stood at the end of the dock checking lashings on a supply drum, and twenty years fell off Ivo like a coat. Thinner. Gray threaded through what had been black. A burn scar on the right forearm where there had been none. But the hands were the same hands — wide, deliberate, the hands of a man who tied knots the way other men prayed — and the way he stood, weight even on both feet, facing the water as if it might say something worth hearing.

"Tomasin," Ivo said.

The man did not turn around. For a long moment the only sound was rope creaking against the cleat and the river going about its business underneath them, indifferent and enormous.

"They told me you were dead," Toma said finally. Still facing the water. "After the tribunal. Some said fled north, some said drowned. My mother lit candles either way. Dead or fled — in our country those are often the same prayer."

"I'm sorry."

"Yes." Toma finished the lashing, tested it, nodded at his own work. Then he turned, and his face was older than twenty years could account for, and utterly without cruelty, which made it harder to bear than hatred would have been. "You are sorry. Good. I am also sorry. We are both sorry, and the villages are still gone, and the maps are still wrong. Sorry is a very portable thing, Thornbury. It weighs nothing. It carries nothing." He picked up his pack strap. "Why are you going up there?"

"A rescue. An expedition went silent."

"An expedition went silent." Toma repeated it slowly, like a man testing ice. "And you think the silence began this month." He looked upstream, toward the bend, toward the gorge with no name, and something moved across his face that Ivo had seen only once before — on the face of a village elder, twenty-two years ago, the day the surveyors' flags went in along a line no one had drawn before: recognition. Grief arriving ahead of schedule. "I knew a girl from that country," Toma said. "Before. Her grandmother kept maps. Not paper — memory-maps. Songs, mostly. Where the salt roads ran, where the dead were fed, where the border wasn't." He smiled briefly, terribly. "Your survey burned the paper ones. The songs they couldn't find."

"The vault," Ivo said. The word came out of him before he decided to say it, pulled by some undertow. "If there were paper maps. Older ones. Pre-colonial. Hidden—"

Toma went very still.

Around them the landing woke: Deckhands shouting, gulls arguing over fish guts, Dekker's voice running an inventory somewhere behind the fuel store. None of it touched the space between the two men on the dock, which had gone quiet as a held breath.

"Who told you that?" Toma asked softly.

"Nobody. I'm asking."

"You are asking." Toma shouldered his pack. Whatever had opened in his face had closed again, carefully, like a hand folding around a coal to carry it. "Then listen to me once, guide, and I will not say it again. If such a room exists — under stone, under water, under whatever your kind uses to hide things — then everyone who has looked for it has wanted it for the same reason. To destroy it. A lie that big cannot survive being proven, so the proof must die instead. And the men sent to kill proof do not send letters first." He stepped past Ivo, close enough that Ivo could smell woodsmoke and river on him, and lowered his voice to almost nothing.

"I carried for the Cartwright expedition. First leg only, to the trading post above the falls. I left them there because my knees are old, not because I feared the gorge — remember that, when you wonder why I'm going back now. And I will tell you what I saw on my last morning among them." His eyes held Ivo's, brown and green and bottomless. "Their security chief counted the porters twice. Once for wages. Once for something else. Writing names in a second book. I have carried freight on five rivers for thirty years, Thornbury, and I know what a second book is for."

He walked up the dock toward the boats, straight-backed, unhurried, a man going home to a country that officially did not exist.

Ivo stood alone at the rail while the fog burned off the water and the sun cleared the ridge and the day became ordinary, except that it wasn't, except that the past had just walked up his gangplank wearing a porter's pack and carrying a warning in a second book.

Behind him, boots on boards — Dekker, cheerful, clipboard in hand. "All accounted for, Thornbury. Eight souls, four tons cut to two. Ready when you are."

Eight souls. Ivo counted the figures moving on the dock and found, for one cold heartbeat, that he no longer trusted anyone's arithmetic but his own.

"Cast off," he said. "We're burning daylight."

The river took them upstream, toward the bend, toward the gorge with no name — and somewhere ahead of them, Ivo was certain of it now, the silence was waiting, and it had begun long before this month.
