## 6. First Blood

The cache was where it should have been — that was the first wrong thing.

Sebastian had learned cartography the way other children learned prayers: by repetition, by rote, by the patient accumulation of coordinates until they settled into something like faith. And a supply cache at Kilometer Sixty-One on the Vell was not a matter of faith. It was a matter of arithmetic. Cartwright's expedition plan, which she had helped draft in a windowless office in the capital while rain argued against the glass, placed the second resupply depot on a gravel shelf above the river's north bank, three hundred meters past the confluence with the Miren tributary, marked by two cairns and an iron stake driven into the shale. She could have found it blindfolded, in fog, in her sleep. She had, in a sense, been finding it for months — tracing its position over and over in the margins of her drafts, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

So when the cairns rose out of the morning mist exactly where they should have been, she felt no relief at all.

"Something's off," she said, before she knew she'd said it aloud.

Ivo heard her. Of course he did. He had the hearing of a man who'd spent twenty years listening to rivers for a living, and lately he seemed to hear everything she said whether she wanted him to or not. He didn't answer. He just stopped walking, tilted his head the way he did when reading water, and let the silence do his agreeing for her.

The porters felt it too. They hung back at the tree line, bundles lowered, faces closed in that particular way she'd come to recognize since the trading post — the look of men who had agreed to carry loads upriver but not to carry trouble. Since the gorge had come into view on the horizon, gray and enormous as a closed door, they had spoken less and less, and what they said to each other was in a dialect Dekker couldn't follow and Sebastian pretended not to study.

She went ahead anyway. That was the thing about being a cartographer: you went to look. The map was never finished until someone's boots confirmed it.

* * *

The cache was a steel-reinforced crate half-sunk into a dugout lined with oilcloth, standard trust issue, stenciled CARTWRIGHT EXPEDITION / PROPERTY OF THE VELL SURVEY TRUST along one side in flaking white letters. The tarp over it had been cut, not untied — one long deliberate stroke, the kind of cut a knife makes when the hand holding it isn't hurried.

Sebastian knelt in the wet gravel and looked inside, and understood, all at once and completely, why Ivo hadn't answered her.

Rifled but not robbed. That was the phrase that came to her, unbidden, from some half-remembered seminar on evidence handling she'd sat through because attendance was mandatory. The crate was chaos, but it was *ordered* chaos. Rice sacks slashed and spilled. Tinned meat opened and left, some of it untouched, rust already freckling the lids. A medical kit unrolled like a gutted fish, sutures scattered, the morphine ampoules — she counted twice — every single one still there. Nobody had taken the morphine. In the settlements upriver, morphine was currency; in the capital it was worth more than her father's house. Nobody had taken the ammunition either, and the spare rifle wrapped in canvas lay where it had been tossed, oiled and loaded, worth a season's wages to any man in these hills.

What was gone: the document case. The flat aluminum box, locked, that Cartwright's plan had listed under *survey materials — sensitive*. Field notebooks. Film canisters. The sealed correspondence pouch addressed to the Trust. Sebastian knew its dimensions because she had signed the inventory sheet. It fit in the space between the rice sacks like a body fits a coffin, and its absence had a shape, and the shape of it made her cold in a way the river fog could not.

"They took paper," she said. "They took paper and left the gun."

Dekker crouched beside her, close enough that she caught the smell of him — tobacco and insect repellent and something underneath, something chemical and wrong that she'd been noticing for two days without naming. He whistled low through his teeth. "Addicts, maybe. Scared off mid-looting."

"Addicts who don't want morphine." She lifted a tin of meat, turned it over. "Who don't want a rifle. Who cut the tarp instead of untying it because cutting is faster and they were in a hurry, but who took time to open the medical kit and check it and leave it." She set the tin down carefully, aligning it with the edge of the crate, because order was how she thought. "They were looking for something specific, Dekker. They found it. Then they left everything else as evidence that they'd been here."

He looked at her then, and for a half-second — no longer, she would tell herself later, half a second at most — his face did something complicated. Not guilt. Something adjacent to guilt, a neighbor of it, the way a man's face does when a plan he's invested in goes sideways in a way he didn't script. Then it smoothed over into professional blankness, and he said, "Or Cartwright's own people came back through and reorganized," and stood up, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

* * *

Ivo found the prints.

Of course he did. Her father had a genius for ground that bordered on the uncanny — he could read a mudflat the way she read an isobar, pressure systems and fronts and the whole invisible weather of passage written in disturbance. He didn't crouch so much as lower himself, joint by joint, a big man folding down into stillness, and then he was quiet for so long that Sebastian came to stand behind him and look at what he was looking at and see, at first, nothing at all.

Then she saw it. The shelf of gravel above the cache was scuffed with their own tracks and the porters' and the general argument of six men and their loads. But along the margin, where the gravel gave way to the black loam under the trees, the ground held a line of boot prints, heading east. Toward the gorge.

"Count them," Ivo said. His voice was flat, which she had learned — in four days of traveling with a man she hated, or something adjacent to hating, or something adjacent to something else entirely — meant fear.

She counted. "Four sets. Maybe five. Two heavy, one light — see, the stride's shorter, the heel strike's shallow—" She stopped. "One of them is limping."

"One of them is limping," Ivo agreed. "Drag on the left foot. Regular. Someone carrying an injury they're walking through."

Amara Osei was an ethnographer, sixty-one years old, and according to the manifest she had a chronic condition in her left knee. Sebastian did not say this out loud. Saying it out loud would make it hope, and hope on the Vell River was starting to feel like a currency that cost more than it bought.

"The prints are fresh," Ivo went on. He touched the lip of one print, where the loam had curled and not yet dried at the edges. "Overnight. Eight hours, ten at most. Rain would have softened them and we had none." He looked up, and the fog moved behind him so that for a moment he seemed framed by it, a figure out of one of the old engravings in her childhood atlas — the explorer at the edge of the unknown, except her father had never looked like an engraving in his life; he looked like what he was, which was tired and hard and sorry. "They're ahead of us. Whoever they are, they're moving toward the gorge with those documents, and they've got a day on us."

"Then we move faster."

"It's not a race you win by running, Sebastian." He said her name carefully, the way a man handles a tool he knows can cut him. "The gorge narrows to a quarter kilometer at the Narrows. If they've got rifles and we come up that slot loud and strung out—"

"If we come up slow, they're gone, and whatever Amara Osei survived to tell us dies with her in a cave somewhere." She heard her own voice rise and made herself bring it back down. Around them the porters had gone very still, watching the two Thornburys argue in a language of restraint that was somehow louder than shouting. "You read the river like scripture, Papa. Fine. Read me this: a wounded woman limps east with four armed men and a box of papers, toward territory your own maps call impassable. What is she doing?"

Ivo was silent a long moment. Fog beaded in his gray-streaked beard. When he finally spoke, it was barely above the sound of the river.

"She's leading them somewhere," he said. "Or she's being led. Either way—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and Sebastian watched him decide, visibly decide, to say the rest. "Either way, she's alive. Twenty years ago I'd have told you nobody comes out of that gorge alive. I'm done telling the river what it can't do."

* * *

They buried what couldn't be carried and carried what couldn't be buried, and the redistribution took an hour that felt like a day. Dekker argued for leaving the medical kit — weight — and Sebastian overruled him with the inventory sheet and a tone she'd inherited from no one, that was entirely her own, forged in conference rooms where senior cartographers had explained patiently that a girl with her surname shouldn't expect assignments of consequence. The morphine went into her own pack. She told herself it was because she was the most careful with supplies. She told herself it had nothing to do with a wounded woman limping east.

They broke camp at noon, which was late, and Ivo set a pace upriver that was brutal and exact, the pace of a man spending capital he'd been hoarding for twenty years. The river climbed with them, growing louder, whiter, more impatient. By midafternoon the fog had burned off and the gorge stood fully revealed for the first time — and Sebastian, who had drawn its walls from survey data a hundred times, who knew its elevations to the meter, stopped dead on the trail and felt the data die in her hands.

No contour line had prepared her. The cliffs went up and up, banded black and ochre, streaked with the white mineral tears of seepage, and between them the Vell ran green-black and deep, compressed into a current that made a sound less like water than like machinery. Birds she had no names for crossed the gap high overhead, tiny as punctuation. On the far wall, almost too faint to see, there were marks — regular, deliberate, pale against the dark stone — that might have been weathering or might have been the oldest thing she had ever looked at.

"That's the place with no name," said the head porter quietly, behind her. He said it in trade-tongue, not bothering to lower his voice, and she understood that he wasn't warning her anymore. He was mourning. "Your maps call it nothing. Our grandmothers called it nothing too — after. Before, it had another word, and the word was a nation, and now only the stone remembers it." He spat, ceremonially, toward the river. "Whatever your papers say, the stone remembers."

Sebastian looked at the far wall a long time. Then she took out her field notebook — her real one, the waterproof one, the one she'd shown no one — and wrote down what he'd said, word for word, and dated it, and initialed it. Evidence handling. Attendance mandatory.

* * *

They found the blood at dusk.

It was a small thing, at first — a brown smear on a rock at the trail's edge, old enough to have lost its red, fresh enough to still hold its shine. Then more of it, a spatter, then a drag mark veering off the main track toward a stand of bamboo, and Ivo raised a fist and the column stopped and the river filled the silence with its grinding voice.

He went forward alone. Sebastian followed anyway, because she was her father's daughter in every way she resented, and together they parted the bamboo and looked down into a hollow where the ground was torn and dark.

Not a body. A campsite that had become a struggle — a cold fire ring, a scattered bedroll, a broken strap, and blood, enough blood, dried in arcs across the stones. And pinned under a stone at the hollow's edge, weighted deliberately, deliberately findable, a page torn from a field notebook. Graph paper. Cartwright Trust stock. The handwriting was Amara Osei's — Sebastian recognized it from the grant proposals, the small severe capitals — and the words were few, written fast, the pen pressed so hard it had nearly cut the paper:

THEY ARE NOT PORTERS. THE SECURITY CHIEF LIES. THE ROOM UNDER THE WATERFALL IS REAL AND HE WANTS IT SEALED WITH US INSIDE. IF YOU READ THIS I AM ALIVE OR I AM NOT. EITHER WAY COME TO THE FALLS. BRING NOTHING THAT RADIOS.

Sebastian read it twice. Then a third time, because her eyes kept snagging on the last sentence, coming back to it the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

*Bring nothing that radios.*

Behind them, back up the trail, she heard Dekker's voice, easy and carrying, explaining to the porters that they'd make camp here, no sense stumbling around in the dark, plenty of time in the morning. Explaining it loudly. Explaining it clearly. Explaining it, she realized, in the direction of the gorge, where sound traveled downslot for kilometers on evenings as still as this one.

Her father had gone rigid beside her. She could feel the rigidity coming off him like heat off a stove. When she glanced at him, he was looking at the page, and then at the bamboo, and then — briefly, terribly — at nothing at all, at some middle distance where calculations were happening that she could not see.

"Papa," she breathed. "The radio schedule. Tonight's transmission window. We're due to report position to the Trust at twenty-one hundred."

"I know."

"And if we don't report—"

"I know what happens if we don't report." He folded the page along its existing creases, with hands that were not quite steady, and put it inside his jacket, against his chest, like a letter from the dead. "And I know what happens if we do."

The fog was coming back up the gorge now, rising off the water in slow columns, erasing the cliffs from the top down, and somewhere above the falls — she could just hear it, a deeper note under the river's grind, a sound like the world holding its breath — somewhere above the falls, a wounded woman was moving through the dark with a box of papers and no one coming, and the only question left, the question that turned rescue into race, was whether the next people to reach her would be carrying stretchers or rope.

Sebastian shouldered her pack. "Twenty-one hundred," she said. "We miss the window. We go dark. And we climb."

For the first time in four days, her father smiled at her — small, grim, proud in a way he had no right to be — and nodded once, and turned toward the falls.
