## 7. The Gorge with No Name

The river changed its mind about the color of the world at dawn.

Ivo had been awake for an hour before that, lying in the wet canvas smell of his tent, listening to the water. Every river has a voice, and the Vell had been talking all night in the low, gravelly register it used when it was carrying more silt than it wanted to — the voice of a river coming down out of high country, out of country that did not want visitors. By the time the light came, gray and thin as old linen, the fog had already begun to rise off the water in columns, and the gorge walls on either side of them went up into that fog until they simply stopped being walls and became a suggestion, a rumor of stone.

He stood at the water's edge with his hands in his pockets and did not look at the far bank. He looked at the near bank, at the mud, at the boot prints of the porters from the night before, and he made himself breathe the way he'd taught a hundred younger guides to breathe: slow, through the nose, four counts in, six counts out, because a man who breathes fast makes fast decisions and fast decisions in the Vell gorge were how widows got made.

Behind him the camp was waking. He heard Sebastian's tent flap first — she was always first, had been first since she was nine years old and he'd taught her that the second riser on an expedition was already a day behind — and then the clink of the cook's pots, and then Dekker's voice, low and cheerful in the way that Ivo had come, over the past week, to find almost physically unpleasant, like a fingernail dragged along the inside of his ear.

"Morning, Thornbury." Dekker came and stood beside him at the water, cupping a tin mug of something too hot to drink. "Fog's thick."

"Fog burns off by eight." Ivo said it to the river. "We make the first narrows by ten. After that, no banks worth the name — we walk the ledge the rest of the day."

"The ledge." Dekker said it lightly, but Ivo caught the small hitch under it, the half-beat where a man's voice checks itself before it commits. "That's the old survey trail, isn't it? The one that isn't on the charts."

Ivo turned and looked at him. Dekker was a big man gone soft in the middle, with the ruddy, open face of a man who wanted to be liked, and for eleven days Ivo had been telling himself that open faces were the most dangerous kind, because they gave you nothing to read. Now he looked at him and said, flatly: "There is no survey trail. There's a goat track that a survey crew widened once, and then the survey never happened, and the trail was never there. That's what the charts will tell you."

"And what will the charts not tell you?"

Ivo looked back at the river. The fog was lifting off it in rags now, and the water showed through, green-black, moving with the patient enormous indifference of all water that has somewhere to be.

"That's a long story," he said. "And I only tell it once."

* * *

They broke camp at six and were in the narrows by half past nine, which Ivo counted as a small victory over the day. The gorge closed around them the way a hand closes around a coin. The walls came in until the sky was a strip of hammered pewter overhead, and the river, robbed of its floodplain, went loud and white and furious, and the ledge they walked was cut into the left wall at the height of a man's chest above the water, wide enough for one person, in places wide enough for one person who was not thinking about anything else.

Sebastian walked point. She had taken point without being asked, three days ago, and he had not taken it back from her, and neither of them had said a word about it, and the silence around that fact was the largest object in the expedition, larger than the gorge. He watched her move ahead of him — her pack riding high and right, her pole testing each stone the way he'd taught her, tap, weight, commit — and he thought, not for the first time, that she had his stride. That was the cruelest joke the years had played on him: that everything he'd given her, she'd kept, and everything he'd taken from her, she'd kept too.

The porters went quiet when the walls closed in. Not the ordinary quiet of men saving breath on a climb, but a different quality of silence, the kind that has a texture. Ivo had heard it before. Twenty years ago he had heard it on this same ledge, in this same narrows, from different men, and he had asked his survey chief why the hired men wouldn't talk in the gorge, and the survey chief — a brisk, clever, ambitious man named Halloran, whose name Ivo had not spoken aloud in two decades and whose face he could still see perfectly, the little gold pen tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil — had laughed and said, *Because they're superstitious, Thornbury. They think the gorge eats names.*

He had laughed too. That was the part that sat in him like a swallowed hook. He had laughed, a young guide of twenty-six with a commission and a theodolite and a future, and he had written it in his field notes — *porters silent in narrows, local superstition re: names* — in his neat surveyor's hand, and that note had gone into the report, and the report had gone into the archive, and the archive had gone into the ministries, and somewhere down that chain of paper a man with a stamp had read *superstition re: names* and understood it perfectly, better than Ivo ever had.

Because the gorge did eat names. That was what the porters had been trying to tell them. Not a superstition. A memory.

At the first rest halt — a shelf where the ledge widened and three men could stand abreast, where a seep of water came out of the rock and hung the wall in curtains of green moss — Ivo took off his pack and sat down facing the wall instead of the view, and Sebastian noticed. Of course she noticed. She noticed everything he did and catalogued it, he was sure, in the same ledger where she kept all his debts.

"You're limping," she said, coming to stand over him. Not *are you all right.* She didn't ask questions she didn't want the answers to. She stated findings.

"Old break. It speaks when it's cold." He flexed the ankle. "It'll shut up by noon."

She stood there a moment longer. The others were spread along the shelf, drinking, chewing, not looking at the two of them, in the deliberate way of people who have already learned that the Thornburys' weather was not to be commented on. Then Sebastian sat down across from him, cross-legged, uninvited, and unwrapped a strip of dried fish and did not offer him any, and said:

"The porters stopped talking an hour ago. All of them. Even Oyelaran's boy, and he hasn't stopped talking since the dock." She said it like a measurement. "You knew they would. You've known since the trading post."

Ivo looked at her. Twenty years old, and she had his gray eyes, which meant that when she looked at him he was looking at a younger version of his own face wearing an older version of his own judgment. He had spent eleven days deciding whether to tell her. He had told himself he was waiting for the right moment, and he knew that was a lie, because there was no right moment for a thing like this; there was only the moment when the lie got heavier than the truth, and the truth, in the narrows of the Vell, in the country with no name, had just become the lighter load.

"Give me until the ledge camp," he said. "Tonight. When we're off this rock. I'll tell you all of it — what this gorge is, and what I did in it."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her decide not to push, and it cost her, and he loved her for the cost, and he hated himself for knowing that he could spend her patience like this, on credit, against a payment he wasn't sure he could make.

"You've had eleven days," she said quietly.

"I know."

"You've had twenty years."

"I know that too." He looked down at his hands. They were a guide's hands, scarred and square, and they had held a theodolite on this very ledge, and they had shaken Halloran's hand in a ministry office in the capital, and they had held a photograph of a girl of four that he had finally, after three years, put in a drawer. "Tonight, Sebastian. You have my word, and I know what my word is worth to you, which is why I'm giving it anyway."

She looked at him for a long moment, and then she stood, and wrapped the fish back up, and said, "Point's moving out," and walked away down the ledge, tap, weight, commit, his stride, his daughter, his judge.

* * *

They made the ledge camp as the light was failing, in a hollow where an old slide had cut a shelf into the wall forty feet above the water, and the roar of the narrows came up to them muffled, like the sea heard through the hull of a boat. Ivo got the fire small and screened, the way you do in country where you don't want to be found, and after the porters had eaten and rolled into their blankets, and after Dekker had made his round of cheerful remarks and gone to his tent, and after the two hired boatmen had drifted to the edge of the firelight with the universal body language of men who know a story is coming and want to be near it without being in it, Ivo stayed sitting by the coals.

Sebastian came and sat across from him. She didn't bring anything. She just sat, and folded her hands, and waited, and it was the waiting that undid him more than any question could have, because she had learned patience from somewhere, and he knew exactly where.

"The survey of '04," he said, to the coals. "That's where it starts. You'd have been four."

"I know the date," she said. "It's the date you left."

"Yes." He turned a stick in the coals. "It's the date I left. It's also the date I arrived, though I didn't understand that for years. Halloran's crew. Six of us, plus twelve porters. Contracted through the Ministry of Boundaries and Records — there really is a ministry with that name, and the joke of it is that they were the one ministry in the country that did exactly what it said. Boundaries. And records." He looked up. "We were mapping the Vell headwaters. That was the brief. Hydrographic survey, watershed delineation, the dull honest work of putting a river on paper. And that's what I thought it was, for six weeks. I was the junior guide and the junior surveyor and I was so proud, Sebastian. I had a theodolite with my name stenciled on the case. I had a commission. I had a wife and a daughter in a rented house in Lowmarsh and I sent money home every fortnight and I wrote to you — you were too young to remember the letters. Your mother kept them. She told me, later, she kept them in a tin."

"I've read them," Sebastian said. Her voice was level. "They're very loving. And very vague. Every one of them, after the gorge, stops describing where you are. You wrote pages about the stars and nothing about the country."

"Because after the gorge I stopped believing the country was mine to describe." He said it simply, and saw her blink, and went on before she could speak. "Here's what happened. We mapped. We were good at it. And the porters watched us being good at it, and they got quieter and quieter, and in the fifth week the head porter — his name was Sela, and I have not let myself forget it — came to Halloran and asked to see our maps. Halloran refused. Sela came to me, because I was the one who ate with the men, the one who'd learned forty words of the river dialect, the one they'd decided was the soft one. And he asked me. Not for the maps. He asked me what the maps were *for.* And I told him the truth as I understood it, which was: watershed records, ministry archives, nothing to do with him. And he looked at me for a long time and he said — I can still hear it, I will hear it on my deathbed — he said, *The maps are for the people who will come after the maps.*"

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them, on the wall of the gorge, a bird — some night bird Ivo had never learned the name of, in a country whose names he had helped burn — called twice and went silent.

"I didn't understand him," Ivo said. "That's the truth and it's no excuse. I thought it was philosophy. I thought it was the kind of thing river people said, the way they said the gorge ate names. I wrote it in my field book. *Porter Sela: maps are for the people who come after the maps.* I thought I was recording color. Local color. I thought I was being *respectful.*" He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "And then we came out, and I delivered the plates, and I shook Halloran's hand, and I went home to Lowmarsh, and eleven months later the Resettlement Edict came down."

Sebastian had gone very still. "The border villages," she said. "The ones in the old gazetteer that just say *depopulated, administrative transfer.*"

"Nine villages." Ivo's voice was steady, which surprised him; he had expected it to break. "Nine villages in the Vell headwaters, on the far bank, in the gorge country. The new border ran straight through them. It didn't follow the watershed — the watershed was the honest line, the line the river itself had drawn, and any fool could see it. It followed our survey. It followed the line that put the far bank — and every village on it, and every field, and every grave — on the other side of a line that had never existed in the history of the world until two men with a theodolite drew it in a tent. And the people were moved. Not asked. *Moved.* Those who went, went. Those who didn't—" He stopped. He made himself go on. "There's a village called Ashen Ford in the old records. It's not in the new records. There are men in this country who could tell you why, and the men who could tell you best are the ones who were paid not to."

"And you?"

"I was already gone." He said it flat. "I'd taken a contract in the western ranges when the edict came. I heard about it the way you hear about weather, secondhand, in a supply town, from a newspaper that used the word *transfer.* And I told myself what every man in that survey told himself: the maps were honest, the use was dishonest, the sin was the ministry's. And then, a year later, I found out about the burning."

"The burning," Sebastian said. "You said that word once. In your sleep. I was fourteen. You'd come back for—" She stopped herself. He watched her decide not to spend that yet. "You said *they burned it* and then you woke up and never said another word about anything, ever again."

"They burned the field books." Ivo looked into the coals. "All of them. The original notes — the village names, the dialect transcriptions, the place-names the porters gave us, every name Sela ever spoke into my notebook. The published survey kept the contour lines and the river courses and erased every name that wasn't an approved name. And then a records office in the capital burned the originals. Officially an accident. Unofficially — I had a friend in the survey office, a draughtsman, and he told me before he left the country forever that the order came from the same desk that commissioned the survey. The same desk. They commissioned the map *in order to burn the map.* The survey wasn't the crime, Sebastian. The survey was the *alibi.* We were the alibi. I was the alibi, with my name stenciled on the theodolite case, and I ran. I didn't testify, I didn't write, I didn't stand in any square with a placard. I ran into the western ranges and I stayed there, and when the blacklist came — when they finally decided a guide who knew too much about the gorge was more useful discredited than employed — I let them. Do you understand? I *let* them. The blacklist was the first honest document anyone had ever issued about me."

Sebastian was silent for a long time. The river roared below them, patient, enormous, carrying its silt down out of the high country, carrying, Ivo thought, everything, always, and keeping nothing.

"Sela," she said at last. "What happened to him?"

"I don't know." The words came out of him like teeth. "I went back once. Seven years after. I told myself it was to make it right, and what I actually did was walk to the edge of the gorge country, look at it for one hour, and walk away, because the far bank had soldiers on it and I had a daughter and I was a coward in the specific shape of a prudent man. That's the whole confession. That's the ballast I've been carrying up this river, and it's why the porters went silent when the walls closed in — because they know what this gorge is, and now you do too, and I'd give anything, Sebastian, I'd give the other leg, to have learned it some other way than by being the man who drew the line."

The fire had burned down to a red breathing. Sebastian sat with her arms around her knees, and she did not touch him, and she did not forgive him, and she did not condemn him, and all three of those absences were more than he could bear and exactly what he deserved.

"The vault," she said finally, quietly. "Amara's *room under the waterfall.* If it's what we think it is — if there are maps in there older than our survey, older than the border, maps that show the country with its own name on it—" She looked up at him, and her eyes in the firelight were his eyes, and they were not gentle. "Then everything you just told me has an ending that isn't a drawer. The names weren't all burned, Dad. Some of them were *hidden.*"

* * *

It was an hour past midnight when the boy came to Ivo's tent.

He was the youngest porter, Oyelaran's boy, sixteen maybe, all elbows and watchfulness, and he stood outside the canvas in the dark and said, in the trade tongue, in a whisper: *The old man wants the guide. The one who doesn't talk. He says the guide will know what it means.*

Ivo crawled out into the cold. The old man was the eldest porter, the one they'd hired at the trading post to replace the men who wouldn't come past the gorge, the one with the burned half of his face that he never explained and nobody asked about. He was sitting up at the edge of the camp, wrapped in his blanket, and he was not looking at Ivo. He was looking across the river, into the black of the far wall, where the fog had come down again and the gorge was once more a country with no edges.

"Listen," the old man said, in the river dialect, slow, so that Ivo would be sure to follow.

Ivo listened. At first there was nothing but the water. And then, underneath the water, threaded through it, faint as a pulse heard through a wall — a sound from the far side of the gorge. A human sound. Thin and rising and falling, and it took Ivo's mind a long, cold moment to resolve it into what it was, because no one made that sound in the gorge country, no one had made that sound in twenty years.

Someone on the far bank was singing. A woman's voice, cracked with weakness, singing a song in a language the border had erased — and the old man's burned face, when he turned to Ivo, was shining wet in the starlight.

"She is singing the ford-song," the old man said. "The song of Ashen Ford. There has been no one to sing it since the burning." He gripped Ivo's wrist with a hand like a root. "Someone is alive over there. Someone who knows the old country. And she is singing it *loudly,* guide. Loudly enough to be found."

Ivo was already reaching for his boots.
