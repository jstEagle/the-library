## 8. Survivor One

The cave had a heartbeat.

Amara Osei had learned this on her fourth day inside it — that the mountain breathed through the stone, slow and cold, an exhalation from some deep lung of water and dark. When she pressed her palm flat against the wall she could feel it: a pulse of damp air, faint as a sleeping child's breath against her wrist. She had begun to time herself by it. Inhale, the rock drew cold into its throat. Exhale, it sighed warmth back out, carrying the smell of wet iron and old moss. She was living inside something alive, and she could not decide whether that comforted her or whether it was simply one more way the gorge with no name refused to let her forget what it was.

She counted days by the light instead. A seam in the cave mouth, high where the stone folded like a curtain, admitted a blade of sun for perhaps forty minutes each afternoon. She had scratched marks beside it with a shard of quartz — eleven strokes now, though she suspected she had missed one somewhere in the fevered stretch after the third day, when her leg had swollen to twice its size and she had lain talking to her grandmother in Twi until the fever broke and the voice went away and left her alone again with the mountain's breathing.

Eleven days since the waterfall. Eleven days since she had heard men die for looking at paper.

She shifted against the pack she used as a pillow and the pain in her thigh flared white, then settled into its familiar dull argument. The wound was better. She had washed it in the seep that ran along the back wall — cold enough to make her gasp, clean enough, she prayed, to keep the rot away — and bound it with strips cut from her shirt hem and, later, from the expedition's signal tarp, which had made a fine bright bandage that she had then covered with mud because bright things got people found. Her mother would have laughed at that. *You hide everything, Ama. Even your wounds.* Her mother had been right about most things and dead about all of them now, twelve years gone, and still her voice arrived at the strangest hours, punctual as the mountain's breath.

She should not have survived. She knew this the way she knew the river's name before the treaty took it — not as opinion but as fact held in the body. When the security chief's men had opened fire at the cache camp, she had been forty meters downstream washing plates, and the sound had come to her first as birdsong, wrong birdsong, too many wings, and then Cartwright's surveyor had run past her with his chest open and his mouth still moving, trying to say something that never became words. She had run. Not bravely, not cleverly — she had simply run, upstream, into the fog, with the ethnographic folios strapped to her back because her hands had been full of them when the shooting started and there had been no time to put them down.

The folios had saved her life. That was the joke she could not stop telling herself in the dark. Forty kilograms of other people's memory, maps drawn on bark and hide and woven cloth, and the weight of them had driven her down into the ravine where the shooters' lanterns didn't reach, had anchored her like ballast while bullets stitched the fog above her head. She had crawled into this cave behind the curtain of hanging roots and she had not left except to drink, and the folios lay beside her now under a rain cape, wrapped in oilcloth, and she spoke to them sometimes the way you speak to a sleeping animal you are afraid will wake.

*Not yet,* she told them, as the light-blade crept toward the quartz shard's shadow. *Not yet. But soon.*

* * *

On the twelfth day, she heard boots.

She had heard boots before — twice, maybe three times, patrols working up the gorge trail, their radios crackling fragments of a language she pretended not to understand but understood perfectly. *Grid search. No survivors reported. Vane wants confirmation.* Confirmation. The word had a taste, like copper coins. They were not searching for survivors; they were searching for evidence that none remained. She had learned to read the difference between men hunting people and men hunting paper, and these men hunted both, and so she had gone deeper into the cave and stopped breathing when they passed, and once a beam of light had swept the root-curtain and laid stripes across her own hand and she had watched those stripes with the detachment of a woman watching weather.

But these boots were different. These boots were many, and slow, and arguing with themselves in low voices, and one of the voices said — she sat up so fast her vision swam — one of the voices said *Thornbury.*

She knew the name the way everyone in the trade knew it. Twenty years dead and still walking: Ivo Thornbury, the guide who mapped the gorge for the survey, the man whose contour lines had become the eviction notices. She had written about him once, early in her career, a footnote in a monograph on cartography and dispossession — *the technician is rarely the architect, but the technician's hands build the house* — and she had wondered, in the way academics wonder, whatever became of him. Now his name came up the gorge trail attached to boots, and below it another voice answered him, younger, clipped, female, correcting his bearings off a chart he clearly hadn't drawn, and Amara felt something move in her chest that she had not permitted herself in twelve days.

Hope. Small, treacherous, warm-blooded hope.

She made herself lie still. This was the discipline the last twelve days had carved into her: hope curdles, hope calls out, hope stands up in the mouth of a cave and gets shot. She listened instead. She catalogued voices the way she catalogued everything — four, five, six speakers, porters among them speaking the river dialect, and the woman again, sharp and precise, saying something about scree slope and sightlines, and a man named Dekker whose tone was smooth as poured oil and who asked questions that were slightly wrong. Asking how far to the falls. Asking whether anyone had come through ahead. Asking, casually, terribly, whether the doctor might have kept any documents on her person.

Amara's blood went quiet and cold. She had heard that voice's cousin before — the security chief, Renn, in the last week at base camp, asking Cartwright over dinner why an ethnographer needed to photograph every plate, why she couldn't just catalogue and move on, smiling his closed smile while the stew went cold between them. She had thought then that she was being paranoid. Paranoia, it turned out, was simply foresight with bad manners.

She waited until the voices moved on up the trail, and then she waited through the whole length of the afternoon light, and then she waited through the mountain's next hundred breaths, and only when full dark had settled did she begin to crawl.

* * *

It took her two hours to cover four hundred meters.

She went on her elbows and good knee, dragging the leg, stopping every twenty body-lengths to listen, following the smell of woodsmoke the way her grandmother had taught her to follow water in the dry season — patiently, gratefully, without dignity. The folios stayed behind, wrapped and hidden in the deep fissure at the cave's rear, weighted with stones. It had cost her an hour of anguish to leave them. But if she died on this crawl, they would die with her, sealed in stone, and the room under the waterfall would keep its secret another century, and the nation with no name would stay erased, tidy as a margin. So she kissed the oilcloth — actually kissed it, feeling foolish, feeling desperate — and left it, and crawled toward the firelight like something born in the dark learning, late, to want the sun.

She smelled them before she saw them: woodsmoke, boiled grain, horse sweat, and under it all the particular human smell of a camp that has walked far and fears what it walks toward. She came up over a lip of stone at the edge of the firelight and the world tilted and she caught herself on a boulder and her hand slipped and gravel sang, and every head at that fire turned at once, and there was no more hiding, no more crawling, no more patience.

So she stood up. On one leg, mostly, swaying, mud-caked, half-starved, holding a branch for a staff, wearing a bandage made of signal tarp under a crust of dried blood. She stood up in the firelight and looked at the faces turned toward her — a young woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a hawk reading ground, a big grey-bearded man whose face she knew from photographs even though the photographs were twenty years old, and others, porters, a nervous man with soft hands — and she heard her own voice come out of her ruined throat, cracked and rusted, saying the thing she had rehearsed against the cave wall for twelve days:

"My name is Dr. Amara Osei. I am the ethnographer of the Cartwright expedition. And I am the last person alive who has seen the room under the waterfall."

The silence after that had texture. She felt it press against her skin.

Then the young woman moved first — not the famous Thornbury, but the daughter, Sebastian, dropping her tin cup and crossing the gravel at a half-run, catching Amara just as her knee finally confessed it could hold no longer. Strong hands. Ink and dirt on the knuckles. "Water," the girl was saying over her shoulder, "get me water, and the kit, now," and there was command in it, unhesitating, and Amara thought: *good, she'll need that,* and then thought nothing at all for a while, because the fire was warm and the water tasted of tin and pine and was the best thing she had ever drunk.

* * *

They worked on her leg by firelight. The grey-bearded man — Thornbury himself, older than his photographs, with a face like terrain that had been glaciated and re-glaciated — cleaned the wound with hands that were gentler than his face promised, and did not ask questions while he worked, which she noted and filed. The daughter held her shoulders. The oily one, Dekker, hovered at the fire's edge asking helpful questions that were all shaped like knives: *How did you escape? Did you see who fired? Where were the others? Do you have anything — papers, notes, anything the doctor might have salvaged?*

"I have my life," Amara said, when she had breath for it. "I find it sufficient."

Dekker smiled. She catalogued the smile.

But it was Thornbury's silence she watched. While the daughter dressed the wound, the old guide sat back on his heels and studied her with an expression she recognized from her own fieldwork among people who had been lied to by governments: the look of a man doing arithmetic with terrible numbers. When their eyes met, he said, quietly, so the others couldn't hear, "How many?"

"Nine," she said. "That I saw fall. There were fourteen of us past the second rapids." She swallowed. "Cartwright may live. Lenn, the photographer — I don't know. Renn ordered it. You understand? Not bandits, not the river. The expedition's own security chief gave the order, and the men who fired were paid from the same purse that bought our tents."

Thornbury closed his eyes for one breath. When he opened them, something had finished settling in his face, some long-argued question resolved the wrong way. "Vane's purse," he said.

"You know the name."

"Everyone knows the name. Few get to hear it spoken up here." He glanced at the fire, at Dekker's back, and lowered his voice further. "Say nothing more tonight. Sleep. Tomorrow—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Tomorrow we talk about the waterfall."

"There is no tomorrow for talking," Amara said, and heard the fever edge creep into her own voice and could not fully leash it. "Don't you understand what I'm telling you? They are not behind us. They are *around* us. Renn's men know this gorge better than your charts do — they've had twelve days to learn it. And there is one among you—" Her eyes went, before she could stop them, to Dekker's broad back, and she wrenched them away, too late, and saw Thornbury see it. "One among you," she finished, softer, "who asks the wrong questions beautifully."

The old guide was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the mountain breathed out its cold breath through the stone, and Amara felt it on her neck like a hand.

"Rest," Thornbury said at last. "I'll take first watch myself."

"Watch the watch," she whispered, as he rose. "That is not paranoia, Mr. Thornbury. I have seen what happens to people who trust the structure around them. I spent my career recording what happens." She let her head sink back against the pack, exhaustion rising like floodwater. "The room under the waterfall is real. Everything your government says isn't there — it's there. Thousands of maps. A whole country on paper that they burned the villages off the earth to match. I can take you to it. God help me, I can still walk."

She was nearly under when she heard the daughter's voice, low, close, meant for the father and drifting to Amara anyway across the small radius of the fire:

"She said *one among us.* Dad. Who is she talking about?"

And Amara, drifting, held her breathing steady and listened for the answer, and heard only the long silence of a man who already knew — and the mountain, breathing in.
