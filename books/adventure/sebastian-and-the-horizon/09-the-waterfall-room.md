## 9. The Waterfall Room

The waterfall did not fall so much as pour itself out of the mountain's shoulder like something being emptied from a jug — a single unbroken column of white noise that swallowed every other sound for half a mile before they even saw it. Sebastian felt it in her teeth first, then her ribs, then the soles of her boots through wet stone: a vibration so total it seemed less like water than like the gorge itself speaking.

Amara Osei walked ahead of them with a stick for a crutch and a certainty that needed no map.

They had left the cave before dawn, moving along the river's northern bank where the mist lay thick enough to drink. Amara had refused to explain anything until they were inside the spray's reach. *You'll understand better when you can't hear yourselves think,* she'd said, and there had been something in her voice — not humor exactly, but its cousin — that made Sebastian hold her questions behind her teeth like stones she wasn't ready to spit.

Now they stood at the base of the falls, and the world was roar and cold and light fractured into permanent rainbows that came apart and reassembled with every shift of the wind.

"Behind it," Amara shouted, or seemed to shout; her voice arrived more as intention than sound. "There's a ledge. Keep your hand on the rock. Don't trust your eyes — the spray lies."

Ivo went first. Of course he went first; some habits were older than his disgrace. Sebastian watched her father press himself against the cliff face and edge sideways into the falling water, one hand flat on stone, his body making itself small in a way that twenty years of mountains had taught him and nothing else had. He disappeared into the white. Then Dekker. Then the two porters who had agreed to come this far — Sefu and the quiet young one called Bram, both of whom had gone pale when Amara described the entrance but had not refused, which Sebastian understood now was its own kind of courage in this country.

Then it was her turn.

The water hit her like a decision. Cold drove straight through her oilskin and found the bone beneath, and the roar closed over her head as if she'd been submerged rather than merely drenched, and for three steps she was blind and deaf and utterly alone inside the noise. Her hand slid on wet granite. She thought, absurdly, of the drafting table at the Cartwright trust offices, the smell of fixative, the way her supervisor had said *your father's name is a liability, Miss Thornbury, let's not put it on anything we care about* — and she gripped the rock harder and kept moving, because she had spent her whole life walking toward things people told her to avoid.

Then the sound changed. It didn't stop — it never stopped, it would stop for no one — but it moved behind her, became a wall at her back instead of a world around her, and she opened eyes she hadn't known she'd shut.

She was standing in a corridor of black basalt, dry as a held breath, lit by a gray-green glow that came from somewhere deeper in. The waterfall curtained the entrance behind her, throwing shifting rings of light across the floor like slow ripples in a pond. The air smelled of cold stone and old smoke and, faintly, of cedar.

"Oh," she said. It was all she could manage.

Her father stood a few paces in, water streaming off him, staring down the corridor with an expression she had never seen on his face. Not wonder. Something older than wonder. Recognition, maybe, or grief wearing recognition's clothes.

"You've been here," she said. It wasn't a question.

"No." Ivo's voice came out scraped. "But I mapped the door without knowing it. Twenty years ago. There's a bend in the river on my old survey sheet that doesn't make hydrological sense. I flagged it as an error." He laughed once, a terrible small sound. "It wasn't an error. It was a secret keeping itself."

* * *

They followed the glow.

The corridor descended in long shallow ramps — no stairs, Sebastian noted, no thresholds, nothing that would trip a person carrying something heavy — and the walls began to change. At first she took the marks for natural veining in the stone. Then the veining resolved into lines. Then the lines resolved into rivers.

Carved maps. The walls themselves were maps, incised into the basalt with a precision that made her cartographer's heart stutter and then ache. Rivers she knew — the Vell above all, running down the wall like a spine — rendered not as decoration but as argument: every tributary named, every confluence marked, every bend annotated in a script of tight, beautiful characters that flowed like water because, she realized with a jolt, they *were* water, the writing shaped by the same hand-shapes that drew the rivers, language and geography grown from the same root.

And the names. The names ran alongside every river in that flowing script, and beside them, smaller, someone had carved transliterations in colonial script — the angular alphabet of the survey schools, her alphabet — as if the original makers had anticipated that someday strangers would come who could read only one language, and had chosen, generously, to meet them halfway.

"They translated it for us," Sebastian whispered. "Why would they—"

"Because a map no one else can read protects nothing," Amara said behind her. Her voice was steadier in here, out of the wind, though her stick tapped the stone with each step. "A map is testimony. Testimony has to be legible to the court that will judge it. They carved for the future. They carved for *you*, girl."

Sebastian turned. In the green light Amara's face looked hewn, her wound-bound shoulder dark, her eyes burning with something Sebastian had seen only once before — in her own mirror, the night she'd signed the rescue contract.

"How far does it go?"

"Further than you can walk today," Amara said. "What Cartwright's people found — what they tried to take — is only the antechamber. Come."

* * *

The room under the waterfall opened all at once, the way a cathedral opens: not gradually but as a change in what space meant.

It was vast. The glow here came from veins of pale mineral threaded through the ceiling, a soft phosphorescence like moonlight trapped in stone, and beneath that light the floor rose in low terraces, and on the terraces lay the maps.

Thousands of them.

Bark-paper scrolls in lacquered tubes. Hide charts stretched on frames of black wood. Copper sheets etched and inked. Woven panels where rivers were threads and borders were absences — deliberate absences, gaps in the weave that said *no line belongs here*. Each piece sleeved, labeled, ordered. A library. An archive. A nation's memory, filed against forgetting.

Sebastian walked down the central aisle as if down the nave of a church, and no one spoke, because there was nothing speech could do here that silence couldn't do better.

She stopped at the nearest terrace and lifted one scroll from its tube with hands that shook despite herself. The bark paper was supple still, kept dry by the mountain's breath. She unrolled it across her palms.

It was a map of the valley they had just walked through. Her valley. The gorge, the river, the terraces — all of it rendered with a fidelity that made her own survey work look like a child's chalk drawing. But it was the annotations that undid her. Around the map's edges, in that flowing script with its generous colonial transliteration, ran lists: village names. Dates. Population counts. And beside each entry, in a different hand, a later annotation in the angular colonial alphabet alone:

*Removed under Treaty Line Adjustment, Year 12.*
*Removed.*
*Removed.*

Seven villages. She counted them twice. Seven villages that appeared on this map, that had existed on this land long enough to be mapped in loving detail — and that appeared on *no map she had ever drawn, studied, or archived.* Because the treaty had drawn its border straight through the valley, and everything on the far side of that line had been not destroyed but *unwritten*. Erased from the record so thoroughly that Sebastian, a professional keeper of records, had reached adulthood believing these valleys empty.

"They weren't hiding from us," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange in the vault, flattened by stone. "All this time, everyone said the tribes upriver were nomadic, transient, that there was no fixed population to count in the census. That's why the treaty called the land vacant. That's the legal basis — I've *read* the legal basis, it's in my training materials—"

"They weren't nomadic," Amara said. "They were documented. Documented here, where the surveyors' masters couldn't burn it. Every time the colonial office redrew the border, the nations on the wrong side of the new line came here and added their testimony. This room is four hundred years of saying *we exist* in the one language empires respect." She touched the terrace edge. "Geography."

Sebastian set the scroll down with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to crush it or clutch it and neither was worthy.

Then she saw the second set of labels, and the awe in her chest curdled so fast it burned.

Each sleeve bore a small tag in fresh ink — ink, not carving, ink within the last months — in a neat administrative hand she recognized instantly, because she had seen it on a thousand routing slips at the Cartwright trust. Catalog numbers. Cross-references. And beside each number, a notation in abbreviated code that any archivist would know on sight:

*DUP — copy held, national archive, series 44-C.*
*DUP — copy held, series 44-C.*
*DUP. 44-C. 44-C. 44-C.*

"What is this?" she asked, though she already knew. She could feel the knowledge arriving in her body before her mind consented to it, a cold spreading outward from her sternum.

Amara came to stand beside her. "Your Dr. Lenn catalogued them. Before the security chief sealed the room. He photographed everything, made his little tags. He was proud of the work." Her voice did not rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "He believed documentation would protect them. He believed the copies in your national archive were a safeguard."

"The national archive doesn't have these." Sebastian heard her own voice go thin and high. "Series 44-C is a restricted annex. Restricted means—" She stopped. Restricted meant sealed. Sealed meant accessible only by ministerial warrant. Sealed meant that somewhere in the capital, in the climate-controlled stacks she had been trained to revere, sat copies of every one of these maps — and had sat, presumably, for however long the copying had taken, while the government that held them continued to publish school atlases showing vacant land.

"They've known," she said. "They've always known. The archive isn't ignorant. The archive is *complicit*. It's not that the evidence was lost — it's that the evidence was filed. Filed is how you kill evidence. You don't burn it, you shelve it where no warrant will ever reach." She laughed, and hated the sound. "God. I used to dream about getting clearance to the restricted annexes. I thought it was the top of the profession. It's the bottom of the grave."

"Girl." Amara's hand closed on her arm, grip strong despite the wound. "Breathe. Fury is fuel. But unspent fury is just fire in a locked room, and right now you are the locked room."

Sebastian breathed. The air of the vault went into her lungs — cedar and stone and old smoke — and she looked down the length of the terrace, at the thousands of sleeves receding into the mineral light, each one a village, a name, a family, a *removed*, and she understood with perfect clarity what she had come here thinking was a rescue mission actually was.

Not a rescue. A retrieval. The expedition hadn't been lost. It had been *silenced*, and the silencing wasn't finished, and the men coming upriver behind them were not chasing survivors.

They were chasing the last unfiled originals.

* * *

She found her father at the far end of the room, standing before a section of the terrace that was different from the rest. Newer. The sleeves here were plain, unlacquered, their tags handwritten rather than typed. Recent additions. The testimony of the living.

Ivo was holding one scroll at arm's length, the way a man holds something he's afraid will bite him. As Sebastian approached, she saw the map it contained: the upper Vell, the region of his twenty-year-old survey. His own linework was on it — she recognized his hand the way you recognize a parent's handwriting on a school form, instantly and involuntarily — copied onto the bark paper by an indigenous mapper who had taken his colonial survey and corrected it, annotated it, *answered* it. Alongside his confident boundary lines ran the flowing script, and beside the script, in translation:

*The man who drew these lines walked our paths and ate our salt. Ask him why he did not ask what the lines would carry away.*

Ivo's face was gray. He set the scroll down as if returning a weapon.

"Ivo." She almost never used his given name. It landed between them like a dropped tool. "The tags. Series 44-C. Did you know? When you worked the survey — did you know there were copies in the capital?"

"No." He said it fast, too fast, and then slower. "No. I swear it on whatever's left of me. We were told the region was unmapped. We were told we were the first. That was the whole romance of it — being first. And we were so pleased with ourselves." His jaw worked. "But I'll tell you what I did know, because you deserve it before anyone else tells you. The villages were on my ground truth. I surveyed *from* them. I ate in them. And when the final sheets went to the ministry, the villages weren't on them, and I told myself the cartographers in the capital must have generalized them out. Standardization. Scale. I gave them every excuse a tired man gives." He looked at the scroll, at his own lines answered in another tongue. "They didn't generalize anything. They executed it. And I signed the sheets."

Sebastian should have said something. Some part of her was already composing the thing to say — the fair thing, the measured thing, the thing a good daughter and a good cartographer would say about systems larger than individuals. But standing in the room where the erased had spent four centuries writing themselves back into existence, she found she had no appetite for mercy built on abstraction.

"You're going to testify," she said. "When we get back. All of it. Under your own name."

"Yes," Ivo said. Simply. As if he'd been waiting twenty years for someone to ask him for something he could actually give.

* * *

They worked until the mineral light told them hours had passed, though none of them could have said how many. Amara directed; Sebastian catalogued; Dekker — watchful, competent, oddly subdued since the cache — carried and stacked where Amara pointed, asking careful questions about weight limits and waterproofing that sounded professional and sat slightly wrong, like a key cut one hair too narrow. Sebastian noticed and filed the noticing away, because there was no room in her now for anything but the work.

Because the work had become clear, and clarity was its own kind of fury.

She selected forty plates — the master testimony, the treaty-era maps, the ones whose absence from any archive would be fatal — and wrapped them in oilcloth against her back. The rest they could not carry and would not abandon, and Amara, reading her face, said quietly: "The room keeps itself. It has kept itself four hundred years. What it cannot survive is being *found* by the wrong people with orders. So we close the door behind us, and we make sure the wrong people follow us, not it."

As if summoned by the sentence, a sound came down the corridor from the direction of the falls — thin, mechanical, impossible to mistake even through the water-roar.

A radio. A voice. Dekker's pack, still sitting by the entrance where he'd shrugged it off to work.

Sebastian was closest. She crossed the antechamber in six strides and lifted the flap, and the voice resolved mid-sentence, clipped and calm and horribly familiar in its administrative blandness:

"—confirm visual on the falls by nightfall. Repeat, package is presumed intact. Do not damage the carrier. Vane wants the woman breathing. The rest are expendable. Acknowledge."

Static. Then, from behind her, Dekker's voice, easy as morning: "Everything alright, Miss Thornbury? Battery indicator's been acting up."

Sebastian did not turn around. Through the curtain of the falls, far downstream, she could hear — or imagined she could hear — engines.

"Acknowledge," she repeated softly, to no one, to the static, to the room full of names behind her. "Consider it acknowledged."
