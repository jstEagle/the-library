## 10. Loyalty's Ledger

The radio woke him before the birds did.

Ivo had slept, as he always did in the high country, with one ear open — an old habit, the kind that outlived its usefulness the way a scar outlives the wound. The camp lay in a fold of the riverbank where the Vell slowed to think, willows combing the water, mist rising off the current in slow white ribbons. Somewhere out in that mist, the transmitter was talking.

Not transmitting. Talking.

There was a difference, and every guide who'd ever worked a relay line knew it. A transmission had a shape — the burst of a scheduled check-in, the flat cadence of coordinates, the formal sign-off. This had pauses. This had a man's voice in it, low and careful, the voice of someone choosing words the way you choose footholds on rotten rock.

Ivo lay still in his bedroll and listened.

The mist muffled it. He caught fragments — *position*, *the woman*, *two days, maybe three* — and then the particular silence of a set being keyed off, the faint electric sigh of a circuit closing. After that, the river, and the birds starting up in the willows as if nothing had happened, and his own heart going hard against the ground like a fist knocking on a door he didn't want to open.

He knew that voice. He'd known it since the dock at Corren's Landing, since Dekker had come up the gangplank with his gear squared away and his smile squared away and his eyes doing neither.

*Two days, maybe three.* Three days to the gorge. Three days to the waterfall Amara wouldn't stop circling in her fever-talk, the room under the water, the room with no name. Someone on the far end of that transmission was being told exactly where to find them, and exactly when.

Ivo got up quietly, the way he'd gotten up quietly for twenty years, and stood at the edge of the willows with the cold coming off the river and looked at the shape of the problem the way he'd once looked at a cornice — knowing that the wrong weight in the wrong place would drop the whole slope.

Dekker slept by the fire ring, rolled in his coat, his rifle in reach the way a hired man's rifle always was. Close enough to seem prudent. Far enough to seem innocent.

Ivo watched him breathe and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the cache — the rifled boxes, the documents gone, the boot prints walking not away from trouble but toward it. It fit. It had fit for days, the way a bad route fits a map until you look closely enough to see the river doesn't actually bend that way. Dekker had volunteered for the rear guard on the portage. Dekker had been the last to see the cache before they found it opened. Dekker had asked Amara, twice, gently, in front of everyone, to repeat the name of the place she'd hidden — as if a man couldn't remember a name he'd already written down.

And now the mist had heard him selling their position to the dark.

* * *

He didn't confront him. Not that morning.

That was the decision, and it cost him more than he'd expected, standing there with the cold in his boots and the whole easy violence of the moment available to him — walk over, put a hand on the rifle, say the word *traitor* out loud and see what broke. He was strong enough. He'd been strong enough for worse things. That was the whole rotten ledger of his life: he had always been strong enough for the worst thing, and had too often mistaken that for permission.

But he looked at the fire ring and he counted what slept around it.

Sebastian, in the lee of the supply packs, her map case under her head like a pillow, her pencil still behind her ear from the night's work. He'd seen the light of her lantern burning past midnight, seen her hunched over the waterproof sheets copying Amara's fragments into something like order, and he had felt the old useless pride and the old useless grief arrive together, twins, the way they always did.

Amara, fevered still, her bandage needing changing, her voice a thread. She had maybe one hard march left in her, and the room under the waterfall was two hard marches away. She was the only living soul who knew the way in. She was, Ivo suspected, the only living soul who knew the whole truth of what Cartwright's expedition had found — and of what had been done to the ones who found it.

The two young crew, Ostrander and Pell, who had signed on for a rescue and were slowly learning they were in something older and colder than a rescue. Good kids. The kind who'd take sides fast and hard if a fight started, and die just as fast and hard on the wrong side of it.

And Dekker, who was strong and competent and had carried more than his share on the portages, who had bound Pell's blistered feet without being asked, who had laughed at Ivo's bad joke two nights ago with a laugh that had sounded — God help him — real.

That was the trap of it. That was what the ledger didn't show on the first page. Loyalty wasn't a thing you could test the way you tested a rope. You couldn't load it and watch it hold. You found out what it was made of at the moment it snapped, and by then somebody was already falling.

If he woke the camp and named Dekker a traitor with no proof but a voice in the mist, one of two things happened. Either they believed him and turned on a man who might, in the gray arithmetic of the mountains, have been reporting to someone he believed was the rescue's own paymaster — a man could talk to a head office without being a knife in the dark — or they didn't believe him, and then Ivo Thornbury was the disgraced guide again, the paranoid old man with the burned reputation, and the fragile thing holding this crew together — the thin, grudging, daily-won trust that had gotten them up two hundred kilometers of bad water — tore along its oldest seam. *Him.* It would always, first, be him.

And if Dekker was what Ivo thought he was, a public accusation bought them nothing but a fight at forty kilometers from help, with a wounded woman and a girl who'd already lost one father to this river country.

No. He'd been a guide long enough to know: you didn't cut a man loose on the rope in the middle of the pitch. You watched him. You re-rigged the anchors around him. And you got the people who mattered to the other side before the weather came.

*Weather was coming.* He could feel it the way he'd felt it twenty years ago, the day before the survey men burned the villages off their maps — a pressure behind the ears, a stillness in the birds.

* * *

The camp woke slow, the mist burning off in rags. Ivo made the fire and the porridge and said nothing, and watched Dekker eat, and watched Dekker watch Amara, and felt the watching wear a groove in him.

Sebastian caught him at it. Of course she did. She had his eye for terrain and none of his talent for pretending he didn't have it.

"You didn't sleep," she said, low, crouching by the fire with her tin cup. Not a question. She'd learned to read him the way he'd learned to read the river, and neither of them had ever once said so out loud.

"Old man's bones," he said. "They complain about the damp."

"You were up before the damp." Her eyes went, briefly, to Dekker, then back — and Ivo understood that she had heard something too, or half-heard it, the way you half-see a shape in fog, and had filed it where she filed everything, in that meticulous archive behind her eyes. His daughter. Twenty years gone and she was still the best mapmaker of her year, and she was reading him like a contour line.

"There's a bend in the river ahead," he said instead. "The charts have it wrong. I want to scout it before the boats go in."

"The charts don't have it wrong." A flicker of the old argument, the old electricity. "I corrected them at Kilometer Thirty. The bend is real, it's just shallower than the survey claimed—"

"The survey was mine," Ivo said. "And I fudged the depth."

She stopped. The fire cracked a willow log. Somewhere behind them, Dekker's voice rose in easy morning talk with Pell, and the ordinariness of it was almost unbearable.

"Why," Sebastian said, and it wasn't about the river anymore, and he knew it, and he let it stand there a moment like a man letting a bridge take weight before he crosses.

"Because a man who fudges one line will fudge another," Ivo said. "Check my soundings against the water yourself. Trust the river, not the map. Not the mapmaker." He met her eyes. "Especially not this one."

She looked at him a long time. He watched her decide — he could see the decision happening, the way he'd once watched her decide at six years old whether to cry or to be furious, and she had almost always chosen furious — and then she stood, and said, "I'll take the soundings," and went down to the boats with the survey line coiled over her shoulder, and he understood that she had heard everything he hadn't said, and had agreed to it, and would never admit either thing.

* * *

They made the bend by noon, and the bend was a lie, just as he'd told her — not on the chart, but in the world. The river shouldered hard against the south bluff there, and the current ran a standing wave that hadn't been there twenty years ago, a slide of green water the length of a church, and above it, on the bluff top, a place where the trees grew wrong. In a line. In a grid.

An old clearing. Cleared by hands, not flood.

Ivo stood in the bow with the spray coming off the wave and looked at the grid of trees and felt twenty years drop away like a pack shrugged off at the end of a long day. He had camped up there. He had eaten tinned mutton up there with men whose names he'd let the tribunal burn, and below him, across the water, there had been a village. There had been smoke from cooking fires and a dog barking at the survey party's boat and a woman beating a rug on a rail, patient, rhythmic, the way you beat a rug, the way you do an ordinary thing in a place you believe is yours.

The village wasn't there now. The map said it had never been there. The grid of young trees said: *something was erased here, and the earth is growing over the erasure, and the earth is slower than paper.*

"Guide?" Ostrander called from the second boat. "Do we run it or line it?"

"Line it," Ivo said. "Rope and walk her through. Nobody runs that wave."

Dekker, at the oars of Ivo's boat, said nothing, and did his work well, and Ivo watched his hands on the oar shafts — steady, competent, hands that had maybe passed a rifle to men who burned a cache and worse — and thought: *I don't know. That's the truth of it. I don't know, and I have to move as if I do.*

That was the ledger. That was what no one told you about loyalty: it wasn't a feeling you confirmed, it was a bet you placed, over and over, with incomplete information, and the interest was paid in sleep.

* * *

They lined the boats through the standing wave and camped that night in a hollow above the water, and Ivo made his second decision of the day, the harder one.

He changed the route.

He did it quietly, at dusk, walking the perimeter with Sebastian, pointing out anchor trees and water sources, and then, at the far side of the hollow, he took out his old route notes — the blacklisted ones, the ones he'd copied by hand in Corren's Landing the week the contract came — and showed her a line drawn in his younger ink.

"There's a game trail here," he said. "Leaves the river, cuts over the shoulder of the bluff, comes back to the water eight kilometers down. Steep, but it holds. It puts a full day between us and anyone following the river."

"Anyone following the river," Sebastian repeated. Not a question. She looked at the line on the page, and then at him, and he watched her do the same arithmetic he'd done in the mist at dawn, and arrive at the same terrible sum. "You think someone's coming."

"I think the cache was opened by people who took paper and left food," Ivo said. "I think paper is worth killing for, and food is not. I think we're carrying the last of that paper in Amara's head and your map case." He folded the notes away. "And I think we stop sleeping in a place where a radio can see the sky."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her want to ask *who* — watched the question come up in her like water in a well — and watched her stop herself, because she was her father's daughter and she knew that an accusation without proof was a hole in a boat. Instead she said: "The trail. Does it hold a loaded boat portage?"

"It holds anything. I carried a theodolite over it once." And a man on a stretcher, he didn't say. And a man who didn't make it. "We move at first light. Before light. And tonight—" he looked back at the camp, at the small figures by the fire, at Dekker's back, straight and loyal and unknowable — "tonight the transmitter sleeps in my pack. Relay discipline. We're in a reported dead zone anyway; no point burning the battery."

"Relay discipline," Sebastian said, and something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion of him, he was almost sure of that, almost — and she nodded once, and went to tell the crew.

* * *

He didn't sleep again. He sat up with his back against a stone and the transmitter in his lap like a sleeping animal he'd caught, and he watched the fire burn down, and he turned the ledger over and over in his hands.

Because here was the page he kept coming back to, the page with his own name on it. Twenty years ago, Ivo Thornbury had been a loyal man. Loyal to the survey, loyal to the paymaster, loyal to the men beside him — and his loyalty had been the mortar in a wall built to erase a nation. He had done every duty perfectly and it had added up to a crime, and the tribunal had seen it, and he had run, and the running had cost him everything except the one thing he'd tried to protect, which it had cost anyway, in the end, because Sebastian had grown up with a hole where a father was and had filled it with fury, and fury was a kind of loyalty too, loyalty to a wound.

So he knew what loyalty could build, and he knew what it could burn, and he sat in the dark with a traitor's radio in his lap and could not make himself certain of anything except this: the people by the fire deserved to reach the waterfall. Whatever Dekker was — knife, fool, or frightened man doing what he thought was duty to a paymaster he believed legitimate — the maps, and Amara, and the name of the nation under the water, those had to come through. Ivo had spent twenty years on the wrong side of that equation. He would not spend one more night there.

Toward morning, Dekker got up to check the boats, as he always did, and paused by the fire, and looked at Ivo, and Ivo looked back, and for a moment — one long moment, the mist moving on the river, the embers ticking — he thought: *say it. Say it now, man to man, give him one door out. A frightened man takes a door; a cornered man takes a knife.*

"Quiet night," Dekker said.

"They're all quiet nights," Ivo said, "until they aren't."

Dekker held his eyes a beat too long — or maybe not too long, maybe exactly the right length for an honest man, that was the poison of it, you could never calibrate it after — and then he smiled his squared-away smile and went down to the boats, and Ivo sat with the transmitter in his lap and understood that he had just watched a door close, or open, or both.

They broke camp in the dark and took the game trail up over the bluff's shoulder, boats on their shoulders too, the crew strung out along the steep line with Amara lashed in a litter between Ostrander and Pell, and the river dropped away below them into the fog it had come out of, and somewhere down that fog, Ivo knew, men were moving toward a camp that was already empty, guided by a voice in the mist that had, this once, told the truth about where they'd been.

It would not tell the truth about where they were going. Not unless its owner found another sky to talk to.

Ivo climbed, and counted heads, and did not let himself think about how many chances a man got to choose the right side of a ledger.

The answer, he was fairly sure, was one fewer than you'd already used.

Behind them, far down the trail, Dekker paused — just once, just for a breath, just long enough to look back the way they'd come — and then climbed on.
