## 11. The Turncoat's Bargain

The rain came at dusk, the way it had come every dusk since the gorge, as though the sky were emptying itself of something it could not bear to keep.

Ivo sat beneath the overhang of the rock they'd named — privately, without ceremony — the Watch Stone, because from its shoulder you could see a full kilometer of the river's bend, and because someone had to watch. He had taken the first shift without being asked. He always took the first shift. Sleep was a country he'd lost his papers for, and he'd stopped trying to cross back.

Below him the Vell ran high and brown, carrying the rain's freight down toward the lowlands, toward the tribunals and the telegraph offices and the world that had already decided what this river was. He could hear it working at its stones. It had been working at them for ten thousand years and would go on working at them long after everyone in this expedition was a stain of memory on someone else's chart. There was a strange comfort in that. The river did not know his name. The river had never blacklisted him.

Behind him, up the slope, the camp was a scatter of dim shapes: the tarp strung between two dead cedars, the cold firepit because smoke drew eyes and eyes drew bullets, Amara's fevered breathing from the driest corner where they'd laid her, the soft clink of Sebastian cleaning the lens of her theodolite with a cloth she kept for that purpose alone. He could pick out each sound the way a deaf man learns to pick out faces. He had been cataloguing his daughter's sounds for eleven days now — the scrape of her pencil, the double-tap she gave her boot heels before standing, the small exhale through her nose when she caught an error on one of his old charts and said nothing, said nothing, said nothing — and the cataloguing had become, without his permission, the most attentive act of his life.

He heard Dekker's boots on the scree before he saw the man. Dekker walked quietly for a big man; he'd learned it somewhere Ivo didn't want to imagine. But scree has its own opinions, and the slope told Ivo everything: one set of feet, unhurried, coming alone.

"Your shift's not for an hour," Ivo said, without turning.

"I couldn't sleep." Dekker settled onto the stone beside him, leaving a careful hand's width of space. He produced a tin of tea leaves from his jacket, and then, with the small flourish of a man performing generosity, a twist of real sugar wrapped in waxed paper. Sugar. On the Vell. Ivo hadn't tasted sugar in nine days, and his body betrayed him with a low, animal attention.

Dekker brewed the tea in a battered cup over the spirit burner they kept for the altimeter, and the smell of it rose sweet and absurd into the rain-smell, and Ivo understood, in the way one understands a trap only after stepping to the edge of it, that this was not a chance conversation.

They drank in silence for a while. The river worked its stones.

"Your daughter's good," Dekker said at last. "Better than good. The way she copied those plates from memory in the boat — I've been on six expeditions, Thornbury, and I've never seen a mind like that. She'll be famous. She should be famous." He turned the cup in his broad hands. "And Osei. You know she nearly died getting to that cave? Walked eleven kilometers on a leg that was wrong in a way legs aren't supposed to be wrong. There's a kind of courage in this camp that I've only read about in dispatches."

"You're stalling," Ivo said.

Dekker smiled, and it was a good smile, an honest-looking smile, the kind that had been built in some office in the lowlands by people whose job was building smiles. "I'm gathering courage. It's different." He set the cup down on the stone between them, precisely, like a piece on a board. "I have a message. From a man named Vane."

The name landed in Ivo's chest the way a stone lands in still water. He had heard it first from Cartwright's own mouth, weeks ago now, or a lifetime ago — *the money behind the money* — and then from Amara's fevered riddles, and each time it had arrived with the same cold signature, the same sense of a door opening somewhere far below the waterline.

"I'm listening," Ivo said, and hated how steady his voice came out.

"Vane knows where you are. Has known for two days. He has men at the Lower Confluence and men at the gorge mouth, and a launch on the river that this rain makes invisible until it's too late to be invisible." Dekker said it flatly, a man reading coordinates. "He doesn't want blood. Blood is expensive and it makes paperwork. He wants the plates. He wants the room under the waterfall to become a story that got exaggerated, a rumor that got out of hand, and he wants everyone who saw it to come home and spend the rest of their lives knowing that no one would print what they saw." He paused. "Everyone except one."

The rain thickened. Ivo watched it stitch itself into the river.

"Osei," he said.

"Osei," Dekker agreed. "She stays behind. She's wounded, Thornbury — you've seen the leg, you've heard the fever. She *cannot* make the descent at speed, and you know it. Vane's men will find a sick woman in a cave, or she'll find her own way downriver in her own time, and either way she'll be *someone else's* story, not yours. Your hands stay clean. That's the whole architecture of the offer. You don't have to do anything. You just have to stop doing one thing."

"And in exchange."

Dekker reached into his jacket and brought out a folded paper, oilcloth-wrapped, dry as a sermon. He didn't hand it over; he laid it on the stone, next to the cup, and opened it so the firelight from the spirit burner caught the first lines. Ivo read them. He read them twice. His eyes, which had spent forty years reading ridgelines and river-gradients and the small lying handwriting of official surveys, read the document the way it wanted to be read, quickly, hungrily, and then read it again the way a man reads a thing he cannot believe.

*In consideration of services rendered to the security of the realm, the tribunal of 19—, in the matter of Thornbury, I., is hereby vacated. All restrictions on professional licensure are lifted. The record is sealed. The record is —*

*The record is clean.*

Twenty years. Twenty years of doors closing in the lowlands, of contracts withdrawn with apologetic letters, of his name spoken in guild halls the way one speaks of a stain on good wood. Twenty years of radio-relay contracts and survey work nobody else wanted, of being the best guide in three provinces and being unable to guide anyone anywhere that mattered. Twenty years of watching Sebastian grow up through the wrong end of a telescope, believing — needing to believe — that the distance was the price and that the price had bought her something. A clean name by proxy. A father-shaped absence that was, in its way, a kind of protection.

And here it was, on one page, in one signature, for the price of one silence.

"They'll lift the blacklist," Dekker said quietly. "Not quietly — *loudly*. A formal restoration. Your name in the guild registry with the word *exonerated* where the word *barred* has been sitting for two decades. Vane can do that with a telephone call. He's done it for worse men." He looked out at the river, and for one unguarded moment his face was tired in a way that seemed almost real. "And Sebastian. You know what her name is worth in the cartographers' guild with a blacklisted father. You've watched her fight that current her whole career. This doesn't just clean your record. It cleans hers. Every door that's been locked to her because of you — it opens. She gets the appointments, the commissions, the archive access. She gets to be the cartographer she already is, instead of the cartographer they let her be."

*That* was the knife, and Ivo felt it go in with an awful, intimate precision, because Dekker — or the man behind Dekker, the man with the telephone — had understood something about Ivo Thornbury that Ivo had never said aloud to anyone, perhaps never fully admitted to himself: that his own ruin he could have carried forever, the way a mule carries a pack, dumb and enduring. But hers. Her whole life bent around the shape of his disgrace, her prospects amputated at the root by a scandal she hadn't committed, and she had signed onto this expedition — he knew it, he'd known it from the dock — partly to prove that she could out-map the man who'd ruined them both. To prove him wrong. To make his name a footnote under hers instead of the other way around.

And he could give her that. Tonight. With a silence.

The river worked its stones. Somewhere up the slope, Sebastian's pencil stopped, and started again.

"Say it plainly," Ivo said. "What exactly do I do?"

"Nothing heroic. Nothing monstrous. The plates come down the river in your pack, and Osei stays at the cave with the supplies we'll 'leave' her — a genuine field cache, Thornbury, real food, real medicine, more than she'd have with us. We report that she chose to stay. She *did* choose to stay, in a sense; she'll have the choice, and the means. And in six months, when the tribunal's done and the vault is a locked room in a locked ministry, Vane's lawyers will find a way for her to come home too." Dekker's voice softened, and here — this was the masterwork, this was the part that had been built in that lowlands office by people who understood men — here it became almost gentle. "Nobody dies. That's the whole offer. You've lost two crew already. You'll lose more if you go on. Every kilometer downriver is a kilometer of men with rifles. You can trade one wounded woman's delay for six lives, including your daughter's. Any guide worth his salt does that arithmetic on a mountain every season. You've done it. I've watched you do it."

It was true. That was the unbearable part. It was *true*. Every expedition was a ledger of acceptable losses, every route a negotiation with the mountain about who came home, and the good guide — the guide Ivo had been, the guide the blacklisting had never managed to erase from his bones — the good guide made the cold call and slept, or didn't sleep, but *led*. Leave the slow one at the high camp with the tent and the stove and come back for them. Come back for them. Except the mountain had never once, in forty years, offered him a signature in exchange for leaving the slow one behind. The mountain only ever took. It was men who had learned to *pay*.

He looked at the paper again. The firelight moved on the words and they did not change. *Vacated. Sealed. Clean.*

He thought about the window. He had never told anyone about the window — not Dekker, not the tribunal, not the woman he'd briefly been married to before the blacklisting finished that too. Nineteen years ago, or twenty, in the lowland town whose name he did not say anymore, he had stood in the street outside a house he no longer had the right to enter, in the blue hour, and looked up at a lit window, and seen his daughter — five years old, six, hair in a wayward plait — bent over a table, drawing. Drawing lines. Drawing a map of something, a map of the garden or the street or the world, a small fierce cartographer already at work, and he had stood in the cold until his feet went numb and told himself: *she's better off. A criminal's name is a weight. She'll carry the map instead.* And he had turned and walked to the river and taken the first contract that would take him far away, and he had called that sacrifice for twenty years, and some nights, in tents exactly like this one, he had almost believed it.

If he said yes now, the window was the last thing he'd ever be able to look through. The clean name would be a clean window, and behind it a daughter who never had to know what it cost, and he would spend the rest of his life — a long life, a comfortable life, a *licensed* life — knowing that the map she drew had been drawn on paper he had bought with a woman's fever in a cave.

He looked at the cup of tea. Sweet. Real sugar. He thought: *they knew I'd want the sugar. They knew about the sugar.*

"Dekker," he said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, roughened by the rain and the altitude and the twenty years. "The man who was on my expedition. The one the porters wouldn't look at. You knew him."

Something flickered across the big man's face. "Kesu. Yes."

"He carried the survey maps. The ones they used to move the villages. He carried them because I drew them and he trusted me." Ivo turned and looked at Dekker directly, and let him see whatever was in his face, all of it, the whole drowned country of it. "They burned his village with the maps' coordinates. He survived. He's upriver right now, carrying our loads, and he doesn't know what I'm being offered, and if I take it, he never will. Because the offer doesn't have a line for him. There's no *vacated* and *sealed* for a man whose nation is the thing being erased." He picked up the paper. It was light. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. "You want to know the arithmetic I do on a mountain, Dekker? It's not who's slowest. It's who's been made slowest, and by whom, and whether the man making the offer has ever once been the one left at the high camp."

Dekker was quiet for a long moment. The rain filled the silence, patient, professional.

"You're going to say no," he said. Not accusing. Almost — and this was the terrible thing, this was the thing Ivo would remember — almost with relief.

"I'm going to say no."

"Then say it fast," Dekker said, and stood, and his silhouette against the grey river went suddenly, unmistakably heavy, the weight of a man carrying something he hadn't carried when he sat down. "Because Vane's men move at first light either way. And Thornbury — the launch on the river, the men at the confluence. That part wasn't leverage. That part was a schedule."

He turned to go, and Ivo caught his arm, and for a moment the two of them stood in the rain with the sweet tea going cold between them, and Ivo said, very quietly, "How long have you been carrying it? The other message. The one you're not being paid to deliver."

Dekker didn't answer. But his hand, briefly, pressed Ivo's arm — once, hard, like a man signaling on a rope in bad weather — and then he was gone down the scree, quiet as he'd come, and the slope told Ivo nothing at all.

Below, the river worked its stones. Above, on the slope, his daughter's pencil had stopped.

And this time it did not start again.
