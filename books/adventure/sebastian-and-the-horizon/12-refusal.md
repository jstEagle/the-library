## 12. Refusal

Sebastian had not meant to overhear. That was the truth she would carry afterward, the small clean stone she set against everything else that was muddy and shameful about that night: she had not meant to.

The camp sat in a fold of the gorge where the river went quiet, as if it too were listening. They had made it through the waterfall room and out again with the plates wrapped in oilcloth and their hearts still hammering from Amara's warnings, and now they were three days from anywhere, camped beneath a leaning shelf of granite that held the warmth of the dead fire like a hand held over a candle. Sebastian had taken the middle watch because sleep had become a country she could no longer enter. She had been walking the perimeter with her boots unlaced so the leather wouldn't squeak against the scree — an old trick, her father's trick, learned from his route notes when she was nineteen and reading them like scripture of a man she hated — when she saw the faint blue pulse of Dekker's radio screen through the gap between two boulders.

He was crouched with his back to her, and he was speaking in a low, careful voice, the voice of a man rehearsing.

"—position confirmed. They have the master plates and at least forty of the loose maps. Osei is with them. Cartwright's location still unknown." A pause. The river filled it, patient and enormous. "No. No, he won't give them up without a reason. That's why I'm asking for the reason. What exactly are you offering him?"

Sebastian stood very still in the cold. The stars above the gorge were hard and close, the stars of altitude, and she felt them pressing down on her like witnesses.

*They have the master plates.* She thought first of Amara, asleep ten feet away with a bandage on her ribs and a knife under her hand even in sleep, because Amara slept the way soldiers sleep now. Then she thought of her father, in his bedroll at the far edge of camp, closer to the drop, because — she understood this only recently — he always put himself between the people he loved and the edge, and had done it so long ago and so completely that he had walked off one entirely.

She should have gone back. She should have woken Ivo. Instead she stayed, crouched in the shadow of the boulder with her heart going like a bird trapped in a room, because some part of her — the cartographer's part, the part that believed every truth was better than a merciful blank space — needed to hear the shape of the betrayal entire.

* * *

Dekker made his approach at dawn.

That was the thing she would remember: he did it in the open, in the gray-gold light with the river running gold below them and the mist rising off the water in columns, and he did it smiling, and he did it well. He waited until the fire was rebuilt and Amara had taken her tea and the camp had that fragile morning peace that Sebastian had learned, on this river, to distrust more than any storm.

"Ivo," Dekker said, crouching by the fire across from her father, his voice pitched for the whole camp to hear, which was itself a kind of weapon. "You and I should talk. Everyone can stay. I'd rather they did, actually. I'm tired of the dark in this business."

Ivo looked up from the map he was annotating — Sebastian's map, her corrections, his old route notes, the two hands merged now into a single document neither of them had remarked upon — and Sebastian watched her father's face do something she had never seen it do. It went completely open. Not surprised. *Ready.* As if some part of him had been waiting for this since the dock, since the gorge, since twenty years ago.

"You've been transmitting," Ivo said. Not a question.

"Every night since the trading post." Dekker spread his hands. "And here's the thing, Ivo — I'm not sorry, and I'm not going to pretend I am, because you've always been able to smell a lie on a man and I'd rather have your anger than your pity. I work for Vane. Have for six years. Good pay, clean ledgers, and until this river I never had to think about what the money bought." He glanced at Amara, and Sebastian saw the flicker there, the human being under the turncoat, quickly suppressed. "But now I know what it bought. I've seen the room under the waterfall. I've held maps older than my country."

"Then you know what you're selling," Amara said quietly.

"I know what I'm *offering*," Dekker said, and turned back to Ivo. "Here it is, in the open, in front of everyone. Vane will lift the blacklist. Not quietly — publicly. Full restoration. Your name in the register again, your license, your standing. The tribunal charges from the Meridian survey — sealed, expunged. Twenty years of being *Ivo Thornbury, the man who mapped villages into graves* — gone. You walk back into the world a free man. And all you have to do is bring me the plates, and when we reach the lower gorge, let Osei stay behind."

The words dropped into the camp like stones into still water.

Sebastian watched her father. She had spent her whole life watching him, in photographs before she ever met him, in the flesh for eleven days now, and she had catalogued his face the way she catalogued coastlines — the set of his jaw when he was wrong, the stillness of his hands when he was frightened, the way his eyes went to the horizon when he didn't want anyone reading them. She had thought she knew every contour.

She had never seen him *tempted*.

It lasted perhaps four seconds. But it was there — she would swear it on every map she ever drew — a flicker in his eyes like light on moving water, a hunger so old it had gone feral. Twenty years. Twenty years of a name that opened no doors, of contracts taken under other men's licenses, of a daughter who had learned his face from a file photograph. Four seconds in which the man who had carried his guilt like ballast across two decades of wilderness looked at the possibility of setting it down.

And then she watched him close his hand around it and put it away, and she understood that this — this four-second war, fought in the open and lost by the better self — was the bravest thing she would ever see him do.

"No," Ivo said.

"Ivo—"

"No." He stood, slowly, his knees cracking the way they had cracked for eleven days, and he looked at Dekker without hatred, which was somehow worse. "You came to me with the only currency I've ever wanted, and you did it in front of witnesses, and I'm going to answer in front of witnesses so there's no version of this where I said yes and regretted it later. The plates go to the tribunal. The maps go to the people they were taken from. And Amara Osei walks out of this gorge on her own feet, or none of us do." He paused. "You should know the offer was good, Dekker. That's the filthiest part. It would have worked on me a year ago. Maybe a month ago."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen what's under the waterfall." Ivo's voice dropped, and the river seemed to lean in to hear it. "And now my daughter is watching."

Dekker's gaze flicked to Sebastian — one heartbeat of naked calculation, a man weighing whether she was leverage or liability — and then he stood, and the smile came off his face like a mask coming off, and underneath it was just a tired man of forty-odd years standing in the cold with his hands in his pockets.

"I'm sorry," he said, and the terrible thing was that he sounded like he meant it. "I actually am. But I've got a brother who does the books for Vane, and a niece whose school exists because of Vane's money, and if I come back empty-handed, all of that goes into the gorge with us. You think you're the only one who's sold something to survive?" He looked around the camp, at each of them, and Sebastian saw that he had already decided, had probably decided last night with the radio glowing blue against his jaw. "The copies are already made. I took them while you slept. I'm taking the downstream route — the one on the girl's chart, the portage past the second cataract. Don't follow me. Vane's men are at the confluence by tomorrow, and they're not negotiators."

He shouldered his pack. At the edge of camp he stopped, and turned back, and said to Ivo, almost gently: "For what it's worth — I hoped you'd say no. I think some part of me needed one man on this river who would."

Then he was gone, down through the mist, and the sound of his boots on the scree faded, and the river took the silence back.

* * *

They spent the morning preparing to move — breaking camp, waterproofing the plates, arguing in low voices about whether to pursue Dekker or outrun him. Sebastian worked with her hands and let her mind go somewhere cold and organized, because there was a thing she had to do and she could not do it while thinking.

She found her father at the gorge edge, checking the portage line against her chart. The morning light came up the river and lit the gray in his beard, and he looked, for a moment, exactly like the photograph from the file she had studied at nineteen — the man who had ruined her family's name before she was old enough to understand what a name was for.

"I overheard him," she said. "Last night. On the radio."

"I know," Ivo said. "You were gone from your bedroll. I checked."

Of course he had checked. Of course he had said nothing, had let Dekker make his play in the open, had stood there with his whole history hanging in the balance while his daughter hid behind a rock listening to a man sell him back his life. She felt the fury rise in her, the old fury, the one that had carried her through the cartography program and onto this river and into this gorge — and then it crested, and broke, and what was underneath it was not fury at all.

"Why didn't you come back?"

Ivo went still. Below them the river slid past, brown and gold and utterly indifferent.

"I was eleven," Sebastian said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she had expected it to shatter. "You left when I was eleven, and I want to be fair, I've spent twenty years being fair to you in my head, building the case for the defense. The scandal. The blacklist. A man has to disappear, fine. A man has to run. But you didn't run, Ivo. You *stayed*. You stayed in the same country, in the same cities, within a week's travel of a house where I was growing up without a father, and you never came. Not once. Not to the door. Not to a window. Not to leave a letter. Twenty years, and I found out what happened to you from a *deposition transcript* I pulled for a university assignment." She heard her voice rise and made herself lower it. "I'm not asking why you left. I've stopped asking why you left. I'm asking why you never came *back*."

The wind came up the gorge and moved the mist in slow banners. Ivo did not look at her. He looked at the river, and his hands — the steady hands, the hands that read rapids and rope and rock — were not steady at all.

"Seb—"

"Don't," she said. "Don't give me the version you'd give the tribunal. I'm not the tribunal. I'm the eleven-year-old, and she's been waiting twenty years, and you owe *her* the answer."

And he turned and looked at her then, fully, the way he looked at a river he was about to take them down — reading it, measuring it, knowing it was going to hurt — and he sat down slowly on the rock, and he said:

"Because I did come back."

The words fell into her like a stone into deep water, and the ripples went out and out and out.

"Eighteen months after I left," he said. "The charges were pending. I'd been living out of a pack, sleeping in stations, telling myself I'd send for you when I had a name worth sending for. And one night in the third winter I couldn't do it anymore. I took the night train and then the bus and then I walked the last four miles in the rain, and I stood across the street from the house on Alder Lane at about two in the morning." His voice was very quiet now, and very level, the levelness of a man holding something heavy with straight arms. "And the kitchen light was on. And through the window I could see you. You'd fallen asleep at the table over your schoolwork — you did that even then, your mother told me, you'd fight sleep like it owed you money — and there was a lamp on and your books were open and your mother came in and put a blanket over your shoulders, and she didn't wake you, she just — stood there a minute, with her hand on your hair."

He stopped. Sebastian realized she was gripping the chart tube so hard her knuckles had gone white, and made herself let go.

"And I stood in the rain and I understood," Ivo said. "I understood that everything I'd touched was poisoned. The survey, the villages, the maps — and now the name. *Thornbury.* If I walked through that door, I wasn't bringing a father home. I was bringing a man the newspapers called a murderer into a house with a child in it. Every friend you'd ever make, every school that would take you, every job you'd ever apply for — I'd be standing behind all of it like a stain on the glass." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she had ever heard from him, dry and broken. "So I stood there and I watched you sleep, and I made a bargain with myself. I told myself: *she's better off. She's better off with a dead father than a disgraced one.* And then I walked back to the road, and I let the rain have me, and I never went back. Because I knew — I *knew* — that if I ever saw your face, I wouldn't have the strength to leave again. And I was right. Look at me. Eleven days on this river and I'd burn the world down before I let anything happen to you. I was right, Sebastian. That's the whole confession. I was right, and it was the cruelest thing I've ever done, and I did it to you, and I told myself it was love."

The river ran. The mist rose. Somewhere above them a bird called once, twice, and fell silent.

Sebastian stood there with twenty years rearranging themselves inside her — the file photograph, the empty chair, the deposition transcript, the route notes she had read at nineteen with a lamp burning and tears she had told herself were anger. All of it the same night. All of it a man standing in the rain on Alder Lane, watching a lamp in a window, choosing the cruelest thing because it was also the loving one.

"You *idiot*," she said, and her voice finally broke, and she didn't care. "You absolute — we would have *survived* it. We would have been poor and ashamed and we would have *survived* it, because we'd have had each other, and instead I got a photograph and you got a window, and twenty years, twenty *years*, Ivo—"

"I know."

"You don't *know*—"

"I know," he said, and now his voice broke too, and she had never heard it, not once, not in eleven days of rapids and ambush and the room under the waterfall, and hearing it was like hearing the river change its mind. "I know exactly what it cost. I've carried the bill for twenty years. I just never had the courage to read it out loud."

She crossed the distance between them — three steps, the shortest distance she had ever traveled and the longest — and she did not embrace him, not quite, not yet; she sat down beside him on the cold rock, shoulder to shoulder, the way porters sit at a fire, the way travelers sit who have a long way still to go. Below them the river went on toward the confluence, toward Vane's men, toward whatever the day held.

"I'm still angry," she said at last.

"Good," Ivo said. "Anger's a good chart. It tells you where the rocks are."

"But you came back for the maps," she said. "Twenty years later. You came back for *this*."

"I came back because they asked for a guide who knew the gorge," he said, "and I was the only one willing to go, and I told myself it was about the maps too. But it wasn't. It was about the window. I've been trying to get back to that window for twenty years, Seb. This river just happened to be the road."

She looked at him then, really looked, and for the first time since the dock — since the dock, since her whole life — she saw not the man who had left, but the man who had stood in the rain. And she leaned her shoulder into his, and he did not pull away, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the river said it for them.

* * *

It was Amara who broke the silence, coming down from the high ground with her field glasses still in her hand and her face gone the color of the granite.

"You need to see this," she said. "Downriver. At the bend."

Sebastian took the glasses and put them to her eyes, and the world leapt close, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the gorge.

At the far bend of the river, where the water narrowed between black walls of rock, boats were coming up. Four of them, long and low and gray, riding deep with men and equipment, moving against the current with the patient, unhurried discipline of a force that has never once been in doubt. At the lead boat's prow, catching the morning light, something glinted — a badge, a rifle barrel, she couldn't tell, and it didn't matter.

"They're a day early," Amara said quietly. "Vane isn't waiting at the confluence."

Ivo stood, and took the glasses, and looked for a long moment. When he lowered them his face had gone still and clear, the face of a man reading a river he knows is going to try to kill him.

"Then we don't go to the confluence," he said. "We go *over* it. There's a route — cliff line, above the falls, no map records it because I never put it on paper. I was the last person to walk it, twenty years ago, and I swore I never would again." He looked at Sebastian, and something passed between them that was not forgiveness, not yet, but was its first cousin. "Get the plates packed. We move in ten minutes."

Above the far bend, faint and getting fainter, the sound of engines came up the gorge like a held breath.
