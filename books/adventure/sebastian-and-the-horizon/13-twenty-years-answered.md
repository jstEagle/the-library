## 13. Twenty Years Answered

The fire had burned down to its red arithmetic — a handful of coals, a language of light that said *we are still here* to anything watching from the dark. Dekker was on first watch upriver, his silhouette folded against a boulder like a man pretending to be stone. The others slept in the shapes exhaustion makes of people: knees drawn up, hands tucked, faces turned away from the wind that came off the gorge carrying the smell of wet granite and, faintly, of the waterfall they had left behind that morning — the room under the world, still humming in Ivo's memory like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Sebastian sat across the coals from him. She had not slept. He could tell by the way she held her pencil — not drawing, just turning it, end over end, the way she had turned it as a girl at the kitchen table while he pretended to read and actually watched her, memorizing her the way he memorized ridgelines, against the day he would need to find his way back.

She had asked the question at dusk. *Why didn't you come back?* And he had said, *Not by the fire. Not with them listening.* Now the fire was low, the camp was breathing, and there was no longer anywhere to hide the answer. There never had been, really. He had been carrying it for twenty years in a pack that weighed nothing and everything, and his shoulders had finally begun to fail under it.

"Seb," he said. Her name in his mouth still felt like a word in a language he'd half forgotten — he knew the shape of it, the weight of it, but his accent had gone strange.

"Don't." The pencil stopped turning. "Don't soften it. Don't do the thing you do where you make the mountain the villain. Just tell me."

So he told her.

* * *

"I came back," he said. "That's the part no one believes, because the record says I ran, and the record is right in every way that matters except the one you're asking about."

The coals ticked as they settled. Somewhere below the ledge, the Vell muttered over stones, patient as a debt.

"After the survey, after the tribunal — I didn't run right away. That's the first lie everyone tells about me, even me. I stayed in the capital for eleven days. Long enough to watch my name go from a byline to a cautionary tale. *The guide who mapped the villages out of existence.* They had it half right. I mapped the region. I drew the watersheds, the passes, the settlements — every hearth-smoke plume I could see from the high country, every footpath, every field. I drew them *because* they were there. Because a map that leaves people off is a broken map, and I was proud of mine. I was proud." He heard his own voice go flat and strange, the voice he used for reporting bad ground. "I handed my plates to the ministry and I thought: *there. Now they can't pretend this country is empty.* I thought I'd done a good thing. It took me four months to understand what I'd actually done. I'd drawn them a shopping list."

Sebastian didn't move. The firelight moved on her instead, and he watched it, because watching her was the only way he could keep talking.

"The border commission used my sheets to fix the line. Not around the villages — *through* them. Through the middle of the Kel-Ashen country, so that half of every family woke up foreign. And then the second thing happened, the thing they don't teach in the survey schools. The villages that fell on the wrong side of the line stopped appearing in the next edition. Not moved. *Removed.* My own cartography, revised by men who had never walked a step of it, until the country I had drawn was a country with a hole in it, and the hole had my name on the title block."

"You could have fought it," she said. Not cruel. Just precise, the way she was precise with a scale bar. "You could have testified."

"I did testify." The laugh that came out of him had no humor in it anywhere; it was just a sound his chest made. "I stood in that tribunal room for six hours. And I watched them take my testimony and file it in the same drawer as my plates, and I understood that in that room I was not a witness. I was an exhibit. The survey needed a man to blame, and I had done the walking, so I was the man. Vane's people — he was younger then, but the money was already his money — they made it very clear what happened to surveyors who kept talking. Two of the porters from my crew had already lost their ferry licenses. One lost more than that. So I did the arithmetic every coward does. I called it survival and I signed the confession they'd drafted for me, and the confession said I had falsified the settlements to inflate my fee, which was a lie so ugly I can still taste the ink."

"And then you left."

"And then I left." He looked at his hands, turned them over. A guide's hands, scarred rope-burn and old frostbite, hands that had held her once and then not for twenty years. "I went north first. Worked the freight lines, the winter roads. Anywhere a man's name was worth nothing so long as his rope work was good. And I told myself I'd send for you when it was safe. I want you to know that I told myself that. That I *believed* it. That every night for two years I composed letters in my head — long ones, good ones, letters that explained everything in the right order so that you'd understand — and every morning I burned them by daylight, because daylight showed me what they were. They weren't letters. They were alibis."

* * *

The wind shifted. Downriver, Dekker's silhouette turned his head, and Ivo lowered his voice without deciding to — the old reflex of a man used to speaking around listeners. He watched Sebastian watch him do it, and saw her catalog it, and knew she was filing it beside the boot prints and the rifled cache and every other small wrongness of the last week. His daughter missed nothing. She never had. That was his doing too, though she'd earned it herself a hundred times over.

"Third winter," he said. "I was working a relay station above the Kessel headwaters. Bad winter. I hadn't spoken to another person in five weeks except over the wire. And I broke. Not dramatically — that's the thing about breaking, Seb, it's mostly quiet. I just got on a train. Rode it three days. Walked the last six miles from the junction because I didn't want to arrive in anything with wheels, didn't want witnesses, didn't want the town to see me and start its talking. I walked in the dark and I knew every step of it, because I had walked it a thousand times in my head. Past the tannery. Past the school. Up the hill."

He stopped. The coals had gone from red to a deep ember-orange, the color of the last light on a summit ridge, the color that means the day is over whether you reached anything or not.

"Your mother's house. The blue one, with the gate that stuck. I stood across the lane by the wall — the wall with the loose capstone you used to sit on — and I told myself I would knock. I stood there long enough for the frost to come up through my boots. And then a lamp came on in the front room."

Sebastian's breath went out of her, a small sound, involuntary.

"You," he said. "It was you. You'd have been — eleven. Eleven, twelve. You were at the table with your books spread out everywhere, the way you do, the whole table conquered, and you had a lamp pulled down close and you were drawing. I could see that even from the lane, even through the glass. Not homework. Drawing. Maps. You had your chin down and your tongue out the corner of your mouth — you still do that, did you know that? You still do exactly that — and you were drawing maps of a place that I recognized, because it was a place I had drawn. You had found my old field sheets, or copies of them, or you were working from memory of the ones I'd left on the kitchen table that last year, and you were *correcting* them. I could see it. Eleven years old, and you had your pencil out, and you were making my maps better."

His voice went, and he let it. There was no shame left in the letting. He had spent twenty years being ashamed of everything; this one thing he would not be ashamed of.

"And I stood there in the frost and I understood — it came into me whole, the way weather comes over a col — that everything I had run from was going to arrive at that window anyway. The name. The tribunal. The men Vane kept on retainer. The story that would follow you into every schoolroom and every hiring hall: *daughter of the guide who sold the villages.* I had spent two years telling myself I was keeping you clean by staying gone. And there you were at the window, already drawing the country they'd erased, already better at it than I'd ever been, and I saw that the only inheritance I had left to give you was the one thing I could still control — my absence. That if I knocked on that door, I gave you my whole name, all of it, the weight of it, and you'd carry it the rest of your life. And if I turned around, you'd carry only your own."

"That wasn't your choice to make." She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "You decided for me. You looked through a window and decided what my whole life would weigh."

"Yes." He did not dress it up. She deserved one thing in her life from him that came without varnish. "It was the worst decision I ever made, and I made it out of love, and I have spent twenty years learning that love is not a defense. It's just a weight. It goes in the pack with everything else."

* * *

"You watched for how long?" she asked, after a while. The fire had gone down to almost nothing. He fed it a wrist-thick stick of the dry pine they'd carried up from the cache, and the light jumped, and for a moment her face was fully lit and he saw that she was crying — silently, furiously, the tears coming down without her permission and without her making a single sound, because she had learned to grieve the way he had taught her to walk a scree slope: quietly, weight committed, no wasted motion.

"Three nights," he said. "I slept in the tannery yard. I told myself one more night, one more night, I'll knock tomorrow. On the third night there was a man's coat on the chair by the window, and a second cup on the table, and I understood that the world had gone on, that it had every right to, that I had forfeited the right to be the second cup." He looked at the fire. "I walked out before dawn. And I never went back, because going back a second time would have meant admitting the first time had been a choice. Do you understand? Every year I didn't come back, the not-coming-back got heavier, until it was a mountain of its own, and you can't climb a mountain you've spent twenty years telling yourself isn't there."

"I waited." Her voice cracked on it, finally, the sound breaking loose. "I *waited*, Papa. Every relay that came through the valley, I asked the operator if there was anything for Thornbury. Every year on my birthday. I learned the survey trade because it was the only thing of yours I had, and I got good — I got *better* — so that when you came back you'd have to look at my maps and know that the thing you threw away had kept growing without you. And you know what the worst part is?" She was wiping her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, angry at them for coming. "The worst part is that you were *right*. About the name. About what it would have cost. Everyone at the trust knew who I was before they knew what I could do. Every door I've walked through had your scandal written over it in letters only I could read. You were right, and I hate that you were right, and I hate that you decided, and I hate — "

Her voice went entirely, and she bent over her knees, and the sound she made was small and terrible and twenty years long.

He did not go to her. He wanted to — his whole body wanted it, the way a rope wants the load — but he had learned something in the gorge that week, watching Amara Osei ration her strength around a wound: that some things must be allowed to finish, and that a man who rushes to stop another person's grief is often just hurrying past his own. So he stayed where he was, on his side of the coals, and he let her cry, and he said, into the dark between them, the last of it.

"I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm not asking for anything. I came on this river to bring people home, and it turns out one of them is me. That's all. I saw you through that window and I chose the version of the world where you got to become what you're becoming without me dragging you down — and I have regretted it every single day, and I would probably choose it again, and those two things are both true, and I don't know how a man lives with both. I only know that I have. And that tonight, sitting here, watching you cry over it — this is the first night in twenty years I haven't been carrying it alone. So if it has to hurt, let it hurt us both. I'm done deciding things for you, Seb. That's the whole of my confession. The rest is just geography."

* * *

She cried for a long time. The pine burned down. Dekker's silhouette did not move; either he heard nothing or he was wise enough to have heard nothing. Somewhere above them, in the black serration of the gorge wall, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, and asked it again.

When she finally lifted her head, her eyes were rimmed red and steady, and she looked — God help him — like her mother, and like himself, and like no one but Sebastian Thornbury, which was the whole miracle of her.

"Say her name," Sebastian said. "Say Mum's name. I want to hear you say it."

"Marguerite," he said, and his voice broke on the second syllable and he let it break. "Marguerite. She was the best thing I ever mapped my way to, and the first thing I ever failed to find my way back to."

Sebastian nodded, slowly, the nod of a surveyor confirming a fix. Then she took out her field book, opened it to a clean page, and wrote something in her small, ruthless hand. She did not show him. She closed the book and put it away inside her coat, against her heart, where she kept the things she was not ready to share — and he recognized the gesture, because it was his.

"Papa," she said. It was the first time she'd used the word on this river. It landed in him like a piton driven home. "If Dekker asks you again — about the amnesty, about the record, about any of it — you tell me the same hour you decide. Not after. The same hour. That's the price of tonight."

"You heard," he said. Not a question. Of course she had heard. She had been listening through the walls of his life for twenty years; a canvas tent was nothing.

"I heard." She looked at him across the dead fire, and there was fury in her still, and grief, and something underneath both that was harder and more durable than either — the thing that remains when a structure has been tested to its load and holds. "And I heard what you said to him when you refused. So we're not finished. But we're not broken the way we were."

She stood, wrapped her coat around herself, and went to the edge of the ledge to take the second watch, because she was her father's daughter and would not sleep in the same night she had wept.

It was full dark when Ivo saw it — saw her see it, rather. She had gone rigid at the ledge's edge, head tilted, the way she tilted her head over a chart when two contour lines refused to agree. Below them, far down the gorge, where the Vell bent around the drowned forest at the old confluence, there was a light. Then another. Then a third, spaced with a discipline no lantern-carrier ever has, moving upriver against the current, toward them, through the dark.

"Seb," Ivo said quietly, already reaching for his boots. "Wake the others."
