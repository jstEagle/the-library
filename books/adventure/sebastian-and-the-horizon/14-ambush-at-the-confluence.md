## 14. Ambush at the Confluence

Sebastian woke to the sound of birds stopping.

It was not a noise, exactly. It was the absence of one — the dawn chorus that had accompanied them every morning since Kilometer Forty simply ceased, all at once, like a door closing on a room full of singers. She lay in her bedroll beneath the lean-to of cut boughs and listened to the river instead: the Vell and its tributary meeting a hundred meters downstream, their two voices braiding into one deep, patient murmur. The confluence. Cartwright's charts had marked it with a tidy blue X and the notation *good water*. Nothing about it had ever suggested an ending.

She was already reaching for her boots when Ivo appeared at the edge of camp, moving low, his hand flat in the air — *down, stay down* — and she understood that the birds had known something the rest of them had slept through.

"They're on both banks," he said quietly. "Maybe thirty. Maybe more. They've been walking up on us since before first light."

Amara stirred under the tarp beside the cold fire pit, her wounded side bound in linen that had gone rust-colored overnight. Lenn was still a kilometer back at the last camp with Cartwright, too weak to move fast; they'd split into two groups at dusk to make better time, and now Sebastian felt the geometry of that decision turn against her like a blade coming around.

"How long do we have?" she whispered.

Ivo tilted his head, reading the forest the way other men read documents. "However long it takes a man who doesn't know this ground to cross a hundred meters of wet fern without making noise." A pause. "Not long."

* * *

There was no time for a plan and no need for one, because Ivo had apparently been carrying the plan inside him for twenty years, folded up like the burned maps he'd once drawn of this country. He moved through the camp touching each person — Dekker's shoulder, Amara's wrist, the two remaining crewmen, Okonjo and Reyes, steady river men who had signed on for wages and were about to be paid in something else entirely.

"Confluence is a killing ground," he said. "Open water on three sides, one trail in from the south. That's why they chose it. So we don't take the trail, and we don't take the water." He pointed east, at the cliff wall rising sheer out of the fog above the river bend — two hundred meters of grey limestone streaked black with seepage, crowned with cloud. "We go up."

Sebastian stared at the face of it. Her whole profession lived in lines like that one: contour intervals stacked so tight they merged into a single inked band, the cartographer's shorthand for *impossible*. "There's no route there. There's nothing on any chart—"

"No," Ivo said. "There isn't." And something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under water — not quite a smile, more like grief remembering what it used to be. "I put it there once. Then I helped burn every copy."

The first shot came out of the southern fern brake while he was still talking. It went high, deliberately high — a warning, or a herding tool, or simply a man testing whether the camp would panic. Around them the forest answered: branches cracking, boots in mud, the wet slap of men fanning out along both banks to close the trap.

"Up," Ivo said again, and this time it wasn't a suggestion.

* * *

What followed, Sebastian would later reconstruct in fragments, because memory refused to hold it whole.

She remembered Okonjo lifting Amara onto his back despite her protest, the ethnographer's arms locked around his neck, her riddles finally reduced to a single repeated word — *up, up, up* — like a prayer with directions. She remembered Reyes kicking dirt over the fire embers even as the bullets started coming down through the canopy, some old riverman's discipline stronger than fear. She remembered Dekker running well, too well, finding holds with a speed that seemed rehearsed, and she filed that away in the cold ledger she kept now at the back of her mind, next to the radio frequency she'd memorized off his kit.

And she remembered her father on the rock.

He climbed the way he read the river — as scripture, as text he'd memorized decades ago and never permitted himself to forget. His hands went into cracks she couldn't see until he was already hanging from them. He called the route upward in a low, flat voice, naming holds like stations of a litany: *flakes left, jam the corner, traverse on the ledge, trust the root but not the stone it grows from.* Twenty years of exile had not rusted any of it. If anything, the exile had preserved him, sealed him in amber at the moment of his greatest skill, so that all those lost years had gone into knowing this cliff by heart.

"Where did you learn this?" she gasped, forty meters up, pressed into a chimney with the river roaring below and the first real bullets beginning to strike stone around them, spitting chips of limestone that stung her cheek like thrown gravel.

"I learned it the year I betrayed everyone," he said, hauling himself over the bulge above her and reaching back down for her wrist. "Climbing away from things. I got very good at climbing away."

* * *

They lost Okonjo and Reyes at the base of the final pitch.

The hired men had not been slow after all — only quiet. While the main body drove the fugitives toward the cliff, a second party had circled ahead along the tributary, and they came out of the trees at the foot of the climb just as the last of the team reached the ledge system halfway up. Sebastian heard them before she saw them: a whistle, two notes, answered by another from across the water. A language of pursuit older than radios.

Ivo looked down, and Sebastian watched him do arithmetic with people's lives, watched the numbers come out wrong no matter how he arranged them. Six climbers on the wall, one of them wounded, none of them fast enough to reach the top before the men below found the start of the route. Unless someone made the men below look elsewhere.

"I'll hold the ledge," Reyes said. He said it the way he might have reported the depth of a channel or the location of a snag — information, plainly delivered. He unslung the expedition's single rifle, the one they'd carried for crocodiles and never once needed for its stated purpose. "Okonjo keeps going. He's got the doctor. That's the cargo."

"You don't have to—" Sebastian began.

"Miss Thornbury." Reyes smiled up at her, and it was the same unhurried smile he'd worn through every rapid since Kilometer Forty, the smile of a man who had made his peace with rivers a long time ago. "My grandmother fished this water before it had your name on it. Whatever nation this turns out to be — hers or theirs or the one in the maps you're carrying — I'd like it to get told true. Go tell it true."

Okonjo set Amara down on the ledge just long enough to grip Reyes's forearm, one motion, wordless, the kind of farewell that has no room in it for anything extra. Then he took Amara's weight again and climbed.

Sebastian climbed. She did not look back, because looking back was a luxury purchased with speed, and speed was the only currency that mattered now. But sound travels up cliffs as faithfully as it travels down rivers, and so she heard everything: the first exchange of shots, controlled and spaced, a man conserving ammunition and buying minutes. The shouts of the hired men discovering that the ledge gave cover and the route above gave none. Then a lull. Then Reyes's voice, clear over the water, singing — actually singing, some low fishing song in a language the border treaty had never bothered to name — right up until the moment the shooting stopped being careful.

After that there was only the river, saying what it always said.

* * *

The top of the cliff was not a summit but a shoulder of moss and wind-bent trees, and beyond it the land folded away northward into cloud forest that no chart in Sebastian's possession described. Ivo brought them over the lip one at a time, counting them in like a man tallying souls, and when he reached four — himself, Sebastian, Amara, Okonjo — he stopped counting and sat down abruptly on a root, as if the number itself had weight enough to knock him over.

"Dekker?" Sebastian asked.

"Gone ahead. Or gone away. With Dekker it's the same direction." Ivo wiped his hands on his trousers, leaving twin smears of blood that might have been his own or might have been the rock's. "He was twenty meters up when the shooting started. Didn't wait. Didn't help. Just — went."

"He knew where they'd circle," Sebastian said. "He climbed like a man following a map he'd already seen."

Her father looked at her for a long moment. Above them the mist moved; below them the confluence glittered, indifferent, beautiful, stained now with something none of its waters could wash out. "When we catch our breath," he said finally, "you'll tell me everything you've noticed. All of it. And I'll tell you why I let him stay on the team after I caught that transmission, and you can decide which of us was the greater fool."

"Not today," Amara said. She was sitting propped against a tree, grey-lipped, her wound seeping again from the climb, but her eyes were clear and furious and alive. "Today we move. Grief is a country you visit. This—" she gestured at the unmapped green rolling away northward, at the smoke of the burning cache still visible far downriver, at all of it, "—this is the country where my people's name is written. We carry it out or we die carrying it. Those are the only roads left."

No one argued with her. Not even the mountain.

* * *

They walked north through cloud forest for the rest of the day, following a ridge line Ivo navigated from a memory he claimed was secondhand — routes shown to him once, twenty years ago, by guides whose names he still would not speak aloud, as if speaking them might cost them something further. Sebastian copied as they went, sketching on waterproof paper with a pencil stub, fixing elevations and bearings the way her mother had once taught her to fix birdsong: by refusing to let it pass unrecorded.

Twice they heard movement below and to the east — pursuit, or the echo of pursuit, hard to say in terrain that swallowed sound and gave back only suggestions. Twice Ivo altered course without breaking stride, and twice the sounds fell behind.

Near dusk, Okonjo, who had not spoken since the ledge, finally spoke.

"There's a village," he said. "Two days north. My grandmother's people. If any of her blood remains, they'll hide us." He looked at Amara, and something passed between them — recognition, perhaps, of two names the maps had lost. "And if none remains, then we'll be the ones who remember where it stood. Either way, we walk."

That night, wedged into a hollow beneath a granite overhang with the rain starting and the fire small enough to cup in two hands, Sebastian finished copying the last of the vault plates from memory — the great coastal chart, the one that showed the drowned kingdom's full extent, its harbors and its shrines and its name written in a script three governments had agreed did not exist. She blew the sand off the ink, rolled the sheets into their oilskin sleeve, and tied the sleeve tight against her ribs, where it rode with every breath.

Below them, far to the south, a light was moving along the river. Not a lantern. Too steady. Too deliberate. A search beam, sweeping the water and both banks, working north.

Ivo stood watch at the mouth of the hollow, and when Sebastian joined him he didn't pretend it was anything else.

"That light will reach the confluence by morning," he said. "Whoever's driving it knows we went up the cliff, because Dekker told them. Which means Dekker also told them where the cliff route ends." He turned to her, and in the ember-light his face looked carved rather than aged, all the softness burned out of it years ago. "Your charts, girl. How many days to that village?"

"Two. Three, carrying Dr. Osei."

"We have one." He looked north, into the dark where the unmapped country waited, and Sebastian saw her father weigh the only thing he had left to spend. "Then tomorrow we go where no chart goes — not mine, not yours, not theirs. Tomorrow we find out if the horizon remembers me as well as I remember it."
