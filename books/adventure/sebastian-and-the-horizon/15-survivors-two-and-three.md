## 15. Survivors Two and Three

The cliff route had cost them a night and nearly cost them everything else. By the time the team came down off the escarpment into the river valley, the light was the color of old pewter, and the mist off the Vell moved through the trees like something that had been waiting for them. Sebastian walked at the front with Amara, her waterproof papers wrapped against her chest, and Ivo limped behind them both, favoring the ankle he'd turned on the descent and refusing, with a stubbornness his daughter had inherited whole, to say anything about it.

They found Lenn on the second morning.

It was Dekker's replacement — a quiet Kessari porter named Ovid — who spotted the signal first: three stones stacked on a flat rock above the waterline, deliberate, geometric, unmistakably human. Not a cairn. A message. *I am here. I am alive. Come carefully.*

Ivo read it the way he read everything in this country, with his whole body going still. "Photographer's habit," he said finally. "Order out of chaos. They teach it to you so you can find your way back to camp." He glanced at Sebastian. "Your Cartwright trained him well."

"He's not my Cartwright," Sebastian said, but there was no conviction in it, only exhaustion.

They followed the stones upstream for half a day, through a stand of birches whose leaves had gone the yellow of old paper, until the river narrowed into a channel choked with fallen timber. There, wedged beneath the root plate of an overturned spruce like a animal in its den, they found him.

Lenn was barely recognizable as the man from the expedition photographs — the one who'd smiled from the trust's promotional materials with a camera slung across his chest like a bandolier. He had lost perhaps thirty pounds. His beard was gray where it had been brown. His left arm was bound to his body with strips of shirt, splinted with green wood, and the binding had been redone so many times that the fabric had worn through in places and been patched with bark fiber.

But his eyes were clear. That was the first thing Sebastian noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. The man had been alone in the wilderness for weeks, wounded, hunted, and his eyes were the calmest things in the valley.

"Don't come straight at me," he said, before any of them had taken three steps. His voice was a ruin, sanded down to gravel. "Walk wide. Show me your hands. All of you."

They did. Even Ivo, who had not been asked politely by anyone in twenty years and who obeyed now without a flicker of resentment, spreading his scarred fingers in the gray light.

"Better," Lenn said. "Now. Who sent you?"

"The Cartwright Trust," Sebastian said. "Rescue charter. Out of Harrow's Landing."

"Names."

"Ivo Thornbury. Guide. Sebastian Thornbury, cartography. Dr. Amara Osei, recovered survivor, third of—" She stopped.

"Third," Lenn finished for her. Something moved behind the calm of his eyes then, the first crack in it. "You have her. You actually got her out." He laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and it turned into coughing that bent him double against the root plate. When he could speak again he said, "Then I'll tell you what I told no one else, because there was no one else. Sit down. This takes time, and we don't have much of it."

* * *

He talked while Ovid built a fire small enough to hide its smoke in the birch canopy, and while Amara sat closest to him, close enough to touch, though she didn't, as if proximity itself were a ration to be saved.

Lenn had been at the waterfall room. He had photographed all of it — the vault, the map plates, the chamber walls inscribed with river-names in a script older than either nation's alphabet. He had photographed the moment the security chief, a broad silent man called Rennick, had understood what they were looking at and gone very still in a way Lenn had learned, over years of photographing powerful men, to recognize as arithmetic. *How many witnesses. How much explosive. How deep the water.*

"We were supposed to find nothing," Lenn said. "That was the expedition's real purpose. Cartwright knew. I didn't, not then. We were funded to survey, to document, to come home empty-handed and file a report saying the gorge held nothing of significance. A clean bill of historical erasure, signed by respectable scholars. It would have ended the question forever. No one would ever dig again after a trust expedition certified the ground sterile."

"But they found it anyway," Ivo said.

"They found it because Amara found it. She'd been reading the trading-post records for years, cross-referencing place-names that appeared in one archive and vanished from the next. She led us to the falls under cover of an ethnographic side-survey. Cartwright let her. I think—" Lenn paused, choosing words with the care of a man laying stones across a river. "I think part of him wanted her to find it. And part of him wanted to be able to say she'd acted alone."

"And when you found it?"

"Rennick sealed the entrance behind us. Told the porters the route was unstable. Then, over three days, he began removing people. First the two junior surveyors — sent 'ahead' to establish a relay camp. Then the cook. Then the radio operator, who'd transmitted something Rennick didn't authorize. I know because I heard the transmission. Half a word. Then silence." Lenn looked directly at Ivo. "You heard it too. At Kilometer Forty. Everyone downstream heard it. That was the last honest sound this expedition made."

Sebastian felt the words go through her like cold water. Her father's contract, her father's dread, the garbled transmission that had started all of it — she had carried that fragment of static for weeks without knowing it was a dying man's voice.

"I survived because I'm useful and harmless," Lenn went on. "A photographer is a witness, but Rennick decided a witness with plates could be handled later. He kept me moving with the main party until the ambush country, then I ran. Broke my arm in the fall. Been living on fish and fear since." He nodded toward Amara. "She found me once, early on. Left me salt and a fire drill. Didn't stay. Said staying was how people died."

"It is," Amara said simply.

"Why run upriver?" Ivo asked. "Everything runs down. Rescue comes from below."

"Because Rennick thinks like a rescue," Lenn said. "He's watching the lower river. Everything below the confluence is his. Up here there's nothing to find except the thing itself." He paused. "And Cartwright."

"You've seen him?"

"I've heard him. Two nights ago. Coughing. Same direction every evening — the old shrine structure on the ridge above the falls, the one the maps call the Watchhouse. He's alone. Rennick left him there." Lenn's mouth twisted. "Left him there the way you leave something you intend to come back for."

* * *

They reached the Watchhouse at dusk.

It was a pre-colonial structure — that much even Sebastian could see without training, though she had more than enough now. Dry-stone walls older than any treaty, roofed with slabs lichen-green as bronze, set on the ridge so that its single doorway faced the falls. Whoever had built it had wanted someone standing here, always, looking at the water. Looking at what the water hid.

Cartwright lay inside on a bed of boughs, and Sebastian's first thought, absurdly, was of her father's study: the same stillness of a man surrounded by his own decisions. Edmund Cartwright had been a substantial figure in the trust's portraits — silver-haired, square-jawed, the very architecture of institutional benevolence. The man on the boughs weighed half of that. His expedition clothing hung on him like shed skin. One leg was wrapped in filthy bandages stiff with old blood. But his mind, when he fixed it on them, was terrifyingly intact.

"Thornbury," he said, before anyone spoke. His eyes had found Ivo in the doorway light and stayed there. "Twenty years, and they send me Thornbury."

"You know each other," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. The temperature of the room had changed; she could feel history condensing in it like breath on glass.

"We've met," Ivo said, and would not elaborate, and Cartwright laughed — a dry, terrible sound.

"Oh, we've met. Ask him about the Meridian Survey sometime, girl. Ask him what he drew and what he burned." He turned back to Ivo, and there was something in his face that was not hatred, or not only hatred. Recognition. The look of one drowning man sighting another. "Well. Come to arrest me, guide? Come to finish what your paymasters started?"

"I came to bring survivors home," Ivo said evenly. "You appear to qualify. Barely."

"Survivors." Cartwright said the word as if tasting spoiled meat. "Yes. Let's talk about survival. Let's talk about what survives a man." He pushed himself up against the wall, wincing, and Sebastian saw Amara move — not to help him, but to stand where she could see his face. Watching. Cataloguing. An ethnographer to the last, studying the rarest specimen of all: power in collapse.

"Do you know why Aldous Vane funded this expedition?" Cartwright said.

"The trust's charter—"

"The trust's charter is stationery." Cartwright's voice cracked and steadied. "Vane came to me four years ago. He'd bought controlling interest quietly, through intermediaries — I signed papers I didn't fully read, which is the privilege of men with reputations. And he laid it out for me over brandy in a room with a better view than any of us deserved. He said: *Edmund, somewhere in that gorge is proof that the border is a fiction written in blood, and if it surfaces, everything built on the fiction shakes. The mines. The concessions. The loans. My entire architecture.* And then he said the sentence I will hear until I die." Cartwright closed his eyes. "He said: *Fund the expedition yourself. Find it. Certify it empty. Bury it so deep that even the memory of burying it dies.*"

The fire popped. Down in the valley, the falls made their long white sound, patient as geology.

"He wasn't funding discovery," Cartwright said. "He was funding destruction. I was the shovel. And I said yes." He opened his eyes and found Amara. "Not because I believed him. Because I'd said yes to men like him before, and each yes made the next one easier, until yes was just the shape my mouth fell into. Thirty years ago I helped write the report that erased the trade roads. Twenty years ago I reviewed the Meridian Survey and passed it clean — knowing what the surveyor's own notes admitted. Your father's notes, girl. I read them. I buried them. I promoted myself on the burial."

Sebastian looked at her father. Ivo stood by the doorway with his face turned slightly away, toward the falls, as if the answer to something might be written there in falling water. He did not defend himself. He did not flinch. He simply stood and took it, the way he'd stood and taken everything since Kilometer Forty, guilt carried like ballast, keeping the ship low in the water.

"So when Amara found the vault," Sebastian said slowly, working it through, "Rennick didn't panic. He executed the plan."

"The plan was always execution," Cartwright said. "The expedition was designed to fail beautifully. Every death downstream is a line item Vane approved. Including mine, if it comes to that. Rennick left me here with a ruined leg because a dead leader is more useful than a live one — grief buys silence, martyrdom buys funding, and the trust will build a memorial to me while Vane burns whatever you're carrying." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Sebastian had seen yet, worse than the ambush, worse than the graves. "I know how it works. I *was* how it works."

Amara stepped forward. She had been silent through all of it, and when she spoke, her voice was quiet enough that everyone leaned toward it.

"You could have refused."

"Yes."

"You could have warned us."

"Yes."

"You could have died honestly, at least, instead of living like this."

Cartwright received each blow without moving, the way old trees receive weather. "Yes," he said again. "And now I can do one thing. One." He gestured with a hand that shook — not from fear, Sebastian realized, but from the effort of holding himself together long enough to finish. "In the lining of my coat there are documents. Vane's letters. The transfer of trust ownership. Instructions regarding the disposition of 'materials and personnel.' Signed. Dated. In his own hand, because vanity is the one corruption that never learns caution. Take them. Use them. I don't ask forgiveness. I ask to be evidence."

No one moved for a moment. Then Ivo crossed the room, knelt beside the ruined man he had known in another life, and began, gently, methodically, to cut the lining free.

* * *

They made a litter that night from spruce poles and the last of their rope, and Cartwright watched them work with the expression of a man watching his own funeral arrangements being handled competently. Lenn, splinted arm and all, insisted on helping, and Ovid took the front handles without being asked.

It was Amara who stood the first watch, and Amara who came to Sebastian at the fire afterward, when the others slept and the falls filled the dark with their endless erasing sound.

"Your father," Amara said. "The Meridian Survey. You know what he mapped."

"Some of it."

"You know what it was used for."

"Some of that too."

Amara crouched beside her, and in the firelight her face was unreadable and ancient and young all at once. "Then understand this. Vane will offer you everything before this is over. Pardons. Money. Your father's name cleaned. He will make it sound like mercy. And when he does, remember the room under the waterfall. Remember that everything he offers you is priced in the same currency he used to buy this man." She nodded toward the litter, where Cartwright slept the shallow sleep of the condemned. "He has never once paid with his own coin."

She rose and went back to her watch, and Sebastian sat alone with the fire and the sound of the water, turning the coat-lining documents over in her hands — Vane's handwriting crawling across the pages like something alive — and listening to her father breathe in his sleep ten feet away, and understanding, finally, that the hardest miles of this river were still ahead of them.

Below the confluence, somewhere in the dark, Rennick was waiting.
