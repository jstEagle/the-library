## 20. Climax: The Burning Archive

The smoke came before the news.

Sebastian smelled it at dusk, a thin acrid thread riding the upriver wind, and her first thought was that someone had lit a cookfire badly. Then the wind shifted and brought more of it, and beneath it something sweeter, resinous, wrong — the smell of pitch and kerosene and old paper catching. She stood on the ledge above the gorge with a chart half-rolled in her hands and watched the sky over the falls turn the color of an old bruise.

"They're burning it," Amara said behind her. Her voice had no surprise in it at all, only a grief so worn it had gone smooth as river stone. "I told you he would. A man like Vane doesn't lose. He erases."

Below them, through the screen of ferns, the waterfall room's entrance glimmered in the last light — that low stone mouth Amara had led them to through passages no survey had ever recorded, where the master plates lay in their cedar sleeves, thousands of them, the drawn memory of a nation that treaties had unwritten. Sebastian had spent three days among them copying by lamplight until her eyes burned, until the lines of rivers ran under her eyelids when she slept. Three days of awe. Now the wind carried kerosene.

She turned and found her father already moving, cinching his pack straps with hands that did not shake, and she understood that some part of him had been waiting for this since the checkpoint, since Vane's soldiers and lawyers and soft-voiced offers, since the moment the old man had smiled and said *families, Mr. Thornbury, think of the families* and Ivo had said nothing at all.

"How long?" she asked.

"Smoke travels faster than men." He glanced at the light dying in the west. "They'll wait for full dark. Fire shows better at night — they want it seen. They want the whole valley to watch their government burn its own history and call it weather." He shouldered the pack. "We go now, while they're still forming up."

* * *

Ivo had held passes before. That was the arithmetic of his life: twenty years ago he had held one open for men who used what came through it to destroy villages, and every mile since had been an attempt to walk back through it the other way. Now there was only this pass, this night, this one chance to carry out what could not be copied — the master plates themselves, the ones whose weight in the hand was proof, not memory.

He went down first, as he always went down first, testing each hold. The route to the vault was a wet stair of roots and slick limestone, and the falls filled the dark with a roar that swallowed footsteps, which was mercy and danger both. Behind him came Sebastian with the empty sledge they'd lashed together from cache poles, then Amara, then Lenn with the camera bag strapped tight, then Cartwright — gray-faced, wheezing, but refusing the porter's arm, because some men can only pay their debts on their feet.

The vault breathed cool air at them like a held exhale. Inside, by shielded lamp, the plates waited in their long sleeves, and Ivo felt again the vertigo he'd felt the first time: here were maps older than any border he'd ever been paid to draw, maps where the rivers ran the same but the names were different, where a country lived entire in ink and nobody had thought to burn it for four hundred years until a financier decided four hundred years was negotiable.

"We take all we can carry," he said quietly. "Sledge takes two hundred. Backs take the rest. We leave nothing that burns."

They worked fast and silent, sleeves into oilcloth, oilcloth into frames. Sebastian moved down the rows reading catalog marks aloud in a whisper, choosing by some instinct of hers — the oldest first, Amara said later; the ones that could never be redrawn from memory. Ivo watched his daughter's hands, sure and quick in the lamplight, and thought that whatever else the world took from them tonight, it would not take this: the two of them bent over the same work, finally, after twenty years of separate roads.

Then Lenn, at the entrance, made a sound like a man swallowing broken glass.

"Lanterns," he whispered. "On the far trail. A dozen, maybe more."

* * *

They had perhaps ten minutes, and Ivo spent nine of them deciding, and the decision cost him everything he had left.

The far trail was the only approach wide enough for the arson party. It climbed along the gorge rim and dropped to the vault through a narrow defile — a throat of rock barely two men abreast, walled on one side by cliff and on the other by a drop into black water. He knew it. Of course he knew it; he had mapped it as a young man with a theodolite and a clear conscience, and the map had been burned with everything else, which meant the men coming tonight knew it only as far as their employer's ignorance of it went.

"I hold the defile," he said. "You go the water way — the lower ledge, downstream. It's wet and it's slow and it's the reason I'm holding the defile instead of coming with you."

"No." Sebastian said it flatly, the way she corrected his charts. "No, we go together, we—"

"Bash." The old name came out of him unbidden, the one he'd used when she was small, and he saw it land on her like a blow. "Two hundred pounds of plates on a wet ledge in the dark needs every pair of hands. One man with a rifle and good ground doesn't need any." He took her face in both hands, rough palms against cold cheeks. "Twenty years I chose wrong. Let me choose right once."

Amara was already lifting the sledge. "The ledge, cartographer," she said gently. "Your father knows rock better than he knows people. Trust him at the thing he's best at."

There was no time for anything else. There is never time for anything else. Sebastian gripped his wrists once, hard, and then she was gone into the dark with the others and the plates, and Ivo climbed alone up into the defile with Dekker's abandoned service rifle — Dekker, who had vanished at dusk without a word, and whom Ivo had privately given up for dead or worse — and a bandolier of flares, and twenty years of guilt to spend.

* * *

They came at midnight, torches first, then shapes.

Ivo let the first three pass below him before he dropped the first flare into the dry brush at the defile's mouth. It went up white and furious, and the column stopped, and a voice shouted orders, and in the flare-light Ivo saw them clearly: soldiers, the same locally born faces from the checkpoint, boys from villages whose names appeared on none of Vane's maps. They didn't want to be here. That was the flaw in the whole design, and he meant to drive a wedge into it.

"The room you're burning holds your grandmothers' names!" he called down, voice pitched to carry over the falls. "Vane pays you to erase your own mothers! Go home and tell them what you were sent to do!"

Someone fired wildly. The bullet sparked off rock a foot from his head, and after that the talk was over, and the work began.

It was ugly, close, desperate work — flares and falling stone and the geometry of high ground, and Ivo Thornbury, fifty-eight years old with a bad knee and a worse heart, gave the defile everything the mountain had ever taught him. He rolled the scree slope twice. He fired the last of the flares into the brush to split their line. When two men tried the cliff side he met them with a piton hammer and the particular fury of a man settling a twenty-year account, and they fell back, and the column stalled in the burning throat of the pass, and above the roar of the falls Ivo laughed — actually laughed — because for the first time in his life he was holding a pass *against* the erasure instead of for it.

Then the fire reached the vault.

He heard it before he understood it — a different roar joining the falls', a sound like the mountain itself inhaling — and he turned and saw the glow rising beyond the ridge, orange eating the stars, and knew they had come around by another trail, a second party, that the defile had been the feint and he had held it beautifully, heroically, for nothing.

"No," he said, to no one. "No —"

And then, because there was nothing left to hold the pass *for*, he held it anyway — harder, meaner, buying minutes with blood now, taking a bullet through the meat of his shoulder that spun him to one knee and getting up, because somewhere below, on a wet ledge in the dark, his daughter was carrying a nation out of the fire, and every minute he stole was a minute she kept.

* * *

Sebastian felt the vault burning in her teeth.

From the lower ledge, hauled along by Amara's grip, she looked back once and saw the ridge crowned in flame, and the sound that came out of her was not a word. The plates pressed against her back in their oilcloth — forty pounds of cedar and ink, the oldest of them, the ones that could never be redrawn — and she clutched the frame straps until her fingers went numb and kept walking, because stopping was dying and the fire had already taken the rest.

"He bought us the time," Amara said, steady as a metronome. "Whatever it costs him, he bought it. Grieve later. Walk now."

So she walked. Down the ledge, through the curtain of spray where the river undercut the cliff, across the shingle bar where Lenn collapsed and was dragged, where Cartwright — old, ruined Cartwright — took the photographer's load without being asked and staggered under it, paying, always paying. The fire painted the clouds above them red all the way to the confluence, and Sebastian walked out of the light of everything her father had ever failed at, carrying the one thing he had ever gotten right.

At dawn they reached the old trading post, and Dekker was there.

He sat on the dock with his rifle across his knees and his head in his hands, and when he looked up his face was wrecked, scorched at the hairline, and Sebastian's hand went to the flare gun in her belt before she saw what he was holding out instead: a waterproof document case, fat with papers.

"I was supposed to be their inside man again," Dekker said hoarsely. "Same as upriver. Same pay, same promises. I went in with the second party to make sure the burn was complete." His voice broke on the word. "I stood in that room while it caught. I watched four hundred years go up, and I watched the men who lit it laughing about bonuses, and I thought about you people walking out of the dark carrying what I was paid to destroy, and—" He shoved the case toward her. "Orders. Names. Vane's signature on the burn authorization, dated before Cartwright ever launched. Payment schedules. My own contract, signed. Take it. Give it to the tribunal. I'm done being the man who sells the same people twice."

Sebastian took the case. It was heavier than it looked. Most true things were.

"Dekker," she said. "My father held the defile so we could get these out. He may be dead."

Dekker closed his eyes. "Then I'll testify to that too."

* * *

They found Ivo at noon, at the top of the defile, alive by the stubbornness that had gotten him blacklisted in the first place.

He was propped against a boulder with his shoulder soaked black, one boot sole hanging by a thread, a dead flare stick still clenched in his fist like a relic. He had a fever already and knew it, and when Sebastian knelt in the ash and scree beside him he looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him — not defiance, not guilt, but something finished. Something at peace.

"Held it," he rasped. "Long enough?"

"Long enough." She was crying and didn't care. "We have the masters. All the oldest ones. And Dekker turned — he gave us everything, Dad, Vane signed the burn order before we ever launched. It's over. It's actually over."

Ivo smiled, and the smile cost him visibly, and he paid it anyway. "Then get me down this mountain," he said, "because I have a tribunal to surrender to, and I intend to walk in on my own feet and testify standing up."

Sebastian got her shoulder under his good arm, and together — slowly, in the drifting ash that fell like gray snow across the ruined gorge — they started down.

Behind them the smoke rose for three days, visible from the lowlands, and every village that saw it understood exactly what had been burned and who had ordered it, and the story traveled faster than any soldier could ride, faster than any lawyer could file, down every river road toward the capital, toward the tribunal, toward the reckoning Vane had spent a lifetime believing his money could outrun.

It could not. The horizon was already closing around him.
