## 21. Homecoming

The river gave them back the way a wound gives back its knife — slowly, and with blood.

Sebastian stood at the bow of the government launch as Kilometer Forty slid past on the starboard side, the relay station's tin roof catching the late light like a struck match. She had left from this dock eleven weeks ago with a rescue manifest of six names and returned with three survivors, two dead to bury, one prisoner in irons below deck, and four hundred pounds of pre-colonial cartography wrapped in oilcloth and strapped beneath her feet like ballast.

The plates rode in the hold because she had refused to let them ride anywhere else. Dekker had offered the cargo bay of his own escort — *safer, sealed, climate-controlled* — and she had looked at him for a long moment before saying that the last time these maps were entrusted to a man's careful logistics, an entire nation was subtracted from the world. They would travel where she could put her hand on them.

Dekker had not argued. He had not argued about anything since the night of the fire, since he'd watched Vane's men pour accelerant over the waterfall room while Ivo Thornbury held the pass above it with a broken rifle and a broken body. Something in Dekker had come apart at the sight of it, some load-bearing wall of him, and what remained was a quiet, cooperative man who flinched when doors slammed. He would testify. He had already signed the statement Amara's advocates had drafted. But trust, Sebastian had learned, was a thing you reissued like currency after a devaluation: same face, less worth.

The dock at Corvenne was crowded beyond anything she'd expected. She had imagined a customs officer, maybe a wire-service photographer. Instead there were hundreds — porters from the trading post who'd refused to go upriver and had evidently decided that shame required attendance; students with hand-lettered signs; old women in the shawls of the river country whose embroidery, Sebastian now understood, was not decoration but script, the writing of a people whose name had been filed out of every atlas like a burr removed from wood.

Amara Osei stood beside her at the rail, her arm still in its sling, her face turned toward the crowd with an expression Sebastian could not read until she spoke.

"They're saying it," Amara said quietly. "Listen."

And under the noise of the dock — the gulls, the winches, the brass band someone had improbably produced — Sebastian heard it: a word rising from the crowd again and again, spoken by people who had been taught to call themselves nothing, or something else, or *from upriver*, or *the gorge people*. The name. Their name. It moved through the crowd the way current moves through water, invisible and total, and by the time the launch's lines went ashore, half the dock was saying it and the other half was learning it.

Amara did not weep. She straightened, as if the name were a spine being set, and walked down the gangway first.

* * *

The tribunals convened in the old assize hall on Wexler Street, a building designed to make ordinary citizens feel like insects under glass. Sebastian attended everything. She told herself it was duty — the plates needed a custodian, the record needed a witness — but the truth was simpler and less flattering: she could not stop watching power fail.

It failed slowly, which was the surprise. She had expected Vane to fall the way men fall in novels, all at once, a single catastrophic revelation. Instead it happened the way ice goes out of a river — groaning, cracking along lines no one had mapped, in pieces.

First came Dekker's testimony, delivered in a flat voice with his hands flat on the rail, describing the payroll, the radio schedule, the order that had come down before the expedition ever launched: *recover nothing, return nothing, leave no witnesses among the local hires.* Then came the expedition's own ledgers, seized from Cartwright's townhouse, showing payments routed through three shell trusts to a company whose only asset was a burn contractor. Then, most damning and most pitiful, came Cartwright himself.

He testified for two days. He was not granted leniency and did not ask for it. He sat in the witness box like a man giving up a seat on a crowded train — relieved, almost courteous — and described how forty years ago a young ambitious surveyor named Edmund Cartwright had accepted funding from interests he chose not to examine too closely, and how the border treaty that followed had used his surveys, and how he had spent four decades telling himself the erasure was bureaucratic rather than murderous, and how financing the Vell expedition had been his final attempt to control the evidence — to be the one who buried it properly this time, with ceremony, so that his guilt could be interred alongside it.

"I thought if I destroyed it myself," he said, "it would finally be finished." He looked at his hands. "Guilt is not finished by destruction. I recommend the tribunal note that."

Vane's lawyers fought everything. They fought the chain of custody of the plates; they fought Amara's standing to speak for a nation whose existence their client's documents proved they had spent money denying; they fought the admissibility of Dekker, of the burned vault, of the graves at the confluence. They were good lawyers. That was the horror of it — watching excellent minds deployed in defense of a proposition as simple as *these people do not exist.* Sebastian filled notebook after notebook, and in the margins of the legal arguments she drew, over and over, the contour lines of a country that appeared on none of the exhibits except hers.

The verdicts came down in stages across six weeks. Vane was indicted on seventeen counts, from conspiracy to murder to cultural destruction — a charge so new its statute had been drafted in emergency session while the tribunal sat. His assets froze in October. By November he was a defendant, gray-faced in the dock, no longer the man who intercepted them at the checkpoint with soldiers and scripture-quotations about families and futures, but a smaller creature entirely, one whose enormous machinery of denial had turned out to be made of paper.

But the border itself — that went elsewhere. In December, the case was referred upward, out of the national courts entirely, to the international body that adjudicated treaties between states. The lawyers explained this to Amara like doctors explaining a referral: your injury is real, but it is larger than this hospital.

Amara received the news standing in the corridor outside the chamber, and Sebastian watched her take it the way she had taken everything — as data first, grief second.

"Larger means slower," Amara said. "Slower means the elders may not live to see it."

"Then we write it down faster," Sebastian said, and heard, as she said it, her father's voice underneath her own — *a map is a promise you make to people who aren't born yet* — and did not say so aloud, because some inheritances you keep the way you keep coals: covered, banked, warm without smoke.

* * *

Ivo convalesced in a room above a chandler's shop near the harbor, because he had refused the hospital and refused, with equal firmness, Sebastian's apartment. "Your neighbors," he said, "read newspapers." As if his face weren't in all of them. As if the whole city had not spent the autumn arguing about him over breakfast.

He was mending the way old timber mends — slowly, and never quite true to its original line. The burns on his hands had grafts that pulled when he flexed. The lung the smoke had scarred made stairs a negotiation. But his mind was untouched, and that was its own kind of trial, because Ivo Thornbury with an untouched mind and a grounded body was a caged thing, and cages made him plan escapes.

Sebastian visited every third day. She brought charts. It was the only language they had ever shared without fighting, and now it had become something more — a joint labor. Together, at the chandler's table under a lamp ringed with moth-corpses, they were building the master restoration: the erased nation's territory drawn from the vault plates, cross-referenced against the burned survey fragments Ivo remembered, against Amara's oral geography, against the embroidered scripts of the shawl-women, against Sebastian's own memory-copies from the long descent, made on waterproof paper with numb fingers while hired men searched the banks behind them.

"You've got the eastern watershed wrong," Ivo said one evening, tapping the sheet with a pencil stub. "The vault shows the source basin feeding north. Your line drains south."

"The vault plate is cracked there. Water damage. I interpolated."

"Interpolated." He said the word the way other men said *heresy.* "You don't interpolate a nation's rivers, girl. You go and look."

"It's a nine-day climb in winter."

"Six," said Ivo, "if you know the scree field above the falls, which no map records because I never wrote it down, because—" He stopped. The sentence had arrived, as such sentences always did now, at the edge of the confession they had already made to each other on the river, the night after Dekker fled: *why didn't you come back; I did come back; I saw you through a window.* Some things, once said, kept needing to be said again in new ways, the way a fracture keeps aching in cold weather.

"Because," Sebastian finished for him, quietly, "you didn't want me to have a criminal's rivers either."

He looked at his bandaged hands. "Something like that."

"Well." She took the pencil from him and drew the drainage north, provisionally, in dotted line. "Then in spring, you'll show me the scree field. When you're out."

The *when* hung between them, deliberate. Because the other matter — the matter they circled the way the launch had circled the dock — was the surrender.

* * *

The old charges had never died. Twenty years ago, Ivo Thornbury had guided a survey into forbidden country under false permits, and when the displacement orders came down — when the villages his own contour lines had located were emptied and burned — he had run. Not just from the tribunal that wanted to question him about what he'd seen and signed. From everything. From the profession, from the country, from a daughter asleep in a house on Alder Street whose window he had stood beneath one April night before taking the coast road south.

The statute had outlasted his flight. Obstruction, falsification of permits, failure to report — small charges, technically. But the tribunal's prosecutors, flush with the momentum of the Vane case, had made clear they intended to reopen the full file: what Ivo knew in that survey year, what he'd handed over, whether his silence had been cowardice or complicity. There were men in the river country who remembered him as the mapmaker who sold the passes. Amara knew it. She had forgiven him — or something adjacent to forgiveness — but forgiveness, she said once, was hers to give and justice's to withhold, and she would not confuse the two.

"He could fight it," Sebastian told the lawyer, a dry woman named Hesk who had come to the chandler's shop with a folder of motions. "The Vane case has made him the most famous guide in the country. Half the press calls him a hero. A good defense counsel could get the old charges dismissed as stale."

"A good defense counsel could," Hesk agreed. "Is anyone offering him one?"

Sebastian climbed the stairs. Ivo was at the window, watching the harbor, wearing the expression he wore when reading a river he didn't trust.

"Hesk says you can fight it," Sebastian said. "Stale charges. Hero narrative. She thinks she can win."

"I expect she can."

"So?"

He was quiet a long moment. Below, a fishing boat came in with its lights already lit, though the evening wasn't dark yet — the short days of deep winter, the year turning over.

"When I ran," he said, "I told myself it was for you. And then I told myself it was shame. And then, after enough years, I stopped telling myself anything — I just didn't go back. That's twenty years of not going back, Sebastian. You don't get to erase that with a hero narrative. That's the one thing I learned from all of this." He turned from the window. "Vane tried to erase a country by burning its maps. I tried to erase myself by walking away from mine. Same crime, different scale. And you don't answer that kind of crime by hiring someone to argue it away. You answer it by standing in the room."

She had known he would say it. She had climbed the stairs hoping, irrationally, that he wouldn't.

"When?" she asked.

"Thursday. The assize hall, nine o'clock. Hesk will be there — I'm not a fool, I'll have counsel. But I'll plead as charged, and I'll ask to make a statement." He almost smiled; it was a terrible, wonderful thing, that smile, worn smooth by pain like a stone in a current. "Twenty years ago I should have stood in front of those magistrates and said what I saw. Better late than interpolated."

* * *

Wednesday night she stayed late at the chandler's, finishing the eastern watershed alone, and found the letter on the table when she came down with the tea.

It was addressed to her, in his blunt surveyor's hand, and it was not long. *If the tribunal gives me years,* it began, *the master restoration is yours entire — my route notes, the scree field above the falls, everything unwritten. Write it. If they give me months, we'll climb it together in spring and you can write it with me complaining. Either way, the map gets finished, and it goes out under both names, because a horizon isn't owned by the man who first saw it. It's owned by whoever walks toward it next.*

*I saw you through a window once and thought I was protecting you. I understand now that windows are for looking through, not hiding behind.*

*Thursday, then. Wear the good coat. Your mother would say so.*

Sebastian read it twice, folded it into the inner pocket where she carried the waterproof copies — the insurance policy that had become, somewhere on the long descent, a creed — and stood a while in the lamplight listening to the harbor sounds through the floorboards.

Then she took out a fresh sheet, pinned the corners flat, and drew, in the upper margin where a title would eventually go, a single unbroken horizontal line. Open at both ends. Unfinished.

Thursday was coming. And whatever the tribunal decided, the line would hold.
