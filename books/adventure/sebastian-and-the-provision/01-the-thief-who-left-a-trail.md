## 1. The Thief Who Left a Trail

The outfitter's name was Brannock, and he had the kind of face that looked like it had been arguing with the sun for forty years and losing on points. He found me at the Salt Lark cantina, where I was nursing a beer I'd been making last two hours, because a woman who moves as often as I do learns that cheap drinks are the only reliable retirement plan.

"You're Lockridge," he said. Not a question. He sat down across from me without asking, which told me he was either desperate or rude, and the way his eyes kept flicking to the door said desperate.

"Depends who's asking," I said. "If it's the law, I'm somebody else entirely."

"It's not the law." He set a folded paper on the table between us. "It's money."

I looked at the paper. It was a handbill, the kind outfitters post in supply towns: *Upriver Survey Expedition — Provision Theft — Reward for Recovery of Goods or Identification of Thief.* Somebody had written my name on it in pencil, along with the words *ask at Salt Lark.* That was sloppy of me. I'd let my reputation get ahead of me again, and reputations are like boots — fine when they fit, but they'll walk you places you didn't mean to go.

"An expedition got robbed," Brannock said. "Up past the Narrows, where the river cuts through the gorge country. They're provisioned for six weeks and somebody walked off with enough food for one person for about three. Dried meat, flour, salt, a water filter. Nothing else."

"Nothing else?" I raised an eyebrow. "No tools? No weapons? No cash box?"

"Not so much as a compass."

That was interesting. Thieves are greedy by definition; greed is what makes them thieves instead of beggars. A thief who takes exactly three weeks of food for one person and leaves the cash box sitting there isn't a thief. A thief like that is somebody with a very specific problem and no interest in anyone else's property.

"How much?" I asked.

"Two hundred now, two hundred when you bring back the goods or the thief."

"And if I bring back neither?"

He shrugged. "Then you've had an interesting walk in the desert and two hundred dollars."

I should have said no. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody's keeping them. I'd spent nine years being nobody — nine years since the Grantham fire, nine years since a warrant went out with my name on it for something I hadn't done, nine years of moving town to town ahead of any lawman bored enough to check the old boards. The whole architecture of my life was built on one principle: don't get involved. Involvement is a door, and doors open from both sides.

But two hundred dollars was two hundred dollars, and the desert east of the Narrows was country I knew like my own handwriting, and — though I didn't say this part out loud, not even to myself — I was curious. Curiosity is the crack in the wall that lets the whole building fall down later, but at the time it just looks like light.

"I'll need supplies charged against the reward," I said. "And I want the expedition's route map."

Brannock pushed both across the table before I'd finished asking, which told me how badly he wanted this handled quietly. Outfitters hate theft the way priests hate doubt. It's not about the goods; it's about what the goods say to the next client.

* * *

I left Salt Lark before dawn, because the desert forgives early risers and bills everyone else. The first day's trail was easy reading. Whoever the thief was, they'd left the expedition camp heading northeast, away from the river, into the badlands where the sandstone breaks up into fins and slots and a man can disappear for a season. Their stride was short — I measured it against my own boot more than once, and came up four inches shorter than mine, maybe five. Light person. Careful placement, though. They picked their footing the way a miser picks coins, never wasting a step, never leaving a heel print where a toe would do.

By noon of the second day I'd learned three things, and none of them fit together.

First: the thief was traveling at night. The scuffs in the cool sand were fresh each morning, and the daytime prints I did find were hours old and wind-softened. Sensible, in that country, in that season. Plenty of experienced travelers move at night.

Second: they were caching. Twice I found little stone cairns beside the trail, and under each one, wrapped in oilcloth, a small bundle — not stolen goods, but things gathered along the way. Desert parsley roots. A tin cup, dented, polished bright. In the second cache, a strip of cloth torn from something blue, knotted around a stick. I stood over that knot for a long time. You don't knot things unless you want them noticed. You don't leave caches unless you expect someone to come looking.

Third: the trail was too clean. Not absent — present, deliberate, legible. Every few miles there'd be a print pressed deep into soft ground, deeper than the thief's weight could account for, as if they'd stamped their foot on purpose. A signature. A breadcrumb.

I've tracked smugglers, runaways, murderers, and one memorable tax assessor who'd decided to fake his own death rather than audit the Verrone family, and I will tell you what every one of them has in common: they do not want to be followed. They break trail on rock. They walk in streams. They double back and brush out their sign. Tracking is mostly the art of reading what a frightened person is trying to hide.

This trail hid nothing. This trail *said* things. Follow me, it said. But carefully. And alone.

I sat on a fin of red stone that evening, eating jerky and watching the light drain out of the gorge country, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of a conversation happening without my consent. Nine years I'd kept my own company, and I'd gotten good at it — good the way a stone gets good at being rained on. And here was some stranger writing me letters in bootprints, and here was me, reading them, and worse, *answering*, because at the third cache I'd added a stone to the cairn. Just one. To let them know I'd been there. Which was, if I was honest, the most social thing I'd done since the last time I paid taxes under a false name.

"Get a grip, Lockridge," I told the sunset. The sunset declined to comment.

* * *

On the fourth day the trail turned toward the river, and I understood why the thief had gone northeast first. The badlands route avoided every settlement, every crossing, every place with eyes. Whoever they were, they weren't running from the expedition camp. They were running from people.

That was also the day I found the grave.

It was small, off the trail in the shade of an overhang, marked with stones and a strip of blue cloth — the same blue as the knot on the stick. A coyote had dug at one corner and someone had re-stacked the rocks twice, recently, hands working fast. I didn't uncover it. Whatever it held, it wasn't mine to see, and besides, the dead keep their own ledgers.

But I stood there a while, hat in hand, because my mother raised me right before she raised me gone, and I thought: whoever buried this did it in the middle of fleeing for their life. People who stop to bury strangers — or friends — mid-flight are people running from something with teeth.

The trail after the grave changed character. The careful foot-placement fell apart. Stride lengthened, then shortened again. There were places where the thief had simply sat down — long impressions in the sand, knees drawn up, facing back the way they'd come. Watching their backtrail. Waiting for pursuit that must have felt inevitable.

I started moving faster myself, and I hated that I did. It wasn't the two hundred dollars anymore. Somewhere between the knotted blue cloth and the re-stacked grave stones, the job had stopped being arithmetic and started being a story, and I have never in my life been able to put down a good story halfway through. It's my worst quality. It's why I read the endings of books first, and it's going to be the death of me, though I didn't know that yet.

* * *

The fifth morning, the trail entered slot country — those corridors the flash floods carve through the sandstone, deep enough that noon feels like dusk and a voice echoes strangely, like the canyon is repeating you back to yourself with editorial changes. The walls narrowed. My shadow disappeared. The air went cool and mineral-smelling, and somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm like a slow clock.

And the trail simply ended.

Not faded. Ended. One bootprint in the damp sand at the mouth of a side channel, pressed deep — that deliberate stamp again, the signature — pointing straight into a slot no wider than a coffin standing on end.

I stood at the entrance a long moment, hand on my rifle, doing the math I always do: one unknown, possibly armed, definitely frightened, in terrain built for ambush. Every professional instinct I owned said wait them out, circle wide, come back with leverage.

Instead I heard myself say, conversationally, to the dark slot:

"I'm not with the expedition. I'm not with anybody. I counted your caches. I saw the grave. I put a stone on your cairn, three days back."

The canyon took my voice and turned it over, examining it.

Silence. Then, from somewhere up in the black throat of the slot, small and cracked and younger than I expected — much younger — a voice said:

"How many are you?"

"Just me."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came again, it had the particular tightness of someone who has been crying recently and has strong opinions about never doing it again.

"There were eleven of us," it said. "You should know that before you decide whether to come in."

I looked down at that single stamped bootprint, pointing inward like an invitation or a dare, and I felt my practiced solitude crack down the middle like river ice in spring — quietly, and all at once, and in a way I suspected was not going to be simple to repair.

I stepped into the dark.
