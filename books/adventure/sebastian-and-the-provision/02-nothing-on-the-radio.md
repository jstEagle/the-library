## 2. Nothing on the Radio

The trail stopped behaving at the third mile, and I should have paid more attention to that.

I'd been reading ground since I was old enough to be trusted with a horse and too young to be trusted with anything else. Nine years of it, most of them spent reading my own tracks out from under other people's eyes. The desert is a ledger if you know how to keep books. Every footprint is an entry. Every broken branch, a margin note. Most people write in a scrawl — they wander, they double back, they leave their whole biography pressed into the dirt without meaning to.

This thief wrote in a straight line.

Not a panicked line. Not a starving one, either, though whoever it was had been eating out of stolen tins for days by the time I picked up the sign outside Redwater Wash. A deliberate line. The kind of line you draw when you want someone to follow it.

I told myself it was luck. Good fortune favors the hired, and all that. Marta Voss, the outfitter who'd put three hundred dollars in my palm back at the Landing, wanted her provisions found and her expedition's thief dragged back by the collar, and I wanted the payday, and neither of us needed the trail to be interesting. Interesting trails don't pay. They just get longer.

But there was this: on the second day, at a junction of cattle paths where any sensible runner would have cut south toward the border towns, the thief had gone north. Upriver. Toward the camp they'd supposedly robbed.

Thieves run away from the scene. That's the whole architecture of theft. You take the thing and you put distance between yourself and the taking, because distance is the only fence the law can't climb. This one had walked a loop like a man returning to check his work. Or like a kid walking home.

I crouched over the prints where they crossed the cattle path — small prints, narrow-heeled, the tread worn nearly smooth, boots bought for growing into rather than grown out of — and I felt something I hadn't felt on a job in years. Not suspicion exactly. Curiosity, which is worse. Curiosity is how rangers end up dead in slot canyons and how women like me end up with warrants. You follow a question far enough and it stops being a question and starts being a witness, and witnesses are heavy things to carry.

I followed it anyway. Three hundred dollars buys a lot of obedience.

* * *

The smoke smell reached me before the camp did.

That's not dramatic flourish; it's arithmetic. Char holds its scent in dry country for weeks, and what I caught on the wind off the mesa wasn't weeks old. It was days. Maybe four, maybe five, laid down while I'd been two ridges over following boot prints and congratulating myself on an easy job.

I went the last mile on foot, leading the mule, keeping to the rock spine where the ground doesn't hold a print. Old habits. The habits that kept me invisible for nine years came up out of me like water finding its level, and I let them, because the smell was wrong. Campfires smell like wood and coffee and somebody's socks drying. This smelled like fuel. Accelerant poured generous, the way you'd douse a thing you never wanted standing again.

The expedition camp sat in a bend of the river where the cottonwoods gave way to willow scrub — a good site, defensible, water on two sides. Marta Voss had described it to me with the pride of a woman who'd provisioned forty expeditions and buried none of them. Two big wall tents. A cook shelter. A gear cache raised on posts against the flood season. A satellite radio in a padded case, because the river country eats radio signals for breakfast and the only reliable voice out here comes from orbit.

What was left of it looked like it had been visited by something with hands and no patience.

The tents were burned to the frames, the canvas melted into gray lace over blackened poles. The cook shelter had been pulled down rather than burned — methodical, almost tidy, the timbers stacked like someone intended to come back for them. The gear cache had been cracked open and emptied with the same thoroughness. Not looted the way desperate people loot, grabbing and running. Emptied the way auditors empty accounts. Everything of value gone, everything else left in a heap.

And the radio —

I found the case first, tossed ten yards from where it ought to have been, and then the radio itself, or what remained of it. Someone had smashed it against the rocks. Not once. The casing was stove in, the antenna snapped in three places, the circuit board inside crushed to green confetti. Whoever did it had taken their time. Had made sure.

You smash a radio so nobody can call out. You don't smash a radio to cover a theft, because a thief wants the world quiet behind him, not deaf. A thief steals provisions and slips away into the hills and prays nobody follows. A thief does not burn two tents with accelerant, strip a camp to its bones, and execute a piece of equipment worth more than the provisions it guarded.

I stood there holding a handful of green confetti and let the arithmetic finish itself, because it was going to whether I let it or not.

Marta Voss had hired me to find a thief. A boy, probably, judging by the prints — sixteen, maybe seventeen, light as a heron on soft ground. She'd been very specific about the provisions. Rice, flour, medical kit, the survey instruments' spare batteries. She'd been less specific about why her expedition had gone silent eleven days ago, why she'd waited nine of those days before hiring anyone, and why she'd looked past my shoulder at the horizon through the whole conversation like she expected it to stand up and accuse her of something.

She hadn't hired me to find a cause. She'd hired me to find a symptom. And symptoms, when you go looking for them alone, have a way of introducing you to the disease.

* * *

I did what professionals do. I worked the site.

There were six bedrolls in the ash. Six. Marta had said eight souls on the expedition — herself downriver at the outfitter's post, seven in the field. Seven bedrolls' worth of people, six rolls accounted for, and none of the rolls occupied recently enough to matter. No bodies. I'll say that plainly because I checked twice, walking the whole bend of the river with my heart going like a pump handle, expecting at every step to find what camps like this usually leave behind when the burning's done.

No bodies. But also no people, and no boats — the expedition had come upriver on two skiffs, Marta said, and both moorings were empty, one of them with the painter rope cut clean and the other simply gone, cleat and all, pried off its bolts.

Scattered, then. Fled, or taken, or dispersed into the canyon country where a person can hide for a season if the land likes them. The desert swallows people whole and polite about it. I knew that better than anyone. I was, in a sense, living proof that a person could fall off the face of the earth and keep walking around on the underside of it.

The thief's trail came into camp from the southeast — those same narrow prints, days older now, the gait uneven in a way that spoke of hunger or a turned ankle or both. The prints circled the camp's edge once, wide, careful, the way you circle a thing you're afraid of. Then they'd gone in. I tracked the approach through the willow scrub and found where someone small had knelt for a long time beside the burned tents. A long time. The knees had printed deep, and there were marks beside them — fingers, pressing into the ash. Once. Twice. Like someone touching the place where a bedroll used to be, checking for warmth that couldn't possibly still be there.

Then the prints left. And here was the thing that took the last of my professionalism and broke it over its knee:

They left toward the gorge.

Away from the border towns. Away from every direction a thief runs. Toward Redrock Gorge, where the walls close to shoulder width in places and the river runs cold and loud and there is exactly one way in and one way out, and every soul within fifty miles knows it. Nobody hides in the gorge. The gorge is where hiding goes to die.

Unless you're not hiding. Unless you're going somewhere the map says you have to go, and the only reason a sixteen-year-old cartographer — because Marta had mentioned, in passing, that the expedition carried a cartographer's apprentice, a quiet boy, Sebastian, fourteen going on forty — would walk into a box canyon is if he was carrying something that mattered more than his own skin.

Or unless he was leading someone there.

I stood at the edge of the burned camp with the wind coming down the river valley carrying ash and river-smell and the particular silence of a place where voices used to be, and I understood that I had crossed some line in the ledger without noticing the entry. Three days ago I'd been a woman tracking a thief for money. Now I was a woman standing in the ashes of a massacre — because that's what it was, whatever word Marta had used, whatever euphemism the syndicate's lawyers would reach for — and the thief's trail ran through the middle of it like a signature.

Mining syndicates don't burn expedition camps over rice and flour. They burn them over maps. Over surveys. Over whatever it was seven people had found up this river that was worth erasing them for. And somewhere ahead of me, in a canyon with one door, was a barefoot-souled boy in boots two sizes wrong, carrying either the only evidence that anyone had ever been here at all, or nothing but his own grief and a stolen tin of peaches.

The sun was dropping behind the western mesas, turning the river to hammered copper. I had maybe two hours of light. The gorge mouth was four miles north, and after dark the temperature would fall fast enough to make the cold river air pool in the canyon depths like something drowned.

I could walk away. That thought arrived on schedule, right on time, wearing its usual clothes: you've got a warrant, Paloma, you've got nine years of invisibility, you've got a mule and a rifle and a system that works, and none of those things survive contact with syndicate business. Walk back downriver, tell Marta Voss her thief headed into the gorge, collect your balance, and be a ghost again by Thursday.

I looked at the knee-prints in the ash. At the fingers pressed into the cold fire. Somebody had knelt here grieving, alone, in the dark, with the people who did this possibly still close enough to hear him cry — and had gotten up and walked toward the gorge instead of away from it.

Nine years ago I'd run from a fire I didn't set, and the running had eaten my whole life, and I'd told myself the story so many times I'd worn it smooth: I didn't do it, but I couldn't stay to prove it, because staying meant the law, and the law doesn't care about innocent. I knew exactly what it cost to flee a thing you didn't do. I knew the weight of it, ounce for ounce, and I knew that boy was carrying it, and I knew — with the dread arriving now, displacing everything else, flooding in where the curiosity had been — that I was not going to walk away.

Which meant I was already dead in one sense and alive in another, and both of those women were going to have to fit in the same pair of boots.

I got the mule settled, cached what I wouldn't need, and started north along the river in the last of the light, toward the gorge, toward the one door, toward whatever was waiting in the dark at the bottom of it.

Halfway there, the wind shifted, and I heard it — faint, upstream, threading through the river noise.

A whistle. Three notes, low, repeated. Deliberate. The kind of sound a person makes when they want to be heard by exactly one someone, and are betting everything that the someone is listening.
