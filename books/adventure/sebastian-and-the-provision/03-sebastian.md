## 3. Sebastian

The trail led me into the gorge the way a good lie leads you into a bad room — smoothly, and with every appearance of your own idea.

I'd been reading it for two days by then, and I'll admit I'd stopped thinking of it as a thief's trail somewhere around noon on the first day. Thieves travel like water: downhill, toward cover, toward whatever's easiest. This one traveled like a letter. Every broken branch pointed where it wanted me to look. Every scuffed stone sat exactly where a careless boot would leave it, which is to say nowhere a careless boot actually would.

Whoever I was following wanted to be followed. That should have frightened me off right there. Instead it did what curiosity does to people like me — people who've spent nine years being nobody in particular — it made me lean in.

The gorge narrowed through the afternoon until the walls came close enough that I could touch both sides at once, sandstone worn glassy by flash floods older than any map. Slot country. Beautiful, if you like beauty that can kill you in eleven minutes when the weather turns. The sky above was still that hard, cloudless blue that desert people learn not to trust, but the air had a weight to it, a wet green smell riding up from the river country below, and I didn't like the arithmetic of that at all.

The trail ended at a bend where the canyon pinched down to barely a body's width. A cairn of three stones sat in the middle of the floor. Freshly built. The mortar was still damp sand.

I stood there a long moment, hand on my rifle, feeling like a woman who'd been handed an invitation she hadn't asked for.

"Alright," I said to the canyon. My voice came back off the stone, flattened and strange. "You've got my attention."

Nothing. Wind, high up. Water dripping somewhere out of sight, patient as a clock.

I stepped past the cairn.

It was the wrong thing to do, or the right thing, depending on how you keep score. Three steps around the bend and something moved above me — a scrape of boot on rock, small, quick — and then a voice, high and cracked with disuse:

"Don't come closer. I have a knife."

Everyone has a knife. What matters is whether they know how to use it, and I could hear in that voice that he didn't, not really, not against anything that mattered. I kept my hands loose and visible and looked up.

He was wedged into a ledge maybe twelve feet up, a thin shape in an expedition jacket gone gray with dust, and he was holding a cartography knife — the little hooked blade draftsmen use for trimming film — in a grip so tight his knuckles had gone the color of bone. Sixteen, maybe. Younger-looking, the way hunger makes people younger. His eyes were doing something I recognized before I could name it: flicking between me and the canyon mouth behind me, calculating exits that didn't exist, running the same hopeless arithmetic I'd run a hundred times in nine years of running.

A cornered animal looks at the threat. A fugitive looks at the doors.

This kid was looking at the doors.

"Paloma Lockridge," I said, giving him the name because lying to someone that scared seemed like kicking a dog. "Ranger, once. Hired to track down whoever's been raiding the expedition stores." I let that sit, then added, honestly, "That's you, I'm guessing."

"I didn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter. "They're dead. All of them. You have to understand they're all dead, and I took the food because otherwise I'd be dead too, and I'm not sorry."

Well. That rearranged my whole afternoon.

* * *

Getting him down off the ledge took some doing. He wouldn't take my hand at first — fair enough; I wouldn't have taken mine either — so I backed off ten paces, sat down on a sun-warmed boulder where he could see everything I did, and talked to him the way you talk to a spooked horse, about nothing, about the weather, about how the light comes down these slots at this hour and turns the stone the color of old honey. Eventually hunger did what persuasion couldn't. He climbed down on his own, moving stiff, favoring his left ankle, and stood there swaying with the knife still in his fist like it was the last fact he owned.

"You're the thief," I said. "The outfitter in Redgate hired me to bring you in. He thinks you took four hundred dollars of provisions."

"He can have them back." A pause. "Some of them. I ate some."

"Some," I agreed. "What's your name?"

Long silence. I watched him decide, watched the war move across his face — trust a stranger or stay alone, and alone had gotten him this far, and this far was a slot canyon with a stolen knife and an ankle going bad. I knew that war. I'd been fighting it so long I'd stopped noticing the noise it made.

"Sebastian," he said finally. "Sebastian Voss. I'm the expedition cartographer."

"The survey expedition. Upriver." I felt the first cold thread of it then, the thing underneath the thing. "Where's the rest of your team?"

He looked at the canyon wall like the answer might be written there.

"We found something," he said. "At the dig site. The mining company said it was a gravel operation, but it wasn't gravel, it was — they were cutting into the aquifer, the deep one, the one the whole valley drinks from. And there were documents. Contracts. Names. Mr. Aldous said we had to report it, that it would ruin them, and then—" His breath went ragged. He pressed the back of his wrist against his mouth for a second, steadying himself, and when he spoke again his voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when someone has rehearsed saying something too many times to survive it any other way. "Then men came at night. Four days ago. They shot Dr. Ferro by the fire. They chased the others toward the river. I heard it. I was in the survey tent and I heard all of it, and I stayed under the table, and I didn't make a sound."

The cold thread went all the way through me.

"They burned the camp," he said. "After. I got out through the back flap and I ran, and I took food from the cache because I thought — I thought if I could get downstream, get to the district seat, tell someone—"

"And instead you left a trail a blind woman could read."

His chin came up. There was a spark in him under all that fear, and I remember being glad of it, the way you're glad to find one green thing growing in a burn scar. "I left it on purpose. I figured anyone tracking me was either a ranger or one of theirs, and either way I needed to see them coming. The cairn was a tripwire. If you were one of theirs, you'd have kicked it over and walked through, and I'd have known."

"And instead I stopped and admired it."

"And instead you stopped." Something almost like a shrug. "So I didn't run."

I looked at this boy — this starved, stubborn, terrified boy who'd survived a massacre by staying quiet under a table and had then spent four days alone in the worst country in the district, building stone riddles for whoever came hunting him — and I understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand things, all in one ugly piece.

First: I hadn't been hired to catch a thief. I'd been hired to catch a witness. Somebody in Redgate wanted Sebastian Voss found and brought back, and now I had to wonder whose money had paid my retainer, and whether the outfitter was just an outfitter.

Second: I was looking at myself at sixteen, if I'd been luckier and worse-off at the same time. A kid with a warrant's worth of trouble and no one in the world, walking a line between silence and survival.

Nine years I'd kept my head down. Nine years of nobody knowing my name, of paying cash, of sleeping facing the door. And here was the exact shape of my old life standing in front of me in a dusty expedition jacket, asking nothing, expecting less, holding a trimming knife like a rosary.

"Here's what happens now," I said. "You don't go back to Redgate. Not with me, not without me. Whoever burned that camp will want the loose end tied off, and you're it."

"Then where do I go?"

"Downstream. District court's at Harrow's Bend. If those contracts are real, they need to be in front of a judge before the monsoon seals the river, and the monsoon—" I glanced up at that too-blue sky, at the wet weight in the air, "—the monsoon isn't waiting politely this year."

"You'd take me?" He said it like a question in a language he'd half forgotten. "Why? You don't know me. You were paid to find me."

"I was paid to find a provision thief," I said. "Turns out there isn't one." I shouldered my pack and nodded up-canyon, toward the high ground, away from the river and the smoke-smell memory of that burned camp. "And because nine years ago somebody looked at me the way I'm looking at you, and kept walking. I've had time to think about how that felt."

He stared at me. Then, slowly, he bent and picked up his own pack — a child's pack, half empty, with a rolled bundle of maps strapped under it that he handled gentler than anything else he owned — and he fell in beside me, keeping a careful arm's length, the way you do with a stranger you've decided to bet your life on anyway.

We'd made perhaps half a mile, climbing toward the rim, when he stopped dead.

"What?" I said.

He was looking back down the gorge, at the thin ribbon of sky between the walls. "There was a radio," he said. "Satellite unit. Mr. Aldous called in our position every night at dusk. The night they came, the radio was smashed first. Before the shooting. Before anything." His eyes found mine, and I saw the cartographer in them now, the mind that plotted terrain and read patterns for a living. "They knew exactly where we were, Ms. Lockridge. Someone told them."

Somewhere behind us, faint as a held breath, an engine turned over in the far distance — down where the gorge met the flats, where no honest road went.

I put my hand on his shoulder and started us moving again, faster.
