## 4. What the Map Showed

I had never shown my maps to anyone who wasn't paid to look at them.

That's not quite true. Professor Ansel used to look at them for free, but she was paid in the currency that mattered, which was interest. She'd spread my drafts across the camp table and run her finger down the contour lines like she was reading a fortune, and she'd say, *Sebastian, you draw the land the way it wants to be seen.* I used to think that was the nicest thing anyone had ever said to me. I still think it. It's just that now the land wants to be seen for the worst possible reason.

The woman who'd cornered me in the slot canyon — Lockridge, she'd said, like a door slamming — sat across the fire from me with her rifle across her knees and her face doing something complicated. I'd spent two days being terrified of her. Now I was about to spend five minutes being terrified of myself, which was worse, because at least with her I'd had the option of running.

"You said you saw something," she said. Not a question. She had the kind of voice that made questions feel like a courtesy she was extending.

"I said I *drew* something," I said. "Seeing is easy. Anyone can see. Drawing is —" I stopped, because that sentence was going nowhere and I'd learned from Ansel to kill a bad sentence before it reproduced. "Can I just show you?"

She nodded once. So I unrolled my field case and took out the maps.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about cartography: it is an act of ruthless honesty. You can't fudge a contour line. The river either bends or it doesn't. The mesa either rises two hundred feet or it doesn't. If you lie on a map, someone eventually walks into the lie and breaks their ankle on it, or worse. So you learn early that the map doesn't care what you wish were true. The map is the one place in the world where you don't get to be a coward.

I'd been the expedition's cartographer for eleven months. I was sixteen, which meant everyone treated me like a mascot who happened to be useful, and I let them, because being underestimated is a kind of armor and I'd been wearing it since I was nine. My job was to map the upper Gila fork for the survey consortium — every arroyo, every seep, every place a road might one day go. I was good at it. I want that on the record. Whatever else happened, I was *good* at it.

Three weeks ago I was mapping a side drainage that wasn't on any of our preliminary surveys, because it wasn't supposed to be anything. A dry wash, a shelf of broken country, nothing. But the wash had fresh tire tracks in it, and tire tracks in country with no road are like a scream in an empty house. I followed them up onto the shelf.

That was where I found the dig.

* * *

"It's here." My finger shook, and I hated it, and I pressed harder to make it stop shaking, which didn't work. "The wash, see, and the shelf above it. I mapped the whole complex. Excavation pits — six of them, and they're not placer pits, they're engineered, shored, drained. There's a processing camp. There's an airstrip they graded in April, I dated it by the creosote they pushed over — the creosote was still green under the blade. That's a federal crime, by the way. Destroying creosote. I looked it up. It felt important at the time to know it was illegal for a small reason as well as a large one."

Lockridge didn't smile. But something around her eyes moved, like water under ice.

"The ore body runs under the river," I went on, because once I'd started I couldn't stop, the words coming out the way water comes out when a dam finally gives. "That's why it's illegal. The consortium's claim doesn't cover it. So the syndicate moved in first, quietly, planning to strip it and be gone before anyone surveyed the fork. Except *we* were surveying the fork. We were the one thing in the way."

"And the grave." Her voice was very even. Too even, the way a river is too even right before it drops.

My finger stopped.

The shelf above the dig had a shallow draw on its north side, screened by a stand of tamarisk that someone had — I still can't write this part in my head without my hand doing the shaking thing — that someone had *improved*. Cut and stacked to make a blind. And behind the blind, the draw had been disturbed. Not dug, exactly. Scraped. The way you'd scrape a hole for a septic line, if you were in a hurry and didn't care what the land thought about it afterward.

I'd walked down into the draw before I understood what I was walking into. I was sixteen and I'd spent eleven months learning to read disturbed ground, and I read it, and then I understood it, and there was a moment — I remember it exactly — where the world went very quiet and very clear, the way it does right before a storm, and I stood in that scraped draw and I knew. I didn't find anything I could point to. No — I'm not going to describe it. The map shows the draw. The map shows the tamarisk. The map shows the scrape marks and the disturbed soil and the drainage that had been rerouted, someone had *rerouted a drainage* to keep water out of it, which is the detail that lives in my chest at three in the morning, because you don't reroute water away from an empty hole.

"I mapped it," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "Every feature. Scale bar, north arrow, date. I did it right. I did it *correctly*, because that was the only thing I could do, standing there. I couldn't bury them. I couldn't — there were eleven of us on the expedition and I couldn't even —" I stopped. Breathed. "So I mapped it. The map is the testimony. I'm the chain of custody."

Lockridge was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere up the gorge, water was talking to itself the way water does before the monsoon, a low conversational mutter, like the river rehearsing what it was going to do to us.

"Eleven," she said finally. "You said eleven of us. How many are left?"

"One," I said.

* * *

Here is the thing about surviving. Everyone thinks the hard part is the moment itself — the running, the hiding in the rocks for two days while the shooting went on, the eating their food because the alternative was not eating. But that part was almost easy. Fear is very simple. Fear is a clean fuel. It burns hot and it tells you exactly what to do: *run, hide, be small, be quiet, be nothing.*

The hard part is afterward, sitting at a fire with a stranger, holding a map that proves eleven people existed, and understanding that the reason you're holding it is that you're the only one who could.

I should have been in camp that morning. I know the argument against that thought — Ansel would have made it, if Ansel were — she'd have said, *Sebastian, if you'd been in camp you'd be in the draw, and the maps would be ash with everything else.* I know it's true. Knowing a thing is true and feeling it are different countries and there's no road between them. I had mapped that country too. It had no ford.

"They came at dawn," I said, because Lockridge needed to hear it and because maybe saying it would move me one mile down that road. "Nine men, maybe ten. Syndicate colors on the trucks. Ansel went out to talk to them. She thought — she was a scientist, she thought evidence was a shield. She walked out with the survey permits in her hand like they were a wall." I looked at the fire. "They weren't a wall."

Lockridge's hand moved, an inch, across the rock between us. Not touching me. Just — present. Like a contour line marking a place where the ground dropped away.

"I was on the ridge doing a triangulation fix," I said. "Two miles out. I saw the smoke and I did the math every survivor does, the math where you calculate whether running toward it is love and running away is cowardice, and I ran away, and I've been running away for three weeks, and I stole food from your outfitter because dead men don't need provisions and I did." My voice cracked on *did* and I let it. "I'm not sorry I stole the food. I'm sorry about everything else."

"Good," Lockridge said.

I looked up. She was watching me with an expression I couldn't read, and then I could, and it was worse — it was recognition. Like she was looking at a map of a country she'd lived in.

"Being sorry is a debt you can't pay," she said. "Don't carry it into what comes next. You'll need both hands."

* * *

"So it goes to the district court," I said. I pulled the map case back toward me and started repacking it, roll by roll, because my hands needed a job or they were going to start doing the shaking thing again. "The dig, the grave, the airstrip, the whole complex. Original field drafts, my field notes, the date-stamped negatives. It's a complete evidentiary package. I've been carrying it for three weeks like it's a bomb, which it is, just a slow one."

"You understand what you're asking," Lockridge said. Her voice had changed. Flatter. Careful. "The district court is six days downriver. The monsoon breaks — what, a week? Ten days? After that the fords are gone and the gorge runs brown and nothing moves until September."

"Then we have a week."

"And the syndicate has nine men, maybe ten, and boats, and money, and every reason in the world to make sure a sixteen-year-old and his map case never reach a courtroom. You understand that too."

"I understand that they already killed eleven people to keep this quiet," I said. "I understand that if I stop now, they died for nothing, and I died for nothing, and the map might as well be ash. Yes. I understand it. I've had three weeks to understand it, alone, in a canyon, eating cold beans." I looked at her. "What I don't understand is why you're still sitting here. You were hired to catch a thief. Congratulations. Case closed. Walk away, collect your fee, go back to being nobody. That's the smart move. I'd do it. I *have* done it."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she was actually going to stand up. And I watched her — I'm a cartographer, I watch things, it's the whole job — and I saw her face do the thing that faces do when the map shows a drop-off where the traveler expected level ground. She'd been hired to find a symptom. She'd found the disease. And every instinct she had, you could see it, every line of her was built for vanishing, for being a ghost in a country full of ghosts, and I had just handed her a reason to be a person again, with a name, in a courtroom, on the record, under the eyes of everyone.

"I had a life," she said quietly, almost to herself. "It was small, but it was mine."

"I'm not asking you to like it," I said. "I'm asking you to get me downriver."

The fire cracked. Up the gorge, the river's mutter climbed half a tone, like it had overheard us and had opinions.

Lockridge looked at the maps, all of them, the whole honest terrible ledger of them, laid out in the firelight. Then she looked at me.

"All right," she said. "But we do this my way, and my way starts with the fact that nobody walks into a courthouse clean. So first we get to the river. And the only people on the river who might help us are people I swore I'd never see again."

She stood, and kicked dirt over the fire, and the dark came down on us like a lid.

"Sleep," she said. "We move at first light. And Sebastian —" She paused, and I felt her not-saying something, the way you feel a contour line under your boot before you see the drop. "When we meet the ferryman, let me do the talking."
