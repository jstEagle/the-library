## 5. The Syndicate's Men

The gorge talks to you, if you know how to listen. Water over stone has a vocabulary — the flat chatter of shallows, the hollow cough of a drop, the long vowel of a deep pool. I'd spent nine years learning to read it, the way a banker reads a ledger, and by the third morning in the gorge with Sebastian, I could tell you what the river was saying from a mile off.

What it said that morning was: *engines*.

Not river engines. Not the putter of a fisherman's skiff or the groan of a dredge. These were high, clean, expensive — the sound of outboards that had never known a hard day, the kind of money that doesn't like noise unless it's making it. Three of them, maybe four, coming up from the downstream narrows.

I had Sebastian flat against the canyon wall before he'd finished hearing them. He'd gotten better at that in two days — better at dropping when I dropped, moving when I moved, eating cold and walking quiet. The boy was a quick study. He'd had to be, to still be breathing.

"Syndicate?" he breathed.

"Sweep pattern," I said. "They're not hunting us. They're *hoovering*. Working the gorge bank to bank, pushing everything ahead of them downstream to the narrows, where somebody's waiting with a net." I'd run that maneuver myself, once upon a time, chasing rustlers across the Salt Flats district. It was a ranger trick. That was the part that sat wrong in my stomach, like a swallowed stone. This wasn't a posse. This was procedure.

Sebastian's face had gone the color of the limestone. "They did the camp like that," he said. "Bank to bank. I watched from the ridge." He didn't say more. He never said more, about that. He just went somewhere behind his eyes and I let him, because there was nothing I could say that wouldn't be a lie.

"Then we don't go downstream," I said. "We go *up*."

"The caves." He said it like a curse. He'd been looking at the caves since we'd entered the gorge — black seams in the canyon wall, a hundred feet above the waterline, weeping runoff. I'd been looking at them too, and I hadn't liked what I saw: the water staining below them was fresh, and heavy. The monsoon was still two hundred miles south and already the gorge was drinking its advance guard.

"The caves," I agreed, and hated it.

* * *

Getting up to them was the worst hour of the climb I'd done in nine years, and I have done climbs that would make a mountain goat file a complaint. The wall was slick with seep, the holds were rotten, and I had a sixteen-year-old on a rope I'd tied myself, which is a special flavor of terror — every knot you tie is a promise, and I've broken a lot of promises in my life. But Sebastian climbed like he drew maps: reading the rock first, planning three moves ahead, no wasted motion. Twice he found holds I'd missed. I tried not to think about what that said about my age.

The cave mouth was tall as a church door and breathing cold. We went in until the daylight went gray, then gray went to nothing, and I lit the candle stub I kept for reading maps at night. The flame bent toward the back of the cave. Air moving. Air moving meant a way through.

Air moving also meant the cave was connected to something, and I didn't know what, and I was about to lead a child into it because the alternative was men with expensive engines.

"Paloma." Sebastian's voice had gone strange. "Look at the water."

The floor of the cave sloped down and forward, and maybe forty feet in, it met the flood. Not seep — flood. Black water, dead still, stretching back into the dark past the reach of my candle. It was higher than it should have been. It was higher than it had any business being, with the monsoon still south of the border and the river below us running green and mean but not yet monstrous.

"The river's backed up," Sebastian said. He crouched at the edge and touched the water, and I saw him do the thing he did — the cartographer's thing, the reading of a country the way other people read a page. "There's a choke downstream. A slide, or a dam, or — " his voice caught — "or they built one. To raise the water level for their equipment." He looked up at me. "If the monsoon hits that choke, the whole gorge floods. Not in days. In hours."

Well. There was a cheerful piece of cartography.

"Then we go through," I said, "before it goes up."

* * *

I'll say this for flooded caves: they keep you honest. You can't hurry and you can't dawdle, because the water is either chest-high or it isn't, and the ceiling is either there or it isn't, and the difference between those two conditions is your whole life. We waded and swam and waded again, the candle stub protected in a tin I'd rigged, Sebastian's maps in oilskin strapped high on his back because he would drown before he let those go and we both knew it.

We came up into air in a gallery big enough to lose a cathedral in, and I was just thinking that maybe the gorge had decided to be kind to us when the sound came echoing down the passage behind us.

Voices. Boots on wet stone. And under it, that clean expensive engine note — they had a pump running, somewhere, or a generator. Men don't bring generators into caves for a casual look-see. Men bring generators into caves when they plan to stay, or when they plan to make someone else's stay permanent.

I killed the candle and put us against the wall, and we listened.

"—swept the lower gallery, nothing. But there's tracks, boss. Fresh. Two sets." A pause, and then a voice I'd have known anywhere, because it was the voice of every foreman who ever underpaid me and every sheriff who ever looked through me: bored, entitled, and cruel the way weather is cruel, without meaning to be and without stopping. "Two sets," it repeated. "The boy and the ranger."

My blood did something cold and slow.

*The ranger.* Not "a woman." Not "some drifter." The ranger. Paloma Lockridge, ex-district, warrant outstanding, nine years a ghost — and these men said my name like it was on their manifest, like I'd been expected.

Sebastian's hand found my sleeve in the dark. He couldn't see my face. He felt it anyway, the way you feel a change in weather.

"How do they know?" he whispered, so low it was hardly breath.

I didn't answer, because the answer was arithmetic and I didn't like the sum. Somebody had described me. Somebody had seen a woman and a boy moving up the gorge and reported it, and the syndicate had put the description together with the theft of a few sacks of flour and a rifle, and they'd known. They'd known a ranger was helping him. Which meant the warrant was no longer a piece of paper in a drawer in some district office. It was a tool in their kit. *Find the ranger, and you find the boy, and the ranger comes with a bounty and no questions asked.* I was worth more to them dead or delivered than I had ever been worth alive and free, and I had walked us both into their gorge like a lamb into a counting pen.

The old instinct rose in me then, the one that had kept me breathing for nine years. It's a simple instinct. It says: *split*. It says: *you are one, they are many, and one can fit through cracks that two cannot.* It says: *you've carried nothing for nine years and that's why you're alive.* It said it in my own voice, calm and reasonable, and it laid out the plan like a patient teacher: go left through the gallery, find the dry passage, double back, let them take the boy's trail and follow it to nothing, because a boy alone in a flooded cave is a tragedy and a woman alone is just weather.

And Sebastian stood in the dark beside me, breathing the way he breathed when he was being brave on purpose, and the instinct met the thing that had been growing in me since a slot canyon two days ago, and the two of them fought it out in my chest like dogs in a sack.

I had spent nine years becoming invisible, and I was good at it. I was the best there ever was at it. And invisibility, I understood in that black gallery with the water rising, is just another word for letting the world be cruel at its own convenience. Every year I'd run, someone else had paid for my silence. The outfitter who'd hired me had paid. The expedition had paid. This boy was about to pay, and the interest on my freedom was coming due all at once, with the monsoon.

"Paloma," Sebastian whispered again. Not *what do we do*. He knew better. Just my name, the way you'd say it to check that a light was still on.

The voices were closer. Boots in water, two men in the lead, the bored voice directing them with the ease of long habit. The gallery had three exits — I'd counted them by feel while I listened, because that's what you do, you do the work even when the work is hopeless. One went back the way we came. One went up, toward air I couldn't confirm. One went down, toward water I could hear moving faster than flood water should.

I took the tin out. I took the candle stub. And I did the thing the old instinct screamed at.

I lit it. In the open. In the gallery, where the light would carry.

Because here's the ranger trick they don't teach you in the brochures: men hunting in the dark are hunting *movement* and *sound*, and a light, steady and still, reads as neither. It reads as a camp. It reads as *already caught*. And men who think they've already caught you get lazy, and lazy men bunch up, and bunched men watch a light instead of the dark around it.

I set the tin on a ledge, flame steady, and took Sebastian's hand, and we went into the down passage, fast and silent, while four armed men converged on a candle.

The down passage dropped quick, the water going from ankle to knee to waist, cold enough to stop the breath. Behind us, a shout — the discovery, the anger, the bored voice gone sharp. A shot, wild, cracking off stone somewhere above and behind. Then another, and then the sound I'd been listening for: the shout of a man finding the water's true depth, and the general collapse of a plan built on laziness.

Sebastian laughed. One short, disbelieving bark of it, swallowed by the dark. "You *lit a candle*."

"Best decoy in the district," I said. "Don't get attached. I only had the one."

The passage turned, and turned again, and the water began to move with purpose, pulling at us, and the sound ahead changed — opened up, went from the close wet mutter of a cave to the long roll of open air and falling water. An exit. Maybe. The gorge doesn't promise you anything; it just changes the subject.

But as we went, hand over hand along the wall, the water rising around us with the patience of something that has all the time in the world, I understood the shape of the trap we were actually in. It wasn't the men. Men, I could handle. Men were weather too, and I'd been outlasting weather my whole life.

It was the knowing. They knew my name. They knew my trade. Which meant that from here to the district court — however many miles, however many days — every soul we passed could sell us, and the syndicate had already bought the list. There was no more moving quiet. There was no more ghost. The only thing left that they hadn't priced was speed.

The passage opened, and daylight — gray, bruised, monsoon-colored daylight — broke over us, and I saw where we'd come out, and I stopped breathing.

The river below the cave mouth had come up ten feet since morning. And down the gorge, around the bend, moving fast against the current, came three boats with expensive engines, running dark, heading for the one settlement on the river between us and the district court.

Ferrymen's Landing. Odile's Landing.

They weren't chasing us anymore. They were going to be waiting where we arrived — and the only person on this river who knew my face and my name was about to have to choose, all over again, whether to sell me.
