## 6. The Ferryman's Landing

I had drawn maps of places I'd never seen, which is a cartographer's way of lying professionally, but nothing I'd imagined of Ferrymen's Landing prepared me for how small it was. Six buildings and a boat ramp, wedged between the cliff and the river like a secret someone had almost finished telling. Smoke from a chimney. A dog that announced us from two hundred yards out and then lost interest in the announcement entirely.

Paloma stopped at the edge of the last bend in the trail and didn't move for a long moment.

"We can't go in there," she said. "Not this one."

"You said it was the only crossing before the gorge."

"I said what I said." She was watching the smoke the way she watched everything — like it might bill a receipt later. "The ferrywoman here knows my face."

"From the warrant," I said. She'd told me about the fire, the nine years, the name on the paper that wasn't hers to carry. Not all of it. Maybe not even half. But enough.

"Odile Vasseur testified at my hearing." Paloma said the name like she was setting down something heavy on the far side of a room. "She looked right at me and swore to what she saw. And what she saw wasn't even wrong. That's the worst part. She was honest."

"So we're honest back."

"That's not how honesty works when there's a price on your head, kid."

"I'm not a kid. I'm the expedition's cartographer."

"You're sixteen and you stole a month of provisions with trail markers neat enough to read like poetry. You're a kid with excellent penmanship."

I didn't have an answer for that, mostly because she was right, and partly because my stomach made a sound that undermined any authority I had left. Three days since the caves. Two since the gunshots in the gorge. We'd eaten the last of the dried figs at dawn and split them unevenly in my favor, which she thought I hadn't noticed, and which I had noticed so thoroughly it kept me up.

Paloma looked at the Landing for another full minute. Then she did the thing she does — the thing where she stops arguing with herself out loud and just moves, like the argument lost on points.

"Rules," she said. "You don't say my name. You don't say *your* name if anyone asks who isn't her. You let me do the talking until I signal otherwise. And if anything goes sideways, you take the boat and you row downstream and you don't look back, because the evidence matters more than either of us."

"The evidence doesn't matter more than—"

"It matters more than everything. That's why we're carrying it." She touched the oilcloth packet inside my jacket, where the maps rode against my chest, and for a second her hand stayed there, steadying me or herself, hard to tell. "Come on. If Odile's going to turn me in, better she does it while we're fed."

* * *

The ferrywoman was older than I expected and built like the dock pilings she presumably maintained — gray, salt-cured, immovable. She was mending a net on her porch when we came up the ramp, and she looked at Paloma for exactly as long as it takes to recognize someone you've testified against, then went back to her netting.

"Lockridge," she said.

"Vasseur." Paloma's voice came out flat and careful, a plank laid over water. "We need a crossing. Or a boat. We can pay."

"With what? You've been living off the land for—" Odile squinted at me. "How long has this boy been eating cactus?"

"He eats less than he complains. It balances."

"I don't complain," I said.

"You complained about the cactus for six hours."

"That was *commentary.*"

Odile set down the net. Something in her face shifted — not softening exactly, more like a lock turning. "Inside," she said. "Both of you. Before someone else sees you standing on my ramp looking like a pair of drowned cats who've read too many newspapers."

The house smelled of woodsmoke and fish stew and some herb I couldn't name and have been trying to name ever since. She sat us at her table without asking whether we were clean, which we were not, and put bowls in front of us before she asked a single question. I want to record that accurately: the questions came *after* the food. In my experience, most adults arrange it the other way around.

I ate like it was a competitive event. Paloma ate slowly, watching Odile, waiting — I could see her waiting — for the trap, the radio call, the knock at the door.

"Why are you doing this?" Paloma finally said. "You owe me nothing. You owed the court the truth and you gave it. That was the end of the transaction."

Odile ladled more stew into my bowl without consulting me. "You think I've spent nine years proud of that day?"

"You told the truth."

"I told the truth as I saw it, and the court used it to hang a girl who was running *from* the fire, not toward it." She sat down across from us. Up close her hands were enormous and scarred, rope-burned, river-marked. "I've had nine years to reread my testimony. I stand by every word of it. And I've regretted every word of it, because they weren't after truth in that room. They were after a name to pin to a syndicate fire, and yours was the cheapest one on the board. I handed them the cheap name."

Paloma said nothing. Her jaw did something complicated.

"So no," Odile went on, "I don't owe you. But I owe myself a correction, and corrections are expensive, so eat your stew."

* * *

They hid us in the boathouse behind the Landing, under the floor, in a crawlspace that smelled of tar and old rope. Odile brought blankets and, at intervals, more food, and news, delivered in a low voice through the floorboards: enforcers had been seen at the upstream ford, asking questions, showing a sketch of a woman and a boy. The monsoon was three days early. The river would seal the crossings soon.

"They know a ranger's helping you," Paloma murmured to me in the dark, once Odile had gone. "Which means they know I'm not working for them. Which means my name's worth money again."

"Then we leave tonight."

"On what? The river's already chewing the banks. And they'll be watching the water." She was quiet a while. In the dark I heard her breathing change, the way it did when she was doing arithmetic she hated. "Nine years, Sebastian. Nine years I've been nobody, and it worked. Nobody is safe. Nobody survives. And now there's a ferrywoman feeding me stew and calling me by my first name like we're neighbors, and I keep waiting for the invoice."

"Maybe there isn't one."

"There's always an invoice."

"Not from everyone." I said it before I could decide whether to. "My expedition leader, Dr. Amara — she used to say that people who keep score aren't your people. And the ones who don't..." I swallowed. The mass grave was very close to the surface of that sentence, the way it always was. "The ones who don't are rare. So when you find one, you don't spend the whole time checking the math."

Paloma was silent long enough that I thought she'd gone to sleep. Then: "Your Dr. Amara sounds like she'd have liked Odile."

"She'd have mapped her."

"Same thing," Paloma said, and I swear I heard something that was almost a laugh, worn thin but real.

* * *

Near midnight, the floorboards above the crawlspace creaked, and Odile's voice came down low and urgent.

"Lockridge. Get up here. Both of you."

We climbed out into the boathouse proper. Odile stood in the doorway with a lantern turned low, and behind her, out on the black water, a boat was coming upriver against the current — engine throttled way down, running dark, the way boats run when they don't want to be counted.

"Syndicate skiff," Odile said. "Third pass tonight. They're not searching the banks anymore. They're watching my landing." She turned and looked at Paloma, and whatever passed between those two women had nine years of interest compounded on it. "There's a channel behind the island, two miles down. My other boat's moored there — the fast one, the one I never tell anyone about. Charts are in the locker. Take it tonight, take the boy to the district seat, and get off my river before the flood takes you instead."

Paloma stared at her. "Why?"

"Because I testified once with half the facts," Odile said, "and I'm not doing it twice." She pressed a wrapped bundle into my arms — bread, cheese, a jar of that unnameable herb, and, inexplicably, a tin of peppermints. "And because your boy here looks like he hasn't slept indoors since the spring thaw. Go on. Both of you. Now."

As we slipped out the back toward the water, I heard the skiff's engine change pitch upstream — closer now, deliberate, a question being asked of the darkness.

Paloma's hand found my shoulder and pushed me low along the dock.

"Don't make a sound," she breathed. "Whatever happens next — *row.*"
