## 7. High Water

The monsoon came early, the way debts do.

I'd been counting on three more days. Three days of dry heat, of cracked mud, of a river still polite enough to stay in its bed. Odile had read the sky the night before and said nothing, just stood on her porch with her arms crossed, watching the clouds stack up over the western mesa like a man stacking sandbags. When I asked her what she saw, she said, "Weather that doesn't respect anyone's schedule," and went inside to sharpen a fish knife.

By dawn the first rain hit Ferrymen's Landing like gravel thrown by an angry god.

Sebastian was already awake. Of course he was. The boy slept like a cat in a house full of dogs — in snatches, one ear open, his maps rolled tight against his chest even under a borrowed blanket. He'd spread them out on Odile's table again before the coffee finished, and I watched him trace the river with one finger, the way you'd check a wound for infection.

"It's going to come up fast here," he said. "The gorge narrows at the Narrows — obviously — but past them it opens into the flats, and the flats flood wide and shallow. If we're on the water when it peaks—"

"We won't be on the water when it peaks."

"—then we ride the crest." He looked up at me with that flat, stubborn certainty he'd been carrying since the slot canyon, the certainty of someone who had already survived the worst day of his life and refused to be impressed by anything after it. "Paloma. Every hour we wait is an hour they get closer. You know that."

I did know that. That was the problem with the boy: he was right so often it was starting to feel like harassment.

* * *

We left mid-morning in Odile's best boat, which wasn't saying much. She called it *the Widow*, and she'd built it herself from cottonwood planks and forty years of stubbornness, caulked with pine pitch, patched in six places with tin. It sat low in the water and handled like a drunk mule, but it was sound, and sound was all we needed.

Odile walked us down to the landing through rain that came sideways now, hard enough to sting. The river had already climbed its banks, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees like kindling.

"Boat's yours," she said to Sebastian, pressing a canvas sack into his hands. Dried fish, hardtack, a water skin. Then she turned to me, and for a moment the old ferryman looked at me the way she must have looked at me nine years ago, across a courtroom, when her testimony helped put a warrant on my head for a fire I hadn't set.

"I testified against you because I believed what I was told," she said. "Not because I wanted to. I've had nine years to sit with the difference between those two things."

"You don't owe me an apology."

"No," she agreed. "But you're owed this: whatever happens upriver, there'll be a place at my table when it's done. Both of you. Don't make me regret setting it."

Then she shoved us off before I could find words, which was probably deliberate. Odile Voss did not traffic in moments. She trafficked in boats, dry socks, and functional sentences.

The current took us like it had been waiting.

* * *

For two hours we made good time. Sebastian steered from the stern with a broken oar and a cartographer's memory of every bend, calling the water before it came — "rock shelf, left side, hug the right" — and I bailed with a dented kettle and tried not to think about how much I'd come to rely on a sixteen-year-old's voice for ballast.

That was when I heard the engines.

You learn to hear engines in the desert the way you learn to hear snakes. This one was wrong — too steady, too powerful, an outboard where no honest boat should be running floodwater. I looked back and saw them rounding the bend behind us: two aluminum skiffs, gray, low-profile, each carrying four men in dark slickers. Syndicate boats. The same make I'd seen moored below the illegal dig, back when I'd thought I was tracking a provision thief instead of a massacre.

"Paloma." Sebastian had seen my face, not the boats. He didn't need to look.

"How many rounds you figure they're carrying?" I asked, mostly to keep my own voice level.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if they run dry before we run out of river."

The first shot skipped off the water ten feet short of us. Not a warning — a ranging shot. Professionals.

"Get down," I said, and put the kettle down, and picked up the rifle I'd kept wrapped in oilcloth under the thwart since the Landing. Old lever action, Odile's, sighted crooked but honest. I'd told myself it was for game. I'd known I was lying when I said it.

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and pauses and some dignity to them. What followed was a running gunfight conducted at twenty miles an hour through brown rapids in horizontal rain, and I want it noted for history that neither side could hit much of anything, and that the river was the deadliest participant.

They came at us in relays — one skiff holding our pace astern while the other swung wide to flank along the cutbank. I put three rounds into their engine housing on the flanking run and missed everything vital, but the splash pattern made their pilot flinch and clip a submerged snag, and suddenly we were down to one boat and a lot of shouting.

Then the river decided to participate.

The Narrows came up faster than Sebastian had called it — or he called it right and the flood simply outran his map, because maps are drawn on days when rivers behave. The channel squeezed, the water stood up, and the *Widow* went from floating to flying. We hit the standing wave at the throat of the gap and the world became brown noise. I lost the rifle overboard reaching for Sebastian as he slid across the thwart. I lost the supply sack somewhere in the next trough. I lost, briefly, any belief that we would surface at all.

We surfaced. The *Widow* shipped half the Narrows but kept her planks, and we shot out the far side spinning, bailer-less, rifle-less, foodless, pursued by one skiff whose pilot clearly knew these waters better than God intended anyone to.

A round took me then. Not clean through — a graze, along the outside of my left arm, hot as a stove lid and then cold as river water. I felt it more than heard it, a wet slap and a burn. Sebastian saw the blood and made a sound I'll remember the rest of my life, half shout, half something breaking.

"I'm fine," I said. "It's a scratch."

It was not a scratch. But it was also not an arm I needed, and that distinction has carried me through worse.

"They're herding us," Sebastian said, and there was something new in his voice. Not fear. Arithmetic. "Look — they're not trying to sink us. They keep pushing us toward the east bank. There's a slough up there, dead water, oxbow. They want us grounded in it where they can walk up and take the maps."

He was right. I could see it now that he'd said it — the patient geometry of the pursuit, the skiff holding always slightly upstream of us, nudging, herding. They weren't hunters anymore. They were sheepdogs.

And I realized, sitting in a leaking boat with a bleeding arm and no supplies, that I had spent nine years and this entire godforsaken week calculating escape. Which route out. Which cover. Which lie. Escape was my native language; I spoke it fluently and without accent.

But escape only works if there's somewhere to escape *to*. And there wasn't. The monsoon had sealed the desert roads. The river ran to the district court or nowhere. And behind us were men who had murdered eleven people over some survey sheets, who would murder two more without slowing their engines.

So I stopped calculating escape.

I started calculating victory, and let me tell you, it's a different arithmetic entirely. Escape asks: how do I get away? Victory asks: what do they need, and how do I take it from them?

They needed the maps destroyed and us dead, quietly, before the water dropped and the courts reopened. Quietly was the operative word. A syndicate that slaughters an expedition in the deep desert can do anything it likes out here — but the closer we got to the district seat, the thinner their armor got. Every mile downriver was a mile closer to witnesses, rangers, record books, law. They couldn't afford noise.

Which meant noise was a weapon.

"Sebastian," I said, tearing a strip off my shirt sleeve to bind the arm, "the radio. The salvaged one. Tell me exactly what it can do."

His eyes went wide, then narrow, then bright — the boy doing his own arithmetic. "Range depends on the antenna. With the mast from Odile's landing gear, maybe thirty, forty miles line of sight. Every ranger station in the district monitors the emergency band."

"And if someone broadcast on it? Loud? Long? Named names?"

"Then everyone hears it," he said slowly. "Including everyone they can't afford to have hear it."

The skiff behind us closed to fifty yards. A man stood in the bow with a shotgun, braced against the bucking water, waiting for a clean angle.

I grinned at him through the rain. He probably couldn't see it. That was fine. It wasn't for him.

"Hold us in the current," I told Sebastian, "and get ready to be famous."

Ahead, through the rain, the river bent toward the flats — and rising out of the flood haze, black against the gray, the mesa the boy had marked on his map as the highest ground for fifty miles.

I put the tiller over and aimed us straight at it.
