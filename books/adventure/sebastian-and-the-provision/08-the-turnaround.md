## 8. The Turnaround

The mesa had a name on Sebastian's maps, which was more than most things out here could say. *Kestrel Butte*, he'd written it, two years ago, in a hand so careful it looked like typesetting. He'd named it after a bird because from the air the rock supposedly resembled one with its wings folded. From the ground, at sunset, bleeding lightly and out of drinking water, it resembled nothing so much as a trap.

I crouched behind a wind-carved fin of sandstone and counted the syndicate's boats again. Three. They'd beached below the talus slope where the river bent, and six men were spreading out along the base of the mesa with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly how many ways off this rock there were. Which was one. The way we'd come up.

"Six," I whispered.

Paloma didn't look up from the radio. She had it open in her lap like a patient on her table — the expedition's satellite unit, the one I'd carried out of the camp in its padded case while everything I loved burned behind me. She'd spent the whole climb repairing it with wire stripped from my compass lanyard and a screwdriver she kept, apparently, in her boot, because of course Paloma Lockridge kept a screwdriver in her boot.

"Six is manageable," she said.

"For whom?"

"For whoever's smart." She twisted something inside the casing. A spark jumped, blue and brief. "We're not going to fight them, kid. We're going to make them irrelevant."

I want to record, for the court record or for whoever reads this someday, that I understood her immediately. That's the part nobody believes about me afterward — the quiet cartographer who suddenly found his voice. People like a transformation story with a clean hinge in it. But there wasn't a hinge. There was just a boy who had spent sixteen years learning that maps are arguments, that a line drawn in ink can move armies and close mines and hang men, sitting on a rock realizing that a radio is just a map made of sound. The territory was already there. All you had to do was draw it where everyone could see.

The problem was the drawing.

"The antenna's cracked," Paloma said. "And the battery's holding maybe forty minutes if we're lucky, twenty if we're honest."

"So we get twenty honest minutes."

"We get one shot. One broadcast. After that they'll triangulate us in—" she glanced at the sky, doing arithmetic with the last light, "—an hour, maybe less. And every syndicate gun between here and the district will know our coordinates to the meter."

"Then we should say something worth an hour."

She looked at me then. Really looked, the way she did when she was reading terrain — except I wasn't terrain, and we both knew by now that I never had been. Nine years she'd survived by being no one. I'd survived four days by being a rumor with a backpack. And here we were, cornered on a bird-shaped rock, about to become the two loudest someones in the district.

"You understand what this does," she said quietly. "Once your voice goes out over every ranger station, once the syndicate hears their own names in your mouth — there's no taking it back. No quiet grave. They can't bury a broadcast."

"That's the idea."

"It's also a death sentence if it fails."

"It's a death sentence if it fails either way," I said. "At least this way it's a loud one."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, but the shape a smile leaves behind when it's been living somewhere a long time. She handed me the handset.

* * *

Here is what I told the district, all twenty honest minutes of it, while the sun went down and six men started climbing:

I told them my name. Sebastian Vey, junior cartographer, Meridian Expedition, commissioned under survey license 4-118. I told them what we found at the Kettlebone dig — the tailings dumped straight into the river channel, the shafts driven under land the syndicate didn't own, the survey stakes pulled and burned. I read coordinates. I read them slowly, twice, the way you do when you want someone copying them down to keep up. Latitude, longitude, elevation, bearing. The dig. The equipment yard. The foreman's tent.

Then I told them about the grave.

There's no good way to say that sentence. I practiced it in my head for four days and there was still no good way. So I just said it: eleven members of the Meridian Expedition are dead in a mass grave two hundred meters east of the Kettlebone adit, and I can lead any officer in this district to them, and I have the field notes to prove who put them there. Names. I gave names. The site boss. The enforcer captain whose boats were at this moment parked beneath my feet. Two names I'd only heard shouted across a canyon at night, men who thought they were talking to each other and not to a boy pressed flat into the dark with his heart going like a jackhammer.

Paloma sat beside me with her rifle across her knees, watching the trail below, and when I stumbled she didn't prompt me. She just put her hand flat on the rock between us, palm down, and left it there until I found the next word. It's strange, the things that hold you up. Not speeches. Not courage. A hand on warm stone.

I ended the way she'd told me to end, back when this was still a plan instead of a prayer: *"This broadcast is logged and timestamped. Copies exist. Killing the witness now proves the crime. Come find me in daylight, in front of everyone, or don't come at all."*

Then Paloma took the handset, and I expected her to sign off.

Instead she said: "Ranger Paloma Lockridge. Warrant number 77-Calloway. I'm the one who's been keeping this boy alive, and I'll be standing next to him when he testifies." A pause. Below us, a man's voice swore, sharp and carrying. "That should save you the paperwork of figuring out who I am."

She set the handset down. The transmitter light died. Somewhere overhead, right on schedule, the monsoon clouds opened their first gray seam.

"Well," she said. "Now we're famous."

"Now we're targets with an audience."

"Same thing, kid. Same thing it's always been." She started gathering our gear with quick hands. "The difference is who's watching."

* * *

They came up the switchback trail in the last light, and here is the thing I will remember longest about that night, longer even than the gunfire: they came carefully. Slower than before. Because the six men climbing toward us were no longer hunting a thief and a stray ranger. They were hunting the testimony. Every step they took toward us now was a step taken on the record, witnessed by forty ranger stations, and every man among them had done the same math I had: a dead witness confirms everything; a live one might recant. We had turned ourselves into evidence, and evidence gets handled gently.

It doesn't mean they stopped shooting. It means they shot to wound, and shouted offers, and hesitated at the bad angles — and hesitation, on a cliff in the dark, kills differently but just as thoroughly.

Paloma fought like someone reading. That's the only way I can describe it. She never seemed to look at the men; she looked at the ground they had to cross, and she was already somewhere else when they got there. She rolled a cache tin down the switchback with a fuse of dry brush tied to it, and when they scattered from the noise she moved us forty meters along the rim through a gully I'd mapped that afternoon without knowing why. Cartographer's instinct, she called it later. I call it the first time my maps ever carried a human being instead of a line.

By full dark we were pinned at the mesa's north shoulder with the river roaring below, risen a full meter since noon, brown and insane with the first floodwater. The syndicate held the trail. The river held everything else.

"There," Paloma said, and pointed at the water.

"That's not a crossing. That's a drowning."

"It's a ford if you believe hard enough." She was already cinching the pack. "Ferrymen's Landing is nine miles downstream. Odile will have boats out looking for us — I told her to, if we weren't at the ford by dark."

"You told the ferryman who testified against you to come rescue us."

"I told Odile," she said, in a tone that closed the subject.

We went into the water together. I will not describe it except to say that the river in monsoon is not water, it is geology moving at speed, and that Paloma's grip on my collar never loosened once, and that when we finally dragged ourselves onto the downstream shingle, coughing, half-drowned, alive, the first thing I saw was a lantern swinging on the far bank and the silhouette of a broad woman poling a barge against the current like the river owed her money.

Odile. Coming for us. For the girl she'd once helped convict.

Paloma saw it too. She lay on her back on the gravel, chest heaving, and laughed — actually laughed, a short disbelieving sound torn out of her like a splinter.

"They're coming for us," I said.

"Everybody's coming for us now, kid." She sat up, soaked, grazed, grinning at the lantern light like it was a sunrise. "That was the whole point."

Behind us, on the mesa, the syndicate's lamps clustered and argued, unwilling to descend into the flood, unwilling to give up, and ahead of us the ferryman's barge swung into the current to collect two witnesses the whole district had heard speak with its own ears.

Nine miles to the Landing. Then the long water to the district court, and the monsoon rising behind us like a verdict.

Paloma stood, and offered me her hand.

"Come on, Sebastian Vey," she said, using my whole name like it was a flag. "Let's go be famous in person."
