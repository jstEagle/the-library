## 9. The Last Ford

The radio broadcast had done its work. Every ranger station in the district knew our names by dawn, and the syndicate's enforcers knew they'd missed their window to kill us quietly. Which meant they'd stopped trying to be quiet.

I heard them before I saw them — engines, three of them, grinding up the wash behind the mesa while Sebastian and I broke camp in the gray half-light. The monsoon had come down all night like a debt collector, and the river below us had gone from a brown ribbon to a brown country.

"They're early," Sebastian said, rolling the maps into the oilcloth with hands that only shook a little now. He'd stopped shaking much at all since the broadcast. Funny what having a voice does for a boy.

"Everything about this week is early," I said. "The rain. The river. My retirement from being nobody."

He almost smiled. Then the first shot cracked off the mesa's flank and spat rock dust between us, and we ran.

* * *

The plan — and I use that word generously, because plans are just prayers with better posture — was simple. The district courthouse sat on the far bank of the Cobre, two miles downstream of Ferrymen's Landing. Odile had told me about the ford below the old smelter works: knee-deep in dry years, chest-deep in wet ones, and this was going to be neither. This was going to be the year the river made its own rules.

We came down through the smelter ruins with the flood roaring through them, water hip-high and pulling like a crowd dragging you toward something you don't want to see. The enforcers' engines echoed off the canyon walls behind us. Ahead, across two hundred yards of churning brown, the courthouse clock tower stood up out of the rain like a finger raised in permanent objection.

"There," Sebastian shouted, pointing at the ford.

I looked. What I saw wasn't a ford. It was a rumor of land, a seam of white water where the current split around some buried shelf, and beyond it a stretch where the surface went smooth and deep and hungry.

"It's too high," he said. Not a question. He'd read my face the way he read contour lines.

"It's exactly high enough," I said, "that nobody will follow us into it."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be. Comfort is for people who aren't carrying evidence worth killing for." I checked the oilcloth bundle strapped to his chest, checked the knot twice, then put my hands on his shoulders and made him look at me. "Listen to me. The river takes what fights it sideways. You go straight at the far bank, feet up if you go under, and you do not stop moving until your boots hit stone on the other side. Can you do that?"

"I can do that."

"You're lying."

"I'm sixteen," he said. "Lying is most of what I've got left."

I laughed — actually laughed, there at the edge of the flood with bullets starting to sing past us again — because what else is there? Fear and laughter are the same animal wearing different coats.

* * *

The last two hundred yards to the ford were the longest of my life, and I have spent nine years collecting long ones.

We ran the smelter's collapsed rail bed, water to our thighs now, the current shoving at us like it had opinions. Behind us the enforcers had abandoned their vehicles and come on foot, four dark shapes fanning out through the rain. One of them had a rifle with a scope, and I could feel him finding his range the way you feel a stranger watching you across a room.

A round skipped off the rail ahead of Sebastian. He flinched but didn't stop. Good boy. Better than good — he was reading the water as we ran, calling out the deep channels before I could spot them, navigating by instinct and training the way I navigated by sand and scat. We made a strange kind of instrument together, the two of us.

The ford opened up ahead, that seam of white water, and beyond it the smooth brown throat of the main channel. On the far side, maybe forty yards of floodwater away, the courthouse steps rose above the high-water line. There were people on them. Umbrellas. A clerk under an awning, watching the river with the idle curiosity of a man whose day has not yet been ruined.

"Paloma." Sebastian grabbed my sleeve. His eyes were huge and steady. "If I go under—"

"You won't."

"But if I do, take the maps. Don't come after me. Promise."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"Promise anyway."

So I promised, which is how I learned that lying is most of what both of us had left.

Then the scoped rifle spoke again, and this time it found me.

Not clean — a graze along the outside of my left arm, hot as a stove lid, tearing cloth and skin and setting my whole side alight. I went down into the flood on one knee and the current immediately tried to collect me, and I understood in one cold bright instant that the river was going to kill me faster than any bullet if I stayed in it.

So I got up. That's most of rangering, when you boil it down. Getting up with style optional.

"Go!" I shoved Sebastian toward the seam of white water. "Now! Straight across!"

He went. I went. And the river took us.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about flood crossings: it isn't swimming. Swimming implies a negotiation. This was being carried — seized by the whole weight of a watershed and shaken like a dog shakes a rat. I lost my boots. I lost my hat, my knife, nine years of careful anonymity peeling off me item by item like the river was undressing the ghost down to the woman. My arm screamed. I kept my head up and my body angled and I watched Sebastian ahead of me, a small dark shape riding the chaos, feet up once when he went under, coming up again, always coming up, until the far bank's pull caught him and dragged him sideways into the shallows below the courthouse steps.

I came ashore thirty yards downstream, on my hands and knees, coughing half the Cobre back into itself. My arm hung wrong-wet and red. Somewhere behind us, across the flood, the enforcers stood at the water's edge with their rifles lowered, because even men who murder cartographers don't shoot people on the courthouse steps in front of witnesses. Fame, it turns out, is armor. Sebastian had known that before I did.

Boots splashed toward me. Strong hands took my elbows and hauled me upright, and I looked up into the face of a court bailiff, a big gray-mustached man who stared at me — soaked, bleeding, bootless, half-drowned — with the expression of a man deciding whether I was a threat or a miracle.

"Ma'am," he said finally, "you can't come in here like that."

"Watch me," I said.

Sebastian was already on the steps, oilcloth bundle held over his head like an offering, shouting my name — my actual name, Paloma Lockridge, spoken aloud in daylight for the first time in nine years — telling anyone who would listen that he had evidence for Judge Amara Voss and he needed it entered on the record *today*, before the river rose again or the syndicate's lawyers arrived, whichever came first.

And here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because everything else in this account is true and this should be too: standing at the bottom of those steps, bleeding into the floodwater, I felt lighter than I had in nine years. Not brave. Lighter. Every alias I'd ever worn, every town I'd slipped out of at midnight, every name I'd never given — the river had taken them all, and what was left standing there dripping on the stones was just a woman with a warrant and a choice already made.

There is a particular freedom in having nothing left to hide. Nobody tells you that either. They tell you anonymity is safety, and it is, the way a coffin is safe.

I climbed the steps. Rain on my neck, blood down my arm, the whole district's attention arriving in whispers — *that's her, that's the ranger, that's Paloma Lockridge* — and none of it mattered, because ahead of us the courtroom doors stood open, warm light spilling out, and inside somewhere a judge was waiting who didn't yet know she was about to become the most important person in the territory.

The bailiff stepped aside. Sebastian walked through first, chin up, maps held tight. I followed, leaving wet bootless prints on the marble like a signature.

Behind us, the river kept rising. Ahead of us, the doors began to close, and a voice from inside — dry, unhurried, official — asked the room:

"Who, precisely, is interrupting my session?"

Sebastian opened his mouth. So did I.
