## 10. What Stays

The courthouse in Redstone Flats had a clock that ran four minutes slow, which the clerk swore was a metaphor and the judge swore was a budget issue. I sat through three days of testimony with my hands folded like a woman who'd never once considered running, and I will tell you honestly: it was the hardest sitting still of my life.

The syndicate's men went first. Their lawyers wore good boots and better cologne, and they talked about *operational misunderstandings* and *contested mineral rights* until Judge Amara Voss told them, in a voice dry as August, that she had seen the maps, the photographs, and a sixteen-year-old boy who could recite coordinates from memory, and that if they said "misunderstanding" one more time she would add contempt to the docket personally.

Sebastian testified on the second day. He wore a shirt Odile had pressed for him and his surveyor's satchel over one shoulder like a shield, and when the defense attorney tried to rattle him — asked how a boy could be sure what he'd mapped from a ridge two miles off — Sebastian set his satchel on the stand, drew out the field book, and read back elevation figures to the decimal.

"I don't guess," he said. "I measure."

The room went quiet the way rooms do when somebody says something true and simple. I watched from the gallery and thought: I carried this kid through a flood so he could say that sentence to a courtroom, and every mile of it was worth the price.

The price being, mostly, me.

* * *

My own hearing came last, tacked onto the end of the session like an afterthought, which in fairness is exactly what it was. Nine years ago a warrant went out with my name on it — Paloma Lockridge, arson, the Meridian Station fire — and for nine years I'd built my whole life around not hearing that name spoken aloud. I'd paid cash. I'd used ranger channels instead of official ones. I'd learned to sleep light and leave early and love nothing that couldn't be abandoned by dawn.

Now here I was, standing in front of a judge, while a clerk read the charges into the record.

"Ms. Lockridge," Judge Voss said, "the state has moved to dismiss. Before I rule, does anyone wish to speak?"

And then Odile Marchand stood up.

I want you to understand what that meant. Odile had testified against me nine years ago. She'd been the ferryman at the Landing when the fire happened, and she'd sworn under oath that she saw my skiff near the station boathouse the night it burned. She wasn't lying, either — she *had* seen my skiff there, because I'd been there, because I'd been the one who found the fire already burning and pulled the night watchman out before the roof came down. The truth got mangled somewhere between her mouth and the prosecutor's pen, and by the time I understood what the warrant said, I was already gone. Running felt safer than explaining. Running always did.

So when Odile rose from the gallery benches, my stomach dropped through the courthouse floor and kept going.

"Your Honor," she said. "Nine years ago I told this court I saw Paloma Lockridge's boat at the Meridian boathouse the night of the fire. That was true. What I didn't say — what I let them talk me out of saying — is that I also saw her carry a man out of it." She turned and looked at me, and her face was as flat and unimpressed as it had been the day she hid us from the syndicate's boats. "I was young and scared of the wrong people, and I've spent nine years knowing it. I'm done."

Then half the gallery stood up. The Landing travels badly — it's two days downriver and nobody there trusts a road — but they'd come. Marta Vasquez who runs the drying sheds. Old Teo who hasn't left the Landing since the flood of '09 except once, and he complained about it for a decade. A dozen others, sunburned and river-marked and entirely unbothered by the formality of a courtroom, telling a judge in their plain way that Paloma Lockridge had run supplies during the high water, patched hulls, hauled sandbags, and generally behaved like a person worth sheltering.

Teo's entire testimony, delivered without being sworn in because he refused to raise his right hand on principle: "She stayed. People who burn things don't stay."

Judge Voss looked at all of us for a long moment. Then she said, "Dismissed," and struck the warrant, and the gavel came down, and I stood there feeling like a woman whose shadow had finally caught up with her and turned out to be friendly.

I cried a little. I'll deny it if you quote me.

* * *

Weeks later, the river had settled into its post-monsoon mood — lower, clearer, running green-gold over the gravel bars — and I was staying at the Landing for what I kept telling everyone was a short visit. I'd said that four weeks running. Odile had stopped pretending to believe me around week two and started giving me chores instead, which is the Landing's way of adopting you.

That morning I was re-caulking a seam on the *Marta B* when Sebastian came down the bank with his satchel and a look on his face I recognized, because I'd invented it: the look of a person rehearsing something.

"You're doing the thing," I said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you walk up like you're going to ask directions and actually you've got a speech prepared."

He scowled. He's gotten better at scowling; the survey office has been good for him. They gave him a desk with a real drafting table and a supervisor who calls him "Mr. Okafor" like he's a grown man, and he's grown two inches and gotten opinionated about contour intervals. The indictment went up the chain — three syndicate directors, the site foreman, two men whose names I only knew as guns in the gorge — and Sebastian's maps are Exhibit A, photographed and laminated and treated with more respect than most people treat him. He carries copies everywhere anyway. Some habits keep you alive; nobody tells you when to put them down.

"The district office wants to extend my apprenticeship," he said. "Through the winter survey season. Full mapping of the upper gorge, the parts the syndicate dug up. Restoration surveys." He paused. "They need someone who knows the terrain."

"You know the terrain."

"I know the terrain." Another pause, longer. He looked at the river instead of at me, which is a family trait he picked up fast. "Odile says you've been fixing boats like you're planning to stay. But you haven't unpacked your pack. It's still by the door. I checked."

"You checked my pack?"

"It's what I do. I observe things." He finally looked at me. Sixteen going on seventeen, with the massacre and the flood and the mesa behind him, and still somehow just a kid asking a question he was afraid of the answer to. "Are you leaving?"

Here's the thing about nine years of running: it stops being a choice and becomes a shape. You get poured into it. Every morning I woke up at the Landing and my first thought, automatic as breathing, was *what's the exit*. Where's the boat, where's the high ground, what's the wind doing. I'd built a whole self out of leaving, and I'd gotten so good at it that staying felt like walking in a house with low ceilings — constantly ducking, constantly braced.

But I'd also noticed things. That I knew which floorboard in Odile's kitchen creaked and had stopped avoiding it. That Marta saved me the ends of the dried apricots because she'd figured out they were my favorite. That when the radio crackled with a report of trouble upriver, my first thought wasn't *not my problem* anymore. It was *who do we send*.

We. There it was. The word had snuck in sometime around the third week and hadn't left.

"No," I said. "I'm not leaving."

Sebastian nodded, very seriously, the way he did when a measurement confirmed itself. Then he ruined the moment completely by adding, "Good, because Odile already gave your bunk to the winter storage crates and she said she'd move them back but she hates moving crates, so."

"So it was decided without me."

"Everything here is decided without you. That's what belonging is. Nobody asks permission." He shouldered his satchel. "Come by the office tomorrow? I want to show you the new base map. I put the Landing on it properly this time. Named features and everything."

"Named after whom?"

He grinned — the first real grin I'd seen on him since the courthouse — and headed up the bank, calling over his shoulder: "You'll have to see the map."

I stood there a while with caulk on my hands and the river talking its endless talk against the hull, and I thought about the woman who wrote that first contract in my ledger nine years ago: *keep moving, trust no one, leave before dawn.* She'd served her purpose. She'd kept me alive long enough to find something worth stopping for.

I went inside, found my pack by the door, and unpacked it. All of it. Boots in the corner, spare shirt in the cedar chest, the old ranger knife on the hook by the stove where Odile had been pointedly leaving space for a month.

Then I picked up the caulking iron and went back to work on the *Marta B*, because winter was coming, and for the first time in nine years, I planned to be there for it.
