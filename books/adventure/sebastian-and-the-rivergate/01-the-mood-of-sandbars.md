## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river gave nothing away for free. That was the first thing Wren Winterbourne had learned, back when they had another name, and it was the last thing they intended to forget.

They stood at the wheel of the *Marguerite* as dawn came up gray over the Rivergate, one hand on the spoke, the other around a tin cup of chicory gone cold an hour ago. The water ran high and brown with the spring flood, carrying fence posts, a dead ewe, somebody's roof thatch — the river's annual accounting of what people had failed to hold on to. Wren piloted through it the way they did everything: without hurry, without flourish, without leaving a mark.

Eleven years on this water. Eleven years of favors traded and questions dodged.

"Sandbar's moved," Tammi said, coming up the companionway with the chart roll under her arm. She was nineteen, all elbows and appetite, the closest thing Wren had to a crew, a friend, and a conscience, in roughly that order. "Or it's gone. Hard to tell with the flood."

"It's moved." Wren eased the wheel a spoke to port. "Two lengths east. The flood scours the shallows every spring, then drops its leavings where it likes. By midsummer it'll settle back and pretend it never happened."

"Like you."

Wren looked at her. Tammi grinned, unrepentant, and unrolled the chart on the binnacle box, weighting the corners with the cup and a coil of line. She'd earned the right to say such things. Wren had pulled her out of the floodwater at Corley Bend six years ago, half-drowned, orphaned twice over, and had never once asked what she'd been doing in the water. Fair trade. She'd never once asked what Wren had been doing before the *Marguerite*.

"Grain for Mallow's Landing," Tammi said, tapping the chart. "Then the Vantor woman's crate at Pell's Wharf, then back down before the fleet traffic picks up."

"Not the fleet traffic." Wren said it lightly, but their eyes went to the downstream horizon, where the water widened toward the salvage yards. "Anything flying the Ferran colors, we give wide berth. Wide as the river allows."

"You think they'll come up this far?"

"The flood brought everything else."

* * *

The *Marguerite* was forty feet of patched oak and stubbornness, a shallow-draught trader built for exactly the life Wren had chosen: too small to be worth robbing, too slow to be worth chasing, too ordinary to be remembered. Wren had paid for her in coin that couldn't be traced, registered her under a name that existed on no other document in the world, and spent a decade making sure that the woman — or the man, depending on the dock, depending on the year — who captained her was nobody in particular.

It was a craft, invisibility. Wren practiced it daily. They kept no ledger of debts, only a memory of them: a bolt of canvas owed to the ferryman at Tull, a favor owed by the widow Sarn, whose roof Wren had patched in a night of freezing rain and never mentioned again. They drank one cup at the Grackle and left before the second, because the second cup was where names got asked for. They knew every shoal, every snag, every drowned fence line in forty miles of river, and they knew which of the riverfolk would lie for them and which would simply forget them, and both kinds were precious.

The flood was the one thing they couldn't pilot around. It came every spring like a bill collector, rearranging the channel, drowning the low pastures, and then, in its going, exposing things.

That was how, three days into the drop, with the water falling a hand's width a day, Wren saw the wreck.

* * *

They were running light, downbound past the Rivergate shallows, when Tammi, up on the bow with the sounding pole, went still in a way that made Wren cut the engine.

"Captain." She was pointing. "That wasn't there last spring."

It lay in the shallows where the flood had stripped away six feet of silt: a hull, black with age, listing hard to starboard, its ribs showing through the torn planking like the fingers of a hand pushed up through a grave. River-worms had been at it for decades. The stern was buried still, but the bow stood proud of the mud, and on the bow, under a skin of algae, there had been a name once. Wren could read only the last three letters, and they were enough to make their mouth go dry.

*—any.*

Not a riverboat's name. Not a name from any registry Wren knew. The lettering had been gilded once, and gilded lettering meant money, and money meant it had come from downstream, from the capital, from the years when the Republic of Vessany still had a capital to send things from.

"Old wreck," Tammi said. "War years, maybe. Lots of boats went down in the Gate back then."

"Lots of boats," Wren agreed. Their voice came out level. They were good at level. It was, along with the shoals, their finest professional skill.

But they took the *Marguerite* in close that evening, when the light was going and the mudbanks were empty, and they anchored where no one anchored, and they looked at the wreck for a long time with a glass. The hull was a packet boat, a government class, the kind the Republic had used for couriers and paymasters. And wedged in the broken rail of the bow, glinting dully where the falling water had bared it, was something that did not belong to the river at all.

A chest. Lead-sheathed, strapped, half-buried in the mud of the foredeck. And on its lid, where the algae had sloughed, a stamp pressed deep into the metal: a tower over crossed keys.

The seal of Vessany.

Wren lowered the glass. Their hands were steady. They noted that, distantly, the way a pilot notes a change in the current — with attention, without alarm, and with a full and immediate understanding that the alarm would come later, at its own convenience.

"Captain?" Tammi, at the rail. "What is it?"

"A sandbar," Wren said. "Nothing. The flood moved the sandbars."

* * *

That night they lay in the *Marguerite*'s narrow cabin and did not sleep. The lamp was out. The river slapped the hull in its patient rhythm, and Wren stared at the overhead and thought about names.

Their old name had a shape. They could still feel it, the way an amputee feels a hand: two syllables, a certain weight on the tongue, spoken last in a courtroom they had walked out of alive while better people did not. Eleven years since anyone had spoken it aloud in their hearing. Eleven years of being the quiet captain of the *Marguerite*, who patched roofs and repaid canvas and never drank the second cup. It had been a good life, or the nearest thing to one that a person built on a deletion could have. The comfort of it had been real. That was the thing no one warned you about — that hiding could feel like peace, right up until it didn't.

And the river, their old teacher, their only honest creditor, had gone and laid a gilded name and a Vessany seal in the shallows like a gift on a doorstep.

*It gave nothing away for free.*

Wren knew the arithmetic. Whatever lay in that lead chest, the Republic had thought it worth a courier boat and a sealed sheathing worth more than the boat. Whatever it was, it had outlasted the Republic, the war, the minister who'd signed the manifests, and every person who might have been looking for it — except now the flood had bared it, and floods were public events, and news traveled faster than boats on this river. Fishermen would have seen the wreck by tomorrow. By the day after, someone would have sold the sight of it to someone who sold things to the salvage fleet, and the salvage fleet flew Odile Ferran's colors, and Odile Ferran counted wrecks the way other people counted money, because wrecks *were* money, and she did not leave them in the water.

And if Ferran came — and Ferran would come — she would bring her dredges and her divers and her ledgers, and she would raise the chest, and she would read whatever was in it. And somewhere in her files, in her memory, in that patient bureaucratic skull, there was a name with two syllables that she had spent part of a career hunting, and a description of the methods of the pilot who had once run the Republic's last blockade, and Wren's methods had not changed, because they had always been good.

Sleep was not available. Wren got up, lit the lamp, and took out the river chart, and looked at the mark they'd penciled that evening at the edge of the Rivergate shallows: a small, careful X, the kind any pilot makes for any hazard.

It didn't look like a treasure map. It looked like a grave marker. It looked like the first entry in a ledger they had spent eleven years refusing to keep.

Outside, the water fell another hand's width. Somewhere under the dropping river, the gilded letters waited, and the lead chest waited, and whatever confession or curse the Republic had sheathed in metal to keep from rotting waited with them.

Wren blew out the lamp.

"Nothing's free," they said to the dark, and the river slapped the hull, taking its time, the way it always did, like something that already knew they'd come back for it.
