## 2. What the River Keeps

The chest sat on the Marguerite's deck all night, and Wren did not touch it.

That was discipline. That was the whole architecture of the life they'd built—discipline laid over instinct like planking over a hull. You didn't open things the river gave you. The river gave nothing; it only lent, and it collected with interest. Wren had watched men pull drowned strongboxes out of the shallows and be dead within a season, not from the water but from what came after—the questions, the neighbors' eyes, the knock at the cabin door at an hour when honest people slept.

So Wren left it under an oilcloth by the stern rail and drank coffee in the pilothouse and watched dawn come up gray over the Rivergate, and told themselves that by noon they'd row it back out and drop it where they'd found it.

By noon the tide had turned and the chest was still there.

* * *

Tammi came aboard at first light, as always, boots wet to the ankle, carrying two loaves and a look that said she'd already heard everything worth hearing on the dock. She was nineteen, quick-handed, orphaned by the flood three years back, and she had never once asked Wren a question about the past. It was the thing Wren trusted most about her, and the thing that made them uneasy, because nobody who grew up on this river was incurious by nature. Curiosity got beaten out of you or buried deep, and Tammi's was buried so deep you could sometimes see it moving, like a pike under ice.

"Morning, Captain." She set the bread down on the chart table and looked directly at the oilcloth shape lashed near the stern. "That wasn't there yesterday."

"No."

"It's got a lock on it. Brass. Fancy."

"The river's full of fancy things."

"Mm." Tammi poured herself coffee without asking, which was also part of the arrangement. "Dockmaster's boy says the wreck showed up after the flood dropped. Says it's sitting high enough now that half the town's been out to gawk. Says there's a man from Corvess offering coin for anything salvaged off her, no questions."

Wren kept their face level. "Since when does Corvess pay for junk?"

"Since yesterday, apparently." She blew across her cup. "You going to tell me what's in the box?"

"I'm going to tell you we've got cargo runs Tuesday and Wednesday, and if we take them both we eat through winter."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got today."

Tammi looked at them for a long moment—she had her mother's patience and her father's stubbornness, and she deployed both like weapons—and then she nodded once and went forward to check the lines, and let it lie. Wren exhaled slowly and understood that they had just spent something. Not trust exactly. Credit. And credit, on this river, ran out faster than money.

* * *

They ran the Tuesday run anyway, because running cargo was the other half of the architecture: a boat that moved on schedule was invisible. Wren took the Marguerite upriver with a hold full of salt fish and barrel staves, poled her through the narrows where the current ran green and fast, and thought about nothing but channel markers the entire way, which was the point of the exercise. The body can be ordered to think about one thing. It obeys, mostly. It's the mind that wanders off leash.

At the Corvess landing, while the mate counted barrels, Wren heard the name again from two dockhands talking low: Ferran. Odile Ferran. Salvage fleet, six hulls, working down from the headwaters. Buying salvage rights, buying silence, buying whatever couldn't be bought taken instead. One of the dockhands spat over the side when he said the name, which told Wren more than the words did.

Wren signed the manifest with their left hand, the way they always did, letters slightly wrong, a signature that belonged to nobody. Then they went back to the boat and stood at the rail and watched the water slide past, brown and patient, holding everything it had ever taken.

Six tides, the dockhands said. After that the wreck would shift into the deep channel and no chain forged would reach her.

The chest rode home in the hold, under canvas, and Wren found themselves checking on it every hour like a sick passenger.

* * *

Wednesday night, alone in the cabin, Wren opened it.

Not impulse. Necessity—or the story they told themselves was necessity. If the fleet was coming, if Ferran was coming, then Wren needed to know whether the chest was worth killing for, because things were only worth killing for when someone believed they proved something. Junk could be sunk again. Proof could not.

The lock was brass and good, but the hasp had rotted, and a marlinspike persuaded it. Inside, wrapped in oilskin gone stiff as bark:

Ship's manifests. Three of them, Vessany navy paper, the seal stamped deep—the twin rivers and the broken crown. Cargo listed as grain. Weights that didn't match any grain shipment ever loaded. Wren had seen ledgers like this before, in another life, another name. Grain was what you wrote when you were moving gold.

Beneath the manifests, a sealed letter. Wax unbroken, the same seal pressed into it, and across the front, in a clerk's careful hand: *For my daughter, Ilse, should I not return.*

And beneath that, folded small, a map. Hand-drawn, annotated in two different inks, showing the upper reaches of the Vessany watershed—a country that hadn't existed on maps for eleven years—and marked, in the second ink, with a symbol Wren knew. Everyone who'd served knew it. The treasury mark. The cache-sign of the Republic's last government-in-exile funds.

Wren sat very still in the lamplight and listened to the river against the hull.

They should burn it. All of it. Feed it to the stove page by page and watch the wax bubble, and by morning there would be nothing but a scorch smell and a story about driftwood. That was the smart move. That was the move that kept the life intact—the schedule, the favors, the left-handed signature, the beautiful weightless anonymity of being no one.

Instead Wren broke the seal.

The confession inside was long, written in a cramped, failing hand, and Wren read it twice before their eyes would accept what it said. The last minister of Vessany—Minister Edran Vantor, whose death the whole river country had toasted eleven years ago, whose drowning had ended the war in every official account—had not drowned. He had lived. He had lived hidden, on Republic gold, moving between safe houses, writing this confession in installments like a man paying off a debt he knew he'd never clear. And the map, the treasury—it was real, it was reachable, and it was enough money to arm every grievance between the headwaters and the sea.

At the bottom of the final page, dated four years ago, the signature.

Wren's hands began to shake.

Not at the minister's name. At the second name, the witness line, the testimony cited in the minister's own account—the statement given by a survivor of the *Larkspur* incident, extracted under coercion, the statement that had sent Vantor running and confirmed his survival to the very people hunting him. Wren knew that testimony. Knew it word for word. Had given it, in a room with a hot lamp and a man who smelled of cloves, in another life, under another name, at an age when they'd have said anything to make the pain stop.

The lamp hissed. The river breathed against the hull. Somewhere upstream a night bird called once and quit.

"You're awake." Tammi's voice, from the cabin door.

Wren hadn't heard the deck boards. That was how far gone they were. They closed the confession too late—Tammi was already looking at the seal fragments on the table, the manifests, the map spread open like a wound.

"I knocked," she said. "Twice."

"Go back to bed, Tammi."

She didn't move. She was holding her hat in her hands, which she did when she was scared, though she'd deny it under torture. "Fleet scouts came through the landing tonight," she said. "Two boats, asking after anyone who'd pulled salvage from the Gate. Dockmaster gave them your name."

"Not my name."

"Gave them *Captain Winterbourne*, is what I'm saying, and the tall one wrote it down like he already knew it was false." She stepped into the cabin, set her hat on the table next to the dead minister's confession, and looked Wren in the eye. "I don't know what you did before I came aboard. I never asked. I'm asking now, once: are we in trouble?"

Wren wanted to lie. Had the lie ready, even—driftwood, bad luck, none of your business, go to bed. But Tammi was standing there with her hat in her hands, and the confession was open on the table between them, and somewhere downstream six hulls were sliding toward the Rivergate with a commander who had hunted people like Wren for a living.

"Yes," Wren said. "Probably."

Tammi nodded slowly, absorbing it, the way she absorbed everything—completely and without visible panic. Then she put her hand flat on the table, next to the map.

"Then here's what I know," she said. "Three years you've paid my wages on time, taught me the narrows, never once lied to me until tonight. Whatever's in that chest, whoever you were before—you're my captain. That doesn't change unless you make it change."

"Tammi—"

"I'm not done." Her voice stayed low, steady, deckhand-steady, the voice you used in weather. "I'll help you. Sinking it, burning it, running it upriver, delivering it—I don't care which. But captain, your hands are shaking, and I've steered beside you through ice and flood and I have never once seen your hands shake. So whichever it is, decide fast. Because those scouts wrote down a name, and names travel faster than boats."

Wren looked down at the confession, at the witness line, at the testimony in their own remembered voice, and felt the full weight of what the river had lent them settle onto their shoulders like ballast.

The past had a weight. Wren had just picked it up, and there was no current strong enough to carry it away now.

"First light," Wren said. "We make for Grayharbor."

"Why Grayharbor?"

Because that was where the exiles lived, Wren almost said. Because that was where a woman named Ilse Vantor had spent eleven years believing her father was dead. But what they said, lighting the lamp higher and reaching for the chart book, was:

"Because we're out of tides."
