## 3. Six Tides

The news came upriver faster than any boat could carry it, which was the first sign that something had gone wrong with Wren's life.

News on the Rivergate traveled the way water found cracks—through lock-keepers who traded gossip for tobacco, through barge cooks who fed half the river's secrets along with its crews, through the loose network of favors Wren themselves had spent nine years weaving. Wren had always known this. Had counted on it, even. A life built on trading small kindnesses up and down two hundred miles of water meant a person was never more than a day from hearing anything worth hearing.

They just hadn't expected to hear this.

It came at Marrow Lock, in the mouth of Sefa Kettle, the lock-keeper's wife, who flagged the *Marguerite* down with a lantern before dawn, standing on the lock wall in her nightdress like a woman reporting a fire.

"Salvage fleet," she said, before Wren had the gangplank down. "Three boats out of Corvess, flying gray. Commander's name is Ferran."

The name went through Wren like cold water through a hull seam.

"They're buying pilots," Sefa went on, oblivious, wrapping her shawl tighter against the river damp. "Paying triple rates, cash, no questions. Old Pem Halloway took their money and now he won't look anyone in the eye. They asked him about the shallows below the Gate. About wrecks. About—" she hesitated, and lowered her voice as though the river itself might be listening, "—about who'd been diving there."

Wren kept their face still. Nine years of practice made that part easy. The rest—the pulse hammering at the throat, the sudden arithmetic running behind the eyes—that they managed to hide behind a yawn and a grunt.

"Ferran," Wren said. "Odile Ferran?"

"You know her?"

"I know the name." Everyone from the old days knew the name. Odile Ferran, the Republic's salvage mistress, the woman who could find anything that had ever sunk, because finding things that sank had been her way of finding people too. Manifests told you what a boat carried. Wreck locations told you who'd been paid to lose it. Ferran had understood that better than anyone in the Ministry of Recovery, and she had used it.

Wren had spent one very bad winter learning exactly how she used it.

"When does she reach the Gate?" Wren asked.

Sefa shrugged. "Two days, maybe three, if the wind holds contrary. They say she's got a diver with her this time. A deep man. One of those who breathes from a tank."

* * *

Wren did not go back to sleep. They stood at the rail of the *Marguerite* while the lock filled around them, lifting the boat toward the morning, and watched the water rise black and patient against the stone.

Six tides. That was what the river would give them. Wren had done the figuring twice already, once in their head and once scratched into the condensation on the cabin glass: the wreck in the shallows below the Rivergate sat in twelve feet of water at low tide and six at high, and every spring flood dumped another load of silt across the shoal. By the seventh tide, the channel would shift, the sandbar would roll, and the wreck would slide off the edge of the known world into thirty feet of moving current where no diver alive could work it. The river was burying its own secret. It had been trying to for years.

Wren had pulled it halfway out.

And now Ferran was coming to pull out the rest.

"Tammi." Wren's voice came out rougher than intended. The deckhand appeared at the cabin door, hair still wet from washing, a biscuit in one hand. "Change of plans. We're not running cargo today."

Tammi blinked. In the three years since Wren had fished them half-drowned out of the flood current at Cinders Bend, Tammi had never once questioned an order, but they had also never seen the captain look like this—like a woman doing sums she didn't want the answer to.

"We going after the chest again?"

"No." Wren turned away from the rail. "We're going to make sure nobody else does."

* * *

The problem, Wren thought as the *Marguerite* worked downriver through the morning mist, was that invisibility had never been a matter of hiding. Anyone could hide. Hiding was cheap—a false name, a flat accent, a face kept forgettable. What Wren had built instead was something far more delicate: a life so ordinary, so woven into the river's daily business, that no one thought to look beneath it. Captain Winterbourne, reliable as a lock gate. The kind of person whose absence would be noticed, but whose presence never was.

Ferran didn't hunt faces. She hunted methods.

That was the thought that sat in Wren's chest like ballast as the miles slid past. Somewhere in the Republic's archives—if the archives hadn't burned—there was a file on a diver who worked the Vessany coast during the last year of the war. A diver who recovered things the Ministry needed recovered, and who had testified, afterward, about things the Ministry needed testimony about. That file would have diagrams. Salvage patterns. Notes on how the diver read currents, how they marked wrecks, how they thought.

And Wren had already gone down to the Rivergate shallows twice. Already run lines in the pattern Ferran herself had helped codify—the spiral search, the tide-timed descents, the habit of working slack water when every other soul on the river stayed ashore. If Ferran got one good look at the dive site, one set of line-marks in the mud, one witness who'd seen a lone figure working the shoals at dawn—

She wouldn't need a face. She'd recognize the handwriting.

"Captain." Tammi's voice, careful, from the wheelhouse door. "There's a skiff been keeping pace with us since the bend. Half a mile back. Been there twenty minutes."

Wren didn't turn around. Turning around was what someone with something to hide would do. Instead they adjusted the throttle a quarter-inch, casual as breathing, and let the *Marguerite* hold her speed.

"Gray hull?" Wren asked.

"Aye. Two men. Not fishing."

"Not fishing," Wren agreed softly. Scouts. Ferran was already on the water ahead of her fleet, or had sent them ahead, sweeping the river for exactly the thing Wren was: a boat behaving strangely near the Gate. Every instinct said to change course, to put distance between the *Marguerite* and the shallows, to be somewhere else entirely when the questions started.

But the chest was still aboard. Hidden under the floorboards of the aft cabin, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sin. And the documents inside it were the only proof in the world that Minister Aldous Vantor had survived the fall of the Republic—and the map to a treasury that three nations would burn each other to claim.

If Ferran got it, she wouldn't sell it. She'd use it. Wren remembered that about her too: she'd never been greedy, exactly. Greed wanted money. Ferran wanted position—the permanent, untouchable kind that came from owning something everyone else feared.

"Tammi," Wren said. "When we round Cinders Point, I want you to drop the dredge lines overboard. All of them. Make it look like we're working the mussel beds."

"Mussel beds?" Tammi frowned. "Ain't been a good mussel take on this stretch in years."

"Exactly. Nobody suspicious works a dead bed. Suspicious people work *good* ones." Wren finally glanced back at the distant skiff, gray and patient on the water. "We're going to be boring, Tammi. Boring as silt. For six tides, boring is the safest thing on this river."

"And after six tides?"

Wren looked downriver, toward the Gate, where the spring current ran brown and strong over a secret the river was trying to swallow. Six tides until the wreck was gone forever. Six tides until the choice Wren had been avoiding since the night they hauled that chest aboard became unavoidable: burn it, deliver it, or drown it.

"After six tides," Wren said, "the river decides for us."

But that was a lie, and they both knew it. Rivers didn't decide. People did. And somewhere behind them, closing at the speed of bought pilots and burned licenses, Odile Ferran was already deciding for everyone.

The skiff held its distance all afternoon. At dusk, as the *Marguerite* tied up at a mooring below Cinders Point, Wren watched its gray shape finally turn and vanish toward the Gate—and understood, with a certainty that settled in the bones like river damp, that it hadn't been following them at all.

It had been counting them.

One boat. One captain. One deckhand. Filed away, the way Ferran filed everything, against the moment she'd need it.

Five tides left.
