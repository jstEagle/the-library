## 4. The Woman Who Counts Wrecks

Odile Ferran kept a ledger the way other people kept knives: close, oiled, and always within reach. It was a plain book, bound in gray canvas, and it held everything she had ever counted—wrecks, wages, debts, men. She was counting now, standing at the rail of the salvage barge *Ledger's Due* as it ground upriver against the spring current, watching the Rivergate country unspool past her in brown water and drowned willows.

Forty-one years old. Nineteen of them spent on the water. Eleven wrecks raised by her own hand or bought cheap from men who couldn't finish what they started. One republic, dead. One treasury, missing.

She wrote nothing down yet. Some numbers you keep in your head until they're true.

"Commander." Her second, a broad-shouldered ex-dredger named Hask, came up the companionway with rain beading on his oilskin. "Pilot says we make the ferry crossing by dark. There's a town—Marrow's Bend. Maybe forty roofs."

"Ferryman?"

"There's one. Old fellow. Runs a flatboat and a rope across the narrows."

Ferran closed the ledger. "Then we'll pay our toll."

* * *

Marrow's Bend smelled of woodsmoke and mud, the way all river towns did, as if the water itself were slowly rotting through them. Ferran walked its single street alone. She'd learned long ago that authority traveled better without an escort; one woman in a good coat asked fewer questions than six men in salvage rig.

The ferry landing sat at the street's end, where the river pinched between two clay bluffs. A rope as thick as her wrist sagged across the channel, and moored to it rode a flat barge with a hand-cranked winch and a roof of patched canvas. The ferryman was mending net in the bow, an old man with forearms like dock pilings and a face that had stopped expecting anything from the world around the time his hair went white.

"You're closed," he said, not looking up.

"I'm not crossing." Ferran stepped onto the planking. "I'm buying your evening."

That got his eyes up. They were pale, river-colored eyes, and they went over her coat, her boots, the ledger under her arm, and came back unimpressed. "I don't sell evenings."

"Everyone sells something. Most just haven't priced it yet." She sat down on the cargo thwart across from him, unhurried, and set the ledger on her knees. "My name is Odile Ferran. I command the salvage fleet coming up from Corren's Landing—three barges, a dive crew, a magistrate's writ for wreck recovery. You'll see us tomorrow at first light, working the shallows below the gate."

"The gate's been quiet since the flood went down," the old man said. "Nothing there but silt and a dead boat or two."

"That's what I intend to find out." She opened the ledger to a page near the back, and turned it so he could see. "This is a list of every licensed ferryman operating between Corren's Landing and the Vessany border. Your name is on it. Josper Hale. Licensed eleven years. Renewal due at midsummer."

Hale put down his net. "What's that to me?"

"Renewals require a clean record. Clean records require cooperation with lawful recovery operations." She said it mildly, the way she might have discussed the weather. "A man who obstructs a writ could lose his license. A man who loses his license loses this rope, this barge, this house on the bluff behind you that I can see from here. And at your age, Josper, where would you start again?"

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—not much of one, more air than sound. "You're the kind they used to send out of Vessany. Before it fell."

Something moved behind Ferran's ribs, small and cold, like a fish turning in shallow water. She didn't let it reach her face. "I'm the kind who does arithmetic," she said. "Vessany is gone. Arithmetic isn't."

"Arithmetic." He spat over the side, into the brown water. "We called it something else when the ministers' men came through. Same coat, though. Same voice."

"Then you know I keep my word, because their word was iron." She tore the page free.

She did it slowly, letting him watch—the sound of the paper parting from the binding, the way she held it up between two fingers so he could read his own name printed there, the license record, eleven years of tolls and crossings rendered into ink. Then she leaned toward the little iron stove at the stern, the one Hale used for boiling coffee on cold mornings, opened its door, and fed the page into the coals.

It caught fast. His name curled black and vanished.

Hale didn't move. But his hands, resting on the net, had gone still in a way that told her everything—that the paper had been his whole margin of safety, and she had just burned it in front of him, and he hadn't even understood yet whether it was punishment or gift.

"That was yours to lose," she said. "Now it's mine to give back. Tell me about the wreck in the shallows—who's touched it, who's pulled from it, who's been asking questions—and at midsummer you'll find a fresh license waiting, signed, with my compliments. Refuse, and I write to the harbor office tonight. No license. No rope. No pension, Josper. You've been paying into the ferry fund eleven years. Imagine losing all of it over one conversation."

The stove ticked. Downriver, somewhere, a loon called, that wild laughing cry they made, and neither of them looked toward it.

"What do you want to know?" Hale said finally.

"Everything since the flood dropped."

He told her. He was old, but he wasn't stupid, and he gave it up in pieces, testing each one, watching her face. A wreck showing in the shallows below the gate, hull ribs black with age—but not so black as they should have been, which meant the river had kept it sealed until the flood scoured it open. A chest hauled out by a riverboat captain. Nobody local. Kept to themselves, traded favors up and down the water, never stayed anywhere longer than a season.

"Name?" Ferran asked.

"Goes by Wren. Wren Winterbourne, they say, though nobody says it like they believe it." Hale shrugged. "Runs a boat called the *Marguerite*. Good pilot. Better than good—I've watched them read water that would drown a bishop. That kind of skill doesn't come off a farm."

No. It didn't. Ferran felt the fish turn again behind her ribs, colder this time. She knew exactly what kind of training produced pilots who could read water like scripture, and she knew which government had paid for it, and she knew, with the particular certainty of a woman who had spent nineteen years counting, that the Republic's last hydrographic corps had numbered exactly two hundred and six officers—and that she had personally signed the transfer papers for most of them.

"Winterbourne," she repeated. "Describe them."

"Hard to say. Youngish. Careful eyes. Hands—" Hale paused, and something almost like respect crossed his ruined old face. "Hands that never stop moving. Like they're always tying a knot you can't see."

Ferran stood. She took a folded paper from inside her coat—a warrant, blank in the name line, pre-signed by a magistrate whose debts she owned—and laid it on the cargo thwart between them. On top of it, she placed a new license form, also blank, also ready.

"One of these gets filled in by morning," she said. "You decide which name goes on it. Not tonight. Tonight you sleep on it, because I want you to understand that I'm patient." She smiled then, and it reached her eyes, which was the worst thing about it. "Patience is the whole trade, Josper. Wrecks don't come up fast. Neither do men."

She left him sitting in the dark with his dead fire and his two pieces of paper, and walked back up the street toward the lighted windows of the *Grackle*, where her crew drank on her coin and the landlord had already been paid to remember nothing.

* * *

In her cabin aboard the *Ledger's Due*, Ferran poured two fingers of plum brandy and did not drink it. She sat instead with the ledger open, and beneath the day's figures she wrote three lines in her small, exact hand:

*Wreck exposed. Chest recovered — party unknown, alias Winterbourne.*
*Six tides before the gate shifts. Confirmed by pilot's chart.*
*Hydrographic Corps, 206 officers. Where are the other 205?*

She had served Vessany too. That was the part no one in these river towns ever guessed, because they expected monsters and she arrived instead as a reasonable woman with a magistrate's writ and a fair price for everything. She had been twenty-two when the Republic fell—young, ambitious, already third officer on a salvage tender out of the naval yard at Corvenne. She had watched the ministries burn from the water, had helped pull survivors and ledgers alike from the sinking customs houses, and had understood, in the way that clever young people understand things all at once, that the fall of a nation is not an ending. It is a liquidation. And someone must count the assets.

She had counted for nineteen years. Other officers had run—scattered north, taken new names, gone quiet in fishing villages. Let them. Cowardice was its own punishment. What Ferran had done was harder: she had stayed, worked, built, obeyed every law of every successor state, and waited for the assets to surface. Because they always surfaced. Rivers especially. Rivers kept everything.

And now the largest asset of all was lying in six feet of silty water below the Rivergate, with a former hydrographic officer holding the key to it.

The treasury of the Republic of Vessany. Fourteen years of tax receipts, war bonds, ministerial reserves—money enough to restart a war, or, if a person were sensible, money enough to simply *stop*. To buy a fleet. To buy magistrates. To buy, say, a comfortable estate above the flood line and a ledger with nothing left to count in it. She was fifty-one months from a pension that would have covered a room and coal rations. The treasury was worth four hundred thousand such pensions.

People would call it theft, if they ever learned of it. Ferran had made peace with that vocabulary long ago. The Republic had stolen its money from its people; the people had stolen the Republic from itself; the magistrates had stolen the wreckage. Everyone downstream of a drowning nation takes a cut. She had merely decided to take hers at the source, with paperwork.

She finished the brandy.

There was a knock, and Hask put his head in. "Scout boat's back, Commander. Two of them shadowing a river steamer below Marrow's Bend. Small craft, patched sails, running lights doused." He hesitated. "Name on her stern, half-scraped. *Marguerite*."

Ferran looked at the ledger a moment longer. Then she closed it, and the sound of the cover settling was very quiet, like a door closing on a room where someone had once lived.

"Signal the scouts," she said. "Follow, don't board. I want to know where Captain Winterbourne sleeps, eats, and prays." She stood, and took her coat from its hook, and allowed herself—for no one was watching—one brief moment of something that was not quite joy and not quite grief, but had the weight of both.

"Nineteen years," she said to the empty cabin, to the river beyond the porthole, to the drowned republic that had made her. "You're nearly counted."

Above deck, the signal lamps began to blink down the dark water, and the *Marguerite*, thirty fathoms astern and closing on the wreck that would decide everything, ran on blind into the night.
