## 5. Debt of a Dead Nation

The town was called Lowmark, which was honest of it. Two streets, one of them flooded every spring, a grain elevator leaning like a drunk against the sky, and a church whose bell had been melted down for shot in some war nobody local had won anything from. Wren tied the Marguerite to the pilings below the elevator and told Tammi to mind the boat.

"You've said that twice," Tammi said. "You only say things twice when you're lying."

"Mind the boat."

They walked up through the mud into Lowmark with the documents sewn flat against their ribs, inside the lining of a coat that had never once been searched, because nobody searches a riverboat captain's coat. Captains are furniture. That had been the whole architecture of Wren's life: be furniture, be useful, be unremarkable, and the past would walk past you on the dock without a glance.

Ilse Vantor lived above a cobbler's shop on the dry street, in rooms that smelled of glue and last winter's smoke. Wren knew this because they'd asked three people in three towns over two years, casually, the way you ask about weather — *you know anyone good with ledgers? anyone from upriver?* — and let the answers drift back like flotsam. A woman who kept accounts for the grain elevator. A woman who mended sails in the off-season and argued politics at the Grackle until men stopped arguing back. A woman with her father's long face and none of his fortune.

Wren knocked.

The door opened on a chain, then all the way. Ilse Vantor was thirty-some, sharp-boned, ink-stained to the second knuckle, and she looked at Wren the way an auditor looks at a column that doesn't add.

"Captain Winterbourne," she said. "You're the one who runs favors up the shallows. People say you don't take coin."

"People say a lot of things."

"They also say you don't talk about where you came from." She unhooked the chain. "Come in before someone sees you standing there looking like a funeral."

* * *

The room held a table, two chairs, a cold stove, and more paper than furniture. Ledgers, mostly — other people's accounts, kept clean and honest, because apparently honesty could survive anywhere if you gave it enough columns. On the wall hung a single framed thing: not a portrait, Wren noted, but a map. The old Republic of Vessany, river country, the whole drowned nation drawn in faded ink.

She caught Wren looking at it.

"My father drew it," she said. "Before I was born. He used to say a country is just a promise with borders, and promises keep worse than maps do." She sat, folded her hands. "You didn't come here for bookkeeping."

"No."

"So say it."

Wren had rehearsed this on the forty miles downriver. Rehearsed it the way you rehearse a lie: until it stops feeling like one. But sitting across from her, under the map of the country her father had helped kill and mourn, the rehearsal went thin.

"There was a flood this spring," Wren said. "It dropped a wreck into the Rivergate shallows. Salvage came off it. Documents."

"What documents?"

"Ship's manifests. Cargo records. And a confession. Sealed. Signed by Minister Edran Vantor."

Wren watched her face do three things in one second — nothing, everything, nothing again. She'd learned control somewhere expensive.

"My father is dead," she said quietly. "He died when the Republic fell. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what everyone was told." Wren pulled the coat lining open and laid the sealed packet on the table between them. "This says otherwise. It says he got out. It says he lived long enough to write down where he put the treasury, and why, and who helped him. It's dated four years after the fall."

Ilse didn't touch it. She looked at it the way you look at a snake that might also be a gift.

"That's impossible."

"It's what the paper says."

"And how did a riverboat captain come to pull my father's confession out of a wreck?" Her eyes came up, and they were not soft. They were her father's eyes, Wren thought, and they had audited better liars than me. "The Rivergate is sixty miles of silt. Nothing sinks there by accident with a minister's seal on it."

"Floods move things."

"Floods move things." She repeated it flatly, tasting it. Then she broke the seal — she didn't hesitate, Wren noticed; whatever else she doubted, she did not doubt the paper — and read.

Wren watched her read. Watched her hand go to her mouth. Watched her press her palm flat against the table as though the room were listing and she needed ballast. When she finished, she read it again, slower, and then she set it down and looked at the map on her wall for a long time.

"He hid it," she said finally. "All of it. The treasury, the reserves, the pay-chests of three regiments. He hid it instead of using it, and he hid himself instead of coming to me." Her voice stayed level, which cost her; Wren could see the cost. "Do you know what we ate the first winter after the fall? Do you know what my mother sold?"

"I can guess."

"Don't guess." She turned. "Don't you dare guess. You have no idea what that man's name cost us. Every door in exile closed when they learned who we were. And he was alive. Four years alive, writing confessions, hiding gold, and he never—" She stopped herself. Breathed. Auditor's discipline. "I'm sorry. You brought this. You didn't make it true."

"No," Wren said. "But I need to tell you something about it, and you're not going to like it."

* * *

Here was the lie, built on the way downriver, plank by plank:

*There was a dying passenger on my boat two winters ago. An old clerk, sick, half-lucid. He made me swear to bring these papers to you if I ever found where you'd landed. He wouldn't say who he'd worked for. He paid me in silence, and I kept the papers because a debt's a debt, and now the river's given me the rest.*

It was a good lie. It had a body in it, a debt, a reason a careful man would hold dangerous paper for two years without reading it. It explained everything except the truth, which was that Wren had known Edran Vantor's signature since before Ilse could write, had watched him sign orders, had — and here the lie pressed its full weight down — testified against him once, under coercion, in a room with wet stone floors, testimony that had helped send him running.

Wren told the lie about the dying clerk.

Ilse listened with her hands folded, and Wren understood, watching her, that she was doing arithmetic. Not believing. Weighing.

"A clerk," she said.

"An old one."

"On your boat. Sixty miles from anywhere."

"The river picks up all kinds."

"Yes," she said. "It does. It picked up you, after all." She stood, walked to the window, stood with her back to Wren. Below, the cobbler hammered at a sole, patient as a clock. "Captain, I'm going to ask you one question, and I want you to understand that I already know most of the answers, because I've spent eleven years learning to find things out, and finding things out is the only inheritance my father left me that wasn't a lie."

Wren said nothing.

"When the Republic fell, there was a list. Witnesses. People whose testimony was used to hunt the ministers who ran. My father's name was on three warrants, and all three warrants cited the same witness — testimony taken under coercion, according to the record, from a pilot who had run the ministry's boats." She turned around. "That pilot disappeared. Changed their name, some said. Some said drowned. Some said bought."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, the cobbler's hammer kept its time.

"Ilse," Wren said.

"Don't." Her voice cracked, first honest sound she'd made. "Don't say your name yet. Because if you say it, then you lied to me just now, in my own house, about a dying man who never existed, while I showed you—" she gestured at the packet, at the map, at the whole ruined geography of her life "—that. So don't say it yet. Just tell me one thing. Are you the witness?"

And here was the moment Wren Winterbourne had been building toward for eleven years without knowing it: the door out of the nameless life, standing open, warm light behind it, and the person holding it open was the daughter of the man Wren had helped destroy.

"I came here to give you the documents," Wren said. "Whatever I am, whatever I was — the treasury is real, and it's yours, and Ferran's fleet will be at the Rivergate within two tides. Those are the facts. Start with those."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I can afford right now."

Ilse looked at Wren for a long moment, and something in her face shifted — not forgiveness, nothing so cheap, but recognition. The look of someone meeting another survivor of the same wreck.

"Two tides," she said. "Then we'll do it your way. Facts first." She gathered the documents, squared their edges with a bookkeeper's precision, and slid them into a canvas satchel. "But captain — the next lie you tell me, even a kind one, even a necessary one, ends this. Whatever *this* is. Understood?"

"Understood."

She walked Wren to the door, and just before opening it she said, quietly, almost to herself:

"He signed it 'to my daughter, who keeps honest books.' He knew I'd find it eventually. He just couldn't face me while he lived." Her jaw tightened. "So help me, whoever you are, you'll help me face him now. Dead or not."

Down on the dock, Tammi was waiting, and Tammi's face was wrong — tight, watchful, a stranger's face wearing a deckhand's expression.

"Captain," Tammi said, low. "We've got company. Two skiffs, gray hulls, no colors. They've been sitting off the point since before dawn." A pause. "And there's a poster nailed to the elevator door. It's got a drawing on it. Bad drawing. But the name underneath—" Tammi swallowed. "The name underneath is one I've heard you say in your sleep."
