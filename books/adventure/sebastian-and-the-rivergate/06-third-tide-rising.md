## 6. Third Tide, Rising

Tammi found the insignia on the second day of the flood tide, when the Marguerite's bilge gave up what the river had been holding in trust.

It wasn't supposed to be Tammi's job to pump the bilge. That was true of a lot of things on the boat—true of navigation, true of the accounts, true of the sealed chest that now lived wrapped in oilcloth under the captain's berth, which Tammi was absolutely not supposed to know about and absolutely did. But Wren had been up all night at the wheel with a chart spread across their knees, and the pump didn't care about anyone's secrets. Water came in whether you were ready for it or not. That was the first lesson of the river, and Tammi had learned it young, hauling nets before they were tall enough to see over the gunwale.

So Tammi pumped. And when the water ran low, they took the hand lantern into the bilge to check the strum box, because a clogged strum box meant a burned-out pump, and a burned-out pump meant swimming.

The bilge was a country of its own. Old grease, old rope, the smell of diesel and tannin and the particular sourness of wood that had been wet for twenty years and would be wet for twenty more. Tammi crawled aft along the keelson with the lantern held high, and that was where they saw it: a canvas tool roll, wedged into the gap between frame and planking, tucked so deliberately that it could not have fallen there. Things that fall land any which way. Things that are hidden are placed.

Tammi should have left it. Tammi knew that even then, in the moment, with the lantern trembling slightly in their grip. There was a whole grammar of boat life about not looking in other people's places, and Tammi spoke it fluently. But the roll was heavy in a way tool rolls weren't, and Tammi's fingers were already working the tie, and the tie was stiff with old wax, and it gave with a small sound like a knuckle cracking.

The insignia was a sunburst struck in pewter, eight rays, the center stamped with the crossed keel-and-crown of the Republic of Vessany. Tammi had seen one before, exactly once—nailed to the doorpost of the customs house in Lowmarsh, the year the Republic came apart, when men from the fleet had gone door to door collecting them. Melt them down, they'd said. Bury the sun. Tammi had been eleven. They remembered the smell of the pewter furnace and the way the whole town had stood in the street to watch, silent, the way you're silent at a funeral or a hanging.

The roll held three more things. A set of papers, water-stained but legible, bearing a name that was not Wren. A signal card, the old Republic river codes, the kind used to talk between boats without radio. And a photograph, soft with handling, of a young person in a Republic naval coat standing on the deck of a ship that Tammi recognized from a hundred stories told in river taverns: the *Coriander*, the minister's escape barge, the one that went down in the Rivergate with everyone's history aboard.

Tammi sat back on their heels in the dark of the bilge and did the arithmetic they didn't want to do.

Wren. The name on the papers was not Wren, and the face in the photograph was younger and cleaner and wearing a uniform, but it was the same face. Narrow jaw. The scar through the left eyebrow, the one Wren had always said came from a winch cable. The eyes, which Tammi had spent three years learning to read across a wheelhouse—the eyes were the same, and they were looking out of the photograph with an expression Tammi had never once seen on the Marguerite: certainty. The certainty of someone who believes the world is ordered and that they stand high in it.

Tammi put everything back in the roll. Put the roll back in the gap. Crawled out into the daylight and stood on deck breathing like a man who'd surfaced too fast.

Above them, the third tide was coming in.

* * *

They didn't confront Wren. Not right away. Tammi worked the rest of the day with the question sitting in their chest like a swallowed hook, and every time Wren spoke—*ease off the throttle, Tam, we'll hold the eddy till the light changes*—the hook turned.

Because here was the thing. Here was the whole rotten, beautiful thing of it. The posters had gone up along the levee two days ago, nailed to every piling between here and the Grackle: **INFORMATION WANTED — PERSONS CONNECTED TO THE VESSANY SINKING — GENEROUS REWARD — BY ORDER OF THE SALVAGE FLEET, CMDR. O. FERRAN.** And beneath the official line, in smaller print, a description that had made Tammi's blood go cold when they'd first read it, because it described not a stranger but a method: *a pilot of unusual skill, known to read water by sound in fog, known to favor the Marguerite or boats of her class.*

Tammi had read that and thought: someone's hunting our captain, and felt fierce about it. Protective. Tammi's whole life was a short list of things worth being fierce about, and the Marguerite and the person who ran her were the top two lines.

Now Tammi stood at the rail watching Wren at the wheel, hands easy on the spokes, reading the water the way the poster said, the way only a handful of pilots alive could read it, and the fierce feeling had nowhere to go. Because either Wren was the person in the photograph—and then what was Wren, exactly, what had Wren done, what was in the chest under the berth that Tammi wasn't supposed to know about—or Wren was someone who had stolen a dead person's face, and that was worse, that was a different kind of terrible, and Tammi couldn't decide which answer they were praying for.

The scouts found them at midafternoon.

Tammi saw them first, two skiffs hanging back at the edge of visibility, in the broken water off Sallow Point where the channel braided. They weren't fishing. Fishermen worked the seams; these two sat in the slack water doing nothing, and boats that do nothing are always doing something. Tammi counted the way they moved when the Marguerite changed speed—one skiff adjusting, the other holding—and knew it for what it was: a bracket. The fleet wasn't following them. The fleet was measuring them.

"Company," Tammi said, and kept their voice flat.

Wren looked once, a glance that lasted half a second, and Tammi watched them do the math the way Tammi had watched them do it a hundred times in weather—reading the two skiffs, the wind, the channel, the light. "They've been there since the second tide," Wren said. "Hold your course. We don't look at them twice."

"Captain." Tammi heard their own voice come out strange. "Those are Ferran's boats."

"I know whose boats they are."

"Do you." It came out wrong. It came out with an edge on it that Tammi hadn't planned, and Wren's head turned, slow, and for the first time in three years Tammi watched the captain actually *look* at them, really look, the way you look at water when it's behaving strangely.

"What's that mean?"

Tammi had a speech. They'd been building it all day, in the bilge and out of it, all the questions lined up like barges waiting to lock through. And now, with Wren's eyes on them and the scouts bracketing them and the third tide lifting the boat under their feet, every word of the speech fell out of their head except the truest one.

"I pumped the bilge," Tammi said.

Something moved behind Wren's face. Not much. A shutter half-closing. But Tammi had spent three years learning to read that face across a wheelhouse, and they saw it, and it was answer enough.

"How much did you see?"

"The roll." Tammi's mouth was dry. "The papers. The—the sunburst."

The Marguerite ran on. The wheel creaked in small corrections under Wren's hands. Somewhere behind them a skiff's engine changed pitch, and neither of them turned to look.

"Say what you're going to say," Wren said quietly.

"Are you them? The pilot on the poster? Are you the one from the *Coriander*?"

Wren was silent long enough that Tammi counted the wheel's creak four times. Then: "I was on the *Coriander*."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Wren said. "It isn't."

"Captain, they're saying—" Tammi's voice cracked and they hated it, hated the crack, hated being young in this moment. "The posters say a Republic officer sold the manifest, sold the route, that the minister's own people opened the wreck for the fleet that sank her. They're saying someone aboard drew the map that got everyone drowned. Is that you? Because I've been running with you three years, I've staked everything on this boat, and I need to know if I've been crewing for the traitor of Vessany or—"

"Or what?"

"Or for someone who's running from it," Tammi said. "I don't know which one I'm asking for. I don't know which one's better."

That landed. Tammi saw it land, saw Wren's jaw work, saw the captain look at the water as if the water might answer for them. And when Wren finally spoke, it was low, pitched under the engine noise, for Tammi alone.

"Both," Wren said. "The answer's both. I drew what I drew because men were holding my head under a pump bilge and asking questions, and I drew it, and the *Coriander* went down with forty-one people, and I lived because I was small and quick and already halfway over the side. That's the truth, Tam. That's the whole ugly shape of it. I didn't sell them for gold. I sold them for air. And I've spent every year since trying to decide if that makes it better or worse, and I'll tell you what I've decided: it makes it *both*. It's both forever."

The river ran. The scouts ran with them, patient as tide.

"The chest," Tammi said. "Under your berth. The one you pulled from the wreck."

"What about it?"

"Is it yours? Are we delivering it, or are we hiding it?"

Wren looked at them for a long moment, and Tammi watched something happen in that face—the shutter opening again, or maybe being taken off its hinges entirely.

"Ask me at the next tide," Wren said. "I'm still deciding."

* * *

They made the night anchorage at the mouth of the Cut, stern to the current, riding light on the flood. Wren took the first watch, which was wrong—Wren always took the last watch, always, it was a law of the boat—and Tammi lay in their bunk under the foredeck and listened to the wheelhouse floor creak and did not sleep.

The skiffs had anchored too, far enough out to be polite, close enough to be a message. Once, in the dark, Tammi heard a signal lamp shuttering out at the water, three long flashes, and then, a minute later, an answer from somewhere downstream. Talking about them. Measuring them. By morning the fleet would know everything the skiffs had seen, and what they'd seen was the Marguerite holding course like a boat with nothing to hide, which either meant nothing to hide or a captain good enough to act it.

Tammi climbed out of the bunk and went forward and sat on the anchor windlass with their knees up, looking at the dark water, and made the decision they'd been circling all day.

Here was what Tammi knew about Wren, the part that no poster could print: Wren had pulled Tammi's brother out of the flood of '19 when every other boat turned away, because turning away was the smart thing and the safe thing and everyone knew it. Wren paid the widow at Sallow Point's mooring fee every season and called it a channel tax so she'd take it. Wren had once run a fevered child forty miles through fog by sounding the banks with a boat hook, alone at the wheel for a full night, and never told anyone, and Tammi only knew because the child's mother told everyone.

And here was what Tammi knew about the person in the photograph: nothing. Nothing except that the photograph was old, and that people were not photographs, and that the river was full of boats that had changed their cargo and their names and gone on carrying.

The bilge hatch was still open from the day. Tammi looked at it for a long time.

Then they went and got the pump handle, and they did not go back into the bilge. Whatever was in that roll, Wren had carried it hidden for years, and Tammi was done digging for it. If Wren was running, Tammi would run with them until running stopped making sense. If Wren was paying a debt, Tammi wanted to be there when the payment came due—not because it was safe, but because three years on this boat had taught Tammi exactly one thing about the captain, and it was this: Wren always, always took the harder berth.

The trouble, Tammi thought, watching the skiffs' anchor lights burn out on the black water like low stars, was that harder for Wren had a way of meaning worse for everyone else.

Toward dawn, Wren came off watch and found Tammi still on the windlass. The captain stood in the companionway a moment, silhouetted, and said nothing, and the nothing was an entire conversation: *I know what you found. I know what you decided. I don't know if I deserve it.*

"Next tide's the third," Tammi said. "You said you'd answer by then."

Wren looked downstream, toward the Rivergate, toward the wreck that was sinking a little more with every tide, and when the captain spoke, the voice was one Tammi had never heard before—stripped, unarmored, young.

"I know where the treasury is, Tam. And I know who has to have it. And I know what Ferran will do to this whole river to get there first." A breath. "So no. I haven't decided. But I'm about to."

Out in the dark, the skiffs raised anchor. Both of them. And turned downstream.
