## 7. The Wreck Speaks

Slack tide came at dawn, and with it the only hour the river would let me down into her throat.

I'd waited three days for this. Three days of watching the wreck from the Marguerite's rail while Tammi ran cargo and asked no questions out loud, though her eyes asked plenty. Three days of Ilse Vantor's face when I handed her a lie wrapped in truth: *your father's papers came up in a net, that's all I know.* She'd taken them like a woman taking bread she suspected was stolen. She hadn't pressed. That was worse.

Now the water lay flat as poured pewter, and I went over the side.

The cold hit first — Rivergate cold, snowmelt cold, the kind that squeezes your chest shut and makes every breath a negotiation. I'd weighted my belt with scrap iron and carried a lantern in a brass housing, because the river at six fathoms is not dark so much as it is *absent*, a place where light goes to be forgotten. My rope paid out behind me, tied to the Marguerite's stern cleat. Tammi held the other end. Her hand on that rope was the only reason my heart kept anything like a steady rhythm.

The hull rose out of the murk like a ribcage.

She'd been a packet boat once — I could see it even now, even furred with silt and beard-moss, even broken along her starboard side where she'd struck the bar twenty years ago. *Vessany Star.* The name was still legible on the transom, gold leaf gone green but stubborn. The Republic had built forty of her sisters, fast and shallow, built to run mail and ministers and money up rivers that didn't want them.

I'd served on one just like her. Different name. Same yard. Same paint scheme, same bell with the seven-pointed star cast into the crown.

My hands knew this ship before my eyes did.

I swam the length of the keel, dragging my fingers through the silt, feeling for the officers' compartment aft. The manifests and the confession in the lead chest had come from there — the chest itself had washed free and lodged in the shallows, which was how the river had given it to me. But chests hold paper. Ships hold everything else.

The compartment door hung open, warped by water and years. Inside, the dark was total. I raised the lantern and the light made a small amber room in the blackness, and things moved in it — a boot sole, pale as fish belly. A coat button. The curve of a skull half-buried where the bunk had collapsed.

I had known he was aboard. The confession said so; the manifests said so; the way the Republic's records ended for Minister Corvan Vantor said so. Knowing a man drowned and looking at his teeth are different countries.

I looked anyway. It felt owed.

His cabin had been small and neat, or had been. A writing desk bolted to the bulkhead, its drawer long since rotted open. A strongbox, empty, lid sprung. And wedged beneath the desk leg — someone had thought to hide it, in the last minutes, in the dark, in the water coming in — a second document case, oilskin-wrapped, tarred at the seams.

My lungs were burning. I almost left it. I want that recorded somewhere, if anywhere is left to record things: I almost kicked off toward the light and the air and the life I'd built on top of this grave like a floor built on a cellar.

But the river doesn't give gifts. It gives debts with pretty wrapping.

I took the case and climbed the rope.

* * *

Tammi had a fire going in the galley stove and didn't say a word as I peeled off the diving suit with my hands shaking too hard to work the buckles, so that she had to come help me, her fingers quick and businesslike, her face turned carefully away. She poured coffee thick enough to stand a spoon in and set it in front of me and sat across the table and waited. She was very good at waiting. It was one of the hundred reasons I'd hired her without references and never regretted it.

The oilskin gave up its secret grudgingly. Inside: a journal, water-swollen but readable, written in a minister's careful clerk-hand. And folded inside the journal, a single sheet in a different hand — hurried, blotted, signed.

Corvan Vantor's last entry began three days before the sinking. The fleet was scattered, the capital fallen, the treasury loaded under false manifests on the *Star* herself. He wrote about the arrangements he'd made. He wrote about the men who'd helped him load it.

Then he wrote about what he'd traded.

I read it twice because the first time my eyes refused the words. He had been stopped at the Rivergate by a Republican patrol — the last loyal patrol, holding the channel against the collapse. They'd boarded him. They'd found the false manifests, because false manifests are only as good as the man who carries them, and Vantor had never in his life carried anything heavier than a pen.

And he had bought his passage through by giving them a name.

Not the treasury's location. Not his co-conspirators. A name. One witness, one testimony, one sworn statement that had already been extracted — the patrol wanted legitimacy, a trial, a record, something to prove the Republic still had law even as it died. Vantor had given them the statement they needed. Given them the man whose signature made it true.

He'd given them me.

*Wren Ashgrave,* the page said, in ink gone the color of old tea. *Boatswain, late of the Vessany customs service. Will confirm under oath the disposition of state funds. Has agreed to testify.*

I had testified. God help me, I had testified. I was nineteen and they had my sister's arrest warrant on the table between us and a magistrate explaining, gently, reasonably, the way powerful men explain things, that my cooperation would see her released to our aunt in the hill country and my silence would see her transported north, where the winters were. I had said the words they put in front of me. I had signed. I had told myself ever after that I'd had no choice, that a nineteen-year-old with a warrant hanging over his family has no choices, only exits.

Vantor's journal said otherwise. Vantor's journal said that a fifty-year-old minister with a nation's gold in his hold had faced the same arithmetic and solved it the same way: with someone else's blood. He'd named me knowing what the testimony would cost me — the Republic fell within the month, and the victors kept lists, and *Ashgrave* was on them, and I had spent eleven years being no one because of a sentence this drowning man had written to save his own skin.

We were the same, then. That was the thing the river had brought up for me to look at. Not proof of his innocence. Proof of our kinship. Two men in the current, each buying breath with the other's drowning.

"Captain." Tammi's voice, low. "Your coffee's going cold and you've read that page four times."

"I sent him here," I said. It came out flat, factual, obscene. "My testimony is why he was running at night, alone, with no escort. The patrol used my statement to justify seizing his papers. He fled the hearing. Took the *Star* upriver in fog to try to reach the exiles' flotilla." I touched the transom's ghost-name in my mind. "He struck the bar in fog because he was hurrying away from me."

Tammi was quiet a long moment. Then, in the tone she used for weather: "You were nineteen."

"He was fifty and he sold a crew of thirty-one people to save himself, and I'm not mourning him. That's not—" I pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes. "It's that we match, Tammi. His cowardice and mine. Same coin, two faces. And his daughter is sitting in a rented room in Harrow Bend believing her father died some kind of martyr, because I let her believe it. Because telling her the whole truth means telling her what I did."

"What *they* did," Tammi said. "Under threat."

"The words were still mine." I looked at the journal, at the neat, doomed handwriting. "Ilse deserves all of it. The treasury map and the cowardice and my name in the middle. She deserves a story with no floors built over cellars." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You know what the worst part is? Eleven years I thought the past was a country I'd emigrated from. Turns out I've been paying rent on it the whole time. Every quiet morning on this boat. Every favor traded, every question dodged. Interest on a debt I never declared."

Tammi reached across and took the journal before I could stop her. She read fast — she always read fast — and her face did something complicated around the page with my name on it, and then she set it down and slid it back.

"So don't pay it hiding," she said. "Pay it standing up."

Before I could answer, the deck above us creaked with footsteps that weren't hers and weren't mine, and a shadow crossed the galley skylight, and Tammi's hand went flat on the table the way it did when she smelled trouble.

A knock at the gangway hatch. Polite. Patient. The worst kind.

Through the glass, in the gray morning light, I saw the braid and the gray coat, and beside the hatch, pinned where any boarding officer would post it, a notice I recognized from eleven years ago, refreshed with new ink:

*By order of Commander O. Ferran, Salvage Fleet. All persons with knowledge of the Rivergate wreck will present themselves for interview.*

She wasn't searching the river anymore.

She'd come aboard.
