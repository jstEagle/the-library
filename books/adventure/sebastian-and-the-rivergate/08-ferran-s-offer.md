## 8. Ferran's Offer

She didn't knock twice. She never would. A woman who knocked twice was a woman who doubted her welcome, and Odile Ferran had never doubted anything in her life except, perhaps, the arithmetic of other people's courage.

I let her in because the alternative was worse. The Marguerite had one hatch and I had one face, and both of them were known quantities now.

"Captain Winterbourne." She stepped aboard like she owned the deck, boots finding the rhythm of the river without looking down. "Or should we do this properly?"

"Properly costs extra," I said. "Coffee's free."

"I don't drink on duty."

"It's morning."

"For me it's always duty." She took the bench across from me anyway, uninvited, and set her hands flat on the table the way an accountant sets down a ledger. Up close she was smaller than the posters suggested, and older—gray coming in at the temples like frost claiming a field, lines around her eyes that hadn't come from smiling. Her uniform was immaculate. That was the thing about Ferran: everything about her was immaculate, including her cruelty.

"You've been busy," she said. "Six tides ago there was a wreck in the shallows. Now there's a hole in the water and a captain who won't answer fleet summons."

"The notice said *interview*. I've been busy interviewing myself. Found nothing."

She smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated to the millimeter, the kind that made you feel included in something clever. "You dive at slack tide. You read current the way priests read scripture. You knew the Rivergate channel would shift before my own surveyors did. Do you know what I thought when I read those reports?"

"That you needed better surveyors."

"I thought: I know this work. I trained beside people who worked like this." She leaned forward slightly. "The Republic's hydrographic corps had maybe forty officers who could have pulled that chest out of moving water. Half of them are dead. Most of the rest are pensioners in Vessany's successor states, fat and grateful. And one of them—" she tapped the table once, softly, "—signed off as Wren Winterbourne on a riverboat license three years ago with handwriting that matches a certain lieutenant's field reports to within a margin my clerks call *uncanny*."

The coffee between us had gone cold. I hadn't poured her any. I'd poured it for myself and then forgotten it existed, which is what fear does—it makes your hands busy with nothing.

"If you have charges," I said, "read them."

"Oh, I have charges. I have eleven years of them. Desertion. False testimony under coercion—which is a polite way of saying what you did in the Kellin Street trials. Aiding the flight of a condemned minister." She said it all pleasantly, like reading a menu. "I could put you in irons before your coffee cools. But I'm not here for that."

"No?"

"I'm here because I'm tired, Lieutenant." The rank dropped out of her mouth like a coin she'd been keeping under her tongue. "I served the Republic too. Twenty-two years. I counted wrecks while ministers stole nations. When it fell, everyone above me got exile or a villa and everyone below me got questions. I got neither. I got a salvage contract and a promise that if I kept the rivers clean of other people's secrets, no one would look too hard at mine." She spread her hands. "And now the biggest secret on the water sits in your hold, and every magistrate from here to the coast wants it burned or buried, and I am the only person standing between them and you."

"You're offering to protect me."

"I'm offering to *finish* you. There's a difference, and it matters." She reached into her coat—not fast, letting me see every inch of the motion—and laid a folded document on the table. Wax seal. Magistrate's ribbon. The gray-blue of the coastal courts. "Full pardon. Expunged record. A new name if you want it, or the old one restored, signed by the same bench that condemned Minister Vantor. They'll do it because I ask, and I ask because they owe me, and they owe me because I know where the bodies are, and some of them helped dig the holes."

I looked at the paper and did not touch it.

"And in exchange?"

"The documents. All of them. The confession, the manifests, the map. And the wreck stays lost. No daughter, no publication, no resurrection of a dead country's ghosts." She folded her hands again. "Wren. Listen to me. I've read that confession—I keep copies of everything. You know what's in it? A coward describing his own cowardice in beautiful handwriting. Ilse Vantor doesn't want her father back; she wants a story she can live inside. You'd be handing her a knife and calling it an inheritance."

"She deserves the truth."

"She deserves a *life*. So do you." For the first time something moved behind her eyes that wasn't calculation—or maybe it was, just a deeper layer of it. "Eleven years, Wren. Eleven years of sleeping with your boots by the door. Of flinching at braid. Of teaching a deckhand to call you Captain instead of Lieutenant because the name fits better. I know what that costs. I pay it too, every morning, in front of a mirror. This paper ends it. One signature. You walk away clean, rich even—the magistrates will add a stipend, they're generous with their guilt—and the river forgets you ever carried anything heavier than cargo."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere upstream a barge horn sounded, long and low, the way they do at dawn.

And I will not lie to you, whoever you are, reading this: I almost took it.

My hand moved. I watched it move, the way you watch someone else's hand, and it crossed half the distance to that document before I caught it on the table's edge and held it there, fingers white against the wood. Because she was right. That was the obscenity of it. Every word was true. I had slept eleven years with my boots by the door. I had taught Tammi a name that fit better than the real one. A signature, and the running stopped. A signature, and I could wake up tomorrow without rehearsing answers to questions no one had asked yet.

Ferran watched my hand the way a fisherman watches a line go taut. She didn't push. She was too good for pushing. She simply sat in the quiet she'd built and let the offer do its work, the way water does, finding every crack.

"What happens to her?" I asked finally. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Ilse. If I sign."

"Fades into the crowd. Gets a small life. Marries a cooper or a clerk. Grows old wondering." Ferran shrugged, elegant. "People do it every day. It's called peace."

"It's called drowning with your head above water."

"Now you sound like him." Something flickered across her face—disgust, or grief, or the place where those two share a border. "Your minister. He said things like that. Beautiful things, right up until he sold his own crew to save his skin. Did you read that part? The part where he names names? Because your name is in there, Wren. Page nine. *The lieutenant who testified against us was promised mercy and given it.* You weren't his victim. You were his receipt."

The words went in like a hook going in—clean entry, no way back out the way they came. I knew what page nine said. I'd read it by lamplight with my hands shaking so badly I'd had to pin the page to the table. But hearing it in her voice, in that reasonable, immaculate voice, was different. Hearing it made it hers, and I hated that, and I hated more that part of me wanted to agree—to take the pardon and the stipend and let the whole rotten ledger close over all of us, the coward and the traitor and the woman who counts wrecks, together, neatly, forever.

That's what corruption is, I understand now. Not a knife in the dark. A warm room on a cold night, and a chair, and someone saying *sit down, rest, you've earned it*, and the door locking so gently you never hear the latch.

I took my hand off the table's edge.

"No," I said.

Ferran exhaled through her nose. Almost a laugh. "No?"

"No." I slid the document back across to her, unbroken seal, untouched wax. "Keep your paper. I've been clean on paper for eleven years and it's the dirtiest I've ever felt. Whatever I am, I'm not going to be your receipt either."

For a moment she just looked at me, and I saw her do the arithmetic—costs, risks, tides remaining—and I understood that she had never expected yes. The offer wasn't bait. It was a measurement. She'd wanted to know exactly how much I was worth, and now she knew, and knowing, she could plan around it.

"Pity," she said, and stood, and buttoned her coat with two precise motions. "The magistrates will be disappointed. So will you, eventually. Everyone who refuses a clean name ends up wanting it back, usually around the time the irons go on." At the hatch she paused, one hand on the frame, and didn't turn all the way around. "One tide left, Lieutenant. My dredges anchor at slack water after next. If the documents cross that channel, I'll know. If they burn, I'll know that too. There's no version of the next two days where you stay invisible. There's only versions where you choose what you're visible *as*."

Then she was gone, down the gangway, boots keeping their perfect time, and the gray coat swallowed by the gray morning until the river took her the way it takes everything—without comment.

I sat a long while with the cold coffee. Then I went below, woke Tammi, and told her to get Ilse Vantor aboard before dark.

"We're past asking permission?" Tammi said, hair sleep-mussed, eyes already sharp.

"We're past invisible," I said. "There's one tide left. Get the boat ready to give."

Tammi looked at me for a long second, and whatever she saw in my face, she didn't argue. She just nodded and started pulling lines.

By noon the Marguerite was loaded with everything I couldn't bear to lose, which turned out to be very little: charts, tools, Tammi's fiddle, a sealed oilskin packet addressed in a hand I hadn't used in eleven years.

By dusk, Ilse Vantor stood on my deck, watching the water rise toward the last high tide, and asked me the question Ferran's paper had almost bought:

"What does it cost?"

"Everything I was hiding behind," I said. "Which was the point of hiding."

She didn't understand yet. Neither did Tammi. But the river did—it was already rising, patient and certain, coming up the channel toward the boat I was about to kill, and it sounded, for the first time in eleven years, like forgiveness.
