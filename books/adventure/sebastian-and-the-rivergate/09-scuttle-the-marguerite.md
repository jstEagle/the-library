## 9. Scuttle the Marguerite

The last tide began to turn at dusk, and Wren felt it in the pilings before they saw it anywhere—in the way the water under the dock went slack, then shivered, then leaned toward the sea like a dog hearing its name.

One tide left. Then the Rivergate would swallow the wreck whole, and everything in it, and every choice with it.

Wren stood on the deck of the *Marguerite* and looked at their boat the way you look at a hand you're about to cut off.

Seventeen years. Seventeen years of gray paint and no name on the stern, of trading favors up and down forty miles of river, of answering questions with shrugs and letting people believe whatever was easiest for them to believe. The *Marguerite* had been more than a boat. She'd been a costume that fit so well Wren had forgotten they were wearing it.

"You're really going to do it." Tammi stood at the rail, arms crossed, not asking.

"I'm going to do it."

"Then I'm helping."

"No, you're not." Wren turned. "You're taking Ilse downstream past the bend, out of sight. When it's done, you come back for me—or you don't. That part's yours to decide."

Tammi's jaw worked. They'd been arguing since noon, since Wren had laid out the plan on the galley table with a charcoal sketch of the channel: the wreck sitting in the shallows like a coin at the bottom of a pocket, Ferran's fleet anchored off the point with three barges and a crane barge and enough men to strip the Rivergate bare by morning. Six tides had become five, four, three—and now one, because Wren had spent two of them being honest, which was the most expensive thing they'd ever done.

The plan was simple, the way drowning is simple. The *Marguerite* drew four feet light, six loaded. The channel at the Gate ran nine feet deep at high tide, narrow as a throat between the sandbars. Sink her square across it, ballast her down, open every seam she had, and nothing with a draft deeper than a canoe got through until someone spent a week cutting her apart. A week was longer than the wreck had. By the time Ferran cleared the channel, the Rivergate would have taken back what it lent.

And the location—Wren would take the chart. The only chart. It would go into the firebox with the coals, and Wren would watch it burn.

"You could just give her the documents," Tammi said quietly. "Ferran. Take the pardon. Walk away clean."

"I did almost take it." Wren heard their own voice come out flat and strange. "That's the thing about her offer, Tammi. It wasn't a bribe. It was a refund. Clean name, signed papers, all my sins refunded at par. And all I had to do was let her dig up a war and sell it to whoever paid best." They shook their head. "I spent seventeen years learning how to survive. Turns out surviving is a skill, not a life."

Tammi looked at them a long moment. Then: "Ilse is coming anyway. I told her the plan."

Wren closed their eyes. "Of course you did."

"She said—" Tammi hesitated, and for once the kid seemed to be choosing words instead of throwing them. "She said nobody sinks a boat for somebody else's father unless they owe him. And she wants to know what you owe him. Before. Not after."

* * *

Ilse Vantor came aboard at slack water, climbing the ladder with the economy of someone who'd stopped expecting help from the world. She was thinner than when Wren had first found her in that rented room above the chandlery, poorer, but there was something set in her face now that hadn't been there before—the look of a person who has decided what things cost and intends to pay.

She didn't ask about the debt. She stood on the deck beside Wren and watched the water lean seaward, and after a while she said, "My father used to say the Republic didn't fall. It drained. Like a marsh at ebb. Everyone who could walk walked away carrying something."

"He'd know," Wren said. "He carried the treasury."

"And you carried him." She turned. "That's what the confession says, isn't it? The part you haven't read to me. Your testimony put him on that ship. You gave evidence under coercion—they made you, or you thought they did—and he ran, and he sank, and he lived, and you've been hiding from both of you ever since."

Wren said nothing. The river said plenty.

"I'm not forgiving you," Ilse said. "I'm telling you we're even. He sold his crew to buy his own boat out. You sold your silence to buy your life. Neither of those debts cancels. But they can both be paid forward." She nodded at the darkening channel, at the lights of Ferran's fleet strung along the point like a carnival that had come to the wrong town. "So pay it."

"I'm paying it tonight."

"With your boat."

"With my boat."

"With yourself, if Ferran catches you aboard when she goes down. She will charge you with something. She'll need someone to punish, and you'll be standing right there holding the axe."

"I know."

"Do you?" Ilse stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something fierce and low. "Because I watched you for two weeks before you came to me, Captain. You order your own coffee like a man ordering for someone else. You walk through towns like the ground might remember you. I know what hiding costs better than anyone alive—I inherited it. So understand what you're buying tonight. Not absolution. Not even safety. Just a name. Yours. Spoken out loud, in front of everyone, attached to something you chose instead of something that happened to you." She held out her hand. "It's a fair price. I'd pay it."

Wren took her hand. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested.

"Get below," Wren said. "Both of you. When I open the seacocks, this deck is going to go fast."

* * *

They cast off at full dark, running without lights, Wren at the wheel and the engine pushed to a speed the old girl hadn't seen in years, the hull drumming against the chop. Behind them, Tammi and Ilse crouched in the well of the tender, ready to drop away at the mark. Ahead, the Rivergate showed as a black seam between blacker banks, and beyond it, the fleet's anchor lights burned in a patient row.

Ferran would have watchers on the point. Wren counted on it. Let her see the *Marguerite* coming. Let her think it was a delivery, a surrender, a captain finally cracking. Every minute she spent deciding whether to intercept was a minute the tide kept falling.

At the mouth of the channel Wren cut the engine and let the current carry them in, quiet as driftwood, and the sandbars rose on either side like the shoulders of something sleeping. Here it was. Nine feet of throat. The wreck lay thirty yards upstream, invisible now beneath two feet of ebbing water, and tomorrow it would lie beneath none.

Wren opened the throttle wide for the last hundred yards—not toward the wreck, but athwart it, laying the *Marguerite* across the channel like a bar across a door—and at the moment the bow swung true, they dropped the tender over the side with Tammi and Ilse in it, and turned back to the wheel.

Below decks, Wren opened the seacocks. All six. Then the fuel lines, cracked and left to bleed into the bilge. Then they stacked the chart of the wreck's position in the firebox, poured lamp oil over it, and struck the match, and watched the last map to the treasury curl and bloom and go to ash among the coals.

By the time Wren climbed back onto the deck, the river was already inside the boat, cold and businesslike around their boots.

The *Marguerite* settled slowly, the way an old animal lies down, and Wren stood at the rail with one hand on the wet wood and let her. There was no ceremony in it. There was no ceremony possible. Just the gray paint going under inch by inch, the wheelhouse windows flooding, the mast light—that unlit, anonymous mast light—tilting against the stars.

"You should have taken the pardon."

Odile Ferran's voice came across the water from a launch fifty yards out, searchlight pinning Wren to the rail like a moth. She stood in the bow with a pistol held low and easy, and behind her the fleet was waking—lanterns, shouts, the crane barge's engine coughing alive.

"The channel's blocked, Commander," Wren called back. "You'll want a salvage crew for your salvage crew. Should keep you busy about a week."

"A week." Ferran's laugh had no warmth in it at all. "And the chart?"

"Burning while you talk."

For a long moment the searchlight held, and Wren watched Ferran weigh it—the wreck gone deep, the chart gone to ash, the documents still out there somewhere in the world, and the woman responsible standing on her own sinking ship waiting to be collected. Water was at Wren's knees now. The deck canted hard to port.

"Come get me, then," Wren said. "But do it quick. I'd rather not drown for free."

They took the boat's boat across, and Ferran met them on the launch deck, and the irons were cold and heavier than Wren remembered. Around the launch, out on the dark water, boats had gathered—fishing boats, oyster skiffs, the ferry from Two Mills, riverfolk drawn by the searchlight like moths of their own, watching in silence as the *Marguerite*'s mast slipped under with a sound like a long exhale.

Ferran followed their gaze, then looked at the crowd lining the banks, dozens of faces in lantern light, and something flickered across her count-the-wrecks face that might have been the first doubt of her career.

"Captain Winterbourne," she said, testing the name aloud, loud enough for the banks to hear. "Or whatever you call yourself. You're under arrest for obstruction, sabotage, and crimes against lawful salvage."

"That's three names," Wren said. "I only need one."

The launch turned downstream, toward the fleet, toward the magistrate's barge Ferran kept for exactly this purpose. And on the banks, in the dark, the riverfolk began to follow.
