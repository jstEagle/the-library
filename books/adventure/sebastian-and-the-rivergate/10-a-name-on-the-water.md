## 10. A Name on the Water

The confession ran to eleven pages in my father's hand, and I read it four times before dawn.

I had spent nine years believing he was dead. The Republic fell the winter I turned nineteen, and the news sheets printed his name among the executed—*Minister Corvan Vantor, shot at the river stairs, body unclaimed.* It was a clean sentence, and I built a grief on it that felt almost holy. Grief for a dead man asks nothing of you. Grief for a coward who bought his life with his crew's is another animal entirely.

He hadn't died at the river stairs. He had paid a ferryman, dyed his beard, and crossed into the delta country under a name I won't repeat here because he doesn't deserve to have it spoken twice. And when the wreck went down in the Rivergate with the treasury aboard—and with forty-three crew who might have been saved—he chose the strongbox over the boats. The chest Wren pulled from the shallows held the proof: manifests in his hand, a map to where he'd hidden what remained, and a confession written late, written scared, written by a man who knew the river keeps everything eventually.

Wren had lied to me about how they got it. I knew that within a day. Nobody salvages a sealed Vessany chest from a flood-exposed wreck by accident, and nobody's hands shake like that over a stranger's signature. But I let the lie stand for a while, because I needed time to decide what kind of truth I could afford.

Then Ferran took them, and there was no more time.

* * *

I published the confession on a Tuesday, which Tammi swore was bad luck and did it anyway beside me.

We printed two hundred copies on the hand-press behind the chandlery in Grayharbor—the same press that usually turned out tide tables and bills of lading. Old Sef, who owned it, read the first page, went gray, and said only, "Set it wide. People should be able to read this from arm's length." He didn't ask whose hand it was. Riverfolk know better than to ask questions they can already answer.

At the top of each sheet, above my father's signature, I set my own testimony. Three paragraphs. I wrote them standing up, because sitting down made me too careful:

*I am Ilse Vantor, daughter of Corvan Vantor, last minister of the Republic of Vessany. My father did not die as reported. He survived by abandoning his crew, and he hid the treasury that belonged to the Republic's people—not to its ministers, not to its creditors, and not to Commander Odile Ferran, who served the same government and now claims it as her pension. I have seen the documents. I sign this knowing what signing costs.*

I didn't mention Wren. Not because I was protecting their secret anymore—they were past secrets, chained in the hold of Ferran's salvage barge—but because their story wasn't mine to tell. They would tell it or they wouldn't. That was the whole point of what came next.

By noon the sheets were nailed to every post from Grayharbor to the Ferry Steps. By evening they were three towns further than any boat of ours had traveled, passed hand to hand, read aloud in taverns by people who couldn't read, argued over, copied out in pencil on butcher paper. A dead nation's money does something to people. It makes them remember they were owed.

And then they remembered Odile Ferran owed *them*.

* * *

Tammi told me about it after, and I'm setting down her account here because she deserves to be more than a footnote in someone else's book.

Ferran kept the Marguerite's captain locked in the forward hold of the salvage barge *Iron Meridian*, anchored off the Ferry Steps. She had thirty armed crew, two gun barges, and the law—at least the version of it the magistrates sold by the sheet. What she didn't have was pilots. Every riverman from the Gate to the delta had gone quiet. Barges wouldn't carry her fuel. Lockkeepers found reasons to close. A woman who counts wrecks should have understood sooner that you cannot dredge a river that has decided not to be dredged.

On the third morning, forty-odd boats came downriver at slack water. Fishing skiffs, coal lighters, the Grackle's owner's pleasure yawl with its brass rails polished for war. Tammi stood in the bow of the lead skiff with a speaking trumpet and no weapon at all, which somehow made it worse for Ferran than any gun would have.

"We've read the confession," Tammi called across the water. "We've read her testimony." A jerk of her thumb toward me, standing on the Grayharbor dock where I could hear every word carried flat and clear over the still water. "Now we're reading you your options. You can put the captain ashore and take your fleet through the Gate by noon. Or you can stay, and we'll show you how many wrecks one river can make."

Ferran came to her rail. Even at that distance I could see she was doing arithmetic—I'd learned that about her secondhand but thoroughly. Thirty guns against forty boats full of people who knew every sandbar, every snag, every channel that could swallow a barge hull-deep in an hour. She was a reasonable woman. That was always her corruption: reasonableness, ambition wearing a sensible face, the pension she believed she was owed.

"You understand," she called back, "that if I give him up, I have nothing."

"You came with nothing," Tammi said. "You just hadn't noticed yet."

It took until noon, as promised. They brought Wren up on deck blinking, wrists roped, and set them in a skiff with two of Ferran's crew as escort, and the escort turned back halfway because forty boats had drifted closer without anyone ordering them to, and Ferran's men were not paid enough to argue with a river. Wren sat alone in the skiff for the last hundred yards, rowing themselves, while the whole waterfront watched and nobody cheered, because cheering felt wrong for something that solemn. Tammi met the skiff at the steps and cut the ropes herself.

Ferran's fleet went through the Gate at half past noon, riding light, stripped of dignity if not of guns. The last anyone saw of the *Iron Meridian*, she was wallowing downstream toward the delta with her commander at the rail, counting—wrecks, losses, years. Some said she'd be back with magistrates and writs. But writs need enforcement, enforcement needs pilots, and the pilots had chosen.

* * *

Wren stayed on my floor for three nights and spoke maybe forty sentences the whole time.

I understood. I had spent nine years constructing a father out of a printed death notice; they had spent longer constructing a person out of silence. When the scaffolding comes down, you don't rush the rebuilding. So I fed them, let them sleep, and on the third night I finally asked the question I'd been carrying since the press room.

"The testimony in the confession. The witness who was coerced. That was you."

They looked at the lamp instead of me. "I was nineteen," they said. "They had my brother's papers. I told them what they wanted about the minister's movements, and they used it, and he ran, and the crew—" A breath. "The wreck told the rest before I could."

"I know what coercion is," I said. "I've read my father's handwriting four times. He had choices at every step, and he chose himself. You had one choice, and it was made with a knife at someone else's throat. Those aren't the same crime, Wren. I'd have signed whatever they put in front of me too."

"It doesn't feel different from inside."

"No," I said. "But it is different from outside. That's why we publish. So the inside stops being the only room you live in."

They were quiet a long time. Then: "My name isn't Winterbourne."

"I know."

"Would you like to know what it is?"

"Only if you want to say it aloud. That's the whole test now."

And they said it. I won't print it here either—that's theirs, and this page has carried enough of other people's names. But I watched them say it, watch their own mouth do it, like a man testing ice that turns out to hold.

* * *

The new boat was Sef's idea, though Tammi built half of it and the riverfolk supplied the rest—planking from the Grackle's old storeroom, a wheel from the drowned ferry *Cormorant*, canvas donated by three widows who claimed the Republic's treasury owed them anyway and this was a fair first installment. She was a shallow-draught packet, ninety feet if she was an inch, built for the Gate's fickle water, and she went together in five weeks because nobody involved believed in waiting.

They named her nothing grand. Just *Testimony*. Tammi painted it on the bow herself, in letters tall enough to read from the far bank, and stood back with paint on her knuckles and said, "Well. If we're going to be honest, we may as well be loud about it."

The first manifest was drawn up on a bright morning with the tide making. Sef acted as witness. I stood at the rail as cargo master pro tem, which meant I had personally supervised the loading of four hundred pounds of printed confessions bound for towns upriver that hadn't heard yet, plus ordinary freight, because a boat runs on freight and truth both.

Wren stood at the wheel with the manifest board on the chart table, pen uncapped, and hesitated.

I saw the hesitation and knew exactly what it weighed. A signature is a small thing—a loop of ink, a legal fiction, a way for strangers to find you later. For nine years that had been the danger. Somewhere along the way it had become the point.

"Captain," Tammi said, not unkindly, "tide's making."

Wren signed. Full name, clear hand, no flourish and no hiding. Then they rang the bell twice, cast off the lines Tammi threw, and took *Testimony* out on the ebb with the confession-laden cargo riding low and honest in her hold.

I watched from the dock until the far bend took them, and I noticed I wasn't afraid for them anymore. That was new. That was the whole difference, maybe: fear is what hiding costs, and it turns out living truthfully costs less. Somebody should have told us both years ago. Somebody probably did.

The river took them north, toward the towns that hadn't heard, toward the treasury that still waited in the dark where my father hid it, toward whatever reckoning the magistrates downstream were already drafting in their comfortable offices. There would be hearings. There would be men with reasonable faces asking reasonable questions about who owned a dead nation's money. There would be Odile Ferran somewhere in it, counting.

Wren would meet all of it with a name on the water behind them and a deckhand at their shoulder, and for the first time in either of our lives, I believed the right side of the ledger was going to win.

Not because it deserved to.

Because now, finally, somebody was keeping accounts.
