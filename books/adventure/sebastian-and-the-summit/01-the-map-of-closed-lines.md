## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The lamp in room 14 of the Cordillera Hotel had a bad wick. Isandro Vane trimmed it with a thumbnail, waited for the flame to settle from orange to a steady white, and went back to work.

The work was the map. It was always the map.

It lay across the desk now, four sheets of drafting paper glued at the seams, covering most of the surface and hanging an inch over each edge. On it, drawn in ink fine as hair, was every railway line in three hundred miles that no longer carried a train. Lines abandoned. Lines closed by order. Lines that had simply stopped appearing in the timetables one season, as if they'd been swallowed. He'd been building it for eleven years, town by town, depot by depot, and it had grown so dense in the industrial valleys that he'd had to switch to a finer nib just to fit everything in.

He worked the way he always worked: coat still on, bag still packed, one boot off and one boot on. The ritual was older than the map. You never unpacked fully. You never let a room become yours. A man who unpacked was a man who could be found, and a man who could be found was a man who could be asked questions, and Isandro Vane had spent eleven years arranging his life so that no one could ask him anything.

Outside, the town of Grayfall Junction did what rail-towns do after ten o'clock. A freight ground through on the mainline, forty cars if it was one, dragging its sound behind it like a dress. Somewhere a dog gave up barking at it. The hotel's pipes knocked twice and went quiet.

Isandro dipped the pen and drew.

Tonight's addition was a spur off the old Kestrel branch — a two-mile stub that had once served a slate quarry above the tree line. He'd gotten the bearings from a stationmaster who remembered it dimly and a brakeman who remembered it precisely, and the two accounts disagreed by half a degree, which meant one of them was lying or both of them were guessing. He split the difference and marked the uncertainty with a small question mark in the margin, because a map that pretended to certainty was worse than no map at all. That was the whole point of the thing. Every solid line on it was a lie somebody official had told; only the gaps told the truth.

There were more gaps than lines, these days. That was the era. The syndicates had spent fifteen years buying up private roads, running them for whatever profit could be stripped out in eighteen months, then closing them and writing closure surveys that read like eulogies — *uneconomic, redundant, no further traffic foreseeable* — while the rails rusted and the towns that had grown up around the depots dried up and blew away. Isandro had watched it happen to maybe forty towns. He had watched it happen, in a way he did not let himself think about, to his own name.

The pen scratched. The lamp hissed. His left boot stayed on because taking it off would mean deciding to stay the night, and he hadn't decided yet. He never decided until morning. Morning decisions could be reversed by noon.

He heard the footsteps on the stairs at half past eleven and knew them before he knew why. Not the landlady — she moved like she owned the wood. Not a guest — guests dragged their heels. These steps came down the corridor light and even, with a pause outside his door long enough to be deliberate, and then three knocks, spaced like punctuation.

"Mr. Vane." A voice through the door, dry as ledger paper. "Telegram."

"I didn't send for one."

"No," the voice agreed. "You didn't."

Isandro set down the pen. He looked at the door for a moment, then at the window — second floor, twelve feet to the stable roof, an easy drop he'd already measured when he took the room, because he measured everything — and then he got up, kept his weight off the boards near the door where they creaked, and opened it with the chain still on.

The man in the corridor was fifty-ish, gray at the temples, dressed like weather rather than fashion: a good coat worn without vanity, boots that had seen actual mud. He held a telegram envelope in one hand and nothing else. No case. No weapon visible, which meant either none or a good one.

"Corvus Hale," the man said. "Attorney. I'm told you're the person to see about dead railways."

"I'm not a person to see about anything. I'm passing through."

"You've been passing through Grayfall Junction for six days, Mr. Vane. You take your meals at the depot café because the hotel dining room has a window facing the street. You declined the landlady's offer of a weekly rate on Tuesday. And yesterday you spent four hours in the records office copying the closure survey for the Kestrel branch." Hale said all this without heat, the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "I don't mean to alarm you. I mean to demonstrate that I did my homework before I came, and that if I found you, other people can."

The chain stayed on. "Other people."

"Other people." Hale held up the envelope. "This is yours regardless. But the message will make more sense if we talk first, and I'd rather talk now than have you vanish and make me chase you across two counties. May I come in?"

"No."

Hale smiled slightly, as if the answer confirmed something he'd hoped. "The corridor, then. It's cold, but I'll be brief."

Isandro weighed it. The man had done his homework, which was either flattering or fatal, and there was no third option. He unhooked the chain, stepped back exactly far enough, and let Hale into the doorway — not the room, the doorway — while he himself stood by the desk with the lamp between them.

Hale glanced at the map. Isandro saw him glance, saw him stop glancing, saw the professional stillness come over his face the way water comes over a stone. Whatever Corvus Hale had expected to find in a cheap hotel room, it wasn't three hundred miles of erased country rendered in ink fine enough to read from across the room.

"That's the Kestrel Range," Hale said quietly. "All of it. Every closed line."

"And some open ones. For contrast."

"The survey team went missing up there nine days ago." Hale set the telegram envelope on the corner of the desk, deliberately away from the map, a courtesy or a test. "Four men. Government survey party, working under commission from the Interior Office. They went up the east fork to run a preliminary line and they did not come down. There's been a search. It found their camp. It found nothing else."

"People get lost in the Kestrels every year."

"These people weren't lost." Hale's voice didn't change at all, and that was what made the sentence land. "Their tents were pitched. Their instruments were calibrated and covered. Their food was cooked and left in the pans. Mr. Vane, they were found the way you find a room when someone has stepped out to fetch water. Except there is no water source within a mile of that camp, and there are no tracks leading out of it. None. In snow."

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, faintly, the landlady laughed at something.

"What does that have to do with me?" Isandro said.

"Because the Interior Office wants the matter handled quietly, and the syndicate wants it handled quieter, and the families want it handled loudest, and everyone involved agrees that the one thing nobody wants is a government expedition crawling over that range asking questions. So they want one competent individual. Unaffiliated. Untraceable." Hale finally looked up from the map, and his eyes were pale and patient and entirely without warmth. "Your name came up."

"From whom?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously."

"Then let's say," Hale said, "that someone who knows your work also knows your habits, and believes you're the only man alive who can cross that range before the snows and come back with an answer. The fee is five hundred dollars. Half now, half on delivery of a report. The report can say whatever you find. Or whatever you don't."

Five hundred dollars. Isandro kept his face still, but arithmetic moved behind it whether he wanted it to or not. Five hundred dollars was a winter. Five hundred dollars was a year of rooms with bad lamps and trains taken in the wrong direction on purpose. Five hundred dollars was the difference between running and resting, and he had not rested in eleven years, and his body had begun sending him invoices about it.

Money that asks no questions is never money that asks no questions. He knew that. He'd learned it young, in a house with a different name on the door, learning that every gift has a deed attached somewhere.

"And if I find them?" he said. "The surveyors."

"Bring back proof of what happened. Bodies, documents, instruments, testimony — whatever the mountain gives you. My client doesn't care about justice. My client cares about certainty. Someone is paying very well right now for this story to end one particular way, and my client would prefer it ended a different one, and is willing to fund the difference." Hale straightened his coat, a small precise motion. "You'll want to know who my client is. I'm not able to tell you tonight. If that disqualifies me, tear up the telegram and I'll trouble you no further."

Silence. Isandro looked at the map instead of the man — at the Kestrel Range in the upper corner, where he'd drawn the summit line in a heavier hand than the others, because he'd drawn it from memory, and memory has a heavier hand than records. A line that had never appeared on any closure survey. A line whose closure had never needed to be surveyed, because the people who could have contested it were dead.

His own name was written into the deeds of that line. He had spent eleven years making sure no document anywhere connected the name Vane to the name Vane. And here was a lawyer standing in his doorway saying *Kestrel* like it was a place and not a wound.

"The telegram," Isandro said.

Hale passed it over. Isandro broke the seal with his thumb and read it under the lamp. It was short. Most true things are.

> VANES STOP SURVEY PARTY KESTREL EAST FORK OVERDUE STOP CAMP LOCATED ABANDONED STOP RETAIN SERVICES INVESTIGATE STOP REPLY WESTERN UNION GRAYFALL STOP HALE

Addressed to *Vanes*. Not Vane. Whoever had sent it knew enough to misspell his name correctly — knew that Isandro Vane received mail under the plural, the way a man receives mail under an alias he invented to insult himself. Only two living people used that spelling, and he had buried one of them and hadn't spoken to the other in nine years.

He looked up. "Who gave you that spelling?"

"A letter of introduction," Hale said. "Old paper. I confess I assumed it was a clerical error until I saw your face just now." Something moved behind the lawyer's patience — not quite curiosity; lawyers file curiosity under expenses. "So it wasn't."

"It wasn't."

Hale nodded slowly, and for the first time since entering the doorway he seemed to be recalculating, a man discovering the bridge he'd crossed was rated for less than his load. "Mr. Vane. I am going to leave half the fee with the landlady against your acceptance, and I am going to leave you alone, and tomorrow at noon I will be at the depot café, where you take your meals because of the window. If you're there, we have terms. If you're not—" he paused at the threshold, coat already buttoned, "—then I'll know the mountain keeps its secrets better than you keep yours, and I'll tell my client so. Good night."

His footsteps went down the corridor, even and light, down the stairs, out into the street, gone.

Isandro stood a long time with the telegram in his hand.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat at the desk with one boot on and one boot off and read the map the way other men read letters from home.

The Kestrel Range occupied the top-left corner, and he had drawn it last spring in a hotel not unlike this one, in a town he'd left before dawn. The summit line ran up the page like a scar: *Kestrel & Summit Railway, chartered 18—, completed 18—, operated eleven years.* He had put the dates in small numerals beneath the name, and then, because the map was honest even when he wasn't, he had written in the margin beside it, in pencil so it could be erased, a single word: *why?*

He had never erased it. Eleven years of erasing everything else about himself — forwarding addresses, surnames on registers, the whole architecture of a traceable life — and he had never erased that pencil word. Some nights he thought the entire map existed to carry it. Three hundred miles of dead track, and every line on it was a question he couldn't ask aloud, and the Kestrel line was the loudest.

Because the Vanes had built it. Because the Vanes had owned it outright, free of syndicate money, the last independent road in the range, hauling ore down from a tunnel at the summit that the company books recorded as exhausted in its second year. Exhausted. His grandfather had died insisting otherwise, and had died poor, and the family had scattered like spilled shot, and Isandro had grown up on stories told in low voices about men in good coats who came to buy and stayed to threaten, and about the winter the last Vane crew went up the mountain and did not come down.

He had been a child. He had been told it was avalanche weather. He had believed it the way children believe things, completely, and then he had grown up and become an engineer, and engineers do not believe in avalanches that leave no debris, and somewhere around his thirtieth year the belief had rotted through, and he had started running — not from the law, not from debt, not from anything a sheriff could name, but from the simple gravitational fact that if he ever stood still long enough, someone might ask him about the Kestrel line, and he would have to answer, and he did not know the answer, and not knowing had become unbearable in a way he managed the way you manage a bad tooth: by never biting down.

Now a lawyer had bitten down for him.

Isandro picked up the pen and, without letting himself think about it — thinking was how you talked yourself out of necessary things — drew a new line on the map. East fork, upper reach, above the tree line. He marked a small square for the camp and wrote beside it, in the margin, the date, and after the date the words *abandoned, cause unknown.*

Then he sat back and looked at what he'd done, and understood that he had already accepted the job. The map had decided. The map always decided; he just did the walking.

Five hundred dollars. Half now. He could take the half, walk out of Grayfall Junction tonight, and be two counties away by Friday, and the money would buy him a season of nowhere. That was the sensible play. That was the play he had run eleven years without variation.

But Hale had said *other people can find you,* and the terrible thing, the thing Isandro turned over and over as the lamp burned low, was that part of him had felt relief when he heard it. Eleven years of being unfindable, and the first stranger who found him had brought news from the one direction Isandro had spent his whole life not looking. It was like a tooth finally breaking. The pain was enormous and underneath the pain was something almost like gratitude.

He trimmed the wick again. The flame steadied.

On the desk, the telegram lay next to the map, and the two words faced each other — *VANES* and *Kestrel* — like the two halves of a sentence he'd refused to finish for thirty years.

Tomorrow at noon, the depot café. He would be there. He told himself he wanted the money, and that was true. He told himself it was survival, and that was true too. But underneath both truths, quiet as a rail joint clicking past at speed, ran a third one he wouldn't say even to himself: whoever had written that telegram had used a spelling only the dead and the estranged knew, and Isandro Vane had spent eleven years making sure no one could find him, and someone had found him anyway, and some starved corner of him was tired, so tired, of being impossible to find.

He packed the pen. He banked the lamp. He kept the boot on.

Outside, far up the valley, so faint it might have been wind, the mainline freight whistled once for the crossing at Grayfall and faded toward the mountains — toward the east fork, toward the camp with the food still warm in the pans, toward whatever was waiting up there that had made four trained men step out of existence without leaving a single print in the snow.

Isandro listened until the sound was gone.

Then he took off the boot, and lay down on top of the blankets in his coat, and did not unpack, and did not sleep, and in the morning he would be at the depot café by eleven, an hour early, sitting where he could see the door.
