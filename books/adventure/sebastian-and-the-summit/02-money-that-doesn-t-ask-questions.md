## 2. Money That Doesn't Ask Questions

The man from Grayfall Junction wore a coat that cost more than Isandro's sled, and he sat down in the hotel's narrow parlor like a man who had never once been kept waiting.

"Vane." He said the name the way you'd test ice with one foot. "Isandro Vane. The engineer."

"That depends who's asking, and what for."

"Corvus Hale. Attorney-at-law, Grayfall Junction." He set a leather folio on the table between them and did not open it. "I represent parties who prefer to remain behind their attorney. You'll forgive the arrangement. It's common in matters of some delicacy."

Isandro poured coffee from the pot the landlady had left and didn't offer any. Outside the window, the rail-town went about its morning: ore wagons, a switch crew arguing over a frozen point, the ten-fifteen freight grinding through on its way to nowhere that mattered. Towns like this one existed because a line had been drawn through them, and they would die when the pencil lifted. Isandro had stayed in forty of them. He knew their whole biography by the smell of the depot.

"You sent a telegram," Hale said.

"I received one. Sending implies I answered. I haven't."

"And yet here we are."

Here we are. Isandro drank his coffee and studied the man. Hale was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, hands soft as ledger paper. His eyes did the thing Isandro distrusted most in a stranger: they catalogued. Doorways, exits, the weight of the man across the table, the value of him. Lawyers and railroad men shared that habit. It was why Isandro had spent eleven years making sure there was nothing in any room worth cataloguing.

"My clients," Hale said, "retained me to find four men. A government survey team, contracted last spring to run a preliminary line through the Kestrel Range. They went in at the end of summer. They were due back six weeks ago. They have not come back."

"People go missing in the Kestrels every year."

"These people were carrying instruments worth more than this town." Hale opened the folio at last and turned it around. Inside was a photograph of five men squinting into sun, a contract of employment, and a figure written in careful numerals that made Isandro's coffee taste suddenly expensive. "That is the fee. Half now, half on recovery of the team or—failing that—their instruments and records. My clients are not sentimental. They want the survey completed or its evidence secured. Either outcome satisfies."

"Either outcome," Isandro repeated.

"The mountains are indifferent to which. So are my clients."

It was too much money. That was the first thing, and Isandro had learned to treat *too much* the way other men treated *not enough*—as a warning rather than an invitation. Money that matched the work was wages. Money that outran the work was bait, and somewhere past the bait there was always a hook, and past the hook a hand, and the hand always wanted something you hadn't agreed to give.

"Your clients are generous."

"My clients are thorough." Hale folded his hands. "They ask only that you find the team, or what remains of the matter, and report to me alone. No newspapers. No talk in depots. No copies of anything you recover. You'll understand that a survey touching certain private holdings generates—complications. Land claims. Litigation. My clients wish to keep the matter quiet until it can be resolved properly."

Quiet. Private holdings. Evidence secured. Isandro set down the cup.

"You're asking me to walk into those mountains and bring back whatever I find, and hand it to you unopened."

"I'm asking you to do a job and be discreet about it. Men have built entire careers on less."

* * *

Eleven years ago, in a town called Meridian Cut, Isandro Vane had unpacked.

Not fully—he had never once unpacked fully, even then—but more than usual. A shelf for his books. His father's drafting tools laid out on the desk instead of rolled in oilcloth. He'd signed a nine-month contract to regrade a spur line, and nine months had seemed long enough to be a man somewhere, to be known at the hardware store, to have a regular table at the regular café where the regular girl brought the regular coffee and knew he took it black without being told twice.

Her name had been Lira. She'd worked the counter with flour on her forearms because she baked before her shift, and she'd laughed at his jokes a half-second late because she thought about them first, and in March she had asked him, casually, terribly, what he was doing after the contract ended.

He hadn't slept that night. Not because of her. Because of the name.

Vane. Say it aloud in any room between the coast and the range and watch what happened: the pause, the recalculation, the sudden interest. The Vanes had built the summit line through the Kestrels—his grandfather's generation, iron in the blood and deeds in the bank—and then the line had closed, the fortune had evaporated, the family had scattered like sparks off a grinding wheel, and every one of them had learned the same lesson in a different schoolroom: the name drew attention the way a dropped drawbridge draws traffic, and none of the traffic was friendly. His father had died owing money to men whose names were never written down. His uncle had changed his surname twice and been found anyway. Isandro, at nineteen, had understood that the only inheritance worth having was the one nobody could trace, and he had begun the discipline of the half-unpacked bag.

In April, three weeks after Lira's question, a man had come to the worksite asking questions about Vane holdings, Vane heirs, Vane claims in the old summit district. Not a lawyer. A polite man with a notebook and a way of standing that made the other laborers drift away from him. Isandro had finished his shift, gone home, packed everything—including the books, including the tools—in under an hour, and taken the night freight out with no destination chosen except *away*.

He'd left a note for Lira. He still remembered composing it, standing in the depot's yellow light, trying to make *I'm sorry* and *it isn't you* and *you're better off* sound like anything but what it was, which was a man choosing fear over flour-armed mornings. He'd mailed it from two towns over. He never learned if she read it. He had made sure he couldn't learn. That was the price of the discipline: you didn't just leave places, you left the versions of yourself that could have stayed, and after enough departures you were mostly luggage.

Winter money was survival money. That was the arithmetic he lived by. Summer paid thin and often; winter paid nothing at all unless you'd banked against it, and a man with no fixed address couldn't bank, only carry. The figure in Hale's folio would carry him through two winters and a new set of instruments besides. And the job itself—finding a lost survey team in the Kestrels—was exactly the kind of work he was good at and exactly the kind nobody asked twice about, because the kind of client who lost a survey team in the Kestrels preferred not to discuss how.

All of which was true, and all of which was beside the point. The point was the hook. He could feel it, fine as a fisherman's leader, running through the whole generous offer: *report to me alone, no copies, evidence secured.* They weren't hiring a finder. They were hiring an eraser with a conscience, or hoping to find out whether he had one.

And yet.

And yet winter was coming, and the Kestrels were the one range he knew better than any living man should know a range, because his family had put a line through it, and if there was one thing worse than walking back into the country that had ruined his name, it was starving somewhere warmer while thinking about it.

* * *

"I'll need advance expenses," Isandro said. "Sled, supplies, a tracker. The high passes are already shedding snow."

Hale produced an envelope without looking away from him. "Half the fee is available on signature. The remainder on delivery."

"On delivery of what, precisely?"

"Whatever the mountain gives you." Hale smiled for the first time, and it was a well-made smile, machined like a coupling. "My clients deal in outcomes, Mr. Vane. Not sentiment."

There it was again—that word, *clients*, deployed like a screen of trees. Isandro took the envelope. It was heavy. Heavy envelopes were either honest or very dishonest, and there was no telling which until you were downstream of them.

"One condition of mine," Isandro said. "If I find those men alive, I bring them out. Whatever your clients want quiet, dead men are quieter than live ones, and I don't deliver quiet."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes—not offense, not surprise. Interest. The look of a man adding a figure to a column he'd already started.

"Naturally," Hale said. "My clients hope for survivors. Survivors testify."

"Testify to what?"

"To whatever happened." Hale rose, buttoned his beautiful coat, and gathered the folio. At the door he paused, and for a moment the machined polish slipped and something older looked out—something tired, or something hungry; with lawyers the two were hard to tell apart. "You have your grandfather's look, you know. There's a portrait of Aurelio Vane in the Grayfall courthouse lobby. Standing on the summit trestle with a level in his hand. The engineers of that family always did have the same jaw."

He left before Isandro could answer, which Isandro suspected was the point. The door clicked. The parlor held its breath.

Isandro sat a long time with the envelope in his hands. Outside, the ten-fifteen's echo had faded and the town resumed its small sounds, and somewhere north of all of it the Kestrels stood white and silent around a secret that four men had walked into and five signatures had paid him to walk out of.

He counted the money once, then again, then swept it into the inner pocket where he kept the map of closed lines—the one document he'd never surrendered to any room, any fire, any year. Winter money. Survival money. Bait money.

He told himself he'd know the hook when he felt it pull.

He was wrong about that. But he signed the paper Hale had left on the table, because the ink was there and the winter wasn't waiting, and because a man who has practiced leaving for eleven years will sign almost anything that points him toward the door.

By dusk he'd bought the sled. By full dark he'd wired the only tracker he trusted, a woman named Odile Ferran working out of Coldwater, two days' ride south. Her reply came near midnight, four words long:

*Coming. What did you sign?*

Isandro looked at the contract, at the clause he'd read three times and still couldn't parse—*all materials recovered to be conveyed intact to the office of C. Hale, esq.*—and blew out the lamp.

*Ask me when you get here,* he wired back.

He did not unpack. He never unpacked. But that night, for the first time in eleven years, he caught himself checking the door latch twice, and lying awake listening for wheels on the street below, and understanding—with the particular clarity that arrives only after the signature dries—that the job had started before he'd said yes, and that somewhere in the dark between Grayfall and this rented room, someone had already decided what he was going to find in the Kestrel Range.

The question—the one that would follow him up every switchback, through every snowfall, all the way to a sealed tunnel mouth under the summit—was whether they had decided correctly.

He fell asleep in his clothes, boots within reach, the map case strapped to his wrist like a pulse.
