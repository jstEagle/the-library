## 3. Gear Without Ghosts

The survey's last camp sat in a bowl of the Kestrel foothills where the map said nothing should be, and Isandro felt the wrongness of it before they were close enough to see a single tent.

They had come up from Grayfall Junction on the old haul road, two days of climbing through pine that thickened and then thinned as the grade steepened, Odile Ferran setting a pace ahead of him that made it clear she considered his legs a formality. She was fifty if she was a day, built like a fence post, with a face weathered to the color of old rope and eyes that moved constantly, cataloguing. He'd hired her at the livery stable in Grayfall because she was the only tracker within a hundred miles who hadn't flinched when he said *government survey team, missing eleven days*.

"Flint," she'd said, spitting into the sawdust. "I read sign for Flint."

Now she stopped at the edge of the bowl and stood very still, the way she'd been doing all morning whenever the terrain changed character, and Isandro came up beside her and looked down at what the government had left behind.

Four tents. Wall tents, the good kind, guyed out taut against the wind, their ridgelines straight. A cook fire ring with a canvas fly over it. The sleds parked and loaded. And everywhere, arranged with the tidy precision of people who expected to come back before dark, the apparatus of a working survey: tripod legs splayed, instrument cases open, a theodolite standing on its plate with the dust cover off.

Nobody was there.

"They left in a hurry," Isandro said, because he needed to say something ordinary.

Odile didn't answer. She was reading the ground.

Isandro let her work. He went down into the camp alone, moving slowly, an engineer's habit of approaching any structure—bridge, building, camp—as something that might fail under him. Up close the wrongness got worse instead of better. The tents weren't just pitched; they were pitched *well*, snow skirts buried, ventilation flaps set for the cold snap that had come through four nights ago. Inside the nearest one, bedrolls lay unrolled and turned back, as if the sleepers had risen that morning and stepped out to check on something. Boots stood beside two of them. Nobody walks out of a camp in the Kestrels without boots.

The cook fire held ash from a fire burned down to gray, not raked out, not banked—a fire abandoned mid-life. Beside it, a Dutch oven sat on flat stones with its lid on. Isandro lifted the lid.

Bread. Half a loaf, baked maybe twelve days ago, frozen solid, untouched. A pot of beans beside it, same story. Enough food for six men for three weeks, stacked in crates with the lids nailed back down after the last resupply, which meant someone had inventoried it, accounted for it, planned around it—and then walked away from all of it.

He checked the instruments last, because instruments were his language. The theodolite was leveled, its plate oriented, sighting notes chalked on a slate leaning against the tripod: readings taken, dated nine days back, mid-morning by the sun angle. The slate ended mid-column. Not finished. Not crossed out. Just stopped, the way a sentence stops when the man writing it looks up at something.

There were no tracks leading out of the camp.

That was the fact Isandro kept circling and coming back to, the way your tongue returns to a broken tooth. Snow had fallen twice since the team vanished—the first dusting eight days ago, a real fall five days ago—and both falls would have softened older sign. But not erased it. Not this snow, this dry and shallow. If six men had walked south toward the pass, there'd be a trough in the drifts you could still read. If they'd scattered, there'd be scatter. If they'd been taken, there'd be drag marks, blood, the churned mess of violence. Odile had spent forty minutes walking the perimeter in widening spirals, crouched, gloved fingers brushing the surface, and when she finally straightened up her face had gone carefully blank, which from Odile was a shout.

"Well?" Isandro asked.

She didn't look at him. She was looking at the treeline above the camp, at the unbroken white rising toward the rocks.

"There's nothing," she said.

"I can see that. What I'm asking is how."

"So am I." She walked to the edge of camp where the ground rose toward the trees and crouched again, and Isandro watched her do the thing he'd paid her to do: read snow like text. She traced the surface with two fingers, lifted them, rubbed them together. She studied a wind-carved saucer of drift behind a boulder. She followed the margin where the meadow met the pines for thirty yards, bent double, patient as erosion.

Then she stood, brushed off her knees, and said, "Come here. Look at this and tell me what you see."

He looked. Snow, slope, trees. "Nothing."

"That's right." Her voice was level, almost gentle, the way you talk to a man standing on a high ledge. "First snow was eight days back. Light, dry, an inch, maybe less. Anything that walked through it after that would leave a print, and anything that walked through it before it would leave a dimple underneath—you dig down, you find the crust broke. I dug. Six places along this margin, where anyone leaving camp would funnel through, going any direction." She pointed at the unbroken line of trees. "Underneath that inch of powder, the crust is whole. All six places. Whole crust, whole length. Nothing came out of this camp on foot. Not before the snow. Not after."

"The horses, then. They rode out."

"No horses in camp. No horse tracks anywhere in the bowl. No drag marks. No blood. No scuff at the tent doors except their own comings and goings, days old, half-healed under the first snowfall." She finally turned and looked at him, and her eyes were steady but something behind them wasn't. "Mister Vane. I've tracked men lost, men hiding, men dead, men who didn't want finding. There is always something. A man leaves sign breathing. Six men don't walk off a mountain leaving less than one rabbit does."

The wind moved through the pines above them with a long dry sound, and Isandro found that his hands were cold inside his gloves in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

"Could they have gone up?" he said. "Toward the summit. Rock wouldn't hold print."

"Rock holds print better than snow, if you know where to look. Scree rolls, lichen breaks, moss scuffed off stone. I read the whole upper margin." She shook her head once, slow. "Nothing went up either. This camp has been sitting here for days like a photograph. Everything in place. Everything mid-task. Bread in the oven, notes on the slate, boots by the beds." She paused. "And no ghosts in it. That's the part I don't like. Dead men leave more than live ones do. This camp's got neither."

Isandro walked back through it again, slower now, forcing himself to inventory rather than feel. Four tents, six men's gear—he counted plates, counted bedrolls, matched the manifest Hale had given him against what lay here, and it matched, item for item, down to the spare barometer wrapped in wool in its case. Six men present as of nine days ago, mid-morning. Six men absent ever since. No direction of departure. No struggle. No note—though he searched every tent, every crate seam, every fold of canvas, and found nothing written anywhere except that slate with its column of figures stopping mid-line.

The last figure on the slate was an elevation. He recognized the format; he'd written thousands of them himself. It was a sightline reading, shot toward some fixed point up-slope, and the number was precise to the foot, which meant the instrumentman had been doing careful work right up until the moment he wasn't.

Isandro copied the number into his notebook, then stood holding the pencil over the page, aware of Odile watching him.

"You knew these men?" she asked.

"No. Never met them."

"But you knew what they were looking for."

He closed the notebook. "Hale hired me to find the team. That's all the knowing I've done."

Odile looked at him for a long moment with those restless cataloguing eyes, and Isandro understood that she was deciding whether to call him a liar, and that she was filing his face away under a heading she'd revisit later. Trackers and engineers had that in common: both professions ran on the assumption that things left evidence, and both professions eventually taught you that the most important evidence was what was missing.

"Eleven days," she said. "In this cold, if they're alive, they're holed up somewhere with a fire, and I'd have found smoke or cut wood or dung. If they're dead, I'd have found them. Birds alone would've told me." She looked back at the camp, at the bread under its lid, the boots by the beds. "So either my trade has failed me for the first time in forty years, or those men left this camp in a way that doesn't make sign. And I don't believe in ways that don't make sign. I believe somebody's spent a lot of effort making sure there isn't any."

The word landed in Isandro's chest like a dropped tool. *Somebody.* Not weather, not misadventure, not a bear. Somebody. Which meant purpose, which meant other hands on this mountain besides theirs, which meant that the money Hale was paying—too much money, money that didn't ask questions—had bought him exactly what too much money always buys.

"We should look at where they were sighting," he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "The slate gives us a bearing. Whatever six trained surveyors stopped mid-reading to look at, it'll be up-slope, along that line."

Odile glanced at the theodolite, at the chalked figures, and for the first time since they'd entered the bowl she smiled, thin and without humor.

"An engineer," she said. "Flint said you'd think like one. Good. Because I track what moves, Vane, and whatever happened here didn't move. You'll have to track what *stayed*." She nodded up-slope, toward the silent white shoulder of the range where the bearing ran out and died against rock. "And I'll tell you now, free of charge, so you can't say nobody warned you. Camps like this—" she tipped her chin at the tents, the bread, the boots waiting by the beds for feet that never came back— "camps like this aren't abandoned. Abandoned means left behind. This was *left*. Like a stage set. Like somebody wanted whoever came looking to find exactly this, and nothing else."

"Find this and turn back, you mean."

"Or find this and keep climbing." She shouldered her pack and started up the margin of the bowl, choosing her line with small unhurried corrections, and Isandro fell in behind her, past the tents, past the leveled theodolite keeping its patient watch on a summit nobody had reached, out of the camp that held everything a search party would need to see and nothing a human being had actually left.

Behind them the wind smoothed the snow over their own tracks, and by nightfall even that much sign of them would be gone.
