## 4. The Surveyor's Notebook

Odile Ferran had read snow for twenty years, and it had never lied to her. People lied. Snow only repeated what they'd done, faithfully, in a script anyone patient enough could learn.

The camp at the tree line repeated nothing.

She walked the perimeter a third time while Vane stood in the middle of it, turning slowly, the way a man turns in an empty room when he suspects the walls. The tents stood in a neat half-moon, guyed taut, snow brushed from their flaps. The theodolite sat on its tripod under an oilcloth, level bubbles centered. A canvas food satchel hung from a pole, and when she opened it she found hardtack, tinned meat, a bag of dried apples—everything arranged, everything counted, everything untouched. A tin cup sat on a flat stone with a ring of rust where the last swallow had dried.

Six men. Cold morning. Tea half-finished. And then—nothing. Not scattered, not fled, not carried off. Just *absent*, the way a word goes absent from the tip of the tongue.

"Vane." She said it once. He didn't answer. She said it again, harder, and he came over, and she held out the cup so he could see the rust ring. "They weren't taken in a hurry. They weren't taken at all, by anything with feet. There's no sign of struggle, no sign of a third party, no sign they packed a single thing. You don't walk off a survey in the Kestrels in October without your coat." One of the bedrolls was still rolled, tied with its strap. "And you don't leave your bedroll tied."

Vane crouched by the fire ring. The ashes had been raked flat. Not scattered—*raked*, deliberate, someone tidying before they left. Or someone tidying after.

"Could they have gone up?" he said. "Toward the summit?"

"In what? They left their boots." She nodded at the tent nearest the fire, where two pairs of spare boots sat side by side under the flap, laces tucked in, dry. "Could they have gone down? Sure. Downhill, no gear, in felt socks. The snow would've kept the prints for a day at least. There's nothing. Not going down, not going up, not going sideways into the timber. Six men walked out of this camp the way smoke walks out of a doused fire."

Vane straightened. He had a face built for keeping things behind it, and she'd watched him keep things behind it since Grayfall—the way his eyes went to the ridgeline every few minutes, the way he checked the backtrail without seeming to. She'd taken it for the caution of a man who made his living not being found. Now, standing in a camp where six men had evaporated, she took it for something else.

"You knew the name of the survey chief," she said. "Before Hale gave it to you."

"I read the telegram."

"You read it twice. You read it the way a man reads a name he already knows."

Vane looked at her. For a moment she thought he'd deny it, and she found she was already deciding how she'd carry the sled down alone. Then he said, "The name's not common. I knew a family by it. A long time ago." And that was all, and his face closed like a door, and she let it go because the light was going and she wanted the camp searched properly before dark.

* * *

She started with the instrument tent, because instruments tell the truth about their owners. The theodolite was a good one, kept well—oiled, zeroed, the case lined with wool. Beside it, a barometer, a hypsometer, a set of stadia rods. And under the folding table, in a leather satchel with a brass buckle gone green, a field notebook.

She knew the type. Every surveyor she'd ever tracked carried one: the gridded pages, the stiff cover, the pencil stub tied to the spine with waxed cord. This one was swollen from damp and dried again, warped like a leaf. She opened it on her knee by the lantern.

The early pages were routine and beautiful in their way. Bearings. Elevations. Sketches of the grade line with little arrows for water runoff. Notes in a small, disciplined hand—*H. Merrick, Oct. 3, station 41, sightline clear to the shoulder, ground firm.* A surveyor's hand tells you about the surveyor. This one was patient. Careful. The kind of hand that checks its work twice and writes *check again* in the margin when it doesn't trust itself.

Then, around the middle of the book, the hand changed. Not sloppier—*faster*. The sketches got bigger. The margins filled. She found a page of elevations for a line that didn't follow the grade at all but climbed, steeply, toward a contour she recognized from her own years in these mountains: the shoulder below the summit saddle, where the old maps showed nothing but rock and snowfield.

And she found words that didn't belong in a survey book.

*Deed says the bore runs under the shoulder. Hale says the closure survey settled it. The closure survey never came up here. Why would it? You don't survey what you've already decided to bury.*

She read that line three times. *Hale.*

The lantern hissed. Outside, Vane was moving through the other tents, methodical, and she heard him pause at each one the way a man pauses at doors in a house where something used to live.

She turned pages faster now. The last dated entry was October 9—eleven days ago. *Found the old culvert. It's not a culvert. It's a portal, plugged and graded over. Someone spent money making a tunnel look like geology. Tomorrow we open it proper. If the bore is what the deed says, the mineral rights never lapsed. They were stolen. I'm going to write it all down and take it down the mountain myself. No wire. No post. Hand to hand.*

The next page was gone. So was the one after. So were the four after that.

She sat very still and looked at the stubs.

Whoever had torn them out had done it in a hurry but not carelessly—each stub was torn close to the spine, clean, one after another, six of them, a block removed whole. You tear out pages like that when you want a record gone but you don't have time to burn the book. Or when you want the pages *somewhere else*.

She turned back through the book slowly, and the cold that walked up her spine had nothing to do with the altitude.

*Hale says the closure survey settled it.*

Hale. Corvus Hale, the lawyer with the soft voice and the money that didn't ask questions, who had hired Vane in a rail-town hotel to find a missing survey team, and who had paid too well and asked too little. She'd told Vane it smelled wrong. Vane had taken the job anyway, because winter money is survival money, and she'd understood that, because she'd eaten her own share of winters.

But if Hale's name was in a dead—or vanished—surveyor's notebook, written with the flat wariness of a man naming a thing he feared—

Then the man who hired them to find the truth might be the man who'd buried it.

* * *

She went out into the blue dark with the notebook under her arm. The temperature had dropped hard; her breath hung. Vane stood by the supply sled, and he was holding something.

He was holding his map case.

She'd seen him handle it a dozen times since Grayfall—the long oiled-leather tube he carried slung across his back, the one with the hand-drawn map of closed lines rolled inside it. He treated it the way a priest treats a book. He'd never once let her look inside, and she'd never asked, because everyone who runs carries one thing they don't explain.

Now he had it open, and the map was half unrolled, and his face in the lantern light was the color of the snow.

"What is it," she said.

He didn't answer. She crossed to him and looked over his arm, and there, lying across the map of shuttered lines, was a single sheet of survey paper—gridded, creased, torn along one edge. Torn the way pages tear when they come out of a book in a hurry.

The stubs in the notebook matched it. She held the notebook open beside it, the ragged edge of the stubs against the ragged edge of the sheet, and they fit the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. Six pages gone from the book. One of them, here, in Isandro Vane's map case.

"Where did this come from," she said. Not a question. Her voice came out low and flat, the voice she used on spooked animals.

"I don't know." His voice was worse than flat. It was quiet in a way she hadn't heard from him yet, quiet the way ice is quiet before it takes weight. "It was in the case when I opened it tonight. It wasn't there this morning. I checked the case this morning. I always check the case."

"Who's touched it?"

"No one. It doesn't leave my back. It doesn't—" He stopped. She watched him run it backward through his head, every hour since Grayfall, and she watched him arrive at the same place she'd already arrived, and his jaw went tight. "The hotel. The night before we left, I had the case off for maybe an hour. While I was at the telegraph office. The room was locked."

"Locked rooms," Odile said, "are locked from the outside by other people all the time."

She took the sheet from him—gently, because he was holding it like something that might go off—and carried it to the lantern, weighting the corners with stones. The page was dense. Bearings, elevations, the same climbing line toward the summit shoulder. And in the margins, in that small disciplined hand, the same words repeated down the page like a man hammering a nail:

*VANE. VANE. The deed reads VANE. They didn't buy the line out from under them. They buried it with the name still on it.*

She heard Vane stop breathing behind her.

"Vane," she said. "The surveyor wrote your name."

"My family name." He came forward slowly, the way a man walks toward a thing in the dark he isn't sure he wants to see. He looked at the page for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere she hadn't heard it go—not flat, not quiet, but *old*, like a voice reading off a grave. "The Kestrel Summit Line. It was built by a family company. It failed. Everyone said it failed because the mountain beat it."

"And your name on the deed?"

"It's a common enough name." But he said it the way men say things they know aren't true, and then he didn't say anything at all for a while, and the wind came down off the shoulder and moved the lantern flame and their two shadows leaned together on the snow and straightened again.

"Think," she said. "The hotel. Who came and went. Who knew you'd taken Hale's money."

"Everyone. Grayfall is a rail town. A man takes a contract, the town knows by supper." He rubbed his face. "The telegraph clerk. The hotel boy who brought the water. A lawyer's runner came by—I never sent for him. I assumed Hale had sent him with a duplicate of the terms. He didn't come up. He just asked if the engineer Vane was stopping there, and left."

"And you let him."

"I let him." Vane's hand closed slowly, as if around something invisible. "I've spent eleven years making sure nobody finds me, Odile. I check rooms. I check back trails. I read a town before I sleep in it. And one runner with a paper walks into my hotel and I let him ask my name." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Because the money was good. Because I wanted it to be Hale's runner. Wanting things makes you stupid. I'd forgotten that."

She rolled the page and held it out to him, and after a moment he took it, and she watched him slide it into the map case with the map of closed lines, the two documents touching for the first time—the record of everything the railroads had abandoned, and the record of one line they'd buried alive.

"Here's what I read in it," she said. "Merrick found a tunnel under the summit shoulder. A plugged one. He found a deed that says the mineral rights under it never lapsed. He wrote it down, hand to hand, no wire, no post—because he already knew whoever owned the syndicate owned the wires and the post. Then six men walked out of their boots." She looked up at him. "Merrick didn't tear his own pages out and slip them into a stranger's map case. He never met you. But somebody in Grayfall knew Merrick was writing things down, and knew an engineer named Vane was heading up the mountain, and decided the safest place for one page was the one place nobody would think to search—the hired man's own kit. Somebody wanted you to find this. *Up here.* Not in town. Up here, where you couldn't take it back."

"Why up here?"

"Because up here it becomes yours. You carry it, you're holding the evidence. You turn back with it, you're carrying it *toward* Hale." She let that sit. "And if you die up here—which is the likeliest outcome, the way the weather's turning—then the page dies with you, and nobody's the wiser, and the mountain closes over the whole business the way it closed over your six surveyors."

Vane stood with the lantern raised, and the light made his face into planes and hollows, and for the first time since she'd signed on with him she saw him look genuinely afraid. Not of the mountain. She could tell the difference. He was afraid the way a man is afraid of a room he grew up in.

"The survey team was hunting a fortune," he said slowly. "And the fortune has my name written into it. And the people who erased the survey knew I was coming before I did." He looked north, toward the summit saddle, invisible now behind the dark shoulder. "They didn't hire me to find the team, Odile. They hired me to *be* found."

The wind rose. Somewhere down the valley, faint under the wind, she heard a sound she'd been listening for since the tree line and hadn't expected to hear so soon: a single note, high and carrying, the kind of note a man makes with a whistle when he's signaling across country to other men.

Odile killed the lantern.

"Get the sled," she whispered. "We're done being found."
