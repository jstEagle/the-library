## 5. Company on the Switchback

The foothills began where the rail grade ended, and the grade ended where a landslide had eaten it forty years ago. I could read the wound from below: a pale scar of broken rock running down the shoulder of the mountain like something spilled. Above it, the switchbacks started—old survey trails cut for mules and men with too much confidence and not enough winter.

We climbed out of the treeline camp at first light, Odile in front, me behind her with the rope coil over one shoulder, the supply sled dragging on its runners behind us both. The sled was Odile's idea. She'd looked at my pack list back in Grayfall Junction, then at the elevation profile of the Kestrel Range, and said, "You're carrying food for two men going up a mountain to die. Get a sled." She'd been right, which was becoming a habit I didn't enjoy.

The morning was hard and clear, the kind of cold that makes your breath sound like work. Frost had welded the grass to itself overnight, so every step cracked a little, as if the ground were complaining about us personally.

"Your map," Odile said, without turning around. "The closed lines. How many are there?"

"Forty-one," I said. "Forty-two if you count the spur at Marrow Bend, but nobody ever counted it officially, so it doesn't exist."

"And this trail we're on?"

"Never a line. Just a survey track. The government cut it in—" I did the arithmetic against the dates in Hale's dossier "—'61. They were looking for a route over the summit. They didn't find one they liked."

"But somebody did," she said.

I didn't answer that. Somewhere above us, past the switchbacks and the scree fields and whatever the syndicate's riders were doing on the lower slopes, there was a tunnel that had no business existing, and a name written into its deeds that I had spent eleven years trying to scrub off myself. I kept my eyes on the backs of Odile's boots and let the climbing do my thinking for me.

* * *

By midday we'd gained enough height that the valley behind us had flattened into a brown-and-gray mosaic stitched with the silver thread of the river. I stopped to check the sled lashings—the left runner had been riding crooked since the second hour, chattering on the frozen ground—and while I was down there, re-cinching the strap, I noticed two things.

First: the flour sack had lost weight. Not much. A pound, maybe two. Enough that when I hefted it, my hands knew before my head did.

Second: the tea tin had been opened. Closed again carefully, lid seated square, but the wax seal I'd pressed over it in Grayfall—I do that because altitude loosens lids and I don't share my tea—was broken and re-stuck with spit, which freezes into a convincing imitation of wax if you're not looking closely.

I sat back on my heels and looked down the slope we'd climbed.

Odile crouched beside me. She didn't ask what I'd found; she'd watched my face change. Instead she turned and looked where I was looking—at the long white fall of the mountainside, the wind-combed snow in the hollows, the dark stitch of pines.

"Tracks?" she asked.

"That's your department."

She studied the slope for a long moment. Then she pointed—not at anything visible, just at a stretch of shadowed snow between two rock ribs, maybe three hundred yards below and off our line.

"There. Something crossed our backtrail last night. Small. Light. It came up from the trees after we made camp and followed our line until the wind filled it in." Her voice was flat, professional, but I'd learned by then to hear the tension under it, the way you hear water under ice. "It wasn't an animal. Animals don't walk in a straight line for three hundred yards."

"Person," I said.

"Small person. Or a person who walks small." She stood. "They're still with us. Behind, not ahead. If they wanted to rob us they'd have done it while we slept."

That was the part I didn't like, and I could see Odile didn't either. A thief takes and goes. Something that follows you up a mountain, quietly, keeping your pace, staying just past the reach of your firelight—that's not theft. That's company.

"We'll set a watch tonight," I said.

"We should have set one last night."

"Yes," I said. "We should have."

* * *

The switchbacks proper began above the old landslide scar, seven hairpins cut into the mountainside by men with picks and a deadline. The grade was steep enough that the sled became a negotiation: I hauled from the front on the flats, Odile took the brake line on the descents, and on the worst pitches we roped together and moved the load in relays, twenty yards at a time, breathing like bellows.

On the fourth hairpin, the trail narrowed to a ledge with a drop on the outside that went down and down into a fold of gray rock. I've walked trestles with rotted stringers that felt more honest than that ledge. We crossed it single file, me first with the rope, testing each foothold, calling the good stones back to Odile like a man reading scripture.

And it was there, on the far side of the ledge, in a shallow scoop of rock that some earlier traveler had probably used as a windbreak, that we found the evidence.

A boot print. Human, small—a boy's size, or a small woman's. Pressed deep, the way a print is when someone has stood in one place for a long time, shifting their weight, waiting.

Beside it, half tucked under a stone: a strip of dried meat, wrapped in cloth.

Odile picked it up and turned it over. "Our meat," she said. "From the sled."

"He fed himself from us and left the wrapper," I said. "Tidy."

"No." She held the cloth up to the light. It was clean, folded with care. "He didn't leave it. He left it for us to find. This is a message. *I'm here. I'm not hiding well. Please.*"

"Or it's bait."

"Who baits a trap with one strip of jerky and a boot print?" She dropped it into her pocket. "Come on. He's close now. He knows we know."

We climbed another hour, and the whole time I felt it—the particular pressure of being watched, like weather building behind a ridge. Twice I stopped and scanned the slopes above us and saw nothing but rock and rime and the occasional insult of a bird. But the feeling stayed, patient as the mountain.

At the sixth hairpin, the trail bent around a buttress of granite, and there, sitting on a boulder like he'd been placed there by a stagehand, was the stowaway.

* * *

He couldn't have been more than fifteen. Thin in the way that comes from growing faster than you're fed, all wrists and collarbone under a coat two sizes too big and patched at the elbows with what looked like sacking. His boots were wrong for the mountain—city boots, smooth-soled, worn through at the heel. His face was smudged with dirt in the way of someone who'd tried to look less noticeable and achieved the opposite. Dark hair cut badly, recently, probably by himself, probably with the idea that a different face would be a different person.

He didn't run. I'll give him that. Most stowaways bolt the moment they're made, and then you spend half a day chasing them across country that punishes running. This one just sat there on his rock, hands visible, knees knocking—from cold, mostly, though fear does the same work—and watched us come around the bend.

"Evening," he said. His voice cracked on it, and he pushed through anyway. "Was wondering when you'd spot me."

"Were you," I said.

"Day and a half." He nodded back down the trail, toward the ledge. "Since the bridge over Coldwater Creek, back in the valley. You crossed it and I got on the sled when you stopped to argue about the rope."

"We weren't arguing."

"You were. About knots." He looked between us with the frank appraisal of someone who has spent his life reading adults for a living, because misreading them costs money or blood. "You wanted a bowline. She wanted a rolling hitch. You compromised on neither and used both."

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Tam."

"Just Tam?"

"Just Tam." And the way he said it told me everything the name didn't: there was a longer name behind it, and a place attached to the longer name, and he had put both of them behind him with the same care he'd used folding that cloth around the jerky. I knew the gesture. I'd invented it.

"How did you find us?" I asked.

"I didn't. I found the sled." He shrugged. "You buy supplies in a town like Grayfall, everybody watches. Flour, rope, survey paper, cold-weather oil. Everybody in that town can read a shopping list like a newspaper. You're going up the Kestrel, and you're going soon, and you're not hiring porters, which means you're either fools or you don't want to be seen. Fools don't buy survey paper." He spread his hands. "So I waited, and I got on."

"Why?"

He looked at me then, straight on, and I saw the calculation happen behind his eyes—the same calculation any runaway makes when an adult asks a direct question: how much truth can I afford?

"Same reason anyone gets on a train going away," he said finally.

I looked at Odile. Odile looked at the sky, which was doing the thing mountain skies do in late afternoon—going from blue to the color of a blade.

Turning back wasn't a question. The nearest settlement was two days down, and the snows were early this year—we'd already seen the signs, the frost line creeping lower each morning, the birds leaving in a hurry. Detouring to deliver a stray cost us four days minimum, and four days in October in the Kestrels was the difference between a difficult crossing and a fatal one. The survey team hadn't come back. Whatever had happened to them was sitting somewhere above us getting colder, and every day we spent not finding them was a day the evidence spent dying.

But taking him meant taking him. Up onto the summit approaches, into whatever the syndicate's riders were sweeping the slopes for, with a boy in city boots and a coat built for a bigger man.

"Can you shoot?" I asked.

"No."

"Walk all day?"

"Yes."

"Cook?"

"I can make anything edible taste worse," he said, "but I won't poison anyone on purpose."

Odile snorted. Traitor.

"Rules," I said, and held up fingers. "One: you keep up. If you fall behind, we don't stop. Two: you do what Odile says on the mountain. She reads it better than I do, and I built things on mountains for a living. Three—" I paused, because rule three was the one I cared about, and I hated that I cared about it. "Three: if we tell you to run, you run, and you don't come back for us. No arguments, no heroics. Nod if you understand."

He nodded. Then, because he was fifteen and constitutionally incapable of letting a silence stand: "What happens at the top? What are you actually looking for?"

"A missing survey team," I said. Which was true, and also the smallest true thing I could fit in the space.

"The government team? The one from the papers?" His eyes lit with the particular hunger of the young for other people's danger. "The papers say they vanished. All six. There's nothing. No bodies, no tracks, no—"

"We know," Odile said quietly.

Something passed over Tam's face then—quick, quickly buried. Not curiosity. Recognition. The look of someone hearing about a disappearance and understanding it from the inside.

"You ran from something too," he said. To me, not Odile.

"I run from weather," I said. "Speaking of which—it's coming. We move."

* * *

We made the high camp at dusk, in a hollow below the seventh hairpin, sheltered on three sides by rock. While Odile rigged the tent fly, I unpacked the sled properly for the first time since Grayfall, taking inventory, and Tam hovered at the edge of the firelight like a cat deciding whether the house was safe.

"Eat," I said, and handed him the pan.

He ate like a boy who had learned that meals are not guaranteed—fast, careful, nothing wasted, licking the pan when he thought we weren't watching. We were watching. Odile caught my eye over the fire, and something wordless moved between us, the thing that moves between people who have both been hungry in places where nobody was watching either.

Afterward, he washed the pan without being asked, using melted snow, and set it to dry on a flat stone, and then he sat down across the fire from me and pulled his bad boots in against his chest and looked at the flames.

"They'll come looking for me," he said. "Eventually. Whoever it is. I want you to know that now, so it's not a surprise later."

"Who?"

"I'm not saying yet." His jaw set, and beneath the dirt and the too-big coat I saw for the first time the shape of the adult he'd be: stubborn, watchful, harder than his frame suggested. "Not until I know whether you'd hand me back."

"Would it matter what I said?"

"No," he admitted. "But I'd like to hear it anyway."

I fed a stick to the fire and thought about the map in my case, the forty-one closed lines, the eleventh year of never sleeping twice in the same bed, the whole architecture of a life built so that no one could ever find me—and how a boy on a sled had found me inside two days, because wanting not to be found is its own kind of signature, legible to anyone else who's running.

"If someone comes asking after you," I said, "they'll find a survey party that doesn't discuss its business. That's all I can promise. It's not nothing. On this mountain, right now, it might be everything."

Tam studied me across the fire for a long moment. Whatever he was looking for, he seemed to find enough of it, because he nodded once and lay back against his pack and was asleep within minutes, the sleep of the young and the exhausted, total and unguarded.

Odile banked the fire low. We took the watch in turns, as agreed, and it was during my turn—somewhere past midnight, the stars hard and close, the boy breathing slow under his coat—that I heard it.

Not from below. From above.

A sound carried thin on the night air, up the switchbacks, from the direction of the summit: a whistle. Three notes, repeated. Not a bird. Birds don't repeat themselves in threes, and birds don't sound like men answering other men across a dark mountainside.

I woke Odile without a word. She listened for thirty seconds, and I watched her face do the thing it had done at the abandoned camp, at the torn notebook pages—the tightening around the eyes, the professional dread of a woman reading bad news in a language only she speaks.

"Grid pattern," she murmured. "They're calling positions. They're above us."

"How far?"

"Close enough that they think they own the night." She looked at the sleeping boy, then at me. "Congratulations, Vane. Your responsibility just got heavier."

Above us, faint and patient, the whistle sounded again—three notes, hanging in the cold like a question nobody wanted answered.
