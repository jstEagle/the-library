## 6. What the Torn Pages Said

Tam had stolen a lot of things in fourteen years, but never a secret.

They sat cross-legged on the supply sled as it groaned up the foothill road, pretending to sleep under the oilskin, and watched Isandro Vane's hands. That was the trick nobody told you about adults: they thought you were asleep, or stupid, or both, and their hands gave away everything. Isandro's hands kept going to the map case. Not the map — Tam had already seen the map, all those closed lines drawn in careful ink like veins cut out of a body. The case. There was something else in it now, flat and folded, that hadn't been there two days ago, and Isandro touched the case the way you'd touch a bruise to check if it still hurt.

Odile Ferran drove the sled and didn't watch anything, which meant she saw everything. Tam had learned that too, fast, because staying alive around Odile required learning it.

The page itself Tam had found by accident, or what passed for accident in Tam's life, which was mostly theft with better timing. Two nights back, at the cold camp below the switchbacks, Isandro had gone down to check the sled runners and left the map case open on a stone. Tam hadn't even meant to take anything. But there it was — a single sheet of survey paper, torn along one edge so the stubs of its missing brothers showed like teeth, covered in a hand so cramped and furious it looked less like writing than like someone scratching their way out of a box.

Tam took it. Then spent a day being terrified about it.

Now, with the light going copper over the Kestrel Range and Isandro walking ahead to test the grade, Tam finally unfolded it again inside the oilskin, where the wind couldn't get at it, and read it properly for the first time.

* * *

The top half was numbers. Tam knew numbers; numbers were honest, which was more than could be said for most things. A surveyor's coordinates, run in columns: elevation readings climbing in steady increments, bearing corrections, a line of triangulation points marching upward across the page like footprints. Whoever wrote this had been measuring something very tall and very specific, and near the bottom of the column the elevations stopped climbing and started repeating — the same figure, over and over, five times, each reading taken from a different station point.

Which meant, Tam worked out slowly, chewing the end of a glove string, that whoever wrote it had been circling something. Walking around it. Measuring it from every side because one side wasn't enough.

The center of the circle was easy to find. The writer had marked it himself, a small dense X with the bearings converging on it, and beside the X, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly gone through:

**SUMMIT WORKING — KM 41.7 — SEALED 1891**

A tunnel. Up near the top of the range, above where any sane line would go, there was a sealed working. And the government survey team — the ones whose tents stood empty in the snow with their instruments still calibrated, the ones Isandro and Odile were being paid to find — had been walking circles around it.

Tam looked up the slope. Isandro was crouched by an outcrop, tapping rock with a little hammer, doing engineer things, looking exactly like a man who had not noticed his map case had been lighter for two days. Below, Odile's silhouette swayed with the sled's motion, patient as a fence post.

Tam went back to the page. The margins were the strange part.

Down the left edge, in the same cramped hand but written looser, faster, almost carelessly — the way you write a thing you've written so many times it doesn't need attention anymore — ran a single word, repeated down the whole length of the paper:

*Vane. Vane. Vane. Vane.*

Seven times. Once beside each triangulation point, once beside the X, once at the very bottom, underlined twice, next to a final line that wasn't numbers at all:

*"Deed held in name of S. Vane & heirs. Verify before descent."*

Tam read it three times. The wind moved the oilskin. Somewhere upslope a rock came loose and clattered down through the scree, and Tam flinched hard enough to fold the page against a boot buckle.

Vane.

Tam looked at the man on the rocks above. Isandro Vane. Who had said maybe forty words since the stowaway was caught, most of them *stay low* and *don't touch that* and *we move at dusk*. Who carried a map of dead railways like other men carried photographs of family. Who had hired on — no, been hired, Tam corrected, remembering the telegram, the money that didn't ask questions — to find a vanished survey team that had apparently spent its last days measuring a tunnel with the word *Vane* written all around it.

It didn't add up to anything yet. It added up to too many things, which was worse.

There was only one way Tam knew to make too-many-things into fewer-things, and that was to hand them to somebody bigger and watch what happened.

* * *

Camp that night was a hollow behind a granite shoulder, out of the wind, with a fire small enough to hide in a coffee tin. Odile cooked. Isandro checked the sled runners again, though nothing had changed since noon, and then sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Adults and their hands. Always giving themselves away.

Tam waited until the food was eaten and the fire was down to a red eye, until Isandro reached for the map case to put it under his head as a pillow, the way he did every night, ritual as prayer.

"Before you do that," Tam said. "You're missing a page."

The knife stopped moving. Odile, rolling her blanket across the fire, stopped moving too. In the mountains Tam had learned that silence had different weights, and this one weighed a lot.

"You went through my kit," Isandro said. Flat. Not angry yet. Counting.

"I went through your kit," Tam admitted, "the night you left it open. And I've got something of yours back, so we'll call it even." They pulled the folded page from inside their coat and held it out across the fire, and their hand was steadier than they'd expected, which felt like a small victory worth banking.

Isandro didn't take it. So Tam set it down on the flat stone between them, unfolded it, and turned it to face the firelight.

For a long moment nobody did anything. The fire popped. Wind worried the edge of the granite overhead with a sound like slow breathing.

Then Isandro leaned forward, and the light caught his face, and Tam watched the color leave it the way water leaves sand — not all at once, but steadily, from the edges in, until what was left was gray and set and much older than it had been ten seconds ago.

His eyes went down the margin. Tam watched them stop at each *Vane*, seven stops, like a man walking past seven open graves. When they reached the bottom line — *deed held in name of S. Vane & heirs* — his jaw did something small and terrible, a muscle jumping once and going still.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out level, which must have cost him, "did this come from."

"It was in your case."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Tam agreed. "But it's the answer I've got."

Isandro picked up the page. Held it close to the fire. Turned it over — blank — and back. His thumb moved along the torn edge, touching the stubs, and Tam saw him match it, silently, against the notebook they'd pulled from the abandoned camp, the one with its last pages ripped out. The tear fit. Of course it fit. Someone had slipped this page into Isandro's map case before they ever left town, and Isandro had carried it for days without knowing, and now here it was, burning its way through him from the inside.

"Someone put this in my kit," Isandro said quietly. "In Grayfall. Before we started."

"Or after," Odile said.

Both of them looked at her. She'd settled onto her blanket with her boots off and her rifle across her knees, and she spoke the way she did everything, without hurry, as if the mountain had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

"Think about it," she said. "The survey team vanishes off a mountain with no tracks. Their notebook turns up with pages torn out. The torn pages turn up in the kit of the man hired to find them. Either somebody wants Vane to follow this trail—" she nodded at the page, at the X, at the name running down the margin like a spine, "—or somebody wants everyone who follows it to end up where the survey team did. Could be the same somebody. Both jobs pay the same."

"The money didn't ask questions," Tam said.

"No," Odile said. "That's what I'm saying."

Isandro wasn't listening anymore. Or he was listening somewhere underneath, in a place the rest of them couldn't get to. He'd read the page four times now, Tam counted, and each time his eyes went slower over the coordinates and faster over the name, until on the fifth pass he skipped the numbers entirely and just read the margins, lips moving slightly, like a man checking a list of debts.

"S. Vane," Tam said. Carefully. Testing ice. "That a relative?"

Nothing. The fire cracked. Above the granite shoulder the first stars were coming out, hard and white, the kind of stars that made the world below feel temporary.

"Sebastian," Isandro said at last.

Tam waited. Odile waited. Even the wind seemed to wait.

"My great-grandfather built a line into these mountains," Isandro said. He said it the way a man pulls a splinter — quickly, hating it, wanting it done. "Summit line. Highest track in the country, for eleven years. Then it closed, and the Vanes lost everything, and the books say it was weather and bad management." He folded the page along its old creases, precisely, like closing a coffin. "The books are wrong."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've spent nine years mapping every closed line in this country," Isandro said, "and every one of them closes the same way. Survey comes in. Survey finds a reason. Line dies. Somebody gets rich in the quiet afterward." He looked up, and his eyes had come back from wherever they'd gone, and they were not soft. "I just never thought I'd be following one of my own."

He put the page away in the map case. Under the map this time. Tam noticed that — the thing buried deeper now, hidden harder, the way you'd hide something you'd decided to keep rather than something you'd decided to lose.

"We should talk about the fact that someone planted it on you," Tam said.

"We will."

"And that the people who erased a whole survey team might know exactly where we're—"

"I said we will." Not loud. Just final, the way a door is final. Isandro lay back, put the map case under his head, and closed his eyes, and that was the end of the conversation whether anyone liked it or not.

Tam lay awake a long while anyway, staring at the stars, turning the word over. *Vane.* Seven times on a dead man's page. An inheritance, if you wanted to call it that — though everything Tam had ever seen inherited had come wrapped in trouble, and this one came wrapped in a massacre and a syndicate and a lawyer who paid too well.

Across the ashes, Isandro's breathing stayed too controlled for sleep. Tam could tell the difference now. Had learned it in nine years of listening to adults pretend.

* * *

Near midnight, Tam woke to voices, low, and kept still and listened the way small people learn to.

"—planted, Odile. You said it yourself. Which means they know I'm coming. Which means either they want me to reach that tunnel—"

"Or they want you to want to reach it. There's a difference."

"Why me? Out of every engineer they could hire, why hire a Vane to hunt a Vane problem?"

Silence. Then Odile, slower than usual, choosing words like footing on scree: "Maybe because a Vane won't quit. Maybe because a Vane has a right to be there, and rights open doors. Or maybe—" and here she paused so long Tam nearly breathed for her, "—maybe because whatever's in that tunnel belongs to you, and they'd rather it belonged to a dead man's heir they can handle than to anyone who might fight for it."

Another silence, longer. When Isandro spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"My grandfather died owing money to men like Hale. My father died not talking about it. I've spent my whole life making sure the name ended with me — no property, no papers, nothing to inherit, nothing to kill for." A breath. "And now some dead surveyor writes *heirs* on a page and puts it in my pocket, and I can't decide if I've been given something or marked."

"Could be both," Odile said. "Usually is, up here."

Tam stared at the underside of the oilskin, heart going hard, and understood suddenly that the thing being hunted up this mountain wasn't a survey team at all. It had never been. The survey team was bait, or evidence, or an excuse — and the real cargo, the thing the syndicate would climb through winter to bury, was sleeping twenty feet away with a map case for a pillow and a name seven times written in a dead man's hand.

Tam had stolen a lot of things. It occurred to them, lying in the dark with the frost forming on the sled tarp, that they had just stolen the most dangerous one yet: a look at what Isandro Vane was actually carrying. Not the page. The page was paper.

The weight. The weight of a name he'd spent nine years trying to starve to death, waking up hungry on the other side of the range.

Toward dawn, Tam heard Isandro get up quietly, strike a match shielded in his palms, and unroll the map of closed lines across his knees. Heard the scratch of a pencil. When Tam risked one eye through the tarp gap, Isandro was drawing — a new line, dashed, climbing from the switchbacks toward the summit, ending at a small dense X.

Above the X, in letters small enough to hide but sharp enough to read, he wrote one word. Then he looked at it a long time, blew out the match, and rolled the map away.

Tam didn't need to see it. Tam already knew how to spell it.

Two days later, from the high shoulder of the pass, Odile stopped the sled, lifted her binoculars to the lower slopes, and went very still.

"Riders," she said. "Six of them. Moving in a grid."
