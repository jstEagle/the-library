## 7. Men Who Move Like Weather

Odile Ferran had learned to read country the way other people read letters, and what the lower slopes were telling her that morning made her put down her tin cup without drinking.

She was on the ridge above camp, checking their backtrail out of habit more than worry. The habit had kept her alive through two winters in the northern bush and one bad year in the delta country, and it had never once lied to her. It wasn't lying now. Below, in the gray light before full sun, the pine cover along the old haul road was wrong. Not wrong the way a broken branch is wrong, or a shifted stone. Wrong the way a sentence is wrong when a word has been swapped for one that means almost the same thing.

Birds, for one. The jays that had worked the slope all week, loud and territorial, had gone quiet in a band perhaps half a mile wide, moving. Not scattered—moving, all together, ahead of something walking up through the trees.

Smoke, for another. Not their smoke. Their fire was banked and cold, ash buried, per Isandro's rules. This was a thread of it rising off to the east where no one had any business being, thin and disciplined, the smoke of men who cooked on a schedule.

And third, the thing she almost missed: the light. Down in the timber, where the canopy should have thrown unbroken shadow, there were gaps. Bright, moving gaps. Sunlight catching on something that turned.

Glass. Binoculars, or a heliograph, or the polished brass of a survey instrument repurposed for looking at men instead of mountains.

Odile crouched, made herself small against a granite spine, and watched.

They came up the mountain the way weather comes, which is to say without hurry and without mercy. She counted nine riders where the trees thinned along the switchback of the old haul road, and she knew there were more she couldn't see, because nine was a number men rode in when they wanted to be seen. The ones who wanted not to be seen would be off their horses, walking the timber on either side. And they were. She could pick out the disturbances now that she knew the pattern: a boot print in a seep, a fern bent and springing back too slowly, the particular stillness of a deer that has stopped being afraid of deer and started being afraid of men.

They were sweeping. Not chasing—sweeping. They moved in a line abreast, spaced even as fence posts, each man working his lane of trees with the patience of a plow. When one found something—a camp ring, a tin, a ribbon of surveyor's tape—he stopped, marked it, and the line closed around it like a hand before opening again and continuing. They weren't looking for the three of them specifically. They were erasing. Every trace of the survey team, every sign that anyone had ever come up this mountain asking questions, was being found and made to have never existed.

She'd seen men work like that before, and it had never ended well for the things they were working over.

Odile slid back down the ridge, choosing her line so her shadow never crossed open snow. By the time she reached camp, Isandro was already awake—of course he was; the man slept like a horse, one ear working—and Tam was still a lump in the bedroll, all elbows and indignation at mornings.

"Company," Odile said.

Isandro didn't ask how many. He came to the ridge line at a crouch, took the glasses from his coat, and looked for a long time. She watched his face do very little, which was how she knew it was doing a great deal.

"Nine visible," he said finally. "Grid sweep. They'll reach the survey camp by midday and find it cold." He lowered the glasses. "They'll find our sign leaving it by afternoon."

"Sign going which way?"

"Up." He said it like a man reading a diagnosis. "There's only one way up from here that doesn't come back down past them."

Tam had surfaced by then, hair like a crow's nest, blinking. "Up is bad, isn't it. Up is where the mountain gets serious."

"Up is where the mountain gets serious," Isandro agreed, and began striking camp with the same unhurried economy he brought to everything, as though they were leaving because they'd chosen to and not because nine men with rifles were climbing toward them. Odile had worked with engineers before, on the delta survey, and she'd noticed it then too: the good ones treated panic as a load-bearing error. You didn't put panic in a structure. It failed at the worst moment and took everything down with it.

"We cut camp clean," Isandro said. "Fire pit broken up, ash scattered, latrine filled and naturalized. Tam, you're on the bedroll pine boughs—pull them, scatter them wide. Odile—"

"Backtrail."

"Backtrail. I want us to look like we were never here, and if that fails, like we went down, not up."

"Down's a lie they'll check."

"Then it's a lie that costs them two hours checking it."

They worked in silence broken only by the small sounds of erasure. Odile had always found something almost holy in it, this undoing of presence. A camp, done right, left less mark than a deer's night bed. She broke the fire pit stone by stone and rolled each one into different brush, kicked the ash into a stream seep, and cut a fresh pine bough to sweep the trampled needles back into a texture that read as untouched. Tam, she noticed, had stopped complaining and was working with a fierce concentration that was its own kind of fear, and she didn't blame the kid. Fear with a task was just fuel. Fear without one was a hole you fell into.

By the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge, they were moving, and the mountain had already changed its mind about them.

It happened in the space of an hour, the way weather turns. All week the range had been their ally—hard going, but going, and the high country had that clean indifference Odile trusted more than any man's kindness. Now the same slopes read hostile. The scree fields they'd have scrambled up without thought became noise: every stone they loosened would roll and ring, and sound carried up a cold mountain like a bell. The open parks of grass that had offered easy travel became exposure—nine riders with glasses could read a moving figure at three miles. The streams that had been water became record: every crossing left wet stones that would darken with frost in a pattern no one could mistake.

Isandro read it the same way, she could tell. He stopped at the edge of the first big scree field, looked up at the long gray fan of it, and didn't go. Instead he cut east, into the timber, taking the harder line, the slower one, trading speed for silence. He never explained his choices and Odile never asked. That was one of the reasons she'd signed on to this job past the point where the money stopped making sense. Some men you followed because they knew where they were going. Isandro you followed because he knew what he was refusing.

They climbed through the morning in the cold shade of the north-facing timber, and twice Odile ranged ahead and twice came back with her jaw tight.

First time: a marker. A strip of red surveyor's tape on a branch, fresh, not theirs, not the missing team's—newer, the ribbon still holding its factory shine. A route marker. Someone had been up this mountain recently enough to tape a line, and had taped it going up.

Second time, an hour later, worse: a cairn. Small, waist-high, built of stacked stones in the middle of nowhere, and Odile circled it twice before she understood what she was looking at. It wasn't a trail marker. It was a cache marker, the kind freight crews used, and the stones had been stacked by hands that expected to come back. Someone was provisioning this mountain. Someone had a plan for it that involved coming and going, loads and men, a schedule.

"Isandro," she said, when she got back to the others. "Somebody's been building on this mountain. Supply points. Marked routes."

He was quiet for a moment. "For the recovery crew, or for something else?"

"For something that needs a road up here eventually. That's freight thinking. That's not search-party thinking."

Isandro looked up the slope, at the white summit hidden behind its collar of cloud, and Odile watched him decide not to say whatever had come into his face. He'd been doing that since the notebook. Since the page with the coordinates and the word written in the margins that had gone through him like a bullet through water—no splash, all depth. *VANE.* She'd seen the name land. She'd also seen him seal it up behind that engineer's face, and she was a patient woman, but patience was not the same as blindness.

They ate cold rations at noon, no fire, sitting in a hollow of granite with the wind moving over the top of them. Tam chewed and glared downhill at nothing.

"They're still coming," the kid said. It wasn't a question. Tam had good ears and had been pretending not to use them.

"Still coming," Odile said.

"How many?"

"Nine we can see. Call it fifteen. Maybe twenty."

"And what happens when they catch us?"

Isandro answered, not unkindly, the way you'd answer a child who'd asked what happens when winter comes. "They erase us. Same as the survey team."

Tam looked at him. "You say it like that's just a thing that happens."

"It is a thing that happens," Isandro said. "To people who carry things other people want buried." And then he stood, because standing was how he ended conversations, and Odile thought: *one of these days I'm going to make him finish a sentence about himself, and it's going to cost him more than the mountain will.*

They moved all afternoon. The timber thickened, then broke, then thickened again, and the gradient steepened until they were climbing on roots and handholds, the sled abandoned and cached under a rock overhang with the gear they couldn't carry, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden so well that Odile herself would have walked past it. Isandro had built the cache like he built everything, with a paranoia that looked, from a distance, like craftsmanship.

The light was going gold and then copper and then gone when Odile, ranging the flank one last time, saw the thing that made the decision for them.

The riders had stopped sweeping.

She saw it in the way the line had changed. All day they'd moved abreast, patient, mechanical, plowmen at their work. Now, in the last flat light, the line had bent. It had bent uphill, toward the timber, toward the fresh route markers and the cairn and the general direction of three sets of boot prints that Odile had done her best to make into no prints at all. They'd found something. A thread. And men like these, once they had a thread, didn't sweep anymore. They pulled.

She got back to the others as full dark came down, and found them in the lee of a granite dome, Isandro studying his map by the blue hood of a shielded lamp, Tam half-asleep sitting up, the way the young can sleep, anywhere, any posture, like a collapse.

"They've got our line," Odile said. "By morning they'll have our camp. By tomorrow night they'll have the cache."

Isandro's pencil stopped.

"So we don't be here," he said. "We move tonight."

"Night climbing," Odile said. "On that grade. In country none of us have seen."

"I know."

"The kid's half dead already."

"I'm not," said Tam, from the ground, eyes still closed.

Isandro folded the map. "They move like weather, Odile. You said it yourself this morning, even if you said it to the mountain instead of me. You don't outrun weather. You just make sure you're somewhere else when it arrives." He looked up at the black mass of the ridge against the stars. "There's a saddle up there. On the old maps it's called the Widow's Stair. If we take it in the dark, we're above them by dawn, and everything they do after that, they do below us."

"And if we're wrong about the saddle?"

"Then we're wrong on a mountain," Isandro said, "instead of wrong in front of fifteen men."

It wasn't, Odile thought, much of an argument. It was, however, the only one on offer, and she'd been in enough tight country to know that the choice between two bad options was not a choice at all but a direction.

They broke camp without light. Isandro doused the lamp and led on memory and the map in his head, and Odile took the rear with Tam between them, one hand on the kid's pack strap to feel the stumble before it became a fall. The mountain at night was a different animal. The cold came down out of the stars like water, and the dark was total under the trees, and every sound was either the mountain settling or the mountain telling them something. Twice Odile heard, far below and behind, thin on the wind, the clink of metal on stone. Men moving in the dark too, now. Men who had decided not to wait for morning either.

The climb went on. It went on past the point where Tam's breathing went ragged, past the point where Isandro called halts of ninety seconds that were just long enough to keep the kid from quitting and short enough to keep the cold from settling in. Odile counted the halts the way she counted everything, and at the ninth halt Tam spoke for the first time in two hours, voice small in the dark.

"Is it true they killed the surveyors?"

Nobody answered. The wind filled in.

"Because my da always said," Tam went on, and the voice cracked and steadied, "that the mountains keep what men give them. So if they gave the mountain bodies, the mountain's got them still. And we're climbing toward them."

"Yes," Odile said, because Isandro didn't, and someone should. "Probably we are."

Tam was quiet a moment. "Good," the kid said finally. "Then they can tell us who did it."

And Odile, in the dark, in the cold, with fifteen men climbing up the mountain behind her and a dead team somewhere above, found herself laughing—low, silent, a shaking of the shoulders only. It was grief doing it, and nerve, and something that had no name yet but would, before this mountain was done with them.

"Move," she said. "Weather's coming."

They climbed.

Above them, unseen, the saddle waited—black against the slightly-less-black sky, a notch in the spine of the range like a door left ajar. And below, patient as frost, the line of men came on through the trees, following a thread of sign up the mountain, toward a cache they would find, a camp that was no longer there, and a trail that went up—up toward the Widow's Stair, up toward whatever the summit kept, and up toward the thing Isandro Vane had been running from for so long that it had finally, on this cold and moonless night, begun running back.
