## 8. The Bridge at Coldwater Gorge

The gorge announced itself an hour before we saw it, as a sound like a river arguing with itself somewhere below the tree line. By the time we came out of the pines onto the shoulder of the slope, the argument had become a roar, and there it was: Coldwater Gorge, a hundred and forty feet of gray air split down the middle by white water, and across it—because I had known it would be there, because my map said so—the trestle.

I had been looking forward to this crossing for two days, which is a strange thing to admit about a bridge that hadn't carried a train in thirty years and hadn't carried anything else in twenty-five. But the alternative was the long way around, down to the ford at the mouth of the gorge and back up the far side, a full day lost through country where riders were sweeping the slopes in a grid behind us. A day was the difference between reaching the summit line before the snows and reaching it after them. So: the trestle.

"Tell me that's not our road," Tam said.

"That's our road."

"It's leaning."

"It's leaning *slightly*," I said. "Leaning is information. Straight bridges are the ones you worry about, because then you don't know what they're hiding."

Odile had come up beside me and stood with her hands loose at her sides, reading the structure the way she read snow—not the whole of it at once but in pieces, in grammar. "You've crossed this one before?"

"No. I've crossed its sisters." I crouched at the edge of the abutment and got my first real look at the near end. "There were eleven bridges on the Kestrel spur when the Vanes built it. My father's ledger lists every one of them by span and timber count. I grew up knowing their names before I knew the names of towns."

Tam had gone quiet at that. They'd been quiet since the notebook page, since the word VANE repeated down the margin in a dead surveyor's hand, since I'd gone white over it and said nothing. I still wasn't ready to say anything. But I could say things about bridges, and bridges were safer than fathers, so I said them.

"The Kestrel spur was built fast and built well, which almost never happens. The crews knew the mountain was only going to give them one season, so they overbuilt everything. Good heart timbers, iron shoes on the bents, double-laced bracing." I pointed. "See how the towers step down into the gorge instead of standing on it? That's not decoration. That's a crew that expected the rock to move under them and planned for it."

"And now?" Odile asked.

"Now it's been thirty years of freeze and thaw, and nobody has walked it since the last line-keeper gave up his rounds." I took off my pack and set it down. "So we don't trust it. We test it. And we cross it the way water crosses ice—one at a time, spread out, no rhythm."

"No rhythm?"

"Bridges fail to rhythm," I said. "A marching column can bring down a sound bridge if they all step together. Everything I do on this crossing is going to be deliberate and slightly out of step, and I want both of you to do the same. If I stop, you stop. If I move, wait until I'm three ties past where I was. Understood?"

They understood. Tam looked like they wanted to ask whether I'd ever brought anyone across a rotten trestle before, and decided against it, which showed good judgment. The truth was I'd crossed worse alone, in the dark, carrying more weight than sense. That was the whole trouble with being good at something dangerous: after enough years, competence starts to feel like permission.

I left the packs with them except for tools and rope, went out onto the deck on my own, and began the work.

* * *

A trestle fails from the bottom up or the top down, and you learn to tell which kind you're walking on within ten steps. Bottom-up failure lives in the bents—the framed towers that carry the spans—and announces itself in lean, in settled footings, in mud and rot climbing the sills. Top-down failure lives in the stringers and the deck, and it announces itself in sound. You learn the sounds. A healthy tie under your boot goes *tok*, dull and confident. A rotten one goes *thock*, higher, hollower, a lie told quickly. A tie that has already given up doesn't make a sound at all; it just moves.

The first forty feet of the Coldwater trestle were honest. The deck was weathered silver but dense, the rails still bolted true, the walkway planks along the north side—put there for the line-keepers, men who had once walked this gorge twice a week in all weather—mostly sound. I tested each plank with my heel before committing my weight, worked my way out to the first tower, and sat down on the rail to look at what thirty years had done to the bent beneath me.

The answer was: less than I feared and more than I hoped. The iron shoes were rusted but seated. The cap beam showed checking—long surface cracks—but no rot. The posts above the mudline were the question mark, and the mudline was sixty feet down, wrapped in spray and shadow, and I couldn't see it and wasn't going to pretend I could. Engineering is mostly the discipline of knowing exactly what you don't know.

I came back and laid it out for them.

"Near half is good. Far half I can't certify from here, but the geometry looks right—the towers are plumb, the spans aren't sagging. The danger isn't the structure. It's the deck. There will be bad ties, and there may be gaps where ties have dropped. We go single file, my route, my pace. Packs on front, not back—if you go through, you fall forward onto the deck instead of backward into the gorge. Rope between us, paid out slack, tied off at the anchors. Questions?"

"One," Odile said. She nodded past me, down-gorge. "What happens if it fails while we're on it?"

I looked at the river. It didn't look like water anymore; it looked like speed. "Then we're in Coldwater Gorge," I said, "and the name stops being decorative. Which is why we're roped, and why we go now, while the light holds, and why nobody stops in the middle to admire the view."

Tam swallowed audibly. Odile just started coiling rope.

We rigged it properly, because rigging properly was the one luxury I refused to skip. Anchors on both abutments—two big boulders and a stump that had once held a signal post, all tested with my full weight plus Tam's. Line paid out through carabiners so each of us could move independently without unclipping. I went first, Odile second, Tam third, twenty feet apart, and I set the rule again at the edge: three ties. Nobody moves until the person ahead is three ties past where they were. Out of step, always out of step.

The middle of the gorge, from the deck, is a place that rearranges you. The roar comes up through your boots before it reaches your ears. Spray hangs in the air even on a dry day, fine as flour, and it has put a skin of black ice on the lower trusses that gleams when the light hits it. Halfway across, with the abutments receding behind us and the far side still a rumor ahead, Tam made a small sound—not a word, just a note of pure animal unhappiness—and Odile said, without turning her head, "Eyes on the deck. Count ties. Nothing else exists."

And Tam counted ties. I heard it, faintly, under the river: numbers in a teenager's voice, steady as a metronome, and I thought, absurdly, of my father's ledger, of columns of figures kept square against chaos, and I kept moving.

* * *

It was at the fourth tower—the last one before the far anchorage—that the bridge told me its secret.

I had been reading the deck tie by tie, heel-testing each one, and the pattern had been consistent: sound, sound, sound, one soft one, sound. Then, under my left boot, a tie went *thock*, and the one beside it went *nothing*, and my heel sank a quarter inch into a gap where a tie should have been, and I stopped.

Not because I was in danger. Because I recognized the gap.

Ties don't drop out of a deck on their own. They rot in place, or they burn, or they're stolen. This one had been *removed*—the hold-down spikes pulled, the tie lifted clean out, the neighboring one spiked back down to cover the work. Someone had taken a tie out of this bridge deliberately, and recently, because the cut ends of the spike holes were still bright.

I knelt and put my eye level with the deck. Down the line, in the long afternoon shadow, I could see it clearly now that I knew to look: a second gap, maybe thirty feet further on. Then a third. Spaced irregularly, spaced the way a man spaces holes when he wants them found only by someone walking slowly and looking—which is to say, spaced the way traps are spaced.

"Isandro." Odile's voice, low, from twenty feet back. She'd seen me stop and read my stillness correctly. "Talk to me."

"Somebody's been here," I said. "Somebody's been here with tools and time. There are missing ties ahead. Deliberate. It's not decay—it's a welcome mat."

Silence, except the river. Then Tam, from the rear: "The riders? Could they have—"

"Riders don't climb trestles," Odile said. "This is footwork."

Footwork. Someone on foot, with a spike puller and hours to spare, who wanted this crossing closed—or wanted it open-looking and broken. Either way, it meant the syndicate's reach extended further than the grid of riders on the lower slopes. It meant someone had walked this deck ahead of us, calmly, in daylight, and gone away satisfied.

I should have turned us back. I knew the arithmetic: unknown number of compromised ties, a hundred and forty feet of air, three people, one rope. The safe play was retreat, the long way around, the day lost, the snows closer.

But there was another arithmetic underneath it, the one I'd been raised on, the one that lived in the ledger in my head alongside my father's timber counts: a removed tie leaves a hole, and a hole in a deck is visible, and visible means avoidable. The saboteur had made the bridge more dangerous, yes. He had also made it *legible*. Every gap was a marker. Every bright spike hole was a sign saying *here, and here, and not here*. A rotten tie hides. A stolen one advertises.

"I can read it," I said aloud, to Odile, to myself. "He did my work for me. Every hole he cut tells me exactly where not to step."

"You're certain?"

"I'm certain about the ones I can see. I'm certain about the spacing logic. I'm not certain there isn't one I can't see." I weighed it, the way you weigh anything on a mountain: not against safety, but against the other dangers. "Daylight's leaving. The riders are behind us. I say we cross."

Odile considered me for a moment across forty feet of failing bridge, and whatever she saw in my face must have satisfied her, because she said, "Then cross," and nothing else, which from Odile was a benediction.

I went on. Slower now, slower than slow, my route no longer following the sound wood but mapping the holes, stepping wide of each bright-cut spike scar, testing every approach with my heel before trusting it, calling the hazards back down the line in a flat counting voice—"gap left, take the rail side, three ties"—so that Odile could pass the words back to Tam. The rope hummed faintly with each of our separate movements, out of step, always out of step.

At the second gap I found the detail that finished shredding my nerves: the tie beside the hole had been re-spiked *loose*. Not removed—loosened. One hard stomp and it would pivot, dump a boot sideways into space. Whoever did this hadn't just wanted the bridge broken. He'd wanted it broken *for someone specific*, someone who would cross carefully, who would find the obvious holes and step around them, straight onto the trap that wasn't obvious. Someone who knew bridges.

Someone who knew me, or knew what I was.

I didn't say any of that out loud. There was nothing to say that wouldn't cost Tam their nerve, and we were eighty feet above a river that ate sound. I filed it where I filed everything now, in the locked drawer next to the torn notebook page and the word VANE, and I kept moving.

* * *

The far anchorage came up out of the shadow like a promise kept. I gained the solid rock of the abutment, clipped off, and turned to talk the others in.

Odile crossed the way I'd taught her, precise, patient, and stepped onto stone beside me with the particular blankness she used when she'd been frightened and declined to mention it. Then it was Tam, alone on the span, twenty feet of open deck behind them and the wind picking up off the gorge, and I watched the teenager's shoulders climb toward their ears and hang there.

"Count ties," I called. "Nothing else exists."

"I'm counting," Tam called back, thin. "There's a hole coming up, you said, the one with the—"

"Rail side. Three ties past the bright marks. You're doing it right."

They were doing it right. Ten ties out. Five. Two. And then, as Tam's boot came down on the tie beside the loosened one—the one I'd flagged, the one they'd avoided perfectly—a sound came up from beneath the deck that I will hear for the rest of my life.

Not a crack. A groan. Deep, structural, the voice of the fourth tower itself, sixty feet of seasoned timber deciding something. The deck shivered under Tam's feet like a struck string.

"MOVE!" I roared, all discipline gone, and Tam moved—three running steps, four, five, arms windmilling, and I caught their pack strap and their collar together and hauled them onto the rock as the whole far end of the fourth span let go.

It didn't collapse. It *unzipped*. The loosened tie went first, pivoting into the gorge with a flick like a tongue, and then the deck ties peeled away span-length by span-length, and then the stringers themselves, the great long bones of the bridge, tore free of the tower caps and swung down and down and went into the river with a white explosion that we felt through the soles of our boots. The fourth tower held—plumb, faithful, overbuilt by dead men who never trusted this rock—but it held nothing. It stood in the falling spray with its arms open, holding air.

The dust drifted. The river closed over the wreckage and kept arguing with itself.

Tam was shaking against my arm. Odile stood at the lip of the abutment, looking down at the empty span where the bridge had been, and her face, when she turned it toward me, was the face of someone revising a map.

"That wasn't the sabotage," she said quietly. "That tower groaned before the deck went. Isandro. Something was already wrong with the fourth bent. Something deep. Your holes were real, but the bridge was dying anyway."

"Or," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, "someone pulled those ties knowing exactly which span was rotten underneath. Knowing that the last person across would break it behind them and call it age."

We stood there while the light failed, three people on the far side of a gorge that no longer had a bridge, looking back at the country we'd come from. Behind us, somewhere down the slopes, the grid of riders was still sweeping. Ahead of us, the trail climbed toward the summit line and the tunnel and the word VANE.

"Whoever cut those holes," Odile said at last, "wanted this crossing closed behind us. Or wanted us on it when it went." She paused. "Either way—they'll know we made it across. The question is whether they know it yet."

Far downstream, faint under the river's roar, I thought I heard it: a whistle. One long note, high and thin, from the direction of the lower slopes.

Not a bird. Birds don't signal in code.

Odile's head came up. Tam went still. And I reached for the map case at my chest, because whatever was coming for us next, I had the feeling the answers were going to be written in the same hand as everything else—torn pages, bright spike scars, and a family name I'd spent my whole life trying to outrun.
