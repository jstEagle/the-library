## 9. Snowline

The snow came at noon, which was wrong. Tam knew it was wrong because Odile said so, twice, in the flat voice she used for facts she didn't like.

"Too early," she said, standing in the lee of a granite shoulder with her face tipped up to the gray. "High country gets snow in the ninth month, sometimes the eighth. This is the seventh." She turned. "We're above the line already. Somebody forgot to tell the mountain."

Tam didn't answer. Tam was busy being cold in a way that was new to them—not the sharp cold of a night spent in a hayloft with a stolen coat, which had a shape and an end to it, but a slow gray cold that got into the joints and stayed. Three days above the treeline. Three days of Isandro setting a pace that felt like being dragged behind a train, and Odile reading the backtrail like a woman checking whether a door was locked, and Tam hauling a sled weight that had been meant for a mule.

They'd cut camp in the dark two nights ago, when Odile spotted the riders on the lower slopes—men moving in a grid, patient as weather, sweeping the mountain the way you'd sweep a floor before company came. Since then it had been climb, eat, climb, sleep in a scrape behind rock, climb. Nobody talked. Tam had filled the silence with arithmetic: how many days of food left (six), how many miles to the summit line (nobody would say), how many ways a person could freeze (Odile had described two, casually, as trail talk, and Tam had not slept well after either).

Now the snow came down fine and dry, like flour through a sieve, and the wind picked it up and threw it sideways, and the world went the color of dishwater.

"We need shelter," Isandro said.

"We need to *move*," Odile said. "Shelter means a fire, a fire means smoke, smoke means—"

"I know what it means." Isandro was already scanning the slope, that engineer's squint, reading the rock the way Odile read snow. "There. Old prospect adit, two hundred yards, maybe more. If the timber's not rotted we can get a fire in the mouth where the draft pulls the smoke uphill instead of out."

"And if the timber *is* rotted?"

"Then we freeze with better scenery."

* * *

The adit was real—black slot in the hillside, half-choked with scree, timber lintel gone gray and soft as old bread. Isandro went in first with the lantern, tested every beam with a knuckle, and declared the inner thirty feet sound. They dragged the sled in, got a fire going in a ring of stones Isandro built with the fussy precision of a man who had opinions about fire rings, and for the first time since the riders appeared, Tam stopped moving.

That was the mistake. Stopping let the cold in. Stopping let the fear in too, the fear that had been running alongside them for three days like a dog they wouldn't look at. Tam sat against the sled with their knees up and their hands in their armpits and watched Isandro and Odile talk in low voices by the fire—miles, ridgelines, the words *switchback* and *scree* and *before the passes close*—and felt something in their chest go tight and hot and young.

"Say it," Tam said.

Neither of them looked up.

"Say what?" Odile said.

"Whatever it is you two keep not saying. I've been counting. Every time I ask where we're going, you say *up*. Every time I ask why, you say *because*. Every time I ask who's chasing us, you look at each other like I'm a kid who'll start crying." Tam's voice cracked on the last word, which was the worst thing it could have done, and they pushed through it out of pure spite. "I'm not going to cry. I stole onto your sled because I was going somewhere and you were going the same direction, and I've pulled my weight, and I haven't asked you for anything except answers, and you won't even give me those. So. Say it. All of it. Or tomorrow morning I walk back down and take my chances with the men on the horses, because at least they'd tell me what they wanted."

The fire popped. Snow hissed somewhere at the adit mouth, sifting in through the gap and dying on warm stone.

Isandro looked at Odile. Odile looked at the fire. Then Odile said, "Your funeral," in the tone of a woman washing her hands, picked up her rifle, and moved to the adit entrance to watch the weather, which was either a kindness or an abandonment and was probably both.

Isandro sat down across the fire from Tam. Up close, in the lantern light, they looked older than Tam had been letting themselves see—lines around the eyes like cracks in old plaster, hands scarred in the particular way of people who work metal and rope and rock. For a long moment Isandro just fed the fire, one stick at a time, arranging them.

"You want the whole story," Isandro said at last.

"I want the truth. You can keep the story."

That got something out of Isandro—not quite a smile, more like a crack in the plaster. "Fair." A stick went in. "You know my name."

"Vane. Isandro Vane. It's on your kit."

"Do you know what a Vane is?"

"A weathervane thing. Points at the wind."

"Points at the wind and goes wherever the wind says." Isandro's mouth did the not-smile again. "My family built a railroad, Tam. Not a spur, not a logging tram. A *railroad*. From Grayfall Junction to the summit of this range—the Kestrel Summit Line. Highest standard-gauge track on the continent, when it was laid. My great-grandfather drove the first spike. My grandfather ran it. Ore cars going down, supplies going up, a hotel at the summit station with a brass band in summer. People came from the coast just to ride it. There are photographs. My mother kept them in a tin and I looked at them so many times I can still see them with my eyes shut."

Tam didn't say anything. The wind outside had changed pitch, lower, heavier, like something big turning over in its sleep.

"And then it closed," Tam said.

"Closed." Isandro said the word like it had a taste. "That's the word on the documents. *Closed for safety. Closure survey certified.* There was a survey, and the survey said the tunnel was failing, the grades were unsafe, the trestles rotten, and the board shut the line, and the Vanes—well. The Vanes had put everything into it. Land, money, the name itself; my grandfather signed bonds with the Vane name on them because a Vane's word was collateral, back then. When the line closed, the bonds came due and the collateral was worth nothing. The bank took the summit holdings. The syndicate that held the bank's paper took the rest. My father drank himself dead in six years. My mother lasted longer and got less for it. And I—" Isandro stopped. Fed the fire.

"And you ran."

"I *left*." Sharp, too sharp. Isandro heard it and made himself go on, quieter. "I left. I was seventeen, same as you near enough. I took an apprenticeship on a mainline crew under a different surname and I never used Vane again if I could help it. Twenty years. You've seen how I live. A room I don't unpack. A map of every dead line on the continent, because dead lines don't ask questions. I told myself I was a man who liked the work. I was a boy who couldn't stand the sound of his own name, and I never grew out of it."

"Why not?" Tam pulled their knees tighter. "It's just a name. It's not the one who closed the line."

"Isn't it?" Isandro looked up, and the lantern light made their eyes look like the adit mouth—dark, with something at the bottom. "A name is a debt, Tam. A name is what they come to collect. The Vanes signed for that railroad with the only thing they had, and when it was taken they were still signing, still liable, still *Vane*, and the name got them nothing but ruin. I did the arithmetic at seventeen. A name that costs more than it pays is a debt. You walk away from debts. That was the whole of my philosophy for twenty years, and it kept me alive, and it kept me alone, and I told myself that was the same thing as free."

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones. Outside, the snow had thickened; Tam could hear it now, a dry whisper against the rock, endless.

"And the survey team," Tam said. "The one we're looking for. What do they have to do with it?"

Isandro was quiet long enough that Odile glanced back from the entrance. Then: "The closure survey. The one that killed the line. I've spent twenty years collecting copies of closure surveys—that's half my map, every line on it died the same way, *closed for safety, certified, sealed*. And the Kestrel survey never sat right with me. I rode that line as a child. My grandfather walked me the whole tunnel once, tapping every timber with his stick, and he was not a man who'd have run trains through rot. So when the lawyer came offering money to find a missing survey team—a team hired, I now think, to go look at the same tunnel the syndicate sealed—"

"You said yes because you wanted to know."

"I said yes because it was winter money." Isandro's jaw worked. "And because I wanted to know. Both things are true. That's the part nobody tells you about being a coward—the reasons are always half true."

"Then say the other half."

"The other half." Isandro exhaled. "The other half is that the notebook page you found—the one with the coordinates, the one with VANE written in the margins—that page is in my family's hand. The handwriting. It's my grandfather's. I'd know it anywhere. He wrote those coordinates, Tam. Before the closure. Before everything. He wrote them and hid them, or tried to, and whatever's at the end of that tunnel at the top of this mountain, my family found it first, and it's the reason the line died, and it's the reason the survey team is missing, and it's the reason there are men on horses sweeping this slope like they're looking for a dropped coin." Isandro looked straight at Tam. "I'm not leading us up here to find a missing team. I'm leading us up here because I'm done running from my own name and I want to see the thing it bought."

The wind rose. Somewhere above the adit, snow slumped off a ledge and hit the slope with a sound like a door closing.

Tam sat with it. They were good at sitting with things—years of practice, sitting with a father's silence, a house gone cold, a road out of town. But this was different. This was a grown person handing them the truth like a hot iron, saying *hold this*, and Tam found that their hands didn't shake.

"So we're not going back down," Tam said.

"No."

"Even with the snow."

"*Especially* with the snow. Snow covers tracks. Snow slows horses. The men behind us are lowlanders in hired coats; this weather is ours now, not theirs." Isandro almost smiled, and this time it reached their eyes. "That's the first thing my grandfather ever taught me about mountains. Weather doesn't care whose side it's on. But it hurts the side that's chasing you more than the side that knows the ground."

Tam looked at the sled, at the food that had to last, at Odile's back silhouetted against the gray mouth of the adit, rifle across her knees, watching the storm like it was a herd she was counting.

"Then I'm in," Tam said. "All the way. Not stowaway, not extra mouth. Crew."

Isandro studied them for a long moment—the way you'd study a beam, Tam thought, testing for rot with a knuckle. "Crew," Isandro said finally. "Then crew eats first tomorrow. And crew carries the lantern on the switchbacks, because my night eyes are shot."

"Deal."

* * *

Odile came back from the entrance when the light started going, and sat, and Isandro told her—briefly, in engineer's shorthand—what they'd told Tam. Odile listened with her chin on her knees.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"You knew?"

"Day one. You flinch when anyone says the name. Nobody flinches at their own name unless the name has teeth." She fed a stick to the fire. "For what it's worth, Vane—I've tracked men for a living, twenty years. Most of them running. The running ones all have the same tell."

"What tell?"

"They walk like the ground's going to ask them a question." She looked up, and the firelight made her face all planes and shadows. "You stopped walking like that about an hour ago. Whatever you decided, decide to keep it."

Outside, the snow kept falling, thick and silent, filling in their backtrail inch by inch, erasing the three of them from the mountain the way the syndicate had erased the survey team. Tam lay down under the tarp with their boots on and watched the fire die to coals, and thought about a grandfather's handwriting on a torn page, and a brass band at a summit station, and a name with teeth.

They were almost asleep when Odile spoke from the dark, very quietly, in the voice she used for facts she didn't like.

"Fire's down. Storm's settling in for the night." A pause. "And there's a light on the ridge below us. Moving. Somebody's climbing in this."
