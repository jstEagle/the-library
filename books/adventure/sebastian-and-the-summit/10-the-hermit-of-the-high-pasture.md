## 10. The Hermit of the High Pasture

I smelled the smoke before I saw the hut.

Not the sour stink of a forest fire—this was coal smoke, banked and patient, the smell of someone who had kept a fire burning in the same stove for years. Odile Ferran had spent eleven winters reading sign for the survey service, and smoke was sign like anything else. It told you someone was home. It told you someone wanted to be found, or had stopped caring whether they were.

Either way, it meant shelter, and shelter meant we were going to knock.

"Smoke," I said, and Isandro Vane stopped mid-stride the way he always did—like a man listening to a rail he couldn't see. Tam, half-asleep on the sled runners, jerked upright and grabbed the rope before the load shifted.

"How far?"

"Over that shoulder of rock. Half a mile, maybe less." I nodded at the gray thread rising against the snow clouds. "Someone's kept a fire through the season. That's not a hunting camp. Hunters move."

Isandro looked at the thread of smoke for a long moment. He'd been quieter since the snow started—quieter even than his usual, which was a silence with a whole rail yard running underneath it. The name VANE in Tam's careful copied letters had done something to him that three days of riders on the lower slopes hadn't. Fear, I could read. Grief I was only starting to.

"It could be the syndicate," Tam said. "A forward camp."

"The syndicate doesn't bank fires," I said. "They burn and move. Trust me, I've read their camps." I didn't say the rest: *I've read what they leave in them.* "Whoever's up there has been living at eleven thousand feet through the shoulder of winter. That's either a madman or a dead man who hasn't been told."

"Could be both," Isandro said, and shouldered the sled rope, and that was that.

* * *

The hut sat in a fold of the high pasture like a stone someone had dropped and forgotten. It was a maintenance shelter—one of the line-keepers' stations from the old summit railway, built of cut granite and iron-strapped timber, roofed in corrugated steel gone the color of old pewter. It should have been a ruin. It wasn't. The windows were glazed and caulked. The snow around the door was shoveled into neat walls. A cable ran from the roof to a lightning mast driven into the rock, and someone had painted the mast in fresh black-and-white bands, the way you marked a hazard on a working line.

Above the door, bolted to the granite, hung a small iron plate. I wiped the rime off it with my thumb. Stamped letters, official once:

**KESTREL SUMMIT LINE — KEEPER'S STATION No. 4 — NO ENTRY WITHOUT WAYBILL.**

"This line's been closed thirty years," Isandro said quietly. "The rails were pulled up below the snowline. The whole right-of-way was struck from the register."

"Tell the roof that," I said.

I knocked the way you knock on a door in bear country—loud, and then standing well back. Nothing. I knocked again, and this time a voice came through the timber, low and rough as gravel under a handcar.

"If you're from the company, the waybill's void and so is the company."

"We're not from any company," I called back. "We're three travelers and a sled, and the weather's about to eat us."

A long silence. Then, from behind the door, the sound of a bar being drawn back—not one bar, I noticed, but two, and a chain besides. The door opened the width of a forearm, and an eye looked out at us. Old. Clear. The color of gunmetal.

It went from my face to Isandro's to Tam's, and stopped on Tam, and something in it changed.

"You're bringing children onto the Kestrel in the first snows," the eye said.

"She's fourteen and she stole onto our sled," I said. "We've been trying to give her back for a week. Nobody will take delivery."

The door opened.

* * *

He was seventy if he was a day, and built like the hut—low, dense, made to hold against weather. He wore a line-keeper's greatcoat, the old pattern, brass buttons gone dull, and he moved through his single room with the economy of a man who had arranged his whole life inside four walls and resented every inch of it that wasn't working rail. A bunk. A stove, black and humming. A workbench under a lamp, and on the workbench—this was the thing I noticed, the thing that made the back of my neck prickle—tools. Not hermit's tools. *Railway* tools. A gauge. A level. A track wrench hung on pegs by size like a surgeon's kit.

"You're a keeper," Isandro said. It wasn't a question. His voice had gone strange.

"I'm a keeper," the old man said. "Anselm. Anselm Roque. Sit down before you fall down, and get that child next to the stove." He looked at our sled load piled in the doorway and his mouth went thin. "And bring that in. All of it. If the mountain wants it, it can knock."

We crowded in. The room was warm the way only a long-kept room is warm, heat soaked into the stone itself. Anselm put a pot of barley and mutton on the stove without asking what we liked, which I decided I approved of. Tam sat as close to the fire as skin allowed, hands out, shaking, and the old man watched her the way you watch a gauge needle—checking her pressure, her temperature, deciding what she could bear.

Then his eye caught on Isandro's map case, half-open where Isandro had shrugged it off, and the corner of a sheet showing—the map of closed lines, Isandro's own hand, the fine ruled tracery of every dead right-of-way in three ranges.

Anselm went very still.

"Where did you get that?"

"I drew it," Isandro said. "Over years. Every line that was closed, abandoned, struck from the register. I'm an engineer. I keep—" He stopped. Started again. "I keep track."

"Show me."

Isandro hesitated. I understood why. That map was the closest thing he had to a self; I'd watched him sleep with it nearer than his rifle. But he drew it out and unrolled it on the workbench, weighting the corners with Anselm's gauge and a tin of tea, and the old man bent over it the way a priest bends over scripture.

His finger came down on the Kestrel Range. On a line I'd seen Isandro ink in with more care than any other—the summit line, the one that went up and up and then, at the top of the sheet, simply stopped, a tunnel symbol and no more.

"Who taught you to draw like this?" Anselm said.

"My father."

The old man's finger stayed where it was. He didn't look up. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into something I had to lean toward to hear.

"And your father's name."

The stove ticked. Tam had gone rigid. Outside, the first real gust of the front came off the ridge and leaned on the hut, and the cable on the lightning mast sang one low note.

"Vane," Isandro said.

Anselm Roque straightened up, and I watched seventy years rearrange themselves around something that had been waiting in him a very long time.

"I kept Station Four for nine years under the Vane company," he said. "I kept it for thirty-one years after everyone said there was no company left to keep it for. I have been waiting for a Vane to come up this mountain since before this child was born." He looked at Tam, then back. "And you come with syndicate riders on your heels and a survey team's ghost behind you. So you'd better sit down, engineer, because the barley can burn and the story can't."

* * *

He fed us while he talked, which I think was deliberate. It's harder to hate a story on a full stomach, and easier to believe one. Barley and mutton and dark bread that had been baked that morning—thirty-one years of solitude, and the man still baked bread on schedule. Routine is a kind of faith. I've never known what else to call it.

"I signed on in the last good year," Anselm said. "The Vanes ran the summit line the way it ought to be run. Ballast trimmed, rails gauged to the sixteenth of an inch, and every keeper's station stocked like a ship. Old Vane—the grandfather—used to ride the line twice a season in an inspection car with brass fittings, and he'd stop at every station and ask the keeper one question: *What does the mountain need that we haven't given it?* Not what does the line need. The mountain." He snorted. "I was twenty-three and I thought that was just rich man's poetry. It wasn't. It was engineering. He understood the whole weight of the rock above his tunnel, and he respected it."

"The tunnel," Isandro said. His voice was flat and careful, the voice of a man walking a rotten trestle.

"The summit tunnel. Nine hundred yards through the peak. It was never just a passage—the mountain's full of ore bodies, and the Vanes' surveyors found the mother lode running right through the tunnel's crown. Silver and something else, something the assays called by numbers instead of names. The Vanes weren't miners. They were railway men. They figured they'd lease the rights, take a royalty, keep the line running." Anselm's jaw worked. "They figured wrong about who else knew what was up here."

"The syndicate," I said.

"The consortium, they called it then. Same men, same money, new name every time the old one got blood on it." He got up, fetched a bottle of something amber and three tin cups, and poured with a hand that did not shake. "The Vanes wouldn't sell. That's the whole of it, engineer. Your family held the deed, the tunnel, and the rights, and they said no—and they were decent enough to say it in writing, politely, twice. The third time there was no letter. There was an audit. There was a safety inquiry that found failures that had never failed. There were the closure surveys—"

"I've seen the closure surveys," Isandro said. "The figures are wrong. I've known they were wrong since I was old enough to read them."

"Wrong how?"

"Gradients falsified. Load ratings cut by half. Snow-load figures from a different range entirely. Whoever wrote them wanted the line condemned, and didn't care that any first-year engineer could see the seams."

Anselm sat down slowly, and for the first time since the door opened he looked old, and tired, and like a man setting down something he'd carried up a very high mountain.

"Thirty years I've told myself they were bought men. Cowards. It was easier." He looked into his cup. "It wasn't cowardice. The chief surveyor who signed the closure report was a man named Aldous Renwick. Good man. Honest. I drank coffee with him at Station Two the week before he signed. He told me the figures were clean and the line was sound and he'd say so in writing if it hanged him." Anselm drank. "It hanged him. They said it was a fall on the scree. His body was found below the switchbacks with his instrument case still strapped to his back, and a man doesn't fall with his kit strapped on, he falls trying to save it. But there was no one to ask, because asking had already become dangerous."

Tam had stopped eating. "The survey team," she said. "The one we're looking for. The one that vanished."

Anselm looked at her a long moment. "Child, what exactly were you told you were looking for?"

"A missing government survey team," I said. "Retracing the closure. Hired by a lawyer named Hale."

The name landed on the old man like a dropped rail. He set his cup down with exaggerated care, the way you set things down when your hands want to do something else.

"Corvus Hale."

"You know him."

"I know the name. It was on the consortium's papers, thirty years ago—junior counsel, the clerk who never signed anything himself. He'd have been a young man. Now he hires children to find the survey he sent up here." Anselm shook his head slowly. "No. That's not right either. He wouldn't send a survey to *find* anything. He'd send one to *not* find it, and then lose it on purpose, and then send someone to make sure it stayed lost. And here you are, three of them, walking up the same mountain in the first snows."

The wind leaned on the hut again, harder. Somewhere above us, on the ridge, snow came off a cornice with a sound like a door slamming.

"And the Vanes," Isandro said. His voice had gone very quiet. I'd been watching him all evening and I'd seen him hold himself together by engineering—by treating the story as figures, as load ratings, as gradients that could be checked. Now the checking was over. "My family didn't just lose the line."

"No," Anselm said.

"They were ruined. I know that much. The bankruptcy, the sale of everything, the name struck from the register. My father never spoke of it. He just—" Isandro stopped. "He just taught me to draw closed lines, and we never stayed anywhere longer than a season, and he died in a town I can't find on any map."

"Ruined," Anselm said, "is what the papers said. Ruined is what was *meant* to be said. Listen to me, engineer, because I'm the last man alive who watched it happen from this side of the snowline. The Vanes didn't lose their fortune. Their fortune was *taken*, piece by piece, by men who needed the deed broken before they could take the tunnel. And when the family wouldn't break—when old Vane's daughter kept appealing the closure, kept filing, kept *writing*—" his voice caught, just once, and he mastered it the way you master a runaway handcar, "—the winter she was supposed to come up and sign the final abandonment papers, the party that came up instead was six men and a doctor's bag, and the party that came down was six men, and the daughter was buried in a valley cemetery with a stone that says *fever*."

The stove ticked. Tam was crying without any noise at all, the way children cry when they've learned it's safer, and I put my hand flat on her back and left it there.

"Murdered," Isandro said. "For mineral rights."

"For a hole in a mountain," Anselm said. "And here is the joke, engineer, the joke I have kept warm for thirty-one years like a banked fire: they killed for it, and they falsified the surveys, and they closed the line, and they got their deed—and they have never taken a single ton of ore out of it. Because the only practical access is the tunnel, and the tunnel's mouth was sealed with a charge the night after the closure, and the charge was set by someone who keyed it to the Vane company's own detonator codes. Their own lock. Their own key. The mountain has been sitting on their stolen fortune for thirty years, and every year the snows come, and every year they send someone up to try the seal, and every year the mountain says no." He smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "Until this year. Until a government survey goes up the mountain and doesn't come down. Until a lawyer named Hale hires a Vane to go find it."

I saw it land on Isandro like a coupling slamming home. He wasn't hired to *find* the survey team. He was hired to lead the syndicate's recovery crew to the tunnel—by blood, by name, by the one key that might still open what the Vanes had locked. He was the instrument. He'd been the instrument since the telegram.

"They didn't hire me despite the name," he said. "They hired me *for* it."

"They hired you to die on this mountain with the others," Anselm said, "after you'd opened the door. And I'd wager my stove the torn pages in your map case are the surveyor's insurance—the part of the record they couldn't afford to let reach the valley. Which means someone in that survey team was honest, and someone in Hale's office is not Hale, and you, engineer, are carrying the only copy of the truth that exists." He leaned forward. "So. The question isn't whether the syndicate murdered your family. It did. The question is what a Vane does about it, thirty-one years late, with the snow coming down and riders below the pasture who will find this hut by morning if they're half as good as the men who came before them."

Isandro looked at his map, unrolled on the workbench—every closed line he'd ever inked, every place he'd ever refused to stop. I watched him look at it the way a man looks at a wall he's been leaning on all his life and only now sees the crack running through it.

Then Tam, who had been silent so long I'd almost forgotten her, wiped her face and said, "You said you've been waiting thirty-one years. Waiting for *what*?"

Anselm Roque got up, went to the bunk, and dragged out a wooden chest that had been built to survive a derailment—iron-strapped, stenciled with the old Vane company mark. He set it on the workbench next to the map of closed lines.

"Ledgers," he said. "Nine years of keeper's logs. Traffic counts, tonnage, maintenance records, assay reports—every number the closure surveys lied about, recorded by the man who actually held the gauge." He put his hand flat on the lid. "The truth, weighed and measured. I kept it the way I kept the fire. I just didn't know anymore who to give it to."

He looked at Isandro, and outside the wind rose to a long howl, and down in the dark below the pasture—far off, but not far enough—I heard what I'd been listening for all evening and praying not to hear.

Horses. More than one. Coming up the switchback road.
