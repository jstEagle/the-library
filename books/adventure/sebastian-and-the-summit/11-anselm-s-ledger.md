## 11. Anselm's Ledger

The horses were still a mile below, maybe more, and the wind was doing what the wind does on a high pasture—tearing sound into pieces and throwing it in every direction at once. But I'd spent eleven years listening for that particular sound, the muffled iron knock of shod hooves on frozen road, and once you've learned a thing like that you don't unlearn it.

Anselm heard it too. He didn't panic. He set his palm flat on the ledger box one more time, the way a man touches a door he's about to close forever, and then he turned to the window and pulled back the oilcloth curtain with two fingers.

"Four riders," he said. "Maybe five. They'll have seen my chimney."

"Can they see the hut from the switchback?" Odile was already moving, sweeping our tracks of tea and bread off the table with her forearm, erasing us from the room the way she erased herself from snow.

"They can see the light." He glanced at the lamp burning yellow on its hook, and something almost like humor crossed his ruined face. "Forty years of habit. A keeper keeps his lamp lit. That's the whole religion of it. And now it's going to get me killed, or save you, or both."

"Then we put it out and go dark," Tam said. "We take the ledgers and we're gone over the back wall before they—"

"No." Anselm said it quietly, and the quietness of it stopped all three of us the way a hand on a bridle stops a horse. He looked at Tam first, then at me. "Boy, those men aren't riding up here to chat. They followed you or they followed rumor, and either way they know someone came to the pasture. If this hut is dark and empty when they arrive, they will read that the way your friend there reads snow—perfectly—and they will come after you across open ground with four horses against your two legs and a sled. You'll be run down before the tree line." He tapped the ledger box. "But if the hut is lit, and occupied, and *burning*—"

Nobody said anything. The wind filled the silence.

"Burning," Odile repeated.

"A man alone in a hut full of paper and lamp oil, refusing to open his door." Anselm shrugged, an old man's shrug, loose as a coat coming off. "They'll believe it. Men believe what's convenient. They'll stand out there in the cold and watch an old fool die rather than ride into a fire, because fire is the one thing their pay doesn't cover. And while they're standing there watching the smoke, you three will be going down the far side of the pasture through the rocks, where no horse wants to follow."

"And you?" I asked, though I already knew. Some questions you ask only so the answer has been said aloud, so it exists in the air between people and can't be taken back.

"I'm eighty-one years old," Anselm said. "I've been dead since 1889, officially. The railway closed my line and closed my life in the same signature. Everything since has been overtime." He smiled, and it was such a gentle smile that it made my chest hurt worse than any threat had. "Besides. Somebody has to hold the lamp."

* * *

He wouldn't be argued with, and there wasn't time to argue anyway. The hoofbeats were closer now—not fast, deliberately slow, the pace of men sweeping a mountain in a grid, checking each square before moving to the next. Methodical. Patient. The patience was the frightening part. Angry men make mistakes. These men were being paid not to.

Anselm worked faster than I'd have credited. He dragged the ledger box to the table and opened it, and for ten minutes the four of us bent over forty years of a murdered railway.

I've kept records my whole working life. I know what honest numbers look like—they have wear on them, corrections, the small honest mess of a real hand doing real work. Anselm's logs had all of it: daily tonnage figures in a firm engineer's script, axle counts, weather notes, gauge measurements taken with the pride of a man who owned the only true gauge on the mountain. And beside them, copied out fair, the official record—the closure surveys, published, stamped, filed in Grayfall Junction.

The two sets of numbers did not agree.

Not by a little. The closure survey claimed the summit line carried less than two hundred tons a year, a failing spur not worth its own maintenance. Anselm's logs showed ore shipments running eight hundred tons a month through the autumn of '88 alone. The survey claimed washouts had rendered the upper tunnel impassable; Anselm had signed maintenance certificates for that same tunnel dated *after* the supposed washout, including a full re-ballasting crew paid out of company funds. Page after page. Claim against fact. Lie against ink.

And on the last sheet, folded twice and brittle as a moth's wing, a copy of the mineral rights filing for the summit section—dated three weeks *before* the closure survey was commissioned, signed by a name I recognized because I'd spent half my life making sure nobody else would recognize mine.

Vane.

My family hadn't lost the line. The line had been stolen out from under them, and the theft had been dressed up as arithmetic.

"That's why they killed them," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, flat and far away. "Not the debt. Not the failure. The rights under the tunnel. The closure survey was the murder weapon and the alibi both."

"Now you see it," Anselm said. "Took me thirty years to see it, and I only ever saw the edges. You—" he pushed the box across the table to me, both hands, the way you hand over something heavier than paper, "—you carry the name. Let the name carry this."

Outside, the hoofbeats reached the bottom of the pasture track and stopped. Voices, low, carrying badly on the wind. Then one voice, louder, calling up toward the hut with the false friendliness of a man who has never once been friendly: *"Keeper! Light's burning! We're coming up!"*

Anselm closed the curtain.

* * *

There was a back way, of course. There always is, on a line-keeper's hut—a maintenance stair cut into the rock behind the woodshed, dropping through a cleft toward the scree fields below the pasture. Anselm walked us to it himself, unhurried, pausing at the shed to press things into our hands: a sack of hardtack, a coil of good rope, a storm lantern with a full reservoir. A keeper provisioning a work crew. Forty years of habit right down to the end.

"The cleft comes out above the black rocks," he told Odile, pointing with his chin because his hands were busy lashing the rope to Tam's pack frame. "Stay left of the waterfall ice—it looks solid, it isn't. You'll lose the light in twenty minutes, so move while you can see. By morning you should reach the old water tower at the second switchback. After that—" he looked at me, "—after that it's your map, engineer. I never could read your kind's handwriting."

Odile took his hands. She held them a moment, tracker to tracker, one professional honoring another, and whatever passed between them needed no words and got none.

Tam hugged him. Just wrapped both arms around the old man's middle and held on, and Anselm let him, patting his shoulder blade twice, awkward as a man patting a horse he's fond of.

Then it was me. I stood there with the ledger box strapped across my back like a coffin lid, and everything I knew how to say seemed worthless, so I said the true thing instead.

"My grandfather used to talk about the keeper on the summit section. Said he was the only man on the whole line who never lied to him about the weather." My throat tightened. "That was you."

"He owed me money," Anselm said, eyes bright. "Never paid. So you owe me double, Vane. Pay it by finishing."

Above us, boots crunched on the pasture frost. The voices had stopped pretending to be friendly.

"Go," Anselm said. "Count to two hundred before you look back. And don't look back longer than that."

* * *

We went down through the cleft in single file, Odile leading, Tam behind her, me last with the weight of forty years of truth grinding against my spine at every step. The rock walls closed overhead until there was nothing but a slot of violet evening sky, and then even that narrowed away, and we descended in the blue dark by feel and Odile's low voice—*"left foot here, ledge, trust it"*—while somewhere above us the world began to burn.

I counted to two hundred. I looked back.

From the mouth of the cleft, framed between the rocks, I could see the pasture glowing orange. Anselm must have started it early, before they even reached the door—lamp oil, paper, forty years of a life fed to the flames all at once, because a fire has to be big enough to be believed. The hut windows blazed. Smoke rolled up black against the last light, and silhouetted on the ridge, small and still, stood the riders, watching. Doing exactly what Anselm had promised they would do. Standing in the cold, watching an old fool's fire, because fire was the one thing their pay didn't cover.

And in the doorway of the burning hut, one figure, upright, unmoving. Holding a lamp.

Even at that distance, even through smoke and tears I refused to acknowledge, I could see that the lamp was lit. A keeper keeps his lamp lit. That was the whole religion of it.

Tam made a sound beside me, small and cracked, and started to turn back, and Odile caught his collar without looking, the way you catch a child near a road.

"Two hundred," she said softly. "You looked. Now we walk."

* * *

We walked. Down through the black rocks, left of the waterfall ice exactly as instructed—I tested it with an ice axe on the way past and the crust gave like biscuit over a well, confirming everything Anselm had ever known about anything. We moved through the night with the glow of the pasture fading behind us and the ledger box digging its corners into my shoulders, and I found I didn't mind the weight. It was the first weight I'd carried in eleven years that wasn't just myself.

Tam didn't speak for an hour. When he finally did, his voice came out of the dark ahead of me, thin with cold and something else.

"He knew they were coming. Last night, when he heard the wind change—he knew. He'd probably known for years it would end like this. And he stayed anyway. All those years, he *stayed*."

"Yes," I said.

"Why? Why would anyone stay somewhere that kills them?"

I thought about the map in my case, the closed lines drawn in my own hand, the ritual of never unpacking, the eleven years of towns entered after dark and left before dawn. I thought about a lamp burning in a doorway.

"Because leaving is easy," I said. "Anybody can leave. I'm the best leaver you'll ever meet. Staying—that's the hard thing. Staying costs. He paid it every day for forty years so that tonight, when it mattered, the truth would already be sitting there waiting for us, weighed and measured." I shifted the box higher on my back. "He bought this night a day at a time. We're spending his savings now. Don't waste them."

Tam was quiet again. But it was a different quiet, and when we stopped at dawn in the lee of the old water tower, I saw the boy looking east toward the hidden pasture with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself once, long ago, standing at a grave I'd been too young to understand.

Grief, turning itself over, becoming something with an edge.

Odile climbed the tower ladder and came back down with her face grim. "Smoke on the southern ridgeline," she said. "More than one fire. They're signaling along the range—our description, most likely, moving station to station." She looked at me, and in the gray dawn light her eyes were the coldest I'd ever seen them. "Anselm bought us the night. We have maybe half a day before the net closes again. The scree fields are the only route they won't ride."

"The scree," I said. "Through Running Dog Pass."

"If we don't break an ankle. Or a neck."

Behind us, high and faint, the mountains were passing our names from peak to peak in fire. Ahead, the scree waited, miles of it, loose stone stacked to the horizon like the ballast of some drowned and terrible railway.

I tightened the straps on Anselm's ledger and started walking.
