## 12. Running the Scree

Tam had learned one thing about mountains in the week since they'd left Anselm's burning hut behind them: the mountain did not care who was chasing you. It would kill you at its own pace, in its own order, and the men with rifles on the slopes below were just another weather it didn't bother to notice.

They came down out of the high pasture before dawn, Odile leading, Isandro behind her with the map case strapped tight across his chest like a bandolier, and Tam last, hauling the sled they'd abandoned two days ago because sleds were for snow and snow hadn't come yet — only cold, hard frost that made every rock slick as greased iron.

The scree field began where the grass gave up. It was a whole slope of broken stone, grey and loose, running from the ridge above them down into a gorge so deep the river at the bottom was only a sound, a long white hiss like something breathing. Half a mile of it, maybe more. No trail. No cover. Just rock on rock on rock, all of it waiting to move.

"Across and down," Odile said. She crouched and put her palm flat on the stones, the way she read everything — snow, rock, ash. "Then up the far side through the notch. It puts us behind the shoulder of Kestrel itself. They can't see us cross if we go now, in this light."

Tam looked at the light. It was the worst possible light, the thin grey half-dark when shadows lie about depth and every boulder looks like a hole.

"And if we don't go now?"

"Then we go later," Isandro said quietly, "and they watch us do it."

Tam could see them without wanting to. Far down the slope they'd descended in the night, small dark figures were moving through the treeline in a line as straight as a fence — the recovery crew, sweeping the mountain in a grid, exactly the way Odile had described men who moved like weather. Methodical. Patient. They weren't hurrying because they didn't need to hurry. They knew the mountain only had so many ways off it, and they were standing on most of them.

"Loose rock rules," Odile said, turning to Tam, and her voice had changed — gone flat and instructional, the voice of someone teaching rather than deciding. "Three points of contact always. Small steps. If the slope starts to run under you, don't fight it — ride it, feet wide, until it stops. And whatever happens—" she caught Tam's eyes and held them, "—whatever happens, you keep moving. Fear wants you to stop. Stopping is how the mountain takes you."

"Cheerful," Tam said, because their mouth did that when they were scared, filled up with jokes like water filling a boot.

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. "Stay between us."

Then they went.

* * *

The first hundred yards were almost easy. The scree near the top was fine and dry, shifting underfoot like walking on a steep beach, and Tam found a rhythm — step, slide, catch, step — that felt almost like dancing, almost like fun, if you refused to look down. So Tam refused to look down. They watched Odile's back instead, her grey coat moving ahead of them like a marker on a trail, and they counted steps because counting kept the fear in a box. Forty-one. Forty-two. The rocks clicked and whispered around their boots.

At fifty-three the mountain cleared its throat.

It started as a sound behind them, below them — a soft slithering rush — and then the whole slope seemed to exhale. A sheet of scree the size of a barn door let go somewhere upslope and came down past them in a slow-motion avalanche of stone, hissing, grinding, and Tam rode it the way Odile had said, feet wide, arms out, surfing a wave of rock that carried them twenty yards sideways and down before it sighed to a stop.

"Good!" Odile called back, not even turning. "That's exactly right!"

Tam's heart was going like a locomotive but some animal part of them was singing, because they'd done it, they'd ridden it, they hadn't fought it or frozen —

Except that was coming.

The slope steepened toward the middle of the crossing, and the stones got bigger, and the spaces between them got bigger, and suddenly it wasn't a beach anymore, it was a staircase built by something drunk, each step a slab tilted at a different angle, and the drop on the downhill side stopped being a slope and became a fall. Tam looked down once — just once, just to check — and saw the gorge breathing its white breath three hundred feet below, and their legs turned to water.

"Keep moving." Isandro's voice, low, close behind. "Don't stop here."

"I'm not stopping." But they were. Their boots had rooted themselves to the slab like the frost had welded them there. Their hands were clamped on a boulder the size of a table, knuckles white, and no signal their brain sent downward made any difference at all. The men below were a fence of dark dots. The ledge was six inches wide. The next step across was four feet away over a gap of nothing, and their body had simply resigned, handed in its notice, sat down inside itself and refused.

"Tam." Odile had come back. She crouched on the slab beside them, balanced like it was a park bench, and looked at them — not at the drop, not at the riders, at them. "Look at me. Not down. Me."

"I can't—"

"You can. You crossed worse than this the night we cut camp at the pasture, in the dark, carrying more than you're carrying now. Your legs already know how. It's just your head telling stories." Her voice stayed level, unhurried, like she had all morning and the syndicate's entire crew wasn't climbing toward them. "So give your head a smaller job. Don't think about the other side. Think about my hand."

She held it out. Steady. Not reaching, not grabbing — offering.

"The gap's four feet," Tam whispered.

"The gap is one step. That's all it has ever been. One step, then another. You've been doing one-then-another since you jumped onto our sled with nothing but a stolen coat and a bad plan." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "Worst plan I ever saw. Worked anyway."

Below them, faintly, a whistle sounded — two notes, repeated. The crew signaling to each other. Closer than before.

"They'll be on this slope in ten minutes," Isandro said from behind, and there was no blame in it, only fact. "Take the time you need. Then take the step."

Tam breathed. Once. Twice. The gorge hissed below. Odile's hand hung in the air between them, steady as a rail.

*You've been doing one-then-another.*

They thought about the sled. About the night they'd run, about how every choice since had been one-then-another, and how none of those steps had killed them yet. They thought — absurdly, in the middle of everything — about the notebook page with VANE written in the margins, and how Isandro had gone white, and how even people who looked unbreakable were just doing one-then-another too.

They took their hand off the boulder.

The world did not end. The slab held. Four feet of empty air went by underneath them like a held breath, and then their boot hit Odile's side of the gap, and her hand closed around theirs and hauled, and they were across, both boots down, alive, laughing — actually laughing, a cracked wild sound they couldn't stop.

"There she is," Odile said.

"There *they* are," Tam gasped, and kept moving.

* * *

They gained the notch an hour after sunrise, all three of them, and dropped behind the shoulder of the mountain into a windless fold of shadow where the crew below became irrelevant — still there, still sweeping, but blind now, reading a slope the trio had already erased themselves from. Odile swept their backtrail with her eyes and pronounced it clean. Isandro checked the map case, checked it again, then sat down heavily on a stone and, for the first time Tam had ever seen, simply breathed.

Tam sat down next to him. Close enough that their shoulders touched.

"You froze," Isandro said. Not cruel. Just true.

"Yeah."

"So did I. First time I ever crossed a trestle alone, I got halfway and my legs quit. Sat down on a tie with a thousand-foot drop under me and stayed there till dark." He turned the compass over in his hands — Odile's father's compass, which she'd pressed on him days ago with instructions to finish it. "Everybody freezes. The trick isn't never freezing. It's starting again after."

Tam looked out at the light coming up the far peaks, gold and enormous, and felt something settle in their chest that they didn't have a name for yet. Two weeks ago they'd been nobody's problem, stealing rides and sleeping in coal sheds. Now there were two people on a mountain who'd waited for them on a ledge, who'd come back for them, who'd said *take the time you need* when every minute cost them danger.

"You'd have left me," Tam said. "Back at the start. Be honest."

"No," Isandro said. "I'd have grumbled the whole way. But I wouldn't have left you."

Odile, scanning the ridgeline, snorted softly. "He complained for a full day after he decided to keep you. I timed it."

"I did not—"

"Nineteen hours," she said. "Give or take."

And Tam laughed again, quieter this time, and it felt like something being earned.

Then Odile went still. All of her went still at once, the way she did, hand rising flat and level, and Tam's laughter died in their throat.

Down the valley they'd have to follow tomorrow — the only route toward the summit tunnel that didn't climb bare ice — a thread of smoke stood up against the sky. Thin. Deliberate. A cook fire, pitched exactly where the trail narrowed between two walls of stone.

Somebody knew where they were going. Somebody was already there.

Odile lowered her hand slowly. "Well," she said. "They've stopped chasing us."

Isandro stood, and Tam watched the tiredness drain out of his face and get replaced by something older and harder, the engineer looking at a bridge he'd have to cross.

"Which means," he said, "they're waiting."
