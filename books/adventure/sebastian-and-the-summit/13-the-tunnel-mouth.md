## 13. The Tunnel Mouth

We came up on the tunnel mouth at the hour when the light goes thin and the mountain stops pretending to be kind.

I had known it was close for the last two miles without needing Odile to say so. You can feel a dead line under your boots if you've spent your life walking them—the grade changes its mind too politely, the ballast stones sit in rows instead of scatter, and every so often a creosote-black tie surfaces through the scree like a rib through skin. The Vanes' summit line had been buried for forty years, but rail doesn't die all the way. It waits.

"There." Tam pointed with the hand not gripping the rope.

The portal stood in the face of the ridge like a held breath. Cut stone, dressed and fitted by men who'd expected their work to be admired, arched over an opening that should have been black. It wasn't. It was gray—mortar-gray, rougher than the surrounding masonry—and it filled the arch from springline to crown.

Walled in.

I'd known it would be. The torn page said *sealed per order*, and Anselm's ledger said whose order, and I'd told myself on the climb that knowing was the same as being ready. Standing in front of it, I learned the difference. Somebody had done this carefully. Not a rockslide, not a collapse dressed up as one. A wall. Laid courses, staggered joints, weep-holes drilled at the base so water wouldn't blow it apart in freeze-thaw. Whoever built it had wanted it to last, and had wanted anyone who found it to know it was no accident of geology.

"Forty years," Odile said quietly, "and nobody's come back to open it."

"Nobody who wanted what's inside," I said.

"Or everybody who did already knows exactly where it is."

She was right, and we both let that sit. Somewhere below us, half a day back down the scree, the recovery crew was climbing. We'd bought ourselves a night and a morning with the collapse at Coldwater Gorge and the false trail Tam had laid across the glacier moraine—Tam, sixteen years old, laying tracks like a professional—but the purchase was spending itself fast. If we were going to breach this thing, we'd do it with the syndicate's men somewhere behind us, closing.

"Charges," I said, and shrugged off the pack.

* * *

Survey charges are small things. A quarter-pound of dynamite wrapped in wax paper, meant for seismic shots—thumping the ground and listening to what echoes back. I'd carried six since Grayfall Junction, telling myself they were for prospecting, which was true, and telling myself I didn't already know what I'd use them for, which was not.

The problem with breaching a walled tunnel is that you don't know what's leaning against the other side of the wall. Forty years is long enough for anything to have slumped, shifted, rotted into a prop. Blow the wall hard and you might bring the roof down with it. So I worked the way my father taught me to work, before I understood what his name cost him: drill shallow, charge light, shoot one hole first and read the rubble.

Tam held the drill steel and turned it while I struck, both of us breathing the thin cold air in ragged pulls. The bit bit into mortar that had gone soft as old bread. Behind us Odile sat on a boulder with her father's compass open on her knee, watching the switchbacks below, and every few minutes she'd say "nothing" or "wind" or once, softly, "still nothing," and each still-nothing felt less like relief than like held breath.

"Isandro." Tam had stopped drilling. "This mortar. It's got stuff in it."

I looked. In the crumbled powder around the hole, pale flecks. Bone meal, maybe—or lime, I told myself, just lime, they used lime in everything—

"Keep drilling," I said.

The first shot went off with a flat cough and a puff of gray smoke. When it cleared, the wall had shed a slab the size of a door, and behind the slab there was darkness, and out of the darkness came air.

That's the thing nobody tells you about sealed places. They breathe when you open them. Forty years of trapped air pushed past us, and it had a smell I knew from wrecked cars and burned depots and one flooded cutting outside Marrow Bend where they'd never recovered everyone. Old death has a sweetness to it. Underneath the stone dust and the frost, the tunnel exhaled that sweetness, and Tam made a small sound and stepped back, and Odile rose off her boulder like a drawn blade.

"Rubble's clean," I said, because saying the other thing out loud would make it real. "Roof's holding. Two more charges, low, and we can walk in."

"Isandro." Odile's voice was very even. "You smelled it."

"I smelled it."

"And you're going in anyway."

"The documents are in there. Whatever else is in there, the reason three people died on this mountain and Anselm burned forty years of his life—that's in there."

She looked at me for a long moment, wind pulling at her gray-streaked hair, and then she nodded once and went back to watching the slope, and I understood that she'd decided something about me in that moment, though I wouldn't learn until later whether it was forgiveness or simply arithmetic.

Two more charges. Low holes, light tamping, sandbags of snow packed over the stems. The second shot cracked the wall along its joints; the third opened it wide enough to walk through, and the mountain's held breath came out again, sweeter now, unmistakable, and none of us pretended anymore.

* * *

We waited for the dust to settle because going in blind is how engineers die, and then I lit the carbide lamp and stepped through the gap in the wall my family had been sealed behind.

The lamp threw its circle ten feet, maybe twelve, and in that circle lay track. Intact, spiked, lined—rails bright where the air was dry, ties sound as the day they were laid. Not abandoned track. Finished track. This line hadn't been closed because it failed. It had been closed because it succeeded, and success was the problem.

Beyond the lamplight the dark went on, and I could feel the size of it the way you feel the size of a cathedral—not seeing, but knowing from the echo. My voice came back to me off walls far away.

"Ore cars," Tam whispered, and swung their own lamp left.

Three of them, on a siding, coupled nose to tail, sitting square and level on rails that had never shifted. Their beds were empty. Their wheels were greased—I could see it, forty-year grease gone amber but uncracked. Someone had maintained these cars. Someone had expected to use them again.

And then the light moved, and I stopped thinking about ore cars for the rest of my life.

They were sitting against the far wall of the chamber, past the siding, where the main bore continued toward the summit. Eleven of them. Maybe twelve; the ones at the ends had slumped together and it was hard to be sure where one ended. They were arranged, and that was the detail that reached through the horror and took hold of something rational in me—they were arranged. Placed in a row, backs to the rock, some with arms folded, some with hands resting on their knees. Someone had straightened them after. Someone had come into this dark with a lamp and grief and however many hours, and laid the dead out decently, because there was no one else to do it and no way to carry them out.

Not fleeing men. Fleeing men die scattered, faces to whatever direction they were running, tangled in each other, reaching for doors. These men were seated. Waiting. Some of the skulls rested against the rock face behind them at the angle of a man leaning back to rest between shifts.

"They weren't trying to get out," Tam said. Their voice had gone strange and flat, the voice of someone reporting weather from very far away.

"No."

"But—" Tam's lamp swept the row, the row that went on past the light. "There's no way out except the wall. They sealed the wall from outside. So they were in here when—"

"When the crew that built the wall walked away," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Yes."

Odile had gone along the row slowly, lamp low, reading the bones the way she read snow. She crouched at the near end of the line, and I saw her tilt her head, and I knew before she spoke that whatever she'd found would make it worse.

"Tools," she said. "Look. Picks, bars, hammers—all stacked. Neatly. Over here." She lifted something small and turned it in the light. A tin cup. "And look at the floor between them and the wall. Scuffing. Lots of it, all in one band, right along the base."

"Weep-holes," I said. The word came out of me like a stone dropping. "I saw weep-holes in the wall. Drainage. Two-inch bores at the base, spaced along—"

"So they knew the wall had holes in it." Odile set the cup down with terrible gentleness. "Air comes through two-inch holes. Water comes through. Light doesn't. And a man can dig at a two-inch hole for a long time and widen it maybe enough to see sky, and then the men on the other side fill it, or he dies trying, or—" She stopped. She didn't need to finish. The scuffed band along the wall's inner base, worn into the ballast by eleven pairs of hands working at the only openings in their world, said the rest.

They had heard the masonry going up. That was the thought that finally broke over me and I couldn't hold it back down. They'd been in here working—shift sounds, lamp glow, the ring of steel on rail—and then the light at the tunnel mouth had dimmed and changed, and the sounds had become different sounds, and within a day they'd have understood. Some of them would have pounded on the wall. Screamed. The men outside would have laid course after course with the noise of fists on the other side of the stones, and kept laying, because they'd been paid to keep laying, and the pay was good, and the men inside were just Vanes' men, and the company had decided what Vanes' men were worth.

"How long?" Tam asked. Nobody answered. Nobody wanted to be the one who estimated it, and put a number of days on what those scuffs represented.

* * *

It was Odile who found the surveyor.

Past the last of the seated dead, where the main bore curved toward the summit drive, a side alcove held a camp that had been lived in rather than died in: a bedroll squared away, a crate for a table, a candle stub fused to a saucer by forty years of wax. Against the crate, propped upright so it would be the first thing anyone saw, leaned a leather satchel with a brass buckle, and on the satchel, scratched deep into the leather with something sharp, four words:

**FOR WHOEVER OPENS THIS**

The man himself was behind the crate, lying on his side on the bedroll, curled the way you curl when you're cold. He'd taken off his coat and folded it under his head like a pillow, and he'd arranged his instruments beside him—theodolite, chain, notebook—in a neat row, the way a surveyor lays out tools at the start of a day's work. He'd known someone like him would come someday. He'd wanted that someone to find everything in order.

His notebook was in the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as August. But it was what was under the notebook that made my hands shake as I lifted them out: a packet of papers, thick as a hymnal, tied with wire. Bills of lading. Assay certificates stamped with grades that made my eyes go hot—a vein in that mountain that would have bought and sold the syndicate twice over. Correspondence. And on top, a single sheet written in a careful engineer's hand, dated forty years ago to the week, beginning:

*To whoever finds this. My name is Elias Vane, assistant surveyor, and I am the last of eleven men in this tunnel, and I have perhaps two days of light left in my candles, so I will write quickly and write everything.*

Vane.

I sat down on the tunnel floor, in the dark among the dead, holding my own name in both hands.

"Isandro." Tam's voice, small. "Your face. What is it?"

"That was my great-uncle," I said. "Elias Vane. My grandfather talked about him once. Said he went west on a contract and never wrote again, and the family stopped saying his name because it hurt." I looked down the row of seated dead, at the scuffed band along the wall, at the folded coat under a dying man's head. "He didn't vanish. He was *walled in*. All of them. And the family name on the deed to this mountain isn't an inheritance. It's a grave marker."

Outside, far below us, faint as a heartbeat, Odile—who had drifted back to the breach with her compass—said one word in a voice I'd never heard her use:

*"Riders."*

I looked at the packet in my hands. I looked at the wall of the tunnel, at the gap we'd blasted, at the gray daylight coming through it and the shapes moving against that daylight on the switchbacks below, coming up, methodical, unhurried, certain.

They hadn't come to seal a tunnel this time. They'd come to make sure it stayed sealed—with everything inside it, including us.

"Get away from the opening," I said, and began stuffing the papers inside my coat.
