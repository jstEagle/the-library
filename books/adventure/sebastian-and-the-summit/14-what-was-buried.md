## 14. What Was Buried

The dark past the third bend in the tunnel did not thin. It thickened.

Odile had tracked things her whole life—elk through alder, men through snow, once a murderer through three days of rain-slicked basalt—and she knew the difference between a darkness that was empty and a darkness that held something. The first kind breathed. It moved with the wind at the tunnel mouth, shifted when a lamp swung. The second kind sat still and waited to be read.

This one held something.

"Slow," she said, and Isandro's lamp came down to knee height without argument. Behind them Tam's breathing had gone shallow and quick, the sound of a person trying to be quiet about being afraid. Odile didn't tell them it would pass. She'd never learned how to lie that gently.

The track ran straight ahead into the mountain, rails bright where their boots hadn't touched them, rusted everywhere else. Ore cars stood on a siding to the left, four of them, coupled, loaded—or so it looked until Isandro lifted his lamp over the first one and the light fell into it and Odile saw what the load actually was.

Rock. Just rock. Ordinary gray rock, hauled up from nowhere, worth nothing.

"They were filling cars," Isandro said quietly. "Not emptying them."

"Or someone wanted it to look like work went on here." Odile crouched at the rail joint and put two fingers to the steel. Cold, but not grave-cold. Dry air moved faintly along the tunnel, a breath from deeper in, and where it moved there was no dust on the rails. "Something's drawing air. There's an opening ahead—a shaft, or a second adit. Air doesn't lie about distance the way light does. We're not near the end of this place."

Tam's voice, small: "How far does it go?"

"As far as it needs to," Odile said, and stood, and led.

* * *

They found the first bones forty minutes in, and Odile knew before the lamp found them, because the air changed again—the faint mineral breath of the tunnel picking up something else underneath, something organic and old and sweetish, the smell she had followed to dead camps in two ranges and never gotten used to and never would.

A man sat against the tunnel wall with his legs stretched out along the rail bed, as if he'd stopped for a rest and the rest had outlasted him. His coat had gone the color of wet cardboard. His boots were good boots, government-issue surveyor's boots, and Odile felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world, because she had knelt beside those same boots eleven days ago in the abandoned camp below the snowline, laced neatly beside a tent, paired with socks rolled inside them.

Six pairs of boots at the camp. Six men who had walked out of their own gear without leaving tracks.

Except they hadn't walked anywhere. They had been brought here.

"No," Tam said behind her. Not a question. A refusal. "No, that's—that's not—" and then Isandro's hand closed on the kid's shoulder and the sentence died, and Odile was grateful for it because she didn't have anything to offer instead.

She made herself look at the man properly. That was the discipline her father had taught her before anything else: look at the dead the way you'd look at the living, because they're still telling you something, and it's usually the truest thing anyone's told you all day.

He hadn't starved. The hands were too fleshed, even dried. He hadn't frozen—the tunnel held a steady chill but nothing like the killing cold outside. She leaned close, careful, and saw the dark stain down the left side of his ribs, gone black and hard. A wound. Old enough to have healed wrong if he'd lived, except he hadn't lived long.

"He was shot," she said. "Down here. Then he bled, and then he stayed."

Isandro's lamp swept the wall beside the seated man, and the light caught scratches in the rock—not tool marks, not drill marks. Letters. Cut deep with something sharp, in rows, the way a man writes when he has time and no paper and knows his time is running out anyway.

Odile read them aloud because someone had to, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved:

*"If you are reading this I was Aldous Merrick, assistant surveyor, Kestrel Range party. They sealed us in on the 14th of October. Hale's men. If you carry nothing else out of this mountain, carry the names."*

Nobody spoke. Somewhere far back toward the tunnel mouth, thunder muttered—the storm arriving, right on schedule, the way weather had been arriving around them for two weeks like something with intent.

"The notebook," Isandro said. "The torn pages. This is who wrote them."

"This is who wrote them," Odile agreed. And then, because the tracker in her wouldn't stay quiet: "And he wrote more than we found. Look at the wall. It goes on past the lamp edge."

It did. The letters marched down the tunnel wall in the lamplight, row after row, and beside them, wedged into a crack in the rock at the seated man's shoulder, was a tin document case, government gray, dented, shut.

* * *

They built a small camp right there, thirty feet from the dead man, because there was no choice in it. Outside, the storm had arrived in earnest—they could hear it now even this deep, a low pressure against the mountain like a hand laid flat on a drum—and the thought of going back up the tunnel to the mouth and out into that white chaos was not a thought any of them could finish.

Odile opened the tin case with her knife because the latch was fused with corrosion, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job perfectly, were papers.

She read by lamplight while Isandro kept watch back toward the entrance and Tam sat with their back against an ore car, knees drawn up, eyes fixed on the wall of writing as if reading it could bring the man who'd cut it back to life long enough to explain himself.

The papers were copies. Merrick had been careful—he'd made carbons, the old-fashioned press-copy kind, smudged but legible. Deeds. Surveys. Correspondence. And at the center of it, a single sheet in a fine clerk's hand listing shares, holdings, percentages—an ownership structure for something called the Kestrel Consolidated Mining & Tunnel Company—and beneath each entry, a name.

Odile read the names aloud, slowly, because names are the sharpest tools there are and you should handle them carefully.

Seven names. Seven men. She recognized two of them from newspapers she'd read in hotel lobbies across three territories, the kind of men whose photographs appeared above articles about progress and development and the future of the mountain corridors. One of them was Corvus Hale's senior partner. One of them, she realized with a coldness that had nothing to do with the tunnel, sat on the land board that had certified the closure surveys—the very surveys Anselm's ledger proved false.

"It's all here," she said. "The whole machine. The closure orders, the falsified surveys, the buyout of the Vane debt at a fraction of its value, and then—" she turned a page, and her throat tightened— "then the mineral rights transfer. Dated nine days after the Vanes were ruined. Nine days. They didn't stumble onto the tunnel's worth. They knew. They always knew. The ruin was the instrument."

"And the survey team?" Tam's voice cracked on the second word. "Why kill six government men?"

"Because Merrick found what we found." Odile tapped the papers. "Because he ran the numbers himself and understood the closure was fraud, and because he was honest, and honesty is expensive. They couldn't buy him, so they buried him. All six of them. Sealed in their own tunnel, where no one would ever look, because no one looks for the dead inside a hole the syndicate itself declared worthless." She looked up at Isandro. "That's why the camp was abandoned mid-task. That's why no tracks. They walked in here under orders—told it was a final inspection—and the orders changed at the mouth."

Isandro had gone very still. In the lamplight their face had the quality Odile had learned to read on the switchbacks: not blankness but compression, everything pushed down into a small hard space where it could be carried. "My family built this line," they said. "My grandfather drove the first heading. And they walled his crew in behind a fraud and let the mountain keep them." A pause. "And then they spent fifty years making sure the name Vane meant nothing but failure."

"Not nothing," Odile said. "A warning. That's worse."

* * *

They worked by rotation after that—Odile insisted on it, because grief and revelation both make people stupid and stupidity in a tunnel kills. Isandro slept first, curled against the ore car with the map case zipped inside their coat even in sleep, and Odile copied documents by lamp while Tam sat cross-legged opposite, holding the flame steady and asking questions in a low voice, one after another, the way the kid processed fear: by converting it into understanding.

"How do we get it out? The papers. If the storm seals us in—"

"We wait it out. Tunnels hold heat better than tents. We've got food for six days if we're strict. The storm will spend itself in two, maybe three."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we get hungry, and then we get clever. In that order."

Tam almost smiled. Almost. Then their eyes went back to the wall of writing, to the row where Merrick had cut, in letters larger than the rest, as if his strength had rallied for exactly these words:

*"Tell the Vanes. Tell them it was never their debt. Tell them the mountain remembers."*

"Odile." Tam's voice had dropped. "Do you think he knew? When he was writing it. That someone would come."

"I think," Odile said, "that a man sealing his own tomb either stops writing or starts writing to the future. He chose. Men like that don't choose wrong." She set down the pen. "Sleep, Tam. Your turn comes after Isandro."

But sleep didn't take her easily when her own turn came. She lay on the folded tarp with the lamp turned low and listened to the mountain breathe, and somewhere in the middle of the night—it might have been hours, it might have been minutes—she heard the sound she'd been dreading without knowing she dreaded it.

Not rockfall. Not water.

Voices. Faint, distorted, coming down the tunnel on the moving air—from the direction of the summit shaft, the second opening, the one the draft had promised.

Muffled. Distant. But voices, plural, and the scrape of boots on ballast, and then a lantern-glow, weak as a dying star, blooming around a bend far ahead.

Odile was on her feet with her hand over Isandro's mouth before Isandro's eyes finished opening, and she put her lips close to their ear and gave them the only report that mattered:

"We're not alone in here. And the storm just took away every door but theirs."
