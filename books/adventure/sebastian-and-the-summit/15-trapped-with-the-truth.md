## 15. Trapped With the Truth

The storm came down the mountain like a verdict.

We heard it before we saw it—a sound like a freight train that never arrived, building in the high passes for two days and then let loose all at once. By the time Odile got the tarp lashed over the tunnel mouth, the wind was carrying snow sideways in sheets so thick they erased the world past ten feet. The Kestrel Range had decided we were staying, and it hadn't asked our opinion.

Inside, the tunnel stretched back into the dark a quarter mile or more, and the cold in there was different from the cold outside—older, patient, the kind of cold that had been waiting since the Vanes built the walls. We made camp forty feet in from the mouth, far enough to be out of the wind's reach, close enough to see the grey light of the storm through the gap where the tarps didn't quite meet. Tam fed the small stove we'd salvaged from Anselm's hut with splintered ore-car planks, and the fire threw shadows up the tunnel walls that moved like people.

Nobody talked much that first night. There was nothing to say that the storm wasn't already saying.

* * *

On the second day I took inventory, because inventory is what I do when my hands need something honest to hold.

Flour: four pounds. Salt pork: a slab the size of a hymnal. Tea, matches, a tin of condensed milk Tam had been hoarding like treasure. Two survey charges left over from breaching the mouth—too wet now to trust, probably dead. Odile's rifle with eleven rounds. The documents, wrapped in oilcloth inside my map case, dry as scripture.

I did the arithmetic twice, hoping the second time would be kinder. It wasn't. Three mouths, storm-bound, at half rations: nine days. Ten if the weather broke early. Twelve if we ate things we'd rather not name, and there were things in this tunnel I'd rather not name either—the bones of the Vane crew sat in the deep workings behind us, walled in by men who wanted their fortune more than their lives, and every time the wind moaned down the bore it sounded less like weather and more like testimony.

Tam sat against the wall watching me count. "How long?"

"Long enough," I said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind engineers give. Everything else is guessing." I closed the flour sack. "Nine days."

Tam absorbed that with a nod that tried to be adult and mostly managed it. The kid had lost weight since the scree; the hollows under the eyes were deeper, but the eyes themselves hadn't changed. Still the same stubborn pilot-light stare that had gotten them stowed on my sled outside Grayfall Junction, back when this was a job instead of a siege.

Odile came back from the mouth, shaking snow off her shoulders. She'd been out three times that morning, standing in the white roar, reading it. She shook her head once. That was all the report anyone needed. The storm wasn't breaking. The storm was settling in like a tenant.

"Then we talk about it tonight," she said. "All of it. No more circling."

"Circling what?" Tam asked.

Odile looked at me. I looked at the fire. Neither of us answered, which was answer enough, and Tam's face went tight with the particular anger of a young person who has just realized the adults have been managing them.

* * *

We talked about it that night, and it went the way I knew it would go, which is to say badly, and then honestly, which is worse.

It started with Tam. Of course it did. "So say it," Tam said, poking the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "Say what we're actually deciding. Because you two keep looking at each other like it's already decided and I'm just cargo."

"You're not cargo," Odile said.

"Then tell me what's in the case. All of it. Not the version where it's 'documents.' The real one."

I'd been dreading this conversation since the deep workings, since the moment Odile pried the oilcloth off the surveyor's last testament and the lamplight fell across names I'd spent twenty years trying not to know. But there was no putting it off anymore, and maybe there never had been. Maybe the whole mountain had been arranged to corner me into exactly this room, exactly this fire, exactly this question.

"The man who wrote those pages was named Elias Wrenn," I said. "He was junior surveyor on the summit line. He survived whatever happened to the crew—hid in the side adit while they bricked up the main bore with his colleagues on the wrong side of it. He lived long enough to write down everything. Every partner. Every payment. Every falsified closure survey. Names, dates, amounts. It's a complete confession, signed by the men who committed the crime, in their own hand, in their own ledger copies." I paused. "And it ends with the deed. The mineral rights under this summit. They're held in trust. For the heirs of Sebastian Vane."

The fire popped. Somewhere back in the dark, water dripped off stone with the regularity of a clock.

"My father's name was Sebastian Vane," I said. "He died when I was six. I grew up on stories about him—the brilliant engineer, the man the syndicate ruined, the family that lost everything when the line closed. I thought we'd been ruined fair. Bad luck, bad timing, the way rail fortunes go. I found out in that adit that we weren't ruined. We were murdered for standing in the way, and then written out of the record so cleanly that even I believed the lie." I turned my hands toward the fire. "Those papers don't just prove the syndicate killed the crew. They prove the syndicate owes my family a fortune. And they're addressed, effectively, to me."

Tam stared at me. "That's why you went white at the notebook. Back on the switchback."

"That's why."

"So what's the problem?" Tam's voice cracked upward with disbelief. "You're rich. You're free. You never have to run again. What am I missing?"

"What you're missing," Odile said quietly, "is what happens after we carry those papers down the mountain."

She shifted, and the lamplight caught the scar along her jaw—the one she'd never explained and I'd never asked about. "Corvus Hale hired us to find a missing survey team. Hale works for the syndicate; we've known that since the money cleared too easy. If we come down with these documents, we're not coming down as witnesses. We're coming down as targets carrying the only evidence that can hang his employers. Eleven bullets, one rifle, three exhausted people, and every man Hale can hire between here and Grayfall Junction." She looked at me. "Or we burn them."

"Burn them," Tam repeated, flat.

"Destroy the papers, scatter, disappear the way Isandro's been disappearing for twenty years. The syndicate keeps its secret. Nobody else dies. We live." Odile said it without flinching, because she was the kind of person who could look at any truth straight on, even the ugly ones. Especially the ugly ones. "I've done the arithmetic too, Isandro. Same as you. Carrying those papers down is a gamble where the house kills you most of the time. Burning them is a certainty where everybody lives poor and free. I know which bet I'd normally take."

"But?" Tam said.

"But Anselm burned his hut for us," Odile said. "Forty years of records, forty years of his life, gone up in smoke in eleven minutes so three strangers could get clear. He stayed behind so we could carry this." She touched the map case without opening it. "I'm not going to decide to throw that away because dying is inconvenient. But I'm not going to decide to keep it because vengeance feels holy, either. So we decide together. All three. And nobody gets managed."

They both looked at me.

I want to say I gave a speech. I didn't. What I did was stand up and walk back into the dark of the tunnel, because there was something I needed to look at before I could answer, and I'd been avoiding it for two days.

* * *

The walled-up bore was fifty yards back, where the main line met the old ventilation adit. Our lamps made small yellow islands in a black ocean. The wall itself was rough-laid stone and mortar, done fast by men who knew they were doing murder and wanted it finished before dawn—and in front of it, arranged with a care that undid me every time I looked, were the things the crew had carried. A brass theodolite, still in its case. A lunch pail. A coat folded over a rock, sleeves tucked in, the way you fold a coat when you expect to come back for it.

Twenty-two men. My father had been dead eight years by then, but his brother was there, and his brother's crew, the men who'd driven the tunnel and struck the ore seam and understood, in their last hour, exactly what the syndicate had decided their lives were worth.

I stood in front of that wall for a long time. Long enough that Tam came and stood beside me without saying anything, and then Odile, so that the three of us made a small congregation in the cold.

"He ran," I finally said. My voice came out strange in the tunnel, flattened by stone. "Wrenn. He hid, he survived, he wrote it all down—and then what? He carried those pages for how long? Years? Decades? And he never brought them down. He died up here with the truth in his pocket." I crouched and touched the mortar. It crumbled a little under my finger, patient as everything else in this place. "You know why? Because he did the same arithmetic we've been doing. Carry it down and die, or bury it and live. And he chose living. He chose it for thirty years, and it bought him nothing. He died alone in a hole in a mountain with a confession nobody ever read."

"That's not fair," Tam said. "He couldn't have known—"

"No. He couldn't. That's the point. None of us knows. Wrenn gambled on safety and lost everything anyway, slowly. Anselm gambled on standing still and lost everything at once—but his ledger is in this case, and his death means something, and Wrenn's thirty years mean nothing except this wall." I stood. My knees cracked in the cold. "I've been running since I was nineteen years old. You both know it. New town, new name on the register, never unpack the bag, never stay past the season. I told myself it was freedom. It was fear wearing freedom's coat. And every year the running cost more than the thing I was running from."

Odile said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing.

"Hale offered me a new name once already," I said. "Back at the hearing he'll offer again—I can feel it coming like weather. Partnership, papers, a life where nobody's hunting me. And here's the thing that took me twenty years and a massacre site to understand: that offer isn't the opposite of running. It *is* running. It's running with better shoes. As long as the price of my safety is other people's erasure, I'm still fleeing. Just in a direction he approves of."

"So what are you saying?" Tam whispered.

I looked at the wall. At the folded coat. At the theodolite that some man had set down carefully, expecting to pick it up again, expecting a tomorrow that men with ledgers had already cancelled.

"I'm saying I'm tired," I said. "I'm saying that for the first time in my life, staying costs less than leaving. Not staying in a place—staying in the truth. Standing in it. Letting it be heavy." I turned around and faced them both, and something in my chest that had been clenched since Grayfall Junction, since before Grayfall Junction, since a boy watched his family's name get scraped off a station sign—it loosened. Not all the way. But some. "We carry it down. All of it. The ledger, the pages, the deed. We walk into Grayfall Junction in broad daylight and we put it on a table in front of God and the railway commission and Corvus Hale himself, and we dare them to make us disappear with the whole valley watching."

"They might kill us," Odile said. Not arguing. Measuring.

"They might. But they'd have to do it in front of witnesses, in daylight, after the syndicate's own signatures are public record. Men who move like weather can't afford to move like that. Weather needs darkness." I almost smiled. "Besides. I've read every closure survey on that line. I know where their story has seams. If they want a fight, they'll have to fight an engineer on ground I've mapped."

"And if the storm doesn't break?" Tam asked. "Nine days of food. What if it's twelve?"

"Then we eat the tea leaves and the boot leather and we keep the papers dry," I said. "Because here's the last piece of arithmetic, and it's the only one that matters. Twenty years ago I learned that stopping anywhere gets people found. This week I learned that getting found isn't the worst thing. The worst thing is being erased so completely that even your own name belongs to somebody else." I put my hand flat against the cold stone of the wall, over the place where the mortar had crumbled, as if I could feel the heartbeat of everyone buried behind it. "They took the Vanes' fortune, their line, and their lives, and they thought they'd taken the name too. These papers are the name, alive. I'm not burning my own name to stay comfortable. Not anymore. Not for anything."

The tunnel was quiet. Even the drip had stopped, as if the mountain itself were listening.

Then Odile reached into her coat and took out her father's compass—the brass one she'd carried through every tracking job, the one she treated like a relic—and pressed it into my palm. Her fingers were warm; the compass was freezing.

"For luck," she said. "And because you'll need to know which way down is, when the time comes. I'd hate for you to get lost on the one trip you actually want to finish."

Tam laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, the kind that comes right before crying or right after—and wiped their face with a sleeve and said, "Grayfall Junction, then. In daylight. Like idiots."

"Like witnesses," I corrected.

* * *

We went back to the fire and divided the flour into thirds, properly, on the scale, the way you do when you've decided to survive. Outside, the storm kept up its long white argument with the mountain, and I found I could hear it differently now—not as a wall, but as a schedule. Storms end. Snows settle. Somewhere under all that white was a trail, and at the bottom of the trail was a town, and in the town was a lawyer who genuinely believed everyone could be bought.

Let him believe it. He was about to meet a man who'd finally stopped running, and I intended to teach him what that cost.

I slept that night without dreaming of stations. For the first time in twenty years, I unpacked the bag—all the way—and hung my spare shirt on a spike in the tunnel wall, and it felt less like surrender than like arriving somewhere I'd been heading all along.

Two days later, the wind changed.

Odile stood at the mouth at first light, and when she turned around she was smiling the thin, sharp smile she saved for blood trails and broken storms.

"Weather's lifting," she said. "Three days of clear. Maybe four." She looked at me, and then at the map case, and then down the mountain, where the world was waiting with its teeth out. "Better eat well tonight. Tomorrow we start walking."
