## 18. Collapse

I'd rigged ore cars before. Not like this—never like this—but Tam had grown up around the salvage yards at Grayfall Junction, where dead engines went to be stripped and nothing that rolled was ever truly trusted. So when Odile said *the charge lines, Tam, show Isandro where they run*, Tam had known enough to be afraid.

The tunnel was a throat. That was the only word for it. The summit bore had been driven straight through the mountain's spine a lifetime ago, and the air inside tasted of stone dust and old iron and something sweeter underneath, like apples gone to rot in a cellar. Our lanterns made small trembling circles. Everything beyond them was black with a thickness Tam could feel against the skin, like wading in cold water that had decided to be air.

Isandro worked ahead, hands steady on the old charge housings, setting the powder Odile had carried up from Anselm's stores in paper-wrapped bricks the size of hymnals. The plan had been said out loud three times, the way Odile always made them say things out loud, because *a plan in your head is a wish, and a plan in your mouth is a promise.*

"The deep workings," Isandro had said. "Where the crew was walled in. Where the surveyor wrote his last pages. Hale and his men will come down the main bore to take the documents off us. We let them get past the first pillar line. Then we bring the roof down between them and the tunnel mouth. Not on them—*behind* them. They're sealed in with their own crime until the thaw brings the law up the mountain. We walk out with the papers and nobody has to die."

"Nobody has to die," Odile had repeated, in the flat voice she used when she was checking a knot. "Say the rest of it, Isandro."

And Isandro had said it, jaw tight: "The charge has to cut clean. If it cuts wide, it takes the pillar line with it. If it takes the pillar line, there's nothing holding the deep workings up but habit."

Tam had watched Odile's face when Isandro said that. Odile hadn't blinked. She'd only said, "Then we cut clean."

Now, in the dark, Tam crouched at the junction where the main bore met the first of the deep galleries, splicing wire by lantern-light. The old line-keeper's spools, copper gone green at the edges but sound at the core. Anselm's wire. Anselm's powder. Anselm's ledger riding against Tam's ribs in an oilskin wrap, next to the surveyor's pages, next to everything the syndicate had killed for. It felt less like carrying documents and more like carrying a lit stove.

"Talk to me, Tam." Odile's voice came low from ten yards back, where she'd taken the second splice. "What are your hands doing?"

"Doing what they're told." Tam's voice came out smaller than intended. "Splice one is made. Insulation's wrapped. It'll hold."

"It'll hold," Odile echoed, and Tam could hear her smiling, and that helped, that helped more than Tam wanted to admit.

Isandro came back down the bore at a crouch, lantern held so its light barely spilled. "Both galleries set. Pillar line's untouched. When it cuts, the roof comes down in the throat, fifty yards behind where they'll be standing." A pause. "Fifty yards is not a lot of mountain, Tam. When I give the word, you don't admire your work. You run for the cross-cut. Odile will be at the switch. You'll be behind her before the echo dies."

"And you?" Tam asked, because someone always had to ask, and it might as well be the youngest.

"I'll be ahead of you," Isandro said. "I built the firing circuit. I'll be the first one out."

Tam believed it. That was the terrible thing—Tam believed it, the way you believe a bridge will hold, right up until the sound it makes tells you otherwise.

* * *

Hale's men came at dusk, which Odile had predicted, because men who move like weather prefer to arrive with the light behind them.

The trio had retreated up the main bore to the cross-cut, lanterns shuttered, and waited in the dark among the bones of the mountain. Tam heard them before seeing anything: boots on old ties, the clink of gear, a voice—Hale's voice, smooth as ballast gravel—saying, "They're in here. Nobody carries that much paper uphill in a storm for exercise. Find them. And Vane—Vane we want breathing. The rest, I don't care."

Breathing. Tam's heart was going like a piston. *Vane we want breathing* meant the offer was still open, which meant Isandro's answer from the night before—*no, and no again, and stop asking*—hadn't been heard as a refusal. It had been heard as a price not yet met.

The lantern-glow came up the bore like a slow tide. Six men. Hale made seven, walking in the middle where it was safest, his city coat absurd and immaculate in the tunnel dust. They passed the first pillar line. Passed the second. Reached the gallery mouth where the surveyor's last camp still sat in its alcove—chair, table, inkpot, and the wall where the man had written his way toward death.

"There," Hale said softly. "You see? He was here. It's all here." And then, louder, with that terrible reasonableness: "Isandro Vane. I know you can hear me. Let's not make the mountain do our negotiating."

Isandro stood up out of the dark.

Tam's stomach dropped. That wasn't the plan—Isandro was supposed to stay hidden until they were deep—but Isandro stepped into the edge of the men's lantern light with the documents held up in one hand, and Tam understood a half-second later, the way you understand a switch thrown ahead of a train: *drawing them deeper.* Every eye on Isandro. Every boot moving forward.

"Mr. Hale," Isandro said, and even now the voice was level, a surveyor's voice, reading elevations. "You're standing on my family's name. Do you know what's holding the roof above you? Nothing. Nothing but the fact that nobody's bothered to take it down yet."

"Bluster," Hale said, but his men had stopped walking, and their lights had begun to wander upward, nervous, along the cracks in the crown of the bore.

"Wire's live, Mr. Hale. Count of three, and the only way out of this mountain is the way the thaw comes—in the spring, with a magistrate." Isandro began backing up the bore, slow, deliberate, herding them with their own greed. "Or you turn around now, and I'll only ruin you with paper."

Hale laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, awful. "You sound like your grandfather. He also thought records could stop men like me."

"He was right," Isandro said. "He was just early."

And Hale's patience, which had worn a reasonable face for nineteen chapters of Isandro's life, finally came off. "Take the documents," he said to his men. "The girl can fall down a hole for all of—"

Tam never heard the rest, because Isandro's hand came down on the plunger, and the mountain answered.

* * *

Later—years later, in dreams that still arrived without invitation—Tam would try to remember the sound and fail, because there was no sound. There was a removal. The world subtracted itself. The lantern flames all bent sideways at once, and the air came through the bore like a fist, and the roof of the tunnel *left*, fifty yards of throat collapsing in a single grinding exhalation, dust so thick the lantern light turned to brown wool.

And then, out of that brown wool, a scream that Tam knew.

"Odile." Tam was already running, back down the bore, into the dust, before anyone could shout a warning. "ODILE."

The switch. She'd been at the switch, the cross-cut junction, forty yards shy of the cut line—forty yards on the wrong side of it, and Tam's mind did the arithmetic with a cold, mechanical cruelty: the charge had cut wide. Not much wide. Ten feet, maybe fifteen. Enough. Enough to take the pillar line with it, exactly the way Isandro had said it might, exactly the way Odile had made Isandro say out loud.

The dust was a solid thing. Tam ran into it and it was like running into wet cloth, into a held breath, into the mountain's own throat closing. The lantern was useless; Tam dropped it and ran by memory, by the rail underfoot, by counting ties the way Odile had taught—*count your steps, child, the mountain can't lie to you if you count*—and at tie forty the rail ended.

The rail ended.

Tam stood on the last intact tie with the void in front of them, a wall of fallen stone from floor to crown, still settling, still breathing grit, and put both hands against it, and screamed into it.

"She was at the switch! She was at the SWITCH—"

Hands pulled Tam back. Isandro. Dust to the eyebrows, a cut over one eye sheeting red, and a face with nothing behind it, no expression at all, which was worse than any expression Tam had ever seen on a human being.

"Get the picks," Isandro said. "Both of them. And the shovels. Go."

"They're behind the—"

"*Go.*"

Tam went.

* * *

They dug until Tam stopped being able to count the hours.

The dust never fully settled; every shovel stroke raised it again until they were digging inside a brown cloud, two shapes and two lanterns and a wall of stone that did not care about them at all. Isandro worked like a machine that had been told it was a person, no wasted motion, reading the fall the way Odile read snow—*the big slab came off the east wall, see the bedding plane, the void'll be behind the small stuff, she'd have made for the switch housing, there's a knee of rock there, she'd have made for the knee*—and Tam believed it, had to believe it, because the alternative was a wall and a silence and a name said once and never again.

"Odile!" Tam's voice was gone to a rasp by the second hour. "Odile Ferran! If you can hear me, knock! Knock on the rock, we'll hear it, we'll—"

And then, faint, from somewhere inside the wall—so faint Tam thought it was their own heart hammering back at them—

*Knock.*

Tam froze with the shovel half-raised. "Isandro. Isandro, listen—"

*Knock. Knock-knock.*

Isandro went absolutely still, head cocked, dust sifting off their shoulders. Then they put their bare palm flat against the rock face, and Tam saw their whole body change, saw something come back into the face that had been empty, and Isandro said, in a voice that cracked down the middle like the mountain had:

"She's breathing. Air's getting through. The knee of rock—she made the knee." And then, louder, pressed close to the stone, shouting into it like a man shouting into a well: "ODILE. WE'RE HERE. WE'RE DIGGING. TALK TO US. KNOCK ONCE FOR YES."

A pause, long enough to die in.

One knock. Then, after a moment, a slow, deliberate, mocking little rhythm—*shave-and-a-haircut*—and Tam laughed and sobbed at the same time, because only Odile Ferran, buried under a mountain, would take the time to be sarcastic about it.

"She's got air," Isandro said, and the machine-voice was back, and it was better than crying. "She's got a void and air. We dig to the void. Small bore, top of the fall, where the bedding plane runs—big rocks come down, small rocks stay put, she taught us that on the scree. We dig a rabbit hole, not a doorway. Tam—"

"Rabbit hole," Tam said. "I know."

They dug a rabbit hole. Isandro set the pattern—two minutes on the pick, then hands, sorting stone by size, stacking the big slabs like masonry on either side of the growing hole so the walls wouldn't slough—and Tam hauled, crawling into the hole on their belly once it got narrow, passing rocks back between their legs, elbows bleeding through their sleeves, the knock coming at intervals now, steady as a clock, *knock, knock-knock*, Odile keeping count for them, keeping them honest, keeping them moving, the way she had kept them moving across the scree and the gorge and the whole white killing length of the Kestrel Range.

At some point Hale's men started digging too, on their side of the fall, back toward the tunnel mouth. Tam heard them—muffled voices, the ring of iron on stone. The collapse had sealed them in with the deep workings, exactly as planned, except that the plan had never accounted for what a man sounds like when he understands he's going to die in the dark unless the people he came to rob dig him out. One of them was crying. Another was praying. Hale's voice, when it came through the stone, had lost every ounce of its reasonableness.

"VANE." A fist against rock. "VANE, THERE ARE SEVEN OF US AND ONE OF HER. DIG YOUR OWN SIDE."

Isandro didn't answer. Isandro drove the pick, and Tam understood, watching the shoulders work, that the silence was the answer, and that it was the loudest thing Isandro Vane had ever said.

* * *

The hole broke through sometime in the black hours before dawn.

Tam was in front, on their belly in a gap barely wide enough for a ribcage, and the pick work was Isandro's behind them, and Tam's reaching hand went through the last skin of stone into moving air—cold, clean, tasting of nothing, the sweetest air Tam had ever breathed—and beyond it, in the light of a lantern that had somehow survived wedged in the knee of rock, a hand.

A hand, gripping a pick handle, using it to knock.

"Odile." Tam got fingers around her wrist. Her pulse was there, fast and hard as a bird's. "Odile, we're through, we're through, don't move, let me—"

"Wasn't going to," she said. Her voice was dust and gravel and underneath it, unmistakably, her. "Wasn't going anywhere. Bad habit of mine, staying put."

They widened the hole with their hands, then with the pick passed forward, and Isandro came through last, folding their long body into the void—a pocket the size of a coffin, a coffin with a living woman in it—and for a moment nobody did anything. Tam held Odile's shoulders. Isandro held a lantern up and looked at her face, at the blood crusted in her hair, at the eyes that found Isandro's and held them with the old flat tracker's steadiness.

"The charge cut wide," Odile said.

"Yes," Isandro said.

"You knew it might."

"I said it might. You made me say it."

"I know what I made you say." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Isandro. My legs."

And then Tam saw it, what the lantern light had been hiding, what the coffin-void had kept mercifully out of sight: the way Odile lay, the angle of her beneath the slab that had ridden down with the knee of rock and stopped, stopped a hand's width short of killing her, and pinned her instead—from the knee down, both legs, the rock settled and seated and not going anywhere that didn't involve more powder and more collapse and a buried woman who had already spent one night under a mountain.

Tam heard Isandro breathe in. Heard the breath stop.

"Don't," Odile said, before Isandro could say anything at all. "Don't you make that face. I've read snow for forty years. I know what's written here." She looked down at herself, then back up, and something almost like humor moved through the dust on her face. "Well. Anselm always said the mountain keeps what it's owed."

"We can dig," Tam said, and their voice broke apart on it. "We can dig, we can—another charge, a small one, we can—"

"You'll bring the whole knee down and we both know it, and I'll not spend my last hours listening to you two dig my grave by accident." Odile said it the way she said everything, flat, checking the knot. Then her hand came out—dust-grey, steady—and took Isandro's sleeve. "Come here. Closer. Both of you."

They crowded into the coffin of stone with her, three people and a lantern, and outside the void, muffled through the fall, Hale's men had gone quiet, listening, and the mountain held its breath around them.

"Listen," Odile said. "The documents leave this mountain. The ledger, the pages, all of it. You carry them to Grayfall and you read them out loud in front of the whole damn town, and you don't stop reading until every name is said. That's the job now. That's the trail, and you're on it, and I'm telling you the way, the last way, so you'll count your steps and not get lied to." Her grip tightened on Isandro's sleeve. "And the compass. In my coat. My father's. It was never mine to keep still. It's yours now, Isandro Vane, and you'll point it at staying, for once in your life, and you'll *stay*."

"Odile—"

"Digging's done," she said, gently, and it was the only gentle thing she'd ever said. "Now it's walking. Get me the compass, and get the papers out of my pack, and then you two sit with me a while, because I've earned that much and you owe it. And when the light comes—" her eyes went up, through all that stone, toward a dawn none of them could see— "when the light comes, you'll dig me out proper, and you'll carry me down, because I am *not* staying in this tunnel, and for once, Isandro, neither are you."

Tam pressed their forehead to Odile's shoulder, and the lantern flame stood perfectly still in the dead air, and somewhere above them, through half a mountain of stone, the first grey light of morning began, without hurry, the way it always did, to find its way down.
