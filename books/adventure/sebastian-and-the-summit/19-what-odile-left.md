## 19. What Odile Left

The digging stopped being work somewhere in the third hour and became something older than work. My hands went numb past feeling and kept going anyway. The snow came down through the broken roof of the tunnel mouth in long grey ropes, and every time I lifted a stone I thought: this one. This one will be the last one between us and her.

Tam dug beside me without speaking. Somewhere around midnight they had stopped shivering, which frightened me more than the silence, so I made them keep moving—lift, pass, stack, lift—and they did it with the dumb obedience of exhaustion, their face a pale smudge in the lantern light.

The collapse had sealed the deep workings behind a wall of fallen rock and twisted charge line, and Odile was on the wrong side of it when it came down. That was all we knew for six hours. Then, near dawn, Tam's shovel struck something that wasn't rock—a gap, a seam where the fall had bridged itself against the old timbering, leaving a crawl-space no wider than a coffin laid sideways.

I put my lantern into the gap and the light found a boot.

"Odile." My voice cracked on it. "Odile Ferran."

For a long moment nothing. Then the boot moved.

Getting her out took another two hours and cost us most of what remained of our strength. Her left leg was broken below the knee, clean but bad, and there was blood crusted black along her temple where a falling slab had clipped her. She was conscious by then—had been conscious, she told us later, for most of the night, listening to us dig, too pinned to shout, conserving her breath the way she conserved everything. When we finally dragged her into the open air of the tunnel mouth she looked up at the falling snow, at the white world, and said:

"You dig like an engineer. Slow and certain."

Then she passed out, and I was grateful, because I didn't have anything to say back that wouldn't have come out as a sob.

* * *

We made camp inside the tunnel mouth itself, thirty yards in, where the mountain's own warmth held the cold at bay and the old Vane crew's storeroom still yielded lamp oil, hardtack, and a crate of medical stores packed in straw as if someone had expected, sixty years ago, exactly this kind of need. I splinted Odile's leg with survey staves and strapping from the ore-car tarps. She woke during it and watched me work with an expression I couldn't read.

"It's bad," I said. "The break. And the cold got into it before we reached you."

"I know what a broken leg is, Vane."

"The cold got into it," I said again, because she needed to hear it plainly, because she of all people deserved plainness. "You may walk again. You will not track again. Not the way you tracked. Not across scree, not through snow, not reading ground. I'm sorry."

She was quiet a long time. Outside, the storm worked at the tunnel mouth like a thing trying doors.

"My father taught me to read snow," she said finally. "On the flats north of Grayfall, when I was seven. He said the ground tells you everything if you shut your mouth long enough to listen." She turned her head and looked down the tunnel, toward the dark where the bones of the last Vane crew lay walled in their own fortune. "I listened my whole life. It's not such a loss. It's just—" She stopped. Started again. "It's the end of a sentence I've been saying since I was seven. Endings are strange even when you see them coming."

"I'm sorry," I said a third time, because it was true and because I had nothing better.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She nodded at the satchel lying against the wall—the documents, the dead surveyor's testament, Anselm's ledger wrapped in oilcloth. "That has to go down the mountain. And I can't carry it. So."

"So," I said.

"So finish it."

* * *

She slept after that, a real sleep, and Tam and I sat by the lamp and took inventory of ourselves the way you take inventory of gear after a disaster—checking each piece for cracks. Tam's hands were still shaking. They'd been shaking since the collapse, since the moment the charge line let go early and the mountain shrugged and Odile vanished under forty tons of Kestrel granite.

"It was supposed to seal the syndicate crew in the deep workings," Tam said, for maybe the fifth time. "Not her. She was clear. I saw her clear."

"She went back for the ledger case," I said. "She thought it was still inside. She went back in when the charges fired." I'd pieced it together from the drag marks in the dust, from the position of her body relative to the fall. Odile Ferran had risked herself for paper, because paper was the whole point, because she understood earlier than any of us that the truth was the only weapon we carried worth its weight.

"Is it her fault or mine?"

"Neither. It's the mountain's fault, and the men who made us run up it. Save your blame for them; they earned it."

Tam looked at me with an expression too old for their face. "Anselm stayed behind. Odile nearly died. You're going to give all this away at a hearing instead of keeping it. What do we get? What does anyone get?"

I thought about the question honestly, because Tam deserved honesty and because I'd been asking myself the same thing since the tunnel.

"We get to stop running," I said. "That's not nothing. I've been running for twenty years and I can tell you exactly what it gets you: nowhere, permanently, with good views."

Tam almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

On the second day the storm broke and the syndicate's local men scattered.

We heard it before we saw it—engines, voices, the crunch of boots in fresh snow down-slope. I went to the tunnel mouth with the rifle and found five of them standing in the white clearing below, leaderless, looking up at the mountain the way men look at a debt they can no longer pay. Their captain—the one Hale had left in command when he went ahead to negotiate—was gone, buried in the deep workings with three others when our collapse sealed the gallery. Whatever chain of authority had held these men together had been cut with the rockfall.

One of them shouted up at us. His voice carried thin across the cold air.

"The papers don't matter! Nobody'll print them! Nobody'll hear them!"

"They'll hear them," I called back. "At Grayfall Junction. In front of the railway commission. In front of God and the telegraph office. Come and object if you like—I'll read your names into the record too. I have them all."

I didn't, quite. But the ledger had names enough, and the dead surveyor's pages had the rest, and the men below knew it. That was the thing about documents: they were patient. They waited in the dark for sixty years and then they walked downhill on their own legs.

The men stood a while longer. One of them spat. Another simply turned and started down, and once the first one moved the rest followed like water finding a grade, because that's what men without orders become—runoff. By evening the clearing below was empty except for their abandoned sled and a scatter of dropped rations, which Tam went down and collected without apology.

"Leaderless," Odile said from her bedroll, watching through the entrance. "Exposed. That's all it ever takes. Syndicates aren't monsters, Vane. They're bookkeeping. Tear out the right page and the whole ledger falls apart."

"You sound better."

"I sound angry. It's similar." She shifted against the pack beneath her leg and winced, then mastered it. "Hale isn't done, though. A man like Hale doesn't scatter. He'll already be writing the story he tells next—the version where he was deceived by overzealous subordinates, where he cooperates fully with the commission, where he walks out of Grayfall with his reputation polished and half his holdings intact."

"Then we bring him the version with dates and signatures."

"And if the commission is bought?"

"Then we bring it to the newspapers. Then to the courts. Then to every rail-town hall between here and the coast." I sat down beside her. "I spent twenty years learning every way there is to disappear, Odile. Turns out all that knowledge works just as well in reverse. Every closed door is also a route."

She studied me for a long moment, and something in her face softened—not much, she wasn't built for much, but some.

"There he is," she said. "The man who was going to vanish forever."

"He's still here. Give him time."

"No," she said. "I don't think I will."

* * *

On the fourth day she called me to her side, and I knew what was coming before she said a word, because she had the compass in her hand.

It was a heavy brass thing, old, the lid worn smooth as riverstone, the needle housing clouded with age. I'd seen it a hundred times on the trail—she checked it the way other people check a watch, a small ritual, thumb over the lid, flick it open, glance, snap it shut. Her father's. She'd told me about him once, one night on the switchback, in four sentences total, which for Odile was a memoir.

"Hold out your hand," she said.

"Odile—"

"Hold out your hand, Vane. I only have the one speech in me and I'd rather not waste it."

I held out my hand. She set the compass in it and folded my fingers over it with hers, and her grip was still strong, whatever the rest of her was now.

"My father gave it to me when I left home. He said: a tracker doesn't follow trails, she follows truth—trails lie, weather lies, animals lie, but north is north. He meant it about more than direction. He meant: when everything else is uncertain, find the one fixed thing and hold to it." She looked at the compass in my fist. "You've been holding to yours for twenty years. The map. The name. The truth of what was done to your family. You just kept it rolled up in a case where nobody could see it."

"Odile, I can't take—"

"You can and you will. I'm not giving it away. I'm assigning it." Her voice hardened into the tone she used on Tam when Tam wanted to quit a slope. "My tracking days are done. Fine. But the job isn't done. The job goes down the mountain, into a hearing room, in front of men whose whole trade is making truth look like opinion. You'll need a fixed point. So." She tapped the compass lid once, sharp. "North is north. Finish it."

I looked down at the compass. Under my fingers I could feel the wear of decades—her father's thumb, her thumb, all those small rituals of finding one's bearing. It weighed more than brass should weigh.

"What do I tell people it is?" I asked, and my voice came out rougher than I intended.

"Tell them it belonged to a woman who read snow," Odile said. "And that she's not finished reading."

* * *

We stayed in the tunnel five more days while the storm spent itself and Odile mended enough to travel by litter. Tam rigged a sledge from an ore-car panel and runner poles; I repacked the documents in oilcloth and wax, three layers, waterproofed against everything short of a river. Each night I opened the map of closed lines and added to it—not new lines, but annotations. Dates. Names. The falsified closure surveys marked in red. The map of everything abandoned becoming, page by page, the map of everything that would be reopened.

On the last night, Odile asked to see it. I spread it on the floor beside her bedroll and she read it for a long time, her finger hovering above the summit line, above the little X where the tunnel breathed its cold air fifty feet away.

"You drew this before you knew any of it," she said. "Before the telegram. Before Hale. You drew every closed line in the country because you couldn't stop looking for the one that closed on you."

"Yes."

"And now?"

"Now I know which one it was." I rolled the map slowly. "Now it's evidence."

Outside, the sky had cleared to a hard diamond cold, and the Kestrels stood white and silent under more stars than seemed lawful. Down-slope, the way we'd come, the mountain waited with all its wreckage—Anselm's burned hut, the gorge, the scree, the graves of men who moved like weather. Up-slope, nothing but the shoulder of the summit and the long descent on the far side toward Grayfall Junction, toward the hearing, toward the end of running.

Odile slept. Tam slept. I sat up with the compass in one hand and the map in the other and did something I hadn't done since I was a boy: I thought about arriving.

At first light we loaded the sledge, and Odile—propped upright among the packs, gray-faced and furious about it—pointed her chin down the mountain.

"Well, Vane," she said. "Lead on. North is north."

I snapped the compass shut, put it in my breast pocket over my heart, and took the first step of the last journey of my running life.
