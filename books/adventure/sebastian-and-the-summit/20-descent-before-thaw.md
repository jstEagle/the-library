## 20. Descent Before Thaw

The mountain let us go the way a fist lets go of something it's been holding too long—slowly, joint by joint, with a tremor in it that said it could close again whenever it wanted.

I felt it first in my boots. For eleven days above the snowline I'd known every step was borrowed, every ledge a rumor, and now the ground under me had gone soft with meltwater and spring was talking to itself under the crust in little gurgling voices, and my body didn't trust it. My body kept waiting for the mountain to change its mind.

Odile rode in the middle of our line on the mule sled, her leg strapped straight, her father's compass gone from around her neck because she'd given it away two days ago on the ridge, pressing it into Isandro's hand like she was handing over something heavier than brass. She hadn't complained once. That scared me more than the riders ever had. Odile complaining would have meant she still had fight left over for words.

Isandro broke trail ahead of us, the way they'd done since the tunnel, and I watched their back and thought about what I'd figured out somewhere around the fourth day of digging Odile out of the rockfall, when my hands were bleeding and nobody had slept and there wasn't room inside me for anything except one clear thought:

Isandro Vane isn't running away from anything anymore.

They're running toward.

* * *

You have to understand what running looked like before, so you know what changed.

When I met them—when they pulled me out of the supply sled outside Coldwater with a knife through the rope and an expression like I was a problem arithmetic couldn't solve—I thought I knew everything about running. I'd been doing it for eight months. I knew how to sleep sitting up, how to eat slow so food lasted, how to read a town from its edges: which door had a dog behind it, which porch light meant somebody who'd feed you and ask nothing, which silence meant somebody who'd call the constable first and feel good about it after.

I ran from a house where love came with conditions stapled to it, and I told myself the road was freedom, and some nights it even was. But mostly the road was just the house with better scenery. The watching never stopped. The flinching never stopped. You carry the thing you're running from folded up small against your ribs, and it weighs exactly what it weighed when you started.

That was Isandro, back then. I see it now. Every hotel room we ever passed, they checked the exits before they checked the beds. They unpacked three things and no more—a razor, a spare shirt, the map case—and the rest stayed rolled like a held breath. When I asked once why we didn't just take rooms at the front of hotels instead of the back, Odile laughed until she had to sit down on a crate, and Isandro said, "Because the back has stairs," and Odile laughed harder, and I didn't get it yet.

The name was a wound they walked around the way you walk around a hole in a floor you've lived with so long you've forgotten the floor used to be whole.

And then Anselm burned his hut for us, and the ledger came down off the mountain in oilcloth against my spine, and Isandro stood in the mouth of the tunnel with the bones of their own family behind them and Hale's voice in their ears offering a new name, a clean name, any name but Vane—

—and said no.

Not loud. That's the thing about Isandro. Everything important they say is almost quiet. They said, "I've had enough names," and turned their back on the offer, and I understood later that in that moment they stopped being a person running from a name and became a person carrying one toward something. Toward Grayfall Junction. Toward a hearing hall full of strangers. Toward the possibility—not the promise, Isandro doesn't believe in promises, they believe in records—that the name could mean what it used to mean before men with ledgers made it mean blood.

Running toward is worse than running away. Nobody tells you that. Running away, all you need is legs. Running toward, you need a reason, and reasons are heavy, and you can't set them down at night the way you set down a pack.

* * *

We came down out of the high country by the old maintenance spur, the one Anselm had shown us on his last evening, drawing it in the ash of his own stove with a burnt stick. "The syndicate's maps end here," he'd said. "They closed this line on paper thirty years ago. Paper is where they live. Walk where the paper isn't."

So we walked where the paper wasn't, and the mountain, which had spent weeks trying to kill us, seemed almost embarrassed about it now. The thaw had opened the lower passes into a kind of loud green chaos—meltwater running everywhere at once, in channels and sheets and sudden waterfalls off shelves of last year's ice, the whole slope ticking and groaning as it shrugged off winter. Avalanches had come down in the night in three places we could see, long pale scars through the timber, and each time one went, far off, like a door slamming in an empty house, Odile would lift her head and listen and then say, "Weather. Not riders." And we'd keep moving.

Because that was the other thing. There were no riders anymore.

The recovery crew was gone—scattered, leaderless, their names written down in a dead surveyor's careful hand along with the names of every man who signed off on a falsified closure, every man who took syndicate money to declare a living railway dead. We'd seen the last of them on the second morning out of the pasture: four figures on the far ridge, not pursuing, just standing, looking down at us the way men look at a fire they've decided not to fight. Then they turned and went, and Odile tracked their backs with the glasses for a long time and finally handed the glasses to me and said, "Look."

"What am I looking for?"

"Nothing," she said. "You're looking at nothing. First time on this mountain." And she smiled, which cost her something with the pain, and paid it anyway.

But the weather didn't sign anything or take anybody's money. The weather had its own ledger, and we were in arrears. The thaw that freed the trails also rotted them; the streams we'd crossed on ice going up were brown torrents going down; and on the third afternoon the sky over the western peaks went the color of a bruise and Odile said, "Front. Twelve hours, maybe less. We need walls tonight or we won't need anything again."

* * *

We found the walls by accident, which is how you find most things worth having.

An old water tower for a section house that hadn't existed since before I was born, squatting in a fold of the hillside with its tank half-collapsed and its pump shed still sound, door hanging open like it had been waiting. Somebody had lived there once—a rail family, maybe, or a line-keeper like Anselm. There were nail holes in the wall boards where pictures had hung, and a child's initials cut into the doorframe with a knife, low down, at the height of a small hand: **E.V.**

Isandro stood in front of those initials for a long time.

I pretended to be busy with the harness. Odile, from her bedroll inside, watched through the doorway and said nothing, because Odile understood things about waiting that the rest of us were only learning.

"It's nothing," Isandro said finally. "Half the range is full of Vanes. We were everywhere the rails went. That's the whole tragedy, if you want it in one sentence—we built everywhere, and then everywhere got taken."

"Could be your great-aunt," I said. "Could be somebody's kid who grew up and drove trains through here and told her grandchildren about the summer she carved her name."

"Could be," Isandro said.

"Could be a home," I said, and immediately wanted to swallow it, because I hadn't meant to say it out loud, and homes were not a subject I handled well even in my own head.

But Isandro didn't flinch. They reached out and put two fingers on the cut letters, the way you'd touch a scar to check if it still hurt, and said, "My grandmother talked about a tower like this. Water tower on the Kestrel spur. She said she and her brother hid in the pump shed during a storm once and their mother found them asleep in the coal bin and didn't even scold them, just sat down in the doorway and waited for the rain to stop." A pause. "I always thought that story was about the rain."

"And it's about?" I asked.

"The waiting," Isandro said. "Somebody staying in the doorway so you'd know where to find them when you woke up." They turned and looked at me, and their face did something I'd never seen it do in all the weeks I'd known them—it unclenched. "Get the stove lit, Tam. This place owes us one night."

* * *

The front hit at dusk like a debt collector.

Wind first, screaming down the valley with a sound like the whole mountain exhaling, then sleet, then snow, wet and huge-flaked, the kind that means the season is turning over its accounts. We got the mule in the shed, the sled under the tower's lean, the stove fed, and then there was nothing to do but sit inside the noise and listen to it try the door every few minutes like a patient animal testing a fence.

Odile slept. Her body had decided healing was a full-time occupation and it was not accepting interruptions. Isandro sat by the stove with the map case open on their knees—the map of closed lines, the one they'd carried rolled tight for years, the one that had started everything—and I watched them unroll it all the way flat for the first time. Both hands. Weight on both ends. It covered most of the floor between us, that map, decades of Isandro's life drawn in ink: every shuttered line, every station that died when the trains stopped coming, every closure date cross-checked against every lie the syndicate had filed. It looked different spread out. Rolled up, it was a weapon or a shield. Flat, under lamplight, with the storm leaning on the walls—it was a record. It was almost a territory.

"You could burn it now," I said. "It's done its job. The ledger's the evidence. The pages are the evidence. That's just—" I gestured. "Old grief, drawn neatly."

"I know what it is," Isandro said.

"So why keep it?"

They were quiet long enough that I thought they hadn't heard, and then they said, "Because when I hang it on a wall someday, I want to remember what it cost to draw." Their thumb moved along a line I recognized—the summit spur, the closed line, our line—traced so many times the ink had gone soft. "Every mark on here is a place I went to prove nobody was coming back. And now every mark is a place somebody might." They looked up. "Do you know what I'm going to do in Grayfall?"

"Testify."

"After that."

I shook my head.

"Buy a trunk," Isandro said. "A real one. Big. And unpack it all the way, and leave it unpacked, and hang this map on the wall of whatever room the trunk sits in, and stay there long enough for the neighbors to learn my name." A ghost of a smile. "Long enough for the name to be boring again. That's the whole ambition, Tam. I want my name to bore people."

Outside, the wind threw a handful of the roof at us and moved on. Inside, the stove ticked. I thought about my own name, the one I'd run from, Tam-something-something, a name attached to a house with conditions stapled to it, and I realized I'd been holding it wrong this whole time. It wasn't a weight. It was unfinished. Names are unfinished; that's the secret nobody tells you when you're fifteen and furious. You don't escape a name. You finish it. You decide what it says.

"E.V.," I said, nodding at the doorframe. "Whoever that was—they finished theirs okay, probably. Carved it low, so it'd still be there when they came back grown."

Isandro looked at the initials a long moment. "Yeah," they said. "Probably."

* * *

The storm broke near dawn the way storms do—all at once, mid-shriek, like a singer cutting off—and we came out into a world scoured and rebuilt overnight. Snow to the hub on the sled wheels, sky rinsed blue, the whole Kestrel Range standing up white and enormous and lit from inside by the early sun, every ridge sharp as a blade laid on silk. The air was so cold and clean it hurt to breathe, and I breathed it anyway, as much as I could hold.

Odile stood at the shed door on her crutch, leg strapped, face gaunt, hair full of snow-melt, and looked at the view like a woman reading a letter from someone she'd forgiven. "Two days to the valley," she said. "Three, with me slowing the pace. The pass road should be open below the treeline. From there—" she glanced at Isandro, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with roads. "From there it's just people. And we know more about people than they know about us."

"We have the ledger," Isandro said.

"We have the pages."

"We have the map." Isandro rolled it as they spoke—but differently now, loosely, without the old compression, like a man rolling up a banner rather than folding away a shroud. "And we have names. All of them. Written down where weather can't reach."

I stood between them with the oilcloth packet against my spine, the dead surveyor's final testimony riding warm off my own body heat, and I thought: this is what grace feels like. Not the church kind—I wouldn't know that kind. This kind. The quiet after violence, when you're still alive and the people beside you are still alive and the truth is still true and the mountain, for once, is on nobody's side but its own. My father used to say grace was for people who deserved it. Standing there in the blue cold with my hands cracked and my ribs bruised and my heart going easy for the first time in eight months, I decided he was wrong about that too, along with most things.

We moved out an hour after sunrise, three figures and a mule on a white road going down, leaving the only tracks on the whole face of the world.

And it was somewhere on that second morning of the descent, crossing a meadow where the meltwater ran bright over stones, that I finally said it out loud, because some things you carry too long turn to stone and some things you say and they turn to wings.

"When this is done," I said, "when the hearing's over and the name is safe—what happens to me?"

Isandro didn't break stride. Didn't look surprised, either, which somehow made it easier. "That depends," they said, "on whether you're asking where you'll run next, or where you'll stay."

And I opened my mouth to answer, and found I already knew, and the knowing frightened me and warmed me in equal measure, because staying was the one thing I'd sworn I'd never be brave enough to do.

Below us, past the last white shoulders of the range, the valley opened up—smoke rising thin and straight from a scatter of towns, and among them, far off, a thread of rail gleaming in the sun, running toward a junction whose name I'd heard whispered all winter like a verdict:

Grayfall.

Where the truth would stand up in front of everyone and speak.
