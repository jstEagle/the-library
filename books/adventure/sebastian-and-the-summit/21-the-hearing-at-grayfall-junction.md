## 21. The Hearing at Grayfall Junction

The hall at Grayfall Junction had been built for freight disputes and funeral wakes, and it had never in its life held as many people as it held that morning. I knew because I'd helped unload the benches from the freight platform at dawn, Odile directing with her one good arm, Tam hauling crates with the fury of someone who had decided that if the world was going to make them carry things, they would carry more than anyone.

By eight o'clock there were people standing along the walls. By nine they were standing in the doorway, and by ten the windows at the back had been opened so the crowd spilling onto the platform could hear. The yard outside was full of trains that weren't going anywhere—every crew in the district had found a reason to be idle, and every idled crew had found a reason to be in the hall.

The syndicate had come too. Not the men who moved like weather; those were gone, scattered or fled or quietly arrested in three towns along the line. What remained was the money. Four lawyers in gray, seated at a table with a rail company seal on it, and behind them, in the second row, a handful of shareholders with the particular stillness of men watching a fire approach their own property.

Corvus Hale was not among them.

I noticed that the way you notice a missing tooth. I had spent three weeks expecting him to be there, expecting the reasonable voice, the reasonable face, the offer that was really a knife with paperwork attached. His chair at the lawyers' table sat empty. His name was on the syndicate's filing. His absence meant either that he had already cut his losses, or that he was somewhere worse, doing something quieter.

I decided not to decide. There was a hearing to get through.

* * *

The magistrate was a woman named Osgood, retired from the capital, brought in because no local judge would touch it—every one of them owed somebody in the room a favor. She had a face like a closed ledger and she opened the proceedings by saying that she had read the filings, all nine hundred pages of them, and that anyone who stood up expecting to win by talking longer than the other side should sit back down now.

Then she looked at me.

"You are Isandro Vane."

"I am."

"The deed before the court names a Sebastian Vane as grantee of the summit corridor and tunnel workings. State your relation."

"Grandfather," I said. "Sebastian Vane built the line. He and forty-one men. Thirty-nine of them are still in the tunnel."

The hall went quiet in the way a rail yard goes quiet when the air brakes release—all at once, and with a weight behind it.

Osgood let the silence sit. Then she said, "Proceed," and I opened my map case.

* * *

I had thought a great deal, on the descent and through the winter and in the weeks of depositions that followed, about what to show them. I am not a lawyer. I am an engineer, and what I know is how to read a line—how to look at a grade and a curve and a cut and know what the men who built it intended, and what they were willing to lie about to get it.

So I didn't start with the deed. I started with the map.

I pinned it to the board Osgood had ordered set up, and I let them look at it: the whole district, every line I had walked and surveyed and recorded in eleven years of not staying anywhere. The closed lines in black. The abandoned grades in red. The tunnels, the trestles, the maintenance huts, the water stops.

"This is a map of everything the rail companies have closed and buried," I said. "Eleven years of work. I drew it because I was running, and a man who is running needs to know where the roads aren't. Every line on it was closed by a closure survey. Every closure survey was certified. And I am going to show you what those certifications were worth."

I took the ledger from its crate—Anselm's ledger, forty years of a line-keeper's records, in a hand that started steady and ended shaking—and I set it on the table in front of the syndicate's lawyers, where they had to look at it.

"Anselm Rook kept this hut on the high pasture for thirty-eight years. He recorded every train, every tonnage, every inspection that passed his section of the summit line. The closure survey of the Vane corridor, certified by the syndicate's own engineers, states that the line was abandoned in disrepair and that the tunnel was sealed due to structural collapse. Anselm's records show ore cars moving through that tunnel eleven months after the certified closure date. They show maintenance crews. They show, in the final entries, a work party of forty-three men entering the tunnel on the fourteenth of March, and they show—" my voice did something I didn't authorize, and I let it, because the room should hear it crack— "they show nothing coming out."

I turned to the last pages and read the entries. The hall listened the way crowds listen at funerals, which is to say completely and without mercy.

* * *

The syndicate's lead counsel was a dry, precise man named Verrin, and to his credit he didn't waste time on outrage. He went for the documents' provenance.

"A hermit," he said. "An illegal squatter in company property, keeping a private book of—what, exactly? Unverified notations. And the map—" he gestured at my life's work as if it were a child's drawing— "drawn by the claimant himself. The claimant, gentlemen, who is the sole beneficiary. A man with every motive to forge."

"Of course," I said. "That's why I didn't rely on them."

I brought out the surveyor's notebook—the one Odile had found in the abandoned camp, the one whose torn stubs had matched the page someone slipped into my map case in a rail-town hotel a lifetime ago. And I brought out the pages themselves, and I called the name I had been waiting all winter to call.

"The court will hear from Wren Castellane, junior surveyor of the Kestrel Range expedition."

She came down the aisle thin and sunburned and steady, a woman who had spent four months being legally dead and had decided she was done with it. She had been the one, it turned out, who had slipped the page into my map case—she'd read my name on the hotel register, seen VANE, and gambled everything on a stranger with the right surname. The syndicate had erased her team. She had crawled out of the range with frostbite and a satchel of originals, and she had spent the winter copying them, notarizing them, and hiding them in three separate banks under three separate names, because she had learned from the Vanes what happens to a document kept in one place.

Verrin cross-examined her for two hours. She didn't blink. She had the originals, the syndicate's own internal correspondence, bought from a clerk who had grown a conscience or a grudge, and by the end of it the four lawyers in gray were passing notes that had stopped looking confident.

* * *

It was Osgood who ended it, near dusk.

She didn't make a speech. She took off her glasses, looked at the syndicate's table, and said: "The closure surveys were falsified. The mineral rights were conveyed by fraud. The deaths in the tunnel were concealed. The court so finds." She put the glasses back on. "The Vane deed stands. The corridor, the tunnel, and the workings revert to the grantee's line. Counsel for the company will remain to discuss damages."

The hall did not erupt. It did something better—it stood. Every rail crew in the district, on their feet, not cheering, just standing, the way you stand for a passing funeral train. Thirty-nine men who had been walled in and written off as a collapse, and a room full of people who ran trains past the mountain for twenty years and never knew, standing up because standing was the only thing left to do for them.

Tam was beside me, and their hand found my sleeve and held on. Odile stood too, on my other side, her ruined arm strapped against her chest, her father's compass in her good hand. She wasn't looking at the front of the hall. She was looking at the empty chair where Hale should have been, and her mouth was a flat line.

"Isandro," she said, low, under the noise. "He's not here because he doesn't need to be. Look at the doors."

I looked. The hall's two doors were open, and in the far one, half in shadow, a man in a good coat stood watching—not the magistrate, not the crowd.

Me.

He raised one hand, not quite a wave, and turned, and was gone into the yard, where a private car I recognized from a telegram signature was already coupled to the evening express.

Corvus Hale had come all this way to watch his case die. And he had left smiling.

* * *

Outside, the platform lamps were coming on, and the first snowmelt was running loud in the culverts. Tam asked me what Hale's smile meant, and I told them the truth: I didn't know yet. But I had spent eleven years reading grades and curves and knowing what men intended from what they built, and a man who watches his empire fall and leaves smiling has built something else somewhere else.

Osgood's clerk caught up with us at the top of the platform steps, out of breath, holding an envelope with the syndicate's seal already broken.

"For the grantee," he said. "Hand-delivered an hour ago. The gentleman said you'd want it after, not before."

Inside was a single page in Hale's reasonable hand. One line.

*Now that you own the mountain, Mr. Vane—you'll want to know who sold it to them in the first place. Ask your family's lawyer. Ask about the second deed.*

I stood on the platform at Grayfall Junction with the hearing won and the paper shaking slightly in my hand, and for the first time in eleven years I understood that the past I'd been running from had a floor under it, and the floor had a door, and someone had just handed me the key.
