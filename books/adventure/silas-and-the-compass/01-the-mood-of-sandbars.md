## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river told you things before it showed you, and Beatrix Yarrow had spent eleven years learning to listen.

She stood at the Magpie's wheel with one hand resting on the brass rail and the other on the throttle, reading the water the way a priest reads scripture — slowly, with attention, expecting meaning in every line. Low-water season had stripped the Kestrel down to its bones. Where the spring floods had run forty feet deep and brown as strong tea, the river now moved shallow and clear over sand, and every sandbar announced itself in the language of ripples.

A V of standing waves meant rock. A long smooth tongue of water sliding into a darker patch meant depth enough to trust, but only just. And a faint seam of silver where the current split around something submerged — that meant a bar growing by the day, hungry, patient, waiting for a boat like hers to make one careless turn.

"There you are," she murmured, sighting the seam a half-mile downstream. She eased the throttle back a notch and swung the wheel two spokes to starboard, and the Magpie leaned obediently into the turn, her old diesel muttering below like a complaint that had gone on so long it had become a kind of companionship.

The Magpie was sixty feet of patched steel and stubbornness, built before the war for a shipping concern that had gone bankrupt twice and been resurrected three times. Her wheelhouse glass was a patchwork of replacements, her deck planking had been sanded down so many times the grain stood up like the ribs of a starved dog, and her engine — a beast she called the Bishop, because it was old, ornamental, and impossible to argue with — drank fuel the way a bishop drank wine. But she floated. She turned when Beatrix asked. And on this river, in this season, that was more than most boats could claim.

Behind her, the cargo deck sat half-empty: a few crates of dried fish bound for the upriver villages, a drum of kerosene, bolts of cloth, a coffin somebody in Marrow Bend had ordered for a grandmother who was, by all reports, in no particular hurry. Beatrix had taken the coffin partly out of respect and partly because the freight money was good and the grandmother's family had paid half up front, which on the Kestrel was a miracle on the order of loaves and fishes.

She needed the money. That was the plain, ugly truth of it, and she had stopped dressing it up in her own head. The Magpie carried a debt the way a mule carries a load — not because the mule understands the ledger, but because the load is strapped on and there is nowhere else to go.

Corven. The name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Aldous Corven owned the Corven Line — fourteen steam launches, two barges, a warehouse at the delta port of Grayharbor, and, through loans and liens and the patient machinery of paper, a piece of the Magpie that grew larger every season. He had bought her note three years back when the bank failed, and he had been unfailingly, unctuously courteous ever since. He called her *Captain Yarrow* in his letters. He sent her small gifts at the New Year — a case of engine oil, once a fine barometer he probably got free. He had never once raised his voice, because he had never once needed to. The interest did his talking for him.

*Another season,* she told herself, the way she told herself every season. *Another season and I'm clear.*

But the arithmetic never worked. Fuel, crew, repairs, the bribes at the locks — the river took its cut, and Corven took his, and what remained was enough to keep the Magpie's belly full and Beatrix's own half-full, and not one coin more. She was fifty-one years old and she owned the shirt she stood in, the knife in her boot, and one-third of a boat the bank of Aldous Corven owned the rest of.

She pushed the thought down where she kept it, under the waterline of her attention, and gave the wheel her full mind again, because the river did not care about her debts and would drown her just as cheerfully rich or poor.

* * *

The morning wore on. The Kestrel ran wide and lazy here in the middle reach, a mile from bank to bank in places, the water so low that whole islands had surfaced — new islands, unnamed, that would drown again with the monsoon and be forgotten. Herons stood in the shallows on one leg like small disapproving clerks. A crocodile the length of her dinghy slid off a mudbank at her approach and vanished without a ripple, which Beatrix respected professionally.

She ran the Magpie alone these days. Deckhand, engineer, pilot, cook — the whole crew was one weathered woman with gray coming in at her temples and hands that looked like they'd been carved from teak and then used hard. She'd had a deckhand last season, a lanky boy named Fitch who'd gone off to work the paddle steamers out of Grayharbor for triple wages, and she couldn't blame him. She'd given him a good reference and a bottle of rum and told him if he ever got tired of being a small part of something large, the Magpie would take him back as a large part of something small.

She talked to herself on the boat, or to the boat — the distinction had blurred years ago.

"Easy, girl," she said as the bow nosed past the bar, the sounder's lead line showing six feet where yesterday it had shown five and a half. "I see it. I see it."

The bar was growing. She made a note of it in the battered logbook she kept chained to the chart table — *Bar at Mile 212 growing, channel shifting north, mark on chart* — because a pilot's memory was good but a pilot's chart was better, and someday she'd be coming downriver in the dark or in the rain, and that note would be the difference between arriving and becoming a story other pilots told over drinks.

That was the work, when it was good. The river was a puzzle that reassembled itself every night, and she was one of perhaps two dozen people alive who could solve it at speed in the fog. There was pride in that. There was even, on certain golden evenings when the light came slantwise down the water and the Bishop ran smooth and the banks unrolled green and endless, something close to joy.

But joy, she had learned, was a fair-weather thing. Underneath it ran the other current — the one that never stopped moving. The debt. The sameness. The sense that she was a good animal in a small pen, running the same eleven miles of river the same way, year after year, while the world beyond the bluffs did whatever the world did without her.

Restless. That was the word her mother would have used, and her mother had not meant it as a compliment. *A restless woman drowns, Bea. The river keeps the restless.* Her mother had drowned, in the end, though not from restlessness — from a flooded crossing and a bad ferryman, which was its own lesson about the river that Beatrix had never quite managed to learn from.

She checked her watch. Eleven miles to the Marrow Bend landing, where she'd take on the coffin and a load of charcoal, and then the long haul up to the head of navigation at Fort Calloway. Ten days, maybe twelve, if the weather held — and the weather would not hold. The air had that pressed, bruised feeling it got before the monsoon, the sky a flat pewter that seemed to be waiting for permission. The almanac said the rains were three weeks out. The almanac, Beatrix had learned, was a work of fiction with numbers in it.

* * *

She made Marrow Bend by early afternoon, backing the Magpie against the current with a touch she was quietly vain about — no bump, no scrape, just the old hull kissing the pilings as gently as a moth landing. The landing was a shelf of rotted planks, a coal-black heap of charcoal sacks under tarpaulins, and a scatter of houses on stilts with laundry strung between them like signal flags of surrender.

The family with the coffin were waiting, as promised, and the coffin itself turned out to be a handsome piece of work, polished camphor wood with brass fittings, and the grandmother turned out to be alive and sitting on the porch, supervising.

"She wants to see it loaded," the grandson explained, embarrassed. "She says she wants to make sure it's a good one."

"Let her look," Beatrix said. "It's her boat ride, near enough."

The old woman inspected the coffin, found it satisfactory, inspected Beatrix, and found her less so.

"You run that boat alone?" the grandmother demanded. "A woman your age?"

"A woman my age runs it better than a man any age," Beatrix said, and the old woman cackled and pronounced her a liar but a polite one, and paid the balance in notes that were, Beatrix noted with a private wince, the old issue — the ones Corven's warehouses had started discounting by five percent, because Corven's warehouses could.

The charcoal went aboard in ninety minutes, forty sacks that made the Magpie sit a hand's width lower in the water, which mattered on a river running five and six feet over the bars. Beatrix recalculated her channels in her head as the last sack came over the rail. Deeper draft, shallower water. The margin between her keel and the sand was getting thin, and it would get thinner all the way upriver.

That was low-water season. Everything was thin. The margin, the money, the patience.

She was casting off when the boy came down to the landing — a fisherman's son, maybe fifteen, breathless, holding a folded newspaper like it was something that might bite.

"Captain Yarrow? Captain, you're going up past the Narrows, yes? To Fort Calloway?"

"I'm going to Mile 300, and then we'll see what the river says."

"There was men." He glanced over his shoulder, and Beatrix followed the glance up the slope to the houses, where a couple of the fisher-folk had stopped their work to watch, and there was something in the way they watched that she didn't like. Something careful. "Government men. A survey team. They were at the camp above the Narrows, mapping the channel. My father carried fish up to them twice." The boy swallowed. "Nobody's carried fish up there in nine days. And the smoke's stopped."

"Smoke stops when a camp moves on."

"They didn't move on." The boy's voice dropped. "Their boat's still tied at the landing. My father saw it from the water. Still tied, still painted, nobody on it. And there was a launch come down from up there two nights back, big and black, running without lights, and it didn't stop at any landing the whole way down." He held out the newspaper. "It's in here. They're saying the survey team got recalled. But my father says boats that get recalled take their boats with them."

Beatrix took the paper. It was a Grayharbor sheet, three days old, and the item was four lines long on the inside page: *Government survey operations on the upper Kestrel have been concluded ahead of schedule, according to the Office of Interior Survey. Results are pending review.* No names. No dates. *Concluded ahead of schedule.* She'd been around official language long enough to know that phrase was what officials wrote when they meant *we would prefer not to discuss it.*

"Thank you," she said, and gave the boy a coin, and he took it and lingered, wanting to say something more, and then didn't, and went back up the slope. The fisher-folk turned back to their nets with the deliberate unconcern of people who had decided a thing was not their business and were rehearsing the decision.

Beatrix stood on her afterdeck a moment, the newspaper folded in her fist.

Nine days. A boat tied and empty. A black launch running dark at night. She turned it over once, the way she'd turn a strange piece of driftwood in her hands, looking for the shape of it — and then she put it down, deliberately, because it was not her business either. She had charcoal to deliver and a debt to feed and a river to run, and the business of government men was government men's business, and every year of her life on this water had taught her the same lesson her mother had: *the river keeps the restless.* Mind your channel. Mind your draft. Mind your own hull.

She went forward, cast off the lines, and took the Magpie out into the current.

* * *

The afternoon light went long and coppery, and the river turned to hammered bronze, and for a while the work was just the work — the wheel, the sounder, the banks sliding past, a kingfisher stitching blue thread along the waterline. She ran the Narrows at slack water with the chart open on her knee, threading the channel between the drowned trees, and came out the far side with the Bishop humming and the sun going down behind the western bluffs like a coin dropped into a well.

She anchored at dusk in the lee of Heron Island, in the deep still pocket she'd used a hundred times, and cooked rice and dried fish on the little stove in the wheelhouse, and ate standing up, watching the water go dark.

The newspaper lay on the chart table where she'd dropped it. *Concluded ahead of schedule.*

She was tired. She was always tired; tired was the water she swam in. But under the tired, that other current moved, the restless one, and tonight it was moving stronger than usual, and she knew why. It wasn't the boy's story, or not only. It was the whole shape of her life seen suddenly from a little distance, the way you see the river whole from a bluff top: the same loops, the same bars, the same water going the same way to the same sea. Eleven years. Another season. Another season and I'm clear. The sentence had become a prayer she no longer expected to be answered, and prayers like that curdle.

She was fifty-one years old, and the most honest map of her life was the chart of the Kestrel, and every year the channels shifted and every year she stayed exactly where she was.

The lamp hissed. The Bishop ticked as it cooled. Out on the water, a fish jumped and slapped back down, and the sound was very loud in the dark.

Beatrix finished her rice, washed the bowl in a splash of river water, and took up the logbook to make her entry. *Anchored Heron Island, 19:40. Weather holding. Charcoal aboard, 40 sacks. Bar at 212 growing.* Her pen hovered. Then, because the log was hers and the boat was hers and the dark was hers, she added a line she'd never written in eleven years of logs:

*Something wrong upriver.*

She looked at the words a long moment. Then she closed the book on them and turned down the lamp.

She was almost asleep in the wheelhouse bunk when she heard it — a sound she knew the way she knew her own pulse, and which had no business being on the river at night in low-water season. An engine. Not the putter of a fisherman's longtail, but the deep, even thrum of a heavy launch, running upstream, running hard.

She came up off the bunk and to the dark glass in time to see it pass, far out in the channel, a long black shape without a single light showing, pushing a bow-wave that glimmered faintly where no glimmer should be. It was running against the current at a speed that would tear the props off anything she'd ever piloted, and it was headed upriver, toward the Narrows, toward the camp where the smoke had stopped.

It passed and was gone, and the river closed over the sound of it, and Beatrix stood in the dark a long while with her hand on the cold glass.

At dawn she'd raise anchor and continue upriver, because charcoal was charcoal and debts were debts. But she knew, with the certainty she felt about bars and channels, that the river had just changed its course under her — that the black launch, the empty boat, the four careful lines in the newspaper were all one thing, one current, and that sooner or later it would swing wide and take her in.

She went back to her bunk. She did not sleep.

At first light, there was a boat coming downriver toward her anchorage — a small, fast government launch, white-hulled, flying the colors, with a single figure standing at its bow. And it was coming straight for the Magpie.
