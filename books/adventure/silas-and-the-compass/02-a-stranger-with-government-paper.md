## 2. A Stranger with Government Paper

The stranger came down the dock road at the hour when the river turns to hammered tin and the mosquitoes rise like smoke off the reed beds. Beatrix Yarrow saw him from the wheelhouse of the *Magpie*, where she was doing what she did every evening in low-water season: arguing with her boat.

She was on her knees in the engine bay with a wrench in one hand and a rag in her teeth, chasing a seep around the packing gland that had been threatening to become a leak since before the rains left. The *Magpie* was forty-one years old, older than she was by a decade and a half, and she had the temperament of any woman of that age who has worked hard her whole life and been thanked for it rarely. She leaked where she pleased. She started when she felt like it. And every year Corven's factor came down to the dock with his ledger and his soft voice and reminded Beatrix exactly how much she owed, and every year the number grew by just enough to keep her rowing.

"Third time this season," she told the gland, tightening the nut a quarter turn. "You want to sink me, do it in deep water where I can at least salvage the brass."

The gland held. It would hold until Thursday, probably. Beatrix spat the rag into her palm, wiped her hands, and stood up through the engine hatch in time to see the stranger stop at the head of her gangplank and look at her boat the way men looked at horses they intended to buy.

He was wrong for the river in about six ways. His boots were city boots, leather soled, no mud on them yet. His coat was good wool, cut for a man who sat indoors, and it was already too warm for the evening, which meant he'd dressed for an office and then traveled in it. He carried a canvas satchel slung crosswise like a soldier, but the way he carried it said clerk, not soldier — strap adjusted twice, buckles squared, everything on him squared, as if disorder were a disease he'd been vaccinated against young. He was maybe thirty-five, lean without being hard, with a face that might have been handsome if it ever relaxed: long jaw, close-cropped dark hair going gray early at the temples, eyes the color of river water over sand — pale, cold, and moving all the time.

And he was standing at the top of her plank reading a paper.

Beatrix knew that posture. Everyone on the Kestrel knew that posture. A man with government paper at a private dock meant one of three things: taxes, seizure, or trouble looking for someone to carry it upriver. Two of those cost her money. The third paid, sometimes, but never cleanly.

She came down out of the wheelhouse, took the ladder to the deck, and crossed to the rail with the unhurried stride of a woman who had learned that officials could be made to wait and liked being seen to make them. She leaned on the rail, arms folded, and let him finish his reading.

"You're moored over my survey marker," he said finally, when he noticed her watching. "Stake 14. I need to sight it."

"That stake's been under six feet of silt since the flood two years back," Beatrix said. "You can sight whatever you like. You'll be sighting mud."

His jaw tightened a fraction. "Then I'll note it as obstructed."

"You'll note it however you like, Mr.—?"

"Crane." He produced a card from inside his coat with a motion so smooth it must have been practiced in front of a mirror. "Silas Crane. Office of Territorial Survey."

Beatrix read the card without taking it. Heavy stock, engraved border, a seal stamped in ink the color of dried blood. She'd seen a hundred cards like it and trusted none of them. Half the men who'd handed her such cards had been exactly what they claimed; the other half had been smugglers wearing the government like a stolen coat, and there was no way to tell them apart until your cargo was impounded or your crew was dead.

"Captain Yarrow," Crane said. "I'm told you're the only pilot on this stretch who'll run above Sorrow Bar this late in the season."

"Told by whom?"

"A shipping agent in Marrow's Landing. He described you as expensive, difficult, and irreplaceable. I found the combination credible."

That got half a smile out of her, the first thing she'd given him free. "He undersold the difficult."

"I have a commission," Crane said, and here he shifted, planting his feet slightly apart, weight even, the stance of a man delivering testimony. "Six weeks ago a survey team went upriver to establish benchmarks along the middle Kestrel. Nine people. Engineers, chainmen, a cook, a camp guard. They were due back on the twentieth. They didn't come. Their last confirmed position is a camp at Mile 212, near the old trading post at Harker's Bend. I'm to go up, assess, recover instruments and records, and determine what happened to them."

"And you need me because?"

"The department has launches."

"The department has launches," Beatrix agreed, "and the department's pilots run the channels the department has charted. Which are the channels that were safe ten years ago." She unfolded one arm and pointed upstream, toward the haze where the river braided wide and shallow and treacherous around bars that moved with every freshet. "Low water's come early this year. The channel above Sorrow Bar has migrated a quarter mile east since spring. Your charts will kill you, Mr. Crane. I won't."

"So I've been advised." He didn't blink at the bluntness; he seemed to file it somewhere. "Which is why I'm at your dock instead of the government pier."

*Why are you really at my dock,* Beatrix thought, and said nothing, because the answer would come out on its own if she gave it room. Men like this always talked too much eventually. It was a flaw in the breed.

"What happened to the team?" she asked instead. "Officially."

"Officially, unknown. Possibly flooded out. The monsoon's edge is early; their camp was low-lying. Possibly they relocated and failed to report. Possibly—" a pause, measured like everything else about him, "—something else."

"Something else," Beatrix repeated. "You mean foul play."

"I mean I don't know, and I don't guess. I document."

"You document," she said, tasting it. "And when you document something the department doesn't want documented? What happens to the paper then?"

Crane looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind the pale eyes — not offense; assessment. Recalculation. "That," he said, "is a fair question, and I notice you asked it before discussing price."

"I always discuss character before price. Price you can renegotiate. Character you're stuck with."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face, there and gone like a fish rising. "Then we understand each other's methods."

"We don't understand anything yet. Show me the warrant."

He hesitated — a real hesitation, a beat too long, the kind that tells you more than the document itself — and then drew a folded paper from the satchel and passed it across the rail. Beatrix read it standing right there, in the last copper light, holding it at arm's length the way she read everything, because her eyes had been going since she was thirty and she refused to admit it.

It was a warrant of inquiry, signed by a deputy commissioner whose name she recognized the way you recognize a name on a wanted poster. It authorized Crane to charter vessels, requisition supplies, examine records, and compel assistance. It did not authorize him to arrest anyone, which meant it was worth exactly as much as the man carrying it and not a penny more.

"Compel assistance," she read aloud. "That's charming. Compel me how?"

"It's a formality. I'd rather hire you than compel you."

"I'd rather you tried to compel me, actually. Then I'd know precisely how much your department respects the people it works through." She folded the warrant along its original creases and handed it back. "Terms. You're hiring the boat, the pilot, and the discretion. Not the silence — I don't sell silence, it's bad collateral — but the discretion. If I say we anchor, we anchor. If I say the channel's wrong, we turn around, and you pay for the day anyway. You eat what I eat, you stand watch when I tell you, and you touch nothing on this boat without asking, including the wheel, especially the wheel."

"Agreed."

"I haven't named a price."

"I assumed the list would continue."

"It will. Provisions are yours. Fuel is yours. Any damage short of my own negligence is yours. And the rate is eight a day, plus twenty now, non-refundable, for the simple reason that men with warrants have a way of finding cheaper boats once their business changes."

Crane didn't flinch at the number, which told her he'd been authorized well above it, which told her the department considered this important, which told her nothing good. He counted the notes out from a flat wallet — crisp territorial scrip, twenties, the ink still sharp — and laid them on the rail cap under a stone from her ballast box so the evening wind couldn't take them.

"How many days?" she asked.

"Up in four if the river allows. A week on site. Down in four."

"Fifteen days, weather permitting. If the monsoon closes the river, we shelter and the clock stops. Nobody pays me to die on schedule."

"Reasonable." He gathered the notes himself and handed them to her directly, hand to hand, and his grip was dry and brief and careful, a handshake like a signature. "One condition of mine, Captain."

"Name it."

"When we reach the camp, I work the site first. Before anyone walks through it, before anything is moved. If there's evidence of what happened to nine people, I want it before the river or the rain or anyone else's boot heels take it away."

Beatrix studied him. Up close, in the failing light, the clerk's neatness fell away a little and she could see the thing underneath it: a kind of hunger, tightly governed, the hunger of a man who had spent years inside an institution and still believed — or needed to believe — that the institution could be made to tell the truth. She'd known that disease before. It was fatal more often than not. But it wasn't corruption, and on the Kestrel, that put him ahead of most men with paper.

"Your site, your rules," she said. "My river, mine."

"Then we have a bargain."

"We have a bargain," she said, "pending one thing."

He waited.

"You sleep aboard tonight, and we cast off at first light. Not because I'm eager. Because I want to watch you for twelve hours before I'm sealed in a steel hull with you for fifteen days. I want to see how you treat a cook, how you handle a delay, whether you drink past the second glass, and whether you talk in your sleep. Fifteen days is a marriage, Mr. Crane. Shorter, and with worse food. But the principle holds."

"That's—"

"Intrusive. Yes. It's also the only due diligence available on this river, and you strike me as a man who respects due diligence." She pushed off the rail and straightened to her full height, which was not considerable, and which had never once mattered. "There's a bunk in the forward cabin. It smells of diesel and the last engineer who used it, who was a better man than either of us. Blankets are in the locker. Supper's at seven, if you want it — rice, dried fish, and chili sauce hot enough to make a grown man honest. Most of my passengers find it loosens the tongue."

"I'll take supper," Crane said. He shouldered the satchel. "And the bunk. And the observation period. For what it's worth, Captain, I intend to observe you too."

"Good," Beatrix said. "Then neither of us will be disappointed, because we'll both see exactly what's there."

* * *

Supper was rice, dried fish, and chili sauce, and Crane ate all of it, including the sauce, without comment, which raised him half a point in her ledger. Most officials picked at river food like it was a personal insult. He ate like a man who had learned somewhere — a posting, a war, a childhood with lean years in it — that you ate what was in front of you and said thank you after.

They sat in the wheelhouse because it was the only room aboard with chairs, such as they were, and because Beatrix liked to keep an eye on the water while she talked. The river had gone from tin to black glass, and the first stars were out, and far upstream there was heat lightning, silent, pulsing behind the hills like a lamp behind a curtain — the monsoon clearing its throat, weeks early.

"Tell me about the team," Beatrix said, ladling herself more rice. "Nine people doesn't vanish. Rivers swallow two, three. Nine is a decision."

"Or an accident with a high body count. Flash floods take whole camps. It happens."

"Not in low water. Not six weeks ago. You know that as well as I do — your own hydrology tables would say so." She pointed her spoon at the window, upstream. "So either your department sent nine people into a river that was safe, which makes them fools, or something happened to them that wasn't the river, which makes it interesting. Departments don't spend money investigating fool's accidents. Somebody upstairs thinks it wasn't an accident. Who?"

Crane set down his fork with the care of a man disarming something. "You ask questions like an investigator."

"I ask questions like a woman who's about to be upriver of nowhere with you when the answers matter. Who signed the warrant?"

"A Deputy Commissioner named Halloran."

"Halloran." She let the name sit. "Halloran's office signs the ore transport concessions too, doesn't it? Out of the eastern ranges?"

Crane went very still. It was the stillness of a man discovering that the person across the table had already opened a drawer he thought was locked. "Where did you hear that?"

"River talk. Concessions, ore, Halloran — it all comes down the water eventually, usually attached to barges that fly the flag and carry things the manifests don't mention." She shrugged. "I don't know anything. I hear things. There's a difference, and the difference is deniability, which is the only currency out here that doesn't lose value."

For a moment he said nothing at all. Then, quietly, in a voice lower than the one he'd used on the dock: "If I answer that question honestly, Captain, I'd be speculating. And I told you I don't speculate."

"But you suspect."

"Everyone suspects. Suspicion is cheap. Evidence is what costs." He turned his fork over, tapped the tines once against the plate — a nervous habit surfacing through the polish, the first unpolished thing she'd seen him do. "Here's what I can give you that isn't speculation. The team's radio went silent on a clear night. Their final transmission was routine — position, depth soundings, all normal. Then nothing. When the relief party's launch went up two weeks later to check, it turned back at Sorrow Bar. Mechanical trouble, the log says."

"You believe the log?"

"I believe mechanical trouble is the most useful failure in the world. It explains anything and proves nothing." He met her eyes. "I requested this commission, Captain. Nobody assigned it to me. I asked for it because the gaps in the record bothered me, and because I've learned that when a record has gaps shaped exactly like nine missing people, the gaps were cut on purpose."

Beatrix looked at him across the little table, at the pale eyes and the controlled hands and the hunger underneath, and felt the old familiar motion start in her chest — the tilt of the scale. On one side: Corven's debt, growing like silt; the factor's soft voice; the knowledge that the *Magpie*'s boiler ticket expired in the spring and no yard this side of the delta would extend credit to a woman already underwater. Eight a day for fifteen days was most of a season's margin. It might clear the worst of the principal. It might buy her another year of owning her own keel.

On the other side: everything she'd ever heard about men with warrants, and the specific smell of this one — the ore concessions, the name Halloran, the relief launch that turned back on a lie, the gaps cut on purpose. Upriver of Mile 200 there was no law except what you brought with you. If Crane's department was dirty, then she wasn't ferrying an investigator. She was ferrying a witness into the hands of whoever had erased nine people, and witnesses upriver of Mile 200 had a way of joining them.

But if he was clean —

If he was clean, then he was the loneliest kind of man on earth: an honest instrument in a dishonest machine, going up a dirty river with nothing but a warrant and a pilot, and the odds were that the river would keep him.

Either way, she realized, watching him stack his plate with unnecessary neatness, she was going to learn which it was long before Mile 212. Twelve hours of observation, she'd promised herself. She was beginning to suspect she wouldn't need twelve.

"First light," she said, standing, collecting the plates. "We cast off at dawn. Sleep light, Mr. Crane. On this boat, the engine wakes before I do, and she's louder."

"One more thing." He rose too, and paused in the wheelhouse doorway, half in the lamplight and half out of it. "The agent in Marrow's Landing said you were irreplaceable. I checked your name against the department's pilot registry before I came. You're not in it."

"No," Beatrix said. "I'm not."

"Why not?"

Because five years ago she'd refused to move a barge load she believed was contraband, and the refusal had cost her the license and gained her Corven's debt, and both facts lived in the same scar tissue she did not open for strangers. "Because I failed an integrity test," she said. "Mine. I declined a charter I shouldn't have taken, and the department declined to forgive me for having standards. You'll find the story in the registry's exclusion file, if you look. Read it tonight. Then decide in the morning whether you still want me."

She expected him to argue, or to promise not to look, or to perform some small diplomacy. Instead he nodded slowly, once, like a man adding a figure to a column and finding the sum improved.

"I'll read it," he said. "Good night, Captain."

* * *

She did not sleep well, which was usual, and dreamed of water, which was not.

In the dream the river was high and brown and carrying things downstream — branches, barrels, a hat, a lantern still burning as it turned over and over — and she stood on the *Magpie*'s roof trying to count the lanterns, because there were nine of them, or should have been, and she kept losing count in the chop. Somewhere below in the forward cabin a man slept whom she had known for less than a day, and in the dream she stood over the hatch listening to his breathing the way you listen to ice before you trust it with your weight.

She woke before the alarm, as she always did, to the gray hour when the river smoked and the birds began their arguments in the reeds. In the galley she built coffee strong enough to strip paint and carried a mug up to the wheelhouse, and there she found Crane already awake, already dressed, already standing at the front windows with the warrant spread open on the chart table beside her father's old river almanac, making notes in a small precise hand.

He'd been up, she judged, at least an hour. He'd read the exclusion file — she could tell because the folder she kept it in, wedged behind the barometer, had been moved and squared again, corners aligned, and because there was a new line in his notebook in that same small hand, and he covered it with his palm when she came in, not quickly, not guiltily, just the reflex of a man who kept his working thoughts to himself until they were finished.

"Well?" she said, setting his coffee down. "Do I pass?"

"You refused the charter," he said, "because the manifest listed agricultural machinery and the draft marks said otherwise. You reported it. The report was buried. Your license was suspended for 'unprofessional conduct,' defined nowhere. And you've been piloting on a lapsed coastal ticket and Corven's goodwill ever since." He looked up at her. "The file calls you a problem. It reads to me like a reference."

Beatrix drank her coffee and said nothing, because there was a lump in her throat the size of the word *reference* and she'd be damned before he saw it.

Below, the *Magpie*'s boiler ticked as it warmed, waking in its own time, loud as promised. The mist was lifting off the water in long rags. Upstream, the heat lightning had moved closer overnight, and the air had that pressure-cooker stillness that old pilots called elephant weather, though nobody remembered why.

"Cast off in twenty minutes," Beatrix said, draining the mug. "Get your kit stowed and lash everything twice. And Crane —"

"Captain?"

"When we're above Sorrow Bar, there's no turning back for a hundred miles. Whatever you're carrying besides paper and scrip — doubts, orders you haven't told me, a second warrant in that satchel — you'll want to show me before the channel narrows. Not after."

Crane held her gaze for a moment. Then he reached into the satchel, and Beatrix watched his hand, and the hand stopped halfway, hovering over something, and did not come out with it.

"After the bar," he said quietly. "There's something you should see. But I want to be sure of the channel first."

Beatrix smiled without warmth and rang the engine telegraph to slow ahead, and the *Magpie* answered the way she always did, grudgingly, then faithfully, and the dock slid away behind them into the mist.

Above Sorrow Bar, she thought, watching the water begin to shoal and braid ahead of them. Above Sorrow Bar, where the river keeps what it takes and gives back only what it pleases. Whatever he was hiding, the river would have it out of him before Mile 150. It always did.
