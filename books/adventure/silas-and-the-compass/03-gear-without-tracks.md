## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The river changed its voice two miles below the survey camp.

I had been standing at the rail of the *Magpie* for the better part of an hour, watching the banks narrow and steepen, when I realized what was different. For days the water had talked to itself — a low, continuous muttering over sandbars, the slap of current against hull, Beatrix's occasional curses at the channel. Now it had gone quiet in patches, pooling between gravel shoals like something holding its breath. The birds had stopped too. No herons stalking the shallows, no kingfishers working the deadfall. Just the creak of the boat's timbers and the far-off complaint of the engine, laboring against a current that seemed to have lost interest in us.

"You feel that?" Beatrix said.

She stood at the wheel with one hand resting on it, not gripping, the way a rider rests a hand on a horse's neck. She hadn't looked away from the water since we'd passed the last fish weir.

"I was about to ask you."

"River goes quiet like this before it gives something up." She chewed the inside of her cheek. "Or before it takes something. Depends on the day."

We came around a bend where a clay bluff leaned out over the channel, and there it was: the camp.

I had built a picture of it in my head during the long pull upstream — the way an investigator builds any picture, from fragments and habit. I'd imagined wreckage. Torn canvas, scattered crates, the archaeology of panic. What I saw instead was order.

Six tents on the bench above the high-water line, ridge lines taut, guy ropes still staked. A cook shelter with its fly spread and its iron stove sitting square on flat stones. The government launch moored at a timber landing, riding light and level, its paint unmarked. A flagpole with nothing on it — someone had taken the flag, or it had been lowered — but everything else exactly where discipline would put it.

Nobody moved among the tents.

Beatrix brought the *Magpie* alongside the landing with the same unhurried precision she'd shown all week, lines out, fenders down, engine reversed to kiss the pilings. She made fast without being told to. Then she stood at the rail beside me, and for a long moment neither of us stepped off.

"No smoke," she said finally. "Cook fire's cold. Ash is gray all the way through — been out days, not hours." Her eyes went along the beach, the treeline, the bluff top. "No tracks coming down to the water. None going up into the bush that I can see from here."

"You read ground the way you read water?"

"Worse. Water lies less." She untied the boarding net and dropped it over the side. "You first, Mr. Crane. It's your warrant."

It wasn't my warrant, strictly speaking. It was the Department's warrant, issued under a seal I'd spent eleven years serving and four days learning to doubt. But I climbed down the net onto the landing planks because she was right, and because my legs needed the decision made for them.

The boards were dry. That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't know yet to call it wrong. Dry planks meant no rain for days, which matched the sky, which meant nothing by itself. The second wrong thing came as I crossed the beach: my boots left prints in the pale sand, sharp-edged, and around them the sand was smooth as poured sugar. Smooth everywhere. Six men had lived on this bench for three weeks. They had hauled water, cut firewood, dragged crates up from the landing. Ground remembers that kind of traffic for weeks in dry season. This ground remembered nothing.

Behind me I heard Beatrix come ashore, and heard her stop, and heard the small sound she made — not a word, just air moving through teeth.

"Silas," she said. She'd started using my name somewhere past the second night, dropping the *Mr.* without ceremony, and I found I liked the sound of it more than was professionally wise. "Come look at this."

She was crouched at the edge of the camp proper, at a trampled patch where the brush met the bench. A latrine trench, screened by a canvas windbreak. Beside it, a woodpile, cut and stacked and half consumed. And pressed into the dirt at the woodpile's foot, a boot heel — deep, deliberate, the print of a man planting his weight.

One print. Nothing leading to it. Nothing leading away.

"It rained?" I asked, already knowing.

"Not here. Not for six days. I'd have run for cover myself." She rose, wiping her hands on her trousers, and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to catalog: the squint she wore when the river showed her something she didn't like. "Somebody swept this camp, Silas. Broom and rake work, maybe hands and branches. Somebody walked in, made one print they couldn't be bothered with, and swept every other trace of themselves out."

"Somebody who wasn't the survey team."

"The team's prints would've been old. Weathered edges. These—" she nodded at the sand along our own path "—these are fresh enough to have been made after the last rain. After your people vanished."

I took out my notebook. It was a habit so old it had stopped feeling like a habit; my hand opened the book and wet the pencil stub before my mind caught up. *Camp reached 0940. Site orderly. No sign of struggle. Ground disturbed post-departure — evidence of deliberate erasure.*

Deliberate erasure. I wrote the phrase and then stared at it, because it was the phrase I'd been circling since the Department gave me the file, and now it lay on the page in my own handwriting, plain as a confession.

* * *

We worked the camp the way I'd been taught to work a site: outside in, low to high, touch nothing twice.

The tents came first. Six of them, and the file said seven men — the seventh, a junior instrument man named Okafor, had gone downriver on leave the week before the disappearance, which was why the Department's arithmetic had never quite balanced and why some clerk had written *personnel discrepancy — resolved* in the margin with the serene confidence of a man resolving nothing.

Inside each tent: bedroll rolled tight, kit stowed, personal effects squared away. Watches on crates. Boots paired. One man had left a paperback novel face-down on his cot, spine cracked to hold his place, as if he'd stepped out to check the cook fire and meant to come back. Another had pinned a photograph to the tent wall — a woman and two children on a pier, sun-flared, ordinary — and nobody fleeing through the bush in the night leaves a photograph behind unless they're leaving in a hurry or not leaving at all.

No blood anywhere. I want to be precise about that, because later, when the Department asked me for my findings, precision was all I had left to offer. No blood on the canvas, none in the sand, none on the instruments, none in the cook shelter where a struggle would have left its signature in flour and spilled salt and knocked-over stools. The stove was clean. The dishes were washed and stacked. There was food in the storage lockers, protected from the damp with the fussy care of a mess sergeant: rice in tins, salt pork in cloth, a sack of beans tied at the neck.

Men do not abandon a camp like this. Men surprised in the night by violence leave overturned lamps and scattered cartridges and the wide-eyed wreckage of fear. Men ordered to leave leave this — neatness as obedience, the last discipline of men who believed, right up until the moment they stopped believing, that the institution they served would protect them.

Or men marched out at gunpoint, quiet, compliant, told that resistance would be noted in their files.

The instruments were the next wrong thing. A survey team's theodolite is worth more than most men make in a year; the levels and chains and stadia rods are the tools of their trade and the proof of their work. All of it sat in its cases in the instrument tent, latched, oiled, arranged on a folding table with labels facing out. I opened each case and checked the contents against the manifest I'd memorized from the file. Everything present. Everything accounted for.

Except paper.

A survey produces paper the way a mill produces dust. Field books. Level notes. Traverse sheets. Reductions. Daily position reports. Seven men, twenty-three days in the field, and the manifest listed eleven folios of maps and data — the crown of the whole expedition, the reason the Department had spent its money and the government its patience.

I counted the map chest twice. Ten folios, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with the Department's wax, stacked in perfect order.

Eleven were listed.

I sat back on my heels in the doorway of the instrument tent and let the arithmetic sit in my chest like swallowed stone. Thieves take everything portable. Vandals take what's easy. Fire takes what it's given. But someone had come into this camp — after the team left, after the sweeping, in daylight or lantern-light, with time and patience — opened a locked chest, known precisely which folio mattered, and taken only that one. Leaving ten others as alibi. Leaving the instruments as set dressing. Leaving the camp itself as a stage, dressed and lit, waiting for an investigator like me to walk onto it and see exactly what he was meant to see.

Except whoever dressed the stage had made one mistake. Or rather, had made a hundred small correct choices and one omission they couldn't have foreseen: they hadn't known that I'd memorized the manifest. They hadn't known that the missing folio would be visible only as an absence — a gap in a numbered sequence, a weight missing from a chest, a silence in a list.

The eleventh folio. The one the file described, in a clerk's bland hand, as *Folio XI — Channel Survey, Upper Reaches, with geological annotations.*

Geological annotations. On a hydrographic survey. I turned that over as I walked the camp's perimeter, and it turned over again on its own, the way a body turns in slow water. Why would a river crew annotate rock? Unless the rock was the point. Unless the channels were the excuse and the annotations were the cargo.

Beatrix found me at the flagpole. I'd been standing there longer than I intended, looking at the bare halyard, trying to make it mean something.

"You've got the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The face of a man doing sums he doesn't like." She came to stand beside me, arms folded, watching the river instead of me, which I'd learned was how she thought best. "Tell me the sums."

"Eleven folios in the manifest. Ten in the chest. Someone took one — only one — and they took it after the team disappeared, after the ground was swept. Everything else was left to look untouched."

"And the team?"

"Seven men. No bodies. No blood. No tracks out. Bedrolls rolled, dishes washed, boots paired." I heard my own voice go flat and official, and hated it, and kept talking anyway because the flatness was the only thing keeping the other thing — the cold spreading thing — at arm's length. "This wasn't a disaster, Beatrix. Disasters are messy. This was an erasure. Somebody removed seven trained men from a government camp without disturbing a teacup, and then tidied the scene for whoever came looking."

She was quiet for a while. Below us the river slid past, brown-green and patient, carrying its silt and its secrets down toward the delta with the indifference of a creature that has outlived every empire built on its banks.

"My father ran cargo on this river thirty years," she said at last. "He used to say there's two kinds of quiet on the water. The quiet of empty, and the quiet of held breath. Empty quiet means nobody's here. Held breath means everybody's hiding." She turned and looked up at me, and her eyes were the color of the water in shadow. "This camp is held-breath quiet, Silas. Whatever happened to your survey team, they didn't wander off, and they didn't drown, and they didn't desert. Deserters take their boots."

I wrote that down too. *Deserters take their boots.* It sounded, in her mouth, like the truest sentence spoken on this bench in weeks.

* * *

By late afternoon I had photographed what could be photographed, bagged the one boot heel print with plaster from my kit, and sealed the ten remaining folios in a canvas case — evidence, though evidence of what, and for whom, I was no longer sure. Beatrix prowled the landing and the beach the whole time, reading the waterline, the driftwood, the silt lines, muttering to herself in the shorthand of a woman who trusted the river's testimony more than any man's.

She called me down to the landing as the light went long and copper.

"Look at the launch."

The government boat rode at its moorings, and I'd given it a glance that morning and filed it as unremarkable. Now, seeing it through her eyes, I saw what she saw. The fuel tank cap hung open on its chain. The bilge boards were pulled and stacked on the deck. The engine cowling was off, set aside with care.

"They drained it," I said slowly. "They took the fuel."

"Took the fuel, pulled the magneto points — look, they're gone — and left the rest pretty as a picture." She tapped the gunwale. "Your survey team didn't disable their own escape. Whoever came for them wanted the camp to look abandoned-by-order, tidy and official, but they also wanted to make sure nobody left under their own power afterward. Nobody comes back to a swept camp and finds a working boat."

"So the sweepers planned for visitors."

"Sweepers always plan for visitors. That's the difference between a sweeper and a murderer in a hurry." She straightened, and the last light caught the sweat on her jaw, and I understood suddenly that she was afraid — genuinely, coldly afraid — and was walking straight into the fear the way she walked into current, reading it, using it. "Question is, Mr. Crane, who sent the sweepers? Because it wasn't bandits. Bandits would've taken the instruments, the rifles, the boots off the sleeping men. This was institutional. This had paperwork behind it."

Institutional. The word landed in me like a hook finding flesh. Eleven years in the Department. Eleven years of believing that the seal on my warrant meant something clean, and here was its opposite number: the same machinery, running in reverse, grinding men into absence and filing the absence as resolved.

"We should go," I said. "There may be more to find upstream, but not at night, and not—"

That was when Beatrix went still.

Not the stillness of attention. The stillness of an animal that has caught a scent it hoped never to smell. Her head came up, her nostrils flared once, and her hand found the rail of the landing and gripped it.

Downstream, around the clay bluff, thin and blue and acrid, came the smell of woodsmoke. Not campfire smoke — too much of it, too sour, carrying under it another note I couldn't name and she clearly could, because her face changed as it reached her.

"Burning paper," she said quietly. "Somebody downstream is burning a lot of paper."

And beneath the smoke, faint but unmistakable on the evening air, drifting up the channel with the land breeze: the sound of an engine. More than one. Diesels, heavy and unhurried, the sound of boats that did not expect to be challenged by anything on this river.

Beatrix looked at me, and I looked at her, and the held-breath quiet of the empty camp suddenly made complete sense. We had not been the first investigators to reach this place. We were simply the first who hadn't been invited.

"Get aboard," she said, already moving, tearing the mooring lines free with both hands. "Whatever burned downriver, it's burning our way."
