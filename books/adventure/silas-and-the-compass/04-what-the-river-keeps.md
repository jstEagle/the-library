## 4. What the River Keeps

The diesels never came for us.

They ran all night somewhere downriver — I heard them through the hull of the *Magpie*, a low grumble carried up the water like a rumor, there and gone, there and gone — and then sometime before dawn they stopped, and the river went back to being only itself: rain on canvas, current on planking, the creak of my old boat thinking her slow thoughts against the mooring line.

I hadn't slept. I don't sleep well in strange anchorages, and this one was stranger than most. A survey camp with its tents still standing and its kettles still hanging and no one left to drink from them sits wrong in a body that has spent thirty years learning what rivers do with people. So I sat in the wheelhouse with a cup of tea gone cold in my hands and watched the camp across the shallows turn from black shapes to gray shapes to the sodden green reality of morning, and I listened to the water, because the water was the only witness left who would talk to me.

Rivers talk all night if you know the language. The *Magpie* talks too, in her own dialect of groans and ticks, and by first light she'd told me something I didn't like: a change in the eddy off the point below the camp, a new note in the way the backcurrent curled where it met the main channel. Something had shifted out there in the dark. Something the river had been holding had come loose.

"Crane," I said, and knocked once on the deck beam above the cabin where he'd bedded down. "Up. We've got company."

He came up the companionway with his coat half-on and his hair flattened on one side, looking like a man who'd been dreaming of government offices and woken into a nightmare with better scenery. He'd slept badly too. Good. A man who sleeps soundly at an empty murder camp is either a fool or one of them, and Silas Crane was neither.

"Company?" He scanned the river. Empty water to every horizon, gray under a low sky, mist standing on the far banks like the ghosts of trees. "I don't see—"

"Not that kind." I nodded at the eddy. "That kind."

It took him a moment. To be fair to him, it takes most people a moment; the eye goes looking for boats, for men, for things that move on purpose. It doesn't go looking for what the river gives back.

But then the light strengthened, and he saw it too.

* * *

It hung in the slack water off the point, turning slowly, face-down, arms drifting wide like a man surrendering to something. A brown jacket. Boots. One boot missing. The river had kept him for days — long enough for the water to have its way with him, long enough that I won't put in these pages what three days in warm low-season water does — but the river keeps what it's given and gives back what it must, and overnight the eddy had changed its mind.

I brought the *Magpie* in close with the current, easy, no engine noise, and hooked him alongside with the boathook before Crane could ask how I meant to do it. There's a knack to handling the dead. You learn it or you don't. I learned it young, on a stretch of the Kestrel above the falls where the water collects debts every spring.

We laid him on the afterdeck, and Crane knelt over him and was quiet for a long time.

"He's one of theirs," Crane said finally. "Survey team. That's their field jacket — khaki, government issue, the number stenciled on the—" His voice caught and steadied. "Number seven. This is Okafor. J. Okafor. He was listed as the team's boatman."

"You knew him?"

"I knew his name. I read the personnel file twice on the way upriver." Crane took off his hat. He was younger than me by fifteen years, maybe twenty, and he had the kind of face that shows everything it thinks, which is a liability in his line of work and probably the reason he was any good at it anyway. "I thought we were coming to find them lost. Stranded. Walked out overland when the supply run failed. That's what the file implied. That's what everyone implied."

"And now?"

"Now I think we're here to count them." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and furious. "One of eleven."

I crouched by the body, because someone had to look properly, and Crane couldn't, not yet, not without losing the thread of himself. The dead deserve a professional eye even when nobody official will ever thank you for it.

"Look at his wrists," I said.

Crane looked. The rope marks were plain enough even now, even after the river's long argument with the flesh: deep grooves, cut in clean, wrapped twice around each wrist and cinched hard enough to bite bone.

"That's not drowning gear," I said. "That's not a man who fell in with a line around him. Somebody tied him before he went in the water."

"Could he have—"

"Tied his own wrists? Twice around, cinched behind the knot so it bites?" I shook my head. "I've pulled eleven bodies out of this river in my life, Mr. Crane. Fishermen, drunks, one customs man nobody ever asked about. Drowned men look like drowned men. Their hands are open. Their wrists are bare. This man went into the water alive, trussed, and weighted."

"How do you know weighted?"

"Because of where he surfaced." I stood and pointed with my chin, upriver along the channel. "There's a scour hole below the camp point — forty feet deep if it's a yard, where the current digs out every dry season. If somebody wanted a body to stay found-never, that's where they'd drop it. Chain him to an engine block and slide him in. And it would've worked, except—" I shrugged. "Except the river moves everything eventually. The flood pulse last week shifted the scour. Rolled whatever was holding him loose enough to let him go. The river doesn't keep secrets, Mr. Crane. It just charges interest."

Crane stared at me. "You can read all that from an eddy?"

"I can read all that from thirty years of an eddy." I turned away from the body, because there's a limit to what a dead man should have to endure of the living discussing him. "Your government sent you upriver to find a lost survey team. Your government's own paper says lost. But your government's paper also says the camp was abandoned, and the camp wasn't abandoned — it was emptied, quick, mid-meal, mid-work. And now we've got one of your eleven surfacing with his wrists bound. You want to tell me again about the warrant in your pocket?"

* * *

We buried him. Not well — the ground was root-tangled and the rain had made the clay heavy as lead, and all we had was a camp entrenching tool and two sets of hands — but we buried him above the flood line, under the biggest tree, facing the water, because a boatman should face the water. Crane said some words. I don't know whose god they belonged to; they were quiet and formal and they sounded like they'd been written by a committee, and somehow that made them right for a government man. I added what I always add, which isn't words. It's a coin, dropped in the grave. Toll for the ferryman. My mother's custom, from a country I barely remember. The river takes its fee either way. Better to pay it forward.

While Crane filled the grave, I searched the camp again, properly this time, with the eyes the night had given me.

Everything I'd noticed yesterday I noticed worse today. The kettles. The instruments left in their cases — expensive brass and glass, the kind of kit that walks away within an hour of any camp going soft. Nobody loots a place where the owners left voluntarily and might come back. And the cook fire: burned down to white ash, cold, undisturbed, no food stores scattered or taken in haste. Men who flee take food. Men who are taken leave it.

And the maps. Crane had flagged it yesterday evening and I'd half-listened, tired and spooked and wanting my bunk. Now I listened with both ears while he laid the folios out on the survey table under the big tent and showed me the gap.

"Eleven folios issued," he said. "Field inventory says eleven returned-or-accounted-for was the standing order. Ten here." He tapped the ledger page, then the neat row of oilcloth map cases. "The missing folio is the ore-sampling record. The one folio in the whole set that says what's actually under the ground along this reach."

"Valuable?"

"To the right buyer? Extremely." He closed the ledger slowly. "Or dangerous. Depending on what it says."

"Which is why whoever emptied this camp took it and left the rest."

"Which is why." He looked at the row of cases the way a man looks at a door he knows opens onto a long fall. "Beatrix. What happens to us if the people who did this learn there's an investigator on the river asking after them?"

I almost laughed. Almost. "Mr. Crane, I heard those diesels last night. Whoever they belong to, they already know somebody's asking. The question isn't whether they learn. The question is whether they learn before we're downstream or after."

* * *

I gave the *Magpie* her morning look-over while Crane packed the evidence — the folios, the ledger, photographs he insisted on taking of the grave and the camp with a little folding camera he produced from his luggage like a magician's dove. She'd taken no harm in the night, but I checked her seams anyway, ran my palm along her strakes below the waterline where I could reach, talked to her the way I talk to her when I'm worried. Thirty-one years old, built of teak and stubbornness, mortgaged to her keel bolts to a man named Corven who smiles like a banker and counts like one too. Every plank of her is borrowed money. Some mornings I think she's the only honest thing in my life, and she isn't even mine.

That was the thought in my head when I straightened up and saw Crane standing at the rail, looking at the scour hole off the point, and I understood that the trip had changed shape overnight and we both felt it.

"Ten more," he said quietly. "Somewhere between here and the head of navigation, there are ten more. Or nine, if any of them ran. Either way—" He turned to me, and I'll give him this: his voice didn't shake. "Either way, this stopped being a search last night. It's an investigation now. A real one. Which means it's dangerous in a way the warrant doesn't cover."

"The warrant never covered anything," I said. "Paper's just paper until there's a gun behind it, and the guns on this river don't answer to your department. They answer to whoever paid for those diesels." I wiped my hands on a rag and looked him square. "So here's my price, and hear it plain, because I'm only saying it once. I'll take you upriver. I'll take you wherever the truth on this river is hiding, and I'll help you dig it out. But we do it my way — quiet, smart, reading the water ahead of us every mile. And if it comes to running, we run together, and neither of us stops to be brave. Brave men float face-down in eddies. I've hooked enough of them out."

Crane held out his hand. Government paper, government manners, and underneath both, something that had survived the night and the grave and was harder than either. "Together," he said. "My way got a man tied to an engine block. I'll take yours."

I shook it. His hand was steady. Mine surprised me by not being entirely.

Then I went forward to cast off, and that was when I saw it — small, easy to miss, the kind of thing the river offers only to people watching it the way I watch it.

Out past the point, riding the seam between the eddy and the main current, came a length of fresh-cut rope. New hemp, pale, hardly waterlogged at all. It rose on the boil of the scour hole, uncoiled on the surface for a heartbeat — frayed end, cut end, not worn-through, *cut* — and sank again.

Somebody had been back to the scour hole. Recently. Checking their work. Maybe last night, maybe in the dark, maybe on one of those unhurried diesels.

And the rope was cut from the *upstream* side.

Whatever they'd weighted into that hole besides Okafor, the river was still holding it — and the men who'd put it there were closer than we thought, and coming down the channel toward us with the current at their backs.

"Crane," I said, without turning, without raising my voice. "Get below and stay there. And bring the folios."

The mist upriver had begun to move against the wind.
