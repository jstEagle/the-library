## 5. Corven's Favor

The river narrowed above the camp, and the country changed its mind about being beautiful.

Beatrix felt the shift before she saw it — in the way the current leaned against the Magpie's hull like a hand testing a door, in the way the banks rose from mud flats to walls of red clay furred with vine. The water here had opinions. It wanted them gone, pushed back downstream, and it spent its strength saying so all day long.

She ran the wheel with two fingers and her whole attention. Up here the channel wandered like a drunk through gravel bars and drowned timber, and the charts — Corven's charts, bought secondhand from a dead man's estate — were ten years stale. So she read the water instead: the smooth glassy tongue where deep current ran, the wrinkled boil where a bar slept just under the surface waiting to open a hull like a letter.

"Bar on the left," she said, not turning. "See how the birds are standing on it? Water's so shallow they're standing."

Silas Crane stood at the rail behind her with his notebook out, writing down everything she said as though her mouth were an instrument of the government. He'd been doing it for two days. She found it less insulting than she'd expected. There was something almost humble in it — a man admitting he was ignorant and meaning to cure himself.

"You read it like text," he said.

"It is text. Every ripple's a word." She eased the wheel over and the Magpie slid left of the bar, her old engine muttering its complaint. "Problem is, the river edits itself every flood. You can't memorize it. You can only learn to listen fast."

"And Corven's charts?"

"Corven's charts are for the barges, the big drafted runs between the towns. Nobody who works this upper river uses paper." She glanced back at him. He had that look again — the one he probably thought was neutral, the one that was actually him deciding something. "You keep looking at me like I'm a page you're trying to turn."

"I'm wondering why a woman who reads water that well owes a man like Corven money."

The question landed where questions always landed — square in the middle of her, where the debt lived like a stone she'd swallowed years ago and never passed. She turned back to the wheel.

"Because boats cost," she said. "And because when my father died, the yard wouldn't release the Magpie's papers until the yard's debts were paid, and Corven bought those debts from the yard at a discount and held them against me at full price. That's not villainy. That's just commerce. It's what everyone does."

"It's what *he* does."

"He's done right by me more often than not." She heard her own voice go flat and defensive and hated it a little. "He advanced me engine parts last dry season when no bank would touch me. He doesn't squeeze. He waits. There's worse men on this river by the dozen."

"Mm," said Silas, and wrote something down.

She decided not to ask what. Some pages were better unturned.

* * *

Harrow Bend appeared in the early afternoon, materializing out of the heat shimmer the way river towns did — first the church spire leaning like a man listening hard, then the tin roofs flashing, then the smell: woodsmoke, fish, tar, and the sweet rot of fruit going soft in the market stalls. It was the last real settlement before the empty hundred miles where the survey team had vanished, which made it the last place to buy fuel, rope, coffee, and lies.

They needed all four.

She brought the Magpie in against the current, kissing the dock with barely a bump, and threw the lines herself because her deckhand was a government man with soft hands and she didn't trust him yet with anything that mattered. Silas hopped down anyway, took the stern line without being asked, and snubbed it off clean enough. She pretended not to notice that he was learning.

"Harrow Bend rules," she said, climbing onto the dock. "You buy nothing without asking the price twice. You drink nothing you didn't watch poured. And if anyone asks where we're bound, we're running timber salvage down at the Sump, boring as Sunday. Not one word about survey camps or warrants. This far upriver, government paper isn't authority. It's bait."

"For what?"

"For whatever wants to eat officials." She tucked her purse into her coat. "Stay within shouting distance."

The town had the loose-jointed feel of a place that existed only because the river allowed it. Dogs slept in the shade of the fuel depot. Two boys fished off a moored barge with lines they clearly never expected to catch anything. A woman sold sugared plantains from a tray balanced on her head and called out prices to Beatrix by name, which meant word of the Magpie's passage had run ahead of them, which meant the river's telegraph was working, which meant anything they did here would be known everywhere else by nightfall.

Beatrix started with the fuel depot because it was honest work and let her ears do their business while her hands pumped diesel. The depot man was old Marn, missing three fingers on his left hand from a winch accident he told strangers about in exchange for drinks. He filled her tanks slow and talked faster.

"Heard you went up past the Deadfall," he said. "Past the old camp."

"Salvage run," Beatrix said. "Sump's got a drowned barge worth cutting."

"Sump's got nothing but mosquitoes." Marn grinned with the few teeth he had left. "But sure. Salvage." He leaned closer over the pump, voice dropping. "You want to know who else came through asking after that camp?"

Beatrix kept her eyes on the fuel gauge and her pulse on a short leash. "Wouldn't mind knowing."

"Four men. Day before yesterday, maybe the day before that — time runs soft up here. Came down from upstream on a launch, black hull, twin screws, riding high like they weren't carrying much but trouble. Wore plain clothes but wore them wrong, you know? Boots too new. Guns under the coats, carried like they're used to them." Mann shook the nozzle, ticked the last gallons in. "Paid for fuel with Corven Shipping scrip."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once, the way it does when something breaks underwater.

"Scrip," Beatrix repeated.

"Corven scrip. Red stamps, the new issue. My cousin works the Corven depot at Salt Landing; I know the paper when I see it." Marn coiled the hose. "I figured you'd know them. Being Corven's boat and all. Thought maybe you were the advance party."

"We're not together," Beatrix said, and heard how carefully she said it.

Marn looked at her for a long moment with his one good eye and whatever wisdom lived in the other. "No," he said slowly. "I don't suppose you are. Because the way they asked about that camp wasn't salvage talk, girl. It was *search* talk. One of them had a photograph. Showed it around the landing, asked who'd seen a government skiff, six men, heading up. Asked it friendly. Friendly like a knife is friendly."

"What'd people tell them?"

"Nothing. Nobody'd seen your survey men come through, which is its own kind of answer, ain't it?" He wiped his hands on a rag gone gray with age. "Six men don't pass Harrow Bend without forty people seeing them. If nobody saw them, they didn't come this way — or they came this way and stopped being six men somewhere close."

Beatrix paid him, counted her change without seeing it, and stood a moment in the depot's oil-smelling shade while her thoughts rearranged themselves into a shape she didn't like.

Corven's men. Corven's scrip. Ahead of them, not behind. Asking after the survey team with photographs and new boots.

There were innocent explanations. There always were. Corven ran half the shipping on the upper Kestrel; his men went everywhere; a launch could be on any errand — cargo inspection, debt collection, a hundred dull things. Scrip circulated. Boots could be new for ordinary reasons.

But four men in plain clothes carrying guns wrong, showing a photograph of vanished government men, two days ahead of the investigator sent to find them?

That wasn't an errand. That was a race. And someone had known the race had started before she had.

* * *

She found Silas at the market, doing exactly what she'd told him not to do — talking to people. He had that trick of standing slightly sideways to a person, notebook closed, hands visible, asking small questions with a face so mild that people answered before they thought about whether they should. A circle of kids had adopted him. An old woman was selling him plantains he hadn't asked for.

"—and the launch went up past the Deadfall," the old woman was saying as Beatrix came within earshot, "same day the rain-birds flew low, which my grandmother always said means—"

"Crane." Beatrix took his elbow. "Walk with me."

He read her tone instantly, thanked the woman, paid too much for the plantains, and fell into step. They walked out along the bluff path above the landing, where the wind came off the water and conversations couldn't be overheard by dogs, let alone men.

"Tell me what you've got," she said.

"A boy saw the launch. Black hull, twin screws, four men. They stopped at the old trading post upstream — the abandoned one — for most of an afternoon. He watched them from the reeds because they frightened him. He says they burned things." Silas's voice was level, but she could hear the effort holding it level. "Papers. A whole box of them, he thinks. Then they went on upriver."

"Burning papers at an abandoned post." Beatrix looked out at the brown water sliding past below the bluff. "That's not tidying up. That's erasing."

"There's more." He hesitated, and she watched him decide to say it, watch the decision cost him something. "The boy said one of the men wore a ring. Silver, with a red stone. He remembered it because the man hit a mule with his stick and the ring caught the light."

Beatrix went still.

She knew that ring. Everyone who dealt with Corven Shipping knew that ring — Vasken wore it, Corven's river superintendent, the man who signed the scrip and collected the debts and smiled with all the warmth of a hook. She'd drunk bad whiskey across a table from that ring a dozen times while Vasken explained, pleasantly, what would happen to her schedule if she missed another payment date.

Vasken. On the upper river. Burning documents. Two days ahead of them.

"Say something," Silas said quietly.

"I'm deciding what to say." She kept her eyes on the river. Below them, the Magpie sat at her moorings, small and battered and faithful, her paint peeling along the gunwale where the sun had been chewing at her all season. *My boat,* Beatrix thought. *My boat that Corven effectively owns until the debt dies.* "Vasken is Corven's right hand. Has been for twenty years. If Vasken is burning papers connected to that survey camp, then either Vasken has gone into private wickedness — which happens — or he's doing Corven's wickedness for him."

"You believe the first?"

"No." Saying it out loud felt like pulling a tooth. "Corven doesn't let men like Vasken have private anything. Vasken's viciousness is company property. It's inventoried."

Silas was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — gentler, and somehow worse for it. "Captain Yarrow. Beatrix. What is Corven to you? Exactly. Not the ledger version. The true one."

She laughed, short and without humor. "You want the true one."

"I think I need it. Both of us do."

So she gave it to him, there on the bluff with the wind pulling at her collar, because he'd earned some truth and because keeping it inside was starting to hurt.

"When I was nineteen my father drowned at the Deadfall — same water we're headed for, which is a joke the river thinks is funny. Left me the boat and the debts both, and the debts were bigger. Corven bought them up. And then — this is the part people don't believe — he *didn't* destroy me. He gave me terms a reasonable man could live with. He sent work my way when work was thin. He advanced parts. Twice, when other owners would have taken the Magpie outright, he waited." She wrapped her arms around the rail. "For eleven years I've told myself that makes him decent. That a shark who chooses not to bite is still, in some accounting, a friend. Because the alternative is that everything I built was built inside the mouth of something that could close whenever it wanted. Do you understand? If Corven's rotten, then I haven't been surviving by my skill all these years. I've been surviving by his *permission*. And I don't know which of those is worse to find out."

Silas said nothing for a while. Below them a kingfisher went down the current like a thrown blue dart.

"My superiors sent me upriver with a warrant and told me the survey team likely deserted," he said finally. "Deserted, with all their instruments, in monsoon country, leaving tents pitched. I believed it because believing it was comfortable. We're a matched pair, you and I. Both of us loyal to institutions that may have already sold us."

"That's a cheerful thing to say."

"I'm told honesty usually isn't." He turned to face her fully. "Here's what matters now. If Corven's men destroyed evidence at that camp — if they're ahead of us cleaning the scene — then whoever sent them knew about the investigation. Knew before you and I ever met. Which means the leak isn't downstream in some office. It's at the top, and it's moving faster than we are."

"Or," Beatrix said, "they're not cleaning a scene. They're finishing a job." The words came out of her cold and certain, and she wished they hadn't. "Six men don't vanish, Crane. Rivers don't swallow whole crews without a trace — I've told you that. But rivers will carry off whatever men are weighted and slipped in quiet water, and by now, four days on, the current's had time to scatter the proof. Somebody knew exactly what the river could be made to keep."

She watched him absorb that — watched the last comfortable assumption die behind his eyes.

"So we go faster," he said.

"We go smarter. Faster gets us dead." She pushed off the rail and started back down the bluff path, her mind already laying the route out like cards. "We take on water and rope and we're out of Harrow Bend inside the hour. We don't stop again. And Crane—" she paused at the top of the dock stairs, looking back at him, "—from here on, we tell each other everything, even the parts that taste bad. Starting with this: if it comes down to Corven's men and my boat, I need to know right now whose side you'll stand on. Because I've spent eleven years paying a man who might have murdered six people, and I can't afford to also be guessing about you."

Silas met her eyes without flinching. "If it comes to that," he said, "you won't have to guess."

It wasn't quite an answer. But coming from a government man, it was nearly a vow, and she took it.

Overhead, far to the south, the light had begun to change — a bruised greenish tint gathering along the horizon like a bruise under skin, and the air, for the first time all season, tasted faintly of iron.

The rain-birds flew low.

Beatrix cast off the lines alone, and the Magpie turned her bow upriver into the darkening day.
