## 6. First Rain

The first drop hit the back of my hand like a cold coin.

I was standing at the Magpie's rail with a chart spread across my knees, trying to make sense of the river's braided channels ahead, when it struck — one fat, deliberate drop, then another, then a hiss across the water that sounded like the whole sky exhaling. Beatrix looked up from the wheelhouse window, and I watched her face do something complicated: not surprise exactly, but recognition, the way a man recognizes a debt collector's knock.

"That's early," she said.

"Early?"

"Monsoon's edge." She stepped out onto the deck, tilted her head back, let three drops land on her cheek like she was taking their measure. "Shouldn't be here for another week. Maybe ten days. The fishermen will have been watching for it, and they'll be wrong-footed too." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "The river doesn't care about schedules, Crane. That's the first thing you learn and the last thing you believe."

I folded the chart. The paper had already begun to soften at the creases, drinking the damp. Everything on this river drank the damp eventually — wood, canvas, documents, men. I'd spent eleven years in government offices where paper was protected like relic bone, sealed in folios, stamped, countersigned, filed in rooms where the humidity was regulated by law. Out here it curled and bloomed with mold in a single night. There was something honest about it. Paper out here told you what it really was: pulp and ink, temporary as anything alive.

"Can we outrun it?" I asked.

She gave me a look over her shoulder that I was beginning to catalog. This one meant *you still don't understand what you've hired*. "Outrun rain? It's not a dog, Silas. It comes down all at once, everywhere, like a lid closing." She nodded upstream, toward a smudge of gray on the horizon that might have been cloud or might have been spray off the shallows. "There's a village about four miles on. Kessarin. Good anchorage behind the point, deep water even in low season. We'll tie up there and let the front pass."

"And after?"

"After we see what the rain does to our schedule." She turned back to the wheel. "And what it does to whoever's ahead of us."

That was the thing about Beatrix Yarrow. She never said the plain sentence when she could say the one underneath it. *Whoever's ahead of us.* Not weather talk. Hunt talk. She'd been thinking about Corven's men since the supply stop at Tarn's Landing, and she thought about them the way she thought about sandbars — constantly, professionally, with a running calculation of how much water lay between them and trouble.

* * *

Kessarin appeared out of the thickening light like something remembered rather than seen: a crescent of stilt houses along a mud bank, fishing nets hung like strange laundry, dugouts pulled up nose-first on the shore, and behind it all a dark line of hills going green-black under the coming rain. Beatrix took us in through the channel behind the point with a confidence that made me stop pretending to read the chart. She didn't consult it once. She watched the water — its color, its texture, the way debris moved or didn't — and steered by some grammar of the surface that I couldn't parse and she never bothered to translate.

We tied up alongside a rickety pier beside a boat twice the Magpie's age, if such a thing were possible, its planks silvered and split but caulked with evident love. An old woman mending a net looked up at us without much interest, then looked again at Beatrix, and something passed between them — a nod, a half-smile, the whole conversation conducted in the economy of people who live on the same water.

"You know her?" I asked.

"I know everyone on this stretch," Beatrix said. "Whether they know me is another matter." She caught a mooring line thrown by a boy of maybe fourteen, made fast, and stood a moment looking up at the sky. The gray had come down lower while we docked. The air had gone thick enough to chew. "Rain'll hold off an hour yet. The edge always hesitates, like it's deciding whether we're worth the trouble."

"What do we do with the hour?"

"We ask questions," she said. "You're the investigator. Investigate."

It was said lightly, but there was an edge under it, and I knew why. Since Tarn's Landing she had been careful — visibly, deliberately careful — about how much she wanted to know. A woman with debts to Corven does not go looking for evidence against Corven. Every fact I uncovered put her deeper in a current she hadn't chosen. And every day she piloted me further upstream anyway. I understood the arithmetic. I'd done the same arithmetic myself, back when I still believed the numbers came out clean.

We went ashore together.

* * *

The village smelled of woodsmoke and fish scales and the green mineral smell that rises off mud just before rain. Dogs trotted between the stilts. Children stopped their games to watch us — two strangers, one of them in a city coat that had no business this far upriver — and then went back to their games with the practiced indifference of children who have learned that strangers are usually nothing.

Beatrix bought dried fish from the old woman with the net, and while she haggled — a performance I suspected both parties enjoyed more than the fish — I drifted toward the center of the village, where a covered platform stood on heavy posts above the flood line. Villagers gathered there in bad weather. A few men sat under it now, playing a game with pebbles and carved sticks, and they watched me come with the flat patience of men who had watched officials come before.

I asked my question carefully. I'd learned by now not to lead with the survey team, not to say *government*, not to use any word that sounded like authority. Authority, up here, was either absent or predatory, and neither version invited candor.

"I'm looking for the old trading post," I said. "Upstream. I heard there was one."

"There was," said the oldest of the men, without moving his eyes from the game. "Burned."

"When?"

He shrugged. "Before the rains. Weeks ago. Maybe a month. Time moves differently upriver."

"Burned how? Accident?"

Another shrug, and this one had weight to it, the shrug of a man declining to know something. But a boy had drifted close to listen — a thin kid, maybe ten, all elbows and stare, wearing a shirt that had belonged to someone much larger — and the boy did not shrug. The boy leaned forward.

"They burned it," he said. "On purpose. With fire-sticks."

The old man's hand came down gently on the boy's shoulder, a gesture that was half affection and half muzzle. But the damage was done. The boy had decided I was safe — perhaps because I'd crouched to his level, perhaps because I'd given him a piece of dried fish I'd bought from Beatrix's pile, perhaps simply because ten-year-olds have not yet learned which questions cost — and he began to talk the way children talk when they finally get an adult who listens: completely, breathlessly, in no useful order whatsoever.

* * *

His name was Dath. He told me he fished the shallows near the trading post with his cousin, setting lines for catfish in the evening. He told me the post had been empty for years, that everyone said it was haunted, that he didn't believe in ghosts but he liked saying the post was haunted because it kept other kids from following him to his best fishing holes. He told me all of this while the first real rain began to fall — not the hesitant edge-drops but the true arrival, a gray curtain sweeping across the river, drumming on the platform roof so hard we had to raise our voices.

And then, in the middle of a story about a catfish the size of his leg, he stopped. Went quiet. Looked at his feet.

"Dath?" I said.

"The men came at night," he said. "Four boats. Big ones, with engines, not paddles. They had lights." He held up his hands to show me how bright, fingers spread wide. "They told us to go home. They had black sticks — fire-sticks — the kind soldiers have. My uncle said we should go, so we went. But I went slow." He glanced at the old man, who was studiously not listening, which is a skill older than any government. "I went slow on purpose."

"What did you see?"

"They carried boxes out of the post. Papers. So many papers. They threw them in a fire they'd built on the mud flat, down by the water, where it wouldn't catch the trees." His voice dropped, and he leaned in as though the rain itself might repeat what he said next. "But the wind was wrong that night. It was blowing off the water. Papers flew. Some of them landed on the bank, past the fire, and the men didn't find them all because it was dark and they were in a hurry. I found one."

My heart was doing something unprofessional. "You found one."

"It had numbers on it. Rows and rows. And a drawing of the ground, like a map but with lines going down into it, like—" he cut his hand through the air, downward, strata. "Like cutting into a cake and drawing what's inside."

A cross-section. A survey cross-section, drawn by someone trained, showing subsurface features. My mouth had gone dry even as the rain fell in sheets around us.

"Do you still have it?"

He shook his head, and my stomach dropped — and then he grinned, gap-toothed, triumphant. "It got wet and fell apart. But I remembered the numbers. I'm good at numbers." He wrote them for me in the dirt of the platform floor with a stick, a column of figures, and I copied them into my notebook with hands that were not entirely steady. I have no idea what they mean. Ore assay values, possibly. Depths, possibly. Coordinates, possibly. But a child had memorized data that armed men had crossed the river at night to burn, and that fact was worth more than the data itself.

"Why did you remember them?" I asked him.

He looked at me like I was simple. "Because they burned the ghost house," he said. "Nobody burns the ghost house. Ghosts don't have enemies."

* * *

I found Beatrix under the awning of the net-mender's stall, and I must have been wearing my findings on my face, because she set down the coil of rope she was inspecting and said, "Tell me," before I'd said a word.

I told her. All of it — the four boats, the engines, the fire on the mud flat, the child's column of numbers drying in my notebook. She listened with her arms crossed and her eyes on the middle distance, and when I finished she was quiet for a long moment while the rain hammered the tin roof above us.

"Four boats," she said. "Armed. At night. Burning survey papers at an abandoned post." She unfolded her arms. "Silas. Your warrant says find a missing team. What does your office think happened to them?"

"I was sent to establish they'd had an accident," I said. "Flood, disease, hostile locals. Something tidy." I heard how it sounded, saying it out loud to her. "I believed it when I left. Mostly."

"And now?"

"Now I think a ten-year-old just described a cover-up with better precision than my briefing did."

She nodded slowly, and I watched her arrive at the place I'd already fallen into — that sickening free-fall where the ground of your assumptions gives way and you realize you've been falling for longer than you knew. But Beatrix being Beatrix, she didn't stay in the fall. She started calculating.

"Four boats means organization," she said. "Engines mean money. Night work means they didn't want witnesses, which means they expected witnesses to matter, which means—" she tapped the rail, counting beats, "—they're not pirates. Pirates don't burn paper. Paper's worthless to pirates. Someone's burning records, and someone with money is paying for it to be done quietly." She looked at me. "How far upriver is the fort where you were supposed to report?"

"Three days' run."

"How far to the delta press town?"

"Nine. Ten, in monsoon water."

She exhaled through her teeth. "So the nearest honest authority is nine days away, and the nearest dishonest one is three." She said it flatly, but I saw what it cost her — because the dishonest authority wasn't abstract anymore. It had a name, and she owed it money, and its men had passed us on this river heading the same direction we were going.

The rain intensified. Somewhere across the village a shutter banged. The river below the pier had changed color, gone the color of strong tea, rising already — a hand's width since we'd tied up, maybe more. Beatrix noticed it the way she noticed everything about the water, with a glance so quick it barely counted as looking.

"River's coming up fast," she said. "Front like this can push six feet overnight in the narrows. We'll want to double our lines." Then, quieter, almost to herself: "And whoever's ahead of us knows this water too."

I touched my breast pocket, where the recovered folio pages rode in oilcloth against my ribs. The hunt had become real somewhere in the last hour — not a missing-persons inquiry, not a bureaucratic errand, but a pursuit with two ends, and we were somewhere in the middle of it, tied to a pier in a fishing village while the sky came down.

Dath appeared at my elbow as we made to leave, holding something wrapped in banana leaf. Dried fish, a child's portion, pressed into my hand as payment — he insisted it was payment — for listening.

"You'll catch the men who burned it?" he asked.

I thought about lying to him. I thought about the truth. I gave him the truth, scaled to size: "We're going to try very hard."

He nodded gravely, satisfied, and ran off into the rain, and I stood there a moment watching him go, thinking that the smallest witness on this river had already done more for the truth than the entire department that signed my warrant.

Behind us, the river kept rising, and somewhere upstream, in the rain, four boats' worth of armed men were learning that not every page had burned.
