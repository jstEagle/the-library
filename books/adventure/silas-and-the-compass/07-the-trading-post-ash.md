## 7. The Trading Post Ash

The rain had thinned to a fine gray needle by the time we reached the old trading post, and the river had swallowed half of it.

I brought the *Magpie* in slow, backing water off the stern wheel until her hull kissed the mud shelf where the dock used to be. There wasn't any dock anymore. There was a black skeleton of pilings and, where the trading post itself had stood — a long, low building of river timber that had watched over this bend for sixty years — there was now a field of ash and charred stumps, steaming gently in the drizzle like something freshly buried.

Silas stood at the rail beside me with his hands jammed in his coat pockets. He hadn't said much since the fishing village. He'd written down every word the boy had given us, cross-checked it against his warrant papers twice, and then gone quiet in the way quiet men go quiet when they've stopped hoping a thing isn't true.

"That's it," he said. "That's the post."

"It's what's left of it."

We went ashore in the skiff, because the *Magpie* drew too much to nose up on the soft bank, and I tied her off to a piling that still stood straight as a church steeple while everything around it had fallen. The mud sucked at our boots. The smell was the worst part — not woodsmoke, which is honest, but that sour chemical reek of paper and lamp oil burned together, the smell of a fire that had been fed on purpose.

"Oil," Silas said, crouching at the edge of the ruin. He touched a dark stain in the mud, rubbed it between two fingers. "They didn't burn it hot enough to take the building down clean. They burned it to take the *contents* down clean."

"Same thing, if you're in a hurry."

"They weren't in a hurry." He stood, wiping his hand on his coat, and looked out across the ash field with an expression I was beginning to recognize — the flat, patient look of a man reading a document nobody else could see. "Look at the pattern. The fire started here, at the records room, and here, at the map chest. Two points of origin. Everything else caught secondhand."

"So they knew what they wanted dead."

"They knew exactly." He walked the perimeter without stepping into the ash proper, cataloguing as he went. "And they missed something. They always miss something."

I'd heard men say that before, usually right before they drowned. But I let him work.

* * *

Sifting a burned building is like dredging a sandbar: ninety parts nothing for every one part something, and no way to know which shovel-load holds the gold until you've moved all ninety.

We worked through the morning. I took the eastern half, where the trading floor had been — scales, ledgers, the iron safe that had once held the post's cash box. The safe's door hung open, empty, its lock drilled neat as a surgeon's cut. Somebody professional had gotten into it before the fire, or during it.

Silas worked the western half, where the records room had been, and he worked it the way I work a channel at low water: inch by inch, feeling for the thing that doesn't belong.

The rain kept up its gray whisper. The river rose against the pilings, a hand-span higher than when we'd arrived. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered like a man clearing his throat before saying something unpleasant.

Around midday I found the first real piece. A corner of ledger binding, half-fused into a lump of charcoal, and inside it — miracle of damp and haste — three legible lines. Entries from the spring: freight manifests. Crates marked as machinery going upriver to a survey depot that, according to everything official, didn't exist yet.

"Crane," I called. "Come look at this."

He came, read it, and went very still. "Machinery," he repeated. "Heavy freight. Upstream. In March."

"Before your survey team ever filed their first report."

"Before they were even *assigned*." He took the lump carefully, wrapped it in oilcloth from my kit. His voice had dropped into that careful register men use when they're standing on ice and listening to it talk. "Someone was provisioning a site upriver months before there was any official reason to provision one."

"Or someone was using the survey as cover for freight that needed hiding."

"Yes," he said. "Or that."

* * *

It was near dusk, with the light going copper behind the rain clouds, that Silas found the page.

I heard him make a sound — not a word, just a small exhalation, the sound a man makes when a fish he's been fighting for an hour finally shows itself in the net. I came over the fallen roof beam to find him kneeling in a shallow depression where the records room floor had partially collapsed into the cellar, holding something flat and black and fragile as a moth wing.

A sheet of paper, charred at the edges, fused along one side into a solid mass where the heat had welded several pages together. But the top sheet had curled away from the fire instead of into it — shielded, maybe, by falling debris, by the wet, by pure dumb luck — and across its face ran columns of figures in a surveyor's precise hand.

"What have you got?"

"Ore samples." His voice was strange. Careful. "Assay results. Look at the header."

I looked. The letterhead was government stock — the same department whose seal sat on his warrant. The date was six weeks ago. And below the date, in the surveyor's notation column:

**Site 14. Quadrant designated BARREN / NO VALUE. Core samples attached.**

Below that, the assay numbers themselves. I'm no assayer, but I've hauled enough freight to know what numbers like that mean when a man writes them down with a shaking pen. Iron ore, high grade. Copper beside it. And a third entry, abbreviated but unmistakable to anyone who'd ever loaded a crate marked MACHINERY: rare earths, quantities that would make a shipping magnate weep.

"This quadrant," Silas said slowly, "is officially empty. I've read the designation myself. It's in the published atlas. *Barren. No value. Excluded from development in perpetuity.* That designation is why the land was never surveyed properly, never leased, never sold. It sits there on the maps like a hole in the world."

"And somebody sampled it anyway."

"Somebody sampled it secretly, found *this*—" he tapped the numbers, gentle as touching a wound, "—and then needed the official designation to stay exactly where it was. Empty. Because empty land can't be bid on. Can't be contested. Can't be audited. You just quietly strip it yourself and let the map keep lying."

The rain ticked against the charred beams. Down at the mooring, the *Magpie* tugged at her line, restless, as if she could smell the weather coming the way animals do.

"The survey team found this," I said. It wasn't a question.

"The survey team found this, wrote it up, and sent it downstream in a folio of reports." Silas turned the page over. On the reverse, faintly, bled through from the sheet beneath, were more columns — more sites, more samples, a whole hidden geography of wealth drawn over a map officially blank. "And then the survey team stopped sending reports. And then the survey team stopped existing. And then the place where their reports would have been archived burned down with them inside it — except they weren't inside it, were they, because there were no bodies at the camp."

"No bodies," I agreed. "Just weighted ones, downstream."

We looked at each other across the ash, and I watched him do the arithmetic I'd already done. Four boats' worth of armed men. Corven's freight moving upriver in March. A burned post drilled open by professionals. And somewhere above us, in the rising rain, a missing folio that named names — because a cover-up this size doesn't get built by one man, and the missing folio wasn't missing by accident. Someone had taken it. Someone who wanted it either destroyed or kept as leverage.

"The page," Silas said. "We need to preserve it. If it dries wrong it'll crumble."

"I've got a biscuit tin lined with waxed cloth. It'll ride better than anything in your government kit."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You don't trust government equipment."

"I trust tin." I fetched it, and together we bedded the page down like a wounded bird, sealing the lid against the damp. It fit in the palm of my hand, that tin. Half an ounce of charred paper. Enough weight to sink every one of us if the wrong people learned we carried it.

Thunder rolled again, closer now, and the first fat drops of the real rain began to come down — the monsoon's true vanguard, arriving a day early, hammering the ash into gray paste around our boots.

"We should get back to the boat," I said. "This stretch floods fast when the rain sets in, and I want the *Magpie* anchored deep before dark."

Silas stood, pocketing the tin with a care that bordered on reverence. For a long moment he looked back at the ruin — at the drilled safe, the twin points of origin, the whole careful architecture of erasure — and when he spoke, his voice had changed. It was quieter. Harder. The voice of a man who had carried a warrant for years and had only just discovered what it weighed.

"When I find whoever signed the order to burn this," he said, "I'm not going to arrest them."

"You're an investigator."

"I was." He turned and started down toward the skiff, rain streaming off his hat brim. "I'm starting to think I'm something else now."

I followed him down through the mud, and behind us the trading post finished dissolving into the river's memory, and ahead of us the *Magpie* rode her line in the darkening water — and that was when I saw it.

A light. Far upstream, around the bend, where the channel narrowed past the drowned willows. Not a fisherman's lantern — fishermen don't run unlit at night, and fishermen don't burn green-white signal lamps in triple flashes.

Three flashes. Pause. Three more.

Somebody upstream was signaling somebody downstream.

And downstream, behind us, out in the rain and the rising dark, a light answered.
