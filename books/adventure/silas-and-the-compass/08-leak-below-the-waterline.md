## 8. Leak Below the Waterline

The river changed its mind at dusk.

All day it had been a brown ribbon of patience, wide and slow and honest about its intentions. Then the banks narrowed where the hills pressed in from either side, and the current quickened like a man who has remembered an appointment, and the water began to carry things — branches, whole trees uprooted with their roots still clawing at the air, fence posts torn from some farm we would never see. Driftwood. The low-water season's debris, all of it flushed loose by the first rains far upstream, riding down to meet us in the failing light.

I stood at the bow with the lantern unlit because Beatrix had said no lights until full dark, and I watched the river bring its wreckage toward us and slide it past. There was something almost ceremonial about it. The flood was announcing itself. Everything the storm had broken upriver was being delivered downstream, and we were steaming straight into the mail.

"See that?" Beatrix called from the wheelhouse behind me. She had to raise her voice over the engine, which had developed a new note in the last hour, a thinner sound, like a singer reaching for the top of her range. "That's the monsoon's calling card. First day of rain up in the high country and everything dead comes down the river at once."

"How much of it is there?"

"More than this." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "This is just the scouts."

I had been on the Magpie for six days now, and I had learned to read Beatrix Yarrow the way she read the water: not by what she said but by what she did with her hands. Her hands were never idle. They rested on the wheel lightly, two fingers and a thumb, the way a rider holds reins with a horse she trusts, and they adjusted constantly, small corrections, conversations held in silence between her palms and the wood. When she was worried, her thumbs moved. Right now both thumbs were moving.

We came around a bend where a cliff fell sheer into the channel and the current set hard against the rock, and I heard her say, very quietly, to herself or to the boat or possibly to me: "Hold together, old girl."

Then the world went sideways.

I never saw what we hit. That was the thing I kept returning to afterward — I was standing right there, eyes on the water, and I saw nothing. A brown shape, maybe. Something the color of the river itself, submerged just deep enough to be invisible and shallow enough to be fatal. The Magpie struck it with a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it, a deep wooden concussion, and then a second sound underneath the first, a sound like tearing cloth, if cloth were made of planks and pitch.

Beatrix killed the engine so fast the boat seemed to flinch.

For three seconds nobody moved. The current took us and began walking us sideways toward the cliff, and the silence below decks was the loudest thing I had ever heard. Then Beatrix was past me, moving down the companionway, and her voice came back up through the deck plates, flat and fast:

"Bilge. Now. Tell me how fast she's filling."

I found the hand pump amidships by feel, in the dark, in water already ankle-deep and cold as confession. I'd bailed boats before — every boy who grew up anywhere near water has bailed something — but never a boat that mattered to anyone, and never with this particular arithmetic ticking in my head: the river outside, the river coming inside, and the question of which one intended to win.

"It's coming in," I called up. "Steady. Maybe — I don't know. Fast."

"Fast isn't a measurement, Crane." But there was no real bite in it. She came back up the companionway carrying the storm lantern, lit now, and hung it on a hook above the bilge, and the light swung wild across the hull timbers and turned the rising water into moving gold. She knelt and put her palm flat against the planking near the turn of the bilge, reading the hull the way a doctor reads a fevered brow. "There you are," she murmured. Almost tender. Almost furious.

"Is it bad?"

"She's holed. Starboard side, forward of the frame, maybe eight inches of split plank. Something with a limb on it — a root ball, most likely, or a whole tree swimming nose-down." She was already pulling a sea chest open, dragging out oakum, a mallet, a sheet of canvas, a pot of something black and tar-smelling that I could smell before I could see it. "Question is whether I can reach it from inside or whether somebody's going swimming."

"The water's freezing."

"The water's moving, which is worse." She looked at me then, really looked, in the swinging lamplight, and I realized she was measuring me. Not my courage — I don't think she believed in courage as a fixed quantity. She was measuring whether I could be given a job and trusted to do exactly that job and nothing else while she did hers. It was the same look she'd given me at the dock when I'd first put government paper in front of her, except warmer by perhaps one degree. Six days of shared danger and shared tinned food had bought me one degree.

"I can bail," I said. "I can bail until my arms come off."

"That's the spirit. Bail like your arms are rented." And she was gone again, out the stern hatch, and I heard her boots on the deck above, moving to the rail, looking down at the hole from outside, judging it against the waterline and the current and whatever private map of the Magpie's body she carried in her head.

I pumped. The pump was old and fought me, a long iron lever on a leather cup that had gone stiff somewhere in the last decade, and every stroke brought up a gout of brown water that I sent over the side and every stroke cost me something I wasn't sure I had. Below the lantern's swing the bilge water rose and fell and rose again, and I understood that I was not emptying the boat; I was holding a line. Buying time. Making sure that the rate of leaving matched the rate of arriving until Beatrix could change the equation entirely.

It occurred to me, somewhere around the hundredth stroke, that this was the first honest work I had done in years. I had spent eleven years in government service, and nearly all of it had been this exact motion — pumping furiously, keeping the level steady, holding a line — except that the water I'd been holding back was paper. Reports that contradicted other reports. Findings rewritten by men who had never seen the river. I had gotten very good at bailing, and very good at not asking where the leak was, and very good indeed at not noticing that the boat was mine too, that I stood on the same planks, that when she went down I would go down with her.

The survey team hadn't asked either. That was the thought that came to me in the dark, in the cold water slopping around my boots, and it arrived with the force of a physical blow. Seven competent people, with instruments and warrants and the full weight of the state behind them, and they had bailed and bailed and bailed, filing their reports, keeping the level steady — and the whole time the hole was above them, in the offices, in the ministries, in the signatures at the top of the page. You can pump forever. If the leak is in the government, the boat still sinks.

"Crane!" Beatrix's head appeared at the hatch, wet to the shoulders, hair plastered to her skull. "I can't get at it clean from outside — the current's got teeth right there. I'm going in from the inside. I need you to keep the light steady and keep pumping, and when I say *now*, you pump like the devil himself is drinking behind you. Understood?"

"What do you mean, from the inside? You're going under the water?"

"Chest-deep, maybe. The forward bilge pockets it. I'll be working blind with my hands and a chisel and a handful of oakum, and I will need approximately four minutes of you being the best pump operator on this river." She was wringing out her sleeves as she talked, matter-of-fact, a woman discussing laundry. "Can you give me four minutes?"

What I wanted to say was that I had never operated any pump on any river, that my hands were clerk's hands, ink-stained and soft, that the most dangerous thing I had done this year was sign for registered mail. What I said was: "You'll have them."

She nodded once, and something passed across her face — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of a reassessment — and then she stripped off her coat, kicked off her boots, and went down into the gold-lit water like it was a room she owned.

* * *

Working blind, she called it, and she was right. The lantern swung with the boat's motion and threw shadows that crawled across the frames like living things, and Beatrix was half-submerged in the forward bilge with only her shoulders and her raised arms above the surface, feeling along the hull for the wound. Every few seconds she'd call a depth reading — "holding, keep it there" — and I would answer, and that was the whole of our conversation for the better part of ten minutes. Holding. Keep it there. Holding.

But something else was happening in that dark, and I felt it happening even as my arms burned and the cold water soaked me to the waist. We were becoming a single machine. Her hands below, finding the split; my arms above, holding the line. Neither of us could succeed alone. Neither of us was pretending otherwise anymore. For six days we had been two professionals sharing a deck, each with our own reasons, our own silences, circling each other with the courtesy of people who expect to part ways. Now there was no circling. There was only the work, and the work required everything we both had, and there is a kind of intimacy in that which polite acquaintance never touches.

"Found it," she said at last, voice tight with effort. "Root spike went in clean. Split runs maybe nine inches along the seam. I'm going to ream it out and pack it. Light steady?"

"Light's steady."

"Now, Crane."

I pumped like the devil himself was drinking. The lever fought me and I fought it back, and sweat ran into my eyes and stung, and my shoulders screamed, and I counted strokes because counting was the only way to keep going — twenty, forty, sixty — and below me Beatrix worked with the mallet and the chisel and then with her bare hands, driving oakum into the split plank fiber by fiber, packing the wound the way you'd pack a wound in flesh, and the black tar smell filled the whole bilge, and the water level dropped, dropped, held, dropped.

"Ninety," I gasped.

"Almost — there — drive it home—" The mallet rang twice against something, a wooden wedge driven crosswise to bind the patch, and then her fist hit the hull from the inside, twice, a carpenter's knock, and she shouted, "That's it! That's it, hold, hold—"

And then, gradually, miraculously, the arithmetic changed. The rate of leaving exceeded the rate of arriving. The gold-colored water began to fall away from the frames, inch by inch, revealing the wet timber beneath, and Beatrix stood up out of the bilge streaming and gasping and grinning — actually grinning, the first grin I had seen on her, fierce and exhausted and young, years younger than her careful face usually allowed.

"There," she said, slapping the patched plank. "Good girl. Good girl." She was talking to the boat, obviously. Mostly.

"You're bleeding," I said. Her forearm, above the waterline mark, was opened in a long shallow stripe where the chisel or the splinters had caught her.

She glanced at it as though noticing a stranger's injury. "River tax," she said, and wrapped it in a rag without breaking stride, and climbed out of the bilge, and sat down hard on the engine housing, and for the first time since I'd known her, Beatrix Yarrow allowed herself to simply breathe.

* * *

Later, with the boat anchored in a cut behind a gravel bar — she would not run the narrows at night with a fresh patch, and I didn't argue — we sat in the wheelhouse with tea heated on a spirit stove and the lantern turned low. Through the windows the river ran silver-black under a sky still bruised with distant lightning, silent thunder, the storm talking in its sleep far upstream. The rain proper hadn't reached us yet. We both knew it was coming. It was in the air like a held breath.

Beatrix had changed into dry clothes and looked almost soft in the lantern light, her hair drying in waves she hadn't bothered to tame, the rag on her arm already going brown. She held her tea in both hands and stared out at the water, and I found I couldn't read her at all, which unsettled me more than the hole in the hull had.

"You did well today," she said finally, not looking at me.

"You did the well. I moved a stick up and down."

"Don't do that." She turned and looked at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not warmth exactly, but the clearing of frost. "Six days I've been trying to decide what you are. Government man, paper man, some ministry's eyes and ears sent to ride my boat and report on me the way they reported on those surveyors." She sipped her tea. "Today I watched you pump for forty minutes without stopping and without whining, and when I told you to trust me with four minutes of your boat's life, you did it without asking why. So here's what I've decided you are, Crane. You're a man who was built for one thing and put to work at another, and it's eating you alive. Same as it eats everyone on this river who works for someone else."

I should have denied it. Some reflex of caution, some clerk's instinct for self-preservation, told me to deny it — to smile and deflect and keep my own counsel the way I had kept it for eleven years. Instead I heard myself say: "The survey team filed everything correctly. Did you know that? Every report, every sample log, every wire summary — correct procedure, start to finish. They did nothing wrong. They bailed perfectly." My hands had gone still around the cup. "And they're dead anyway, or scattered, or worse, because the leak was never in the boat. It was in the shipyard. It was in whoever built the thing and sold it knowing it would sink."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the silent lightning flickered again, farther off, or closer — impossible to tell.

"And does your warrant let you say that out loud?" she asked quietly.

"My warrant," I said, "is a piece of paper signed by men whose names are going to be in the missing folio, if we ever find it. My warrant is the reason seven people are dead. I've been telling myself for six days that I'm investigating a disappearance." I looked down into my tea. "I'm starting to understand that I'm investigating my own employers, and that everything I've been sent up this river to do is a lie wearing my signature."

The words hung in the wheelhouse steam. I had never said anything like them aloud, not to anyone, and having said them I felt lighter and more terrified in equal measure, the way a man feels stepping off a ledge he's been standing on for years.

Beatrix set down her cup. She studied me for a long time, and whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find it, because when she spoke her voice had lost its last edge of assessment and gained something quieter.

"Well," she said. "Now you're useful."

It was, from Beatrix Yarrow, practically an embrace. I laughed — the first real laugh of the whole journey — and she almost smiled, and for a moment the wheelhouse was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the spirit stove.

Then she stiffened. Her head turned toward the window, toward the dark stretch of river downstream, and her whole body changed, the warmth shuttering away like a lamp behind a door.

"Lights," she said. "Downstream. Two of them, running without lamps till now — just showed." She was already moving, snuffing our own lantern, plunging us into darkness. "Nobody runs these narrows at night for pleasure, Crane. Somebody's coming upriver after us. And they've just started looking."
