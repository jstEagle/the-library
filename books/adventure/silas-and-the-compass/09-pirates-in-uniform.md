## 9. Pirates in Uniform

The rain came in the night and left by morning, as if it had only stopped to look at them.

Beatrix felt the change before she saw it — a flattening of the river's voice, the water going glassy and wide-eyed between the banks. After days of whitecaps and driving sheets, the Magpie sat quiet on her mooring line at the edge of a backwater below the old ferry crossing, and the silence had teeth in it. Silence meant someone could hear you. Silence meant you could be heard.

She stood at the rail with a tin cup of chicory going cold in her hand and watched the downstream bend, where the current slid around a drowned willow and came apart into long, oily spirals. Silas was still asleep below, or pretending to be, curled around the folio's oilcloth wrapping like a man sleeping beside a lit stove. She'd heard him turn over four times in the night. Guilt kept worse hours than fear did.

The river gave her the patrol boat a full minute before her eyes found it.

It came around the bend low and mean, riding deeper than any survey launch had a right to ride — armored wheelhouse, twin exhausts breathing gray smoke, and off the stern staff, snapping wet and bright against the gray morning, the pennant of the Territorial Water Office. Government colors. The real ones, stitched proper, not the painted rags the river pirates flew when they wanted farmers to open their strongboxes without a fight.

Beatrix set down the cup with great care, the way you set down something you might need to throw.

"Silas," she said, not loudly. "Company."

He was up the companionway in seconds, hair wild, coat half-on, the warrant he no longer carried ghosting through his posture — that instinct to reach for paper that would save him. His hand found his breast pocket and closed on nothing. He looked at her.

"How many?"

"One boat. Six I can count, maybe eight." She was already moving, kicking open the hatch to the engine bay, dragging up the bilge cover. The bilge was a black soup of rainwater, grease, and the Magpie's own old blood — forty years of leaks and patches settling into a sludge at the bottom of her hull. It stank like a swamp's armpit. "Get the folio."

"You're not serious—"

"I'm always serious about the bilge. Wrap it twice more. Push it under the stringer aft, where the frame curves in. Then lie down flat and don't come up until I say."

He hesitated one heartbeat too long, and she grabbed his collar and put her face close to his. "Listen to me. They're looking for stolen documents. If they find them on this boat, we die out here and the river keeps us, same as it kept the survey team. If they don't find them, we're just a woman and a clerk going fishing. Which story do you want to be standing inside of?"

Something changed behind his eyes — she'd seen it building for two days, the slow collapse of the official man and the assembly of whatever was going to stand in his place. He went below without another word.

* * *

The patrol boat came on with the arrogant economy of something that had never once been challenged on the water. It didn't signal. It didn't hail. It simply turned across the Magpie's bow at fifty yards, throwing a wake that rocked Beatrix on her feet, and backed its throttle down until the two hulls drifted together with a groan of rubbing timber.

Six men on deck. The one at the rail wore a Water Office inspector's cap with the brim soft from sweat, and under the cap he had the face of a man who'd been paid to stop being a fisherman a long time ago. The others wore government blue over the kind of boots pirates wear, and every one of them had a rifle slung muzzle-down, which was regulation, and hands resting near the slings, which was not.

"Vessel, ahoy," the inspector called. "Water Office inspection. Kill your engine and stand fast."

Beatrix leaned on the rail and let her shoulders drop into the shape of a tired woman with nothing to hide. It wasn't hard. She was tired, and there was plenty to hide, and the trick — the whole trick, the one her father had taught her on this same river twenty years ago — was to make the lie small enough to fit inside the truth.

"Inspection," she repeated, and yawned into her hand. "Captain Yarrow, Magpie, out of Harrow Bend. You people finally got a budget for patrols, or is this a special occasion?"

The inspector's eyes flicked along her deck, cataloguing. "Special occasion. There's been a theft of government property upstream. Documents. We're searching all traffic moving downriver."

"Well, you can search mine twice if it makes you feel useful." She gestured grandly at the boat — the rusted stove pipe, the coiled lines, the crates of canned peaches she'd bought at the fishing village with money she could not spare. "You'll find six tins of peaches, a broken sextant, and enough bilge slime to float a coffin. Help yourself."

Two of the blue-coated men came over the rail without asking permission, which was answer enough about what kind of inspection this was. One went forward. One went aft, toward the cabin, and Beatrix's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of treachery against her chest.

"The clerk," the inspector said. "Who's below?"

"My passenger. He's ill. River fever — you're welcome to go breathe on him if you want it."

That bought her a pause. Fever was the one cargo nobody wanted to inspect. But the inspector was already stepping aboard, boots ringing on her deck like he owned the planks, and the man going aft had reached the cabin hatch.

"He'll live or he won't either way," the inspector said. "Open it."

* * *

Below, in the dark that smelled of lamp oil and mildew, Silas Crane lay flat on his back beneath the dinette bench with the oilcloth bundle pressed against his chest, listening to the boots overhead come through the planks like a pulse.

He had done the arithmetic three times and gotten the same terrible number each time: the bilge was thirty inches deep, the stringer gap was barely wide enough for the folio, and the inspection party was six men with rifles and one man with authority. Against that: one woman above him telling lies, one boat held together with paint and stubbornness, and himself — a man who, eleven days ago, had believed with his whole trained heart that a warrant was a wall and the government stood behind it.

The cabin hatch scraped open. Light fell in a blade across the floorboards, an inch from his face.

"River fever," said a voice above him, young, uncertain. A rifle barrel entered his field of vision, propped carelessly against the doorframe while its owner crouched to peer under the bench. Silas closed his eyes and made his breathing long and wet and labored, the way his grandmother had breathed in her last winter, and he thought: *this is what it costs, this is the price of the truth, paid in installments.*

"Fever, all right," the young voice said, backing off. "Look at him. I'm not touching him."

"Search it anyway," said another voice, harder, older. Boots crossed the cabin. Drawers opened. The false bottom of the chart drawer — Beatrix's hiding place for smuggled tobacco, in the years she'd run it — was pried up and dropped back down. A lantern was lifted, swung, set down. Somewhere a tin of peaches rolled and was kicked.

Then, close and sudden, a hand seized his ankle.

Silas kept his eyes shut and his breath rattling. The hand squeezed once, testing, the way a butcher tests meat, and let go.

"Fever," the older voice said again, but slower now, and Silas understood with cold clarity that the word had stopped being a shield and started being a question. "Where's he bound?"

Above, muffled through the deck, Beatrix's voice came easy as Sunday: "Downriver. Doctor at Tallowmouth. Paid passage, poor fool caught it swimming at the landing like an idiot. You want to write him up for it, there's a form, I'm sure."

A pause. Long enough to have weight.

"Bring him up," the older voice said. "Let the captain see him."

* * *

They dragged Silas up the companionway by both arms, and Beatrix watched it happen with her jaw set loose and her fists set tight, doing the mathematics of the deck in the space of a breath. Six rifles. Two on her forecastle, two amidships, the inspector at her rail, one at the patrol boat's gun. The sixth was hauling Silas upright, and Silas played it beautifully — sagging, coughing, gray-faced, letting his head loll like a man whose bones had been borrowed from someone older. Either he was a natural liar or he'd been rehearsing honesty so long that dishonesty came out polished.

Either way, she owed him.

"That's him," she said. "Told you. Fever."

The inspector circled Silas at arm's length, the way you circle a horse you suspect of biting. Up close, Beatrix could read him properly now: the cap was Water Office, but the tan line under it was a hatband from a different hat entirely, something wide-brimmed and civilian, and the callus on his trigger finger was rope callus, river callus, not range callus. Corven's men had worn the same hands. The government colors were a costume, and costumes were the most dangerous thing on the water, because a pirate in his own colors would rob you and be done, but a pirate in uniform needed you to disappear politely, with paperwork.

"You're Captain Yarrow," the inspector said. Not a question.

"I'm Captain Yarrow."

"Corven Line has a contract on this stretch. Fuel, towage, salvage rights." He smiled with the bottom half of his face. "Funny thing. Nobody mentioned a Magpie operating independent."

"Nobody mentioned a patrol running this far up, either. Funny river full of funny things." She took a slow drink of the cold chicory, watching him over the rim. "You want to inspect my papers? I've got papers. Harbor registration, toll receipts, a bill of health from Tallowmouth, stamped. Or you want to keep guessing at my business, which I'll warn you takes longer."

Something moved behind his eyes — respect, or its ugly cousin, recalculation. Men like him respected nothing except resistance priced correctly. Too little and you were prey; too much and you were worth killing. She had priced herself exactly in the middle, and she watched him decide to believe her, because believing her was cheaper than not.

"Search the hold," he said anyway. "And the bilge."

The word landed in her stomach like a swallowed hook. *Bilge.* Of course. Every captain hid things in the bilge; therefore every searcher looked in the bilge; therefore the bilge was the worst place on the boat, and it was exactly where she had told Silas to put the only thing on the river worth killing for.

One of the blue coats hauled up the bilge cover and leaned over with a lantern, and Beatrix made herself watch, made herself stand there with her cup in her hand and boredom on her face while the light swept the black water below her decks. The stringer ran shadowed along the port side. The frame curved in aft, a dark pocket of angle and grease. The lantern passed over it once.

Twice.

"Nothing but slime," the man reported.

"Check again."

He checked again. And Beatrix, standing at the rail with her heart going like a paddlewheel, did the only thing left in her repertoire: she got angry, because anger was the one emotion inspectors were built to dismiss.

"Oh, for the love of—" She set down the cup hard enough to ring. "You've been aboard ten minutes and cost me a tide. Search the rivets next, why don't you. Search the seams. My boat is forty years old, she leaks from everywhere God could find a seam, and the bilge is a graveyard of everything the river's given back. Fish heads. A boot. Once, a cat. If your stolen papers are down there, they're the best-preserved thing in the whole swamp, and I'd like to meet whoever wrote them, because I could use waterproof ink."

The silence stretched. The inspector looked at her. She looked back, and let him see the whole weary architecture of a woman with debts and deadlines and no patience left, and underneath it, buried so deep even she could barely find it, the defiance burning like a coal in a banked fire — *come on, then. Come and find it. Come and make me.*

He broke first. They always broke first; that was the secret nobody in uniform ever learned.

"Log her position," he said to the man with the notebook. "Magpie, Yarrow, master, southbound, passenger one, medical. We'll be seeing you again, Captain. This stretch is going to get very well patrolled."

"Lucky river," Beatrix said.

They went over the rail. The engines woke, grumbled, gathered themselves, and the patrol boat swung away downstream trailing its bright flag and its gray smoke, and Beatrix stood at the rail and waved at it, actually waved, a small ironic flutter of the fingers, because the last thing a hunted thing should do is look relieved.

She held the wave until the boat rounded the willow bend. Then she set down the cup, walked to the bilge cover on legs that had gone strange and distant beneath her, and pulled it up.

Silas was already climbing out of the after pocket, the oilcloth bundle clutched to his chest, soaked to the waist in bilge water and shaking — not from the cold, she thought, though the cold was bad enough. From the other thing. From having lain still while a stranger's hand tested his ankle like meat.

"They checked it twice," he said hoarsely.

"They checked it twice," she agreed.

"How."

"Because I told them the truth." She hauled him the rest of the way up and steadied him with a grip on his forearm, and found she didn't let go right away. "I told them the bilge was garbage, and I was furious about it, and fury is the one costume that fits me better than any lie. Never hide a thing where you'd hide a thing, Silas. Hide it where you'd complain about it."

He looked at her — really looked, the way he'd begun looking at her since the warrant burned, since the leak, since the nights of bailing side by side — and shook his head slowly, water dripping from his hair onto her deck.

"You lied to armed men of the government," he said, "without blinking. And I'm the criminal."

"You're learning." She released his arm and turned away to hide whatever her face was doing, because her hands had finally started to tremble now that it was safe for them to, and she hated witnesses to that particular ceremony. "They logged our position. 'We'll be seeing you again.' That wasn't courtesy. That was a bookmark. They'll report to whoever sent them, and whoever sent them will do the sums, and the sums won't balance — a fevered clerk and a peach-hauling scow don't add up to why a patrol boat burned a morning on us."

"So they'll come back."

"They'll come back with better questions." She looked downriver, at the smooth gray water and the empty bend, and felt the fear in her chest finish its slow transmutation into something harder and more useful. Fear was a passenger. Defiance was crew. "Which means we don't give them time. We run tonight, dark water, no lights, past the narrows before anyone does those sums."

Silas followed her gaze downstream, then upstream, toward the high country where the gorge waited with its throat full of rising water.

"And after the narrows?" he asked quietly. "Where does the river take us?"

Beatrix picked up her cold chicory and drank it like it was a toast.

"Downstream," she said. "All the way. Whatever's at the bottom of it."

Behind them, far off around the bend, faint as a memory, a whistle sounded — two short blasts and one long, the Water Office signal for *vessel sighted*, passed from some unseen station to some unseen ears, and the river carried it away downstream faster than any boat could follow.
