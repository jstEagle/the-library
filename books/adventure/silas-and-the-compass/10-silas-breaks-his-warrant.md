## 10. Silas Breaks His Warrant

The rain had followed them all day like a debt collector — patient, unhurried, certain of payment. By the time Beatrix nosed the *Magpie* into a cutbank of overhanging fig and killed the engine, Silas's warrant case had soaked through at one corner, and he found himself holding it away from his chest as though it were something dead.

Which, he was beginning to understand, it was.

He climbed down into the cabin while she worked above — checking lines, testing the anchor, muttering to the boat in that low private language she used for no one else. The cabin smelled of lamp oil, wet canvas, and the ghost of a hundred cargoes. He set the oilcloth packet on the little table bolted beneath the porthole, sat across from it, and did not touch it for a long time.

Through the round glass he could see the river sliding past, brown and swollen now, carrying whole islands of debris downstream. A fence post. Half a roof. Something pale and bloated he chose not to identify. The river was in its monsoon mood, and everything loose in the world was being carried toward the delta whether it consented or not.

His orders were in the packet. His commission. The letter of instruction signed by Deputy Director Halloway himself, with the ministry seal pressed deep enough to feel under a thumbnail. *Proceed upriver. Locate survey team. Recover all documents and instruments. Report findings to this office only.*

*To this office only.*

He had read those words a hundred times since the camp. At first they'd meant nothing — bureaucratic boilerplate, the usual territorial fussing of men who guarded information the way dogs guarded bones. But the drowned man had changed the arithmetic. And the burned trading post had changed it again. And the patrol boat flying government colors, searching for *stolen documents* — that had finished the sum entirely.

Silas Crane had spent eleven years in government service, and in eleven years he had learned exactly one lesson worth learning: institutions do not lie to protect themselves from strangers. They lie to protect themselves from the people inside them.

He was inside them.

Above him, Beatrix's boots crossed the deck. He heard her pause near the stern rail, heard the small metallic sound of her flask opening, then closing unopened. She hadn't drunk since the patrol boat. He'd noticed. She probably thought he hadn't.

He opened the packet.

* * *

The paper was good stock, heavy, watermarked with the ministry's triple-key crest. Halloway's signature sprawled across the bottom in violet ink, confident as a man signing someone else's death warrant — which, Silas thought, was precisely what it might have been. Five names were listed below his own: the survey crew. Okafor. Lindqvist. The Maren twins. A junior cartographer named Pell whose handwriting on the recovered fragments had been careful, almost loving, each sample point annotated like a letter to a friend.

Four names unaccounted for. One face-down in the shallows with a chain wrapped twice around his ribs.

Silas laid the pages out on the table. Orders, commission, travel authorization, a second letter of introduction to the fort commandant at Vell's Landing — *nearest friendly post*, Halloway had said, *in case of difficulty*. In case of difficulty. As if the difficulty had been anticipated. As if the route home had been planned before Silas ever boarded the *Magpie*, and planned around the assumption that whatever he found upriver would need to be contained.

He thought about the fort at Vell's Landing. He'd never been there, but he knew the type: a garrison commander with a comfortable arrangement with the shipping interests, a telegraph line straight back to the capital, and a yard where a boat could be quietly impounded and two passengers quietly detained pending review. Pending review. The phrase had a way of becoming permanent.

And Corven. Corven's name wasn't on any of these papers, but Corven's money moved through every channel these papers touched. The shipping magnate owned the barges that hauled ore nobody admitted mining. Owned warehouses along the middle river. Owned, if half of what Beatrix had told him over cold rice and lamplight was true, the debts of half the pilots on the Kestrel — including, until recently, the woman overhead right now, coiling rope in the rain.

If Silas walked into Vell's Landing with the folio and his warrant, he would be delivering everything — the evidence, the witness, the boat, the woman — directly into the hands of the men who had murdered five people to keep it buried.

He had known this for three days. He had known it the way you know a storm is coming: intellectually, from the color of the sky, while telling yourself the barometer might be wrong.

Tonight the barometer was not wrong.

Footsteps on the companionway ladder. Beatrix ducked into the cabin, shaking rain from her hair, her oilskin gleaming dull yellow in the lamplight. She took in the spread papers, the open packet, his face. She didn't ask. She poured tea from the dented kettle on the spirit stove instead — two cups, no question about the second — and sat down across from him, cradling hers.

"You've got the look," she said.

"What look?"

"The look of a man arguing with a document." She blew on her tea. "I've seen it before. Usually on the faces of bank examiners, right before they find something the bank doesn't want found."

"The fort at Vell's Landing," Silas said. "How far?"

"Day and a half, water willing. Less if the flood does the work for us." Her eyes came up, sharp and level. "Why?"

"Because I'm supposed to deliver you there. Deliver *this* there." He put his hand flat on the orders. "Friendly post. Secure storage. Telegraph access."

"And?" She said it gently, which was worse than saying it hard. "You've figured it out, then."

"I've known it since the trading post. Maybe since the camp." He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "No. That's a lie, and I've had enough of lies today, even mine. I've known it since Halloway handed me the commission and said *discreetly, Crane, very discreetly*. Eleven years in the service, and I heard *discreetly* and thought it meant efficiency. It meant *deniability*. It meant that when this went wrong, no one in the capital would ever have heard my name."

Beatrix set her cup down. "So go to the fort anyway. Give them the folio, wash your hands, catch a steamer home. Men have done less and slept fine."

"I couldn't sleep fine."

"No," she agreed quietly. "I don't suppose you could. You bailed bilge water with me till three in the morning and apologized to the pump. You're not built for sleeping fine."

He almost smiled. Then the weight came back, because the weight never really left; it just waited for gaps in conversation to settle in.

"There's something else," he said. "Something I should have said days ago, and didn't, because saying it makes it real."

"Say it anyway."

He looked at the papers. All that good heavy paper, all those seals, all that confidence in violet ink.

"My superiors sent me upriver knowing what happened to the survey team. Or suspecting it strongly enough that they planned around it. They gave me a warrant and a purse and pointed me at a grave, and the plan was always that I would either find nothing — or find something and bring it back to be buried deeper." He turned the commission over so the seal faced up between them. "They didn't send an investigator, Beatrix. They sent a cleaner who didn't know he was a cleaner. And the only reason I'm still breathing is that I hired a pilot stubborn enough to keep us alive long enough for me to notice."

She was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed on the deck above them, soft and constant, like fingers tapping on a coffin lid.

"So what are you going to do about it?" she asked.

* * *

What he was going to do about it required kerosene, and the ship's galley had a small tin of it for the stove.

He stood at the aft rail with the papers in one hand and the tin in the other, and the rain had thinned to a drifting mist that caught the last grey light. Beatrix leaned against the rail beside him, arms folded, watching without comment. She had not tried to stop him. She had not encouraged him either. She had simply come up on deck when he asked her to witness, because — she said — things done in secret rotted, and things done in front of a witness held their shape.

"That's Halloway's signature," she observed.

"Yes."

"He sign anything else for you? Anything useful?"

"Just these."

"Pity. I'd have liked to burn his actual hand."

Silas struck the match. The wind off the water tried to take the flame, and he cupped it, and touched it to the corner of the commission.

Paper catches slowly at first, reluctantly, the way old loyalties do. Then all at once. Fire ran up the page and the triple-key seal blackened and curled, and the violet ink seemed to glow for a heartbeat before the flame swallowed it, and Halloway's confident signature blistered and lifted away on the smoke like a bird leaving a sinking ship.

Silas fed the rest in piece by piece. Orders. Travel authorization. The letter of introduction to the commandant at Vell's Landing — he held that one longest, watching the fort's name char and vanish, watching the word *friendly* become ash.

The heat stung his fingers. He let it.

"You know what this makes you," Beatrix said, watching the embers spin off downstream. "As far as the ministry is concerned, you're not an investigator anymore. You're a fugitive carrying stolen property. Same as me."

"I know."

"And if we're caught, there's no warrant to wave. No office to appeal to. No one coming."

"I know."

"They'll call it theft. Treason, maybe, if Corven's friends are creative. You'll lose your pension, your name, probably your freedom." She turned to look at him fully, and in the dying light her eyes were the exact color of the river. "Do you understand what you're giving up?"

He watched the last of the commission dissolve. Eleven years. A career built carefully, brick by brick, on the assumption that the institution was crooked only at the edges, that the rot was local, correctable, a matter of finding the right honest man and handing him the file. He had believed that at thirty. He had still half-believed it at forty. Standing here, with ash on his cuffs and a murdered crewman behind him and a burned trading post behind that, he found the belief finally, completely gone — and discovered that underneath it there was something simpler and older and much harder to kill.

"It isn't giving up," he said. "It's putting it down. There's a difference. Giving up is when you stop believing the thing can be done. This—" he nodded at the water, where the last ember hissed out— "this is believing it can only be done somewhere else, by some other means, than the ones I was given."

"Poetic," Beatrix said. "For a government man."

"Ex-government man."

"Ex-fugitive-in-progress." But she was smiling faintly, and she knocked her shoulder against his, and the contact was brief and warm and steadied something in him that the fire hadn't.

He turned to face her. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the walk to the rail, and the rehearsal fell apart immediately, the way rehearsals do.

"I need to ask you for something," he said. "And it's more than I have any right to ask."

"Those are usually the interesting ones."

"Not Vell's Landing. Not the fort. Take me past it." He drew a breath. "There's a press town in the delta. Meridian Bar. Independent papers — the *Tidewater Ledger*, the *Confluence Star* — printers who've survived three administrations trying to shut them down. There's a federal circuit judge, Ada Okonjo, who holds court there twice a season, and by every account I've ever heard she cannot be bought, frightened, or managed. If that folio goes anywhere official, it dies. If it goes to Meridian Bar — to print, and to her courtroom at the same time — it becomes too public to bury. Too many hands on it. Corven can't drown a newspaper the way he drowned a survey team."

Beatrix was silent. Below them the river muttered against the hull, high and restless, full of everything it was carrying away.

"Meridian Bar is six days downriver," she said at last. "Through the gorge road, or around it if the slides have closed it, which they will have. Through water I don't trust with a wounded hull. Past patrol boats, past Corven's people, past every sandbar on the Kestrel wearing a different flood-season shape than the one I memorized." She looked at him. "You're asking me to run my boat — my *whole world* — six days deeper into the mouth of the wolf, on behalf of a man who until a month ago was just a fare with a fat purse."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you put me ashore at the next village and I finish it alone, badly, probably dying, and you'll have lost nothing but a passenger." He held her gaze. "But I don't think you'll say no. Because you read that melted page at the trading post same as I did, and I've watched you carry it ever since. You're angry, Beatrix. You've been angry since the drowned man came up out of the shallows wearing a chain. You can pretend it's just business — fare is fare, cargo is cargo — but it stopped being business the day you decided to hide that folio in your own bilge rather than hand it to men in government colors. You're already in this. I'm just asking you to choose the direction."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere upstream, thunder spoke once, low and rolling, a warning or a promise.

Beatrix looked out at the darkening water for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was quiet, almost lost under the river.

"My father piloted this stretch for thirty years," she said. "Taught me every bend, every lie the current tells. He used to say the river doesn't care whose side you're on — it only cares whether you're paying attention. Corven owns banks and barges and half the magistrates on the middle Kestrel. But he doesn't own the water. Nobody owns the water." She pushed off the rail and straightened, and something in her bearing shifted — the weariness burning off, the pilot coming forward. "Meridian Bar. Six days. We'll need provisions at the next village, and we'll need to move at dawn, and if the gorge road is closed we'll have problems I don't even want to describe yet."

"Thank you," Silas said.

"Don't thank me yet." She paused at the companionway, one hand on the rail, and glanced back at him with an expression he couldn't wholly read — part warning, part something softer. "You broke your warrant tonight, Crane. From here on, you're not cargo and you're not a fare. You're crew. Crew pulls weight, stands watches, and doesn't argue with the pilot about the channel."

"I can live with that."

"We'll see." She started down the ladder, then stopped. "One more thing. That folio — the missing one, the one we're betting six lives on. You said the pages we recovered were fragments. Samples. Ore assays from land that's supposed to be empty."

"Yes."

"Then whoever tore the rest out wanted the *names* gone, not the numbers. Numbers prove ore. Names prove conspiracy." Her eyes narrowed. "Which means somewhere ahead of us, someone is still carrying that folio — and they haven't destroyed it yet, or they'd have burned it with the trading post. They're keeping it. Moving it. Which means it's close."

Silas felt the cold slide down his spine, slow as the current under the keel.

Upstream, the thunder rolled again — closer now, and the first fat drops of the night's rain began to strike the deck like footsteps.
