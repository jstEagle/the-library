## 11. The Gorge Road Closed

The rain came down in ropes.

Beatrix stood at the Magpie's wheel with her collar up and her eyes narrowed against the gray, watching the river climb the banks like something escaping a cage. Two days of monsoon had turned the Kestrel from a brown ribbon into a rolling beast, and every hour it rose another hand's width up the pilings, up the rocks, up the drowned willows that waved their branches under water like drowning men.

Silas came up from the cabin with two mugs of tea and set one on the chart table without a word. He'd learned that much about her — she didn't like to talk while running water.

"Kettle's leaking again," he said.

"Everything on this boat leaks. It's how she breathes." She took the mug. It was terrible tea, stewed black, and she drank it anyway.

They had been running since dawn, and running was the wrong word. Crawling, more like. The current fought them at every bend, and the rain made the channel markers useless — half of them were under, the rest were leaning at angles that lied. Beatrix ran on memory and instinct, reading the boils and eddies the way Silas read his documents, and even that was getting harder, because the river she knew was disappearing under the river it was becoming.

"Should be the gorge by midday," Silas said. He'd spread the survey map under an oilcloth and weighted the corners with brass fittings. "There's a town marked here. Gorge Landing. Fuel, I'd think. Telegraph."

"There's a landing," Beatrix said. "Fuel, maybe. Telegraph, if the line's still up. Town, if you're generous."

They came around the long bend below the gorge an hour later, and Beatrix knew before she saw it. She knew by the water — the current had gone strange, slack and confused, backing up on itself in slow spirals, the way it did when something downstream was holding its breath. She throttled down and stood very still.

"What is it?"

"Quiet." She listened. Under the drum of rain on the cabin roof, under the engine's laboring knock, there should have been the gorge — a low, constant thunder, the sound of the Kestrel forcing itself through the narrows three miles on. Every pilot on this river knew that sound the way a man knows his own heartbeat.

The gorge was silent.

She brought the Magpie in toward the landing, past the mooring stakes, and the town assembled itself out of the rain piece by piece. A fuel dock. A warehouse with its doors open. A row of houses climbing the hillside, dark and empty. And above it all, where the river should have run clean through the gorge road, a mountain of earth and broken trees lay across the channel like a fist slammed down on a table.

Landslide. The whole south slope had let go. She could see the scar of it even through the rain, a raw brown wound a quarter mile long, and the river had backed up behind it into a lake that hadn't existed a week ago, drowning the landing, the low fields, the bottom floor of the warehouse.

Silas came to stand beside her at the rail. He didn't say anything for a long time.

"That's the safe channel," he said finally.

"That's the only channel."

"There has to be another way around. A portage, a road—"

"The gorge road's under that." She nodded at the slide. "The high road washed out two days ago, if the rain up-country is anything like it is here. And the east trail is a goat track in dry weather." She turned and looked at him, and let him see it plain on her face. "We're not going around, Crane. We're going through, or we're not going."

"Through that?" He looked upriver, at the gorge mouth, and she watched him try to read it the way she did. He couldn't. Nobody could who hadn't spent twenty years learning the Kestrel's handwriting. "It's flooded. It's — Beatrix, look at it."

"I'm looking at it."

"It's a wall of water between two walls of rock."

"It is," she agreed. "And in about a day, maybe two, the rain will peak and start to fall off, and the river will drop, and for a few hours the gorge will run high but honest — big water, clean lines, no surprises. That's the window. Every pilot on this river waits for the window or dies waiting for a better one that doesn't come."

"And until then?"

"Until then we're stuck here." She said it flat, because there was no other way to say it, and she watched the word land on him. Stuck. Trapped. A tin can with two wanted people inside it, sitting on a flooded plain with every hour of rain closing another ring around them.

She tied up at the fuel dock — what was left of it, a foot of freeboard showing above the brown water — and went to find the harbormaster.

* * *

The harbormaster was a woman named Odekirk, seventy if she was a day, who had stayed through the flood because, she said, somebody had to count the drowned. She sat in the upper room of her house with a stove and a shotgun across her knees and told Beatrix what Beatrix had already guessed.

"Slide came down three nights ago. Took the Kestrel house and the ferry landing with it. Eleven souls, soon as we can count. River's backed up six miles. It'll cut through when it's ready — it always does — but it'll cut through ugly, and it won't be done for days."

"I don't need days," Beatrix said. "I need a window."

Odekirk looked at her over the shotgun. "You're the Magpie. Yarrow. I know your hull. You're thinking of running the gorge."

"I'm thinking of a lot of things."

"Yarrow." The old woman set the shotgun down and leaned forward. "I've lived on this water sixty years. I've seen four men run the gorge in flood and I've seen one body come out the bottom. The river doesn't care how good you are. The river doesn't even know you're there."

"I know how the river feels about me, ma'am. I've had the relationship a long time."

"Then you know the window you're talking about may not come at all. If the rain holds, the river holds, and the slide holds, and you sit here till your pursuers come down the high road and find you tied up like a Christmas goose."

Silas, who had been quiet by the door, spoke up. "Pursuers."

Odekirk glanced at him, then back at Beatrix, and something in her old face shifted — not surprise, but the weary recognition of a woman who had seen trouble come up this river in every color the government printed. "Patrol launch came through yesterday morning," she said. "Before the slide, running hard. Government colors, eight men, asked after a riverboat heading upriver. Asked after a government man." Her eyes went to Silas's soaked coat, his city boots, his face that had never once fooled anyone on this river. "I told them I hadn't seen a soul."

"Thank you," Silas said.

"Don't thank me. I lied to them because I don't like their kind, not because I like yours." She stood, joints cracking like kindling, and went to the window. Through the rain-streaked glass, the drowned valley stretched away gray and endless, and the gorge mouth sat in the distance like a closed door. "You can anchor in the old millpond, back of the warehouse. Out of the current, out of the channel. Any boat coming up or down will pass you by. But Yarrow — if they come looking, they'll come looking everywhere. There's no hole on this river deep enough to hide a boat that doesn't want to be found."

"I know," Beatrix said. "I've spent my whole life in holes exactly that deep."

* * *

They anchored in the millpond as the light failed.

It was a strange, drowned place — the old mill had burned years back, and all that remained were the brick stumps and the rusted wheel housing standing out of six feet of floodwater, and the Magpie rode at anchor among them like a ghost moored to the bones of another ghost. The rain kept on. It had stopped being weather and become a condition, a fact of the world, something that had always been and would always be, and Beatrix sat in the wheelhouse with the lamps low and listened to it hammer the roof and felt the walls of her trap drawing in by inches.

Silas sat across the chart table from her with the survey pages spread out under the lamp, working at them again with his magnifier, though they'd been over them a dozen times. She watched his hands — long, careful hands, a clerk's hands, moving over the water-stained paper with a tenderness she found herself noticing more than she wanted to.

"The ore samples," he said, not looking up. "The assay numbers in the margin. If they're real, it's not gravel claims Corven's protecting. It's a mountain. It's the biggest mineral strike on this river in fifty years, hidden inside a survey boundary that was redrawn to make it vanish." He set the magnifier down. "That's what the team found. That's why they're dead."

"Then the folio isn't just evidence," Beatrix said. "It's the mine itself, on paper. Whoever holds it owns the story."

"Whoever holds it is worth more alive to Corven than either of us." He said it evenly, but she saw his jaw tighten. "We're the packaging."

"Charming."

He almost smiled. It was a thin thing, worn down by the last two hundred miles, but it was there. "You could still turn me in, you know. Take the launch to the nearest fort, hand over the folio, collect whatever Corven's paying for my return. It's not too late for you."

"It was too late for me around the time I hid a government folio in my bilge," Beatrix said. "Besides." She looked out the wheelhouse window at the drowned mill, at the rain, at the black shape of the gorge miles upriver, holding back its lake like a held breath. "I don't much like being packaging."

The lamp hissed. The rain hammered. Somewhere out in the flooded dark, a branch cracked and fell, and both of them went still, hands drifting — hers to the shotgun under the wheel, his to the revolver he still carried like a man who resented it — until the sound resolved into nothing but the river doing what rivers do.

They didn't sleep much. She took the first watch in the wheelhouse, wrapped in a blanket, and he took the second, and in the dead hours she came up to find him standing at the window with a mug of the terrible tea gone cold in his hand, watching the gorge.

"I keep thinking about them," he said quietly, without turning. "The survey team. Eleven people. They were doing their jobs. They found something true, and they wrote it down, and men came in the night with rope and weights." He looked at her. "And now the truth is on my boat, and I keep thinking — I will not let this river take it. I've lost everything else to institutions. Not this."

"The river takes what it wants," Beatrix said. "But it can be argued with. I've been arguing with it twenty years, and I've won more often than I've lost."

"And the gorge?"

She looked up at the black gap in the hills, where the backed-up lake pressed against the slide, waiting.

"The gorge," she said, "is going to be the longest argument of my life."

* * *

The rain peaked on the second day, and did not stop.

It came down harder than before, if such a thing was possible, a solid gray roaring, and the millpond rose until the Magpie's anchor line stood straight down and the old mill wheel housing went under entirely. Beatrix stood at the rail and watched the valley become a lake become a sea, and she felt it in her bones the way she felt weather in the old scar on her knee: the river was gathering itself. The lake behind the slide was rising faster than it could seep through. Somewhere up there, the water was finding its shoulders, setting its feet, preparing to throw the mountain off its back.

When it did, it would go through the gorge like the wrath of God, and after it passed — if she read it right, if the rain broke when it should — there would be a window. High, violent, honest water. A day, maybe less.

They would have to be at the gorge mouth when it opened. And to get there they would have to cross six miles of open flooded country under a sky full of rain, in a boat that leaked, past a shoreline that any patrol launch could sweep with a searchlight.

Silas came up beside her. He didn't ask what she was thinking. He'd stopped asking; he'd learned to read her face the way she read water.

"Odekirk says the high road's closed," he said. "Landslides above the pass. Nothing's coming overland for days."

"Nothing official," Beatrix said. "Corven's men don't wait for roads to open. They wait for boats." She turned her collar up. "And there's exactly one boat on this stretch of river, and it's us."

The rain fell. The lake rose. And far off through the gray, at the mouth of the gorge, Beatrix saw something that made her go cold all the way through — a light. Moving. A searchlight beam, sweeping the flooded water at the foot of the slide, patient as a lighthouse.

Somebody was already at the window.

They were watching the door.
