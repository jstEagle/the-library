## 12. Night Ambush at the Anchorage

**BEATRIX**

The anchorage behind Gull Rock was the only shelter for twenty miles, and Beatrix hated it on principle. A good anchorage was a trap that hadn't sprung yet — a bowl of still water ringed by walls, with one mouth to the river and every sound you made coming back at you doubled off the stone.

She anchored anyway. There was no choice in it. The Magpie's patched hull had held through the afternoon, but the patch was weeping slow and steady, and Silas had spent the last hour bailing by lantern light while she nursed the engine at half-throttle up the last reach of channel. The boat needed a night of quiet. So did they both.

"Port anchor's set," Silas called from the bow, his voice flattened by rain. "Bottom feels like gravel."

"It is gravel. Thirty feet down, maybe less." She killed the engine and let the silence come in, that thick river silence broken only by rain ticking on the deck boards and the groan of the anchor chain. Above them Gull Rock stood black against a sky the color of wet slate, its shoulders already streaming with runoff. The gorge lay two miles upstream, closed to them, thundering somewhere out of sight like a beast pacing behind a door.

Silas came aft, wringing out his hat. In the lamplight his face looked older than it had at her dock, weeks and a lifetime ago. "How long do we wait?"

"Until the river tells us." She checked the bilge — six inches of water, no worse than an hour ago. The patch would hold if nothing hit it. "The surge peaks tomorrow, maybe the day after. When the crest comes through the gorge, the level here will jump three feet in an hour. Then it starts falling. That's our window."

"And until then?"

"Until then we're a rock in a bowl of soup, and everyone who wants what's in my bilge knows exactly where we are."

He didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing days ago, which she appreciated more than she'd ever say aloud. Instead he went below and came back with hardtack, dried fish, and the folio wrapped in oilcloth, which he'd taken to carrying against his chest like a second heart.

They ate without talking. The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the gorge walls and kept rolling, longer than any thunder had a right to.

Beatrix took first watch because she always did, and because she didn't trust her own sleep tonight. She sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low, watching the black water, listening to the river breathe. Her hands rested on the wheel out of habit. The Magpie trembled under her palms, faint as a pulse.

She thought about Corven's voice on the wire. *Bring me the man and the paper, girl, and we'll call it even.* Even. As if twenty years of her labor could be weighed against two murders and a drowned survey team. As if she were cargo he could route.

"You should sleep," Silas said from the companionway. He'd appeared without sound, which she was beginning to suspect was a professional habit rather than a personal failing.

"In a minute."

He settled onto the bench beside her instead of leaving, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away. Outside, the rain came down harder, and the river rose by inches, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

**SILAS**

Silas woke to a wrongness he couldn't name.

It wasn't a sound. It was the absence of one — the rain had softened, and beneath it the night had gone too quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone has just stopped moving in it. He lay in the narrow bunk below, staring at the overhead, counting heartbeats. Then he heard it: a low, rhythmic knock, far off across the water. Oars. Muffled oars, wrapped in cloth, the way men row when they don't want to be heard.

He was up the ladder before he'd finished deciding to move.

Beatrix was already at the wheelhouse window, motionless, one hand raised for silence. Through the streaming glass he saw nothing but dark water and darker rock. But she was looking at something, and her stillness had the quality of a drawn blade.

"How many?" he breathed.

"Two boats. Maybe three. Coming around the north point, riding the eddy so they don't have to row much." Her voice was flat and calm, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "Corven hires locals when he can't spare his own men. Fishermen, mostly. Good with boats, better with knives."

"Guns?"

"Some. They'll want the folio intact. That means they'll board us, not sink us." She turned, and in the dimness her eyes caught the lamplight. "Which gives us one advantage."

"What advantage?"

"They have to come to us."

She was already moving, killing the lamp, dropping the wheelhouse into darkness. "Get below. Take the folio. If I go down or the boat does, you take the dory and run downstream alone. Don't wait for me. Don't be noble. Noble gets people killed on this river."

"I'm not leaving you."

"That wasn't a request, Crane."

"It wasn't an answer, Yarrow."

For a moment neither spoke. Rain drummed on the roof. Out on the water, the muffled knocking grew closer, patient, inevitable.

Then Beatrix smiled — a thin, fierce thing he'd learned to fear and love in equal measure. "Fine. Then make yourself useful. There's a block of pig iron in the engine room, forty pounds, chained to the frame. Cut it loose. And find me every length of line aboard that isn't holding the boat together."

"Why?"

"Because," she said, moving to the stern windows, "the river brought us this anchorage, and the river is going to help us keep it."

* * *

**BEATRIX**

She felt it before she could prove it — a change in the water's voice against the hull, a deepening hum through the timbers. Upstream, past the closed gorge, the storm had been gathering its strength all day behind the landslide, backing up like a fist drawing back. Every hour the pool behind that choke grew deeper, and every hour the weight pressing on the slide grew greater.

Rivers always break their own dams eventually.

She didn't know when. Nobody knew when. But she knew what happened in an anchorage when it came: the whole bowl of still water would rise fast and turn, the eddies reversing, the current swinging through the gap like a gate thrown open. Any boat caught broadside in that first surge would roll. Any men standing on her deck when it hit would go into the water, and the water would not give them back.

So her plan was simple and terrible: survive long enough for the river to fight for her.

The first boat came out of the dark at the port bow — a long, low fishing skiff running dark, six men in it, faces smeared with ash against the lamplight she didn't have. Grapples went over her rail with soft thuds. Boots hit her deck. Beatrix stayed low behind the wheelhouse, knife in her teeth and a boathook in her hands, and let them come.

They were quick and quiet and professional. Two went forward to secure the bow. Three came aft, toward the cabin where Silas waited. The sixth — big, scarred, carrying himself like the leader Corven's money had bought — paused at the rail and put a whistle to his lips.

Beatrix swung the boathook out of the dark and took him across the throat.

Not deep — she wasn't a murderer, whatever else she was — but deep enough. He folded with a strangled sound, and the whistle skittered across the deck planks, and the night split open anyway as the second boat surged in from the starboard quarter, men shouting now, muzzle flashes stitching white holes in the rain.

A bullet kissed the wheelhouse beside her ear and blew splinters across her cheek. She rolled, came up inside a gunman's reach, and drove the boathook's brass ferrule into his knee. He screamed and went down. The deck tilted underfoot — the Magpie groaning as six extra men shifted her balance — and somewhere below, Silas was hammering at the pig-iron chain with a cold chisel, each blow ringing through the hull like a bell tolling.

"BEATRIX!" His voice, raw with effort. "I CAN'T—"

"HOLD!" she screamed back. "THIRTY SECONDS!"

Because she could feel it now, rising through her boots, through the deck, through the soles of her feet and into her teeth. The river's hum had become a roar. The water in the anchorage, still black and mirror-flat between the flashes, had begun to move — sliding toward the gap, swirling, climbing. Gull Rock's base had vanished. The anchor chain came taut with a sound like a struck cable, humming.

The gunmen felt it too. The boarding party hesitated, boots shifting on a deck that had begun to lean.

Then the world broke open.

* * *

**SILAS**

Later, Silas would never be able to describe the sound except as the end of something. Not an explosion — a release, vast and total, as though some enormous hand had lifted the gorge's blockade away and said *go.*

The surge came through the anchorage gap like a wall of brown muscle. It hit the Magpie amidships and threw her sideways; Silas went off his feet, clutching the folio to his chest, slamming into the engine room bulkhead. Through the open hatch he saw the river rise four feet in the space of ten seconds, saw the anchored skiffs swing wildly, saw men lose their footing on the Magpie's canted deck and grab for rails that were suddenly angled like ship's ladders.

One of them lost his grip.

The river took him the way it takes everything — instantly, indifferently, completely. He was there, and then he was a splash, and then he was gone, swallowed by water moving faster than any man could swim.

The others understood at once, in the wordless way animals understand fire. The boarding party broke. Men leapt for their skiffs, missed, hit the water, thrashed. One made it to a gunwale and hung there while the skiff surfed sideways down the anchorage, spinning. The grapple lines snapped taut and parted with cracks like rifle shots, taking chunks of rail with them.

The Magpie herself swung wild on her remaining anchor, and Silas heard the terrible music of her pain — timber flexing past its tolerance, the patched section of hull grinding against something submerged, a new sound joining the old ones, deeper and wetter.

"BEATRIX!"

She was in the wheelhouse, fighting the wheel, hair plastered to her face, and she was laughing — a wild, gasping sound that had terror underneath it. "SLIP THE ANCHOR! WE RIDE IT OUT OR WE SWING INTO THE ROCKS!"

He hauled the windlass with his arms burning, and the chain ran free, and then they were loose in the flood, the Magpie swept bodily through the anchorage gap and out into the main channel, surfing the crest of the surge like a leaf on a spilling cup.

The river carried them a mile downstream in minutes, through rain and chaos, past floating debris and the distant cries of men in the water — cries that faded behind them and did not stop, and would not stop, and Silas pressed his face against the wheelhouse glass and made himself listen, because those were the sounds of what survival cost, and he refused to look away from the bill for it.

* * *

**BEATRIX**

Dawn found them anchored again, twelve miles downstream, in a flooded side slough where the current was kind. The rain had gentled to a gray drizzle. The river around them was high and brown and full of the storm's debris — branches, fence rails, a drowned pig turning slowly — the whole watershed's wreckage on its way to the sea.

Beatrix surveyed her boat and felt each wound as her own.

The starboard rail was gone in an eight-foot stretch, torn away with the grapples. The wheelhouse sported a bullet hole framed by splinters, at head height, at the spot where she'd been standing. The patched hull section had opened again during the grind through the gap; the bilge pump ran constantly now, and won the fight only barely. Her beautiful, battered Magpie sat lower in the water than she'd ever sat, breathing through a wound that wouldn't close.

"She'll make the delta," Silas said quietly, beside her. It wasn't quite a question.

"She'll make the delta," Beatrix agreed, and didn't believe it, and knew he didn't either.

Below them, the river ran on, swollen and triumphant, having done in one night what armies couldn't. Somewhere upstream, the gorge was open now — the slide blown through, the road clear. Which meant the hunters' road was clear too. Whatever boats Corven had waiting above the gorge could come through now, fast, with the flood at their backs.

Silas followed her gaze upstream and understood. "How long do we have?"

"A day. Less, if they're motivated." She turned from the rail and found him watching her, and something passed between them in the gray light — not comfort, exactly, but recognition. Two people standing in the wreckage of the night, still upright, still together.

"Then we don't stop," he said. "We run the rest of the way on this flood, day and night, and we beat them to the delta."

"On a hull that's opening like a bad argument." She almost laughed. "You've learned how rivers work, Crane."

"I've learned how you work. Close enough."

She wanted to kiss him. She didn't, because the watch needed keeping and the bilge needed pumping and the river gave nothing for free — not even moments. But she let her shoulder rest against his, and let it stay there, and that was its own kind of vow.

Behind them, far upstream, a thin gray smudge of smoke rose above the tree line where the gorge met the sky. Engines, running hot through the flood, coming down fast.

Beatrix watched it for a long moment, then went to the wheel.

"Hold on to something," she said. "We're going to need every mile this river gives us."
