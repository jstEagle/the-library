## 13. Love Against Duty

The storm came for them in earnest on the third night of the wait, and the gorge answered with a voice like the end of the world.

Silas had heard storms described all his life — in dispatches, in reports, in the careful bureaucratic language of men who had never stood under one — but nothing in that vocabulary prepared him for the sound the Kestrel made when the monsoon finally committed itself whole. It was not rain. It was the sky surrendering its entire cargo at once, and the river rising to meet it halfway, and somewhere below the anchorage where the *Magpie* rode at four anchors and two mooring lines, the gorge was swallowing boulders and giving back thunder.

They had tied off in a slot of dead water behind a rock island — Beatrix called it the Kitchen, because the current eddied there like water in a pot — and even so, the boat shuddered every time the surge came through, a long rolling concussion you felt in your teeth before you heard it.

Silas sat at the little table under the lamp, the recovered survey pages spread out before him inside oilcloth sleeves, and tried to work. He had been trying to work for three hours. The numbers swam. Not because they were difficult — he had spent eleven years making difficult things legible to men who signed his pay — but because every few minutes the hull would groan, and he would find himself listening not to the storm but to the wheelhouse above, where Beatrix Yarrow had been sitting her watch alone since dusk.

He gave up pretending at half past ten, folded the pages away, and climbed the ladder.

* * *

The wheelhouse was a small glassed box of wood and brass, lit by a single gimbaled lantern, and it was shaking like a drum someone was learning to play. Rain came at the windows sideways and turned the world beyond into gray rope. Beatrix stood at the wheel she didn't need to hold — the anchors held them; the wheel was habit, or prayer — with her coat collar up and her hair plastered flat, watching the black water as if it might try something while she blinked.

"You should be sleeping," she said without turning. She always knew. He'd stopped wondering how.

"So should you."

"I sleep when the river does." A ghost of a smile. "It's being very awake tonight."

He came to stand beside her at the window. Close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not quite. They had been not-quite-touching for days now, a geometry both of them maintained with the precision of surveyors, and Silas was tired of it in a way that had nothing to do with exhaustion.

"The pages are legible," he said. "Mostly. The heat-sealed folio survived better than I expected. There's a shipping ledger interleaved with the ore samples — consignments routed through Corven's warehouses, dated before the survey team ever filed a report."

"Which means the ore was moving before anyone official knew it existed."

"Which means the survey wasn't a survey. It was an audit nobody ordered." He watched the rain crawl down the glass. "I keep trying to find the version of this where my department didn't know. Where it's just Corven and his pirates and some corrupt customs men. And the math won't let me. The warrant I carried — the authority behind it — it came from too high up. Someone wrote my orders knowing what I'd find. Or knowing I'd never be allowed to report it."

"And you burned them anyway."

"Burning them was easy." He said it quietly, and found it was true. "The hard part is everything after. Eleven years, Beatrix. I did everything right. Exams, appointments, commendations. I believed the institution was the thing itself — that serving it was the same as serving whatever it was supposed to protect. And then I stood in an empty camp with no blood in it, and I understood that the institution would spend me exactly the way it spent those surveyors. Efficiently. Without paperwork."

The lantern swung as the hull took a swell. Her hand went to the wheel, steadying herself more than it.

"What did you lose?" he asked. "To yours. The navy — you've never said why you left."

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. The storm filled the silence, vast and indifferent.

"My father commanded a gunboat on this river," she said at last. "Twenty-two years. He taught me to read water before I could read letters. When he died, the service offered me his old berth — not command, mind. Clerk's rank, 'on account of circumstances.' A woman could hold a wheel but not a commission, and everyone pretended this was reason rather than policy." Her jaw tightened. "So I took my inheritance — what was left of it after the pension board finished with him — and bought a rotten hull named after a thieving bird, and I've been free ever since."

She said *free* the way other people said *alone*.

"That's not freedom," Silas said. "That's exile with good posture."

Her laugh surprised out of her, short and real. "You're not wrong. But it's mine. Every debt on that boat is mine, every scar on the hull is mine, and when Corven's money keeps the diesel flowing, at least I know whose leash it is. Institutions lie to you about who holds the other end. A patron only lies about whether he'll pull it."

"He pulled it," Silas said. "We just haven't heard the snap yet."

"No." She was quiet a moment. "No, we haven't."

* * *

The worst of the front hit around midnight, and they went below deck because there was nothing a wheel could do against weather like that, and pride was a luxury the bilge pump couldn't afford. The cabin was low-ceilinged and close, lamplit, the walls sweating with condensation. Beatrix made tea on the spirit stove — real leaf, hoarded, she admitted, for occasions of catastrophe — and they sat on the bench across from the chart table with their knees almost touching, the mugs warming their hands, the storm hammering the deck overhead like a fist demanding entry.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Burning the warrant. Coming all this way instead of stopping at the fort."

"Yes," Silas said, and watched her face change. "And no. Both at once, permanently. That's what conscience is, I've learned. It's not a clean instrument like duty. It doesn't resolve. You just carry it and choose which weight hurts less today." He turned the mug in his hands. "If I'd gone to the fort, I'd be safe, and the folio would be in a staff safe, and by spring it would be lost or reclassified or eaten by some colonel's ambition, and the survey team would stay a line item marked *missing, presumed drowned*. I couldn't do that. But I may have gotten us both killed, and I cost you—" he gestured upward, at the groaning bones of the ship— "*her*. Whatever happens at the gorge, you're going to lose her, aren't you? Running it flooded. Even if we live."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. The lantern light moved on her face.

"Yes," she said simply. "Maybe nine chances in ten. Maybe worse. I've run the gorge eleven times in thirty years of water, and I've seen it eat boats crewed by better pilots than me. Flooded, at monsoon peak—" She shook her head. "But the alternative is waiting here until Corven's men come down the bank, and that's ten chances in ten. So." She drank her tea. "The river takes its tithe. It always has. My father used to say you don't own a boat, you borrow it from the water, and the water has excellent memory for debts."

"Like Corven."

"Worse than Corven. Corven can be shot. The river just *is*." But she smiled when she said it, and he saw what he had been seeing for days now and refusing to name: that she loved the thing that would kill her, without resentment, without illusion, completely. It was the most honest love he had ever witnessed, and it terrified him, because he recognized it.

Because he was beginning to feel it too, and not for the river.

* * *

The surge came at one in the morning — a true flash flood pulse off the high country, Beatrix said later, the kind that lifted the whole anchorage two feet in a single breath and dropped it again. The mooring lines shrieked. Something heavy struck the hull downstream side and ground along it, and for ten seconds Silas lived inside the sound of the *Magpie* possibly dying, and then it passed, and the boat settled, and the bilge pump cycled twice and quit.

In the dark after the lantern had swung wildly and steadied, he found that her hand had closed over his wrist. Not holding him. Just — anchored. The way you anchor to something that floats.

Neither of them moved.

"My wife left me eight years ago," Silas said, into the dark, because the storm had made confessional of him and he could not stop it. "Not dramatically. She just got tired of coming second to a career that barely noticed her. She said, *you love the work more than you love anything, and someday you'll notice that's because the work asks nothing back.*" His throat worked. "I told myself she was wrong for six years. Then I stood in that empty camp and I heard her voice like she was standing behind me."

"Was she?"

"Yes." He exhaled. "The work asked nothing back because I chose something that couldn't answer. Files don't hold your hand at midnight. Promotions don't—" He stopped.

Beatrix's thumb moved once, slowly, across the inside of his wrist. Over the pulse.

"And you?" he managed. "Anyone. Before."

"A boy on the timber boats, when I was nineteen. He drowned in the shallows at Low Bend — the *shallows*, Silas, waist-deep water, snagged foot, drunk on courage." Her voice stayed level the way steel stays level. "After that I decided the river got enough of my heart without my handing it pieces of other people too. Boats are safer. Boats sink honestly."

The lantern flame leaned. Outside, the rain redoubled, and beneath it, faint and continuous, the gorge roared like a furnace.

"Beatrix." His voice came out lower than he intended. "When we run it tomorrow — if we run it —"

"When," she said. "Say *when*. On this boat we say *when*."

A breath that was almost a laugh escaped him. "When we run it. I need you to know that I have spent my entire adult life being loyal to things that did not know my name. Departments. Warrants. An idea of order that turned out to be a curtain hung over a smuggling route. And then some clerk assigned me a missing-persons file, and I walked onto a rotten bird of a boat, and for the first time since I was a child, I am loyal to something that knows exactly who I am." He turned his hand under hers, until palm met palm. "Whatever happens in that gorge. Before, during, after. I wanted you to hear it from me and not from the way I look at you, which I'm told is not subtle."

"It's not subtle," she agreed softly. "I've been watching you not-look at me for two hundred miles. For a trained investigator, Crane, you're a disaster."

And then she kissed him.

Or he kissed her — afterward neither could reconstruct the sequence, and it mattered to neither of them. What remained was the fact of it: salt and tea and rain-cold lips warming, her hand sliding up to the back of his neck, the chart table digging into his hip as she drew him in with the same decisive competence she brought to a rapids, no hesitation, no apology, as though she had read this channel long ago and was simply waiting for the water to rise.

The storm hammered. The hull groaned. Somewhere below, the bilge pump muttered its patient work, and none of it mattered, none of it, not the folio or the warrant or the men with guns drifting down the darkened banks — because here, in a shaking wooden room the size of a coffin, two people who had given their lives to institutions and been given receipts in return were choosing each other, freely, without rank or registry, and the choice required no signature.

When they finally broke apart, her forehead rested against his, and she was breathing hard, and laughing a little, helplessly.

"Well," she said. "That's a channel opened."

"Charted," he corrected. "Pilots chart channels."

"Mm. And how would you rate the passage?"

"Hazardous," Silas said. "Repeatedly advisable."

Her laugh broke open fully then, bright and unguarded, and it was the finest sound the storm had ever failed to cover.

* * *

Later — much later, wrapped in blankets in the narrow bunk, her back against his chest, the lantern turned low — they lay listening to the river rage, and Silas thought about how strange it was that peace should find him here, of all places: hunted, debt-ridden, committed at dawn to a stretch of water that killed professionals. He should have been afraid. He was, distantly, the way you're aware of a distant fire. But her breathing had slowed against him, and his hand rested over her heartbeat, steady as a clock, and he understood that whatever the morning took from them, it could not take this back. Some things, once run, could not be unrun.

"Silas." Her voice was drowsy, half-gone.

"Mm."

"If the wire crackles tomorrow — if it's Corven — don't answer it. Promise me. Whatever he offers. Whatever he says about me." A pause. "There are things a man like that will say that are true, and they'll be the knives."

He pressed his lips to her hair. "I promise."

She slept. He did not, quite — he drifted in that border country, her warmth against him, the storm slowly spending itself overhead toward dawn — and it was in that half-sleep that he heard it.

The wireless set in the corner of the wheelhouse above, left switched to receive, crackled to life through the deck planking. A voice, thin with distance and static, clipped and courteous and utterly familiar from a hundred intercepted transmissions:

"*Magpie*, *Magpie*, this is Corven. Come in, Captain Yarrow. We should talk about your debts — and about the man sleeping in your bunk."

Silas went cold from crown to heel.

Beside him, unaware, Beatrix breathed on, slow and trusting, while upstairs the voice that owned half her life said her name again, patiently, like a man setting a trap he already knew would hold.
