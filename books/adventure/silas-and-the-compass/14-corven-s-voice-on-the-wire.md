## 14. Corven's Voice on the Wire

The storm had a rhythm to it now, and Beatrix had learned its verses the way she'd learned the river: the long drumroll on the cabin roof, the pause that wasn't mercy but only the sky drawing breath, then the crash again. She sat in the wheelhouse of the wounded *Magpie* with her boots up on the chart table and did not sleep. Sleep was for boats at anchor in fair weather, and neither condition applied.

They were wedged into a slough behind a drowned willow island, three miles below the gorge road, engines cold, riding lights doused. Somewhere upstream — close enough that the thought of it kept her jaw tight — men with rifles were waiting out the same rain, and they knew roughly where she was because Corven's money bought good information the way it bought everything else.

The radio hissed on the shelf above the helm. It had been hissing all night, static and ghost-voices bleeding through from stations she couldn't name, and she'd let it run because silence in a storm was worse. But at some hour past three, the hiss changed character. It gathered itself. It became directional, deliberate, like a man clearing his throat in a dark room.

Beatrix sat up slowly.

"Magpie." The voice came through wet with interference but unmistakable, each word laid down like coin on a counter. "Magpie, this is Corven. Answer, Beatrix. I know you're listening. You never could leave a radio silent. It'd be like leaving a door unlocked."

She looked back through the curtain into the cabin. Silas lay on the bench under the porthole, folio wrapped in oilcloth beneath his head like a pillow stuffed with dynamite, and he was awake — she could tell by the stillness. Sleeping men fidgeted. Awake men pretending to sleep went rigid as drowned things. She put one finger to her lips, though he couldn't see it, and turned back to the set.

Her hand hovered over the transmit switch. Every sensible instinct she owned told her not to touch it. A position fix could be pulled off any transmission longer than ten seconds; she'd done it herself to fools who chattered across open water. But Corven already knew where she was, or near enough. And there was a question burning a hole through her chest that she had carried since the trading post ash, since the weighted corpse rising out of the brown water, since the moment she'd seen Corven's barges moving upriver ahead of her and told herself a dozen comfortable lies about coincidence.

Comfortable lies were for people who hadn't lost a survey team, a hull patch, and most of a night's sleep to this river.

She keyed the switch. "This is Magpie." Three seconds, no more. She released it and waited.

Static. Then, warm as brandy: "There she is. I wondered how long you'd make me talk to weather."

"I'll keep it short," Beatrix said. "My battery's low."

"Your battery's fine. You've got a generator and you're too careful to run it while you're hiding." A chuckle rolled through the static, unhurried. That was the thing about Corven's voice that twenty years of debt had taught her to read the way she read water over a sandbar: the surface said one thing, the depth said another. On the surface, warmth. Beneath, the flat patience of a man who owned the dock your boat was tied to. "You always were careful, Beatrix. It's why I lent to you when every other house on the river laughed at a woman with a wooden hull and a bad surname. Careful people pay."

"What do you want, Corven?"

"Want? I want to help you. That's all I've ever wanted." Rain hammered the wheelhouse roof as if to dispute it. "Let me tell you what I see from my office, girl, because you're too deep in the trees. I see a government man who walked onto your boat with a warrant and a purse, and I see that boat now shot full of holes, hunted by pirates, running flooded water in monsoon season. Ask yourself who benefits from that. Not me. I'm sitting dry in Harrow Bend wondering how my best captain got mixed up with dead surveyors and stolen paper."

"The surveyors are dead?" She kept her voice level. It cost her something. "I hadn't heard that confirmed."

The pause was small — half a breath, no more — but Beatrix heard it the way she heard the change in current before an eddy took the bow sideways. Half a breath of silence where a surprised man would have said *dead? who told you that?* Corven didn't ask. Corven adjusted.

"A man in my position hears many things," he said smoothly. "Some true, some not. What I know is that whatever Crane is carrying, it's worth more trouble than a woman in your situation can afford. And you, Beatrix, are in a situation." Papers rustled somewhere on his end; she pictured his office, the green glass lamp, the ledger books with her name in them going back eleven years. "Four hundred and sixty pounds against the *Magpie*. Interest accruing since the spring layover. Plus the engine parts I fronted you in June, plus storage fees at my dock for a hull that hasn't paid berthage in two months. Call it five hundred even by Friday."

"You called to dun me during a monsoon?"

"I called to forgive you." The word landed soft and enormous. Outside, lightning stitched the clouds above the gorge, and for one white instant she saw the rain standing silver on the black water, thousands of small spears falling in silence before the thunder arrived to claim them. "Every pound of it. Wiped clean. The ledger closed, the paper burned, the *Magpie* yours outright for the first time in her life — and yours means free, Beatrix. Free hull, free woman, no more seasons spent hauling his lordship's barge freight at rates I set. You could go anywhere. Downstream past the delta towns where nobody knows what you owe or whom. You could be done."

Beatrix found that her hands had gone cold on the wheel, which she wasn't steering, which was holding nothing, which was just something to grip.

"And in exchange," she said.

"In exchange you bring Mr. Crane to me. Unharmed, unconsulted, and carrying everything he took from that camp. The folio, the maps, whatever notes he's made. That's all. No blood, no drama. He gets put on a train home with a story about bureaucratic confusion, and you get your life back." A smile lived in his voice now; she could hear it the way you hear a current under calm water. "He's not your cause, Beatrix. He's a stranger who paid you. Payment received, service rendered. Anything else is sentiment, and sentiment sinks boats."

*So does betrayal,* she thought. *I've seen the wrecks.*

She keyed the switch. "Why does a shipping magnate care about a government folio?"

Another adjustment, that half-breath of recalibration, quicker this time, less well hidden. The storm filled it. When Corven spoke again, the brandy had gone out of his voice, and what remained was the voice underneath, the one she had heard only twice in eleven years — once when a deckhand stole from him, once when a rival's tug burned at anchor within sight of his office window.

"Because careless people dug where they shouldn't have," he said, "and their carelessness is now sitting in my river on my debtor's boat, and I am tired of it. Bring me the paper and the man, Beatrix. Do that, and you walk away whole. Don't—" the transmission crackled, surged, steadied— "don't make me send other people to do it badly. My people are thorough but they lack your finesse. We both know what happened to the last crew that handled this cargo carelessly."

There it was.

Not a confession. Confessions were loud, desperate things, wrung out under lamplight. This was quieter and worse: a man so certain of his ownership — of the river, of the officials, of her — that he'd stopped bothering to build the sentence properly. *The last crew.* Not *the missing surveyors*. Not *whatever happened upriver*. He knew there had been a crew. He knew what had been done to them, and he knew it the way she knew the draft of her own hull: intimately, operationally, without needing to think.

Beatrix stared at the radio's green-lit dial and felt something in her chest go very still and very clear, the way the river went clear in the dead channels where nothing stirred the silt. Eleven years. Eleven years of hauling his freight at his rates, of swallowing his little jokes about *my best captain*, of telling herself that debt was just weather, that everybody served somebody, that a patron who lent to a woman with a bad surname must have a decent heart under the ledgers. She had defended him to Silas on the second night, wine in tin cups, saying *Corven's a shark but sharks don't lie, that's the difference between him and the government.*

Sharks didn't lie. They also ate the survey crew and burned the trading post and sent gunmen to an anchorage, and then called up on the wire in the rain to offer forgiveness like it was a favor, like the debt ran the other way, like she owed him her gratitude along with her hull.

"Beatrix?" Corven's voice, patient again, the mask reassembled. "You've gone quiet. I know how you think. You're doing arithmetic. Let me save you the trouble: whatever Crane offered you, I'll triple it, and mine doesn't come with pirates attached. There is no version of tomorrow where keeping that paper serves you. There's only versions where you're rich and free, or versions where you're at the bottom of the channel I will personally drag until I find you. I'd rather write the first ending. I've always liked you."

She thought about the drowned man surfacing by the camp, arms drifting, the river finally giving up what men had tried to make it keep. She thought of the child in the fishing village describing the burning papers, flames reflected in eyes too young for that. She thought of Silas burning his warrant in the galley stove and watching the official seal curl and go to ash, and saying, quietly, *I'd rather be right than employed.*

"Corven," she said, and keyed the switch, and let herself smile, though there was no one to see it and the smile had no warmth in it anywhere. "Do you know what I've learned about this river in thirty years?"

A pause. "Enlighten me."

"It keeps everything. Everything anybody ever gave it — bodies, boats, secrets, promises. It doesn't forgive and it doesn't forget, it just holds it all down in the cold and the dark and waits." She watched the rain crawl down the wheelhouse glass. "You put six surveyors in it. Maybe seven. I've seen one of them. So here's my answer, and you can enter it in your ledger: no deal. Not for five hundred pounds, not for five thousand, not for a fleet of new boats and my name in gold on the stern. You can keep your forgiveness. I wouldn't know what to do with a gift from a man who pays his debts in other people's blood."

Silence on the line. Long enough that she checked the dial to see if the connection had died.

Then Corven laughed. It was not a pleasant sound; it was the sound of a man crossing an item off a list, and what remained on the list was short.

"Well," he said. "Eleven years, and I finally learn what you're made of. Pity it's worthless metal." The warmth was entirely gone now, and somehow that was a relief, like setting down a weight she hadn't known she was carrying. "You understand what happens next. I have men above you and men below you, and by morning the gorge road will have company. Whatever you think you're running, Beatrix, you're running it inside my pocket. Last chance. Bring me the paper and I'll still let the *Magpie* live. That's more than she deserves, and more than I've offered anyone this season."

"She's not yours to let live."

"No," he agreed, almost gently. "But she's mortgaged to me, and I foreclose on Friday. Sleep well, Captain. Oh — and tell Crane his superiors send their regards. He'll want to know how far up this goes. It goes further than his warrant. Further than his director. When he figures that out, he'll come to me on his knees, and you'll wish you'd taken my offer while it was still on the table."

The click of the disconnect was tiny, domestic, obscene. Static rushed back in like floodwater through a breached hull, and Beatrix sat with her hand still resting on the dead switch, listening to the storm fill the silence Corven had left.

*Further than his director.* She turned the phrase over. It should have frightened her, and part of it did — the old animal part that wanted to run the boat downstream in the dark and take her chances with the pirates. But underneath the fear, something else was settling into place, hard and load-bearing, like a keel finding gravel. Because if it went further than Silas's director, then the folio wasn't just evidence of smuggling. It was evidence of the kind that made directors and magistrates and whoever stood above them burn camps and sink crews, and that meant it was worth exactly as much as Corven feared it was. Worth killing for. Worth, possibly, changing things.

Worth the gorge.

The curtain moved. Silas stood in the cabin doorway, oilcloth-wrapped folio tucked under one arm, hair flattened by sleep, eyes fully awake. How long he'd been listening she couldn't say and didn't ask.

"He offered to wipe the debt," she said. It wasn't a question and didn't need to be.

"He offered." Silas came forward into the wheelhouse, and the lightning showed her his face — not pitying, which she'd have hated, but grim and level, a man looking at a chart of dangerous water and not flinching from it. "And you told him no."

"I told him the river keeps everything." She reached up and switched off the radio, killing the static, and in the sudden quiet the rain sounded almost gentle. "He's coming for us, Silas. Men above us and below us, he said, and he wasn't bluffing — bluffing costs him nothing, and Corven never spends without return. By first light we'll have company on both banks."

Silas set the folio on the chart table between them, square in the middle, like a piece in a game whose rules had just changed. "Then we'd better decide what we do with the hours we've got left."

Beatrix looked past him, through the streaming glass, toward the black wall of the gorge where the river went mad in the dark. The rain was easing. She could feel it in the sound on the roof, in the way the water in the slough had stopped climbing the pilings. Two days, maybe three, and the gorge would run passable — passable the way a knife fight was winnable.

"First," she said, "we find out exactly what's in that folio that scares a man like Corven enough to kill for it. Get the lamp. If I'm going to die for a stack of paper, I intend to know what's written on it."

* * *

They spread the recovered pages across the chart table under the gimbaled lamp, and the storm leaned close over their shoulders to read along.
