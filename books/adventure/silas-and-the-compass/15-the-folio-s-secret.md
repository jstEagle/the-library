## 15. The Folio's Secret

The rain had settled into its long argument with the roof of the trading post's surviving storehouse, a tin sheet Beatrix had propped over the gap where the old thatch had burned through. Under it, the sound was constant, drumming, almost hypnotic — rain on tin, river on pilings, and beneath both, the slow tick of the Magpie's cooling engine as she sat at anchor in the drowned willows.

Silas Crane had not slept properly in three days. He sat now at a table made from two crates and a plank, an oil lamp turned low to keep its flame from guttering in the damp, and spread before him the contents of the oilskin packet he'd carried against his chest since the night at the anchorage: eleven pages, half-charred at their edges, water-stained into a brittle brown lace, rescued from the ash of the trading post and dried — badly, he admitted — over the Magpie's stove.

Eleven pages out of what should have been forty. The missing folio. The one thing the survey team had carried out of their camp or died trying to carry, the one thing Corven's men had gone upriver ahead of them hunting, the one thing armed strangers had burned papers to destroy at this very post.

And yet here were eleven pages that someone had missed.

He flattened the first one under the lamp glass — an actual pane of glass scavenged from a broken lantern, the only way to read paper this fragile without tearing it. The ink had run in places, feathered into gray ghosts, but the surveyor's hand was a careful one, a draftsman's hand, and enough of it survived.

*Station 44-K. Core sample, depth 2.1 meters. Assay pending.*

That was all it said. A line of technical language, dry as a ledger. But Silas knew what Station 44-K was. He had walked the coordinates himself, three days ago, when they'd found the abandoned camp. 44-K sat on the east bank of the Kestrel, forty miles above the confluence, in a district every map in the provincial capital marked as *unallocated crown land — no mineral value, no settlement potential*. Land designated empty so that it could be given away quietly.

Land that was not empty.

He turned the page. Another station. Another assay. And this time, in the margin, in a different hand — smaller, hurried, pressed hard enough into the paper that even the water hadn't erased it entirely — a note:

*Copper. High grade. Vein extends north. Do NOT log in main register.*

Silas read it four times. The first time his eyes simply refused the words. The second time they took them in and his hands began to shake. By the fourth reading he understood what he was holding, and why eleven men had vanished from a survey camp on a quiet bend of the Kestrel, and why the government that sent him to find them had sent him with a warrant and no escort and no authority to call for reinforcements.

They hadn't wanted the team found. They'd wanted the *evidence* found — by someone expendable, someone who could be handled afterward.

Someone like him.

* * *

Beatrix came below at midnight, wrung out from checking the anchor lines twice in the rising current. She found him still bent over the table, lamp glass weighted on the page, and something in his posture made her stop on the ladder's last step.

"You look like a man reading his own death warrant," she said.

"I might be." He didn't look up. "Sit down, Captain."

She sat. She had earned the right to that word from him somewhere in the last hundred miles, though neither of them had marked the moment. She pulled her boots off, set them by the stove, and waited, because she was a woman who understood that some things had to be spoken in order.

"The survey team wasn't mapping the river," Silas said. "Not only the river. They were doing mineral assays along the banks. Stations every few miles. And the results —" he slid the second page out from under the glass and turned it toward her — "the results were being kept out of the official record. Deliberately. Look at the marginalia. 'Do not log in main register.' That's not a surveyor's note. That's an instruction. Someone told them what to write down and what to leave off the books."

Beatrix picked up the page by its corner, the way you handle a snake you're not sure is dead. She read it in silence while the rain hammered the tin overhead.

"Copper," she said finally. "On land marked empty."

"On land marked empty *by the same ministry that issued my warrant.*" He leaned back, and the crate creaked under him. "Do you understand what I've been carrying, Beatrix? I thought I was chasing smugglers. I thought Corven was running ore downriver in barges marked as ballast, bribing customs men, paying off harbor officials. Petty corruption. Ugly, but survivable. Men like that get caught eventually because they're greedy at the wrong scale." He tapped the page. "This isn't the wrong scale. This is the ministry itself. The survey that proved there was nothing worth protecting on that land — the survey whose disappearance I was sent to investigate — was falsified from the top. The team found copper. Real copper, high grade, miles of it. And instead of logging it, they were ordered to bury the findings so the land could be transferred — quietly, cheaply, legally — to whoever held the concession."

"And the concession holder is—"

"Is above Corven." He heard his own voice go flat and cold. "Corven is the river man. Corven moves the ore, pays the pirates, keeps the water clean of witnesses. But the concession — the transfer documents, the ministerial signatures — those come from the capital. From Deputy Minister Halloway's office. Possibly from Halloway himself. And there are names here—" he gathered the third and fourth pages, the ones with the shipping manifests copied in the surveyor's hand, evidence the team must have collected from the trading post before it burned — "names here that connect Halloway's office directly to Corven's shipping company. Dates. Tonnages. Signatures."

Beatrix set the page down and looked at him across the lamplight, and her face did something he had learned to read the way he'd learned to read the river: a stillness that meant the current underneath had changed.

"How many names?" she asked.

"Seven that I can make out. Maybe more on the pages that burned." He swallowed. "Two of them signed my warrant."

The tin roof rang with rain. Somewhere out in the flooded willows, a heron gave its harsh evening cry and was silenced by the dark.

"So," Beatrix said, after a long moment. "You weren't sent to find the truth. You were sent to lose it politely. Find whatever scraps were left, file a report saying bandits or accident, close the file, come home, get a commendation."

"Yes."

"And if you'd filed that report?"

Silas looked at his hands. They were an investigator's hands, ink-stained, precise, hands that had spent twelve years believing that the machinery of law was heavy and slow but fundamentally honest — a great millstone that ground fine, if slowly, and always in the right direction.

"Then in six months," he said, "I'd have been posted somewhere far away, or retired with honors, or dead in a convenient accident. Because the moment I closed that file, I'd have become part of the record. Part of the cover. One more name on the list of people who knew and said nothing." He laughed, and it came out bitter. "I used to wonder why the warrant was so strangely worded. Limited scope. No authority to subpoena. No authority to arrest. Report findings to the ministry directly, not to the courts. I thought it was bureaucratic timidity. It was a leash. They built me a box and told me it was an office."

Beatrix reached across the table and put her hand flat on the pages, keeping them from the draft that breathed through the storehouse wall.

"Then we don't take this to your ministry," she said. "We take it where you said. The delta press town. The *Meridian Courier*. Judge Anselm Voss, if half of what they say about him is true."

"If half of what they say about Voss is true, he'll need more than half of what we have." Silas drew the full stack toward him again, all eleven pages, and laid them out in sequence the way he'd once laid out evidence in a courtroom that no longer existed for him. "Listen to me, Beatrix. I want you to hear this clearly, because you've risked your boat and your life on my warrant, and my warrant is ash now, and what's left is just — us. Eleven pages. Half-melted. A dead man's handwriting. Against seven officials, a shipping magnate with a private army, and every court between here and the capital." He met her eyes. "If you put me ashore at the next village, I would understand. I'd find another way. Alone."

She was quiet for long enough that he thought she might actually say yes.

Then she said: "Silas. When I was nineteen, I watched a harbormaster in Corven's pocket seize my father's boat over a forged inspection. My father had papers too. True ones. Proving the inspection was bought. Nobody would print them, nobody would hear them, and he drank himself into the river inside two years." Her voice didn't rise; it never rose; it went quieter and harder, like water narrowing into a gorge. "I have spent fifteen years learning every sandbar on this river because the law wouldn't hold the men who broke my family, so I made the river my law instead. Don't you dare offer me the chance to walk away from the first real chance I've ever had to break them back."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night.

It went faster with four hands. Beatrix turned pages while Silas transcribed, copying every legible line into his field notebook in a fresh, plain hand — because the originals were dying, he could feel them going, the charred edges flaking, the water-ghosted ink fading a little more each time light touched them. Whatever happened to the originals, the transcription had to survive. He wrote in duplicate as the hours wore on, one copy for the folio's hiding place in the bilge, one copy sewn into the lining of his coat, because he had finally learned the lesson the river had been teaching him since the abandoned camp: never let the truth exist in only one place.

By two in the morning they had the shape of it whole.

Station 44-K through 61-K: nine confirmed high-grade copper assays on land scheduled for transfer. A marginal instruction, unsigned but in an administrative hand, ordering the results excluded from the register. Three manifest copies showing Corven Shipping moving unregistered cargo — tonnage consistent with raw ore — downriver on dates matching the transfer schedule. And on the final recoverable page, the one Silas had nearly missed because it was the most damaged, folded into quarters and fused with creosote from the fire: a letter fragment.

Half a letter. The salutation burned away. The signature burned away. But the body survived, and it was addressed — the honorific fragment *"...the Deputy Minister's office will expect..."* made it unmistakable — from Corven to Halloway. It discussed the *"inconvenience at the upper stations"* in terms that made Silas's blood run cold, because the word *inconvenience* was doing work no inconvenience should do. It discussed payment schedules. It discussed, in a single chilling sentence near the fragment's broken edge, *"the matter of the survey personnel, which I trust is now concluded to everyone's satisfaction."*

Concluded. Eleven men. Concluded.

"They knew," Silas said aloud, to the lamplight, to Beatrix, to the rain. "Halloway knew. Not afterward — beforehand. This letter reads like a man confirming arrangements already made. 'Which I trust is now concluded.' You don't trust that a massacre has been *concluded* unless you approved it in advance." He set the fragment down as if it might detonate. "This isn't corruption that grew a murder. This is a murder that was budgeted."

Beatrix stood at the little stern window, arms crossed, watching the black water slide past the hull. When she spoke, she didn't turn around.

"My debt to Corven," she said. "Fourteen years I've paid it. Every season, every run, every favor he asked that I could stomach, and a few I couldn't. I told myself he was a hard man in a hard trade, that the river makes monsters of everyone eventually and at least Corven's monsters stayed useful." Her reflection in the wet glass was faint, ghosted, like one of the fading pages. "He killed eleven men who'd done nothing but measure the ground honestly. And then he sent gunmen after me — me, who scrubbed his decks when I was sixteen years old." She turned. In the lamplight her face was calm, which frightened him more than fury would have. "So we're past the point where this is about your career, Silas. Or my boat. If we stop now, we're just two more names on his list of inconveniences. There's no anchorage behind us anymore. Only forward."

"Forward to Voss," Silas agreed. "But understand what we're bringing him. I've spent tonight convincing myself, and I'll spend tomorrow convincing you, and I want you convinced, because there will be a moment — maybe several — when someone offers us money, or safety, or silence, and asks us whether eleven half-burned pages are really worth it." He began packing the originals back into the oilskin, wrapping each one in waxed cloth. "Here's the answer we give. These pages aren't worth eleven lives. Nothing is. But they're the only thing standing between those seven names and the next eleven people who measure the ground honestly. The next survey team. The next investigator with a leash and a box called an office. The next captain whose boat gets seized over forged papers." He tied the oilskin cord tight. "The stakes stopped being ours the moment we read these pages. Now they're systemic. Either the rot goes all the way up and gets named, or it goes all the way up and stays comfortable. There's no third option on this river."

Outside, the rain slackened for the first time in two days — not stopping, but easing, the hammering dropping to a steady wash. Beatrix cocked her head at the change the way a sailor cocks an ear at a shifting wind.

"That's not mercy," she said. "That's the storm reorganizing. It'll come back harder from the south before dawn." She pulled her boots back on. "And when it eases for good — a day, maybe two — the gorge road opens, and everything Corven has left comes down it after us."

Silas looked at the oilskin packet, small and unremarkable, the weight of a government's shame wrapped in waterproofed canvas no bigger than a bread loaf. Seven names. Eleven graves. One river between them and the press town, and the river currently trying to drown the entire valley.

"We should move at first light," he said. "Before they do."

Beatrix was already climbing the ladder to check the anchor, but she paused at the top, silhouetted against the gray pre-dawn leaking through the tin roof, and looked back down at him.

"Silas. When we deliver this — when Voss prints it and the arrests start — what happens to you? Your ministry. Your commission. Everything you were."

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and had paid for it in full.

"I think," he said, "that I stopped being what they made me the night I burned the warrant. What I am now is what you made me. A man on a boat, with the truth in an oilskin bag, going downstream." He smiled, tired and real. "I find I prefer the job description."

She went up into the rain to fight the river for their anchor, and Silas sat alone a moment longer with the lamp, rereading the letter fragment one last time — *which I trust is now concluded to everyone's satisfaction* — and then he blew out the flame, because light through a window was a beacon, and somewhere downriver, closing through the dark, men were being paid to follow exactly that kind of light.

At first light, the Magpie raised anchor and turned her bow downstream, into the falling rain, toward the gorge.
