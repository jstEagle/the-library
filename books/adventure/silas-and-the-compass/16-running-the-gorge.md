## 16. Running the Gorge

The rain did not stop so much as lose its argument with the world. All night it had hammered the Magpie's deck like a debt collector's fist; by the hour before dawn it had softened to a steady hiss, and then, near first light, to a drizzle that seemed almost apologetic. The clouds did not lift so much as thin, letting through a gray luminance that turned the river into hammered pewter.

Beatrix Yarrow stood at the wheel before the light came, because she had not slept and would not pretend otherwise. She had spent the night doing what she always did before an impossible thing: reading the water in her head, over and over, until the river ran through her mind the way it ran through its channel — every eddy, every boil, every rock wearing its crown of foam.

The gorge began two miles upstream. In low water it was a bad stretch of white water that honest pilots portaged around or paid her to take them through at a crawl. In flood it was something else entirely. It was a mile and a half of river squeezed between walls of black granite, dropping ninety feet, and when the Kestrel rose, the gorge did not become more dangerous in any way a sane person could measure. It became a different animal. It swallowed its own rocks and made new ones out of standing waves. It ate the sound of everything else.

She had run it twice in high water. Both times she had been younger, the Magpie had been whole, and no one had been shooting at her.

Now the rain was easing, which meant the window was opening — and closing. Every hour the river dropped, the rocks came back. But every hour also brought Corven's men closer down the cut from the anchorage, and the launch they'd heard grinding up the back channel in the dark would reach the gorge mouth by midmorning if it reached it at all. Dawn was the only door. She meant to go through it.

Silas came up on deck as the sky went from charcoal to slate. He looked terrible and he knew it, and there was something in his face that had settled over the last three days — a kind of quiet that wasn't calm but was done pretending to be anything else. He carried two mugs of coffee and the folio wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, where it had lived since the ambush, against his chest, like a second heart.

"You're going to try it," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going to try it."

"The rain's still falling."

"Barely." She took the mug. "River's peaked. It'll hold for maybe six hours before it starts to fall, and once it falls, the teeth come back. This is it. Now or we wait for Corven's launch and let him decide how this ends."

"And if you're wrong about the peak?"

"Then I'm wrong, and we die, and the folio goes to the bottom, and Corven wins." She said it flatly, without theater, because theater was a luxury for people who hadn't watched their boat take splinters two nights ago. "I'd rather be wrong at speed than right at anchor."

He set his mug down on the chart table and didn't argue. Instead he asked the question that mattered: "What do you need me to do?"

"Exactly what I say, exactly when I say it. Nothing else. If I shout *starboard*, you throw your whole weight on that line. If I shout *down*, you get flat on the deck and you stay there. And whatever happens — whatever you see coming, whatever you think is about to kill us — you do not touch this wheel. It's mine. Understand?"

"It's yours," Silas said.

* * *

They cast off in the last of the dark, engine at half, running the flooded backwater above the gorge mouth where the current was gentler and she could line up her approach. The Magpie sat lower than Beatrix had ever seen her sit — bilge pumps cycling every four minutes instead of ten, the patched hull section forward of the engine room sweating a thread of water that never quite stopped. Two nights of storm and one ambush had left her beloved, battered boat held together by stubbornness and pine tar. She would hold. Beatrix had decided she would hold, and on the Kestrel, decision was half the craft.

The gorge announced itself before they saw it. First as a change in the air — a cold breath rising off the water, smelling of stone and churn. Then as a sound, a long low roar building under everything else, the voice of forty thousand cubic feet per second going through a hole too small for it. Then, finally, as sight: the river ahead simply ended, pouring away into a trench of mist between walls that leaned toward each other like conspirators.

Beatrix brought the Magpie around and lined up on the tongue.

Every rapid has a tongue — the smooth V of green water where the main current enters clean, before it breaks. Find the tongue, commit to it, and the rapid carries you honestly. Miss it, and the rapid decides for you. At this volume, the tongue was wide as a road and moving faster than anything she'd ever steered into. She could see it clearly now in the graying light: a lane of relatively unbroken water, flanked on both sides by chaos, descending into the mist like a stairway into a furnace.

"Silas." Her voice was level. She made it level on purpose. "Life vest tight? Knife loose in the sheath?"

"Tight. Loose."

"When we go in, count nothing, pray nothing, and watch my hands. I'll be busy."

He almost smiled. "Understood, Captain."

She opened the throttle.

* * *

The entry hit them like a decision made by someone else.

One moment the Magpie was planing across glassy flood-water; the next the river dropped away beneath her hull and she was falling — not far, six feet maybe, but falling, bow pitching down, engine screaming as the wheel spun free of load, spray coming over the foredeck in a single green sheet that hit the pilothouse windows like a thrown bucket. Beatrix had braced for it. Her boots were wedged, her knees locked, her hands at ten and two, and when the bow slapped down and buried itself and came back up streaming, she was already correcting, feathering the wheel a quarter-turn to starboard to keep her nose on the tongue.

The speed was obscene. The gauge said twelve knots. The gauge was lying; the current was doing half the work, and the ground they covered felt closer to twenty-five. Trees on the banks went backward like fence posts past a train window.

"Hold!" she shouted, though Silas was already flat behind the engine housing, and the first real wave arrived.

It came off the right wall — a reflected roller, the flood surge bouncing off granite and folding back across the channel, eight feet of green glass standing up and traveling. Beatrix saw it early because she knew the wall, knew the bend, knew that at this height the reflection set up exactly opposite the old quarry cut. She took it at a slight angle, bow-on-quarter, the only way a boat of the Magpie's beam could take a wave that size without broaching.

The wave lifted the stern, walked the whole boat sideways two feet, and set her down again. Everything not tied down went flying. The coffee mugs. A coil of line. Silas's hat, gone forever, spinning away astern like a black bird. The Magpie groaned from keel to rail and kept going.

"Good girl," Beatrix breathed, and hauled the wheel over hard to port, because ahead — dead ahead, right where the tongue should have continued — the water was boiling white over something that had no business being there.

A new rock. Flood-born, or flood-revealed; impossible to know, and no time to care. At this flow it wore no crown of foam, just a long smooth hump of water with a dimple in its center, the telltale she'd been taught to read before she could read letters. Water flowing over a shallow rock lies down and goes quiet. Water over deep water stands up and roars. The quiet was the danger.

"Rock! Dead ahead!" she called, and threw the Magpie into the hardest turn of her life.

* * *

There is a moment in every serious rapid when the pilot stops steering the boat and starts steering the fight between the boat and the river. Beatrix entered that moment now.

The turn put them onto the seam — the visible line where fast water met faster water, a rope of turbulence lying across the channel like a fallen spar. Crossing a seam at speed is a beating; the hull slams from one current into another, the whole boat shudders, the wheel goes light then heavy then light again. But the seam led left, and left was where the channel bent, and the bend was where the gorge's first great drop waited: the Staircase, three ledges in a quarter mile, each one bigger than the last.

She rode the seam like a bucking horse, letting it carry her while she fought to keep the bow pointed downriver rather than across. Across was death. Across was broaching, and broaching in the gorge meant the river would roll the Magpie like a log and pin her against a wall until the hull caved.

The first ledge came up fast. It was a pour-over, a place where the whole width of the river slid over a granite shelf and fell four feet into a churning trough below. At normal water you threaded the slot on the left where the shelf was worn down. At flood, the slot was underwater and the entire ledge was a single moving waterfall, and the trick — the only trick — was to hit it square, straight, committed, bow perpendicular to the lip, throttle up, and ride the drop like a ramp.

"Straight!" she yelled, for herself as much as for him. She squared the boat. She opened the engine to full.

The Magpie went over the lip.

For one full second they were airborne. Not metaphorically — the hull left the water, all of it, a forty-foot wooden vessel with two souls aboard flying through the gray morning air above a waterfall, and Beatrix felt the wheel go weightless in her hands and heard Silas make a sound that was not a scream and not a prayer but some third thing, and then the bow came down into the trough below and the world turned to foam.

The impact tried to fold the hull. She felt it in her spine — a compression, a shudder, the particular sick flex of a wooden boat hitting water harder than wood likes. The pilothouse windows starred white with spray. For two seconds she could see nothing at all, nothing but churning white from wall to wall, and she steered blind on feel alone, on the pull of the wheel, on thirty years of the river talking to her hands.

Then the foam thinned, and the channel was there, and the second ledge was ahead, and she was still pointing downriver.

* * *

The middle of the gorge was the part she feared least and respected most: the Weir, a long brawling stretch of standing waves, house-high haystacks of water marching downstream in ranks, none of them breaking clean, all of them waiting to catch a hull and flip it end over end. There was no line through the Weir. There was only the general direction of survivable, and reflexes.

She steered from wave to wave the way a boxer moves through punches — reading each one a half-second before it arrived, quarter-turn here, correction there, riding up the faces and sliding down the backs, the Magpie surfing, plunging, leaping, taking green water over the bow again and again until the deck ran like a creek. Once, twice, three times a wave caught them wrong and shoved the stern around, and each time Beatrix wrestled the boat back before the yaw became a spin. Her arms burned. Her shoulders screamed. She had never been more alive.

Somewhere in the middle of it, glancing back for a fraction of a second to check on him, she saw Silas Crane — government man, paper man, a creature of offices and warrants — braced against the engine housing with the folio clutched inside his coat, drenched to the bone, hair plastered to his skull, and *grinning*. Actually grinning, a wild white slash in a soaked face, and something in her chest cracked open along an old seam. That was the moment, she thought later. Not the tender nights, not the honest talk. That moment, mid-Weir, when she saw what he looked like when everything false had been washed off him.

Then a lateral wave took the boat and drove her toward the left wall and there was no time for anything but survival.

The wall was sheer black granite, slick as oiled glass, and the current pressed them toward it with the patience of a hand closing. Beatrix knew what happened next if she let it: the Magpie would touch, the current would pin her, and the pressure of the whole Kestrel would grind her hull to kindling against the stone. She had seen it happen to a barge once, years ago. The crew had climbed the wall like flies. The barge had still been there a month later, folded in half.

"Starboard! Starboard, *now*!"

Silas threw himself on the line she'd rigged aft, hauling with everything he had, and the combination — her wheel, his weight, the engine at full — swung the bow away from the stone with perhaps a boat-length to spare. Close enough that she could see the individual lichens on the rock. Close enough that spray off the wall hit her face like a slap.

And then the gorge narrowed, and the last drop appeared, and it was worse than anything above it.

* * *

The Gate. Pilots called it that the way sailors name storms — with respect that was really fear wearing a better coat. A single gap between two fang-rocks, barely wider than the Magpie herself, funneling the entire flood into a chute that dropped eight feet into the gorge's final cauldron. At low water it was a thrill. At peak flood it was a keyhole, and they were the key.

She lined up from fifty yards out, because after that there would be no lining up, only holding. The approach water was confused, crosscurrented, trying to shove her left while a back-eddy off the right fang tried to grab her stern and swing her broadside. She counter-steered constantly, small corrections, small corrections, keeping the bow threaded on an invisible wire stretched from her wheel to the gap.

"Fangs don't move," she muttered. "Water moves. Trust the water between them."

Twenty yards. Ten. The roar was total now, a physical weight, a sound she felt in her bones rather than heard. The gap yawned — narrow, wet, walled in foam, absolutely certain.

She did not close the throttle. Closing the throttle at the Gate was the classic killer; a boat without way on became a leaf, and leaves went where the river sent them. She kept the power on, kept the bow true, and drove the Magpie straight into the throat of the gorge.

The walls closed around them. Granite on both sides, close enough to touch, water compressed between them into something that was less like current and more like a solid thing happening to them at speed. The boat accelerated violently, shot through the gap in perhaps two seconds, dropped eight feet, and buried her bow to the foredeck in the cauldron beyond.

Green water came over the pilothouse. Actual green water, a full ton of it, crashing across the roof and down the front windows, and for a horrible instant Beatrix was steering from inside a wave. Then the Magpie surged up out of the hole, shedding the river off her decks in sheets, listing hard to starboard — taking water, definitely taking water, the pumps would confirm how much — but upright, driving, *through*.

The roar began to fall behind them. The walls drew apart. Ahead, the river widened, slowed, spread itself out across the drowned valley below the gorge, and the gray light of full dawn lay on it like a benediction.

Beatrix realized she was shaking. She realized she was laughing too, a low unhinged laugh that had nothing to do with joy and everything to do with still existing.

* * *

The outwash ran easy for a mile, brown and swollen and deceptively calm, carrying them down toward the delta lowlands at a pace that felt, after the gorge, like floating on a lake. Beatrix throttled back to a third and let the current do the work while her hands stopped trembling enough to steer fine lines.

Silas crawled up into the pilothouse, soaked through, hatless, alive, the folio still inside his coat because he had never once let go of it, not through the airborne second, not through the wave over the wheelhouse, not through any of it. He looked at her the way men look at things they don't have words for yet.

"You got us through," he said.

"We got us through. You were on that line when I needed you on it." She glanced at the pump gauge and her stomach tightened. "But listen. She's shipping more than I like. More than the patch should account for. The Staircase impact — I think it started something working loose down in the strakes, something the bilge can't outrun for long."

"How long?"

"I don't know. An hour. Three. Long enough to reach the delta fork if the river's kind." She looked back upstream, at the mist-wrapped walls they'd come out of, and her blood went cold in a way the gorge hadn't managed. Because there, faint in the distance, threading down through the cut from the anchorage, she could see movement on the bank — men with rifles, moving fast, and behind them, just entering the gorge mouth where the water had dropped just enough to make it possible, the low silhouette of a powered launch with its engine open wide.

Corven hadn't waited for midmorning. Corven had gambled on the same window she had.

"They're running it," Silas said quietly. "Behind us."

"Amateurs," Beatrix said, and there was no satisfaction in it, because amateurs in that water died loudly, and professionals with a grudge and a local pilot might not die at all. "Which means we've bought minutes, not miles. And the Magpie is bleeding."

She put the wheel over and gave the old boat everything her wounded hull could bear, running the brown flood down toward the delta fork, watching the pump cycle shorten — five minutes, four — while the pursuers' roar grew faintly, terribly, in the gorge behind them.

"Silas," she said, and her voice was steady the way it had been steady at the Gate, "get the folio ready to move. Whatever happens to this boat in the next hour — the papers swim out dry, and you swim out with them. Promise me."

He looked at her, and at the water rising inch by inch in the bilge, and did not promise, because he was honest even now.

Behind them, out of the mist at the gorge's mouth, the launch emerged — still afloat, still coming, faster than it had any right to be.
