## 17. What the River Takes

*Beatrix*

The launch came out of the mist like a promise kept by a cruel god.

Beatrix saw it in the corner of her eye as she took the Magpie into the last stretch of the gorge — the long gray hull shouldering through the spray, Corven's men at the rail, one of them already swinging something long and black up onto his shoulder. She had no time to hate them. The river had her whole attention now.

The outwash opened ahead of her the way a fist opens — sudden, wide, brown water braiding itself into a dozen channels between gravel bars that had been islands an hour ago. In low season this was a place she could read like her own handwriting. Now it was a stranger's scrawl, rewritten by three days of rain, every channel lying about its depth, every bar moving under the surface like something alive.

"Left fork," Silas shouted from beside her, folio strapped to his chest inside an oilskin wrap he'd lashed there with two turns of line. He'd been reading the water over her shoulder for six days now; he knew enough to shout before she did.

"No." She hauled the wheel hard right. "Left's deeper but it bends back toward them."

The rifle cracked. The sound came flat off the water, and then the sound after it — the round going through the pilothouse window and out the other side, taking glass and a sliver of the doorframe with it. Beatrix didn't flinch. Flinching cost hands their place on the wheel.

"Down," she said.

Silas went down behind the engine housing without argument, which she noted, distantly, was the kind of thing she'd have found admirable any other day.

The right channel narrowed. That was the gamble — narrow meant fast, fast meant the current would carry them past the launch's guns before they could reload properly, but narrow also meant the snags were closer together and meaner. She felt the Magpie answer the wheel with a sluggishness that made her stomach drop. The old girl was taking water faster than the pumps could argue with her. Somewhere below their feet, in the dark of the bilge, the gorge had left its signature: a sprung seam, maybe two, planks working loose in frames bent when they'd hit the second wave wrong.

"Magpie," she said quietly, to no one, to the boat herself. "Come on. Come on, love."

The channel shot them through. Gravel hissed along the hull like sand poured over a drumhead. A dead cedar leaned out of the flood on their port side, roots gripping nothing, and Beatrix threaded the gap between its crown and a submerged bar with perhaps a boat-length to spare either way. Behind them the launch bellowed, committed to the left fork, and for thirty seconds there was only the roar of water and the hammering of her own heart and the small, terrible sound of the bilge pump laboring like a man climbing stairs on his knees.

Then the channels braided apart, and the launch was hidden behind a gravel bar the size of a church, and Beatrix let out a breath she'd been holding since the Gate.

"We made it," Silas said, rising.

She didn't answer. She was listening. Not to him — to the boat. To the particular note of the hull under her feet, the vibration that came up through the deck plates and into her boots and, over twenty years, into her bones. Every captain who loved a vessel learned to hear it speak. And the Magpie was speaking now in a voice Beatrix had heard exactly once before, the day she'd bought her, half-sunk at a derelict dock, and it was saying what it had said then:

*I'm done.*

"She's shipping it bad," Beatrix said. "Get the dory loose. Get the folio in it. Move."

Silas looked at her — really looked, the way he did when he was deciding whether a sentence was data or a wall — and then he moved. No argument. She loved him a little for that, in the part of her that wasn't already grieving.

* * *

*Silas*

He had watched men lose things before. He'd watched surveyors lose years of work to a match; he'd watched a widow sign away a claim with a hand that didn't shake until the pen was down. He thought he understood loss as a category.

He did not understand it until he watched Beatrix Yarrow try to save her boat.

The dory was lashed amidships, twelve feet of clinker-built cedar that had ridden the Magpie's roof rails since before Silas had ever seen her. He got the lashings off with numb fingers while Beatrix held the bow into the outwash current, using the last of the Magpie's strength to shelter the smaller boat from the worst of the flow. Water came over the transom in cold fists. The bilge water was above the floorboards now. He could see it sloshing against the underside of the engine housing, black and patient.

"Folio first," she said again, and he cinched the oilskin bundle under the dory's center thwart, lashing it down the way she'd taught him to lash everything — twice around, half hitch, twice more.

Then he turned to help her, and stopped.

She wasn't steering anymore. She'd dropped to her knees at the engine housing with the inspection panel open, both arms buried to the shoulder in the dark, doing something down there that involved force and repetition and a rhythm too steady to be panic. Her hair had come loose and hung in wet ropes across her face. She was talking — low, continuous, words he couldn't make out, in a register he recognized from funerals.

"Beatrix." He put a hand on her shoulder. "We have minutes. Maybe less."

"I can get her to the shallows." Her voice came up out of the engine room raw. "If I can keep her turning, I can beach her on the bar, we can careen her, patch her at low water—"

"The bar's underwater. Everything's underwater. There is no low water for a month."

Her arms kept moving. "I ran the gorge," she said. "Nobody runs the gorge. I ran it. She earned—" and there the sentence broke, and what came through the break was not a captain's voice at all, and Silas Crane understood suddenly that he was watching grief arrive ahead of the death it belonged to, the way thunder sometimes comes before the lightning if the storm is close enough.

He knelt beside her. He didn't take her arms out of the engine. He just stayed there, shoulder against hers, and said, "Not yet. But soon. And I need you at the tiller when it happens, because I can't hold her bow into this current alone, and if we go over broadside we lose the folio too."

That reached her. He watched it reach her — the word *folio*, the arithmetic of sacrifice she was better at than anyone he'd ever known. Her arms slowed. Stopped. She closed the inspection panel with both palms, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child.

"All right," she said. "All right."

* * *

*Beatrix*

There is a thing no one tells you about losing a boat, which is that it takes longer than sinking.

The Magpie died by inches, and Beatrix steered her through every one of them. She brought her in toward the lee side of the flooded bar where the current slackened, feathering the throttle, keeping the hull square to the chop so she wouldn't roll. Silas got the dory over the side and held it against the hull with a boathook. They passed across what could be saved: the folio, already secured. The chart case. The tool roll. The shotgun. A tin of tea, because Beatrix insisted, and Silas had the sense not to ask why.

The rest — the brass compass her father had owned, the galley with its dented kettle, the bunk where she'd slept ten thousand nights, the wheel itself, that wheel, worn smooth in the places her palms had polished it — all of it stayed aboard, because a boat is not cargo. You do not strip your dying.

When there was nothing left to pass, she stood in the pilothouse a moment longer than she needed to.

"You were older than me when I got you," she said. "And you were ugly, and the man selling you lied about your engine, and I knew it, and I bought you anyway." The water was at her ankles now, cold through her boots. "Twenty-two years. You never once left me on a bar I didn't deserve."

The Magpie settled a little, as if leaning into her voice.

Then the current took the stern, and Beatrix stepped over the rail into the dory, and that was that.

They rowed clear — Silas pulling, Beatrix holding the boathook, neither speaking — and turned once, both of them, at the same moment, as if they'd agreed to it without agreeing. The Magpie lay over slowly to starboard, taking the flood into her open windows with a strange gentleness, the way the river does everything eventually. Her funnel went under first. Then the pilothouse, the glass catching one last flare of gray light through the rain. Then just the roofline, and the masthead with its little iron crow, which had been painted and repainted so many times it looked less like a bird than like the idea of one.

And then the river closed over her, and there was a boil of foam, and a ring of ripples, and nothing.

Beatrix discovered she was standing up in the dory. She didn't remember standing. Silas had a fist in her collar, holding her, not roughly, just holding, and her own hands had his wrist in a grip that must have hurt, and she was making a sound she would deny later, some animal noise torn straight out of the oldest part of her.

"Let go," she said.

"No," he said.

She let go anyway — of him, not of it — and sat down heavily in the stern sheets, and the rain came down on both of them, and the place where the Magpie had been smoothed over and became simply river, as though she had never been.

* * *

*Silas*

They drifted a mile downstream before either of them spoke.

The outwash spread them among the braided channels, and Beatrix — even hollowed out, even soaked and shaking — read the water well enough to pick the channel that bent away from the main current, away from the route the launch would take if it had survived the forks. It probably had. Boats like that always did. But distance was distance, and dusk was coming, and the rain was easing at last into the fine gray drizzle that meant the monsoon's heart had passed over them.

He wanted to say something. He had spent his career saying things — questions, reassurances, the calibrated sentences of an investigator — and he had never in his life been so aware of how useless all of it was. You could not investigate a woman's grief. You could not cross-examine it into feeling better.

In the end he said the true thing, because it was the only thing he had.

"I'm sorry. I know that's nothing. I know it doesn't weigh anything against—" He gestured at the drowned river. "But I'm sorry. She carried us through. I'll not forget her."

Beatrix was bailing with the tin, slow mechanical strokes, not because the dory needed it much but because her hands needed work or they'd start shaking visibly. She glanced at him.

"You called her 'she' without being told," she said. Her voice was wrecked but dry. "First time you've gotten that right all trip."

"What else?"

"Nothing else." She bailed. Bailed. Then, quieter: "My father built her hull number into the stem post. Carved it. Nobody knows that but me. So somewhere down there—" She stopped. Started again. "Somewhere down there she's still got her name on her. That matters to me. Don't ask me why."

"It doesn't need a why."

"Everything needs a why. That's what you are." But there was no edge in it. She set the tin down and looked upstream, at the stretch of brown water that held everything they'd lost, and her jaw worked once. "Corven took my debt. Then he tried to take my life. Now he's taken my boat. Do you understand what that means, Silas? It means I'm free."

"That's a strange way to put it."

"It's the only way to put it. A man can't own you if you've got nothing left to repossess." She almost smiled. It was the most terrible smile he had ever seen, and the bravest. "Everything I had in the world is on the bottom of the Kestrel except you, the papers, and a tin of tea. So we're going to make those three things count."

* * *

*Beatrix*

They made camp on a gravel rise that would be an island tomorrow, under an overturned dory and a tarp strung between two oars. Beatrix built a fire from driftwood despite the drizzle, because fire was a thing you did, a discipline of the living, and she needed disciplines tonight more than comfort.

The folio lay between them, wrapped in oilskin, dry as a sermon. Twenty-two years of her life traded for a stack of paper. She turned her hands over and looked at her palms — blistered from the wheel, rope-burned from the lashings — and decided they'd been worth spending.

Silas was asleep sitting up, or nearly; his head kept dipping and jerking back. He'd pulled them both through the gorge with his weight on the lines and his nerve holding, and he'd held her collar in the dory when she'd have walked into the river after her boat, and he hadn't said one word about either. She looked at him a long moment, feeling something settle in her chest that had no name yet and didn't need one tonight.

Then she heard the engine.

Far upstream, muffled by rain and distance, but unmistakable — the flat industrial knock of a government launch running its forks, coming downriver, sweeping the channels. Looking for wreckage. Looking for survivors. Looking for a folio.

Beatrix killed the fire with one kick of driftwood ash and put her hand over Silas's mouth before his eyes were fully open.

"Dory in the water," she breathed. "Now. Quiet as prayer."

Because the river had taken the Magpie, and taken her grief with it, and left only this: two people, one boat's worth of papers, and a mile of dark water between them and the men who wanted it all to end.

She slid the dory off the gravel without a splash, and the current took them, and the searchlight swept the channel they'd just left.
