## 18. Downstream Pursuit

The Magpie went down with her bow toward the sky, the way a proud animal dies, and Beatrix did not watch her finish. She watched instead the folio wrapped in oilskin, lashed to Silas's chest, and the dory — Corven's own dory, salvaged from the wreckage like a joke the river had decided to tell — riding low in the outwash current.

"Row," she said, and her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using it.

Silas rowed. He was bleeding at the temple where a stanchion had caught him, and his hands shook on the oars, but he rowed. Behind them the gorge spat white water and the last visible piece of the Magpie — her stern lantern, still burning, impossibly, as it spun away into the foam. Then that was gone too, and there was only the roar fading behind them and the wide brown water ahead, and the fog already gathering off the flooded banks like something summoned.

Beatrix took the tiller. The dory was sixteen feet of borrowed wood with a cracked thwart and a bilge that drank faster than any honest boat should. She'd bailed her out once already since the gorge. She would bail her a hundred more times if the river asked.

"They'll have heard us come through," she said. "Sound carries down this valley for miles. Corven's launch was below us at the anchorage — he'll be running up to meet whatever comes out."

Silas glanced over his shoulder at the wall of mist behind them. "Then we're rowing toward him."

"We're rowing *past* him." She pointed with her chin at the left bank, where the flood had drowned the treeline and turned a slough's mouth into an open door between two leaning cedars. "There. Backwater channel. It's not on any chart worth having, but I ran it twice in low water years ago, hunting moonshiners for a magistrate who didn't pay me either. It cuts four miles of river and comes out below Harker's Bend."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we drown somewhere new." She heard how it sounded and found she didn't care. Grief had burned everything soft out of her and left only the hard grain. "Row."

* * *

The slough swallowed them whole. Fog closed over the entrance behind like a held breath released, and suddenly the world was ten feet wide: black water, grey air, the wet shoulders of trees sliding past close enough to touch. The launch's engine noise — when it came — arrived muffled and directionless, a growl somewhere inside the fog with no body attached to it.

Beatrix steered by memory and by the water itself. A backwater in flood has its own grammar. Where the current pressed smooth against a trunk, the channel bent; where leaves swirled in slow circles, an eddy waited to trap a small boat and hold it while bigger boats closed in; where the surface dimpled upward without wind, a sunken fence or a drowned cart lay just under the keel, waiting to open her seams. She read all of it aloud, half to herself, half to Silas, because saying it kept her from thinking about the Magpie settling into forty feet of cold water with her name still painted on the transom.

"Port your stroke," she murmured. "There's a deadfall under that green slick. Trust me."

He trusted her. She felt it arrive, unasked, the way warmth arrives through a wall — not a decision but a fact. Somewhere in the storm-bound days at the gorge anchorage, between the flash flood and the lamplight and the things they'd said to each other about what institutions take from people who love them, trust had stopped being a bargain between them and become simply the ground they stood on. She hadn't named it then. There was no time to name anything now.

The engine sound faded astern. For nearly an hour there was nothing but dripping canopy and the knock of the oarlocks and the small industrious slosh of water in the bilge. Beatrix bailed with a tin cup one-handed while steering with the other, and Silas rowed until his palms blistered and bled, and neither of them spoke about the boat they'd lost or the men they'd swept away in the flood surge or the word *love*, which had been spoken once, quietly, in the dark of the storm, and not repeated since — not because it was doubted, but because repeating it might make it into something that could be taken away, and they had both learned what could be taken away.

Then the fog thinned, and the slough gave them out into the main river again, and Beatrix saw the smoke.

A smudge of it, far downstream, hanging above Harker's Bend where the channel narrowed against the bluff. Not chimney smoke. Coal smoke. The launch, holding station at the bend, guarding the door out.

"He's not chasing," she said slowly. "He's herding. He knows these channels feed back into the main stem eventually. He's sitting on every exit he can think of."

"Can he think of this one?"

"He can think of most of them. He's bought half the pilots on this river at one time or another." Her jaw tightened. "But he never bought me. And that's his mistake. He thinks like a man who owns the map. He doesn't think like the river."

She let the dory drift in the slack water under the drowned bank, oars shipped, and made herself look at the situation the way she'd look at a sandbar: not as an obstacle but as a shape, with a shape's logic. The bend below them was a long right-hand curve against a high bluff. The launch sat mid-channel at its throat, engine idling, searchlight hooded but ready. Any boat coming down the fairway would be silhouetted against the bright water upstream before it ever got close. Any boat hugging the bluff would be seen against the pale rock.

Any boat, she thought, except one that wasn't running the channel at all.

"The cut-off," she said. "Under the bluff there's an old logging chute. They floated timber down it thirty years ago. The mouth is hidden behind that rock shelf — you can't see it from midstream unless you know to look for the seam of white stone. It comes out *below* the bend, fifty yards past him, in the shadow of the bluff itself."

"In flood?"

"In flood it's a chute, not a channel. We'll hit that water doing better than eight knots and we won't steer so much as pray our way down it. Rocks the whole way." She looked at him. "I'm not going to pretend that's a good trade. But staying here is no trade at all. When the fog lifts — and it will lift by mid-morning — we're a duck on a pond."

Silas wiped the blood from his temple and looked at the chute's hidden mouth for a long moment. "You've run it?"

"Once. In a heavier boat. In low water." She almost smiled. "It tried to kill me then, too. But it failed, and I remember every foot of it."

"That's the most romantic thing you've said to me," Silas said, "and I include the storm."

"Save it," Beatrix said. "If we live past the next ten minutes, remind me, and I'll say something worse."

* * *

They came at the chute with the current, oars backing water to hold their line, and the seam of white stone slid by on their left exactly where her memory said it would be, and then the river simply *took* them.

The chute was a throat of green water between granite walls barely wider than the dory herself. They went down it standing up in the hull, Beatrix at the stern with the steering oar jammed against her hip, Silas forward with his arms locked around the oilskin-wrapped folio as if it were a child. The walls blurred. Spray hit them like thrown gravel. Once — only once — the hull struck glancing off a submerged block and the whole world became a shriek of wood on stone, and Beatrix threw her weight against the steering oar and felt the boat answer, feel her *answer*, the way the Magpie used to answer, and something in her chest that had been dead since the gorge flickered and did not go out.

Then daylight. The chute spat them into the main channel fifty yards below the launch, in the long blue shadow of the bluff, moving fast.

For three full seconds Beatrix allowed herself to believe it had worked.

Then the launch's searchlight snapped on, and the engine roared, and the morning filled with the sound of a man being cheated.

"Down!" she shouted, and Silas dropped into the bilge water as the first rifle shot cracked across the water and slapped the bluff face somewhere above them. The second shot hit the dory. She heard it — a sound like a knuckle rapping a door — and felt the new leak begin beneath her, cold and insistent.

The third shot found Silas.

He made no cry. He simply folded sideways against the gunwale, hand flying to his scalp, and when his fingers came away they were red. A graze — she saw it in the way he moved, the way he swore, low and furious rather than frightened — but blood poured freely down his face and dripped from his jaw into the bilge, and for one white-hot moment Beatrix forgot the launch, the river, the folio, everything, and was only a woman looking at blood on the man she loved.

"I'm fine," he said. "It's fine. *Go.*"

She went.

She drove the dory across the channel in a flat-out sprint, using the launch's own turn against it — Corven's pilot was swinging wide to bring his bow guns around, and every second of that swing was distance she stole. Shots followed them, wild now, the shooter firing from a pitching deck at a target weaving through the chop. One round punched through the transom. Another clipped the gunwale a hand's width from Beatrix's wrist and sent a splinter spinning away downstream.

And then the fog — the same fog that had betrayed them at the bend — came back for them.

It rolled off the flooded fields in a single great bank, the way fog does when the sun finally gets its teeth into the water below and pulls the moisture up wholesale, and it swallowed the channel whole. The searchlight became a blind yellow coin hanging in nothing. The engine noise smeared and doubled and lost its bearing. Beatrix put the dory's nose into the thickest of it and killed their wake by slipping into the flooded timber along the eastern shore, where the water was six feet deep over what had been pasture, and rowed slow and silent between the tops of buried fence posts.

Behind them, the launch blundered up and down the channel, sounding its horn, firing once at a log.

* * *

They drifted in the drowned pasture for the better part of an hour, close enough together that their shoulders touched, not speaking. Beatrix tore a strip from her shirt hem and bound Silas's head, her hands steady until the knot was tied and then not steady at all, and he covered her shaking fingers with his own and held them there.

"You thought it was worse," he said. Not a question.

"I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I spent two days in the storm telling myself that if we lived, I wouldn't— that I'd keep the important part of me back. Some reserve. Some ballast. Because everyone I've ever given the whole of myself to, the river took, or Corven took, or the work took." She laughed, and it came out broken. "And then you bled, and there wasn't any reserve. There was just me, standing there with my whole chest open, watching red come out of you."

Silas turned his head and kissed her palm, blood and all. "Good," he said. "Ballast is just weight. You're the one who reads the water."

She wanted to answer that. She didn't get the chance, because the fog chose that moment to shred, and through the gap she saw two things at once: the launch, far upstream now, giving up the search and turning back toward the bend —

— and, directly ahead, half-hidden in the flooded willows, a shape she knew the way you know a scar.

A riverboat. Small, old, half-sunk to her gunwales in the shallows, abandoned — but whole. Intact. A hull, a deckhouse, a mast stepped and bare.

Beatrix stared at her, and the grief and the hope in her rose together and became indistinguishable, the way floodwater and river become indistinguishable, and she heard herself say, in a voice that did not sound like hers:

"Silas. Get out the oars. The river isn't finished taking things from Corven today — and it's not finished giving, either."

The launch's horn sounded again, far off, angry and lost. And Beatrix Yarrow, boatless twelve hours and already unfaithful to her mourning, turned the leaking dory toward the wreck in the willows.
