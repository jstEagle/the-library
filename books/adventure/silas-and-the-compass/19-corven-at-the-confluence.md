## 19. Corven at the Confluence

*Beatrix*

The fog came off the water like a held breath.

Beatrix cut the engine to a whisper and let the dory drift, one hand on the tiller, the other pressed flat against the hull as if she could read the river through her palm. She could. Thirty years of it lived in that hand — the shudder of current against planking, the difference between water moving over sand and water moving over mud, the cold breath a deep channel exhaled through fog.

Ahead, hidden somewhere in the white, the Kestrel met the Sorrel. The confluence. Every river rat on this stretch knew it for what it was: a graveyard of boats and a nursery of lies. The two rivers braided together over miles of shifting bars, channels that were true on Tuesday and treacherous by Friday, and no chart ever printed survived a season there.

Behind them — she couldn't see them, but she could feel them the way you feel a storm cell building past the horizon — Corven's launch was coming. The *Iron Meridian*, forty feet of government-surplus steel with twin screws and a searchlight, carrying Corven himself and six men who had already fired on them once in the backwaters. Silas sat in the bow with a graze wrapped in bandage along his ribs, holding the folio against his chest like a child holding something too precious to set down.

"Tell me we're not just drifting," he said quietly.

"We're reading," Beatrix said.

The fog thickened. Somewhere upstream, an engine note changed pitch — the launch slowing, its captain cautious now, wary of the same water she was. Good. Caution would keep him in the main channel. Caution was exactly what she needed from him.

She'd been running this confluence since she was nineteen years old, running cargo ahead of the law and occasionally ahead of worse things, and if the river had given her one gift beyond a living, it was this: she knew where the *Serpent's Tooth* lay. A sandbar with a spine of hardpan under it, shaped like nothing on any survey, growing season by season out of the silt the Kestrel dropped when it slowed to meet the Sorrel. In low water it showed itself plainly enough that even fools avoided it. But the monsoon had raised everything four feet, and now the Tooth wore a smooth brown cap of moving water, indistinguishable from the safe channel beside it — unless you knew to look for the seam. Unless you knew that the current over the bar ran a half-knot slower than the channel, and that slow water left a line in the surface as fine as a hair, visible only to eyes that had spent their lives looking.

Corven didn't have those eyes. Corven had charts, and charts said open water.

"You're going to run us aground on purpose," Silas said. Not a question. He'd learned to read her silences over three hundred miles of flight.

"I'm going to run *him* aground on purpose." She eased the tiller over. "We're going to look like we're lost. Because we are lost, a little. And then I'm going to give him a reason to take the shortcut."

"And the shortcut is—"

"The Tooth." She felt the first tug of the confluence's mixed currents take the dory's stern, swinging them gently, and corrected without thinking. "He'll come around the point thinking he's cutting us off before we reach the delta side. It's what any sensible man would do. Sensible men drown here all the time."

Silas was quiet a moment. "Beatrix. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong." She heard the flatness in her own voice and let it stay. "It can't go wrong. That's the whole cruelty of it. I've known this bar since before Corven owned his first warehouse. The river doesn't care whose money is on which boat."

The fog swallowed sound and gave it back strangely. The launch's engines seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, circling, hunting. Then a voice rolled across the water through a loudhailer, distorted but unmistakable, warm as bad whiskey:

*"Miss Yarrow! Captain Yarrow! Let's not make this difficult."*

Beatrix closed her eyes for half a second. Twenty years of favors, advances, rescues-from-ruin, all of it packed into that voice like cargo into a hold. She had owed Corven more than money for most of her adult life. She had told herself it was friendship with interest attached.

The radio crackled at her elbow — the little set they'd salvaged with the dory, still tuned to the frequency Corven's people used. His voice came through clean now, almost gentle.

*"You're a businesswoman, Beatrix. So am I. Hand over Crane and the papers, and we part friends. You keep your life, your name, whatever's left of your reputation. Refuse, and I take both boats and everything in them, and the river keeps what the river keeps."*

She keyed the transmit switch. Her thumb hovered there a moment longer than it needed to.

"Corven," she said. "You burned eight people alive in a survey camp and weighted the ninth into the current. There's no parting friends from that. There's only the river now."

A pause. When he answered, the warmth was gone, and what lay underneath it was older and colder than either of them. *"So be it."*

The engines surged.

"There's our reason," Beatrix murmured, and opened the throttle.

* * *

*Silas*

They ran.

The dory was a leaking, overloaded shell of a thing, salvaged from the Magpie's wreckage with the folio dry inside a tarred canvas wrap and almost nothing else worth saving — and Silas had watched Beatrix grieve that boat with a silence worse than weeping, so he understood exactly how much this small craft cost her pride. But Beatrix flew it. She flew it the way she'd flown the Magpie, the way she'd flown the gorge: with total attention, total economy, not a motion wasted, not a doubt permitted.

Fog tore past them in rags. Behind, the searchlight of the Iron Meridian bloomed and swelled, a yellow sun rising through the murk, and the launch's bow wave became audible — a long hiss of displaced water closing fast. They were heavier and slower and they were losing, and that was the design.

"They're coming around the point!" Silas shouted over the engine.

"I know!" Beatrix stood at the tiller, hair plastered to her face, eyes fixed on water that looked to him like every other stretch of brown flood. "When I swing wide, get down flat. Bullets skip off water, but I'd rather they skip over us."

The first shot came before she finished the sentence. It cracked somewhere to their left and whined away over the fog. Then a ragged volley, wild, firing blind into white. Silas flattened himself over the folio and felt the dory heel hard as Beatrix threw them into a wide, lazy arc toward the delta shore — a wounded, stumbling turn, a boat that had run out of river.

It was the finest lie he had ever seen told, and it was told entirely with a tiller.

The loudhailer again, triumphant: *"There! Cut them off — take the near channel!"*

Silas lifted his head. Ahead, the fog thinned for one merciful instant, and he saw it: two waters, apparently identical, running side by side toward the delta. To the right, the wider sweep. To the left, the shortcut — smooth, inviting, a lane of brown glass between two lines of drowned willow.

And between them, invisible, a hairline seam in the current that only one person alive could read.

"He sees it," Silas breathed. "God help me, he actually sees it."

"He sees what I want him to see." Beatrix's voice was very quiet. "Hold on to something that loves you back."

The launch swung left.

For three seconds, four, the Iron Meridian planed across the smooth water, beautiful and fast and doomed, twin screws churning, Corven's silhouette standing proud at the wheel — and then the bow struck the hardpan spine of the Serpent's Tooth at full speed.

The sound carried across the whole confluence. Steel on stone, a shriek and then a boom, and the forty-foot launch rose at the bow as though trying to leap the bar, hung there a horrible instant — Silas saw men thrown forward, saw the searchlight cartwheel through the fog — and slammed down hard, driven up onto the sandbar's back, high and dry and tilted, wedged like a fish on a hook.

The engines screamed, racing free in reverse gear, chewing foam. Someone aboard began shouting orders. Another voice — Corven's, stripped of every ounce of warmth — cut through them all.

Beatrix brought the dory around in a slow circle, out of rifle range, and killed the engine.

They drifted in the fog and watched.

* * *

*Beatrix*

She had imagined this moment, in the dark hours of the last week, in more shapes than she cared to admit. She had imagined gunfire. She had imagined Corven dead, or herself dead, or both of them standing in some smoky room while the law finally did its slow work. She had not imagined this: silence, fog, and a powerful man's ship stranded on a bar of her own naming while the river rose around him inch by patient inch.

Because that was the thing about the Serpent's Tooth. It wasn't just a bar. It was a trap with a clock in it.

The monsoon hadn't finished. Upstream, the Kestrel was still gathering what the mountains gave it, and every hour the confluence climbed another few inches. The Tooth would go under completely by nightfall. Corven's launch, holed and heeled and pinned, would follow it under. And the sandbar's soft shoulders — the ones she had carefully *not* put him on — would soften into quicksand as the current undermined them. Men could wade off it now. By dusk, wading would mean drowning.

"Beatrix." Silas stood beside her, and she realized he'd been watching her face, not the wreck. "What are you thinking?"

"That I should hate this," she said. "And I don't. That frightens me a little."

Across the water, figures moved on the launch's deck. One man had already gone over the side, testing the shallows, and stood knee-deep on the bar's downstream shoulder waving the others toward him. Corven remained at the rail. Even from here she could read the set of his shoulders — the man was calculating, always calculating, weighing the loss of the launch against the loss of time, time against the folio, the folio against his neck.

Then the loudhailer found them one last time through the thinning fog.

*"Yarrow."* No warmth left at all. Just gravel and arithmetic. *"You've made your point. Name your price. Anything. The debt, the boat, a new hull, double — name it."*

Beatrix took the radio handset. Her hands were steady. She noticed that with distant surprise, the way you notice weather.

"Corven," she said, "you never once asked me what I wanted. Twenty years, and every favor, every advance, every rescue came with the price already written. So here's mine, and it isn't for sale." She looked at the stranded launch, at the small furious figure at its rail, at the river rising around him with the patience of all rivers. "I want you to sit on that bar and watch the water come up, and understand that it was never me who beat you. I just steered. The river did the rest. The river does everything, eventually."

She set the handset down.

"Will he drown?" Silas asked quietly.

"Not if he's smart. He can walk off the downstream shoulder within the hour, wet and humiliated, and spend the rest of his life explaining a lost launch to men scarier than him." She turned the dory's bow toward the delta. "But he'll do it knowing the river chose. That's a wound that doesn't close."

The fog was lifting at last, torn into long banners by a wind out of the south. Ahead, the delta opened — a vast glittering maze of channels, reed islands, and far-off brown sails, and beyond it, faint as a promise, the smoke of the press town.

Silas touched her arm. "There's a boat behind us."

She looked back. On the bar's downstream edge, Corven's men were lowering something into the shallows — the launch's tender, a steel skiff, seaworthy enough. Figures climbing down to it. Corven last, pausing at the rail to look across the water at her dory, a long look she returned without blinking.

Then he stepped down into the skiff, and the current took him, and the great Iron Meridian settled a little deeper onto the bar behind him, groaning, like something being laid to rest.

"He'll run for the fort," Silas said. "Try to reach his people before we reach the press. Burn what he can't buy."

"Then we'd better not lose any more time admiring the view." Beatrix opened the throttle, and the dory swung south into the widening delta, into the light breaking through the fog.

Behind them, muffled by distance and water, the river rose over the Serpent's Tooth — and the launch's mast, tilting slowly, went down like a sinking star.
