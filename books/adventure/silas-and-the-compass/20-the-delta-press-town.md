## 20. The Delta Press Town

The delta announced itself before we saw it — a change in the water's voice, from the river's single-minded roar to something broader, more patient, a hundred channels murmuring among themselves like old men arguing in a courtyard. Beatrix stood at the tiller of our leaking dory with her eyes half-closed, reading that murmur the way other people read print.

"Salt by tomorrow," she said. "You'll taste it on your lips before you see it."

I tasted it that evening. We had been three days in the dory since the confluence, three days of bailing and fog and backwater channels so narrow the reeds brushed both gunwales at once, and my shoulder where the bullet had grazed me had gone from hot to merely throbbing. The folio rode wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, tied down with the same cord I'd once used to bind government dispatches. It seemed heavier every day. Not the paper — the knowing.

On the fourth morning the reeds opened and there it was: Saltmere, the delta press town, all tin roofs and drying nets and telegraph wires strung between poles like the rigging of some vast derelict ship. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys. Somewhere a press was running — you could feel it through the water, a rhythmic thudding under the town's noise, patient as a heartbeat.

Beatrix brought us alongside a rotting public dock and made fast without ceremony. She looked at the town the way she looked at sandbars: assessing, unimpressed, already planning her line through it.

"You're sure about this one?" she asked. Not for the first time.

"The *Tidewater Ledger*," I said. "Independent. They've run stories about Corven's shipping lines for two years and never been bought off. And Judge Amara Voss holds federal circuit here — she was pushed out of the capital for refusing to sign an eviction order. If anyone downstream is honest enough to act, it's these two."

"Honest enough to act," she repeated softly. "That's a low bar to be gambling our lives on."

"It's the only bar left."

She untied the cord at my chest herself, then stopped, her hands on the oilcloth, and looked at me. In the flat morning light her face showed every mile of the river we'd come — the sleepless nights at anchor, the ambush, the gorge, the moment the Magpie went down beneath us and she'd watched twenty years of her life fold into brown water. She hadn't cried then. She didn't cry now. But her hands stayed on the folio a moment longer than they needed to.

"If they don't act," she said, "we've lost everything for nothing."

"If they don't act," I said, "at least the truth will be in print, and Corven will have to burn down a newspaper instead of a survey camp. That's progress. That's how it starts."

"That's a terrible comfort, Crane."

"I'm an investigator. Terrible comfort is my specialty."

She almost smiled. Then she handed me the folio, and we went up into the town together.

* * *

The *Ledger*'s offices occupied the second floor of a building above a chandlery, reached by stairs that groaned like the dory had. The newsroom smelled of ink, tobacco, and river damp. A woman with ink-stained fingers and grey-shot hair looked up from a composing stone as we entered, took in my bandaged shoulder, Beatrix's salt-cracked hands, and the oilcloth bundle between us, and set down her type stick with the deliberation of someone who has waited years for exactly this kind of visitor.

"You're not from the capital," she said.

"No," I said. "We're from upriver. From where the capital doesn't look."

"My name is Hesper Lund. I edit this paper." She wiped her hands on her apron, which only redistributed the ink. "And you're going to tell me why two people who look like they've been through a war have walked into my office carrying what I very much hope is what I think it is."

I unwrapped the folio on her composing table.

For a long moment she didn't touch it. She bent over the pages — the half-melted sheet from the trading post ash, the water-stained survey records, and the missing folio itself, recovered from the bilge where I'd hidden it from men in uniform, deciphered by lamplight during the storm while Beatrix bailed and the rain tried to drown us both. Survey data. Ore assays. Coordinates. And names — officials' names, written in a surveyor's careful hand in the margins, noting who had signed off on designating mineral-rich land as empty, who had ordered the team's route changed, who had been paid through which shell company in which port.

Hesper Lund read for ten minutes without moving. When she finally straightened, her face had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with the size of what she was holding.

"This is the Kestrel survey," she said. "The team that vanished. The government said they were lost in the spring flood."

"They were murdered," I said. "Weighted down and given to the river. One of them surfaced near their own camp. The orders came from above — from Corven's operation, protected by officials whose names are in those margins. The survey found ore on land that had been officially declared worthless, and someone was selling that land's future for pennies on the dollar. The team wouldn't recant their findings. So the findings were burned, and the team followed them into the water."

"And you're the investigator sent to find them." Her eyes narrowed. "Sent by whom?"

"By the same chain of command that buried them. I didn't know it when I took the warrant. I burned the warrant somewhere above the trading post." I met her gaze steadily. "I'm not here as a government man anymore, Ms. Lund. I'm here as a witness."

She looked at me for a long time. Then at Beatrix, who had said nothing and intended, I suspected, to say nothing until it mattered.

"The judge," Hesper said at last. "Voss. She takes tea at four. If you're going to stand in front of a federal judge with evidence of murder and corruption, you'll want to do it before Corven's people learn you've reached the delta. Which means now. Which means you'll want to know that his company keeps a warehouse on this waterfront and at least six men on its payroll who carry guns the way dockworkers carry hooks."

"We know about his men," Beatrix said. Her first words in the room. "We've spent the last week being shot at by them. Six is nothing."

Hesper Lund looked at her, and something passed between the two women — recognition, maybe, of a certain kind of stubbornness that doesn't care about odds.

"Four o'clock," Hesper said. "I'll send word ahead. And Mr. Crane—" she gathered the folio pages with surprising tenderness, "—before you go anywhere, my typesetters are going to make copies of every page. If anything happens to you, or to me, or to this building, the story goes out anyway. That's the first rule of this trade: never let the truth live in only one place."

It was, I thought, the soundest doctrine I'd heard from any institution in months.

* * *

Judge Amara Voss received us in a rented room above the customs house, because the delta had no courthouse worthy of the name and she had stopped pretending otherwise. She was younger than I expected, broad-shouldered, with a federal seal on a ribbon around her neck and the wary posture of a woman who had learned that authority in the provinces was mostly theater unless you personally supplied the substance.

She read the folio in silence while tea went cold beside her. Hesper Lund sat by the window, watching the street. Beatrix stood against the wall with her arms crossed, positioned — I noticed — where she could see both the door and the window, out of habit rather than fear.

When Voss finished, she closed the folio and rested her hand flat upon it, as if holding something down against wind.

"Do you understand what you're asking me to do?" she said.

"I'm asking you to issue warrants," I said. "Arrest warrants for the officials named in those margins, and a seizure order on Corven's shipping assets pending investigation. The evidence supports it. You know it supports it."

"What I know," Voss said, "is that two of the men named here sit on the bench above me in the capital. What I know is that the last federal officer who crossed Corven was reassigned to a customs post so remote it doesn't appear on maps. What I know is that if I sign these warrants, I will need every honest constable in this delta to enforce them, and I will need this newspaper to put the story in front of the country before Corven's friends can bury it. And what I also know—" her hand pressed harder on the folio, "—is that eight people went up that river to make a map and never came home, and no one has said their names aloud since. So yes, Mr. Crane. I will sign."

The room was quiet. Through the floorboards, faintly, I could feel the press of the *Ledger* still running — Hesper had gone back to work the moment we left, typesetting through the afternoon, and the rhythm came up through the building like a pulse.

"There's a condition," Voss added. "You two. You'll give sworn statements. Everything — the camp, the body, the patrol boat, the ambush, the gorge, all of it. On the record. Because warrants are paper, and paper burns. Witnesses are harder to burn."

"Not impossible," Beatrix said from the wall. "We've watched them try."

"Then you'll be well compensated for the risk," Voss said dryly. "There's a reward posted for recovery of the survey property. I suspect you qualify."

Beatrix unfolded her arms slowly. I saw the thought move behind her eyes — reward money meant a hull, meant caulking and paint and a boat of her own again — and then I saw her set the thought aside, deliberately, the way she'd set aside everything else on this river when it mattered.

"After the arrests," she said. "Pay me after the arrests. I don't take money for a job that isn't done."

Voss studied her for a moment, then inclined her head — the smallest bow I ever saw a judge give anyone.

"Madam," she said, "you may be the most solvent person in this room."

* * *

They came for Corven's warehouse at dawn.

I stood on the customs house steps and watched the constables go in — twelve of them, armed, moving with the nervous energy of men doing something unprecedented in living memory. Voss had worked through the night signing papers; Hesper's special edition had hit the streets at midnight, and by morning the headline ran across the front page in letters tall enough to read from across the harbor:

**THE RIVER GAVE UP ITS DEAD: SURVEY TEAM MURDERED — MINISTER'S SIGNATURE ON THE ORDERS**

People crowded the docks reading it aloud to each other. An old fisherman read the list of the dead — eight names, printed in full — and took off his hat. A woman pressed the paper to her chest like something warm. The delta, which had known for years that something rotten floated down from upstream, finally had it in ink, and ink, unlike rumor, could not be drowned.

By noon the first arrests rippled upstream. Telegraph wires hummed all day — I watched the operators' shutters clattering in the customs house like insects — warrants confirmed in two river towns, a port commissioner taken at his breakfast table, Corven's shell companies frozen by order of a bank examiner who, Hesper told me gleefully, had been waiting years for permission. Each report arrived as a slip of paper, and Voss pinned them to the wall of her chambers in a growing constellation, and each pin was a small, irreversible fact.

Corven himself was not among them. His launch had been seen heading upriver two days before we reached Saltmere — toward the gorges, toward higher water, toward whatever refuge he imagined the upper Kestrel still held. Voss issued the warrant anyway. "Rivers," she said, "only flow one way. He'll come down eventually. The law can wait at the bottom."

But vindication, I was learning, is a strange meal. It fills you and leaves you hungry at once.

I gave my statement that afternoon, four hours on the record, my voice going hoarse over the details of the camp and the drowned man and the burned warrant. Beatrix gave hers after me, terse and exact, describing sandbars and currents and the flash flood surge with the same precision she'd give a chart. When it was done, the clerk asked her if she wished to add anything further.

"Yes," she said. "The Magpie. Forty-one feet, oak on elm, built by my father. She carried us through all of it and she deserves to be in the record. Put her down as a casualty."

The clerk looked at me. I nodded. He wrote it down.

* * *

Evening found us on the public dock, sitting side by side with our legs hanging over black water, watching the lamps of Saltmere come on one by one along the shore. My shoulder ached dully. Beatrix had a mug of something rough and local and held it without drinking. Down the waterfront, Corven's seized warehouse stood dark under a constable's lantern, its doors chalked with the seal of the court.

"It doesn't feel the way I thought it would," I said.

"No?"

"I thought it would feel like an ending. Eight names in print, warrants signed, the machine starting to turn. Instead it feels like—" I searched for it. "Like reaching a bend in the river and seeing that the channel just keeps going. More river behind every bend. More names above the ones we caught. Corven still out there, and whoever taught him it was safe."

Beatrix turned the mug in her hands.

"When my father died," she said, "everyone told me grief closes. Like water over a stone. It doesn't. It just teaches you to steer around it." She glanced at me sideways. "This is the same. The arrests won't close it. The trial won't close it. Maybe nothing closes it. But the river moves, Crane. That's the whole lesson I've got. It takes what it takes, and then it moves, and if you're still floating, you move with it."

Ahead of us, far down the delta's throat, the last light was dying over the open water — the free water beyond the charts, beyond the warrants, beyond everyone's reach. Somewhere out there was a horizon with no patron's debts and no government paper attached to it.

"Voss mentioned a reward," I said carefully. "Once the seizures clear."

"She did."

"And you said you'd wait until the job was done."

Beatrix smiled — small, tired, real. "The job's nearly done. And I've been thinking about hulls. There's a yard two streets over with a battered thirty-footer sitting on blocks, and the owner wants rid of it." She took her first drink of the mug and grimaced at it, which somehow made the smile warmer. "I'll need someone at the caulking who's good with lamplight and patience. You have any experience with oakum, Crane?"

"None whatsoever."

"Good. I'll teach you. It's like investigation — mostly finding where things leak."

We sat a while longer as the lamps brightened and the press of the *Ledger* thudded on somewhere behind us, printing tomorrow's edition, printing the day after's, printing the names of the dead until the whole country would know them. Upstream, past all the dark bends, Corven was running before the law with a river full of witnesses behind him. The water would bring him down in time. Rivers only flow one way.

But that was tomorrow's current. Tonight there was a dock, and a lamp-lit town, and a woman beside me sketching hull lines in the air with her finger, and ahead of us — at last, at the end of every chart — open water.

"Two weeks to refit," Beatrix said. "Then we cast off."

"Where to?"

She pointed her mug down the delta, at the darkening sea-road where the river gave itself away.

"Nowhere in particular," she said. "Free water. I hear it's unmapped."

And for the first time since I'd accepted that warrant on a stranger's dock, the future sounded like something worth sailing into.
