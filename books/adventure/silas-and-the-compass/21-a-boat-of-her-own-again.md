## 21. A Boat of Her Own, Again

The hull sat on the mud flats below Tarrow's Landing like something the river had chewed and spat out and forgotten. Forty feet of blackened oak, ribs showing through the planking in places like the bones of a fish picked clean by gulls. Her name had been painted over twice — *Fair Constance*, then something illegible beneath a skin of tar — and her sternpost was sprung wide enough to put your fist through.

Beatrix Yarrow stood on the flats at low tide with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and looked at her for a long time.

"You don't want that one," said old Hesk, who owned the salvage yard and had buried two wives and three boats in his sixty years on the water. "She's rotten to the turn of the bilge. I'm only asking forty because the iron's worth something."

"I'll give you thirty."

"For that? Twenty-five, and you haul her off my mud yourself."

"Thirty," Beatrix said again, "and I haul her today, and you throw in the windlass off your dead trawler."

Hesk spat into the mud, which was how he signed things. "Done. You're a fool, Yarrow. But I've seen fools raise dead boats before." He squinted downriver toward the delta haze. "I've also seen them drown doing it."

*The Magpie was dead.* That was the sentence she carried everywhere now, folded up small inside her chest like a letter she couldn't stop rereading. Three weeks since the outwash took her — since Beatrix had stood chest-deep in brown water clawing the folio free of the flooding cabin while her boat went down around her, keel first, like a woman sinking slowly into sleep. Three weeks since she'd watched the last six inches of wheelhouse slide under and felt something go under with it that no reward money could float again.

They'd given her a medal and a check. The federal judge — Voss, the honest one, the rarest animal on the river — had shaken her hand in front of a crowd and called her *a credit to the service*, which was funny, because she'd never been in any service, and because the same government whose colors she'd lied under in Chapter Nine had spent eleven years making her life difficult at every customs dock from here to the headwaters. The check was generous. Corven's seized accounts had been liquid, at least; whatever else could be said of the man, he'd kept good books right up until Silas Crane's newspaper friends got hold of them.

Arrests were still rippling upstream. Every few days another name surfaced in the delta papers — a harbormaster, a survey clerk, a colonel retired comfortably to a hill station who'd suddenly found himself answering very uncomfortable questions. Corven himself sat in a federal cell awaiting trial, refusing counsel, saying nothing, which Beatrix understood completely. Men like Corven didn't talk because talking meant admitting the river had beaten them, and men like that would rather hang.

She didn't think about him much. She thought about the Magpie constantly.

About the way her engine — a ancient Kelvin she'd rebuilt herself at nineteen, the winter after her father died — used to catch on the second compression stroke, always the second, never the first, like an old man clearing his throat before he spoke. About the way the wheel had a soft spot at two o'clock where the spokes had worn smooth under her father's hands and then hers. About the sound she made running light through the shallows above the Sandbar Reach, that particular humming in the planks that Beatrix had known meant *you're fine* the way other people knew it from their mothers' voices.

Twenty-two years on that boat. She'd learned to read water sitting in its wheelhouse before she could read print.

And the river had taken her anyway, and the river was not sorry, because the river was never sorry. That was the first thing it taught you and the last thing it ever told you: *the river forgives nothing.* It only moves on.

So she supposed she would have to move on too.

* * *

Moving on, it turned out, looked like standing knee-deep in someone else's rot with a crowbar in her hands.

She beached the Fair Constance on the shingle below the yard at dawn, and by seven she had the first plank off, and by nine she knew exactly how bad it was, and by ten she had stopped caring, which was the important part. Rotten garboard strakes. Rotten transom knees. A mast step gone so punky you could push a thumb through it like bread. But the frames — the frames were good. Solid grown oak, salt-hardened, true as the day they were bent. And the keel was honest timber, scarred but straight, and when she sighted down it from stem to stern she saw no hog, no twist, nothing the river had managed to warp.

A boat is not her planks. Any fool can buy planks. A boat is her bones, and these bones were good.

Silas arrived at noon with his sleeves rolled and a paper sack of meat pies and a look on his face she was still learning to read — that investigator's face, calibrated for concealment, that kept surprising both of them by turning transparent whenever he looked at her.

"You bought a wreck," he said.

"I bought a hull."

"You bought a wreck," he repeated, setting the pies down on an upturned dory, "and you're going to rebuild it with your own hands, and I'm going to stand there watching you do it, feeling useless, until you put a caulking iron in my hand and tell me what to do."

"That's the general shape of it."

He smiled. He'd been smiling more lately, Silas Crane, former instrument of a corrupt bureau, current byline of half the front page in the delta. The warrant-burning had cost him his career and he'd never once mentioned missing it. Sometimes at night, in the rented room over the chandlery where they both now slept — in separate beds, mostly, though the wall between the rooms had a habit of coming open — she caught him writing by lamplight, pages and pages, and once she'd asked him what, and he'd said, *the whole thing, start to finish, everything we know, names and dates and soundings,* and she'd said, *won't that make more enemies,* and he'd said, *yes,* and turned the page.

That was the thing about Silas. He'd walked away from the law because the law was rotten, but he hadn't walked away from the work. He'd just changed employers. The truth was a hard mistress too, but she paid better than Corven ever had — paid in the only coin Silas had ever wanted, which was knowing he hadn't helped bury anything.

"Show me the caulking," he said, and rolled his sleeves higher.

* * *

It took six weeks. Six weeks of low tide labor and high tide floating the hull to shift her seams, of steam-boxing new oak for the strakes, of Silas learning — badly, then adequately, then, one miraculous afternoon, well — to drive oakum into a seam without cutting his thumb off or driving the cotton clean through into the bilge. His hands blistered. He complained about it in the flat, precise way he complained about everything, cataloguing each wound like evidence, and Beatrix discovered that listening to him complain was one of her chief pleasures in life, which told her something she wasn't ready to say out loud yet.

The town watched. Tarrow's Landing was a small place and a nosy one, and a woman rebuilding a dead boat on the public shingle with a disgraced investigator for a shipwright's mate was better than the regatta. Old Hesk came down most mornings with coffee and unsolicited opinions. The chandlery woman, Mrs. Okafor, extended credit against the reward money with a raised eyebrow and the remark that *Yarrow women always did come back from the water*, which Beatrix chose to take kindly even though it referred to a drowning two generations back.

Children gathered on the seawall to watch Silas work, because Silas worked like a man performing surgery on a patient he respected, and children find that funny. One of them asked him if he'd been a carpenter before he was a policeman, and he said, gravely, "No. Before this I arrested people. This is better," and the children laughed, and Beatrix, planing a strake with her back to them, laughed too, quietly, where they couldn't see.

There were bad days. The day the replacement transom knee split in the clamp and she stood there holding two halves of expensive oak and wanted to put the crowbar through the nearest intact plank. The day the rain came sideways for fourteen hours and washed their covering tarp into the channel and they chased it down the shore in the dory — *the dory*, the one salvaged from the Magpie, the one piece of her old boat that still floated, patched with canvas and prayer — and hauled the tarp out dripping and Silas said, gently, "We could stop, you know. Buy a steel hull. Something modern. Something that doesn't hate you."

"She doesn't hate me."

"I know. I said *doesn't*. It was a compliment."

But the worst day was quiet, no rain, no disasters, just an afternoon in week four when she went below to fit a floor timber and found herself kneeling in the bilge of a strange boat with oak dust in her hair, and her hands stopped moving, and she stayed there a long time, kneeling in the dark of somebody else's keel, grieving.

Because that was the thing nobody warned you about losing a boat. It wasn't like losing a house. A house holds your things. A boat held your *self* — every version of you that had ever steered her, every storm you'd survived together, every mile of water you'd read and misread. The Magpie hadn't been property. She'd been twenty-two years of Beatrix Yarrow, made visible, made seaworthy, and the river had taken all of it down in ninety seconds of green water, and there was no salvage for that, none, and some mornings the knowledge of it sat on her chest like ballast.

Silas found her there. He didn't say anything clever. He climbed down into the bilge beside her, which was not designed for two people, and sat with his shoulder against hers among the sawdust and the tar smell, and waited.

"He used to bring me down here," she said finally. "My father. When I was small. Said a boat tells you the truth in her bilge, same as people. Everything leaks somewhere. The trick is knowing where."

"And does this one?"

"Not yet. Give me a month."

He put his arm around her shoulders, awkwardly, in the way of a man who had spent his life handling documents and was still learning to handle people. "Then we'll find where she leaks," he said. "Together. That's rather become our method."

She let out a breath she felt like she'd been holding for three weeks. "You're getting sentimental, Crane."

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

* * *

They launched her on a gray morning in late ebb, with no ceremony, because Beatrix had seen enough ceremonies to last a lifetime and besides, the river didn't care about ceremonies. Mrs. Okafor came down, and Hesk, and perhaps thirty others, and a child broke a bottle of cheap wine against the stem because the child had seen it done, and the name — painted fresh that week in white on black, in Beatrix's own hand, in her father's style — caught the light for the first time.

*MAGPIE.*

Not Magpie the Second. Not Magpie Reborn. Just *Magpie*, because Beatrix Yarrow was not a sentimental woman, and because names are not property, they're inheritances, and this one was hers to pass on.

She slid off the ways clean, took the tide without a complaint, and lay against the mooring lines like she'd been built there. Silas stood on the shingle beside her, paint-flecked, blistered, grinning like a man at his own wedding.

"Well?" he said.

Beatrix ran her palm along the rail — new wood, raw wood, wood that smelled of the steam box and the shop and not yet of the river — and felt the strangeness of it, and the rightness underneath the strangeness, the way you feel a stranger's face becoming familiar.

"The river forgives nothing," she said. "But it moves on."

"So do we?"

"So do we."

They took her out that afternoon on the ebb, just the two of them, no cargo, no charter, no purpose except to learn her voice. The new engine — a secondhand Kelvin, of course; Beatrix had standards — caught on the second compression stroke, and Beatrix laughed out loud at that, alone at the wheel, a short startled laugh like something breaking loose. The Magpie ran light and stiff, green seams weeping a little at the turns the way new-boats do, and every mile Beatrix learned her: where she fell off in a quartering sea, how much helm she wanted running down-swell, the exact note her planks hummed at eight knots through the shallows off Gull Point — different from the old boat's note, higher, younger, hers own self and nobody's ghost.

Silas stood beside her at the wheel and didn't touch it. He understood, without being told, that the wheel was hers, wholly, the way the pen was his. He just watched the water and pointed out birds and once, quietly, when they passed the mouth of the Kestrel far off to starboard, gray-green in the distance behind its bar, he looked at it for a long moment and then looked away, and neither of them said anything, because some rivers you carry with you instead of sailing up them again.

At dusk they anchored in the lee of the point, and Beatrix cooked on the new stove, and the tide turned beneath them with that long deep breath the river takes at slack water, and she felt it come up through the keel into the soles of her feet, and it felt like being spoken to in her first language by a stranger with a familiar accent.

"We'll need work," Silas said, over the plates. "Money doesn't hold still."

"No. It doesn't." She looked at the darkening water, at the far lights of the delta towns coming on one by one downstream, at the black line of the channel she could read even now, even blind, even in her sleep. "There's freight to be had. There's always freight. And there's—" She stopped. Frowned slightly, the way she did when the river showed her something early.

"What?"

"There's a charter inquiry came through the chandlery yesterday. I haven't answered it yet." She turned the paper over in her hands. "Fellow wants a boat and a pilot who knows the uncharted channels above the gorge. Says he's a mapmaker. Says the government surveys are all compromised now — which is true — and he wants independent soundings done, proper ones, published openly. Paid half in advance."

Silas raised his eyebrows. "Mapmaker. Or smuggler?"

"Could be either. Could be both. Most interesting people are." She set the paper down. "We'd be going back up past the gorge. Past everything."

"Past where we nearly died."

"Past where we *did* die, a little." She met his eyes. "I'm not afraid of it. Are you?"

Silas considered the question with the seriousness he brought to all questions, which was one of the things she loved about him, and then he said, "No. But I'd like to bring the charts we made. And I'd like it noted for the record that if this mapmaker turns out to be another Corven, I get to write the exposé this time and you get to run the boat aground on him."

"It's a deal."

Outside, the tide ran fair, and the first stars came out over the water, and the new Magpie pulled at her moorings the way young things pull at reins — not frightened, not restless exactly, just *ready*, the way a river is ready, all of it, every mile of it, moving on.

Beatrix finished her tea and looked downriver, where the channels braided out into the dark toward the open delta, and felt the grief in her chest shift its weight at last — not gone, never gone, the river forgives nothing — but moving, like ballast shifting to meet a following sea, settling her deeper, trimming her true.

Tomorrow she'd answer the letter.

Tonight, the tide was theirs.
