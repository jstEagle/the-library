## 22. Free Water

The charter letter came on a Tuesday, folded inside a oilskin packet that smelled of tar and distant places, and Beatrix read it three times at the galley table before she let herself believe it.

*The Hydrographic Society of the Upper Delta*, the letterhead said, though Silas told her later it was really six retired river pilots, a cartographer with bad lungs, and a widow whose husband had drowned running a channel no chart had ever named. They had money — not much, but honest money — and a simple, impossible request: *map the uncharted braids between the Kestrel's mouth and the sea. Channels no government has bothered with. Channels no company wants found. Go where the water goes, and tell us what you learn.*

No patron. No warrant. No debts.

"Six months of provisions," Silas said, doing sums on the back of an old tide table with a carpenter's pencil. "Charts, ink, the good camera. If we're frugal — and we've had practice at frugal — it carries us through the dry season and into the next."

"And if we're not frugal?"

He looked up at her over the rim of his spectacles, and there was something in his face she was still learning to read, something that had nothing to do with warrants or folios or the careful, guarded man who had first walked her dock with government paper in his fist. "Then we'll be not-frugal somewhere beautiful," he said. "I've decided that's an acceptable risk."

Beatrix laughed — a real laugh, from low in her chest, the kind the river had knocked out of her years ago and was only now, slowly, giving back.

Outside, the morning fog was burning off the water in long silver banners, and the new Magpie sat at her moorings with her fresh-caulked seams tight and her paint still smelling of the yard, and Beatrix Yarrow, who had once believed she would never love another hull the way she'd loved the first, felt the old familiar ache of affection and let it be.

* * *

They provisioned through three long days.

Silas proved better at it than either of them expected — a man who had spent his life in offices had a clerk's memory for lists and a surveyor's instinct for weight and balance, and by the second afternoon he had the hold stowed like a piece of engineering: water low and centered, grain forward of the mast, the camera and chart case in the driest locker, wrapped in waxed canvas triple over.

"You've done this before," Beatrix said, watching him lash a crate with a hitch she recognized as a boatman's hitch, properly laid, not the imitation landmen usually managed.

"My grandfather," Silas said, not looking up. "He ran timber on the northern fork, before the companies bought the banks. He taught me knots the way other grandfathers teach scripture. I'd forgotten most of them." He pulled the lashing tight and sat back on his heels. "My hands hadn't."

Beatrix crouched beside him in the hold, in the smell of fresh timber and tar and lamp oil, and put her hand over his. His fingers were ink-stained and rope-burned and steady.

"They remember," she said.

They worked the rest of the day without many words, and the words they didn't say were the good kind.

* * *

On the last evening before casting off, they walked the bank together, past the fish-drying racks and the chandlery and the little shrine to the drowned that stood at the tide line with its candles guttering in the river wind. Beatrix had never been a woman for shrines. But she stopped there now, and Silas stopped with her, and they stood a while in silence.

"Who do you light them for?" Silas asked at last. He had watched the fishermen come and go for weeks; he knew the ritual.

"The old Magpie," Beatrix said. "And the survey team. And a man named Odell who taught me to read water when I was nine and thought I knew everything." She was quiet a moment. "And Corven, I suppose. Not that he deserves a candle. But hate is a kind of ballast, and I'm done carrying his."

The river slid past, brown and patient, carrying silt from mountains neither of them would ever see toward a sea neither of them could imagine. Somewhere out in the braids a heron lifted off a sandbar, unhurried, ancient.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Silas said. "The folio. All those names, all those arrests — half the provincial government in chains, Corven's empire seized, the newspapers full of it for a month. And the river didn't change at all. Same water, same silt, same herons. It just kept going." He shook his head slowly. "I spent ten years believing institutions were the permanent thing and rivers were the scenery. I had it backwards."

"Most people do," Beatrix said. "That's why they drown."

She bought two candles from the tin box at the shrine's base — the box was never locked; the drowned kept their own accounts — and lit them both, and they watched the flames steady in the wind-shadow of the little stone wall, and then they walked back to the boat through the blue evening with their shoulders touching.

* * *

They cast off at first light on the turning tide.

It was the kind of morning the river saved for its favorites: fog lying in the cutbanks like spilled milk, the water running glass-smooth in the eddies and seamed with current-lines further out, the air cool enough to see your breath and smelling of mud and green things and distance. Beatrix took the wheel herself. Silas cast off the mooring lines and coiled them without being told, and the new Magpie slid out into the channel the way a word slides off a tongue — clean, inevitable, hers.

There were no horns. No officials on the dock. No one to see them off but a fisherman mending his nets, who touched his cap, and a dog who trotted the length of the pier barking once at the wake, and then lost interest.

"Course?" Silas asked, standing beside her at the wheel, chart case under his arm.

"West channel as far as the split at Gull Island," Beatrix said. "Then we leave the chart behind. Everything past the split is ours to find."

He unrolled the Society's chart on the chart table — the great blank stretch of the delta braids, marked with the cartographer's honest notation: *unsurveyed. Local knowledge varies. Good luck.* — and pinned the corners down, and for a long moment they both just looked at it. All that white space. All that water, moving through it at this very moment, cutting new channels and abandoning old ones, doing whatever it pleased, answerable to no ministry and no magnate and no map.

"Ten years ago this would have frightened me," Silas said. "All that unknown."

"And now?"

He considered. "Now it looks like the only honest thing left."

Beatrix put the wheel over, gentle as a hand on a shoulder, and the new Magpie leaned into the turn and took the west channel, and the fog closed behind them like a door.

* * *

The days found their rhythm the way days do on a working boat, which is to say they stopped being days at all and became a single long act of attention.

Mornings: soundings. Silas in the bow with the lead line, calling depths in that clear surveyor's voice — *and a half, and a quarter, by the mark, twain* — while Beatrix worked the wheel through water that changed its mind every hundred yards. Noon: the sun shots, the brass instruments, the slow patient mathematics of fixing a position on a river that refused to hold one. Afternoons: the sketching and the notes, Silas's fine drafting hand laying down channel after channel on the blank chart, Beatrix dictating the character of the water — *gravel bar, shifting, avoid in ebb; snag, half-submerged, marked with a willow branch; deep hole, good anchorage, willow shade, fireflies at dusk* — everything the government charts had never thought worth recording, because it was worth nothing to anyone who wanted to own the river rather than know it.

Evenings were the best. They anchored in backwaters where the current went soft and the birds came down to drink, and they cooked on the little stove, and they compared notes by lamplight, and sometimes they talked and sometimes they didn't, and the not-talking was as easy as the talking, which Beatrix had learned was the rarest thing a person could find on any water anywhere.

On the fourth night, anchored in a channel so new it had no banks yet, just a braided ribbon of fresh water wandering through a drowned willow flat, Silas came up from below with the finished sheet in his hands — the first of them, ink still sharp, every sounding his, every line drawn from nothing.

"Look at it," he said, and his voice was not quite steady. "Nobody has ever seen this. Nobody has ever *known* this. And now it's known."

Beatrix took the sheet from him and held it to the lamp. There was his careful lettering: *Channel of the Fourth Braid, surveyed October —, by S. Crane and B. Yarrow.* Both names. He had put both names.

"Well," she said, when she could speak evenly. "Now we're permanent."

"Rivers aren't permanent."

"No," she agreed. "But knowing them is. That's the trick of it. The water moves on and takes everything with it, except what we wrote down. That stays." She handed the sheet back. "That's the only immortality I ever wanted."

Silas looked at her across the lamplit chart table, and the lamp flame stood perfectly still in the windless backwater, and neither of them moved for a long time, because there was no need to. They had already crossed every line there was to cross, back in the gorge country, in the storm, in the long leaking dory with the bullets in the fog. Everything since had been not a falling in love but a settling of it, the way silt settles in a slack-water bend, layer on layer, building land.

"Come here," he said, and she did.

* * *

The braids took them west and south and west again, and the delta opened around them like a hand unclenching.

They found channels the charts swore were land, and land the charts swore was channel. They found an abandoned ferry landing with a bell still hanging in its frame, and Silas rang it once, just to hear it, and the sound went out flat across the water and came back from nowhere. They found a family of otters who adopted the Magpie for three days and stole a biscuit. They found, on the ninth day, a channel so clear and deep and perfectly sheltered that Beatrix anchored in it just to stand on the foredeck and look at it, and she said, "This one gets a name," and Silas said, "Name it after the old boat," and she said, "I was going to," and they stood there grinning at each other like children, because they had earned the right to be children, out here where no one was keeping score.

At night the stars came out the way they only come out on open water, in their thousands, doubled in the river's calm so that the boat seemed to hang suspended in a sphere of light, turning slowly, going nowhere and everywhere.

"Do you ever miss it?" Beatrix asked him once, late, both of them in the cockpit with a blanket and the last of the tea. "The work. The institutions. Being *somebody* in the capital, with a desk and a title and people who had to listen to you."

Silas was quiet long enough that she thought he might have fallen asleep. Then: "I spent my whole life thinking the truth was a thing you delivered. A report, a folio, a case file — you carried it up the chain and handed it to the people whose job it was to act on it, and that was the whole of the work." He turned his cup slowly in his hands. "Out here I've learned it's a thing you *live in*. You don't deliver the truth. You keep it. Sounding by sounding. Day by day." He looked at her. "I don't miss the desk. I miss nothing. That's the strange part. I keep waiting to miss something, and it doesn't come."

"That's not strange," Beatrix said. "That's just what free water feels like, the first season. You keep bracing for the debt to come due, and it never does, and eventually you stop bracing." She smiled into the dark. "Takes about a year. I'll tell you when you're there."

* * *

They were six weeks out, deep in the western braids where the charts gave up entirely and even the Society's letters had run out of advice, when they found the channel with no bottom.

It was late afternoon, the light going long and gold, and Beatrix was at the wheel running a narrow braid between two reed islands when the water changed color under the bow — deepened from tea-brown to a black so pure it looked solid, a lane of dark water perhaps thirty yards wide running straight as a canal into the heart of the delta.

Silas swung the lead. It ran out the whole line and kept going. He hauled it in, wet to the very top of the rope, and looked at Beatrix.

"Old channel," she said slowly. "A main channel, once. The river abandoned it and the silt sealed the mouth behind it, and now it just sits there. Deep and dead and hidden." She brought the boat down to a crawl, staring at the dark water with a pilot's hunger. "There could be anything in there. Old wrecks. Sunken cargo. Channels off this channel that nobody's seen in fifty years."

"Or it could be thirty miles of nothing."

"Or," Beatrix said, and put the wheel over, and the new Magpie slid off the bright water into the dark lane like a coin into a well, "it could be the best find of the whole survey."

The reed banks closed behind them. The light went green and dim and wonderful. Somewhere ahead, in the drowned heart of the old channel, something waited — a wreck, a story, a secret the river had kept longer than either of them had been alive — and Beatrix Yarrow stood at the wheel with the man she loved at the lead line and no one in the world to answer to, and drove her boat into the unknown at a careful, joyful five knots, and the water, which forgives nothing, forgave them this: the going on.

The chart lay open on the table behind them, blank and waiting.

They had ink. They had time. They had each other, and the whole moving world of the river, and not one debt between them and the sea.
