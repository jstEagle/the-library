## 1. The Water Gives Back

The river had been eating the trestle for three days, and by the fourth it had swallowed the middle span whole.

Dominic Sandoval stood on the north abutment with a clipboard he wasn't writing on and watched the Kestrel Run do its work. The water was the color of strong tea, thick with snowmelt and torn-up bank, and it moved the way water moves when it has somewhere important to be — fast, low, and without any interest in what stood in its path. The trestle's pilings, black with creosote and forty years of standing in the current, leaned downstream like tired men. One of them had already gone. He'd heard it in the night from his room at the waystation, a groan and then a crack, and this morning the gap was there, a missing tooth in a line of timber that had once carried ore trains twice a day.

He made a few marks on the clipboard anyway. Numbers, mostly. The kind of report that said *inspected, condemned, no further action recommended* without ever quite saying it. It was good work if you could get it — the rail companies paid a flat fee for a man to walk a dead line and tell them what they already knew, and they never asked a man like Dominic where he'd learned to read a bridge, or whose signature was on the license he no longer carried.

The flood was already going down. That was the thing about spring floods; they came up like a mob and left like a debt collector, taking what they wanted and dropping what they didn't. The waterline on the pilings had fallen a foot since dawn. By afternoon it would fall another. And the riverbed, when the Kestrel gave it back, would not be the riverbed that had been there before.

He knew that. He'd seen it before, on other lines, in other years. Floods were honest that way. They dug up whatever a valley had tried to bury.

* * *

By noon he was walking the south approach, boots sinking in mud that sucked at every step, when he saw it.

At first he took it for a root ball — a knot of black wood the receding water had stranded in the open channel, forty yards downstream of the trestle, wedged against a shelf of gravel the flood had newly cut. Then the light moved off a cloud and came back, and the knot of wood had corners.

It was a chest. Iron-strapped, oak-ribbed, maybe two feet long, half sunk in the gravel with its lid tilted at an angle, like a man sleeping with his head off the edge of a bed. The straps were rusted to the color of dried blood. Something was stamped into the lid, a round seal the size of a dinner plate, but the mud had filled the stamping and he couldn't read it from where he stood.

Dominic stood in the mud for a long time and did not go to it.

He had a rule about things the river gave back. The rule was: leave them. Rivers didn't return things out of kindness. They returned them because they were done holding them, and what a river was done holding was usually something somebody else had been done with too — done with, done over, done burying. In thirty years of walking closed lines he had found culverts full of dynamite, a payroll safe with its door swung open and empty, and once, in a canyon on the old Meridian Extension, a trunk of personal letters that he had read exactly one page of before closing it and reporting the location to nobody at all.

But the chest was downstream of the trestle. Downstream meant it had come from upstream. Upstream was the pass, and the pass was the Veraghen Range, and the Veraghen Range was the one place on the map he had spent thirty years not thinking about.

He went to it anyway. He could have told you, later, that it was professional curiosity, and he would even have believed himself.

The gravel gave under his boots as he waded the shallows to the shelf. Up close the chest was worse than it had looked from a distance — the oak gone soft as bread at the waterline, the iron straps flaking orange at a touch. The seal in the lid was a stamping of crossed tools under a mountain peak, ringed with lettering in a script he had not seen since he was a boy copying it out of his father's engineering manuals.

*Veraghener Staatsbahn.* The State Railway of Veraghen.

A country that had been two other countries and then none for thirty years now.

Dominic crouched in the mud and put his hand flat on the lid, and for a moment he simply breathed. The river ran past his knees, cold through the canvas of his trousers, and the trestle groaned upstream, and he thought: *walk away.* He thought it the way he thought it about every closed line, every sealed tunnel, every document with an official seal on it. *Walk away, Sandoval. You're good at it. It's the one thing you've never failed at.*

He worked the lid open with his knife.

The hasps were rusted through and gave without a fight, which somehow made it worse. Things that had been buried well fought you. This had been buried badly, or buried in a hurry, or buried by men who had believed someone would come back for it soon.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had gone to the consistency of old skin, were plates. Zinc survey plates, a dozen of them, each one etched with the fine ruled linework of a route survey — profiles, alignments, grade calculations, cross-sections of a mountain. He lifted the top plate into the light and the water ran off it in sheets, and the etched lines caught the sun and threw it back in thin silver threads, and Dominic Sandoval, who had not been afraid of anything in eleven years, felt his hands begin to shake.

He knew this line. Not from the plates — from before the plates. The alignment climbed the north wall of the Veraghen pass, and there was only one alignment that had ever been drawn up that wall, because there was only one route that had ever been *proposed* up that wall, and the proposal had died with the men who made it. Every engineer in three countries knew the story, or the shape of the story: the Staatsbahn's last great project, the tunnel through the pass, abandoned when the nation itself came apart. Abandoned, the histories said, for want of funds.

The histories were written by men who had never seen the plates.

He turned to the second plate, and the third. The linework was immaculate. The hand that had ruled it was a hand he knew — not from Veraghen, not from the histories, but from a set of bridge calculations his own instructor had made the class copy out, forty years ago, at a school that no longer existed in a country that had since changed its name. The small, severe numerals. The way the 4s were open at the top. The little hooked serifs on the grade percentages, like the tail of a fish.

He had spent two years of his life learning to draw like that man. Half the engineers of his generation had. Aurelio Vassek's plates were the standard the schools taught to, and Aurelio Vassek had gone into the Veraghen pass in the spring of the last year of the war with a survey crew of eleven men, and none of them had come out, and the route had been sealed, and the country had dissolved, and the maps had burned, and that was supposed to be the end of it.

The end of it was lying in a riverbed forty yards from a trestle, wrapped in oilcloth, in Dominic's hands.

He turned the last plate over. The reverse side carried a handwritten note in surveyor's grease pencil, the letters pressed hard enough to bite the zinc:

*Fault is live. Do not build. — A.V.*

Below it, a date. Eleven days before the survey crew vanished.

Dominic sat back on his heels in the freezing water and looked upstream, toward the pass. The peaks were still white with winter. Somewhere up there, behind the rock and the snow and thirty years of official forgetting, was a tunnel that eleven men had died proving should never be opened — and a fault line that had not stopped being live just because the country that mapped it had stopped existing.

And now there was a flood, and a washed-out trestle, and a chest that had been buried badly or in a hurry, and a river that had decided, after thirty years, to give it all back.

He should burn them. He knew that with the same certainty he knew the load limits of a timber span. Plates could be copied; plates could be read by anyone with a surveyor's eye; plates in the world meant questions, and questions meant men asking them, and men asking questions was the one weather Dominic Sandoval had spent eleven years learning to stand clear of.

He wrapped them back in the oilcloth instead.

It took him a while to get the chest closed. The lid had warped. He laced the hasps down with a length of baling wire from his pack, and then he stood there holding the whole rotten thing under one arm like a dead animal, mud to his thighs, the river pulling at his legs, and understood that he had just made the second-worst decision of his life.

The first had been eleven years ago. He tried not to keep score, but the truth was he kept score of everything.

The chest went into the crawl space under the waystation, behind a loose foundation stone, wrapped in a grain sack. He told himself it was temporary. He told himself he'd decide in the morning. He told himself a great many things that night, lying awake under a roof he'd be gone from by week's end, listening to the Kestrel Run drop and drop, uncovering more of a riverbed that had been keeping secrets.

In the morning there was a telegram waiting at the waystation office, and the clerk read it out loud because Dominic was holding a cup of coffee and had his hands full.

It was addressed to *the inspecting engineer, Kestrel Trestle,* which was not his name but was close enough for a telegraph clerk. It read:

*UNDERSTAND SPRING FLOOD HAS EXPOSED MATERIALS AT KESTREL WASHOUT SITE. PREPARED TO PAY GENEROUSLY FOR ANY HISTORICAL DOCUMENTS RECOVERED. INQUIRE ALDOUS VANE, VANE CONSOLIDATED. NO QUESTIONS ASKED.*

Dominic set down his coffee very carefully, the way a man sets down something he does not trust his own hands with.

He had never met Aldous Vane. But he knew the name the way a burned man knows the name of the fire — Vane Consolidated had swallowed the last railroad Dominic had worked for, and had buried it so thoroughly that the men who'd built it had scattered like startled birds, and one of them had changed his name and never stopped walking since.

*No questions asked.* Vane was a man who bought questions in order not to ask them.

Dominic looked out the office window, south along the line, toward the pass he had spent thirty years not thinking about. The peaks stood white and silent against a hard blue sky, and somewhere under all that snow was a fault that was still live, and eleven men who had never come home, and a route that a very reasonable, very wealthy man had just offered to pay a fortune to keep exactly where it was.

Dominic picked up his coffee. It had gone cold.

He was still holding it, still looking at the mountains, when the clerk cleared his throat and asked, in the easy way of a man making small talk, whether the inspecting engineer had found anything interesting down in the washout.

Dominic Sandoval smiled the smile he had practiced for eleven years, the one that opened no doors and answered nothing, and said:

"Not a thing."
