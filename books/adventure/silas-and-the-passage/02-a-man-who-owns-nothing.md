## 2. A Man Who Owns Nothing

The room above the feed store cost four bits a night and smelled of lamp oil and mouse, which suited Dominic Sandoval fine. Rooms like that didn't invite questions. Nobody asked a man in a room like that where he'd been or where he was going, and if the floorboards talked when he walked on them, they at least had the decency to keep his secrets to themselves.

He set the chest on the table and did not open it for a long while.

That was the discipline, and it was the only discipline he had left: the waiting. He'd learned it young, running lines through country that changed hands faster than the maps could be printed — you never touched a thing the moment you found it. You sat with it. You let it tell you what it wanted from you, because everything wanted something. A document wanted to be read. A secret wanted to be sold. A man who found both in one night wanted very badly to believe he was only curious.

Outside, the river had dropped six feet since morning. He could hear it working at the trestle pilings with that patient, grinding voice, the sound of water doing arithmetic it always finished. By week's end there would be nothing left of the washout but mud and memory, and by month's end nobody in Harrow's Bend would remember why the line had closed at all.

Dominic struck a match and lit the lamp, then lit the little iron stove in the corner, because what he intended to do required fire.

The papers came out first.

They were the ordinary kind — correspondence, requisitions, a crew manifest gone soft as cloth with rot. He read them anyway, all of them, because reading was how he kept his hands honest. A letter complaining about wages. An invoice for blasting powder, forty cases, signed by a name he didn't know. The manifest listed thirty-one men. The last page of the last folder was a payroll note marked *final settlement*, dated eleven days after the survey's official abandonment, and beneath it, in a different hand, a single line:

*Balance owed to families of the nine.*

Nine men who hadn't lived long enough to collect. Dominic folded that page small and put it in his shirt pocket, against his sternum, where he kept the things he told himself were souvenirs. Then he fed the rest to the stove, sheet by sheet, watching the ink lift off the paper in brown ghosts before the flame took it. The room filled with the smell of burning money and burning names, and he stood over the stove like a man warming himself at a fire he'd have to answer for.

The plates went into the fire too. That was the plan. That had been the plan since the riverbank, since he'd knelt in the wet gravel with the chest yawning open at his knees and felt the cold of it come up through his gloves.

He got three of them in before he stopped.

They were glass, those plates — photographic negatives, each one wrapped in waxed paper gone brittle as autumn leaf. The heat made them sing, a thin high note, and then the emulsion began to blister, and for half a second Dominic saw what was burning: contour lines. Elevations. A hand-drawn cross-section of the pass, rendered in the fine confident strokes of a surveyor who trusted his instruments more than God.

And above the title block, stamped in violet ink that fire hadn't reached yet, the seal. The double-headed eagle. The old mark of Vellmark, a nation that had been erased from every map and treaty table thirty years ago, back when Dominic was still learning to read a transit.

He pulled them out with his bare hands. It wasn't bravery. He simply moved before the part of him that understood consequences could vote, and afterward he stood at the basin running cold water over burned fingers, three glass plates dripping on the boards, and heard himself say aloud, to no one:

"Curiosity."

The word sounded exactly like what it was.

* * *

There were eleven plates in all. Eight survived. He laid them out on the bed in the lamplight and told himself he was cataloguing, that a professional engineer documented everything, that documentation was not the same as keeping.

It was slow work, reading negatives. He held each plate up to the flame-light and read the world backward — black sky, white stone, the rail alignment drawn as a pale thread climbing toward the pass called Kestrel's Notch on the new maps and something older, unpronounceable, on these. The dead nation's engineers had been good. Better than good. Their elevations matched his own mental figures for the range within inches, and their alignment ran true and gentle and *cheap*, following grades any railroad would weep for, until mile sixty-one.

At mile sixty-one the thread stopped being a line and became an arrow, straight through the shoulder of the mountain. Fourteen miles of tunnel. And beside it, in the margin, in that handwriting — the handwriting he knew, the handwriting he'd have known blind, drunk, at the bottom of a river — a note so small he had to hold the plate a hand's width from his eye:

*Fault zone active. Do not build. I will prove it or die proving it.*

Dominic set the plate down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

He knew that hand. He had learned his own drafting style imitating it, ten years ago, in a drafting office in a city that no longer appeared on maps either, under a chief surveyor named Aurel Vasch who drank black tea and believed that a lie on a drawing was a murder committed in advance. Vasch had taught him that. Vasch had signed the payroll note in the folder. *Balance owed to families of the nine.* And now Vasch's handwriting had come up out of a river thirty years and four hundred miles from anywhere he should have been, addressed, it seemed, directly to Dominic Sandoval, who had changed his name twice and his trade never.

"Do not build," Dominic said to the empty room. "Well. Someone's about to."

Because that was the thing he couldn't burn, couldn't fold away, couldn't drink enough to blur: he knew who was buying the pass. Every man on the riverbank knew. Aldous Vane had been buying right-of-way along the Kestrel corridor for two years, quiet as silt, and everyone knew what quiet money meant when it was Vane's. It meant the tunnel. It meant fourteen miles through the shoulder of the mountain, driven fast and cheap, through whatever the rock had to say about it.

Through the fault.

Dominic sat down on the edge of the bed, among the plates, and put his head in his hands, and did the accounting he always did, the ledger he kept behind his eyes. On one side: eight pieces of glass, worthless to anyone who couldn't read them, dangerous to anyone who could. On the other side: a fortune, offered soon enough, for "historical documents." And underneath both columns, in the space where honest men wrote their names, a blank.

He had sold things he shouldn't have kept before. That was the truth of his life, the whole architecture of it. At twenty-six he'd sold a route map to a rival line and watched two hundred men strike camp. At twenty-nine he'd sold testimony — just silence, really, silence dressed as testimony — after the Marrow Creek bridge went down, and taken a payment that bought him a new name and a permanent flinch. He told himself each time it would be the last sale, and each time the next thing came up out of the ground or the water or another man's desperation, gleaming, priced, and Dominic Sandoval discovered again that he was a man who owned nothing except the ability to walk away, and that walking away had a market value too.

He slept badly, the plates wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboard by the stove, and dreamed of white threads through black mountains, and woke before dawn with his burned fingers throbbing in time with the river.

* * *

The telegram came at noon, delivered by a boy who didn't knock so much as materialize, held out the yellow slip, and vanished without waiting for a reply — which told Dominic the sender had paid for delivery and discretion together, and there was only one kind of sender who did that.

He read it standing in the doorway, letting the cold air move across it as though the words might change if he chilled them enough.

TO THE ENGINEER RETAINED FOR TRESTLE INSPECTION HARROWS BEND STOP ALDOUS VANE INTERESTED IN HISTORICAL DOCUMENTS OF ANY NATURE RECOVERED FROM WASHOUT SITE STOP GENEROUS COMPENSATION STOP INQUIRE MERIDIAN HOTEL SUITE FOUR STOP NO DOCUMENT TOO SMALL STOP VANE

*No document too small.* Dominic almost laughed. There it was — the whole man in nine words. Aldous Vane didn't threaten, didn't beg, didn't even presume. He simply announced that everything in the world had a price and that his checkbook was the scale. Dominic had met him once, seven years ago, across a conference table in a room where his last employer — old Halloran, decent, doomed Halloran — had slowly understood, sentence by sentence, that his railroad was already sold out from under him. Vane had been courteous throughout. He'd shaken Halloran's hand at the end. He'd shaken Dominic's too, and looked at him a moment too long, the way a buyer looks at stock.

Dominic read the telegram four times. Then he folded it along its creases, precisely, and put it in his shirt pocket with Vasch's payroll note, and stood in the doorway a long time watching the river run.

Here was the shape of it, plain as a grade profile. Take the plates to Suite Four, collect whatever "generous" meant — five thousand, ten, enough to vanish for a decade, enough to buy a coast somewhere and a new name and a boat he'd never sail — and the fault stayed a rumor. The tunnel went in. Somewhere around mile sixty-one, some spring, some shift, some ordinary Tuesday, the mountain would shrug, and fourteen miles of steel and however many souls rode it would come down into the dark, and no one would ever know it had been foretold in violet ink by a dead man who died proving it.

Or keep the plates. Keep them, and keep them hidden, and stay hidden with them — invisible, nameless, safe — and the tunnel went in anyway, because eight pieces of glass in a floorboard saved no one. Keeping wasn't enough. The plates would have to travel. They would have to reach someone with the power to stop Vane, and getting them there meant becoming visible, means carrying a name through country where Vane's money had already bought the eyes.

There was a third option, the old option: burn them tonight, finish what he'd started, and let the future arrive unwitnessed like weather.

Dominic went inside. He built up the stove fire. He took the oilcloth bundle from under the floorboard and held it over the open door of the stove, and his hands — steady, always steady, the steadiest part of him — did not move.

"Curiosity," he said again, but the word had worn through, and what showed underneath was older and less flattering. It was the face of Aurel Vasch saying *a lie on a drawing is a murder committed in advance*, and it was the faces of the nine unpaid men, and it was, worst of all, the simple unbearable fact that Dominic Sandoval had spent fifteen years surviving by owning nothing, and a man who owns nothing can be bought with anything.

He shut the stove door on the empty fire and rewrapped the plates.

In the morning, he told himself, he would decide. In the morning he would take the low-paying repair job posted on the yard board — Mile Forty-Seven, trestle and drainage, wages barely worth the ink — because a man hiding in plain sight needed work the way a lantern needed oil, and because the washout site was at Mile Forty-Seven, and because whatever he decided, he wanted to make the decision standing on the ground where the river had given it up.

He pinned the telegram to the wall by the lamp, where he could see it.

Suite Four. No document too small.

Downriver, faint under the wind, he could hear the pile driver starting up at Forty-Seven — a slow iron heartbeat, someone else's crew going to work on someone else's line. Dominic turned down the lamp and lay down on top of the blanket with his boots on, the way men sleep when they mean to leave quickly, and listened to that heartbeat all the way down into a dreamless dark.
