## 3. The Crew at Mile Forty-Seven

The job posting was nailed to the depot board in Corben's Ferry, half-hidden behind a notice about cholera precautions, and it was perfect: *Bridge crew wanted, Mile Forty-Seven, washout repairs. Low pay. Hard work. No questions asked.*

No questions asked. Dominic had built a life out of those three words. He read the notice twice, memorized the foreman's name — Ilsa Brandt — and burned nothing this time except the match he struck against the board to light his pipe.

Mile Forty-Seven sat where the river bent mean and shallow over a shelf of gray shale, a quarter mile of trestle that the spring flood had chewed like a dog with a bone. Two spans were gone entirely. A third sagged in the middle like a tired man leaning on a fence. The repair camp was four tents, a cook shack on stilts, and a stack of creosoted pilings that smelled like tar and money someone else had already spent.

Ilsa Brandt met him at the railhead with a clipboard she didn't consult and eyes that did all the measuring she needed. She was broad through the shoulders, forty or fifty, hair cut short for practicality rather than fashion, and she looked at Dominic the way he imagined a customs officer looks at a suitcase.

"Sandoval," she said, reading his paper. "Structural engineer."

"Ten years on lines in the eastern ranges."

"Eastern ranges." She handed the paper back. "We don't get many of those applying for two dollars a day."

"Money isn't everything."

"No," she agreed. "But it's usually something." She turned and walked toward the bridge, and he understood he was expected to follow. "You'll be working under my second. He's a driller named Tomas who believes the river has opinions, and an apprentice named Wren who is either sixteen or lying about it. You'll eat when the bell rings and you'll sleep where there's room. Any objections?"

"None."

"Good. Because I only ask questions once, Mr. Sandoval, and I didn't ask any just now. Don't make me sorry about that."

* * *

Tomas was a barrel of a man with a beard that started somewhere around his collarbones and a crucifix of railroad spikes hanging from his neck. He greeted Dominic by spitting into the river, watching the current take it, and nodding as if the river had approved the introduction.

"She's high-tempered this year," Tomas said, by way of hello. "You feel how the ground hums? That's her talking. Old water remembers old things."

"I've heard that," Dominic said, and felt the plates in his pack like a hand pressed flat between his shoulder blades.

Wren he met at dusk, crouched on the broken span with a lantern, sketching the damage in a battered notebook. The kid was thin as a rail fish, all elbows and watchfulness, with ink-stained fingers and a way of going still when approached — the stillness of something that had learned being noticed was dangerous.

"You're the new engineer," Wren said, not looking up.

"Apprentice engineer, apparently."

"Wren." A pause. "Just Wren."

"Just Wren," Dominic agreed, and asked no questions, and watched some of the stillness go out of the kid's shoulders like air from a valve.

That night he ate nothing. It wasn't deliberate; it was habit worn so smooth it barely registered. There was a system to it — eat heavily every third day, lightly otherwise, keep the body cheap to run, keep the mind sharp, never owe anyone the courtesy of sharing a table long enough for them to learn your face by firelight. He took his tin plate, filled it, carried it to the edge of the camp as if to eat alone, and tipped the beans quietly into the dark below the cook shack.

He'd done it a hundred times. In a hundred camps.

"You dropped your dinner."

Dominic turned. Ilsa Brandt stood behind him with her own plate and an expression of mild arithmetic, as though calculating what beans cost against what labor was worth.

"Wasn't hungry."

"Mm." She looked at the dark below the shack, where the beans had gone, then back at him. "Third night running, if I'm counting. I count. It's most of my job." She held out her plate. "Here. Trade me. Mine's got more gravy."

"That's not—"

"It's not charity, it's gravy redistribution." She set the plate in his hands with the finality of a woman spiking a rail. "A man who doesn't eat doesn't work. A man who doesn't work gets sent downriver. And I need every pair of hands I can get before the company inspector comes through, because the inspector is a fool and the bridge is worse. So eat, Sandoval. Consider it bridge maintenance."

She walked away before he could refuse properly, which he suspected was the entire design of it.

Dominic stood in the dark holding a stranger's plate, feeling something he hadn't felt in years and couldn't immediately name. It took him until the beans were cold to identify it. It was the sensation of having been seen — not hunted, not measured for weakness, but simply *noticed*, the way you'd notice a loose bolt. Something worth tightening before it failed.

He ate the beans. All of them.

* * *

From Ilsa Brandt's side of things, the new engineer was a locked door walking around on two legs.

She'd hired enough men to know the types. There were drifters, who worked hard and vanished soft; there were failures, who worked soft and stayed forever; and once in a great while there were men like this one, who worked like the bridge was a debt they owed personally and who flinched — actually flinched, a small thing, a hitch in the breath — whenever anyone said their name too kindly.

Sandoval knew stress loads by looking at them. She'd watched him stand under the sagging third span for ten minutes without moving, then tell her exactly which piling would go first and roughly when, and he'd been right to within a week. Men didn't learn that in school. They learned it standing on something while it came apart underneath them.

So she fed him. Not because she was soft — the crew would have told you otherwise, loudly — but because she'd buried two men in eleven years of bridgework, and both times the coroner's report had said the same thing: a man running on empty makes mistakes, and bridges do not forgive mistakes. If Sandoval was running from something, well. The river ran from nothing and it still ended up in the same sea as everything else.

Besides, Wren had taken to him, and Wren took to nobody.

* * *

The days found their shape. Dawn whistle, coffee thick enough to stand a spoon in, and then the long percussion of the work — hammers on pilings, the groan of the derrick, Tomas arguing with the river in two languages and losing gracefully in both.

Dominic discovered that hiding inside work was even better than hiding inside silence. Nobody questioned a man who volunteered for the worst jobs. He waded the shallows to sound the footings. He crawled the underside of the sagging span with a lantern strapped to his head, tapping, listening, reading the timber like correspondence from an enemy. At night he drew repair plans on butcher paper by lamplight, and Ilsa studied them with her arms crossed and said, "Where'd you really learn this, Sandoval?" and he said, "Eastern ranges," and she let it lie, and both of them pretended that was an answer.

Wren began appearing at his elbow during these sessions, ostensibly to hold the lantern. Ostensibly.

"The old line," Wren said one night, chin on crossed arms atop the drafting crate. "Before the war, when the border was different. Did trains come through here?"

"Some did."

"My grandfather drove one. Freight, over the pass. He said there was a tunnel the company sealed up and nobody would say why." The kid's eyes came up, quick and bright. "You ever heard of that?"

The lantern flame leaned in the draft. Somewhere behind his ribs, where the plates rode sewn into the lining of his pack, Dominic felt the old cold open one eye.

"Heard of it," he said carefully. "Mostly stories."

"What kind of stories?"

"The kind that keep apprentices awake past their bedtime." He rolled the butcher paper closed. "Bedtime, Wren."

"I'm nearly seventeen."

"Then you're old enough to obey a foreman's schedule." But he said it without edge, and Wren grinned — the first grin Dominic had seen from the kid, crooked and sudden as weather — and took the lantern off to bed.

* * *

It was Tomas, of all people, who broke him open a little.

They were setting a new piling in the shallows, chest-deep in water gone warm with the season, when the driller went suddenly still and held up one spiked-crucifix hand.

"Listen."

Dominic listened. Water, wind, the far complaint of the derrick. "Nothing."

"Exactly." Tomas's eyes were wide and white in his wet beard. "The river stopped talking. She does that when something old is near. My grandmother said—" He stopped himself, laughed at himself, shook the water from his beard. "Superstition. Forgive me, engineer."

But that night, sitting on the cook-shack steps with the others, Tomas told the story anyway, because the story was clearly older than his discretion. About the dead nation across the range, the one that had dissolved thirty years ago into three newer, hungrier countries. About its engineers, who built like priests — tunnels and viaducts that outlasted the country itself, standing abandoned in the passes like temples. And about the survey crews who went into the mountains one autumn and did not come out, all of them, and how the company sealed their route and burned their offices, and how the river had been chewing at that country's bones ever since, spitting pieces of it downstream.

"They say the flood this spring was the worst in thirty years," Tomas finished, turning his spike-cross over in his fingers. "Thirty years. Like she was holding her breath all that time. Like she finally decided to give something back."

Nobody laughed. The fire cracked. Wren had stopped breathing altogether.

And Dominic sat very still in the circle of warmth he had spent two weeks refusing to join, and understood that the story had found him — that it had been waiting downstream all along, patient as water, and had washed up at Mile Forty-Seven wearing his own handwriting.

"Engineer," Tomas said suddenly, kindly, watching him. "You've gone pale as a piling. Eat something."

Ilsa was already holding out a plate.

* * *

Later — much later, when the camp slept and the river had found its voice again — Dominic knelt by his cot and unsewed the oilcloth packet from the lining of his pack.

The plates caught the lamplight, dull silver stamped with the seal of a country that existed now only in stories Tomas told to frighten rivers. He turned the first one over. The surveyor's notation ran along the margin in that hand he knew better than his own, the small precise figures, the little hooked crossbar on every numeral seven.

At Mile Forty-Seven, the flood had given back more than a chest. It had given back a question, and the question was this: how long could a man stay invisible among people who kept noticing he wasn't eating?

He wrapped the plates away, sewed the lining shut with clumsy stitches, and lay down to not sleep.

Above the cook shack, pinned to the message board where the morning shift would see it first, a telegram had arrived on the evening boat. None of them had read it yet.

It was addressed to D. Sandoval, care of the Mile Forty-Seven camp, and the sender's name was Aldous Vane.
