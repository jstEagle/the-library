## 4. What the Plates Show

The lamp in Dominic's bunkhouse room had a cracked chimney that threw a seam of light across the wall, and by that seam he worked, three nights running, with the plates laid out on a blanket like a deck of cards belonging to a dead man.

He had told himself the first night was curiosity. He told himself the second night was thoroughness — a man ought to know what he'd failed to burn. By the third night he had stopped telling himself anything at all. The work had him now, the old pull of it, lines converging toward an answer the way water converges toward a low place.

The plates were glass negatives, each one six inches square, and they were beautiful in the way that only doomed things are beautiful. Whoever had made them had been meticulous. Every elevation line was ruled clean; every annotation sat in a hand so steady it might have been typeset. The seal appeared in the corner of each plate — the double-headed eagle over crossed hammers, the mark of a republic that had come apart thirty years ago in a season of riots and unpaid soldiers, its borders redrawn by men who had never walked the ground they were dividing.

Dominic knew the hand. That was the thing he could not burn.

He had known it from the first plate he'd pulled from the chest, before the floodwater had even dried off his boots. *A. Reznik, Chief Surveyor.* He had apprenticed under a man who had studied under Reznik, back when Dominic was young enough to believe that a railroad was a promise you made to a landscape. Reznik's notation system was famous in a narrow circle of engineers — the way he marked uncertainty not as error but as honesty, small circles at every point where the ground had refused to be known. *Here the mountain has opinions,* one of his instructors used to joke. Reznik drew circles where other men drew lines.

And Reznik had drawn circles all through the pass.

Dominic spread the plates in sequence and saw it emerge: a route that left the river valley two miles above the washout and climbed northeast through a saddle no living map showed, dropping into a tunnel bore that ran beneath the shoulder of the range itself. Not over the pass. Through it. A straight shot, eleven miles of tunnel, the kind of engineering that turned a four-day crossing into ninety minutes and turned whoever controlled it into the owner of every freight rate west of the mountains.

It was the route Vane would kill for. It was, quite possibly, the route Vane was already killing for.

But the circles. God, the circles.

They clustered along the tunnel alignment like a rash. Dominic pulled out his straightedge and followed the bore line across plate after plate, and the pattern resolved into something cold sitting in his stomach: the tunnel did not merely pass near a fault. It ran *along* it. Eleven miles inside a fracture in the earth's crust, a seam where the range was still quietly tearing itself apart, a movement measured in millimeters a year — until the year it wasn't.

He found the test galleries then. Smaller bores, branching off the main alignment, driven into the fault zone itself to measure what the rock would do. The notes on those plates were different. The steady hand had begun to shake — not much, but enough that Dominic could see where Reznik had gone back over his own figures twice, pressing harder the second time, as if pressure could make the numbers behave.

*Gallery 4 closed. Instrument lost to convergence. Rock flow exceeds all projection.*

*Gallery 7 abandoned. Two men injured. Recommend full reassessment of alignment.*

*Recommendation repeated. No response received.*

And then the last plate, the one wedged at the bottom of the chest beneath a layer of river silt that had sealed it like a tomb. Not a survey plate at all but a letter, photographed onto glass — someone had wanted it preserved, had wanted it to survive even if paper didn't. Dominic held it up to the lamp seam and read it three times because the first two times he refused to believe it.

*To whoever finds this: we have recommended abandonment of the bore eleven times. The ministry has answered with twelve orders to continue. The fault is active. We have proven it with instruments, with mathematics, and last Tuesday with the lives of Branko and Ilya, who are dead because a minister in the capital needed this tunnel finished before an election. If you are reading this, know that the mountain will close this bore whether we finish it or not. We ask only that our findings be carried somewhere they cannot be buried. We were engineers. We believed the ground would speak for us if we gave it words.*

There was no date. There didn't need to be. The nation was gone; the ministry was gone; the election was dust. But the fault was still there, still moving, still patient, and somewhere in the capital of the country that had inherited these mountains there was an archive where a letter like this was supposed to end up — and hadn't.

Dominic set the plate down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Outside, the wind was working at the eaves of the bunkhouse, and below it he could hear the small sounds of Mile Forty-Seven settling into sleep: Tomas snoring through the wall like a landslide in slow motion, Wren's cot creaking as the kid turned over, Ilsa's lamp in the foreman's shack burning late the way it always did. Four people. Five counting himself. A skeleton crew patching a branch line nobody important cared about, which was exactly why he'd taken the job.

Nobody important cared about Mile Forty-Seven. But somebody important cared about the chest in the riverbed, and in three days nobody had come asking — which meant they weren't asking yet.

He thought about burning the letter plate. His hands actually moved toward the stove before he stopped them. Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: if Vane got the plates, Vane got the route — the real route, the surveyed one, with eleven miles of bore already half-driven by dead men whose work was still sound everywhere except in the one place soundness mattered. And a tunnel built on Reznik's alignment would stand for five years, ten, long enough to be loaded with freight and passengers, until some ordinary Tuesday the ground spoke again and took whatever was inside it.

Silence meant the tunnel got built. Silence meant he stayed invisible, kept his pay, kept his namelessness, and let the mountain collect its debt from strangers.

Telling the crew meant —

He didn't finish the thought. Telling them meant drawing a circle around them, marking them the way Reznik marked uncertain ground: *here the mountain has opinions, and here the men who follow this line may die of them.*

Dominic blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to Tomas snore, and hated how much he already knew he would miss the sound if he ran tomorrow.

* * *

They came on the fourth day, and they came badly.

Dominic spotted them from the cut where he was setting grade stakes — a hired wagon and two riders coming up the branch line road at the pace of men being paid by the day, not the mile. The wagon wore no company markings. The riders wore coats too good for the road. He had spent nine years learning to read trouble at a distance, and this read clear as a signal flag: *Vane Consolidated, advance inquiry, polite stage.*

"Company men," Wren said, materializing at his elbow the way the kid always did, silent as spilled flour. "For us?"

"For the washout." Dominic kept driving stakes. "We're just the scenery."

Ilsa came down from the trestle wiping her hands, and Dominic watched her take their measure in one pass — the wagon, the coats, the way neither rider dismounted until the wagon stopped. She'd been a bridge foreman for twenty years; she knew what money looked like when it came to look at you.

The man who stepped down was soft-spoken and gray-suited, with the unhurried manner of someone who had never once been told no by anything smaller than a government. "Morning," he said. "Name's Corliss. We're making inquiries on behalf of parties interested in the spring flood damage. Recovered property, that sort of thing."

"There wasn't property recovered," Ilsa said. "There was a trestle that fell down and a mess we've been cleaning since."

"No documents? Papers, maps, containers of any kind? The flood scoured a good stretch of the old riverbed. Historical materials surface in floods surprisingly often." Corliss smiled like a man describing weather. "My employer is a collector."

"What employer?" Ilsa asked.

"I'm afraid the gentleman prefers his acquisitions discreet." Corliss produced a card and offered it around; nobody took it, so he set it on the wagon's seat. "There's a standing offer. Generous compensation for any items recovered from the washout, no questions regarding how they came into your possession. And — " the smile widened by a degree — "a matching sum for information regarding anyone who *has* recovered such items. Discreetly, of course."

"We'll keep it in mind," Ilsa said, in the voice she used on drunks at the company store, and Corliss tipped his hat and rolled back down the valley the way he'd come.

They stood in the road watching the dust settle. Tomas spat. Wren said, "What kind of collector pays reward money?"

"The kind who isn't collecting," Dominic said.

It was out of him before he could stop it, and Ilsa turned her head slowly, the way she did when a span shifted under load. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning collectors want things to look at." He kept his eyes on the road. "That man wants things to disappear."

The silence stretched. Wren looked at him. Ilsa looked at him. And Dominic understood, standing in the road with grade stakes in his hands, that he had just stepped to the edge of the only choice that mattered: tell them everything and hang the weight of a dead nation's secret around three necks that hadn't asked for it — or say nothing, stay scenery, and let the gray suits come back one day with fewer questions and more men.

"You've seen something," Ilsa said. Not a question.

"I've seen a flood," Dominic said. "I'm going to check the upstream scour before the light goes."

He walked away from them up the riverbank with his heart going like a driven pile, and behind him he heard Wren say, low, to Ilsa: "He doesn't eat when something's wrong. Watch. He won't touch supper tonight either."

He kept walking. Upstream, at the edge of the scour, the river had exposed more than the chest — a corner of rotted timber, a rusted iron ring, the ghost outline of a case that had once held instruments. And nailed to a stone above the high-water line, half peeled by the flood but legible, a survey marker bearing a double-headed eagle and a single word in a language the new maps had erased:

*OBSERVATORY.*

Reznik's word. Reznik's circle, drawn on the land itself.

Dominic stood looking at it as the light went copper and then gray, and made himself face the arithmetic one final time. Three people down at Mile Forty-Seven who noticed when he didn't eat. One magnate who paid for silence and bought men like Corliss by the dozen. Eleven miles of fault line waiting under the mountain, patient as a debt.

He could run tonight. He'd run before; his feet knew the way.

Instead, when he came down the bank in the dark, the bunkhouse lamp was lit and Ilsa was waiting on the step with two cups of coffee and no questions on her face — which was somehow worse than questions, and better.

"Supper's cold," she said. "Sit down anyway."

Dominic sat. And reached into his coat.
