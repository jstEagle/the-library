## 6. The Dry Season Turns

The blasting started on a Tuesday, and Dominic knew it before he heard it — felt it first, a tremor in the coffee he'd poured himself at dawn, the surface shivering into rings like something breathing under the skin of the world. He stood in the doorway of the line shack with the tin cup halfway to his mouth and waited for the sound to arrive, rolling up the valley a full three seconds behind its own echo, and when it came it was wrong. Too deep. Too long. The kind of report that meant powder laid in quantity, in rock that had no business being asked to move.

Vane's crews had come up from the south end while the dry season held, forty men and a steam shovel and enough dynamite to argue with a mountain. They were cutting the pass approach — grading a roadbed where no roadbed belonged, according to any survey Dominic had ever trusted. According, specifically, to the plates sewn into a canvas roll beneath his bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping like a debt.

He drank the coffee anyway. It tasted of iron.

* * *

By midweek the blasts came twice a day, morning and late afternoon, punctual as church bells, and the crew at Mile Forty-Seven worked through them with the resigned rhythm of men who had decided the mountain's opinion was not their department. Ilsa ran her gang along the trestle approaches with her usual economy — no wasted words, no wasted steps, her chalk marks appearing on timbers like signatures only she could read. Tomas drilled. Wren hauled and fetched and asked questions until even Tomas ran out of superstitions to answer.

Dominic kept his head down and his hands busy and told himself, for the eleventh day running, that what happened south of the pass was not his affair. He had sold his opinions years ago, one by one, and the price had always been the same: keep walking, keep your name out of ledgers, keep your mouth shut around men with money.

Then the early blast came.

It was not scheduled. It came at dusk, half an hour after the afternoon shot should have been the last, and it came wrong — a cracked, stuttering boom, two charges firing nearly together instead of clean, and Dominic was already moving before the echo finished, because he knew that sound. He'd spent eleven years learning every way rock could lie about its intentions, and that stutter was the sound of a face letting go.

"Tomas!" He was running the ballast shoulder, boots skidding on loose stone, shouting into the smoke drifting up-valley. "TOMAS!"

The slide came off the cut slope above the old haul road in a single grinding exhalation — not the thundering mass he'd braced for but a tongue of it, a few hundred tons of shattered limestone chasing itself downslope, and it caught Tomas exactly where Dominic had known it would: at the base of the cut, where the drill steels leaned against the rock like a row of unlit candles.

They dug him out in nine minutes. Dominic counted them, because counting was the only thing keeping his hands steady. Ilsa took the big stones; Wren clawed at the fines with both bare hands, saying nothing, making a small sound like a hinge; Dominic went in last, after they'd uncovered the shoulder, and found Tomas's jaw and then his chest, still rising, dust-caked, furious.

"Who fired that shot?" Tomas rasped, before anyone asked if he was all right. "Who in God's name fired it? It wasn't on the board."

It hadn't been. Later, a Vane foreman would shrug and call it a misfire set off by seepage water, an accident of the dry season cracking and the groundwater shifting, nothing anyone could have done. But Dominic had walked the cut's toe that morning, idle, telling himself he was stretching his legs, and he had seen the fracture pattern in the hanging wall — seen it and thought of a plate in his oilcloth bundle, plate fourteen, contour lines converging on this very slope like fingers closing. The dead nation's engineers had flagged this cut. Had written, in that handwriting he knew better than his own: *unstable above grade; do not bench.*

Nobody had read their notes in thirty years. Nobody living, anyway, except him.

That night he sat on the shack step with the lamp turned low and watched the smoke still hanging over the pass like a held breath, and understood, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own obituary, that the next slide would not miss. That Vane's crews would keep benching that slope until the mountain did what mountains do, and the men who died would be men whose names Ilsa chalked on timber, men who noticed when Wren didn't eat.

He was tired of being the only one who knew things.

* * *

He told them on the third night after, in the mess shack, after Tomas was back on his feet with a bruise the color of a storm front down his whole left side. He'd meant to find a gentle way in and discovered, standing there with his cup going cold, that he didn't own one.

"The plates," he said. "From the washout. I have them."

Ilsa looked up from her ledger. Wren stopped mid-reach for the bread.

"I never sold them. I should have — God knows it's the sensible thing, it's the thing I've done my whole life, sell the thing and walk away clean." His voice sounded strange to him, rusty in places, like a switch thrown for the first time in years. "But I opened them. And I can read them. And I'm going to tell you what they say, and then you're going to wish you'd never fed me."

So he told them. He spread the reconstruction across the table — his night work, weeks of it, graphite tracings over the old surveyor's plates, the sealed route drawn in lines fine as hair. He showed them the alignment crossing the range not over the pass but under it, through the fault the dead nation's engineers had proven unstable at the cost of their own lives. He showed them plate fourteen, the cut above the haul road, the notation in faded ink: *unstable above grade; do not bench.* He showed them the final page, the one he hadn't been able to bring himself to translate aloud until now — the surveyor's warning, addressed to a ministry that would be rubble within the decade, describing the fault as alive, creeping, patient, and begging whoever found these notes to bury the route rather than build it.

"They died proving it," Dominic said. "Eleven men, in a tunnel you can still walk into if you know where the portal is. And Vane has the money to hire men who'll never see those notes, and the powder to argue with the mountain until the mountain wins. Tomas almost won second prize last week."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside a rail joint ticked as the temperature fell.

Wren spoke first, sixteen years old and steady as a plumb line. "You've had these since the flood."

"Yes."

"And you were going to—" Wren's jaw worked. "You were just going to sit on them. While they blasted."

"Yes." Dominic didn't flinch from it. He'd learned that much: the only way through a truth was straight at it. "I told myself it was safer for everyone. I told myself a lot of things. I'm very good at telling myself things. It's how I've stayed alive."

Ilsa had not touched the plates. She sat with her arms folded, looking at the contour lines the way she looked at a span she didn't yet trust, and when she finally spoke it was slow, weighing each word like ballast.

"You said Vane offered you a fortune for anything recovered from the washout."

"He did. More than I'd earn in twenty years here."

"And you kept the plates instead."

"I kept the plates instead." He heard how it sounded. Like a confession, which is what it was. "I don't fully know why. Curiosity, I told myself. But curiosity doesn't cost sleep."

Ilsa unfolded her arms. She turned the top plate toward herself, studied the seal — the double-headed eagle of a country that existed now only in archives and graveyards — and then looked up at him with an expression he couldn't parse and was afraid to hope about.

"There's a surveyor's archive in the capital," she said. "My father filed bridge reports there for thirty years. They take original documents. They don't lose them, and they don't sell them, and once something's in a drawer there, it takes an act of parliament and a bribe the size of a cathedral to get it out." She tapped the surveyor's final letter once, her nail clicking on the glass. "This goes in a drawer, Vane's route dies in committee. Not because we shouted. Because the paper says what it says."

"It's four days' hard walking over the range," Dominic said. "Five, with weather. And the snows are—"

"Early. Yes. Everyone keeps saying so." Ilsa stood, and something in the set of her shoulders made the room feel smaller and sturdier at once. "Wren sews better than either of us. Those plates go into a coat lining. We go up before the snows close the pass, all of us — Tomas included, and he'll say yes, so don't waste breath arguing with him, he's already named the mule."

Dominic stared at her. "You understand what you're volunteering for. Vane knows I have these. Sooner or later he'll know you helped. He ruined my last employer with a telegram and a lawyer. He doesn't need soldiers."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "He needs the mountain, and the mountain works for us." A ghost of a smile crossed her face, gone as fast as it came. "Besides. You noticed when Wren didn't eat. You counted the minutes when we dug Tomas out — nine, I heard you. A man who counts minutes like that isn't selling anything ever again. I'm betting the plates on it."

Wren was already clearing the table, already reaching for needle and waxed thread, already talking about which coat had the heaviest lining and whether the mule could carry feed for five. Tomas appeared in the doorway with his arm bound and his face gray and said, "I heard most of that through the wall, and yes, I've named the mule, and nobody is carrying those devil-plates over a mountain without someone who knows how to pray to them properly."

And Dominic stood in the warm clutter of the mess shack — the lamp, the bread, the ledger, the people — holding the only map of a buried country, and felt the strangest sensation of his life, stranger than the flood, stranger than the blast: he was not alone with what he knew, and he had not had to buy the company, and he could not for the life of him remember why he'd ever thought walking away clean was the sensible thing.

"We leave at first light day after tomorrow," Ilsa said. "Before Vane's next shot."

Outside, far to the south, the mountain grumbled — another unscheduled blast, or perhaps just the fault, turning over in its sleep.
