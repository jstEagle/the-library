## 7. Up Before the Snows

The plates rode against Wren's ribs like a second heartbeat.

Ilsa had sewn them into the lining of his coat herself, three nights before they left Mile Forty-Seven, working by lamplight with a sailmaker's stitch she'd learned from her father on the coast. Eleven steel plates wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen, flat against the left side, from collarbone to hip. *If anyone asks,* she'd said, threading the last knot, *you're a skinny apprentice carrying nothing worth stealing.* She'd looked at him then with an expression he couldn't name — something between apology and trust — and Wren had decided in that moment that he would rather die than let the coat come off.

Now, four days into the high country, he was beginning to understand what he'd volunteered for.

* * *

They walked in single file along the old pack trail, five of them: Ilsa breaking trail at the front with her ice axe, Tomas behind her driving the mule, then Wren, then Dominic, and Halvorsen — Vane's former clerk, gaunt and apologetic as a stray dog — bringing up the rear. The sky had been the color of dirty wool since dawn, and the wind came down off the peaks in long, flat gusts that smelled of iron and coming snow.

"Storm's two days out," Dominic said, falling into step beside Wren. He'd been saying it all morning, recalibrating. "Maybe one."

"Then we walk faster," Wren said.

Dominic glanced at him. In the gray light his face was all angles and stubble, and there was something in his eyes Wren had learned to read over the past weeks — the engineer's habit of measuring everything, including people. "You're not built for this pace," Dominic said. It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic. "Your legs are shorter than ours. You're carrying weight you haven't told us about."

Wren's hand moved to his coat before he could stop it.

"I know what's in it," Dominic said quietly. "I put half the stitches in myself." He looked up the trail, where Ilsa's silhouette bent into the wind. "I'm just telling you the truth. If it comes down to it, we lighten the load."

"We're not leaving the plates."

"I didn't say leave them." Dominic's jaw worked. "I said if it comes down to it—"

"You did," Wren said. "On the first night. You told Ilsa the smart play was to split up, take different routes, meet at the archive. You've been doing sums ever since. I can hear the abacus clicking." He kept walking, breath steaming. "You always have an exit planned. That's what you are. But we're not your exit, Mr. Sandoval. We're the cargo."

Dominic stopped walking.

For a moment Wren thought he'd gone too far — that the man would simply turn around, take the low trail south, and vanish the way he'd vanished from every job, every town, every life for fifteen years. That was the story everyone told about Dominic Sandoval. The man who owned nothing. The man who was never anywhere long enough to be missed.

But Dominic just stood there in the wind, looking at Wren with an expression like a man reading a survey of ground he'd thought he knew, and finding a fault line running through it.

"Ilsa notices when you don't eat," he said finally. It made no sense, and it made perfect sense. "Tomas prays for you every night, out loud, badly. Halvorsen gave you his spare socks without being asked." He turned back up the trail. "Do you know how long it's been since anybody noticed anything about me? Don't answer that." And he walked on.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a granite shoulder, huddled under one tarp, and Wren woke twice to find Dominic awake, sitting up, watching the dark line of the valley below them through a cracked spyglass.

"Riders?" Ilsa asked the second time, low.

"Maybe." Dominic handed her the glass. "Two points of light in the trees at the river bend. Could be hunters. Could be nothing."

"Could be Vane's men."

"Could be." Dominic lay back down and pulled his blanket to his chin, and Wren, watching from the edge of sleep, saw that he'd positioned himself between the rest of them and the valley. He hadn't done that the first two nights. The first two nights he'd slept on the outside edge, nearest the escape route.

Somebody had rearranged the sleeping order. Wren was almost certain it hadn't been discussed.

* * *

The river nearly killed him the next afternoon.

It shouldn't have been there at all — the map showed a ford, knee-deep, braided channels over gravel — but the same early storm that was chasing them down from the peaks had already reached the high snowfields, and the river came down brown and violent, swollen to triple its width, dragging whole pines in its current like straws.

"It's passable," Ilsa said, standing at the bank with her fists on her hips. "Barely. We cross at the bend, where it splits around the island. Rope line, one at a time, mule last."

"And if the mule panics?" Halvorsen asked.

"The mule doesn't panic," Tomas said, patting the animal's neck with a huge, gentle hand. "Mules are smarter than men. Mules do not fight wars for magnates." But his eyes, when he looked at the water, were doing the same arithmetic everyone else's were.

They crossed in order: Ilsa first, setting the rope; then Halvorsen, who went white to the lips and made it; then Tomas, hauling the supply pack overhead; then Dominic, moving with a flat economy that told Wren, again, that this man had crossed a hundred rivers exactly like this one, alone, in worse conditions, and never once thought about who was watching him do it.

Wren went fourth-from-last. He was halfway across, boots on the slick gravel of the channel bar, water tearing at his knees, when the flood surge came down.

He heard it before he understood it — a deepening roar upstream, a change in the river's voice — and then the island disappeared. The channel that had been shin-deep became waist-deep in the space of a breath, and the cold hit him like a wall, knocking the air out of his chest, and the current took hold of him the way a hand takes hold of a coat sleeve.

He went down. Gravel rolled under his boots. The rope tore through his gloves, burned his palms, and then his grip failed altogether and the river had him.

The world became noise and brown water and cold so total it was almost heat. He tumbled, struck something soft — a submerged pine, maybe — felt it sweep him sideways, and thought, with a strange floating clarity: *the coat. The plates are in the coat. If I lose the coat—*

Hands closed on his collar.

Not the rope. Hands. A fist knotted in the fabric at the back of his neck, and an arm like an iron bar across his chest, and a voice shouting something he couldn't hear over the water. He was hauled, inch by inch, against the current, and the current fought the whole way, and then gravel was under him again and someone was rolling him onto his stomach and pounding his back while he coughed up half the river.

When he could see again, Dominic was kneeling over him, soaked to the skin, breathing like a bellows, both hands still locked in Wren's coat.

"You idiot," Dominic said. His voice cracked on the second word. "You absolute — you went for the middle channel, why did you—"

"The rope slipped," Wren managed.

"The rope didn't slip. You let go to grab the pack strap." Dominic shook him once, gently, furious. "The pack. Not even your own pack. Tomas's."

"Tomas is old," Wren said stupidly.

Dominic stared at him. Then he sat back on his heels in the shallows and laughed — a short, broken sound, half laugh and half something else — and dragged a wet hand down his face. Behind him, Ilsa stood thigh-deep in the current holding the rope with white knuckles, and Tomas was wringing out his hat, and Halvorsen was saying *thank God, thank God* in a small repetitive voice, and the mule stood on the far bank regarding them all with vast equine contempt.

"The supplies?" Ilsa called.

"Gone," Dominic said. "Pack's gone downstream. Half our food, the tent fly, the spare rope." He said it flatly, the way he read elevations off a grade chart. Then he looked at Wren, still gasping in the shallows, and something crossed his face that Wren would remember for the rest of his life — a war fought and finished in the space of a heartbeat.

Because Wren had seen it. Everyone had seen it. For one instant, Dominic Sandoval had done the sum — one boy, eleven plates, a storm coming, supplies gone, odds worsening by the hour — and the old answer had been right there, ready, worn smooth from use: *cut loose, travel light, the mission outranks the men.* Fifteen years of survival lived in that instant.

And then Dominic had reached past the answer and grabbed the boy instead.

"Right," Dominic said, standing, wringing out his sleeves. "New plan. We can't make the high traverse without the supplies — we'd starve on the glacier shelf. So we go through the tunnel."

Everyone went still. Even the wind seemed to pause.

"That tunnel's not on any living map," Ilsa said slowly. "That's the dead nation's line. The unfinished bore."

"It's on my map," Dominic said. "It's been on my map for six years. It runs clean under the pass for nine miles, dry, graded, ventilated — until the fault section. After that, nobody knows, because everybody who worked on it died proving they shouldn't have." He looked at each of them in turn. "It's also the one place Vane's riders can't shadow us, because they don't know it exists. And we'll be underground when the storm hits."

"And if the fault section isn't stable?" Halvorsen asked.

"Then we learn what the original crew learned," Dominic said. "Hopefully more cheaply than they did."

Tomas crossed himself. Ilsa spat, which Wren had learned was her version of prayer. And Wren, shivering in the shallows with river water running out of his boots, felt the plates shift against his ribs as he stood — eleven pieces of steel, warm now from his own body — and understood that the coat had just become the most important object in the mountains.

* * *

They made the tunnel mouth at dusk, a black arch in the mountainside choked with thirty years of scree and alder, half-swallowed by the slope itself. Dominic cleared the entrance with the axe while the light died, and when they stepped inside, out of the wind at last, the silence was enormous — the held-breath silence of a place that had been waiting a long time for someone to come back.

Dominic lit a lantern. Its glow ran down the bore ahead of them, illuminating rails furred with mineral bloom, timber sets still square and true, and walls where someone had painted numbers and arrows in a language none of them spoke except, apparently, Dominic.

"How far to the fault section?" Ilsa asked.

"Six miles," Dominic said. He raised the lantern higher, and the light caught his face, and Wren saw that behind the exhaustion there was something new in it — something that looked almost like purpose. "Six miles, and then we find out whether the dead were wrong."

Far behind them, back down the valley they'd climbed out of, a thin sound carried on the dying wind — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable.

Hoofbeats.

Lots of them.
