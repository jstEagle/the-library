## 8. The Fault Line

The storm found them an hour after they entered the tunnel, and it found them the way winter finds things — all at once, without negotiation.

Dominic heard it first as a change in the wind at the portal behind them, a low moan climbing to a howl, and then the snow came sideways into the mouth of the bore like a living thing with an appetite. Wren had the lantern. Ilsa had the lead rope. Tomas brought up the rear, favoring the leg he'd wrenched in the slide, and none of them said what all of them knew: there was no going back now. The pass above was already white. By morning it would be a hundred feet of moving mountain.

"Keep to the left wall," Dominic said. "The old crews cut drainage on the right. If the frost heaved it, there'll be shafts."

"Shafts," Wren repeated, in the tone of someone adding a word to a list of things to fear.

"Forty feet down, some of them. The water table through here is a liar."

The lantern light swung and steadied. They walked into the dark of a tunnel that had been abandoned for thirty years, and the tunnel, Dominic thought, had not gotten the news. The rails were still down in places, green with corrosion but true. Timber sets stood every four feet, black with age but sound, and the air was dry as paper and cold as a held breath. Somewhere behind the rock, water moved — he could feel it in his teeth, a pressure, a patience. The fault. The thing the dead nation's engineers had died proving. It was down there in the dark, awake and waiting, and every step they took into the mountain was a step across its spine.

They made perhaps two miles before Ilsa called a halt. Her lips were gray. Wren's hands, holding the lantern, had gone clumsy, and Tomas had stopped pretending his leg was anything but bad. They needed fire, and fire meant fuel, and there was no fuel in a tunnel but the tunnel itself.

It was Wren who saw the glow first.

Not firelight — a reflection. Far down the bore, where the lantern's reach failed, something caught the light and threw it back, faint and yellow and wrong for rock.

They went toward it the way people go toward anything in the dark: too fast, too quiet, hands out.

The camp was intact.

That was the only word for it. The old nation's crews had left in a hurry — everyone knew that, the withdrawal, the collapse, the flags coming down from a country that had simply stopped — but they had left the way soldiers leave, not the way fugitives leave. A cook stove stood against the wall with its flue still assembled. Blankets were folded on canvas cots. Tin plates hung from nails. A tool rack held drills and hammers in their proper places, and on a table beneath a lantern glass gone black with soot, someone had left a ledger, a bottle of ink frozen solid in its well, and a letter.

The letter was under a tin cup. The cup had been placed there deliberately, Dominic saw — a weight, a promise. *I will come back for this.* The man who wrote it had not come back. No one had come back for thirty years.

"Wood's dry," Ilsa said, already moving, because she was the kind of woman who grieved by working. She pulled planking from an unused timber set, and Tomas got the stove lit, and the camp filled with light and the smell of coal smoke and something almost like the past warming its hands.

Dominic stood at the table. He put on his gloves. Then he lifted the cup and took the letter.

The handwriting stopped his breath.

He had known whose it was since the river. He had known from the first plate, from the sure, slanted hand, the surveyor's shorthand with its peculiar crossed sevens — a hand he'd spent two years of his apprenticeship imitating, badly, while the old man who owned it corrected him with a pencil stub and infinite patience. Aurel Kessler. The man who had taught him that a line on paper was a promise made to everyone who would ever ride it.

The letter was dated the last autumn of the nation. It was addressed, simply, *To whoever is competent.*

*We have finished proving what we feared. The fault is not dormant. It is active, and it moves with the seasons — a slow breath, millimeters, but the instruments do not lie and I will not lie to you either. The ministry has ordered the tunnel driven through anyway. They call our findings pessimism. I call the men who will die in this bore in thirty years' time my colleagues, and I am writing this so that when they die, someone will know it was not an accident. It was a decision. It was made in a warm room by men who will not be in the mountain when it closes.*

*The plates in this camp are the complete record. If you have found this letter, you have perhaps found them as well. Do not let them be buried. Do not let them be used. A survey is a moral document. It says: here is what the ground will bear, and here is what it will not. Everything else is negotiation with gravity, and gravity does not negotiate.*

*If the fault is ever breached, seal it. Not with a signature. With stone.*

Dominic read it twice. The second time he read it aloud, because Wren was watching his face and Ilsa had stopped stacking wood, and some things are not meant to be read silently.

Nobody spoke for a long while. The stove ticked. Somewhere far back along the bore, the mountain made a sound like a man shifting his weight in sleep.

"He knew," Tomas said at last. "Thirty years ago, he knew, and they built it anyway."

"They never finished it," Ilsa said. "The war came. Or the money ran out. Whatever killed the country killed the tunnel first."

"And now Vane wants to finish it," Dominic said. "With his own crews. On his own schedule. With his own name on the dedication plaque." He set the letter down with a care that surprised him. His hands were not steady. "He's had the plates' existence since the telegram he sent me. He doesn't care what they show. He thinks he can buy the ground the way he bought everything else."

"Can he?" Wren asked. It was not a naive question. It was the question of someone who had watched the world work for seventeen years and wanted, badly, to be told otherwise.

"No," Dominic said. And then, because he had made a promise to himself somewhere on the trail, in the dark, in the cold: "Not if this reaches the archive."

* * *

They heard the horses before they saw anything — no. That was wrong. There were no horses. There was the sound of boots on old rails, and the scrape of a rifle barrel against timber, and then a lantern flared at the far end of the bore, small as a star, and a voice carried down the tunnel with the flat clarity that cold air gives.

"Mr. Sandoval. We know you have the stove lit. We can see your smoke."

Four of them. Dominic counted the lanterns — four, and one held high and still, which meant a man holding it for others to shoot by. Vane's riders had followed them up the pass and into the tunnel, and they had come in behind, and the portal they'd entered by might as well have been on the moon.

"Walk out slow," the voice said. "Leave the tin box and the papers on the table. Nobody needs to be cold longer than they have to."

Ilsa's hand found a drill hammer. Tomas, gray-faced, put himself between the light and the table without appearing to decide to — his body simply went there, the way a wall goes where a wall is. Wren pressed back against the cook stove, and Dominic felt the coat with the plates in it, sewn into the lining against Wren's ribs, and understood in one cold flash that the voice was wrong about what to ask for.

They didn't want the tin box. They wanted the coat.

"Mr. Sandoval," the voice said again, closer now, and there was something almost friendly in it. "Mr. Vane sends his regards. He says the offer stands. He says it stands doubled."

"It stood doubled in the pass," Dominic called back. "It stood doubled at the waystation. It'll stand doubled at my funeral. Tell him I've stopped counting."

"Sir, I'm telling you as one professional to another—"

"You're telling me as a man with a rifle to a man without one. Let's be accurate."

The lanterns came on. Four men in storm gear, rifles low but not down, and their leader was young and tired and looked like a man who had ridden through a blizzard on an order he didn't fully like. Dominic catalogued him the way you catalogue anything that might kill you: good boots, bad hands, finger inside the trigger guard because he was cold and sloppy.

"Take the coat off the boy," the leader said.

And Tomas — Tomas, who believed the tunnel was haunted, who had crossed himself at the portal, who had carried Wren's pack for six miles on a bad leg without one word of complaint — Tomas stepped forward and spread his arms like a man barring a door.

"You want the coat," Tomas said, in his slow, accented, immovable way, "you take it off a dead man."

It happened the way these things always happen, which is to say faster than thought and slower than regret. The leader's hand twitched. The rifle came up — whether by decision or by cold-stupid accident, Dominic would spend the rest of his life not knowing — and the shot went off in the confined dark of the tunnel like the mountain itself cracking, and Tomas turned, and folded, and went down across the table with the letter under him, his blood coming fast and black in the lantern light.

Ilsa's hammer took the nearest man across the jaw. Wren screamed — not fear, fury, a sound Dominic had never heard from her — and the lanterns scattered, and there was a chaos of boots and swinging steel and the smell of powder smoke, and then the riders were falling back down the bore, dragging their leader, because four men with rifles in the dark are brave and four men with rifles against a hammer and a screaming apprentice and a man who has nothing left to lose are merely four men in a tunnel.

Then it was quiet, except for Ilsa.

Dominic knelt. The wound was high in the chest, and Tomas was breathing in a wet, terrible rhythm, and his eyes found Dominic's with an awful clarity.

"The plates," Tomas said.

"They're safe. They're safe, old man."

"The letter says seal it." A bubble of blood at the corner of his mouth. His hand, huge and cracked and gentle, closed on Dominic's wrist. "With stone. Not paper. He said — with stone. You tell them. You tell them what I—" His eyes went past Dominic, up, to the roof of the tunnel, to the mountain over it, and whatever Tomas saw there, he was not afraid of it. "I will hold the door," he said, in the voice he used for prayers, and then he did not say anything again, ever.

* * *

They buried him in the camp, because there was no other place and no other choice. Ilsa broke up a cot for boards. Wren sewed the plates deeper into her coat with hands that did not shake, which frightened Dominic more than shaking would have. They laid Tomas under the timber sets with his drill hammer on his chest, and Ilsa spoke over him in the low, rough cadence of her home valley, and Dominic, who had not prayed since the accident, found his lips moving anyway.

Afterward they stood in the firelight, three of them where there had been five, and Dominic felt something close over in his chest — not grief alone, grief was only part of it. It was the last of the old arithmetic, the survival math, the *cut loose, travel light* — burning away like fog in sun.

Vane would not stop. He had sent riders through a blizzard. He had put a bullet into a driller with a bad leg. A committee could be bought; a magistrate could be bought; Dominic could be bought, had been bought, and knew exactly the price of himself. The only thing Vane could not buy was the archive — a public room, a sealed record, a truth that once read aloud could not be unread. And beyond the archive, the mountain itself. Kessler's letter had said it and Tomas had died saying it and Dominic finally, finally believed it:

*Seal it with stone.*

"The pass closes tomorrow," Ilsa said. "Maybe tonight."

"Then we go tonight," Dominic said. He looked down the bore, toward the far portal, toward the storm and the summit and the capital beyond it, and his voice came out level and cold and certain. "All the way through. We carry him with us, and we carry the letter, and we finish what the old man started."

Wren lifted the lantern. "Which way?"

Dominic turned toward the deep dark, where the fault breathed behind the rock.

"Through," he said.
