## 9. The Crossing

The storm arrived the way Ilsa's mother used to say winter did in the high country — not falling so much as arriving, all at once, as if it had been waiting behind the ridge for someone foolish enough to come looking.

She stood at the mouth of the unfinished tunnel and watched it come, snow moving sideways in sheets, erasing the world beyond ten feet. Behind her, inside the mountain, Tomas lay on a bedroll soaked through with blood that had stopped being bright and gone the color of old rust. Wren knelt beside him with hands that wouldn't stop shaking. Dominic stood apart, as he always did, holding the surveyor's letter like it might tell him something different if he read it a ninth time.

"We can't carry him over the pass," Ilsa said. She said it flat, because there was no other way to say it. "Not in this. Not fast enough."

"He'll die if we leave him here," Wren said.

"He might die either way." Ilsa turned to look at them both. "But he won't die alone, and he won't die cold, and whatever happens, those plates reach the archive. That's what he was shielding. That's what we owe him."

Dominic folded the letter with the care of a man folding a flag. "There's another way," he said.

Isla had learned, over these weeks, to hear the change in his voice when he stopped calculating and started deciding. It dropped half a tone. It got quieter. She'd hated it at first — the sound of a man closing doors — but she'd come to trust it more than any confident voice she'd ever worked under.

"The engineers' magazine," Dominic said. "Back along the gallery. The dead crew left their charges when they abandoned the tunnel. Case after case of gelignite, thirty years in the dark, but the tins were wax-sealed. Some of it will still be live."

"You want to bring down the tunnel," Ilsa said. "With us inside it?"

"With us *ahead* of it." He pointed back into the black throat of the passage, toward the fault chamber where they'd found the camp, the skeletons, the letter. "The route runs through the fault. Vane knows it now — or he will, once his riders stop chasing us long enough to read what we read. If the tunnel stands, he digs. He has the money to dig for twenty years. But if the fault section comes down — if it comes down *right* — no engineer alive will certify that bore again. The route dies. The map dies. Everything Vane wants dies with it."

"And the proof," Ilsa said. "The plates. The letter. You said the archive was the only way anyone would ever believe—"

"I know what I said." His jaw worked. "I'm telling you the truth may already be buried in a man who's bleeding out forty feet from us. I'm telling you we might trade the proof for the thing itself. A sealed fault is its own testimony. Nobody builds through it. Nobody has to believe anything."

Wren looked up from Tomas, eyes red-rimmed and furious. "He took a bullet for those plates."

"Yes," Dominic said. "And I'm asking whether he'd want us to take bullets for paper."

Nobody answered. The storm filled the silence with its white roar.

Ilsa thought about bridges. She always did, in the hard moments. A bridge was a promise made against your own doubt — you drove the pilings knowing the river would test them, and you slept anyway, because the alternative was never crossing anything. She had spent her whole life building promises over water. This was the first time anyone had asked her to build one out of ruin.

"Show me the charges," she said.

* * *

They worked by lantern light in the old magazine alcove, three hundred yards back along the gallery, where the dead nation's engineers had stacked their blasting stores in neat pine cases stenciled with a script none of them could read. The wax seals had held on perhaps a third of the tins. Dominic handled each one like a sleeping animal.

"These two galleries meet at the fault chamber," he explained, chalking marks on the tunnel wall while Ilsa checked fuses with fingers gone clumsy from cold. "The original crew cut pilot bores off the main heading — that's how they proved the fault was live. If we shoot the main charge at the chamber mouth and collapse the pilot bores behind it, the whole section comes down in one mass. No pocket. No crawl-through. A million tons of mountain sitting on a fault line that wanted to move anyway."

"And Vane's riders?" Ilsa asked. "Where are they when this mountain comes down?"

"That," Dominic said, "is the part I've been thinking about hardest."

Because the riders had caught up at dusk — four of them, torchlight bobbing in the storm, voices echoing up the gallery from the south portal. They'd found the horses. They'd found the tracks. And they knew, the way hired men always know, that the people they were hunting had nowhere left to go but forward, into a tunnel with a mountain sitting on top of it.

"They'll follow us in," Wren said. "They have to. Vane doesn't pay them to lose us at the finish line."

"No," Dominic agreed. "He doesn't."

Ilsa saw it land on him then — she watched it cross his face like weather. The plan they were building wasn't just a wall. It was a trap with the door swinging shut, and every second they spent placing charges was a second the riders spent closing distance.

"How long to set it?" she asked.

"If we're quick, and lucky, an hour. Then we run for the north portal, light the fuse train, and pray the mountain does what mountains do."

"And if the riders are faster than we are?"

Dominic met her eyes. In the lantern light he looked older than she'd ever seen him, and calmer, which frightened her more than panic would have.

"Then we find out which of us the mountain prefers."

* * *

They placed the charges the way Ilsa imagined the dead crew would have — carefully, professionally, with the particular tenderness of people who respected the thing that could kill them. Dominic set the main shot at the fault chamber itself, tucking tins into the rubble of the old camp, beneath the rotted canvas of a tent that had sheltered dying men. Wren ran fuse line with the quick, sure hands of an apprentice who'd finally stopped being an apprentice somewhere on this mountain and hadn't noticed yet.

Ilsa packed the side bores. Her arms ached. Her hands were cut. Somewhere above them the storm hammered the mountain like a fist on a door, and she thought: *let us in, let us in, we're coming.*

Tomas stayed at the campsite with the last of the lantern oil and Wren's coat over him, refusing — absolutely refusing — to be moved further. "You'll send word," he said to Ilsa, gripping her wrist with a strength that surprised her. "When the plates get there. You'll send word to my sister in Valdrov. Tell her I died north of the pass, working. Working is what I want on the stone."

"You're not going to need a stone," Ilsa said. "You're going to need a new drill bit, because I'm not carrying yours home for you."

He laughed, and then coughed, and then laughed again, and she walked away before her face could betray her.

The riders entered the tunnel at midnight.

Ilsa heard them before she saw them — boot heels on stone, echoing up the gallery, and a voice calling out, reasonable and cold: "Sandoval! Mr. Vane only wants the documents! Walk out and the rest of you walk free!"

Dominic lit the first fuse.

The spark hissed away into the dark like a living thing, and they ran — all three of them, up the grade toward the north portal, through air that tasted of powder smoke and old stone, while behind them the boots grew louder and the voice called again, angrier now, and Wren stumbled and Ilsa caught her arm and hauled her upright without breaking stride, and Dominic ran last, running backward for three strides to check the fuse line glowing faint in the black, and Ilsa understood in that moment that she would follow this man anywhere, not because he was good — she still didn't know if he was good — but because he had finally decided to be *theirs*.

They came out of the north portal into the storm's white teeth and turned east, climbing, the pass narrowing above them, snow to their knees, then their thighs. Behind them the mountain waited.

At the count of ninety — Dominic counting aloud, voice shredded by wind — the world ended politely.

It didn't sound like an explosion. It sounded like a door closing. A deep, muffled concussion rolled through the rock beneath their feet, and then a longer, grinding roar, the sound of a million tons finding the floor that thirty years of geology had been waiting to give them. Snow sloughed off the slopes above the portal. Dust breathed out of the tunnel mouth like the mountain exhaling.

Then silence, except for the storm.

"It's done," Wren whispered. "It's really done. The route's gone."

"The route was always a lie," Dominic said. "Now the lie has a headstone."

Ilsa looked back once. The portal was half-buried already, snow filling what the blast hadn't. Somewhere behind that wall of stone were four men who had chosen to be on the wrong side of it, and a man named Tomas bleeding out beside a lantern, and thirty years of dead engineers who had tried to warn everyone and been buried for their trouble.

The map was gone. The plates were gone — sealed in the shot, crushed past any reading, past any theft, past any committee or courtroom or magnate's greed. Dominic believed they'd buried their only proof, and maybe they had.

But Ilsa had spent her life around men who thought paper was the strongest material there was. She knew better. Stone remembered. Mountains testified. And somewhere in the capital, an archive held the surveyor's name, and archives had a way of answering letters decades late.

"Move," she said. "We've got maybe three hours of light left in this storm, and the col is still ahead of us."

They climbed. The storm began, at last, to spend itself — the sheets thinning to flakes, the flakes to a fine dry sifting, and through a tear in the clouds the late sun struck the eastern peaks and set them burning gold. Ilsa climbed toward that light with Wren's hand in hers and Dominic a pace ahead breaking trail, and she thought about Tomas's laugh, and about the word he'd chosen — *working* — and how it was the right word, the only word, for all of them.

They crested the col as the sun went down.

Below them, the far slope fell away into a valley where, impossibly, impossibly far below, a single lamp burned in a waystation window — small and yellow and stubborn, the first human light on the other side of everything.

"There," Wren said, and her voice cracked on it.

Dominic stood looking down at that lamp for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was strange — stripped of everything Ilsa had come to recognize in it.

"I haven't crossed this range in eleven years," he said. "Last time, I came over alone, with nothing, and I told myself that was the way to live."

"And now?" Ilsa asked.

He looked back at them — at her, at Wren, at the empty space where Tomas should have been standing — and something in his face let go, some gate finally swinging open after years of rust.

"Now I'm going to knock on that door," he said, "and give them my real name."

They started down.
