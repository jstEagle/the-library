## 2. A Stranger's Last Camp

The trouble with high altitude is that it strips everything down to essentials: breath, footing, and the exact weight of your regrets. Above five thousand meters there's no room for pretense. The mountain takes one look at you and says, *that's all? That's what you brought?*

I was thinking about this on the fourth morning of the Kessler trek, watching a client named Dietrich attempt to eat a protein bar with gloves on, when Pasang stopped mid-stride ahead of me and did the thing he does—went very still, the way Sherpas go still when something is wrong that they've decided not to waste words on.

"What," I said.

"Smoke." He pointed his chin east, toward a shelf of rock below the ridge we'd been skirting. "Old smoke."

He meant the smell of a fire that had burned down to coals and stayed there, tended by someone too weak or too stubborn to gather more wood. I knew the smell. It's the smell of people who have stopped planning for tomorrow.

"We don't have time," said Dietrich, around a mouthful of bar.

Dietrich had paid me in cash, in advance, through an intermediary who didn't ask questions, which should have told him everything he needed to know about the nature of his guide. Off-the-books treks are how I'd been eating since Nepal revoked my permit and Peru made noises about doing worse than revoking things. The route we were walking wasn't a route so much as a rumor with elevation gain, threading between two borders that neither government loved visitors crossing. It paid triple my legal rate. Triple my legal rate was still less than what I owed Dario Voss, but it kept Voss's collectors from visiting me in person, and that was worth something.

"We have time," I told Dietrich. "We don't have a choice. If someone's up there dying, and we walk past, and anyone ever finds out, none of us are welcome in this range again. Including you, and including whatever alias you're traveling under."

That got his attention. People who pay cash in advance are always traveling under something.

* * *

We found him in a hollow below the shelf, half-sheltered by a tarp strung between two boulders with the optimism of a man who'd once believed weather could be negotiated with. He was old—not mountain-old, which is a different category, but genuinely old, seventy at least, with a white beard gone yellow at the edges and hands that looked like they'd been carved from driftwood by someone in a hurry.

He was propped against his pack like it was the only friend who'd stayed, and beside him sat a tin cup of snowmelt and a small brass compass, and he was watching us come down the slope with an expression I can only describe as *relieved and annoyed about it.*

"Four of you," he rasped, as we crowded into his hollow. "Took you long enough. I've been listening to you crash around the hillside since dawn."

"Sir," I said, crouching, "can you tell me your name?"

"Silas." He coughed, wet and long, and I didn't need a stethoscope to know what that meant. Fluid where fluid shouldn't be. High-altitude pulmonary edema, or pneumonia, or just the plain fact of a body deciding it was finished. Up here, the distinctions stop mattering. "Silas Vane."

"Mr. Vane, we're going to get you down. We'll rig a litter, take turns—"

"No." He said it with such finality that I actually shut up, which for me is a minor miracle. His eyes were pale gray and terribly clear, the way eyes get at the end, all the fog burned off. "You're not taking me anywhere. I know what this is. I've known for three days. I'm not spending my last ones being dragged down a scree slope by strangers while my ribs crack."

"You could live," I said. "People survive this."

"People survive lots of things." He looked at me then, really looked, and I felt it like a searchlight. "Not me. Not here. And not wanting to be rude, guide, but you look like a man who knows the difference between surviving and living better than most."

Pasang made a small sound that might have been agreement. Traitor.

I gave Silas the standard speech anyway—the litter, the descent, the clinic at the trailhead, oxygen if we were lucky—and he listened with the patience of a man humoring a child's theory about the moon. When I ran out of speech, he held out his tin cup.

"Water first. Then we talk."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about dying strangers: they take their time. They want to talk. And because you're the last audience they'll ever have, they talk to *you*, specifically, as though the universe assigned you the shift.

Silas Vane talked for two hours while his clients—I mean my clients—set up camp out of earshot and Pasang brewed tea that nobody drank. He'd been a prospector, on and off, forty years. Rivers mostly. Gold, sometimes, but he said gold was just the excuse; what he'd actually been prospecting for was distance. From a wife who'd left, from debts of his own, from a daughter he'd walked out on when she was six years old because staying felt harder than leaving, and he'd spent four decades discovering that leaving was the hardest thing of all, it just took longer to notice.

"She'll be fifty-one now," he said. "If she's alive. Mara. She had her mother's temper and my stubbornness, God help everyone." He laughed, which turned into the cough again, and I held the cup while he rode it out, and when it passed he gripped my wrist with those driftwood fingers and didn't let go.

"I'm not going to make this complicated," he said. "I don't have the breath for complicated."

"Then don't."

"There's a map. In the oilskin, under my pack frame. And a letter, sealed. I want you to take them downriver—to the settlements on the Sengkar. Find her, or find someone who knows her, or find her grave if it comes to that, and give her the letter. The map—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been shame, or its expensive cousin, regret, "the map is hers too. Everything on it is hers. I just couldn't carry it anymore."

"Carry what?" I asked, because I'm a professional and professionals ask questions even when they already dread the answers.

"The provision," he said. "What I set aside. What I owed."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one luxury I can't otherwise afford: my first thought was *gold.* My second thought was *how much,* and my third thought was a quick, ugly calculation involving Dario Voss, the number I owed him, and the exchange rate between a dead man's secrets and a living man's freedom. I stood there holding a dying stranger's cup and auctioning off his trust in my head, and the worst part—the part I keep coming back to—is how easy it was. How smooth. Invisibility isn't just a survival strategy; it's a moral one. You can justify anything from inside a shadow.

"I'm not a good man to entrust things to," I said. "Ask around. Ask anyone."

"I did," said Silas. "Asked the universe, more or less. It sent me a guide with haunted eyes working an illegal route, which means you're either a criminal or a coward, and cowards don't work routes like this." He smiled, and it transformed his whole ruined face into something I suddenly understood a daughter might have loved. "Criminals, on the other hand, keep promises. It's the only currency you people respect."

"That's cynical."

"I'm dying above the treeline with a stranger's hand on my cup. I've earned cynicism." He released my wrist and fumbled under the pack frame, and I helped him because my hands moved before my judgment caught up—they've always been faster than the rest of me, my hands. The oilskin bundle came out black with age and soft as cloth. Inside: a map, folded into eighths, its creases gone to lace. And a letter, sealed with candle wax, with a single word pressed into it before it hardened:

*MARA.*

"Two conditions," Silas said. "One: you read nothing but the name. The rest belongs to her. Two: you don't sell it. Whatever you think it's worth, it's worth more delivered, and I don't mean in money." His breathing had gone shallow and fast, and I recognized the rhythm of a man rationing sentences. "Swear it. Out loud. I need to hear a voice say it, not just a face nodding. Faces lie. Voices mostly don't."

"Voices lie constantly," I said. "It's faces that are honest."

"Wren." I hadn't told him my name. Pasang must have, or Dietrich, or the wind. "I don't have until you finish being clever."

So I swore. Out loud, in the thin cold air, with three clients and a Sherpa within earshot and a dying man's gray eyes fixed on mine: I would carry the map and the letter to the Sengkar settlements, I would find Mara Vane or her grave, I would deliver both intact, and I would read nothing but the name. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. Voices lie constantly, but apparently mine lies well.

Silas closed his eyes. "There," he whispered. "That's the provision settled. Forty years to make one delivery." He was quiet a moment, and I thought he'd gone, but he opened his eyes again and said, almost conversationally: "She liked rivers. Even at six. Used to stand at the edge and dare the water to do something. I hope she never learned to swim. I hope she stayed scared of it. Fear keeps people on the bank, and the bank is where the living happens."

"That's terrible advice," I said.

"It's father advice. Different genre entirely." He smiled at whatever he saw past my shoulder—the sky, maybe, or the ridge, or a six-year-old daring a river. "Get some sleep, guide. You've got a long way to go down."

* * *

He died at 3:40 in the morning, though I only know the time because I was awake, which I usually am, and because I checked my watch, which is a habit of people who measure everything except themselves. It was quiet. No last words, no dramatic clasp of hands. Just a change in the rhythm of his breathing—like a river easing into slower water—and then no rhythm at all, and the wind coming over the ridge filling in the silence like it had been waiting for the vacancy.

I sat with him until dawn. I don't know why. There was nothing to sit with, strictly speaking; the man had left the premises. But it seemed poor form to treat his body like luggage the moment the tenant departed, so I sat, and I thought about my father, who died in a hospital with a nurse holding his hand because I was on a mountain at the time, and about how the mountains keep collecting these payments from me, and about whether I'd ever once in my adult life done a single thing without calculating the return.

At first light, Pasang came and stood beside me without a word. He looked at Silas, then at me, then at the oilskin bundle in my lap, which I hadn't noticed I was holding like a sleeping animal.

"You buried many people?" he asked.

"On mountains? Enough."

"He was lucky," Pasang said. "Most people die alone down there." He nodded at the valley, at the world. Then he went to wake the clients, because grief is a luxury and we were on a schedule.

We buried him above the treeline because the ground above the treeline is the only ground that doesn't argue. It took two hours to dig a meter down through frozen scree, and Dietrich complained the entire time, which I found oddly comforting—some things stay constant at any altitude. We stacked stones over him, a cairn without a summit prayer, and Pasang tied a bit of prayer flag cord to the topmost rock, and I put the brass compass in Silas's hands before we covered him, because a prospector shouldn't go out without one, and because I have enough things that point at what I've lost.

The map and the letter went into the inner pocket of my jacket, against my chest, where I told myself they'd be safe from rain.

Where I told myself a lot of things.

* * *

We made good time that day, downhill, the air thickening gratefully with every hundred meters. By late afternoon we'd reached the treeline, and by dusk we were camped in scrub pine with actual firewood, and Dietrich was complaining about the food, and everything was normal, and I sat apart from the fire turning the oilskin bundle over in my hands like a man palming a coin he hasn't decided to spend.

Here's the arithmetic I was running, and I'll lay it out flat because I've spent years pretending I'm more complicated than I am: I owed Voss eleven thousand dollars and change. Men who deal in maps and deeds—whatever this provision was—could clear that twice over. One meeting, one envelope, one signature, and I could walk back into Nepal with papers, with a name that opened doors instead of closing them. Silas was dead. Dead men don't check on deliveries. The promise had been made to a heartbeat, and heartbeats don't file complaints.

And underneath the arithmetic, quiet as old coals, the other thing: *Mara. She liked rivers. She used to stand at the edge and dare the water to do something.* A voice saying a daughter's name like it was the last word of a language he was forgetting.

I put the bundle back in my jacket. I told myself it was because selling it would take time I didn't have, contacts I didn't have, and that the smart play was to get down to the river towns first and assess the market. All true. Every word true. That's the trick of self-deception—it works best with accurate ingredients.

I was still telling myself this when Pasang touched my arm and pointed back up the slope we'd descended, to the ridgeline we'd crossed at noon.

Two figures stood against the last light. Too far to see faces. Wrong posture for trekkers—no packs, no poles, standing the way men stand when they're not admiring the view but measuring it. Measuring us.

"They've been there twenty minutes," Pasang murmured. "Since before we camped."

Nobody climbs to that ridge by accident. Nobody stands on it at dusk without gear unless they're looking for something specific, and there was only one thing on this entire mountain in the last week worth looking for, and I could feel it against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Kill the fire," I said.
