## 3. The Promise

Silas died the way he'd lived, apparently: badly timed and stubborn about it.

I'd sat up with him through most of the night, because there's a kind of arithmetic to these things that doesn't show up in any guidebook. A man dying alone above the treeline is a weight on a conscience, and I've carried enough weight at altitude to know you don't add more than you have to. So I sat. I fed him snowmelt when he could swallow. I listened to him talk about a river I'd never seen, in a country I'd been blacklisted out of, told by a voice that kept fraying like old rope.

He talked mostly about the daughter.

"Mara," he said, around three in the morning, as if testing whether the name still worked. "Mara Vane." Not his surname. I noticed that. Dying men notice things about themselves they spent their whole lives avoiding.

"You'll find her," I said, because it was easier than saying *why me* or *I don't do this anymore* or *I'm not a good enough person for what you're asking.*

"She'll be angry." He smiled at the stars like they owed him money. "Good. Anger means she cared once."

I didn't answer that. Around us the trek slept in two tents, my four clients dreaming whatever off-the-books trekkers dream—summits, probably, or tax evasion. The wind came down off the ice fields with that particular midnight honesty, the kind that strips a man down to his decisions.

"Map's in the oilskin," Silas said. "Under my left side. Letter's inside it. Sealed."

"I know."

"You looked."

"I'm a guide," I said. "Looking is the job."

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into that wet rattle I've heard twice before and never forgotten. When it passed he was quieter, smaller, like the mountain had taken another bite of him.

"Wren Kavanagh," he said. My name. I hadn't given it to him. One of the porters must have, or he'd heard it from the clients, or—and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome—he'd known who I was before we ever stumbled onto his camp. "They say you're the best high-route man between here and nowhere."

"They say a lot of things about me. Most of them are why I can't work here legally."

"That so?"

"That's so."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, and then he did something I wasn't prepared for. He reached out and gripped my wrist with a hand that had maybe an hour of strength left in it, and he held on like I was the last fixed line on a bad face.

"I'm not asking you to raise her," he said. "I'm asking you to hand her a piece of paper. That's all. Any fool can hand over a piece of paper."

"Most fools would sell it first."

His eyes found mine in the dark, and there was nothing dying in them at all. "That's why I'm not asking a fool."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and indifferent, the way it does above the treeline, no color to speak of until the sun cleared the eastern ridge and set the icefields burning. Silas made it to first light. He held on for the sun specifically—I'm fairly sure of that, because he turned his face toward it when it broke the ridge, and he said, very clearly, "There she is," though whether he meant the sun or the daughter or something else entirely, I couldn't tell you.

Then he stopped breathing, without drama, without a last word, just a pause that never picked itself back up.

I checked anyway. Two fingers to the throat, a full minute, because you owe the dead your doubt before you give them your certainty. Nothing. The wind moved through the scrub. Down in the tents, my clients were starting to stir, complaining in advance about the cold.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about death at altitude: the body becomes a logistics problem almost immediately. Grief is a luxury of people standing at sea level. Up here, a corpse is weight, hygiene, morale, schedule. Every instinct I had—the ones that had kept me alive through two blacklistings and a decade of Voss's ledgers—said *cover him with stones, mark the cairn, move on, you're behind pace.*

So that's what I started to do.

Except.

Except he'd asked me for one thing, and it wasn't stones. It was a promise, and I hadn't said yes yet, and now the window for saying yes was closing at the speed of decomposition. There's a version of me—a younger one, the one who existed before Nepal, before Peru, before Dario Voss got his hooks into my finances and my reputation both—who'd have said yes out loud, solemnly, with feeling.

The current version did the math instead. Map and letter, weighed against four days of lost route time, a burial in frozen ground, and a promise to a stranger that could only cost me. Against that: a clear conscience, which I'd learned to live without, and a debt to Voss that no conscience was going to touch.

The math said walk away.

I dug the grave anyway.

Not deep—the ground wouldn't allow it, half-frozen scree over permafrost, every shovel stroke ringing like a struck bell. But I worked at it while the clients watched from a respectful distance with that particular tourist expression, the one that says *this is authentic* and *should we be helping* and *is this included in the fee.* I stacked the spoil into a cairn afterward, waist-high, because a man who tracked a daughter across decades deserved more than a depression in the dirt.

Before I lowered him, I took the oilskin packet from under his left side.

I want to be honest about this part, since honesty is apparently the theme of my morning: I told myself it was payment. He'd offered it. He'd insisted. A dying man's last transaction, freely entered into, and God knows I was owed wages by somebody, somewhere, even if it was a corpse. That's the story I ran in my head while my hands did the work. Clean exchange. Guide services rendered. Nothing sentimental about it.

The oilskin was stiff with age and wrapped around something flat and heavy. Inside: a map, hand-drawn on what felt like sailcloth, river country inked in a confident draftsman's hand—channels, cutoffs, a stretch marked with symbols I didn't recognize, and coordinates in margins gone soft with handling. And folded within it, sealed with wax that had cracked but held, a letter addressed in block capitals:

**FOR M. VANE — TO BE DELIVERED UNOPENED**

No address. No instructions beyond the name. Just the name, sitting there like a door I hadn't agreed to knock on.

I put it inside my jacket, against my chest, where the map rode warm and faintly greasy against my skin for the rest of the day, and I told myself that too was practical. Oilskin or not, paper doesn't survive pack straps and rain. Body heat, body shelter. Purely logistical.

You can lie to yourself all day at altitude. The thin air doesn't care. But some part of me—that part that had sat up all night listening to a dying man say *Mara* like a prayer he'd gotten wrong for forty years—knew exactly what I'd done. I'd accepted the job. I just hadn't signed anything.

Pragmatism, I told the mountain. The mountain declined to comment.

* * *

We made poor time that morning, which suited me fine, because poor time meant my head stayed down and my eyes stayed on the trail and I didn't have to think about the letter pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat. The clients were subdued. Death does that, even a stranger's death, even at a distance. One of them, the banker from Singapore, asked me if I'd known the man.

"No," I said.

"He gave you something."

"He paid me," I said. "For the burial."

Which was true, in the way that most of my statements were true—technically, narrowly, with the important parts amputated. The banker nodded and dropped back, satisfied or polite, and I spent the next hour cataloguing everything wrong with my situation.

Item: I was carrying a document that at least one person had died near, possibly because of, and that a professional prospector had judged valuable enough to trade his last hours of comfort for safekeeping.

Item: I was in debt to Dario Voss to a sum that had stopped being a number years ago and become a weather system—permanent, atmospheric, shaping everything beneath it.

Item: Voss had eyes everywhere, because paranoia is cheap and informants are cheaper, and I was currently leading an illegal trek through terrain where being seen was the same as being reported.

I was turning item three over when Dev, my senior porter, dropped back beside me and touched my elbow. Dev doesn't waste gestures. He pointed with his chin at the ridgeline to our west, maybe eight hundred meters up and two kilometers back along our own track.

Two figures. Small with distance, but moving wrong for locals—too upright, too steady, walking like men being paid by the hour rather than traveling by habit. They'd stopped where our track bent around a spur. One of them raised something to his face.

Binoculars. Or a camera. At that distance, and with Voss's budget, I'd have bet on both.

"Porters?" Dev offered, without conviction.

"At this altitude? In those boots?" I'd caught the boots through my own glass a moment later—synthetic, city-stiff, the kind that blister a man on day one and announce *outsider with money* from a kilometer away. "Keep everyone moving. Same pace. Don't look up again."

Dev looked up again, briefly, professionally, and then didn't. "Voss?"

"Who else sends suits to the middle of nowhere?" I handed the glasses back. "Nobody follows an illegal trek unless they already know about it. Which means somebody talked. Which means—"

"Which means they know it's you," Dev finished. He'd been with me three seasons, back when my seasons were legal, and he had the porter's gift for finishing sentences you didn't want completed.

I glanced at the ridge again despite myself. The figures had resumed walking—not toward us, not away, just parallel, keeping pace, patient as interest accruing. Scouts. Voss didn't send scouts for small debts. He sent scouts when he smelled something worth collecting, and he'd send the collectors after, and the collectors didn't carry binoculars. They carried other things.

My hand went, without permission, to my chest. To the map. To the letter with its block capitals and its name I had no right to know and every intention, apparently, of finding out.

*Payment,* I reminded myself. *Just payment. You dig a grave, you take the fee. That's commerce, not sentiment.*

But I walked faster after that, and I didn't stop checking the ridgeline, and somewhere below the level where I keep my lies organized, I understood that the arithmetic had changed. This wasn't a fee anymore. This was a thing two men on a ridge wanted badly enough to climb for, and things like that have a gravity all their own. They pull. They bend your route. They decide, eventually, whether you come down the mountain at all.

Behind us, the cairn shrank into the landscape, one more stone among ten thousand, marking a man who'd finally stopped running ahead of his regrets.

Ahead of us, the trail dropped toward tree line, toward rivers, toward the low green country where the map's inked channels waited—and where, I would learn soon enough and the hard way, Dario Voss's reach was longer than any mountain shadow.

The scouts kept pace until dark.

They were still there when I closed my eyes.
