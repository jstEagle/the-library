## 4. Debt Collectors

There is a particular pleasure in being owed money that I have never found in having it. Money, once possessed, is merely a fact. A debt is a story — a living thing, a rope with two ends, and as long as you hold one end of it, the other end belongs to someone who has to keep moving toward you or hang themselves trying to get away.

Wren Kavanagh owed me forty-one thousand dollars, give or take the interest I applied with a generosity that would embarrass a loan shark. That was the arithmetic. The poetry was everything else: the blacklisting in Nepal after the Annapurna mess, the quieter one in Peru that nobody ever put on paper but everybody whispered about, the permits Wren could no longer pull, the clients who no longer answered emails. I had not built all of that myself — a man's reputation does most of its own demolition if you simply refuse to catch him when he falls — but I had made sure of the timing. You don't push a person off a cliff. You just make sure there's nothing soft at the bottom, and then you wait for them to jump.

So when my office manager, Priya, knocked twice — her signal for *this will annoy you* rather than *this will ruin you*, though the categories overlap more than she'd like — I was already in a good mood. It was raining in Kathmandu, which always improved my temperament. Rain keeps tourists indoors, and tourists indoors spend money on things I sell.

"Voss," she said, "you should hear this before it gets around."

"That's what I pay you for."

"You don't pay me enough for this." She set down her tablet like it had bitten her. "One of our scouts came off the border trek early. The one we didn't sanction."

"The one Kavanagh took," I said, because I keep track of my assets even when they've stopped being mine. "Off the books. Thinks I don't know."

"He knows now. That's the problem." She pulled up a photo — grainy, long-lens, the kind of image that costs more per pixel than most people earn per hour. A camp above the treeline. Tents. And a figure sitting apart from them against a rock, wrapped in what looked like every blanket he owned, gray-faced, still.

I recognized the stillness immediately. I'd seen it once before, in a hospital corridor in Huaraz, when a man I'd insured decided the insurance wasn't worth the waiting. There's a posture the dying adopt that no healthy body can fake. They stop performing. Even the liars among them stop lying, because the audience is leaving.

"Who is he?"

"Prospector. Old-timer. Goes by Silas. The porters say he'd been up there since the passes opened, alone, coughing blood into a handkerchief like it was a hobby." She paused, and I gave her the look that means *get to the part you're afraid of*. Priya is the only employee I've ever had who can read my looks, which is either an asset or a liability depending on the decade. "The scout says Kavanagh stayed behind with him. Sent the clients on with the assistant guide. Sat with this dying stranger through the night."

"Went off-book, abandoned his paying clients, and played nursemaid to a vagrant." I turned the tablet toward me. "That doesn't sound like Kavanagh. Kavanagh doesn't do anything without calculating the angle."

"There's an angle." She hesitated again, and I confess the hesitation pleased me almost as much as what followed. "Silas offered him something. The scout couldn't get close enough to see it clearly, but the porters talked. An old map. Sealed letter. The old man said—" she checked her notes, though I suspect she knew it by heart, "—he said it was worth more than gold, and that gold was exactly what it concerned."

* * *

Let me tell you about river gold claims, because I spent that evening learning about them, and because understanding what happened next requires understanding what a piece of paper can do when the right people want it burned.

Along certain stretches of the lower rivers — monsoon country, the drowned green labyrinth where the water owns everything nine months of the year and pretends otherwise for three — land titles are less documents than rumors. Villages have farmed the same banks for generations on claims nobody ever registered, because registering meant acknowledging a government that had never done anything for them but tax. Then someone finds gold in the gravel bars, or worse, someone finds out that someone else might find gold, and suddenly those unregistered claims become the most valuable paper in the province. Whoever holds deeds — real ones, stamped ones, the kind a court honors — holds the river. Whoever holds the river holds everything that floats on it, mines in it, farms beside it, or flees along it.

And whoever holds nothing holds a debt. Which brings us back to me.

I made some calls. I am good at calls; I have a voice that opens doors and a memory that closes them behind people. By midnight I knew three things. First: a prospector named Silas had spent thirty years working the tributaries, half-legend, half-nuisance, a man who claimed more ground than he ever worked. Second: he had, at some point in his long and apparently remorseful life, secured legal title to a stretch of riverbank that at least four parties — including two men who owned boats, one who owned politicians, and one who owned me, in the sense that I owned his debts — would very much like to see dissolved. Third: he was dying, and dying men do their accounting.

The question was never whether the map existed. Rumors of that kind arrive on their own, like weather. The question was who reached it first.

Kavanagh had a head start of roughly a day and a half, a guide's legs, and — this was the detail that kept me awake, staring at the rain on the window — a reason to run. A man with nothing to lose walks. A man with forty-one thousand dollars of debt and my name stapled to it runs, but he runs where I point him, because every road he takes, I've already paid for the tolls.

Or so I believed, right up until the moment I learned he'd gone off-book without telling me, which meant he'd stopped using roads I paid for, which meant the rope had gone slack, and slack rope, in my experience, is how people slip knots.

* * *

Priya found me at dawn on the roof of the building, drinking tea I had no intention of finishing, watching the city assemble itself out of fog.

"The enforcers," she said. "You're actually sending them."

"I'm sending a conversation."

"You're sending Ferro and the twins to intercept a mountain guide over a map that may not exist."

"Ferro doesn't intercept," I said. "Ferro converses. The twins provide visual aids." I handed her the tablet back. "What did the scout say about the daughter?"

She blinked. "The what?"

"Silas. Dying men don't hand strangers maps for free. The porters said he wanted something delivered. A letter. Which means a recipient. Find me the recipient, Priya, because the recipient is the second copy of everything we want."

"We don't know he has a daughter."

"Everyone has a daughter eventually. Or a son, or a creditor, or a grave they can't rest in. Somebody is owed something by that old man, and whoever it is will come looking for the estate." I sipped the cold tea and grimaced at it, which was fair; it had earned the grimace. "Pull the trek's manifest. Check who else knew the route. Check the checkpoint logs — somebody downstream owes me favors, and I want eyes on every boat that leaves that stretch of river for the next month."

"And Kavanagh?"

"What about him?"

"You're going to have to decide what you actually want from him. The map, or the man."

It was a good question, which annoyed me. Priya asks good questions the way other people spill coffee — accidentally, and always on something important.

Here is what I told her, and here is what I will tell you, since you've come this far: I wanted both, but I have never confused wanting with needing. The map was worth a fortune. The man was worth more, though not in the way Priya meant. Wren Kavanagh was the last loose thread from a bad year — the Annapurna season, the lawsuit that settled quietly, the client who didn't come home — and threads are only dangerous when they're attached to something. Cut the thread, and the whole garment starts unraveling in ways that reach my name. Keep the thread, pull it gently, and it leads you back to every knot.

Besides — and I'll admit this under no oath — there was the matter of craft. I respect competence the way musicians respect rivals. Kavanagh read weather better than my paid forecasters, handled clients better than handlers, and survived disasters that should have been fatal with a luck so consistent it stopped being luck and became a skill. People like that don't stay broken. They mend themselves in the dark and then they bite the hand that held the needle. I had seen it before. I preferred to see it coming.

"Both," I said finally. "But the map first. Debt collects itself if you let it breathe."

* * *

Ferro came to my office at noon, which was itself a message; Ferro arrives when he decides to arrive, and I permit it because Ferro is the only man I employ who frightens me slightly, and I consider that a form of quality control.

He sat without being asked. He is a large man in the way that furniture is large — present, load-bearing, impossible to move without a plan. The twins stood by the door, identical and patient, like bookends waiting for a shelf.

"You want the guide brought in," Ferro said. Not a question. He reads briefings the way I write them — for the subtext.

"I want the map brought in. The guide is welcome to accompany it."

"And if the guide objects?"

"Then remind him of the arrangement." I slid a folder across the desk. I keep folders on everyone; it's not paranoia, it's filing. This one held the Peru material — the incident report that officially didn't exist, the witness statements that officially did, the photographs of Wren Kavanagh walking away from a base camp that was about to become a search grid. "Page three. Remind him that the Peruvian authorities reopen cases when new evidence surfaces, and that I am in possession of a great deal of evidence, all of it new, all of it surfacing at my convenience."

Ferro didn't open the folder. He never opens the folders. "He'll run."

"Of course he'll run. Running is what he does. It's practically his profession — he guides people through places, which is just running with customers." I leaned back. "But running costs money, and he's broke, and every outfitter from here to the coast knows his face. He'll need a boat. Boats need pilots. Pilots need fuel, permits, and silence, and I own the silence on that river the way other men own houses. Let him run. I want to know where he runs *to*. A man's destination tells you what he's carrying."

"And if what he's carrying is worthless? Old paper, dead man's fantasy?"

"Then I've spent a little money confirming a rumor, and Ferro, I have never once regretted spending money on a rumor. Rumors are options. Options are cheap until they're priceless." I stood, which ended meetings; it's one of the few reliable laws of physics left. "One more thing. The old man mentioned a daughter. Somewhere downstream there's a woman who may or may not know she's about to inherit a river. If Kavanagh contacts anyone — anyone — I want the contact traced before the conversation ends."

Ferro rose. At the door he paused, which he almost never does, and turned back with an expression I'd describe as professional curiosity if I thought he was capable of it.

"The guide," he said. "Kavanagh. In Peru. Was any of it true?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the city went on buying and selling, borrowing and owing, the great honest dishonesty of commerce that I have served faithfully my entire life.

"Some of it," I said. "The best lies are mostly true. That's why they work." I smiled, and I mean the smile sincerely; I have practiced it for decades and it has never once failed me. "That's also why page three works. Now go collect my debt."

They left. I stood at the window and watched the rain come down over the rooftops, thinking about rivers, about how water always finds the lowest ground and pools there, and about how everything of value in this world — gold, deeds, guilty men — eventually washes downhill to where someone patient is standing with a net.

Forty-one thousand dollars, plus interest, plus inconvenience. I added a line item for inconvenience. Wren Kavanagh would understand. It's the oldest fee in the world, and I have never met a runner yet who didn't end up paying it.

By nightfall, Ferro and the twins were on a bus east, and by morning they'd be on the river, asking questions in the gentle way Ferro asks questions, and somewhere ahead of them — wet, broke, haunted, carrying a dead man's promise in a waterproof sleeve against his chest — my debtor was about to discover that the noose I'd tied years ago had never actually loosened.

It had only been waiting for him to walk into it.

Which, knowing Wren Kavanagh, was precisely what he was doing.
