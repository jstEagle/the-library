## 5. Downriver

Three days of walking will strip a person down to the essential parts. By the second morning I'd stopped being a mountain guide with a reputation and a debt and become simply a thing that moved through trees, ate when it could, slept badly, and listened for footsteps that weren't its own.

I'd left the trek's route at a place where the trail forked around a granite shoulder, told my two remaining clients there was a landslide ahead — which was even true, in the sense that all lies are true somewhere if you walk far enough to find them — and paid them back half their fee out of the money I didn't have. The older one, a retired dentist from Pune with better boots than mine, had looked at me with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself once, facing some guide or other who was lying to me with tired eyes. He hadn't argued. He'd just said, "You're running from something," and I'd said, "I'm running toward something," which is what liars say when they want to sound like poets.

The truth was simpler: Voss's scouts had come over the ridge asking after a man carrying a map, and one of them had described me with the kind of accuracy that suggested he'd studied my file. Not my face from memory. My *file*. Which meant Voss wasn't sending boys anymore. He was sending readers.

So I went down instead of across. Down is always cheaper than across, though it costs you more later — knees, pride, altitude you'll have to buy back at interest. I dropped out of the border country through cloud forest that dripped like a bad roof, followed a stream that became a creek that became a river with opinions, and on the third afternoon walked into a town called Kamali Bazaar with blistered feet, forty-one rupees, and a map folded so many times against the rain that the creases had become part of the cartography.

Kamali Bazaar is the kind of place that exists because the river decided it should. A single street of tin-roofed shops climbing away from the water, a temple with a loudspeaker, three tea stalls competing for the same six customers, and a boatyard where old hulls went to be honest about their age. The monsoon was still a rumor — a heaviness in the air, clouds stacking up over the western hills like men waiting outside a creditor's office — but everyone in town talked about it the way people talk about a relative who's coming to stay and always stays too long.

I bought tea first. Priorities. Then I sat at the tea stall nearest the boatyard and did what I do best, which is become furniture. Invisibility isn't a talent; it's a discipline. You keep your eyes soft, your hands still, your mouth occupied with tea, and you let the town tell you about itself. Towns love to talk. They're lonely.

Here is what Kamali Bazaar told me in an hour:

One. There was a man named Prakash who owned boats and money-lent, and his rates were criminal but his discretion was professional, which made him the only game in town for someone who needed cash without questions. Two. The river below the bazaar ran through country that would flood by week's end, and every pilot worth the name was either hauling cargo upriver ahead of the water or drinking tea and waiting for it to pass. Three. And this came to me sideways, from two boatmen arguing over cards — there was one pilot who took no passengers at all, not at any price, a woman called Mara who ran supplies alone and had turned down offers that would make a sensible person weep.

A woman who refuses money. In my experience there are only three reasons a person refuses money: they have enough, they're stupid, or they're hiding from something that money makes louder. I had enough sympathy for the third category to fill a river valley.

* * *

Prakash operated out of a shop that smelled of diesel and betel, behind a counter stacked with rope coils and one glass case containing, inexplicably, a single taxidermied fish with an expression of permanent grievance. He was a small man built like a knuckle, with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair and the patient eyes of someone who has heard every hard-luck story ever invented and improved upon several of them himself.

"You want a boat." He said it the way a doctor repeats your symptoms back to you before delivering bad news.

"I want a boat."

"Boats are expensive."

"So I've heard."

He looked me over. I knew what he saw: a tall foreigner gone lean and brown, hair cut badly with a knife, clothes that had been good gear two years ago and were now a costume of themselves, and eyes that had done too much arithmetic lately. Guides get a certain look when they're finished. Dogs get it too. Prakash had probably seen a dozen of me wash through his town.

"You are trekker," he said. "Guide. From the north side." Not a question. My stomach performed a small controlled descent. "There was a man here yesterday also asking about boats. Also walking fast. He asked about you, actually."

"Did he."

"He wanted to know if a foreigner had come down from the passes. Tall. Carrying something flat." Prakash smiled with genuine warmth, which somehow made it worse. "I told him no foreigners come through Kamali except on their way to nowhere, and nowhere is not a real place, so how could anyone be going there?"

"And he believed you?"

"He believed what he paid to believe. He gave me five hundred rupees to watch for you." Prakash tapped the counter. "You can give me six hundred to forget I watched."

This is the economy of river towns, and I respect it the way I respect gravity. I put my forty-one rupees on the counter, which got a laugh out of him, and then we negotiated. What I had was a compass — a good brass one, a gift from a client whose life I'd once saved on a glacier and who never understood that I'd have saved him anyway, that it was just the job. It hurt more than I expected to slide it across the counter. Not because of its value. Because of what it meant. A compass is a promise you make to yourself about always knowing where you are. I'd been lying to mine for years.

Prakash weighed it in his palm, unimpressed by sentiment, and lent me nine thousand rupees against it plus a rate he quoted with a straight face and I accepted with a straighter one. Then he sold me a boat.

"It's called the *Lakshmi*," he said, leading me down to the water. "She is fourteen years old. She leaks a little."

"A little."

"She leaks in a way that builds character. You bail her twice an hour, she carries you anywhere. You forget to bail her, she teaches you humility." He gestured at a long shallow riverboat pulled up on the mud, painted a green that had once been cheerful and was now philosophical. "Outboard is included. Fuel is extra. Everything is extra. This is why I am rich and you are not."

I looked at the *Lakshmi*. I looked at the sky, where the clouds had continued their patient stacking. I thought about Voss's readers working their way down the valleys, methodical as erosion, and about a dead man above the treeline whose grave I'd marked with stones because I couldn't dig deep enough in frozen ground, and about a letter in my jacket that weighed nothing and everything.

"I'll take her," I said.

"Of course you will," said Prakash. "Everyone takes her. That is her tragedy."

* * *

I spent the rest of the daylight loading the boat and learning her moods. She leaked from two places as far as I could find — a seam near the bow that wept steadily, and a spot amidships that seeped only when the hull flexed, which meant whenever the river disagreed with her, which meant always. Bail twice an hour, Prakash had said. I bailed four times in the first hour and revised my opinion of both the boat and my life choices upward slightly, since at least my problems now floated.

I planned to leave at first light. Running a river at night, alone, in a boat I'd known for six hours, ahead of a monsoon, is the kind of decision that gets guides blacklisted, and I already had that particular honor twice over. But morning felt wrong. Morning felt like giving the readers another night to close the net. The river doesn't care when you launch; it only cares whether you can read it. And I could read water. Water was the one language I'd never been lied to in.

So I decided on dusk. I was checking the fuel line for the fourth time when I saw her.

She came down the bank from the upstream landing with the unhurried stride of someone who owns every step she takes, a compact woman in a salt-stiffened canvas jacket, dark hair tied back with what appeared to be electrical wire, carrying a crate of engine parts like it was a handbag. She went to a boat moored at the far end of the yard — a low grey workhorse of a craft, scarred paint, engine cowl dented and repainted, the whole vessel radiating the specific dignity of something maintained by someone who loved it without ever saying so. She loaded her crate, checked her lines, tested her engine with two pulls, and then stood at the tiller for a moment looking downriver with an expression I couldn't read from that distance and didn't try to. Some faces charge admission.

The card players from the tea stall drifted past me toward the landing, and I caught the tail of their conversation, which was apparently a continuing negotiation.

"—asked her again today," one was saying. "Four thousand to run the merchant's rice down before the water comes. Four thousand!"

"And?"

"And she said no. Same as she says to everyone. She says—" he pitched his voice higher, mimicking, "—'I don't carry passengers.'"

"Rice isn't passengers."

"That's what I told her! She said rice complains less than passengers but it's the same boat ride, and she doesn't do rides." He spat philosophically into the mud. "Woman turns down more money than I see in a season. There's something wrong with her."

"There's something wrong with everyone," said his friend. "Hers is just more profitable than ours."

They laughed and moved on, and I stood there with my hand on the *Lakshmi*'s gunwale doing math I didn't like.

Because here was the thing. I knew rivers, but I didn't know *this* river. The map showed the route Silas had taken decades ago — a tributary, a confluence, a stretch marked in his cramped hand with warnings that might as well have been written in weather: *shifting bar here*, *rock garden after high water*, *the channel moves, ask locally*. Ask locally. The dead man's own handwriting telling me I needed help. And the local help, the only pilot in town who clearly knew this water well enough to run it solo ahead of a flood, refused all passengers on principle.

I could go alone. People did. Sometimes they even arrived.

Or I could do the thing I was worst at in the world, worse than swimming in cold water, worse than apologizing: I could ask a stranger for help and mean it.

* * *

I waited until she'd finished her checks, because interrupting a pilot mid-inspection is how you get thrown off a landing, and then I walked the length of the boatyard with my best approximation of a man who is not being hunted. She watched me come. Of course she did. Pilots watch everything; it's the whole profession. Her eyes went over me the way I'd gone over the *Lakshmi* — cataloguing flaws, estimating seaworthiness, arriving at a value.

"I need a pilot," I said.

"No."

"I haven't told you where yet."

"Don't need to. Answer's the same everywhere." She turned back to her engine.

"Downriver. Past the confluence. Maybe further."

That got a pause. Small, but I was watching for it. Her hands stopped on the cowling latch for exactly one heartbeat before resuming.

"Past the confluence is flooded forest by Friday," she said. "Nobody runs it now."

"Somebody has to."

"Not my problem." She straightened and faced me fully, and up close her face was younger than her hands and older than her voice, which is a specific kind of damage I recognized professionally. "Find one of the local boys. They'll take your money and drown you cheaply."

"They don't know the water past the confluence."

"And you know that how?" Her eyes flicked, quick, to my jacket — to the shape of the folded map under the oilskin wrap. Something moved behind them. Recognition, maybe, or its cousin. "You've got paper," she said. "Old paper."

"I've got a destination."

"Everybody's got a destination. The river doesn't care." She stepped off her boat onto the planks between us, and I noted that she placed her feet exactly where the boards were sound, without looking, the way I'd place them on scree. "Whatever you're carrying, whatever you're running from — I don't take passengers. Not for money, not for favors, not for stories. Especially not for stories."

"I wasn't going to tell you a story."

"You were deciding which one." Almost a smile. Not quite. "Go home, trekker."

"I don't have one."

Something crossed her face at that — not pity, I'd have resented pity, but a flicker of the same tired arithmetic I carried. Then it closed over, smooth as water over a sunk stone.

"Then learn to swim," said Mara, and stepped back aboard, and pulled her lines in, and started her engine, and left me standing on the landing with a leaking boat, borrowed money, a dead man's map, and the distinct impression that I had just been measured by the only person on this river who could save my life and found wanting.

Above the western hills, the clouds finished their stacking and began, very quietly, to move.

I looked at the *Lakshmi*, and the *Lakshmi* leaked at me, faithful to her character. Dusk was coming. So was the rain. So, somewhere behind me on the trails, were men who read files.

Alone, then. I could do alone. Alone was the one thing I'd always been excellent at.

I untied the line, and that's when I noticed Mara's boat hadn't actually left — she'd only backed it out into the current and let it sit there, idling, holding station against the flow with small corrections of the tiller, watching me. Watching what I'd do. Testing the hull of me, the way you test any strange boat before you trust it with your life.

And I realized, with a feeling I couldn't name and didn't try to, that the answer to her question — the one she hadn't asked yet — was going to matter to her more than she wanted it to.

I raised a hand. She didn't wave back.

But she didn't leave, either.
