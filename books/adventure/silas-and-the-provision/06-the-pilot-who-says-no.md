## 6. The Pilot Who Says No

There are three kinds of people who come to a river pilot asking for passage, and I can sort them before they finish their first sentence.

The first kind are tourists. They say things like *adventure* and *authentic* and they haggle over a price that wouldn't cover my fuel, and they want their photograph taken at the wheel as if steering a boat through monsoon water were a selfie opportunity and not a trade you bleed for. I charge them double and forget their names before they're out of sight.

The second kind are desperate. They don't haggle. They count out crumpled notes with shaking hands and they pray to whatever god runs rivers that I'll say yes, and I usually do, because desperation I understand. Desperation I've been.

The third kind don't ask at all. They stand at the edge of my dock with the particular stillness of people who have learned that wanting things out loud is dangerous, and they wait for the river to decide for them.

The stranger who came up the mud path that evening was the third kind.

I was mending net when I saw them — a figure in a rain shell gone gray with wear, moving down from the town with the economy of someone who'd learned to walk quietly in places where noise costs. Not running. Running draws the eye. But moving like a person who has somewhere to be and would prefer the somewhere to remain a secret.

I kept my head down and my hands busy. First rule of the river: see everything, acknowledge nothing.

They stopped at the end of the dock, close enough that I could hear the water working at the pilings, and stood there the way you stand at a door you're not sure you have the right to knock on. I let the silence stretch. Silence is free, and I'd already priced it into my evening.

"You're the pilot," they said finally. Not a question, but not quite a statement either. A voice worn smooth at the edges, gender indeterminate in the way voices get when they've been used mostly for short sentences and long silences.

"I'm the pilot who says no," I said, and kept mending.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were about to. I could hear it from here. It sounds like *downriver, how fast, how much*."

A pause. When they spoke again there was something almost like a smile in it, dry as old rope. "You get a lot of people asking?"

"I get a lot of people. The asking is always the same." I bit the twine, tied off the knot, and looked up.

And that was my mistake, looking up. Because the stranger had a face like a river map — every line leading somewhere, most of the routes marked *dangerous* — and the eyes above it were haunted in a way I recognized the way you recognize weather. Not scared. Not guilty, exactly. Haunted the way you get when you've carried something too long and it's grown into the carrying, when you couldn't set it down now without part of you coming off with it.

I knew those eyes. I saw them in the mirror some mornings, before the river fog burned off.

"Downriver," they said. "How fast, how much."

I laughed before I could stop myself. It came out rusty, like a winch that hadn't turned in a season. "You've got nerve, I'll give you that."

"I've got money. Not much of it, but some." They crouched, keeping their weight off the old planks, and I noticed the hands — rope-scarred, mountain hands, the hands of someone whose element was rock and ice and who was a long way from home. "And I need to move. Tonight, if possible. Before the front comes in."

I looked at the sky. It had been bruising purple all afternoon over the western ridges, the monsoon front stacking itself up like a debt coming due. Any pilot with sense was tying down tarps and doubling mooring lines, not planning a run.

"Nobody runs ahead of that front," I said.

"Nobody with sense."

"And you?"

"I stopped having sense somewhere around the second time my life fell apart." They said it flatly, no self-pity in it, which was somehow worse. Pity I could have dismissed. This was just a person reporting facts about a ruined landscape.

"Where downriver?" I asked, and hated myself for asking, because asking was the first plank of a yes.

The stranger hesitated. Then they crouched lower, and from inside the shell they brought out a folded square of oilcloth, and from the oilcloth a paper — old, soft at the folds, hand-drawn. They opened it on their knee, careful as a man handling a bird with a broken wing.

And my hands stopped moving.

I knew that river.

Not the way you know a river from a chart. The way you know a face. The way you know the sound a specific set of rapids makes at specific water, the way you know which bank the fig trees lean from, the way you know a bend where your father once — 

I made myself breathe.

The map was old. The ink had gone brown. The hand that drew it was confident and a little show-offy, the way his hand always was, flourishes on the north arrow, little marginal notes in a slanted script. *Good water here in low season. Careful — the channel shifts after floods. There's a fishing family past the second bend, good people, tell them Silas sent you.*

Silas.

The name went through me like cold water through a wetsuit. I hadn't heard it spoken in — I did the arithmetic and didn't like the answer. Twenty years, near enough. Twenty years since a tall man with a prospector's burn and a laugh too big for the boat sat on the dock where this stranger was crouching and told me about the river he'd found, the river he'd drawn, the river he'd promised — 

He'd promised a lot of things. That was the whole problem with Silas Vane. The promises were real when he made them. It was the keeping that he couldn't manage.

"You know this water," the stranger said. It wasn't a question either. They'd been watching my face, and my face had betrayed me, and I could have cursed myself for it. "You went still when you saw the second bend."

"I went still because you're dripping river water on a hand-drawn map, and whoever drew it would be annoyed."

"Whoever drew it is dead."

The words landed between us and lay there.

I looked at the stranger again, properly this time, and the haunted eyes made a new kind of sense. That's what that look was. That's what carrying a dead man's paper does to you.

"Sorry," they said, and for the first time the flat voice cracked, just a hairline. "That was — I've been saying that sentence too long. It comes out wrong."

"How did he die?"

"Slow. Cold. Above the treeline, in a camp he couldn't get out of." The stranger folded the map along its worn creases, reverent, and I watched their hands and thought: *you didn't know him, but you're holding him like you did.* "He gave me this. And a letter. And a job to do with them. That's all I know, and half of it I wish I didn't."

The letter.

Something turned over in my chest, slow and heavy, like a hull taking water.

"Where downriver?" I asked again, because I apparently hadn't learned anything from the first time.

The stranger gave me the name of a village I'd last seen from a boat when I was nineteen years old, sitting beside a man who pointed at the far bank and said, *that's where I'll put the house, Mara-girl. When I get the claim worked. That's where you'll grow tomatoes and hate me properly, in person, instead of from a distance.*

I said no.

I said it the way I say everything, which is quickly and in a voice that doesn't invite appeals. "No. Find another pilot."

"There are no other pilots running that stretch. Not in this season. Not with this water." They rose, stiff, and I saw how tired they were, tired down in the bone, the tiredness that money doesn't fix. "You're the only one who knows it."

"Then I suppose you'll drown in it. That's a river's business, not mine."

"Fair," they said. And — this was the infuriating part — they didn't argue. They folded the map away, tucked it into the oilcloth with the same maddening gentleness, and stood there a moment looking at my boat. My *Kestrel*, forty years old, patched in six places, the truest thing I've ever loved. "She's a good hull," the stranger said. "You keep her well."

"I keep her well because I don't take fools and fugitives out in her ahead of storm fronts. Good night."

They nodded. They actually nodded, as if I'd given them useful information, and walked back up the mud path toward the town, unhurried, and I sat on my dock with my mended net in my lap and told myself the evening had gone exactly as I'd wanted it to.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about saying no: sometimes it's the yes that's hiding underneath, wearing a disguise.

I cooked rice I didn't taste. I checked the mooring lines twice, then a third time, which is a thing I do when I'm lying to myself about something, because my hands always know before I do. The front came closer. You could feel it in the way the air lay on the skin, thick and electric, and in the way the river had gone strangely quiet, holding its breath the way rivers do before they're asked to do too much.

I got out the tin box.

It sits at the bottom of my sea chest under a false bottom, and I open it maybe twice a year, and I hate that I opened it that night. Inside: a photograph gone soft at the corners, a man and a girl on a boat deck, both squinting, both laughing. A river stone, gray, veined with white, that he'd said came from the headwaters of the very river on that stranger's map. And a letter, unopened, postmarked eleven years ago, forwarded through four towns before it found me — because Silas Vane couldn't even abandon a person accurately.

I hadn't opened it. Eleven years, and I hadn't opened it. There is a kind of anger that you keep the way you keep a pilot light: small, constant, and the only thing keeping the room from going completely dark. Opening the letter felt like it might put the light out.

The stranger had said *a letter*. Carrying one. Delivering one, maybe, to someone downriver, to someone in a village whose name I hadn't heard said out loud in twenty years, and the coincidence sat in my chest like a swallowed hook.

Because it wasn't a coincidence. I'd known that on the dock. The map was his hand; the route was his route; the destination was the place he'd pointed at and promised. Some part of me had known before the stranger finished unfolding it, and had said no anyway, because saying no to Silas Vane, even dead, even secondhand, even through a stranger with haunted eyes — saying no was the one skill he'd left me fully trained in.

I went out and sat on the dock.

The river talked to itself in the dark. Downstream, maybe three days at speed, there was a village where an old woman named Auntie Rosa still owed me a debt of rice and gossip, where the fig trees leaned the way they leaned, where a channel shifted after every flood exactly the way a dead man's marginal note said it did. And somewhere between here and there was a front coming down out of the mountains with a hundred miles of rain in its pockets, and somewhere behind the stranger, I'd have bet my boat, were men whose footsteps I could almost hear, because nobody walks that quietly toward a dock at dusk unless they're walking away from something worse.

I thought about the way they'd held the map. Like a bird with a broken wing. Like something that wasn't theirs but had been entrusted to them, which is a heavier thing to carry than anything that's actually yours.

I thought: *not my business.*

I thought: *the river doesn't care whose business it is. The river just runs. Everything on it either floats or doesn't.*

I thought about a tall man with a laugh too big for the boat, and a promise about tomatoes, and the particular flavor of a girl's hatred at nineteen, which is the purest and most stupid fuel there is, and how I'd burned it for twenty years and it still wasn't out, and how tonight, for the first time, it had flickered — because the stranger had said *he's dead* in a voice that cracked, and dead men can't keep promises, and dead men also can't break them again, and I didn't know which of those facts made my chest hurt more.

The first rain came at midnight. Big, slow, scattered drops, the scouts ahead of the army, hitting the tin roof and the river and my shoulders like a question being asked three times.

I went inside. I took the tin box out again. I held the unopened letter over the lamp for a long time, turning it, reading my own name on it — *Mara Vane*, in that slanted, show-offy hand — and put it back unopened, because some doors you only get to open once, and I wanted to be standing on dry ground when I opened mine.

Then I got my charts out, and my tide tables, and I did a thing I hadn't done in years: I planned a run. Downriver. Through the second bend, past the fishing family, up the shifting channel, to a village whose name I said out loud, alone, in a boat that smelled of lamp oil and rain, just to hear what it did to my mouth.

It tasted like floodwater and tomato vines and a promise twenty years drowned.

The rain found its rhythm. The river rose a hand's width by two in the morning.

And at dawn, when I heard boots on my dock — more than one set, heavy, unhurried, the walk of men who get paid to walk toward people — I was already standing in the doorway of my own boat with my arms crossed, and I knew, before I saw them, before the stranger came up the path at a half-run behind them with that haunted look gone sharp and cornered, exactly how my no was about to turn into something else entirely.

The men reached the dock. The stranger shouted my name — my actual name, which I had never told them.

And I picked up my boat hook.
