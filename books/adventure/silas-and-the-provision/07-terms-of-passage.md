## 7. Terms of Passage

They found me at the fish market, which was either an insult or a compliment. An insult, because I'd chosen it precisely because nobody respectable went there before dawn—the stalls reeking of mud and diesel, the vendors haggling in three languages over catfish that had been dead too long to argue about. A compliment, because it meant Voss's men were better than I'd given them credit for. Two of them, working the crowd like a net closing, and by the time I spotted the first one's face—broad, sunburned, wearing the patient expression of a man who'd done this enough times to enjoy the waiting—I already knew where the second one stood.

Behind me. Of course.

I did the math without wanting to. The market backed onto a drainage channel, the water black and slow. One exit past the enforcer at my back, and he'd be standing on it. The crowd was thinning as the morning heat came up, and thin crowds don't hide you; they just make you easier to point at.

"Wren Kavanagh." The voice behind me was mild, almost friendly, which is how Voss trained them. Shouting attracts attention. Courtesy gets cooperation. "Dario would like a word."

"Dario can write me a letter," I said, and kept walking toward the fish stalls like a man with somewhere to be.

He fell into step beside me instead of stopping me, and that was worse. He was the one they called Ferro—not because of any iron in him that I could see, but because Voss liked names that sounded like tools. He had a face like a closed door and hands that hung at his sides with the loose readiness of someone who'd learned to fight on riverbanks, where there are no rules except gravity.

"You've been hard to find," Ferro said.

"I've been easy to find. You've been slow."

That earned me half a smile. "The old man on the mountain. Silas. You were seen with him. Two days before he died." He said it conversationally, the way you'd mention weather. "People talk, up there. Herders. The trekking party you abandoned—they talked too. Interesting story they told. A guide who takes his fee and then walks off into the fog carrying nothing but a dead man's map."

"I carried a dead man's promise," I said, and immediately hated myself for it, because it was true and truth is a thing you don't hand to men like Ferro.

His smile widened by a degree. "A map. Yes. Dario's very interested in maps. Especially ones that show old claims along the lower river. Gold country, from before the flood surveys. There are men who'd pay a great deal for paper like that." He stopped walking, and I had no choice but to stop with him, because the second enforcer had drifted close enough now that continuing forward meant walking into his chest. "Or," Ferro went on, pleasantly, "there are men who'd pay a great deal to see Wren Kavanagh delivered to certain authorities in two countries, with documentation, testimony, and a boat ticket. Dario keeps both options open. It's what makes him such a reasonable business partner."

"I'm not his partner."

"No," Ferro agreed. "You're his investment. And investments that run get collected." He held out one broad hand, palm up. Patient. Courteous. "The map, Wren. Then we'll talk about your debt like civilized people. Maybe even reduce it. Dario's generous when he's getting what he wants."

Here's the thing about being cornered: your body knows before your mind admits it. My pulse was doing arithmetic I didn't like. The map was sewn into the lining of my jacket, against my ribs, and it felt suddenly less like paper and more like a target. I thought about handing it over. I want to be honest about that—I genuinely considered it. Hand it over, watch Ferro burn it or pocket it, negotiate whatever crumbs of freedom remained, and go back to being nobody, which had been the whole plan since Nepal. Invisibility is safety. That was the creed. That was the whole religion.

But Silas had died looking at me with eyes gone the color of glacier melt, and made me say the words out loud, and I have a defect—you can call it a flaw, I call it a defect—that once I say a thing, I can't un-say it. Not to myself. Not ever.

"I don't have it," I said.

Ferro sighed, the way a teacher sighs at a student who's bright but lazy. "Wren. We watched you buy a boat yesterday. A leaking one, from a man who laughed all the way to the bank. Leaking boats need pilots. Pilots cost money you don't have." He tilted his head. "You're going downriver. To the gold country. With a dead prospector's map. And you're telling me you don't have it."

"The boat's for fishing," I said.

"Of course it is."

And that's when the boat hook came off the rack.

I didn't see her arrive—I never saw Mara arrive anywhere, she moved through crowds the way current moves under ice—but I heard the metal scrape free of the drying racks by the channel, and then the second enforcer, the quiet one behind me, made a sound like a man discovering that his knee bends the wrong way now. He sat down in the mud among the fish guts, holding his leg and reconsidering his career.

Ferro turned, and Mara was there, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe accurately what she looked like in that moment. Not tall. Not big. Just absolutely certain. She held the hook the way other people hold opinions—with total commitment and no interest in discussion—and she planted its butt end on the cobbles between herself and Ferro like a border marker.

"Market rules," she said. Her voice was flat as still water. "No collections on the docks. Ask anyone."

"This isn't the docks," Ferro said.

"It's my town." She let that sit. "Same thing."

I want to note that I had no idea why she was doing this. As far as I knew, she'd refused me passage twice, insulted my boat, my planning, and my general fitness for life, and had made it abundantly clear that my existence was a problem she intended not to have. Yet here she was, holding off Voss's best like a lock gate holds a flood, and the only explanation I had was that some people hate wolves more than they dislike strays.

Ferro looked at her, then at his partner sitting in the muck, then at me, recalculating. Men like Ferro aren't brave; they're efficient. Efficiency says: not here, not now, not worth the noise. "This isn't finished, Kavanagh," he said. "Dario doesn't forget. And neither do rivers. Everything that floats comes downstream eventually." He straightened his jacket, stepped around Mara with exaggerated care, helped his partner up, and walked out of the market at a pace designed to look unhurried.

The whole crowd pretended not to have watched. That's river towns for you. Everyone sees everything and no one saw anything.

* * *

She didn't speak until we reached her boat—a working pilot skiff tied up below the fish stalls, low in the water, engine cowling scarred with old repairs. She stowed the boat hook, coiled a line, did three small unnecessary tasks with her hands while deciding whether to throw me in the channel.

"Why?" I asked.

"Because I hate them," she said. "Not you. Them. There's a difference." She finally turned and looked at me, and her eyes did that thing they do—measuring, weighing, finding you lighter than advertised. "You're going downriver. Past the checkpoint, past the narrows, into monsoon country, alone, in a boat that leaks, with those men hunting you. You'll die by Thursday."

"It's a sturdy boat."

"It's a coffin with a paint job." She wiped her hands on her thighs, a gesture I'd learn meant she was about to commit to something she resented. "So here's how this goes. I'll take you downriver. I know every channel, every sandbar, every place the water lies about itself. I'll get you where you're going faster than that tub of yours could float there."

"In exchange for?"

"Money," she said, and then, seeing my face, something flickered behind her eyes—irritation, maybe, or honesty. "Half up front. Half after. Standard rates, plus hazard pay, because I'm ferrying a man with a price on him through a flood season."

"That's everything I have."

"Then it's everything you owe." She shrugged. "But money's the small part. Here's the large part, and if you break it, I put you off mid-channel wherever we happen to be, and you can swim your promises to shore." She stepped closer, and up close she smelled of diesel and rain and something green, like crushed leaves. "You don't ask me anything. Not where I learned the river. Not why I'm running this route in flood season when every sane pilot is tying up. Not who I am, not where I've been, not what I left. Nothing. You ride my boat, you haul lines when I tell you, you keep your mouth shut about everything except water and weather. Those are the terms. Take them or drown on your own."

It was, I reflected, the most honest contract I'd been offered in years. No fine print, no leverage dressed up as friendship. Just a wall with a door in it, and the door only opened one way.

"What's downriver?" I asked. "For you? Why does a pilot with a good reputation run a suicide route with a stranger?"

Her jaw tightened. "That," she said, "is exactly the kind of question the terms cover."

"Right." I looked at the water, black and patient, already thinking like a river—finding the way around, always around. "One counter-term."

Her eyebrows went up a fraction. Nobody negotiated with her, apparently. "Go on."

"When we're through—if we're through—I'll pay you everything I have, and it won't be enough, and I'll know it. So add this: whatever happens down there, whatever you see, whatever I'm carrying—" I touched my jacket, where the map lay against my ribs like a second heartbeat, "—you don't ask either. Not what it is. Not who gave it to me. Not why a man with debts like mine turns down money he desperately needs. You steer, I stay silent. Symmetry."

For a long moment she just studied me, and I had the uncomfortable sense of being read like water depth—soundings taken, hazards noted, chart amended. Whatever she found, she didn't share it.

"Symmetry," she repeated, tasting the word. "Fine. But understand something, Kavanagh. I'm not your friend. I'm not your rescue. I'm the reason you're not dead in a fish market, and that buys you a seat and my silence. Nothing else."

"Understood."

"And if those men come aboard—"

"They won't come aboard."

"If they do," she said, "I'll handle it. But you should know I've thrown passengers overboard before, and I slept fine after."

"I believe you completely," I said, "and it's the most reassuring thing anyone's told me all year."

Almost—almost—a smile. It crossed her face like a fish shadow under the surface, there and gone, and I understood I'd probably imagined it. She untied the line and nodded at my leaking coffin of a boat moored further down.

"Bring yours. We'll tow it. Spare parts, spare hull, spare everything. If it sinks, it sinks behind us where it won't take us down too." She swung aboard her skiff and looked at me across the widening gap of dark water. "We launch tonight, ahead of the front. The barometer's falling and the clouds over the ridge are stacked like bad news. Once we're on the water, there's no dock, no market, no town until the lower reaches. Just the river and whatever it decides."

"And what does it usually decide?"

She started the engine, and the sound filled the space between us, ending the conversation before it began.

"It decides," she said, "and we find out."

* * *

I spent the afternoon preparing for a journey I might not survive, which is most afternoons, honestly, but this one had weight to it. I drained my remaining cash into fuel, rope, tar for the worst of my hull's leaks, and food that would survive damp. I wrote no letters, because there was no one to write to, and that fact sat differently than usual—less like freedom, more like a room with the furniture gone.

At dusk I stood on the bank and watched the sky do what Mara had promised. The clouds over the western ridge had built themselves into a wall, blue-black and boiling, and the air had that pressed, electric feel of a world holding its breath. Somewhere upstream, the mountains were already drowning. All of that water was coming down here, looking for us, patient as debt.

Voss's patience, the river's patience. Everything in my life had become a matter of things closing in.

Mara appeared beside me without announcing herself, as usual, a canvas bag over her shoulder and the boat hook across her back like a rifle.

"We launch in an hour," she said. "Sleep on the boat. Eat now. And Kavanagh—" she paused, and for a moment the flat certainty cracked just enough to show something underneath, something tired and old and guarded, "—whatever you're carrying, whoever's chasing you for it, I hope it's worth what it costs. Because downriver, everything costs. The river doesn't take payment. It takes collateral."

"I know," I said. "I've been paying collateral for years."

She looked at me a moment longer than necessary, like she was filing that away in some ledger of her own, then walked down to her skiff without another word.

I followed. Behind us the storm light faded, and ahead the river ran black and fast toward the sea, carrying everything it took and keeping nothing, which struck me, as I stepped aboard, as the opposite of my problem.

My problem was that I kept everything—every name, every debt, every promise—and it was starting to sink me.

The engine coughed, caught, and held. Mara took us out into the current, bow pointed downriver, and the last lights of the town slid away astern like something I owed.

By morning, I'd learn what the first rapid could do. By morning, the terms of our passage would be written in white water.

But that night there was only the river, the rain beginning far off like applause we hadn't earned, and the woman at the wheel who'd saved my life for reasons she refused to explain—and who was steering us, though neither of us knew it yet, straight toward hers.
