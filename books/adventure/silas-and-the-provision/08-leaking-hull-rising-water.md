## 8. Leaking Hull, Rising Water

**WREN**

The sky over the river town had gone the color of a bruise that morning, and by the time we loaded the boat it had graduated to something worse—the green-black of a sky that has decided, personally, to ruin your week.

Mara stood on the dock with her arms crossed, watching me stow crates like she was grading an exam I hadn't studied for.

"You're loading wrong," she said.

"I'm loading fine."

"You're loading like a trekker. Weight high, weight aft. On this river, in this weather, you load low and forward or you'll be swimming before the second bend." She stepped past me, took the crate out of my hands, and set it where she wanted it, which was apparently everywhere I hadn't put it. "Also, your tarp is a joke."

"The tarp is fine."

"The tarp is decorative." She yanked it off, refolded it into something half the size and twice as useful, and threw it back at my chest. "Where did you learn to run water?"

"Where did you learn to be pleasant?"

She almost smiled. Almost. It was the closest thing to warmth she'd produced since the boat hook incident, and I'd learned to treasure these moments the way a man treasures coins he knows are counterfeit.

The boat was called the *Perla*, which was optimistic, because there was nothing pearl-like about her. She was maybe seven meters of river-worn aluminum with an outboard older than both of us combined, a hull that had been patched so many times the patches had patches, and a bilge pump that worked on what Mara described as "a philosophy of intermittent effort." I had paid for her with borrowed money from a lender whose smile I already regretted, and every hour she sat at the dock she was costing me interest I couldn't pronounce.

But she floated. That morning, anyway.

We launched ahead of the front because Mara said we had no choice, and when Mara says you have no choice, you tend to believe her. The barometer—or her knee, or the birds, or whatever ancient instrument she actually trusted—had told her the monsoon would break tonight, and everything downstream of us would turn into a brown, snarling artery. Better to run ahead of it than inside it.

"Better to drown in daylight," I said, casting off.

"Better to drown moving," she corrected, and opened the throttle.

The town fell away behind us—the fish market, the leaning customs house, the lender's office where I imagined his smile widening as the interest compounded—and the river took us. It was wide here, sluggish, the color of milk tea, carrying whole trees sideways like it was bored and looking for something to do with them. The banks were low and green and crowded with palms leaning over the water like gossips.

For about two hours, it was almost peaceful. I sat amidships with the map tube across my knees and let the engine's drone do the thinking for me.

That was a mistake. Mara noticed immediately.

"Put that away."

"Put what away?"

"You know what. The map. You've been petting it since the dock." Her eyes stayed on the channel ahead, but her voice had an edge on it now, freshly honed. "You keep touching that tube like it's going to hatch."

"It's just paper."

"Nobody runs a flooded river with armed men behind them for *just paper*." She eased off the throttle as the channel narrowed around a sandbar, threading us between two deadheads sticking up like drowned fingers. "So either you tell me what's on it, or I start guessing, and I promise my guesses are less flattering than the truth."

I looked at the back of her head. At the set of her shoulders. At the way her hands rested on the tiller like they'd grown there.

"It's a route," I said. "River route. To some land upcountry."

"What kind of land?"

"The kind someone died next to."

That landed. I watched it land—a small stillness in her spine, a fractional slowing of the boat as her thumb eased on the throttle without her deciding to. Then she caught herself and pushed the speed back up, angry at herself for the slip.

"Someone died," she repeated flatly.

"An old prospector. Above the treeline, stranded. His party was gone, his legs were done, and he had a map and a letter and one request." I turned the tube over in my hands. "I'm delivering them."

"To who?"

"That's between him and the addressee."

"So you don't know."

"I know enough."

"Do you?" She glanced back at me then, and her eyes were doing that thing they did—measuring, weighing, finding me lighter than I claimed. "Because here's what I know, Wren Kavanagh. I know men who carry maps like that don't deliver them. They sell them. They trade them. They get cornered by whoever's chasing them—which, by the way, those two at the dock yesterday were not tourists—and they hand it over to save their own skin. That's the natural life cycle of a map like that. So either you're different, or you're lying, and I haven't decided which yet."

"And if I'm different?"

"Then I'll be pleasantly surprised, which will be a novel experience for me."

The rain started an hour later—not the monsoon itself but its advance scouts, fat drops that hit the water like thrown stones and raised little craters all around us. The light went strange and underwater-colored even though we weren't under it yet. Somewhere behind us, the sky went fully black along the ridge line, a wall of it, advancing at walking pace with all the patience of a thing that has never once been late.

Mara looked at that wall for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, in a voice stripped of all the armor: "We're not going to outrun it. We're going to arrive *with* it. There's a difference, and the difference is that we'll be wet."

* * *

**MARA**

He didn't know boats. That was the first thing. He was strong and quick and he'd saved our cargo in a moment I hadn't asked him to care about, but he didn't know water, and water doesn't forgive ignorance—it invoices it.

By mid-afternoon the Perla was taking water.

Not dramatically. Not the romantic ship-in-the-storm kind. Just a slow, patient seep along the port seam, the old patch weeping where the flex of the hull in chop had worked it loose, filling the bilge at a rate that was insulting rather than alarming. The pump ran. The pump rested. The pump contemplated its philosophy. The water rose.

Wren found it the way he found most things—by sitting down in it.

"We're sinking," he announced.

"We're seeping," I said. "Sinking is louder."

He bailed while I drove, which was the correct division of labor, and I'll grant him this: he bailed without complaint, with a dented cooking pot, in rhythm, like a man who understood that panic is just wasted strokes. The rain thickened around us until the far bank dissolved and the world contracted to a circle of gray water maybe fifty meters across, with us at the center of it, and the black wall of the storm behind us eating the horizon one kilometer at a time.

"Talk to me," he shouted over the rain. "About the river. Where does it get ugly?"

"Everywhere. It gets ugly everywhere. But specifically—" I pointed through the murk. "Past the confluence, the channel splits. Left side is deep and fast and has a ferry wreck in it nobody ever salvaged. Right side is shallow and slow and full of logs coming down with the flood. And directly ahead of both options is a wall of rain, so pick your poison."

"Which would you pick?"

"Neither. I'd pull into the side channel at Kilometer Forty and wait out the worst of it."

"We can't wait. They're behind us."

"They're *behind* us," I agreed, "in a truck, on a road, in this. Nobody drives enforcement boats into the leading edge of a monsoon. Even Voss's people have survival instincts. Probably. It's rumored."

He kept bailing. But I could feel him turning it over, the same way I'd been turning it over since the dock—the question underneath the question. What was on that map that made two hard men drive three days to ask polite questions about a stranger? What was worth this?

So I asked it. Out loud. Because I was tired and wet and the truth was I'd been circling it for two days like a bird over a fish.

"What's really on the map, Wren?"

The bailing stopped. One second, two. Rain drummed on the aluminum roof of the world.

"Land deeds," he said finally. "That's what Silas told me. Deeds to claims along this river. Old ones. Legal ones."

"Deeds to gold claims."

"He didn't say gold."

"Everyone says gold," I said. "Nobody carries a map through hell for grazing rights." I eased us around a floating mat of hyacinth the size of a tennis court. "Deeds like that, on a river like this—they're worth more than the gold ever was. A paper claim beats a shovel claim every time. Whoever holds those papers owns the argument, and arguments on this river get settled with money or blood, usually both."

"Then it's good I'm not selling them."

"You say that now."

"I say that always. It's the one consistent thing about me."

I laughed. I hadn't meant to—it came out sideways, surprised out of me, and I hated it, and I think he heard me hate it, and something in the air between us shifted half a degree warmer and neither of us acknowledged it, which was its own kind of agreement.

* * *

**WREN**

The side channel at Kilometer Forty was barely wider than the boat, a green throat of water sliding off the main river between walls of bamboo and fig, and Mara took us into it at speed without slowing down, which told me everything about how many times she'd done it. The rain followed us in, softer under the canopy, drumming on leaves instead of skulls.

We tied off to a fig trunk the size of a cathedral pillar and sat in the leaking boat and listened to the storm arrive.

It arrived the way a freight train arrives if the freight train were made entirely of water and intent. First the wind, bending the bamboo flat, then the sound—vast, white, total—then the rain itself, a solid thing, a curtain, erasing the main river, erasing the far bank, erasing the idea of a world beyond ten meters. Lightning walked along the ridge behind us, unhurried, counting.

Under the canopy it was dim as evening. Mara rigged the tarp—her fold, not mine, and it held, which I chose not to mention—and we ate cold rice and dried fish and passed a canteen back and forth, and the water in the bilge crept up another finger-width, and the pump slept on.

"Your turn," she said.

"My turn for what?"

"Stories. It's a long night and you know everything about me that a person can know, which is nothing, and I know you were blacklisted in Nepal and Peru and you owe Voss more than you'll admit and you talk to that map tube like it's a relative. That's an unfair distribution."

"That's not stories, that's a dossier."

"A dossier with gaps." She pulled her knees up, and in the green half-light she looked younger and older at once, the way rivers look. "Start anywhere. Start with why a mountain guide ends up owing an outfitter his soul."

I considered lying. It was my default setting, and I was good at it. But there was something about the rain, the total-ness of it, the way it erased everything outside the boat, that made lying feel like extra work.

"I took a client up Kangchung in bad conditions because he paid triple," I said. "He wanted the summit photo for his company's annual report. Weather turned. We got down, but a porter broke his leg on the descent—badly, badly enough he doesn't guide anymore either. The outfitter's insurance called it negligence. My negligence. The client denied asking for the push. Voss owned the debt after the lawsuit, and Voss buys debts the way other men buy furniture." I turned the canteen in my hands. "Nepal blacklisted me. Peru did it later, for a smaller sin with a bigger price. After that, the only work left was the work that doesn't have paperwork. Which is how you end up above the treeline with a dying stranger, because nobody legal would hire you to be there."

Mara was quiet for a while. The rain filled the silence the way water fills a hull—completely, patiently.

"The porter," she said. "Do you send money?"

"Every month. It's why the debt never shrinks."

Something moved across her face—not softness exactly, more like a recalculation. She'd built a model of me, I realized, sometime during the boat hook and the dock and the first rapid of conversation, and the model had just failed a stress test, and she was annoyed about it in the specific way of a person who hates being wrong.

"You could have sold the map," she said. "In the town. Before you found me. Someone would have bought it. You'd be free."

"I could have."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

And there it was, the real question, the one she'd been steering toward all day through all the questions about gold and deeds and natural life cycles of maps. Why. Why does a drowning man hold onto someone else's promise instead of using it as a raft.

I looked at the map tube between us, rain-hummed and shadowed, and I gave her the truest thing I had.

"Because he asked," I said. "And because I've spent ten years being the man who doesn't keep promises, and I wanted—I *wanted*—" I stopped, restarted. "There's a version of me that delivers this. I've never met him. I thought maybe, if I did this one thing, I'd get to."

Mara stared at me for a long time. The lightning lit her once, twice, blue-white, and in the flashes her expression was doing something complicated that I couldn't read and she wouldn't explain.

"That's the stupidest reason I've ever heard," she said.

"I know."

"It's a terrible business plan."

"I know."

"It's—" She stopped. Looked away, out at the rain, at the bamboo bent double, at the black wall of the storm that had swallowed the entire world. When she spoke again her voice was lower, rougher, aimed somewhere past me. "My father used to say a promise is the only cargo that gets heavier the longer you carry it. He said it like it was a warning."

"Was it?"

"I used to think so." She reached down, found the bail pot, and handed it to me. "Bilge," she said, and that was the end of the story, the door closing as smoothly as it had opened.

I bailed. She watched the channel mouth. The rain came down like judgment, and the Perla seeped beneath us, patient as a debt.

It was near midnight, by the lightning-clock, when she stiffened. Not much. Just her shoulders, and the tilt of her head, the way a dog goes still before the thunder arrives.

"Wren," she said. "Kill the lamp."

I killed the lamp.

Down the black throat of the side channel, far off, small and getting smaller—or closer and getting bigger, impossible to tell—there was a light. An engine light. Running dark except for that one pinprick, moving against the current, moving upstream.

Moving toward us.

Mara's hand found my wrist in the dark and gripped it, and her whisper was barely breath: "Nobody comes up this channel at night in a monsoon. Nobody. Unless they're following somebody."

The pinprick of light swung once, like an eye opening.

Then it began to grow.
