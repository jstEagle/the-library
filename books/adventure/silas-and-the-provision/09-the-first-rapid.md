## 9. The First Rapid

There are two kinds of people who run rivers at night: the desperate and the dead. The dead have the better excuse. At least they're not frightened.

I was somewhere between the two categories when I cut the engine and let the current take us, and I want it on the record that I knew exactly how stupid this was. I'd run the Serra Narrows maybe forty times in daylight, twice at dusk with a full moon and a prayer, never once blind in the dark with a hull that leaked like a confession and a passenger who lied about why he was on my boat.

"Engine?" Wren asked from the bow. His voice had that flat calm I'd learned to distrust—the calm of someone who has decided that panic is a luxury item.

"Intake's fouled. Weeds or worse." I lifted the lantern so he could see my face, which was doing its best impression of a woman who had everything under control. "We go through on current alone."

"In the dark."

"The dark's not the problem. The dark is honest. It's the river that lies."

He didn't laugh. He'd stopped laughing around the time the first thunderhead rolled over the ridge an hour back, and honestly I preferred him silent. Talking passengers were like loose cargo—eventually something got dented.

The Narrows began where the valley pinched shut between two walls of black basalt, and the river, insulted by the constriction, turned into something with opinions. In daylight you could read it: the green tongue down the left side, the hole below the second bend that would flip a canoe for sport, the eddy behind the split rock where you could catch your breath if you caught it right. I knew all of it. I'd known all of it since I was nineteen years old and too stubborn to die.

But knowing a rapid in daylight and running it blind are two different marriages to the same woman.

"Paddle's under your seat," I said. "When I say left, you paddle like your life depends on it, because statistically it might."

"And when you say right?"

"That means we've already made a mistake and I'm trying to make it interesting."

I felt him shift, finding the paddle in the dark. There was a competence to the way he moved—not guide-competence, mountain-competence, all economy and no flourish—and it annoyed me how much I noticed it. You don't notice things about passengers. Passengers are freight that talks.

The roar came up to meet us before the white water did, that deep throat-sound the Narrows made when it was feeling itself. My hands did what twenty years of hands do: found the tiller, found the angle, found the line I couldn't see but could feel in my teeth. Left of the black rock. Trust the tongue. Don't look at the hole, because the river will give you exactly what you stare at.

"Hold on," I said, and then the world went sideways into noise.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rapids at night: they are mostly sound. The water hits the hull in a language without words, and your body translates faster than your mind can. A long smooth hiss—good, we're on the tongue. A sudden hollow boom against the portside—bad, that's a wave standing up where it shouldn't be. And then the worst sound, the one I'll hear in my sleep until I die: a grinding scrape along the keel, shallow rock, the boat lifting, tilting—

"Wren—"

"I've got it." He wasn't shouting. That was the strange thing. Everything else was screaming—water, wind, the boat complaining in every joint—but his voice came through level as a spirit gauge. "Weight left. All of it. Now."

I threw myself left because my body believed him before my brain did. The hull came off the rock with a shudder that I felt in my spine, and then we were through the first gate and into the churning middle section where the river quit pretending to be navigable.

The lantern had gone out somewhere in the chaos. Total dark now, except for the pale ghost-line of foam, and the lightning strobing far upstream like a photographer who kept missing the shot.

"Cargo," he said. "It's shifting."

"It can shift. It's rope and rice."

"It's sliding toward the starboard gunwale. If it goes over, we list, and if we list—" He paused, and I heard him doing arithmetic. "Where's the next hole?"

"Below the second bend. Thirty seconds, maybe less."

"Then I need thirty seconds."

And here is the part I still don't fully believe, and I was there. He let go of the paddle. In the middle of the Serra Narrows at night, in flood water, he let go of the only thing keeping us steerable, crawled aft over the shifting cargo like the boat was a hillside and he was descending it, and started re-lashing the load by feel. I heard the rope creak, heard him working knots blind—I know knots, and those weren't passenger knots, those were the kind you learn when a mistake costs fingers—and I put every ounce of myself into the tiller and the current and the memory of a river I'd been running since before he was born, probably.

The second bend came up fast. I felt it before I saw it, the way the current leaned hard right, herding us toward the hole, and I hauled the tiller across and screamed "LEFT" with everything my lungs had left, and from the stern, without stopping his knot work, Wren said, "Already leaning," and the boat answered both of us at once, swinging wide, the hole growling past our starboard side close enough to wet my face with its breath.

Then open water. Then quiet, the relative kind, the river unclenching its jaw.

I ran us into the eddy below the split rock—the one place in three kilometers of white water where the river forgives you—and killed what little momentum we had, and sat there in the dark shaking, listening to the rain start again on the canopy overhead.

Wren finished his last knot, felt his way forward, and settled onto the thwart beside me like a man getting off a bus.

"You let go of the paddle," I said. It came out angrier than I meant. Most true things do.

"The cargo was going to capsize us. Paddling wouldn't have fixed that."

"You don't know that."

"No," he agreed. "I don't. But I've spent enough years watching loads come loose on steep ground to know what it looks like from underneath." A pause. Rain on the hull. Somewhere upstream, thunder rearranging the sky. "You held the line. Blind. On a river you'd only described to me in insults."

"That's how I describe everything I love."

I said it before I could stop it, and then there was that silence, the specific silence of two people who have accidentally been honest in the same minute. He didn't push on it. That, more than the knot work, more than the calm voice in the worst water I'd run in years, was the thing that got under my skin. Most men would have made a joke of it, claimed it, worn it like a trophy. He just sat with it, letting it be mine.

"We should bail," he said finally. "Before the leak gets ambitious."

"There's a tin cup under the tarp."

"A tin cup."

"This is not a luxury vessel, Kavanagh."

* * *

We bailed by turns while the eddy held us, passing the cup back and forth in the dark like a ritual neither of us had been raised in but both needed. The rain thickened, then thinned, then committed. Upstream the lightning moved off behind the ridgeline, sulking.

"You've run that rapid before," he said, after a long stretch of nothing but water sounds. "At night."

"Twice. Once on purpose."

"What happened the other time?"

"Flood took my moorings out at the old camp above the Narrows. Boat, me, everything, straight into it. I came out the bottom holding a piece of gunwale and half a thermos." I passed him the cup. "The thermos survived. I drank coffee out of it for another six years. Felt like a miracle every morning."

"That's the whole trick, isn't it," he said quietly. "Deciding what counts as the miracle."

I didn't answer. Some sentences you have to let sit in the bottom of the boat with the bilge water.

My father used to say the river keeps score but forgets the names. I'd spent most of my adult life making sure no name of mine ever got written in its ledger—no debts, no partners, no passengers, no history that anyone upstream could trace. And here I was, bailing a leaking boat with a stranger whose face I'd seen for the first time properly tonight, lit by lightning, wearing an expression I recognized from mirrors: a man deciding, moment by moment, whether to keep going.

"You could have sold me out at the checkpoint town," I said. "Voss's people were asking. The ferryman told me."

"They were asking about me, not you."

"They were asking about whoever came downriver with a map. That's me, Kavanagh. Whatever's on that paper of yours, it's in my boat now."

Another silence, longer. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, careful, the voice of a man testing ice.

"If it comes to it—if they corner us—you take the boat and go. I'll deal with them."

"Deal with them how? You're one ex-guide with bad knees and a debt."

"I'm very good at disappearing. It's the one skill I've perfected."

"Yeah, well." I took the cup back, scooped, dumped, watched the river swallow what we gave it. "I'm good at vanishing too. Turns out it's a crowded profession."

He laughed then—a short, surprised sound, real, the first one I'd gotten out of him since the dock. It did something unfair to my chest, and I decided not to examine that, the way I'd decided not to examine a lot of things lately, a policy that was going great.

"Get some rest," I said. "Below the Narrows there's a long pool, then the water braids out through the delta channels. Daylight water. We'll make the lower villages by tomorrow evening if the rain doesn't get ideas."

"And if it does?"

"Then we get creative. I'm told I'm good at that."

I tied off in the shallows at the head of the pool, rigged the tarp, and lay back against the cargo he'd lashed so well I couldn't find a loose end anywhere. That detail kept surfacing as I drifted—the knots, done blind, done fast, done right. You learn a lot about a man from his knots. His said: patient, thorough, someone who expects things to come apart and prepares for it anyway.

A man braced for loss. I knew the type. I saw him in the mirror some mornings.

Sleep came slow, and when it came, it brought the old dream—the one where I'm nineteen and the river is taking the boat and I let it, because letting go is easier than fighting. But in the dream tonight there was a voice saying *already leaning*, calm as a spirit gauge, and the boat came through, and I woke once in the small hours to check the tie-off and found Wren awake too, sitting up in the dark, keeping watch without being asked.

Neither of us said anything. Some trusts are announced; the real ones just show up and start bailing.

Toward dawn the rain stopped, and the river dropped a hand's width, and I lay listening to the water settle and thought about the map folded in his jacket, the thing Voss wanted badly enough to send dogs downriver after it. I'd told myself I took this job for the money and the excuse to disappear. Both true. But lying there in the grey light, watching the mist lift off the pool, I admitted the third reason, the one I hadn't said out loud even to myself: whatever Silas—that was the name the ferryman had given, the dying prospector, the ghost at the top of this whole mess—whatever Silas had hidden downriver, the route on that map was a route I knew. A route I'd known since childhood. A route my father used to point at and say, *someday I'll tell you what's up that channel*, and then died before telling me anything ever again.

If the map led where I thought it led, then I wasn't piloting a stranger to his inheritance.

I was piloting him to mine.

I sat up in the wet grey dawn and watched Wren sleep, this man I'd known four days and trusted with my life for exactly one night, and understood that the question was no longer whether I'd help him run the river.

It was what I'd do when we reached the end of it—and whether either of us would survive what was waiting there, because downstream, in the first light, I could see fresh boot prints in the mud on the bank, heading the same direction we were.
