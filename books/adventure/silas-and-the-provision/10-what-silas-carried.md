## 10. What Silas Carried

The rain had followed us for two days, not the monsoon proper—Mara said that was still weeks out—but a gray, persistent drizzle that made the river feel like it was breathing under a wet blanket. We'd tied up in a slough off the main channel, a green throat of water choked with water hyacinth, where the current couldn't reach us and neither, with any luck, could Voss's men.

Mara slept in the bow, curled under a tarp like a cat that had decided the boat was hers and I was merely tolerated furniture. I should have slept too. God knows I needed it. But I had the map out under the lantern, and I couldn't stop looking at it.

I'd been carrying the thing for over a week now, and I realized, with a small and uncomfortable jolt, that I had never actually *read* it. Not properly. I'd studied the river route—the bends, the confluences, the little inked X where the deeds supposedly lived. That was the practical part, the part that kept us alive and moving. The rest of the map I'd treated like wrapping paper around a prize.

That was a mistake. Maps are never just maps. Any guide worth their salt knows that. A map is a confession. It tells you what the person who drew it loved, what they feared, what they couldn't stop thinking about. I'd read enough of them—left behind in teahouses, tucked into dead men's jackets, traded for passage—to know that the margins were where the truth lived.

Silas's margins were crowded.

* * *

The first thing I noticed was the handwriting. On the route itself, the ink was sure and economical—a working man's hand, the kind that had spent decades marking territory in shorthand. But in the margins, the letters were different. Smaller. Careful. The way you write when you're writing something you don't want anyone to see, but can't stop yourself from writing anyway.

There were dates. Dozens of them, scattered along the edges like birds on a wire. *Kathmandu, '89.* *Pokhara, '91.* *Surkhet, '94.* Some had little notes beside them. *School fees paid—anonymous.* *Saw her at the market. She has her mother's laugh.* *Sent money through the priest. Don't know if it arrived.*

I sat very still with the lantern turned low, and I read them all.

The girl in the notes was named—no. The girl in the notes was never named. That was the thing that undid me. Silas had spent twenty-some years tracking his daughter's life from a distance you could measure in guilt, and he had never once written her name. As if writing it would make it real, would make the distance a choice instead of a sentence. As if the name was a door he'd locked himself out of and the key was too heavy to carry.

There were sketches. That was what stopped my breath. In the lower margin, in pencil gone soft with age and handling, he'd drawn her. A small face, maybe six years old in the earliest one—round-cheeked, fierce-eyed, a smudge of a mouth set in an expression I recognized from every market stall and ferry dock in three countries: the look of a child who has already decided not to trust you. He'd gotten it exactly right. You could tell he'd gotten it exactly right because it wasn't a flattering drawing. It was a *true* one. He'd drawn her the way you draw someone you've studied from across a river, across a road, across the whole width of your own cowardice.

Below that, a later sketch—she was maybe twelve, all elbows and attitude, standing at the rail of a boat with her hair whipping sideways. He'd captioned it in that small careful hand: *Pilots the ferry already. Her mother must be furious. I am proud. I have no right to be.*

I looked over at Mara, asleep in the bow, one hand loose near the tiller even in sleep, the way river people sleep. The same hands. The same set to the jaw. I'd noticed the resemblance weeks ago and filed it away as coincidence—river country was full of stubborn-jawed people. It wasn't coincidence. It had never been coincidence.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about promises: they're easy to make at a deathbed, when the dying man's eyes are wet and the mountains are going purple behind him and everything feels like a scene from a story where you're the decent kind of person. It's much harder three weeks later, in a leaking boat, with an outfitter's enforcers somewhere behind you and a debt on your back like a second spine.

I'd been telling myself the whole way downriver that the map was payment. That was the story I'd built: Silas owed me for the burial, for the carrying, for the promise extracted under duress from a dying man, and the map was fair trade. Clean transaction. I'm a guide. I understand transactions. They don't ask anything of you except that you show up and do the work and take the money.

But transactions don't have sketches of children in the margins.

I turned the map over and looked at the back. More dates. More notes, sparser now, the handwriting shakier. *Heard she runs her own boat now.* *The priest says she asks about the mountains. I can't. Not yet.* *Maybe when the claim pays out.* And then, near the bottom, in ink that looked fresher than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: *If you are reading this, stranger, then I am dead and she is still waiting and so am I. One of us has to stop.*

I put the map down on the thwart and pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes.

Because here was the thing I'd been circling for days without landing on: I knew exactly what Silas had been doing, all those years of margins and sketches and anonymous school fees. I'd been doing it myself. Different geography, same discipline. I'd spent years tracking my own life from a distance—watching the person I used to be the way you watch a stranger across a market, noting the changes, sending nothing, approaching nothing. *Saw myself in a window in Lima today. Didn't recognize the face. Good.* That kind of thing. Invisibility as safety. Distance as discipline. The long slow art of never arriving anywhere.

And Silas had died mid-crossing. He'd made it as far as a stranger with a compass and a bad reputation, and he'd handed the whole crossing over to me, and I had accepted it as a *transaction*.

* * *

Mara woke near dawn. I heard her before I saw her—the particular rustle of a river pilot taking inventory of her boat by sound alone: the hull, the lines, the sky. She sat up under the tarp and looked at me sitting in the stern with the lantern burned down to a stub and the map in my lap, and her eyes narrowed.

"You didn't sleep."

"Didn't need much."

"Liar." She crawled aft and put the kettle on the little spirit stove, and for a while there was just the hiss of water and the drip of drizzle off the tarp and the green smell of the flooded hyacinths. She poured tea into two tin mugs and handed me one without looking at me, which was its own kind of trust, and sat down across from me with her boots on the thwart.

"What's on that map that's kept you up all night?"

I weighed it. I want that on the record: I weighed it. There was a version of me that told her the truth right then, sitting in the gray light with the tea steaming between us. *Your father tracked you for twenty years. He drew you at six and at twelve and he never once wrote your name because he couldn't afford the weight of it. He died with your face in his margins.*

And there was the other version, the one that had been running my mouth for years: *Nothing. Route notes. River bends.* I chose the other version. I told myself it was because she wasn't ready, or because the timing was wrong, or because a promise made to a dying man about a sealed letter was a promise about the letter and not the map. All true, maybe. Also I was a coward, and cowardice always dresses itself in timing.

"Route notes," I said. "River bends. Depths."

She watched me for a long moment. Mara had a way of looking at you like she was reading water—checking for the undertow under the smooth surface. I'd seen her do it to the river a dozen times, and the river always gave itself away eventually. I was trying very hard not to.

"Fine," she said finally, and drank her tea, and I thought I'd gotten away with it, and then she said, quietly, not looking at me: "My father ran these rivers. Before I was born, or when I was small—I don't know which. My mother wouldn't talk about him. Said he loved the country more than the people in it." She turned the mug in her hands. "I used to make up stories about him. That he was a spy. A saint. A coward." A shrug that cost her something. "Never could decide which fit."

The map sat in my lap like a live coal. *A coward,* I thought. *You decided right, and I'm holding the evidence, and I can't give it to you because giving it to you means admitting I've been carrying it since the slough, since the checkpoint, since the night I read a dead man's letter by candlelight and learned his daughter's name and said nothing.*

"River bends," she said again, softly, and it wasn't a question anymore. It was a verdict she was choosing to suspend. "Okay, Kavanagh. River bends." She stood, coiled the tea towel over her shoulder, and looked downriver at the gray wall of cloud stacking up over the hills. "That front's moving faster than the radio said. We should make the confluence by noon or we'll be pinned here a week."

She moved forward to raise the anchor, and I sat alone in the stern with a dead man's confession in my hands and a living woman's trust sitting wrong in my chest, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the treeline.

The promise was going to cost more than I'd paid. That was the thing about Silas—he'd been a prospector, and prospectors know that the true price of anything is only ever revealed when you're too far in to walk away. He'd handed me a map worth money and a promise worth more, and I'd taken both, and now the margins were filling up with a girl's face and a debt I couldn't settle with anyone's money but my own.

I folded the map away, careful with the creases, careful with the sketches, and went forward to help her with the anchor. The rain was thickening. Somewhere behind us, I didn't doubt, Voss's men were doing their own arithmetic.

"Kavanagh," Mara said, not turning around. "When we get where we're going—whatever's at the end of this map—you'll tell me. Won't you. When it matters."

The current took the boat before I could answer, which was a mercy, because I didn't have one that wasn't a lie.
