## 11. The Checkpoint

*Wren*

The trouble with rivers, I'd learned in the last three days, is that they only go where they go. A mountain gives you options—ridgelines, gullies, the coward's descent down scree. A river gives you a corridor with your name on it, and at the end of every corridor somebody builds a checkpoint.

Mara spotted it first, because Mara spotted everything first. She was standing at the tiller with her weight on one hip, chewing the inside of her cheek, and she said, without turning around, "Smoke."

I looked upriver. The morning haze sat low on the water like it was paid to be there, and through it, maybe two kilometers on, a gray thread of woodsmoke rose from the left bank where the channel narrowed between fish-drying racks.

"Camp?" I asked, though I already knew.

"Nobody camps there." She cut the engine to an idle and let us drift. "That's the customs post at Ban Kham. Or what passes for one. One boat, one officer, one rifle that's older than both of us." Her jaw worked. "Officer Deng."

"You know him."

"Everyone knows Deng. He waves through the rice boats and shakes down anyone who looks like they can pay." She finally turned, and her eyes did that thing they did when she was measuring me against something I couldn't see. "You look like you can pay."

"I can't. That's the entire architecture of my life right now."

"Then we have a problem, because he's not alone today." She nodded past my shoulder, and I turned in time to see it: two more figures on the bank beside Deng's sagging customs hut. They stood wrong for officials. Officials slouch. These two stood like men being paid by the hour to stand, which is its own kind of posture—weight forward, hands loose, eyes doing laps of the river.

One of them raised binoculars.

I did what any reasonable person does when binoculars find them at two kilometers. I dropped below the gunwale like a sack of wet rope.

"Subtle," Mara said.

"He's looking at us."

"He's looking at every boat. It's a river. Boats happen." But she was already easing the throttle back, killing our wake, letting us become just another slow thing on brown water. "Who are they?"

"They're Voss's men." Saying it out loud made my stomach do something structural. "The big one's called Ferro. I don't know the other one."

"How do you know the big one's called Ferro?"

"Because three years ago in Huaraz, Ferro broke a friend of mine's hand to make a point about a debt, and he introduced himself first. He's polite that way." I kept my head down and watched water seep up through the hull boards, pooling around my boots like the boat was crying on our behalf. "Voss found me. Or found someone who talked."

"Deng," Mara said flatly.

"What?"

"Deng. You think Voss's men just happened to be standing at the one checkpoint on this stretch of river the same morning we're running it? Deng sells information the way other men sell fish—cheap, fresh, and to anybody with coin. I've paid him myself, twice, to lose paperwork." She exhaled through her nose. "I told you never to ask about my past. This isn't the past. This is the present, and the present is that we're about to float past three men, one of whom knows your face."

I lay there in the bilge water and considered my options, which were: get arrested, get beaten, or get creative. Historically, creativity had gotten me blacklisted from two countries, so my track record was mixed.

"Options," I said.

"We turn back."

"Back is four days of poling through flooded channels with half our supplies soaked. And they'd just follow."

"We beach and walk around."

"Through twelve kilometers of leech forest carrying everything we own. With them watching the bank the whole time."

"So we go through." She said it quietly, testing the weight of it.

"We go through," I agreed, and then, because honesty seemed suddenly cheap compared to what was coming, added, "I'm sorry. Whatever happens back there—it's me they want. If I step off at the checkpoint and give them twenty minutes of theater, you can be gone before—"

"Sit up, Kavanagh." There was something in her voice I hadn't heard before. Not softness exactly. More like a door opening one inch in a house I'd assumed was empty. "You got me into this channel. You'll get yourself out of it. That's the deal. Nobody gets left at checkpoints."

I sat up.

* * *

*Mara*

He looked terrible sitting in the bilge water, which I confess gave me a small, uncharitable satisfaction. Three days of his company had taught me that Wren Kavanagh wore competence like a coat but fear fit him badly—he kept tugging at it, adjusting it, trying to make it look intentional.

I knew that feeling. I'd worn it for years.

The plan, such as it was, took shape the way plans do on rivers: out of whatever was floating nearby. We had a hold full of dried fish I'd bought in Tha Seng, ostensibly as trade goods, actually as ballast. We had a story—I was running cargo to my cousin's landing downriver, and the foreigner was a buyer I'd picked up, a quiet type, bad with languages. We had one asset that couldn't be planned: Deng owed me nothing, liked me less than nothing since the second time I'd bribed him, and would sell us both for the price of a decent bottle. Which meant the plan's real foundation was speed and nerve, in that order.

"Talk fast and wrong," I told Wren as the checkpoint grew legs and then faces. "If you speak the language well, he'll want to know why. Bad foreigners are boring. Boring gets waved through."

"My language skills peak at 'where is the toilet' and 'how much.'"

"Perfect. Lead with 'how much.' It's the most honest sentence in any economy."

Ferro hadn't moved from his spot by the drying racks, but the binoculars were down now, and that worried me more than the binoculars. Men who watch put the glasses away when they've decided. The other one—lean, young, restless—had drifted down to the waterline where our wake would arrive, positioning himself the way you position yourself when the job is confirmation rather than discovery.

They weren't fishing for us. They were checking a net they'd already set.

I killed the engine fifty meters out and let momentum carry us in, hands easy on the tiller, face arranged into the particular boredom of a woman who has done this run two hundred times and been cheated at least half of them. Beside me, Wren arranged himself into a slouch that was almost convincing, eyes down, thumbing through a creased paperback he'd produced from somewhere—a romance novel, dog-eared, absurd. Later I would ask about that book. Later there would hopefully be a later.

"Mara Vane." Deng stood on his rotten jetty with his rifle slung backward like a scarf, which was either carelessness or a message. His voice carried the wheedling warmth of a man about to invoice you. "Running late in the season. Water's high."

"Water's always high somewhere, Deng. That's why they call it water."

He laughed the way he laughed at everyone, a sound with no floor under it. Then his eyes went to Wren, lingered, came back to me. "Your passenger. Papers?"

"He's a buyer. Fish, salt, diesel parts. He pays cash and talks to nobody, which is my favorite kind of customer." I held out a folded stack of notes—not enough to insult, too much to ignore. "For the crossing fee. And the mooring fee. And the fee for the fee."

Deng took the money, counted it with his lips moving, and pocketed it. For one whole second, the second I'd budgeted for, the world behaved.

Then the lean man at the waterline said something to Ferro, low and quick, and Ferro started walking toward the jetty, and the second ended.

* * *

*Wren*

There is a specific cold that starts in the base of the spine when you recognize a man who has broken someone you loved, and that cold arrived right on schedule as Ferro crossed the jetty. He hadn't changed. Same build like a load-bearing wall, same shaved head going gray at the temples, same unhurried walk of a man who has never once needed to hurry because everyone else eventually comes to him.

Beside me, Mara's hand rested on the tiller, and I could see her doing arithmetic with it—the distance to midchannel, the current, the angle of departure. Good pilot math. Doomed pilot math. Ferro was ten meters away and closing, and the lean kid had already stepped onto the jetty's far end to close the box.

So I did the only thing I'm genuinely good at besides reading weather: I became someone else.

I stood up—too fast, clumsy, hungover-clumsy—and thrust the romance novel at Deng with a stream of mangled dialect and pointing gestures, the universal pantomime of the tourist grievance. *This book. Page forty. The innkeeper's wife. Where is the rest? How much for the rest?* I said "how much" eleven times in nine seconds. I made it obscene. I made it pathetic. I made myself the most annoying object on that jetty, a thing so tiresome that violence would feel like unpaid labor.

Deng's face performed a small tragedy. Ferro slowed—not stopped, slowed—and his eyes moved over me with the flat inventory of a man checking a crate against a manifest. I felt the look pass over my face like a searchlight and I gave it nothing to catch: red-rimmed eyes, slack mouth, the aggrieved bewilderment of a man whose paperback had betrayed him.

"The innkeeper's wife," I repeated, in three languages, none of them fluently. "Page forty. How much?"

"Get him off my jetty," Deng told Mara, with feeling.

She didn't need telling. The engine caught on the second pull—she'd left it warm, of course she had—and we slid off the jetty planks while I was still mid-grievance, still waving the novel, still the worst passenger on the Mekong's tributaries. The current took us. The checkpoint shrank. My heart was going like a drum line.

And then Ferro, standing at the end of the jetty with the flood sliding past his boots, did not shout, did not wave, did not signal the lean kid to anything at all. He simply raised one hand—not a wave, a gesture, two fingers touching his own temple, a salute or a promise—and held it there until the bend took him away.

"He recognized me," I said.

"No," Mara said. "He suspected you. There's a difference, and right now it's the width of this channel." But her hands were tight on the tiller, and she opened the throttle wider than the channel deserved.

* * *

*Mara*

We ran hard for an hour, putting bends between us and Ban Kham, and neither of us spoke, which is its own kind of conversation. I know what silence means on my river. That silence meant we were both counting the same numbers: how long before Deng's radio crackled, how fast a phone call travels versus a boat, how many landings ahead had a road, a truck, a man waiting.

At the confluence where the channel widened and the current went lazy, I finally cut the throttle and let us drift, because a drifting boat hides its intentions even from itself.

Wren sat amidships with the ridiculous novel still in his lap, staring at it like it had personally failed him. Water sloshed in the bilge. Somewhere behind us, thunder was organizing itself over the mountains, the monsoon front arriving fashionably late and planning to stay the season.

"He touched his head," Wren said. "Two fingers. You saw it."

"I saw it."

"In Huaraz, after Ferro finished with Tomás's hand, he did the same thing. Touched his temple. Tomás asked him what it meant." Wren turned the paperback over in his hands. "Ferro said, 'It means I remember.' That's their whole culture, those men. Memory as a weapon. Voss doesn't forget debts, doesn't forgive them, doesn't write them down even—he keeps them in people, and Ferro's the ledger."

"And now you're an entry."

"Now I'm an entry." He looked up at me, and for once there was no performance in his face, no slouch, no cleverness. Just a tired man on leaking water asking a question he didn't want answered. "Mara. Why did you really take this run? Not the bargain, not the boat hook, not the hard terms. The night I showed you the map sketch, you already knew that bend of river. You knew it the way I know ridgelines. Somebody drew you that route a long time ago."

The thunder rolled closer. I watched the water, because the water doesn't ask things.

"I told you the terms," I said. "No questions about my past."

"This isn't your past. This is Ferro's memory, and it's pointed at both of us now, and I need to know if I've dragged you into something or if you walked in with your eyes open."

I should have held the line. I'd held it for years; it was load-bearing. But he'd stood up on that jetty and made himself a fool with a romance novel so that I could keep my name out of Deng's mouth, and there are debts that accrue interest whether you acknowledge them or not.

"Both," I said. "I dragged myself in. You just happened to be holding the map." I nodded at the waterproof roll lashed under his thwart, Silas's map, the thing all this blood and fuel was ultimately about. "My father ran this river. Before I was born, and after. Every bend of it. If your map is what I think it is, then whoever drew it learned the river from the same school I did—from the same man, maybe. And I have spent six years making sure nobody ever connected me to that man, because the last time someone did, I lost everything I had left of him." I met his eyes. "So yes. Eyes open. Now row, or steer, or whatever you do on a boat, because the storm's coming and I don't intend to be on open water when it lands."

He didn't push further. That was the thing about Wren I was starting to distrust most: he knew when to stop asking, which meant he was saving the questions, keeping them somewhere, and a man who saves questions intends to spend them.

We ran on through the rising wind. And thirty minutes later, rounding the bluff above the Nam Khong landing, we both saw it at the same time and neither of us said it aloud, because saying it would make it true:

A black patrol boat, twin engines, riding high and empty at the dock.

Voss's colors on the hull.

Ahead of us. Waiting.
