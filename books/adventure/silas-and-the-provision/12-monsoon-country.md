## 12. Monsoon Country

The rain came on the third night after the checkpoint, and it did not come like weather. It came like a verdict.

I'd run this river eleven seasons, and I'd seen monsoons arrive politely—building for days, a gray rumor on the hills, giving you time to tie down your tarps and your regrets. This one arrived like a debt collector. One hour the sky was bruised and quiet, the next the whole world was a wall of water falling out of heaven with no interest in explanations.

"Get us into that channel," Wren said, pointing left, and for once I didn't argue, because arguing requires breath and breath was going into the tiller.

The side channel was one I'd used before, a sliver of brown water threading into flooded forest, and I took us in on memory and prayer, the two things I trust least. The boat groaned. The canopy closed over us like a hand. And then, abruptly, the roar behind us softened, and there was only the rain on leaves—a sound like applause from an audience that would never stop.

We tied off to a fig tree the size of a cathedral and looked at each other, soaked to the bone, and Wren said, "Well. That's that, then."

"That's that," I agreed.

Neither of us said the obvious thing, which was that we might be stuck there for days, or a week, or until the river decided it was finished with us. Pilots learn early not to argue with rivers. They always win, and they always gloat.

* * *

The first day was logistics. I'm good at logistics. It's people I'm bad at.

We had a tarp, a cook stove with maybe four days of fuel, a sack of rice, a tin of dried fish, a jar of chili paste I'd bought in a fit of optimism, and half a bottle of rum that Wren produced from their pack with the sheepish air of a person who has been caught hoarding.

"You had rum this whole time," I said, "and you waited until we were nearly killed at the checkpoint to mention it?"

"I was rationing it for an emergency."

"We were nearly killed."

"Exactly. And we weren't. So it's still an emergency reserve." They poured two caps full and handed me one, and we drank rum out of bottle caps under a tarp in a drowned forest while the rain hammered down like it had a personal grudge, and I thought: this is the strangest situation I have ever been content in.

I didn't say that either. I'm getting ahead of myself.

The boat sat low in the water, wedged in the channel with the current pushing at her stern, and I spent the morning re-caulking the seams I'd already re-caulked twice since we met. Wren bailed. We didn't talk much. Talking, in my experience, is what you do when you can't stand the silence, and I have always been able to stand the silence. Silence was the thing I'd built my whole life out of, the way other people build houses out of timber.

But by the second day, the silence started doing something strange. It started feeling less like mine.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being trapped: it strips you down. A boat is a small country with a population of two, and there's nowhere to be diplomatically absent. On the river, when I didn't want to talk to a passenger, I could go stand at the bow and let the wind do my conversating. Here the wind was busy. The wind had a monsoon to run.

So we talked. Or rather, Wren talked, in the way of people who fill silence the way water fills a hull—steadily, without malice, until you notice you're taking on more of them than you meant to.

They told me about the mountains. Not the guide-brochure mountains—the real ones, the cold ones, the ones where Wren Kavanagh had been somebody before being somebody became the problem. They talked about a client who'd ignored a turnaround call and lived, and how that was worse than if he'd died, because he wrote a letter of complaint. They talked about the blacklist, though not the details of it, and I noticed the details were missing the way you notice a missing tooth—by the shape of the gap.

"Voss holds the paper on that story," they said, staring at the rain. "Whatever version you've heard, he's got the version with teeth in it. He keeps it the way other men keep photographs."

"And you've never told yours?"

"To who?" They almost smiled. "People who'd believe me don't have the power to help. People with the power to help get paid not to believe me."

I knew that arithmetic. I'd been doing it my whole life in a different currency.

* * *

That night the rain eased to a steady drumming, and we lit the lamp, and I cooked rice with too much chili paste because the alternative was blandness, and Wren ate it without complaint even though their eyes watered, and I decided that was a character reference.

"Your turn," they said.

"My turn for what?"

"Whatever it is you're not telling me. You don't have to tell it. But you've been circling it for two hundred kilometers, and you circle it like a pilot who won't look directly at a rock shoal. Which, incidentally, is how pilots hit rock shoals."

I should have told them to mind their own business. That was the old script, the one I'd been running for years: the pilot who says no, the woman who takes no passengers, the river rat with no history. It was a good script. It had kept me alive and, more importantly, it had kept me unexamined.

But the lamp was warm, and the rain made the whole world private, like the forest had drawn curtains around us, and Wren was looking at me with that expression—not pity, not curiosity exactly, but the patient attention of a person who has learned that everyone is carrying something and most people just want to set it down for a minute without being judged for how long they've carried it.

"I had a brother," I said.

The words came out before I'd decided to say them, which is how the true ones always come.

"His name was Tomas. He ran boats too, before me. He taught me everything—how to read water, how to hear a rapid before you see it. He used to say the river talks to you all day, every day, and most people just aren't polite enough to listen." I turned the bottle cap in my fingers. "He drowned. Eight years ago. A commercial run, overloaded, a company that cut maintenance budgets and paid the inspectors to look elsewhere. The same company, as it happens, that Voss now does half his freight through."

Wren didn't say anything. Good. Wren was learning the most important skill in the world, which is shutting up when a person is in the middle of it.

"I took the settlement money and bought my first boat, and I told everyone I was fine, and I was fine, technically. I ate. I piloted. I didn't die. But I stopped taking passengers, because passengers talk, and talk asks questions, and I had built this whole life on the theory that if nobody knew anything about me, nobody could take anything else away." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "It's not a bad theory. It's just that it works. That's the trap. You make yourself invisible and it *works*, and then one day you're forty years old and you're drinking rum out of a bottle cap with a stranger in a flooded forest because it's the first time in eight years you've been anywhere you couldn't leave."

The rain filled the pause. Wren let it.

"Your brother sounds like he'd have liked this boat," they said finally.

"He'd have hated this boat. She leaks like a confession."

"Then he'd have liked the company."

I looked at them. They weren't flirting, or not exactly, or not only. It was worse than that. It was *kind*.

* * *

The third day the water rose around us and the channel became indistinguishable from the forest, and we were no longer marooned on a river so much as moored in a lake that had eaten a jungle. Fish swam between the tree trunks. A heron stood on our gunwale at dawn with the entitlement of a landlord, and Wren fed it rice, and it left, ungrateful.

We played a card game with a deck missing the sevens, which Wren insisted made it more interesting and I insisted made it broken, and we argued about it with a ferocity that had nothing to do with sevens. We inventoried the fuel and did the math on the rice and came out with a number that neither of us said out loud. Wren told me about a monastery in the passes where they'd once sheltered a winter, and the old monk who'd told them that a compass doesn't tell you where to go, it only tells you where you're facing, and Wren had hated that at the time and thought about it every day since.

"What about you?" they asked, on the fourth night. "Before the river."

"Before the river there was a house," I said. "Upcountry. Land my mother came from. I haven't been back since I was small enough to be carried." I said it lightly, the way you set down something fragile, and I felt the old shape of the truth pressing against the underside of the lie—not a lie exactly, just a door held shut. *Almost everything but the truth,* I thought, and didn't examine why the truth had a shape at all, why it had edges I could feel.

"And you never went back?"

"The river goes one direction," I said. "That's the whole point of it."

Wren looked at me for a long moment, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being read—not my face, not my words, but the current underneath them, the way I could read a river's real channel beneath its surface glitter. They knew something. I couldn't say what. But they looked at the lamp and then at me and said, "Fair enough," and let it go, and somehow that was more unnerving than if they'd pushed.

* * *

On the fifth night, the rain finally thinned to a whisper, and the sky over the drowned trees went the color of a healing bruise, and I lay in the boat under the tarp listening to the forest drip and couldn't sleep.

Wren was awake too. I could tell by the breathing. There's a particular quality to the breathing of a person who's pretending to sleep, a carefulness to it, and after eight years of solo boats I have become an expert in the sounds other people make when they think no one's listening.

"You awake," I said. Not a question.

"Define awake."

"You're thinking about the map."

A pause. The rain ticked on the tarp.

"I'm thinking about a lot of things," Wren said.

"Silas," I said. "The prospector. You never talk about him. You talk about the mountains, and Voss, and the blacklist, and the monastery, and the client who lived, and every other thing, but not him. And he's the reason we're here."

The silence stretched long enough that I thought they'd do the thing I would have done, which is roll over and end the conversation with their back. Instead Wren sat up, and the lamp had burned low, and in what was left of its light their face looked younger and older at the same time, which I hadn't known was possible.

"He was dying when we found him," they said. "Above the treeline, stranded, his party long gone or never real. He had a map and a letter and one request, and he was in no position to enforce it, and I could have walked away with nothing and been no worse off." They were quiet a moment. "I've spent years telling myself I'm the kind of person who'd walk away. It's easier to plan around a person like that. You always know what they'll do."

"And you didn't walk away."

"I made him a promise I didn't understand yet." They looked at their hands. "I keep turning it over. What he was really asking. Whether I understood it even now."

"What was the promise?"

"To deliver the letter. To find his daughter and give it to her." Wren's voice did something small and strange on the word *daughter*, a hairline fracture I might have missed if the night hadn't been so quiet. "That's all. Find her. Give it to her. Let her decide what he was worth."

"And will you?"

"That's the thing I'm thinking about," Wren said, "when I'm pretending to sleep."

I lay back and looked at the underside of the tarp, where the lamplight made a small warm world, and I felt an odd ache I couldn't name—something about fathers and letters and promises, something stirring in a room of myself I'd kept locked so long I'd forgotten it was a room. Tomas had a letter too, once. The company's letter. Three paragraphs and a settlement figure. I'd burned it. I'd burned it and kept the ashes in a jar for a year before I threw the jar in the river, and I had never told a living soul that, and I didn't tell Wren either.

"Whoever she is," I said, "I hope she takes the letter."

"Why?"

"Because a person should get to decide what their dead are worth," I said. "Nobody else gets to decide for them. Not the river. Not the debt collectors. Not the person carrying it." I heard my own voice go rough and didn't care. "Even if it breaks them to read it."

Wren was quiet for a long time. When they finally spoke, their voice was careful, the way a foot is careful on a wet rock.

"Mara. If it were your father—"

"He's dead," I said, too fast, too flat.

"I didn't ask if he was dead. I asked if he were your father. If there were a letter. Would you want to read it?"

And the strange thing, the thing I've turned over a hundred times since, is that I didn't answer. I opened my mouth and the room behind the locked door was suddenly, horribly loud—sketches in a margin, a name I hadn't heard spoken in thirty years, a man on a river dock turning away—and I said, "Sleep, Kavanagh. We run at first light," and rolled toward the hull.

Wren didn't push. That's the thing about Wren; they never push, they just leave doors open behind them, and you find yourself walking through them days later wondering whose idea it was.

But as I lay there in the dark, listening to the water rise against the hull, one thought kept circling like the heron: *Wren never said the daughter's name.* Not once, in all these nights. Not once, in all this rain.

I told myself it was grief. I told myself it was privacy. I told myself a great many things, and the river rose all night, and in the morning we found the first thing the flood had brought us—a waterlogged bundle caught in our anchor line, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a knot I recognized because my father had taught it to me.

I didn't tell Wren that either. Not yet.
