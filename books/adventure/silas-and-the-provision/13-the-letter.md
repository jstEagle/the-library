## 13. The Letter

The rain had been falling for three days when I finally broke my promise.

Not the big one — not yet. The one to Silas, the dying-man's bargain that had gotten me shot at, sunk, and soaked past the point of dignity. That promise was still intact, folded up inside my jacket like a splinter working its way toward the heart. No, this was a smaller betrayal, the kind you can rationalize at two in the morning with water drumming on a tarpaulin roof: I read his letter.

Let me explain how a person arrives at that kind of night.

* * *

The side channel where we'd tied up after the sabotage was less a mooring than an apology — a slot of brown water between two drowned trees, deep enough to float what remained of our ambitions and shallow enough that Voss's patrol boats couldn't follow without scraping their props on submerged fence posts. Mara had found it the way she found everything, by some private conversation she held with the river that I wasn't invited to. She'd cut the engine half a kilometer back and poled us in through the flooded cane, silent as a heron, and then she'd looked at the little pocket of stillness she'd chosen and nodded once, as if the river had passed her test.

We'd been there two days. The boat — or what the enforcers' knives had left of it — sat low in the water with its stern under a tarp, taking on just enough seepage to require bailing twice a day, which Mara insisted was good for morale. "Gives you a job," she said. "People without jobs start thinking."

She was right about that. Thinking was the last thing I needed.

The flood had taken half the map. I'd watched it go — the lower third, the part with the survey marks and the boundary stones, dissolving into pulp in the bilge water while I stood there holding a bail bucket like a man trying to argue with weather. But I'd memorized it. Weeks of staring at that paper in bad light will do that; the lines were printed on the inside of my eyelids now, every contour, every hand-inked notation, including the ones that didn't make sense until later. And the letter — the sealed letter Silas had pressed into my hands above the treeline with his last strength — had survived dry in its oilskin sleeve, because I'd kept it against my chest, because apparently some part of me understood its value before my head did.

Mara slept in the bow, wrapped in the one dry blanket, her breathing slow and even in a way I envied. I sat in the stern with the lamp turned down to a coal and the letter in my lap, and I told myself stories.

Here's the thing about promises: they're easy to keep when keeping them costs nothing. Silas had asked me to find his daughter and give her the letter. Fine. Noble, even. But he hadn't said anything about the contents. He hadn't said *don't read it*, which any reasonable lawyer would tell you leaves a gap wide enough to drive a guilty conscience through. And I had reasons. Practical ones. If the letter named a town, a family, a direction, it would help me complete the delivery once we reached somewhere with a post office or a church registry or any of the ordinary machinery that finds people who don't want to be found. I was going to need that machinery, because the map's destination — the deeded land upriver — was clearly where the daughter was meant to be, but clearly wasn't where she was, since the daughter was presumably living her life somewhere and not waiting thirty years in a flood zone for a courier.

That was the story I told myself. It was a good story. It had logic in it, and even a little virtue.

It was also a lie, and I knew it was a lie, and I opened the letter anyway.

* * *

The seal had been wax once, cheap wax, the kind that goes brittle. Under the oilskin it had held, but it cracked now at a touch, and I felt the small sound of it like a bone breaking in a quiet room. I glanced at the bow. Mara hadn't moved. The rain covered everything anyway; it had been covering everything for days, erasing the world down to water and waiting.

Silas's handwriting filled both sides of a single sheet, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, the lines getting shakier toward the bottom as though I could watch the years pass as I read. There was no date. There was no address. There was only a name at the top, written first and hardest, pressed into the paper like a man driving a stake:

*Mara.*

I stopped breathing. I'm not proud of how long I stopped breathing. Long enough that the lamp flame swam in front of me, long enough that the rain seemed to get louder, filling the space where my pulse should have been.

*Mara.* Not a common name out here, but not unheard of either. Rivers are full of Maras. Half the saints in the calendar answer to it. Coincidence was not only possible but probable, and I clung to that probability the way a drowning man clings to driftwood that is visibly too small for him.

Then I read the first line, and the driftwood went under.

*If you're reading this, my girl, then someone kept their word to a foolish old man, and I hope they were gentle with you, because I never learned how to be.*

I read it three times. The words didn't change. That's the cruelty of letters — you can reread them all night hoping the ink will rearrange itself into something survivable, and it never does.

He wrote about a girl born in a river town during a flood year, born screaming so loud the midwife laughed and said she'd argue with the water itself. He wrote about leaving — not the why of it, not really, just the shape of it: debts, a claim gone bad, a shame he couldn't look at straight, and the coward's arithmetic that says a child is better off with one parent missing than with two drowning. He wrote that he'd meant to come back in a season. Then two seasons. Then the seasons stopped meaning anything, because time does that to men who run; it stops being a road and becomes a wall.

And then — this was the part that undid me — he wrote about watching her grow up from a distance. A paragraph of it. He'd come back, once, maybe eight years ago. Stood across the river at the market day and seen a young woman haggling over rope, all elbows and certainty, and known her instantly, the way you know your own hands. He'd bought nothing. Said nothing. Walked back to his boat and told himself next year, and the year after that he'd been too sick to make the trip, and the sickness had become the map, and the map had become a stranger on a mountain with steady hands and tired eyes.

*I watched you argue a fishmonger down to half price,* he wrote, *and I have never in my life been so proud or so ashamed, and I could not tell you which one kept me from crossing that street.*

I sat very still. Rain on canvas. Lamp guttering. Somewhere out in the flooded dark, a bird made a sound like a question.

Because I'd seen that woman. I'd seen her two weeks ago, standing on a dock refusing passengers with a boat hook in her hand and a face like a locked door, and I'd thought: *there's someone else running from something.* I'd thought it with a certain professional respect, the way one runner tips his hat to another.

Her name was Mara. Of course it was. Her name was Mara, and she piloted exactly the stretch of river that Silas's map described, and she'd looked at my rough sketch of the route with an expression I'd filed away as suspicion and should have filed as recognition, and she'd refused to help me and then couldn't sleep and then helped me anyway, and every single piece of it fit together with the horrible click of a lock turning.

Mara Vane. The letter didn't give a surname in the opening — but further down, in the shakier script, he'd written it plainly, the way you write a thing you've rehearsed for decades: *Mara Vane, born in the flood year, daughter of Silas Vane.* And Vane. I turned the name over. Vane, Voss — no relation there, thank God, the universe wasn't that cruel — but Vane was a river name, a boat-family name, and there were Vanes in the villages we'd passed, Vanes who'd nodded to Mara at the checkpoint with the easy familiarity of kin.

She was sitting forty feet away — twelve feet away, this was a small boat — sleeping under a blanket, and I was holding her father's last words to her in my hands, and I had promised him, and I had promised *her* that I'd never ask about her past, and those two promises had just collided head-on in the dark, and I was the wreckage.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I want it on the record that I did it badly.

I refolded the letter along its original creases, which took longer than it should have because my hands weren't steady, and I resealed it — badly, with a drop of candle wax that didn't match the original and wouldn't fool anyone who looked closely, though I told myself she'd never look closely, people don't examine seals, people trust couriers, that's the entire profession. I slid it back into the oilskin sleeve. I put the sleeve back inside my jacket, against my chest, where it had lived since the mountain, and I lay back against the gunwale and stared at the underside of the tarp and listened to the rain and did not sleep at all.

Somewhere around the gray hour before dawn, Mara stirred. I kept my eyes closed and my breathing staged, the oldest trick in the guide's book, and I listened to her move through the boat — the soft knock of her knee against the thwart, the creak of the bail bucket, the unhurried slosh of her doing my job for me because I'd failed to do it. Then she settled again, closer to the middle now, and after a while she spoke, quietly, whether to me or to the rain I couldn't tell.

"You breathe different when you're awake," she said. "Fakers always breathe too evenly."

I opened my eyes. She was looking at the water, not at me, her profile a shadow against the gray.

"Couldn't sleep," I said, which was true, and therefore safe.

"Nobody can, in this." She tipped her chin at the rain. "River's going to come up another meter tonight. We'll have to move or climb."

"We'll move."

"Mm." A pause. Water ticked against the hull. And then, in a voice so casual it was obviously not casual at all: "You talk in your sleep sometimes. Did you know that?"

My stomach performed a maneuver I usually associate with Class IV rapids. "Do I."

"Not words. Just names." She pulled the blanket higher. "You said *Silas* a couple times. And once you said *I didn't know.*" She shrugged, a small motion barely visible in the dark. "Didn't know what?"

"That's the trouble with sleep-talk," I said, aiming for wry and landing somewhere nearer to strangled. "No footnotes."

She almost smiled. I saw it happen at the corner of her mouth and then get arrested, like a bird changing its mind about a branch. "One of these days, Kavanagh, you're going to have to tell me what you're actually carrying in that jacket. You hold it like it's a baby. Or a bomb."

"It's documents," I said. "Boring ones."

"Everything about you is boring ones," she said, "except none of it is," and she rolled over and faced away from me, and the conversation was over, and I lay there in the wet dark holding a dead man's confession against my heartbeat, wondering how much of it she'd already guessed.

* * *

Morning came the color of dishwater. We moved the boat up the channel as she'd predicted, poling through water that had swallowed the banks whole, past rooftops and treetops and a fence line running along under the surface like a sunken thought. We worked well together, I'll say that. She'd call the depth changes and I'd shift the cargo, and neither of us wasted words, and if there was a current running underneath our silence that had nothing to do with the river, neither of us named it.

But I caught myself watching her. That was new — no, that was a lie, I'd been watching her since the dock, but now I watched differently, the way you watch a landscape after learning there's gold in it. The set of her jaw when she shoved the pole home: his jaw, maybe, from some photograph I'd never see. The way she argued with the current and expected to win: *she'd argue with the water itself.* The pride and the shame, walking together down a market street, unable to cross.

Twice she caught me at it. Twice I looked away too fast, which is worse than looking, and the second time she said, "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at me like I owe you money."

"I was thinking about the route," I said. "Past the confluence. The channel splits, and I can't remember which fork the map showed."

Something flickered behind her eyes — quick, guarded, gone. "Left fork," she said. "The right one drowns out this time of year. Your map would've shown that, if it was any good."

"It was a good map," I said, before I could stop myself.

"Yeah?" She planted the pole and held us against the current, studying me with that flat pilot's stare that takes measurements and files them somewhere permanent. "Whose map was it, Wren?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging since the mountain, arriving at last in broad daylight with nowhere to hide, and every honest answer I owned came up at once and jammed in my throat like a cork. Tell her. Tell her now, standing in ankle-deep water in a dying boat, tell her the man whose name she says in her sleep is her father, tell her the letter against your ribs has her name at the top in ink the color of tea —

"The prospector's," I said. "The one we buried. It was payment. He had no one else to leave it to."

Which was true. Every word of it true. And the lie lived in what I left out, in the great sweating mass of it, in the name *Vane* sitting in my mouth like a stone I couldn't spit or swallow.

Mara held my gaze a moment longer, weighing something. Then she pushed off, and the boat swung into the current, and she said, "Payment for what? Nobody pays a man that much just for digging a hole."

"For a promise," I said.

She snorted. "What kind of promise costs that much?"

"The kind," I said, looking at the back of her head, at the rain flattening her hair, at the daughter of Silas Vane steering us upriver toward land her father had deeded to her thirty years too late, "that you make to a dying man."

She didn't answer. The channel narrowed ahead, the left fork opening green and drowned between the trees, and somewhere behind us — far downstream, faint under the rain, but unmistakable to anyone who'd spent the last month listening for it — came the low diesel cough of an engine that wasn't ours.

Mara heard it too. Her shoulders changed, the pilot coming forward in her, and she said, without turning around:

"Wren. Get the bail bucket. And whatever's in that jacket — I hope it floats."
