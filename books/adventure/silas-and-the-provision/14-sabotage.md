## 14. Sabotage

I've been running rivers long enough to know that the water itself is honest. It doesn't lie to you, doesn't smile at you while it's picking your pocket. It just does what it does, and if you drown, it's because you made a mistake or the river made a point. Everything dishonest in my life has come from the banks—people, mostly. People with soft hands and hard eyes.

So when I woke on the third morning below the checkpoint, moored in that narrow green slough where the current barely whispered, and felt the wrongness of the boat before I understood it—the way she sat too low in the water, the way the dawn light came through the hull seam where it had no business coming through—I knew before I looked that this was not the river's doing.

The river doesn't use knives.

"Wren." I said it quietly, the way you'd say *snake* in a tent. "Wake up."

They came up out of their bedroll fast, faster than I'd have credited from someone who'd spent the night snoring like a landslide. Their eyes went where mine went. The waterline. The dark thread of it, rising.

We'd taken on maybe four inches overnight. The bilge, which I'd pumped dry before sleeping, was a small black lake.

"Back seam," I said, and was already moving, throwing gear onto the dry crate, the fuel tin, the lamp, the food bag. "Port side, low. That's not wear. That's wear *accelerated*."

Wren crouched at the stern, ran a thumb along the planking, and came back with a smear of black pitch and, beneath it, a cut. Clean. Deliberate. The kind of cut you make with a blade drawn slow, so the wood parts instead of splinters, so the damage doesn't show from outside until the river has done the rest of the work for you.

"Somebody was here," Wren said. Their voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when they were frightened and didn't want to be.

"Somebody was here while we slept," I said. "Which means somebody knew exactly where we were. Which means—" I stopped, because finishing the sentence meant admitting what I'd been not-saying for two days. "Which means the checkpoint wasn't the end of it. It was the *map*. We showed them where we sleep."

The boat was the *Alba*, and she was a bad boat when I bought her and a worse one now, but she was the only thing between us and a hundred kilometers of flooded country on foot. I watched her settle another centimeter and felt something in my chest go tight and hot. You can mock a person for loving a bad boat. Go ahead. But a bad boat that has carried you is yours, and watching her die slowly in a backwater while you stand there holding a lamp is a particular kind of grief.

"Pump," I said. "I'll find the cut."

* * *

Here is what I learned about the cut: it was not one cut. It was three, staggered along four feet of seam, each one plugged after with a dab of pitch that would hold for a day, maybe two—long enough for us to be well out in the main channel, in the teeth of the rising water, when the plugs failed and the river came in all at once. Whoever did it hadn't wanted to kill us. They'd wanted to kill us *in the middle of nowhere*, slowly, and take their time picking through what floated.

I knew the style. I'd seen it before, years ago, on boats that owed money to men who collected in kind. There's a whole grammar to this work, and I'd promised myself I'd forgotten the language.

"Voss," Wren said. Not a question. They were bailing with the cooking pot, working in a rhythm that was almost calm, which was how I'd learned to read them: the calmer the hands, the louder the fear.

"Or his men. Same thing. He doesn't hire people who improvise." I pressed my palm against the planking as if I could hold the river out by stubbornness. "They'll be watching the channel. Waiting for us to come apart in the open, then they'll come collect the map and whatever else is worth taking, and the river will be blamed for everything, because the river always is."

Wren stopped bailing. "The map."

"What about it."

"Where is it."

"Where you put it. In the oilskin. In your shirt. You haven't let it out of your shirt since the checkpoint, so don't look at me like that."

They didn't take it out. They just stood there in the gray light, water slopping around their ankles, and I watched them do the arithmetic I was also doing. Half the boat's freeboard was already a memory. The pump was a hand pump and we were two people and the river does not care about your schedule.

"Options," Wren said.

"We run her to the far bank, beach her, and walk." I said it and hated it immediately, because saying it made it real. "Through the flooded forest. Three days, if the water's kind. Four if it isn't. With what we can carry."

"The supplies."

"Half of them stay with the boat."

Wren looked at the food bag, at the fuel, at the tarp, at everything we owned in the world, and I saw them do the other arithmetic, the one where you count what a person needs to survive and find the number comes up short. I'd done that math before too. It's the math that teaches you how little a person actually is.

* * *

We didn't make it to the far bank.

I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever ledger the river keeps. We *tried*. I got the Alba moving on the pump-and-pray system, Wren bailing in shifts with the pot while I held the tiller and nursed her out of the slough into the channel, and for about twenty minutes it looked like it might work, the way things look like they might work right up until they don't.

The first plug let go at the mouth of the channel. I heard it—a soft *tick* like a knuckle cracking—and then the sound of water coming in the way water comes in when it's been invited. The second plug went before I could turn her. The third I never heard at all; I just felt the Alba go heavy and stupid under my hands, wallowing, the tiller going slack in my grip like a dead man's arm.

"Bank," I said. "Now. Whatever bank."

There wasn't a bank. There was a drowned willow island, half-submerged, its crown just breaking the brown water, and I aimed the boat at it the way you aim at anything when aiming is all you have left. We hit the willows at speed. Branches took the hull with a sound like tearing cloth, and the Alba shuddered, sighed, and sat down in the water between two trunks with the finality of an animal lying down to die.

We got the gear out. Some of it. Wren went into the flooding hold twice—twice, into water that was already at their chest, in a boat that was visibly going down, and I stood on the willow roots screaming at them to get *out*, and they came up the second time with the fuel tin and the lamp and the food bag held over their head like an offering, and I have never in my life wanted to hit someone and kiss them in the same second before, and I didn't enjoy finding out.

Then the Alba rolled onto her beam ends among the willows, and that was that. Twenty-two years of river under my feet and I stood in a drowned tree watching a bad boat drown, and I'll tell you what I felt, since we're being honest with each other: I felt rage. Not the hot kind. The cold kind, the kind that files itself and waits. Somebody had knelt in the dark not six feet from where I slept and cut three lines into a boat's hull, quiet as a surgeon, and then walked away whistling, and the river would get the blame, the river always gets the blame, and somewhere downstream a man with soft hands was already counting his money.

"Mara." Wren was wringing out the food bag. Rice, mostly ruined. Some of it salvageable if we ate it in the next day, which we would. "The map. Check the map."

"It's fine. It's in your shirt. You checked it an hour ago."

"I want to see it."

So they took it out, there among the willows, and unwrapped the oilskin, and I watched their face do something complicated.

"What," I said.

"The oilskin's fine. The oilskin did its job." They turned it over. "But I had it folded in quarters, and the fold lines—" They opened it flat across a willow stump. The map was old to begin with, Silas's pencil and ink gone brown and soft, and the water hadn't gotten to it, but the *sweat* had, days of it, pressed into the creases, and along the two main folds the paper had gone gray and friable, the lines blurred into ghosts. Wren touched one of the fold lines and a flake of the paper came away on their fingertip, and I watched them understand, the same way I'd understood at dawn, that something deliberate had been done to us.

"It's not gone," they said, too fast. "I know it. I've been over it every night, every line, every bearing, every—" They stopped. Swallowed. "I memorized it. All of it. The whole route. I can redraw it. I *have* redrawn it, in my head, a dozen times."

"You memorized a map you've been carrying in your shirt for a week."

"I memorize things." They said it defensively, and then, quieter, like a confession: "It's what I do instead of sleeping."

I looked at them—soaked to the ribs, shivering, standing in a drowned tree with a ruined map and a dead boat and a face like a man reading his own sentence—and I felt the rage in me turn a corner. Not at them. At the whole arrangement. At Voss, who'd built a machine for making people like Wren, people who memorize maps instead of sleeping because sleeping is when the past gets in. At Silas, dead on a mountain, whose map had gotten two more people drowned in willows. At myself, most of all, for knowing this grammar and speaking it anyway, for taking a stranger with haunted eyes onto my boat because I wanted to disappear and they were the best disappearing act on the waterfront.

"Right," I said. "Grim adaptation time."

* * *

We salvaged what the willows would give us. The tarp. The lamp. The fuel tin, dented but sealed. Half the food, and by half I mean the half that wasn't rice porridge now. The cooking pot, which had served as a bailer and would now serve as a cooking pot again, which I suppose is a career. My knife. Wren's knife. The letter, which Wren carried the way they carried the map, and which I did not ask about, because we had a bargain, and because some part of me had noticed that Wren had stopped meeting my eyes since the checkpoint, and I was saving that observation for later, the way you save the last of the dry rice.

We inventoried standing on the willow roots, water to our shins, and the inventory was short enough to memorize without effort.

"Three days' food if we're careful," Wren said. "One lamp. Two knives. No boat."

"No boat," I agreed.

"And the flooded forest is that way." They pointed east, where the trees stood in water to their waists and the water went back and back into the green dark, unbroken, all the way to the horizon. Somewhere out there was high ground. Somewhere out there was a village I'd sworn I'd never see again. Somewhere out there, if the elder still lived, were people who knew my name and my mother's name and a story about a man who'd come looking years ago and been turned away, and I was about to walk into all of it because a boat had died and a promise had teeth.

"You know where we're going," Wren said. Not a question either. They'd been watching me, of course. They watch everything. "You've known since the slough."

"I know a place," I said. "It's not a place I go."

"Is it far?"

"Two days through the flood, if the water's kind."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we find out what kind of swimmers we are."

Wren looked at the drowned forest, and at the dead boat, and at the gray sky where the monsoon was stacking itself up for the evening's performance, and I watched them make the choice I'd already made, and there was something almost restful in that, in standing next to someone who does the same arithmetic you do and arrives at the same bad answer.

"Then we walk," they said. And then, because they're Wren, and because they apparently cannot let a disaster pass without making it worse: "For what it's worth, I'd have sold the map by now. To anyone. For less than Voss offered. You're a bad influence."

"You're the one who memorized it."

"Somebody had to. You were busy being furious."

"I'm still furious."

"I know," Wren said, and shouldered the food bag, and stepped off the willow roots into waist-deep water, and held out a hand behind them without looking, without making it a thing. "Furious is useful. Furious keeps you moving. It's despair that drowns people."

I took the hand, because the water was deep and the day was long and because—this is the part I'd have denied under oath, a week ago—a person can only run out of reasons not to trust someone for so long.

Behind us, the Alba settled another inch among the willows, her stern going under, the river closing over her like a page turning. I didn't look back. Looking back is how you drown in a flooded forest, and besides, I already knew what I'd see: a bad boat, a good run, and three knife cuts in the dark that meant Dario Voss knew exactly where we'd slept, which meant he knew exactly where we were going, which meant the walk east was going to cost us more than dry rice.

The water rose past my belt. The forest swallowed us, green and dripping and endless.

And two days ahead—two days of leeches and drowned trails and a village I'd promised myself I'd never see again—somebody was waiting who knew my father's name.

I just didn't know it yet.
