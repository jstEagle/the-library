## 15. Walking the Flooded Forest

**WREN**

The boat died at dawn, and we buried it standing up.

That's the thing nobody tells you about losing a vessel: you don't get a ceremony. You just stand in waist-deep water, watching the last of your supplies bob away like they've got somewhere better to be, and you do the math on what's left. One dry bag. One machete. Half a map, pulped to lace. One letter, thank God, sealed in oilcloth against my ribs where I'd been carrying it like a second heartbeat.

And one pilot who was looking at me like I'd personally arranged the flood.

"Say it," Mara said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever joke is loading behind your teeth. Say it now, before we start walking, because after that I'm not going to find anything funny for a while."

I considered. The honest joke was *well, at least we don't have to bail anymore*, and I could see from her face that she'd already thought of it and hated it, which meant saying it would cost me a finger or an ally. I chose the ally.

"We walk north," I said instead. "The village on Silas's map—the one he marked with the little church. It's maybe eight kilometers through the flooded forest if the water stays at this level."

"And if it rises?"

"Then we swim, and I complain the entire way."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was the kind of almost-smile that people wear when they're holding something heavy and can't spare a hand to put it down. Then she shouldered the dry bag and started wading toward the tree line, and I followed, because following her had become, somewhere over the past week, the most sensible thing I did.

Here's what drowned jungle looks like: it looks like a cathedral someone flooded out of spite. The trunks rise black and straight out of brown water, their crowns still green, still living, because these trees have done this every year since before anyone drew a map of anything. Between them, the water moves in slow, muscular currents. Things live in it. I tried not to think about what things.

Mara moved through it like she'd been born there, which, I was beginning to suspect, was not far from true. She read the current off the surface the way other people read headlines—there's a snag under that ripple, there's a channel along that root wall—and she steered us by touch, one hand trailing in the water, correcting our line without ever seeming to decide to.

"How do you know this place?" I asked, somewhere around kilometer three.

"I know rivers."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get." She said it without heat. It was just a wall, and she'd built it long ago, and my question had bounced off it the way a thousand questions had before. But then, ten steps later, quieter: "My mother ran cargo up here. Before."

*Before.* The word hung there, doing all the work of a biography.

We walked. The water dropped from waist to knee as the ground rose in invisible increments, and by midday we were slogging through mud that sucked at our boots with the enthusiasm of something that wanted to keep us. Mosquitoes found us. Leeches found us. I killed eleven leeches and named none of them, which I considered a mark of personal growth.

It was Mara who saw the smoke first—a thin gray thread rising above the canopy, maybe half a kilometer off our line. She stopped so suddenly I nearly went into her back.

"What?"

"Nothing," she said. But her voice had changed. "Keep walking."

We kept walking. And twenty minutes later, when the trees opened onto a shelf of high ground and I saw the village—stilt houses, drying racks, a church with a tin roof gone orange with rust—I noticed that Mara had gone very quiet in a way that wasn't about caution.

"You know this place," I said.

"I told you. I know rivers."

"Mara."

"Don't." She turned, and for the first time since I'd met her, she looked genuinely unsteady—not afraid, exactly, but like a woman reading water she didn't trust. "Whatever happens in there, let me talk. Don't say my name. Don't say—" She stopped herself. Swallowed whatever came next. "Just let me talk."

I raised both hands, which in leech country is a gesture of considerable vulnerability. "Your lead."

**MARA**

I want to say I didn't know why I was shaking. That would be a lie, and I promised myself, coming downriver with this stranger and his dead man's map, that I would stop lying to myself even if I never managed it with anyone else.

I knew the village. Of course I knew it. Ban Kham. My mother used to tie up here when I was small enough to ride the cargo in a sling against her chest, and later, when I was big enough to be useful, I bailed bilge and hauled rope while she drank tea with the headman's wife and argued prices with a laugh in her voice. There was a woman here, Auntie Seng, who taught me to gut fish and told me I argued like my mother and would die stubborn like her too. I hadn't been back in nine years. Nine years is nothing to a river and everything to a person.

Wren gave me the lead, which surprised me. Everything about Wren surprised me lately—the quick hands in the rapid, the way they'd memorized half a drowning map without being asked, the way they watched me like I was a puzzle worth solving rather than a tool worth using. I didn't trust it. Trust had gotten my mother killed and my father vanished, and I had built my whole life on the principle that the only safe passenger is the one on another boat.

But I took the lead anyway.

Auntie Seng saw us before we reached the stairs. She was on her porch shelling beans, tiny and brown and sharp-eyed as a kingfisher, and I watched recognition move across her face like weather.

"Mara Vane," she said. Not a question. "Nine years, and you come walking out of the flood like a bad debt."

"Auntie." My voice cracked on the second syllable, which I hated. "You remember me."

"I remember everyone who owes me fish-gutting lessons." She set down her bowl and looked us both over—the mud to our thighs, the dry bag, Wren's face with its two-day beard and its guide's flat watchfulness. "And who is this?"

"Wren. They're—" I hesitated. Every word I picked felt like a stone I'd have to carry later. "They're keeping a promise."

"Hm." Auntie Seng looked at Wren the way she used to look at fish that might be off. "Promises are heavier than gold and rot slower. Feed them both, then. Come."

She fed us rice and dried fish and a sour soup that tasted like the forest had been boiled down to its essentials, and while we ate, the porch filled. Word travels fast in a river village; word about a stranger walks, but word about Mara Vane runs. Old men who'd known my mother. Women who remembered me as the girl who fell off the dock twice in one afternoon. A boy of maybe twelve who stared at Wren's boots like they were from another planet, which, fair.

And it was good. I'll admit that. It was warm in a way I'd forgotten warmth worked, sitting among people who knew my name without me having to earn it fresh. For about an hour I let myself forget the sunken boat and the men with knives and the map dissolving in my memory like sugar.

Then old Thongdee came up the stairs.

He was the oldest man in the village, or close enough—shriveled down to sinew and opinion, eyes gone pale as river fog. He lowered himself onto the bench across from me with the care of a man handling his own bones, looked at me for a long moment, and said:

"You have his chin."

I blinked. "Whose?"

"The farang prospector." He said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "The one who came looking for you."

The porch went quiet. Even the boy stopped staring at the boots.

"He came—" My mouth was dry. "When?"

Thongdee counted on his fingers, which took a while, because his fingers had opinions. "Eight years ago. Maybe nine. Rainy season, like this one. He came up from the south in a hired boat, sick already—you could see it in him, the cough, the way he held his side. He asked for the daughter of Lien Vane. He had papers. Deeds, he said. Land." The old man's voice flattened. "He sat right where you're sitting."

"What did you tell him?"

What happened next happened fast, and it happened to me rather than being done by me. Auntie Seng spoke first, from the doorway, and her voice had an edge I'd never heard pointed at me before.

"We told him there was no daughter here."

"Why?" The word came out of me like something torn. "Why would you—she was your friend, my mother was your—"

"Because men with deeds and sickness and far-off debts bring trouble, child." Auntie Seng's hands were folded tight. "Because your mother came through here two years before that, running from something, and she made us swear—if anyone ever came asking after her or hers, we'd never seen them. She paid for that promise. In silver, and in the friendship we broke to keep it." Her eyes were wet and furious at once. "So when the sick farang sat on this porch with his papers and his photographs and asked for Mara Vane, we sent him downriver. We told him no such person existed. And he believed us, because he was dying and tired, and he thanked us for our honesty."

Photographs. The word landed in me like a hook finding flesh.

"He had photographs?"

"Of a girl," Thongdee said. "At different ages. School age, then older. He kept them in a tin box, and he showed them to us one by one, like a man showing scars. He said—" The old man paused, and when he continued, his voice had gone gentle in a way that was worse than cruelty. "He said, *I missed all of it. I only want her to have what's hers.*"

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs just reported for duty and took me to the edge of the porch, where I gripped the rail and looked out at the brown water swallowing the forest, and I breathed, because the alternative was making a sound I couldn't take back.

Eight years. Eight years ago, a dying man had sat on this exact bench, holding pictures of me—of a life he'd watched from a distance I was only beginning to measure—and been turned away by people I loved, on orders from a mother I'd mourned, and he'd gone back down the river alone to finish dying.

And I had spent those same eight years hating him.

"Mara." Wren's voice, low, beside me. Not touching me. Just there, the way they'd learned to be—present without presuming. "Breathe."

"You knew." I turned on them, and the fury arrived right on schedule, because fury is easier than grief and always has been. "On the boat. When you looked at the margins of that map. You knew something."

"I knew he tracked your life. Sketches, dates. I didn't know he'd come here." Wren held my stare, steady as a mooring post. "I'm learning the rest the same time you are."

"Liar."

"Probably," they admitted. "But not about this."

Behind us, Thongdee cleared his throat, and there was something else in it now—something careful.

"There is more," he said. "Before he left, the farang gave me something. He said, *if a girl with Lien's chin ever comes up this river, give her this, and tell her I'm sorry I listened.*"

He reached into his shirt and brought out a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with fishing line, worn soft as cloth from eight years against an old man's skin.

I didn't take it. I couldn't make my hands work.

So Wren took it—of course they did, hands quicker than mine, always—and held it out to me across the space between us, and said, quietly enough that only I could hear:

"Whenever you're ready. No sooner."

Downriver, faint under the sound of the flood, came the low diesel knock of an engine that neither of us recognized—and both of us feared.
