## 17. Confession in the Rain

The rain had been falling for three days without once committing to a mood. It drizzled in the mornings like it was embarrassed to be there, then gathered its courage by afternoon and came down in sheets that turned the flooded side channel into something between a lake and a rumor of one. We were marooned under a fisherman's lean-to on a sliver of high ground the river had forgotten to swallow, and I had run out of things to say to Wren Kavanagh that weren't true.

That's a dangerous place to be with a person.

I'd noticed it the night before, actually—how our conversations had been circling a hole in the middle. We talked about water levels and fuel and whether the tinned fish had gone off (it had; we ate it anyway), and every so often Wren would look at me like they were doing arithmetic they didn't want me to see. I know that look. It's the look you give a ledger when you already know the number is bad.

"You keep staring at my map," I said, morning of the fourth day, because it wasn't theirs anymore than it was mine, and because I was tired of pretending not to notice. "Or what's left of it."

Half of Silas's map had drowned in the backwater when Voss's men gutted our hull. What survived was a sodden triangle of paper that Wren had dried over the lamp like a nursemaid with a sick child, turning it, coaxing it, memorizing it line by line until I half suspected they could redraw it from memory with their eyes shut. Maybe they could. That should have comforted me. Instead it sat in my chest like a stone I kept swallowing and coughing back up.

Wren looked up. Rain drummed the tin roof of the lean-to, and underneath that sound there was the other sound, the big slow breathing of the flood, and underneath *that* there was whatever Wren was about to say, which I could feel coming the way you feel a rapid before you see it—a change in the texture of the silence.

"Mara." They said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "There's something I have to tell you. And you're going to want to hit me, so maybe sit down first."

"I've been sitting down for three days," I said. "It hasn't improved my temperament."

"Fair." They took a breath. People think mountain guides are calm people. They're not. They're people who have learned to be very still while terrified, which is different, and worse, and I recognized it because pilots are the same animal.

And then they told me.

They told me about the border mountains, and the dying prospector above the treeline, and the promise made to a stranger with frost in his beard and hours left in his body. They told me about the map traded for a vow: *find my daughter, give her what's hers.* They told me about Voss's scouts on the ridge, the flight downriver, the leaking boat, all of it—the whole architecture of coincidence I'd been living inside for two weeks without knowing I was living inside anything at all.

Then they reached into their jacket and brought out an envelope, soft-cornered, oil-stained, sealed with wax that had cracked years ago.

"This is his letter," Wren said. "He wrote it for her. For you, Mara. The daughter's name in it is Mara Vane."

The rain got very loud. Or I did. Hard to say which.

* * *

Here is what I will tell you about fury, since apparently I'm the expert now: it doesn't arrive all at once. It arrives in installments, like a bill.

First installment: the name. *Mara Vane.* My mother's surname, the one I stopped using when she died because carrying it felt like carrying her coffin through every room I entered. He knew it. Of course he knew it. He'd chosen it with her.

Second installment: the map. All those nights on the boat when I'd caught Wren studying it by lamplight and thought, *there's a person running from something*—and they were, but they were also guarding something, and the something was mine. My father's riverside sketches. His little marginal notes in a hand I hadn't seen since I was nine years old and he walked out of our lives with a pack frame and a promise to send for us. The route he used to trace on the kitchen table with a blunt pencil while I sat on his knee: *someday, little fish, someday we'll run this water together.*

Someday. Prospector arithmetic. Someday is the currency men like him pay debts in.

Third installment, and this is the one that burned: they knew. How long? Long enough. Long enough to watch me refuse passengers at the dock, long enough to bargain with me, run rapids with me, trade stories with me by lamplight in this exact lean-to—everything except the one true thing. I had told them about my mother. About the village that stopped saying my father's name in front of me out of kindness, which is its own kind of cruelty. I had handed them the raw meat of my life and they had held their secret like a winning card.

"You read it," I said. My voice came out flat, which frightened me more than shouting would have. Shouting is honest. Flat is what comes after. "You opened his letter. You read words he wrote for me and then you looked me in the face for—" I did the math, and the math made it worse. "Weeks."

"The letter was sealed," Wren said. "I read it two nights ago. First time. I swear it on—" They stopped. Whatever they were going to swear on, they'd evidently decided they didn't own enough of it to swear with. "I was going to tell you. I was trying to find a way that didn't—"

"That didn't what? Cost you anything?"

"Yes," they said simply. "Exactly that."

At least they didn't dress it up. That's the thing about Wren Kavanagh I kept tripping over: they were a liar about facts and brutally honest about motives, which is backwards from most people and somehow harder to hate cleanly.

"Why?" I asked. "Why hide it? What did you think I'd do—leave? We're marooned in a flooded forest. Where exactly was I going to storm off to?"

"I thought you'd stop trusting me," Wren said. "And I need you to trust me. There's a man downstream who wants what your father left you, and the only way either of us gets through this is if we're rowing the same direction." A pause. "Also—and I know how this sounds—I thought if you knew, you'd look at me differently. And I didn't want you to. Not yet. That's the selfish version. That's the true version."

The rain hammered down. Somewhere across the drowned channel a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining, and I thought: *my father is dead.* I had known it abstractly since Wren first told the story of the man above the treeline—I'd known it the way you know weather coming over a ridge, as information—but it landed now, all of it, the whole weight of him. Dead on a mountain, alone except for a stranger, holding a map of my river and a letter with my name on it, having spent his last breath not on water or food or shelter but on making sure something of his reached me.

Anger is easier than that. Anger has somewhere to go.

So I stayed angry.

"Get out," I said.

"It's a lean-to," Wren said. "There's no out."

"Then stand in the rain. You've earned it."

They looked at me for a long moment. Then they set the letter down on the plank between us, squared it so the corner faced me, like a document being served, and stood, and walked out into the downpour without a word. That, more than anything, made me want to throw something at them. Even their obedience was infuriatingly decent.

* * *

I sat with the letter for a long time.

I want to say I read it immediately. I didn't. I sat looking at it the way you look at a rapid you have to run, reading the water, finding the line, telling yourself the holes are survivable. The wax seal was cracked but intact. His thumbprint was in the wax—pressed there deliberately, I realized later, the way illiterate men sign, except my father could write, so it wasn't a signature, it was a touch. He had touched this envelope knowing it would reach me, and pressed his thumb into it so that some physical part of him would arrive even if the words failed.

Prospectors are sentimental about strange things. Gold, mostly. But also names, and rivers, and daughters, apparently, when it's too late to be any use.

I broke the seal in the end because the alternative was sitting in the rain next to Wren, and pride is a current too—it carries you places you wouldn't walk to on purpose.

The paper inside was soft as cloth. The handwriting was the blunt pencil hand from the kitchen table, gone shakier, crowded toward the top of the page like a man rationing his strength.

*Mara,*

*If you're reading this, then somebody kept a promise for me, which is more than I deserve, because I never kept the ones I made you. I told you I'd come back for you. I did come back. Fourteen years ago I stood at the edge of your village and a woman told me you were better off without me, and she was right, and I let her be right because being right was easier than being sorry. I have been sorry ever since, which is worth nothing, I know. Sorry is what cowards feel instead of doing.*

*I found land. Good land, high ground, where the river can't take it no matter how fat it gets in the wet season. I meant it for you. I drew it all out—the map, the marks, the boundaries. It isn't gold. I chased gold my whole life and it never once chased me back. This is better than gold. This is ground that stays.*

*I don't ask you to forgive me. Forgiveness is a door and I nailed mine shut from the outside years ago. I only ask that you take what's yours. Not for my sake. For yours. Every person needs one piece of earth that can't be taken, argued away, or sold out from under them. I couldn't give you a childhood. Let me give you that.*

*Your father,*
*Silas*

*P.S. — If the one who brings you this is still standing nearby, feed them. They'll argue. Feed them anyway. People who keep promises to strangers are always hungry, and always too proud to say so.*

I laughed. That's the thing nobody tells you about grief—it keeps terrible company. There I was, crying like the monsoon itself, and the laugh came up through the middle of it, ugly and wet, because of course he wrote a postscript. Of course he managed to be funny in a deathbed letter. He used to do bits at the dinner table, my father, voices and impressions, my mother laughing into her hand trying to stay stern. I had buried all of that under fourteen years of anger and it came up out of the ground like bones after a flood.

I walked out into the rain.

Not to forgive anyone. To read the rest of the letter, because there was a second page, and because the lean-to's lamp was guttering and I wanted the grey daylight of the storm to read by, and because—fine, because the postscript had gotten under my skin, and Wren was standing at the edge of the trees getting thoroughly soaked, arms crossed, making a show of studying the flooded channel as if it might reveal something new in the four hundredth minute of observation.

I stood beside them. Close enough to share nothing, close enough to be heard over the rain.

"He says to feed you," I said. "He says you'll argue."

"He's not wrong," Wren said. Their voice was careful. They were giving me space the size of a province and pretending it was nothing.

I read the second page silently. It was shorter. Mostly it was the map references—boundary markers, the old fig tree, the notch in the ridge—but at the bottom, in smaller writing, almost squeezed in:

*The land borders the village. Tell them the deeds cover their fields too. Voss's people have been buying paper and calling it ownership. Paper can be beaten with older paper. Mine is older.*

I looked up. Downstream, somewhere past all this brown water, Dario Voss was moving, and he was moving toward the same ground my father had died trying to give me. The letter wasn't just a confession. It was ammunition. It was, in its stubborn dead-man's way, a plan.

"Wren," I said.

They turned. Rain ran off their chin. Up close I could see what the last weeks had cost them—the hollowed cheeks, the hands that never quite stopped moving—and I understood suddenly that everything they'd done, including the lying, had been done by someone drowning quietly and calling it swimming. I knew that swim. I'd been doing it since my mother died.

"I'm still furious with you," I said.

"I'd be worried if you weren't."

"But my father trusted you with the only thing he had left. So I'm going to trust you too—not because you've earned it, but because he bet on you, and I'm not going to let his last good decision be wasted on my grudge." I folded the letter along its old creases and put it inside my shirt, against my skin, where the rain couldn't get it. "This changes when we're dry. You and I are going to have a much longer conversation, and you are going to answer every question I have, in order, without editing."

"Understood," Wren said. And then, because they were incapable of leaving well enough alone: "For what it's worth—he talked about you. At the end. Not your name, he was past names, but he described you. Said you'd have your mother's stubbornness and his river sense, and that you'd probably be angry, and that you'd be right to be."

The rain filled the pause.

"And?" I said.

"And he was right on both counts," Wren said. "The angry part and the right part."

I almost smiled. Almost. It's important to maintain standards.

We stood there together in the downpour, two soaked liars and truth-tellers on a shrinking island, and I was still deciding what we were to each other when Wren went very still—that guide's stillness, the terrified kind—and tilted their head toward the channel.

"Engine," they said.

I heard it a heartbeat later. Low, throttled down, moving careful through flooded timber the way only someone who knew these waters—or had a chart of them—would dare. Coming upriver. Toward us.

Toward the land my father had left me.

"Voss," I said, and the word came out steady, and I noticed that for the first time in fourteen years I wasn't afraid of what was coming for me.

I was done running. Apparently it runs in the family.
