## 18. The Last Crossing

**Wren**

There is a particular kind of clarity that arrives when you've run out of good options. It's not peace. It's more like the moment on a mountain when the weather turns and you stop calculating and simply move, because the arithmetic is done and the only thing left is your body and your will. I'd felt it twice before in my life. Once on the West Ridge of Gaurishankar, once in a jail cell in Cusco. Both times I survived by being nobody, by slipping away.

This time there was no slipping. Voss's patrol boat sat at the landing below the village like a black beetle on the water, engines ticking as they cooled, two of his men smoking on the deck with the loose-shouldered confidence of people who have never once been the ones being hunted. Behind us, the flooded forest breathed and groaned. Ahead, upriver, the land Silas had deeded sat on a shelf above the coming flood, and the only way to reach it before the crest—and before Voss—was the boat Voss had brought to stop us.

"You're smiling," Mara said. She stood beside me in the treeline, water to her shins, her hair plastered to her skull. She did not look like someone who appreciated smiles.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's a terrible smile. Like a dog deciding about a porcupine."

"It's the smile of a person doing math," I said. "Two of them on deck. Probably a third at the wheel. Voss himself below or ashore. One boat, two of us, and a river about to eat everything it can reach."

"And the math says?"

I watched the deck. The smokers were on the downstream rail, their backs half-turned, arguing about cigarettes or cards or the wisdom of working for a man who was about to be very rich or very arrested. The wheelhouse windows were fogged. The river behind the boat was rising in visible increments, brown water climbing the pilings like something patient and enormous coming up for air.

"The math says," I said, "that a man like Voss never once imagined anyone would steal his own boat from him. That's the flaw in every fortress. The door is only guarded on the side people are expected to come from."

"And we're expected to come from?"

"Anywhere but here."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off her chin. I had learned, over the past weeks, to read her silences the way I read weather—there were silences that meant *no* and silences that meant *not yet* and one particular silence, rare as a blue moon, that meant *I have already decided and I'm letting you catch up*. This was that one.

"Wheelhouse is mine," she said. "You handle the lines. And Wren—if we do this, we do it all the way. No half measures, no clever exits. The crest hits that shelf by nightfall. If we're not above it when it comes, the river decides for us."

"No retreat," I said.

"No retreat," she agreed, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before. Not fear. Something under fear, older and steadier. Her father's river was about to try to kill her one more time, and she was done being polite about it.

* * *

**Mara**

I have run that river in flood twice before, and both times I was younger and both times I lied to myself about the danger, which is its own kind of equipment—every pilot carries some. This time I didn't lie. This time I stood in the wheelhouse of a stolen patrol boat with rain hammering the glass and the gauge showing the river up nearly four meters and climbing, and I told myself the truth: the crossing ahead would kill us if we were slow, kill us if we were greedy, kill us if we were unlucky. The only thing it forgave was precision.

Wren came up from the deck soaked through, coiling lines with the quick economy of someone who'd spent a life making themselves useful in bad places. They'd cut the mooring so clean the men on the rail hadn't noticed until the gap opened, and by the time the shouting started we were already in the channel, and by the time the first shot cracked across the water we were around the bend and the river had swallowed the sound.

"Third man was asleep in the cabin," Wren reported. "He'll have a headache and a story nobody believes."

"Voss?"

"Ashore. I saw him on the levee as we pulled out." A pause. "He was smiling."

"Of course he was. He thinks we've made a mistake he can profit from." I fed the wheel a correction as the current grabbed us, shoving the bow toward the cutbank. "He's spent his whole life believing everything is for sale. It's the only religion he has. And right now he's certain that somewhere between here and the deed house, we'll understand that too."

"Do you think we will?"

I glanced at them. Wren was watching the water, but I knew the question wasn't about the water. It was about the map folded in the oilskin inside their jacket, the letter, the promise made to a dying man on a mountainside. It was about the debt that had ridden on Wren's shoulders like a yoke since before I met them.

"No," I said. "I think we're past the part where we could sell out even if we wanted to. That's what this is." I nodded at the river, the rain, the whole drowning world. "The point of no retreat isn't a place. It's a decision you made earlier without noticing."

The channel narrowed. The flood had erased the banks entirely—trees stood to their shoulders in brown water, and the current ran through them in braided, muscular strands, each one strong enough to roll us. Somewhere under all that water was the channel I'd known since I was six years old, and I was steering by memory and by the behavior of the current, the way the boils and seams told me where the deep water ran, the way my father taught me before he taught me anything else, before he taught me what it was to be left.

"Deed house shelf is eleven kilometers up," I said. "The crest is moving faster than we are. We have maybe two hours of margin, and that's if the narrows don't eat us."

"And if they do?"

"Then Voss gets his river after all." I put the throttle down and the engines answered with a sound like a held breath released. "Hold on to something. The narrows don't negotiate."

* * *

**Wren**

I want to record, for whoever eventually reads this or doesn't, that the narrows were the single most frightening forty minutes of a life that has not been shy about frightening moments. I have hung from ice walls in wind that erased my own screaming. I have crossed a glacier at night with a client whose headlamp died and whose faith in me was the only rope between us. None of it was this.

The narrows in flood were a single throat of water compressed between drowned ridgelines, and the river came through it like a mob. The patrol boat—Voss's boat, heavy, built for intimidation rather than agility—surfed down standing waves taller than the wheelhouse, and every time we dropped into a trough I was certain, with a calm and detailed certainty, that this was the one where we didn't come back up.

Mara drove it like she'd been born at that wheel, which, as far as I could tell, she nearly had. She read the water the way I read rock—instinctively, continuously, her hands making a hundred small corrections a minute, each one meaningless alone and together the only reason we were alive. At the worst of it, when a strainer—a whole tree, root ball the size of a room—came spinning out of the flood dead ahead, she didn't panic. She waited. Three seconds that lasted a season. Then she put the boat hard against the current seam and the river itself flung us sideways, past the tree close enough to count the branches, and we were through.

"Your father," I shouted over the engines, because it was true and because the truth seemed suddenly important, "would have been proud of that."

Her jaw tightened. "My father," she shouted back, "taught me to wait. Everything else he taught me by leaving." But her hands on the wheel had gone gentler, and I understood that I had given her something, though I couldn't have said what.

We came out of the narrows into the wide flooded plain above it, and the land Silas had deeded rose ahead of us—a shelf of high ground with the deed house on it, an old stone building with a tin roof, and around it the village, sandbagged and half-evacuated, people moving on the levee like ants who have finally accepted the rain. And behind us, downstream, closing fast, came a second boat. Voss's other patrol boat, the one we hadn't known about, running the flood with the same arrogance we'd just survived, except with more horsepower and less to lose.

"Margin's gone," Mara said quietly.

I looked at the river, at the crest coming down the valley—a visible thing now, a raised line of water moving like weather—and I did the last of the math. We could run for the landing and probably make it. We could beach the boat, sprint for the deed house, and let Voss's men come up the levee behind us into a village full of witnesses. Or we could do the thing that had been sitting in the back of my mind since the narrows, the reckless, all-in thing, the thing with no exit.

"The weir," I said.

Mara went still. "The old diversion weir? That's not a channel, that's a rumor. It hasn't been passable in thirty years."

"It's passable at this level. The flood's over the top of it by two meters. And it cuts straight to the deed house landing while the main channel swings wide." I turned to face her. "Voss's pilot won't take it. He doesn't know the water and he's got too much boat and too much fear. It buys us twenty minutes."

"It kills us if I'm wrong about the level."

"Are you wrong?"

She looked at the weir—distant, half-drowned, the old concrete lip invisible under churning flood—and I watched her do what her father must have done a thousand times: read the water and bet her life on the reading. When she looked back at me, she was smiling, and it was a terrible smile, like a dog deciding about a porcupine, and I recognized it because it was mine.

"Hang on to something," she said.

* * *

**Mara**

The weir was everything I remembered and worse, and I remembered it from stories, which should have been warning enough. My father ran it once, the year before he left, with a boat full of survey gear and a crew who wouldn't stop talking about it for a decade. He said the trick was to come at it slightly downstream of square, throttle up through the boil above the lip, and then—this was the part he said with the look he saved for important lessons—*trust the boat more than your fear*.

I came at it slightly downstream of square. I throttled up through the boil. And when the world went white and the hull slammed the flooded lip and for one long, screaming second we were airborne over water that had never been flown over in my lifetime, I trusted the boat more than my fear.

We landed hard enough to knock the compass off its mount. We shipped a ton of river over the bow. Wren, who had been braced on the bow cleat with a line wrapped twice around one fist, came up dripping, whooping—a sound I had never heard from them, wild and unguarded, the sound of a person who has stopped running—and I found that I was making it too.

The back channel carried us, fast and clean, straight as an arrow toward the deed house landing, while behind us the main channel stayed empty. Voss's boat never even turned. Twenty minutes. Maybe more.

We came alongside the landing as the first of the crest water lipped over the lower steps, and the villagers were already there—drawn by the engine noise, by the sheer improbability of a patrol boat coming down the dead channel. Old faces, young faces, the elder Tomás with his white hair plastered down, and every one of them staring at the boat, at us, at the black paint and the name on the transom: *PROVISIONER II*. Voss's name for his own greed, sailing into their village under our hands.

"Voss is coming," Wren said, jumping to the landing, hauling the line. "Two boats, maybe twenty men. They'll be here within the hour. The map—" They pulled the oilskin from inside their jacket, and I saw their hands were shaking, and I saw that it wasn't fear shaking them. "The map goes to the council. Now. Before he lands. Before anyone can buy it, take it, or burn it."

Tomás looked from Wren to me and back. "And you? What do you want out of this, stranger? Everyone wants something."

Wren looked at the map in their hands. I watched them hold the whole weight of it—Voss's debt, the years of running, the promise made to a dying man above the treeline—and I watched them set it down, all of it, like a pack finally shrugged off at camp.

"Nothing," Wren said. "That's the point. It was never mine to want." They held the map out to Tomás. "It's his. Silas's. And hers." A nod at me, and something in their face I'll keep private. "Deliver it where it belongs."

Tomás took it with both hands, the way you take something that might be a live coal.

And that was when the first shot came—not from the river, but from the ridge road above the village, where a gray truck had stopped and men were spilling out of it with rifles, and Voss himself stood at the truck's door in a dry coat, looking down at us with the expression of a man arriving at an auction he intends to win.

The crest hit the lower village steps. The river rose an inch, then another, patient and enormous.

"No retreat," I said.

"No retreat," Wren said, and we turned to meet him.
