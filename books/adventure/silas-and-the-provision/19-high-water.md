## 19. High Water

The river had been talking to us all night.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean it the way you'd mean it if you'd spent three days walking through a drowned forest with water up to your ribs, listening to something enormous and patient moving through the trees like a landlord coming to collect. The river had been talking, and by morning it had stopped being subtle about it.

We came out of the flooded jungle at first light, Mara ahead of me with the machete she'd taken off one of Voss's men—she hadn't asked where he was when she took it, and I hadn't asked either—and the village opened up around us like a held breath finally let go. Stilt houses standing in brown water. Canoes tied to porch posts. A church roof poking above the floodline with its cross bent sideways, which felt about right for everything I'd been through since a dying man traded me a map for a promise I still didn't fully understand why I'd made.

And at the far end of the main channel, on the only dry ground left—a low bluff of packed earth above the waterline—stood the deed house. Silas's land. The reason for all of it.

Mara stopped when she saw it. Just stopped, mid-stride, and stood there in the rain with water streaming off her jaw and her knuckles white around the machete handle.

"That's it," she said. Not a question. She'd known since the confession in the rain two nights ago, since I'd told her the truth and watched her face do things I'll spend the rest of my life trying to describe. "That's his."

"That's yours," I said.

She didn't answer that. Some words are too heavy to pick up in front of witnesses, and the whole village had turned out to look at us—the strangers who'd come walking out of the drowned forest, one of them a daughter returned from thirty years of absence, the other one nobody knew yet but everybody would by nightfall. Word travels fast in river country. Faster than boats. Faster than floods, sometimes.

We waded the last stretch. An old woman met us at the base of the bluff—Dona Esmeralda, though I didn't know her name yet—and she looked at Mara for a long moment, then at me, then at the bundle wrapped in oilcloth under my arm, and she said, "You're expected."

"By whom?" I asked, though I already knew.

"The man with the boat," she said. "He came an hour ago. He has soldiers' eyes and a merchant's mouth." She spat into the water, which I took as a review. "He is waiting at the deed house. He says he will wait until the water takes it if he must."

* * *

Voss had set himself up on the deed house porch like he owned it already. Two enforcers flanked him—the ones who'd slashed our hull back in chapter fourteen of my nightmares, though I tried not to think of my life in chapters anymore; it encouraged melodrama—and behind them, on the steps, sat maybe forty villagers, arranged in a loose crescent with the wary patience of people who had watched outsiders argue over their land before and knew how these scenes usually ended. Usually with the outsider winning.

Voss looked good. That was the thing about Dario Voss—he always looked good. Dry despite the rain, some trick of posture or tailoring or sheer refusal, his boots polished, his smile calibrated. When he saw me he spread his hands like a priest welcoming home a sinner.

"Wren Kavanagh." My name in his mouth sounded like a lien. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy," I said. "Actually, you can't. Your guys sank his boat."

"I heard about the boat." He clicked his tongue. "Regrettable. But you know how it is on the river—things leak, things sink, things get lost." His eyes went to the oilcloth bundle. "Things get found again. Come inside, Wren. Let's settle this like professionals."

"We can settle it right here," I said. "Your audience seems interested."

Something flickered across his face—annoyance, quickly filed away. He'd wanted this private. Private meant leverage, and leverage meant I signed whatever he put in front of me. Public meant witnesses, and Voss hated witnesses unless they were buying.

"As you like." He gestured, and one of the enforcers produced a document case, laying papers on the porch rail: my debt, itemized with interest calculated at rates that would embarrass a loan shark with a conscience. "Your balance stands at a figure you cannot pay in three lifetimes. Or—" he tapped the map bundle, "—you hand that over, and we part as friends. Clean slate. You walk away free. You might even walk away rich, if you're smart enough to take a finder's fee."

"And what exactly do you think is in there?" I asked. "Gold?"

"I think it's deeds," Voss said smoothly. "River claims. Gold claims along this stretch, filed decades ago by a prospector named Silas Vane. Claims that are legally mine to purchase, once the instrument transfers. The old man had no heirs. Abandoned property escheats. It's all very tidy."

"He had an heir," I said.

Voss's smile didn't move, but something behind it did. "Wren. Don't complicate this."

"Too late. Complicating things is the only skill I have left."

I unwrapped the oilcloth. The map came out into the rain—Silas's map, the one that had cost me a boat, a reputation's last shreds, and most of my illusions, its margins dense with his handwriting and those small pencil sketches of a girl growing older year by year, drawn from memory, from distance, from whatever mixture of love and cowardice had kept him watching from afar instead of walking up to a door. I'd memorized every line of it by now. Every contour. Every name. I could redraw it from nothing, which was fortunate, because half of it had drowned with our boat and the other half had been living in my head ever since.

But the map itself wasn't the point. The point was folded inside it, dry because I'd kept it against my chest the whole way: the letter. And tucked behind the letter, the thing Voss actually wanted and had never seen—the deeds themselves, pulled from their oilskin sleeve, real paper, real seals, real signatures.

I held them up.

"These aren't gold claims," I said loudly enough for the crescent of villagers to hear. "I know, because I had them read. Twice. By people who don't work for Dario Voss." I watched his face carefully as I said it. "These are land deeds. Title to this bluff, the high ground above the flood line, the channel rights, the ferry landing. Deeded by Silas Vane to his daughter, Mara Vane. Who is standing right there."

Every head turned. Mara stood at the edge of the porch steps, rain-soaked, expression unreadable, and I saw her decide—not to speak, not yet, just to let me finish what I'd started. It was the hardest gift anyone had ever given me: silence, freely offered, while I talked my way through a minefield.

"There is no gold," I went on. "There never was. What there is, is fraud—Voss has been selling 'river gold claims' up and down this waterway for two years, backed by documents he doesn't hold and never did. Half the investors in this room's province bought into them. Am I wrong?"

A murmur ran through the villagers. Somewhere in it, a voice—I'd learn later it was the schoolteacher—said, "My cousin bought shares in the Voss Consolidated."

"My brother-in-law too."

Voss raised his hands. "Gentlemen. This is a desperate debtor slandering—"

"Your own ledger says otherwise." I nodded at the document case. "Go ahead. Check the itemization. Page four. 'Acquisition costs, Vane instruments.' You've been carrying these deeds on your books as assets for eighteen months, Voss. You knew they existed. You knew they were land, not gold. And you've been telling investors gold anyway, pocketing the difference."

That was the part I'd pieced together in the flooded side channel, lamplight and rain, Mara asleep across the boat from me, going through Silas's margins line by line. The old man hadn't just tracked his daughter. He'd tracked Voss. He'd known what Voss was doing with his name and his filings, and the letter—it was addressed to the village council as much as to Mara—said so in careful, furious prose. Silas Vane had died holding the one piece of paper that could hang Dario Voss, and he'd trusted a stranger to carry it home.

I still didn't know why me. Maybe because I was nobody. Maybe because nobodies deliver.

"So here's the settlement," I said, and my voice cracked on it, which I decided to allow, because some cracks are load-bearing. "I owe this man money. Real money, I won't pretend otherwise. And he wants this map as payment. Fine." I turned to the crescent of villagers, to the gray-haired man at its center whom Dona Esmeralda had whispered was head of the council. "Sir. I'm surrendering this map—not to Dario Voss, but to this village council, as evidence, along with the letter, in the matter of the fraud committed against the investors of the Voss Consolidated. The deeds themselves belong to Mara Vane. That's not negotiable, by me or anyone."

The councilman rose slowly. Behind him the villagers were on their feet now, and the sound they made wasn't a cheer—it was quieter and worse than that, the sound of forty people doing arithmetic about money they'd lost and a man who'd taken it.

Voss's enforcers shifted. One of them put a hand inside his jacket, and Mara moved—just one step, casual as weather, the machete suddenly visible in hers—and the hand came back out empty. River country has rules about weapons drawn in front of a crowd, and even hired men know them.

"Wren." Voss's voice had dropped all its warmth, which was honestly a relief. "You understand what you're doing. That debt doesn't vanish because you've found a crowd to hide behind."

"It doesn't vanish," I agreed. "It gets voided. Because the debt was secured against proceeds from the Vane instruments—which you've just been publicly shown to have misrepresented. Fraud vitiates contract. I had a lawyer explain it to me in a bar in Manaus, and he charged me two beers, so it's probably solid." I shrugged. "Besides. You can't collect from a man with no assets. I own a compass and a grudge. Take your pick."

For a long moment, nobody moved. The rain fell. The river kept talking below us, louder now, and I glanced past Voss's shoulder and saw what I'd been hearing all morning: the water had come up over the lower channel during the last hour, sliding silent and brown through the trees, swallowing the moored canoes one by one. The flood crest was arriving early. The river was done negotiating too.

Voss looked at the crowd. At the councilman. At the rising water. At me. And I watched him do the math every predator does eventually—the moment when the prey turns out to be standing on higher ground. His mouth opened, closed, opened again.

"This isn't finished," he said.

"It never is," I said. "But it's finished here."

He gathered his papers. Slowly, deliberately, a man refusing to hurry for anyone's benefit, though his hands weren't quite steady. The enforcers fell in behind him. At the bottom of the steps he paused, and for one strange second he looked almost tired—an aging man in the rain whose long game had just collapsed in front of forty witnesses and a rising river.

"You were the best guide I ever blacklisted," he said.

"I'm going to need that in writing," I said.

Then he was gone, down into the channel, into a boat, into the brown water heading downstream, and the crowd's noise broke over us like the flood itself—questions, shouts, the schoolteacher already demanding the ledger be examined, Dona Esmeralda laughing in a way that suggested she'd bet on us from the start.

Mara appeared beside me. She didn't say anything for a while. We just stood there on Silas's bluff, on her land, watching the water climb.

"You gave it away," she said finally. "The map. Your way out."

"It wasn't my way out," I said. "It was his way home. I was just the courier." I looked at her. "Turns out I hate running. Always did. I was just good at it."

She studied me with those pilot's eyes that took the measure of water and men both. Then, quietly: "The crest will hit tonight. The deed house is the highest ground for twenty miles. There are families down in the stilt quarter who won't make it to the church."

I was already rolling up my sleeves. "How many boats?"

"Three that float. Four if we're lucky."

"Lucky's been following me around all week," I said. "Let's go be generous with it."

And that's how I came to spend the climax of my own story hauling strangers through a flood—but that's the next part, and the next part starts with a rope, a child, and the exact moment the river finally got loud enough that nobody could pretend it was talking anymore.
