## 22. Silas and the Provision

### Wren

A year ago, if you'd told me I'd be standing on a dock at dawn handing out life vests to pilgrims, I'd have laughed in your face and then checked the exits.

But here I am. The *Provision* — Mara named her, and I didn't argue; you don't argue with a woman who owns the boat and most of your waking hours — sits low in the brown water with eleven passengers aboard: four farmers hauling seed yams to the market at San Isidro, two nuns going upriver to bless something that probably doesn't need blessing, an old man with a caged rooster who has opinions about everything, and a family of three who paid double for the front bench because their youngest has never seen the gorge and won't stop asking whether there are crocodiles.

"There are no crocodiles," I tell him for the fourth time.

"Are you sure?"

"I'm sure there are no crocodiles where we're going."

This is technically true. The crocodiles are downstream, past the checkpoint, and they mind their own business better than most people I've known.

I check the mooring line, check the fuel, check the sky — old habits, the ones that kept me alive through years when checking was all I had. The monsoon season is turning over again. You can smell it coming, green and metallic, like the river rehearsing its lines. In another month the water will climb the banks and swallow the lower fields, and everyone will move uphill and wait it out, the way they've done for generations. But today the water is calm, and the light comes down the valley long and gold, and the stone on the hill above the dock catches it first thing every morning before anything else gets a share.

Silas's stone. Mara had it cut from river granite, plain as a fence post, and she carved the words herself with a chisel she borrowed from the ferry mechanic and refused to give back until she was satisfied:

**SILAS VANE**
**HE WENT LOOKING AND FOUND IT LATE**
**BUT HE FOUND IT**

The villagers thought the last line was strange until Mara explained it, and then nobody thought it was strange anymore. That's the thing about this place I'm still learning: grief here isn't hidden away or dressed up. It's carried out to the dock, set on the table, passed around with the rice wine. It took me six months to stop flinching when someone asked me a direct question about my past. It took me eight months to answer honestly.

"You're talking to the boy again," Mara says behind me, "instead of untying the line."

"The boy is my most demanding client."

"The boy is going to make us late, and then his mother will lecture you again about punctuality, and you'll stand there taking it like a man being scolded by weather."

"I take it gracefully."

"You take it like a post." She shoulders past me, unhooks the mooring line in one motion — she can do it blind, drunk, asleep, mid-argument, which is most of her operating states — and nods at the helm. "Take us out, guide. Papers are stamped, cargo is lashed, and the nuns are already praying, which means we've got divine clearance."

I take the wheel. The engine turns over on the second try, coughs, catches. The *Provision* slides off the dock and into the current, and the valley opens ahead of us the way it does every morning, green walls rising into cloud, water braiding and unbraiding around sandbars that won't exist next month.

A year ago I was running this same river in a leaking boat with half a drowned map memorized in my head and two of Voss's men somewhere behind me. Now the boat doesn't leak, the map hangs framed in the ferry office under glass — what survived of it, ink bloomed to ghosts but still legible, still Silas's careful hand — and Voss is a story people tell each other at night, getting less true with every telling. He ran downstream ahead of the flood and the authorities both, and somewhere south of the border he discovered that a man who defrauds villages out of their land deeds makes enemies faster than he makes money. Last I heard — and I hear things; guides always hear things — he was selling fake permits in a port town where the police were already asking questions. I don't wish him dead. I used to think that made me soft. Now I think it just means I'm not carrying him anymore.

The debt died on the day I handed the map to the village council instead of to him. Not just the money — the whole architecture of it, the ledger in my head where every favor owed and every name blacklisted lived rent-free. It turns out a debt you can't pay off can still be dissolved. You just have to stop agreeing to it.

"Wren." Mara, beside me at the rail, watching the water. "The Kavanagh file came back."

My hands go still on the wheel. Three months ago, on her suggestion — her insistence, really, delivered in the tone she uses when arguing is decorative — I'd sent word north through a lawyer in San Isidro: a formal petition to clear my name in Nepal and Peru, citing witnesses, citing the outfitter fraud, citing everything I'd spent ten years assuming was permanent. I hadn't told anyone I'd sent it. She'd found the receipt in my jacket pocket, because of course she did, and said nothing for two weeks, which from Mara is a kind of applause.

"And?"

"And the Nepal charge is dismissed. Witness testimony, the works." She pauses, letting it land, letting me squirm. "Peru's still processing. Bureaucracy moves like a river in dry season. But it's moving."

I look at the water for a while. There's a feeling in my chest I don't have good language for — something between relief and vertigo, like setting down a pack so heavy you'd forgotten your own posture. For ten years, *Wren Kavanagh* was a name that closed doors. I built a whole self around avoiding it. Invisibility as safety. And now some clerk in Kathmandu has stamped a piece of paper and the doors are just... doors.

"You could say thank you," Mara says.

"I could say a lot of things."

"Start with thank you."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it so plainly that she looks briefly alarmed, then covers it by punching my arm hard enough to leave a mark.

### Mara

He cried a little. He'll deny it. I'm writing it down anyway, in the logbook, in the margin, where the important records go.

Not sobbing. Just the eyes doing that thing where they shine too much and the jaw sets and the voice comes out one note too flat. I know the signs. I invented them. My father had the same tell, according to the letter — the letter I read alone in the rain a year ago, kneeling in the mud outside the deed house while the flood came up the street and Wren stood in the doorway not watching me, giving me the privacy of someone pretending not to be there, which is its own strange kindness.

I keep the letter in a tin box under my bunk now, along with the chisel and a dried flower from the grave that I put there during a moment of sentimentality I will deny to my dying day. Some nights I read it. Most nights I don't. Both are fine. That's what I learned this year — that grief isn't a door you close or a room you stay locked in. It's a passenger. It rides along. Sometimes it talks too much and sometimes it sleeps the whole way, but it's allowed on board, and you don't throw passengers overboard, even the ones who broke your heart twenty years before you knew they had.

The morning run goes easy. Farmers off at San Isidro, yams and all, the rooster screaming insults at the nuns the entire way. At the gorge the boy — the mother's son, seven years old and fearless in the specific way of children who have never actually been in danger — leans so far over the rail that Wren hooks a finger in his collar without even looking up from the wheel, and the mother laughs, and the boy laughs, and Wren tells him about the time the river ate our boat out from under us, edited heavily for content, and the boy demands to know if Wren was scared.

"Terrified," Wren says.

"Liar," I say.

"Strategically terrified. There's a difference."

There is a difference. I've watched him teach it all year — to the village kids who hang around the dock, to the young guides he's training for the high-country treks now that his papers are coming clean, to me, though I'd never admit the curriculum applies. Wren Kavanagh spent a decade believing that skill was something you hoarded and trust was something that got you killed, and now stands on a dock every morning explaining knots to children, and if you pointed out the contradiction he'd get that flat-voiced look and change the subject to fuel levels.

At noon we tie up at the landing below the hill, and the pilgrims — a small group this week, walking the ridge trail to the shrine — ask if anyone can show them the path, because the last flood rearranged it and the old markers are underwater. Wren looks at me. I look at Wren. This is our whole marriage negotiation, conducted in glances, and we've been married exactly none times, and engaged zero times, and living in the same drafty house above the ferry office for eight months, and neither of us has raised the subject because raising subjects is for people who need words, and we communicate fine in glances.

"Go on," I say. "I'll handle the loading."

He takes the pilgrims up the trail, and I watch him go — the way he checks the footing ahead of the slowest walker without making a show of it, the way he points out the flood line on the trees, the way one of the pilgrims, a woman with a walking stick, asks him something and he stops entirely, turns fully, gives her the whole of his attention. A year ago he'd have answered over his shoulder, already moving, already gone. Invisibility as safety. Now he stands still in the middle of a trail in full daylight and lets himself be seen.

I didn't do that. I want to be clear about that. I offered him a place on the ferry and he took it, but the rest — the papers, the teaching, the staying — that was him choosing, over and over, in small ways, on ordinary days. Belonging isn't a place. He told me that once, drunk on rice wine after the flood receded, and I threw a sandal at him for being sentimental, and then lay awake all night knowing he was right.

The stone on the hill catches the afternoon light. From the landing you can see it if you know where to look, white-gray against the green. Some mornings I row out and sit under it with coffee and tell my father about the ferry runs, the water levels, the boy and the rooster, the idiot I share a house with. He went looking and found it late, but he found it. I've decided that counts. I've decided a lot of things count this year that wouldn't have counted before — a promise kept badly and late and through fire and flood still counts, a daughter's anger that curdles into forgiveness still counts, a man who chooses a stranger's dying wish over his own clean escape still counts.

The provision, Silas called it, in the letter. Not gold. Provision. What a man leaves behind so the people he failed can stand. Land, and a name on a stone, and a stranger willing to carry the weight of it upriver through a flood.

It was enough. It is enough.

* * *

### Wren

Evening. The ferry is tied up, the office is locked, the pilgrims are camped at the shrine, and Mara is on the porch mending a net with the focused violence she brings to all domestic tasks. I sit on the steps with the compass in my hand.

I sold it in the spring — the brass one, the one that pointed me across three countries and out of more trouble than it ever pointed me into. Sold it to a collector in San Isidro for enough to buy my stake in the ferry run, which felt right: the instrument of running converted directly into the means of staying. But the buyer turned out to be a sentimental fool, and last month he sold it back to me at cost, saying a compass should belong to someone with a fixed point. So now it sits on my knee pointing north, useless and beloved, a souvenir of the man I used to be.

Mara finishes a knot, bites the thread, looks at me. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The brooding-at-dusk thing. The 'I used to be a different person' thing. You do it maybe twice a month. It's very atmospheric. The tourists would love it."

"The tourists are at the shrine."

"Then brood efficiently. Dinner's in an hour." She goes back to the net, then pauses. "The lawyer sent a second letter. It's on the table. I didn't read it."

"You read everything else I own."

"That was inventory. This is mail." A beat. "Also I respect the contents."

I go inside and open it. The Peru ruling. Dismissed. All charges. Witness statements, the fraud conviction against Voss entered into record, a formal apology from the licensing authority that reads like it was written by someone being forced at gunpoint to apologize, which it probably was. Effective immediately, my guiding license is valid in both countries. Clean papers. Ten years of closed borders, opened by a stamp.

I walk back out to the porch and hold the letter up. Mara examines it, nods once, and returns to her net, which is her way of celebrating — but her ears have gone pink, and I catch the corner of her mouth doing something it denies doing.

"So," she says. "Nepal. Peru. High country. You could take real treks again. Real money. Clients who tip."

"I could."

"You could be gone four months of the year."

"I could."

She ties off the net with unnecessary force. "Well. The ferry needs a second pilot anyway. If you're going to be useless."

"Mara."

"Don't make it a moment, Kavanagh."

"It's not a moment." I sit down across from her, compass in one hand, letter in the other, and I find that the speech I've been drafting for weeks — the one about choices and roots and the difference between running and arriving — dissolves completely, and what's left is just the truth, plain as the stone on the hill. "I'm not going anywhere. I might guide in the high season. I'll be back before the water rises. That's the arrangement. That's the whole offer."

She looks at me for a long moment, the lamplight catching the scar on her knuckle from the boat-hook night, the night this all started, more or less.

"That's acceptable," she says.

From Mara Vane, that's a love poem. I've learned her language. It took a year and a flood, but I speak it fluently now.

Later, after dinner, I walk up the hill in the dark with the lantern, the way I do some nights when the house feels too warm and the river is calling its low constant note. The stone stands silver in the starlight. I sit down beside it, the way you'd sit beside an old client at a campfire, and I tell him the news — the papers, the season ahead, the boy who asked about crocodiles, the woman downstairs who says *acceptable* and means *stay forever*.

"You'd laugh at me," I tell him. "Ten years ago you'd have laughed hardest of anyone. Invisible man, fixed point. Doesn't scan."

The river answers the way rivers do, which is to say it keeps on keeping on, patient as a promise.

And then, because peace is not the same as quiet, and because a year of rootedness hasn't cured me of noticing things — I see the lantern light moving on the far bank. Two figures, walking the flood-line trail where no village lies and no pilgrims go. One of them carries the long case of a surveyor's transit.

Mara appears at my shoulder without sound, the way she does. We watch the lights together.

"Prospectors?" she says.

"Maybe," I say. "Or somebody surveying for a dam. Or somebody who heard there's valuable deeded land upriver and thinks the paperwork's gone soft since the flood."

Her hand finds mine in the dark, grip hard, familiar. Down below, the *Provision* tugs gently at her mooring, ready.

"Well," Mara says. "We kept one promise. We can keep another."

The lanterns on the far bank turn upstream — toward the land, toward the stone, toward everything Silas left us to hold.

"Not tonight," I say. "Tonight we sleep. Tomorrow, we're guides."

And for the first time in longer than I can measure, that sentence doesn't taste like running.

It tastes like home.
