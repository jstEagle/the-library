## 1. The Weight of Good Weather

The sky over the Cane Basin had been lying to Gideon Whitlock all morning, and he'd known it the way you know a lover is about to leave — not from any single thing, but from the accumulation of small betrayals. The wind had gone too quiet at noon. The cattle had stopped moving. And now, at four in the afternoon, with the sun still stubbornly above the horizon, the light had turned the color of old brass — that sickly green-gold that meant the atmosphere was holding its breath.

"Green means go," Gideon murmured to himself, thumbing the wheel of his rig through a washboard stretch of county road. "Green means go, and everybody else means hide."

He was talking to the truck because there was no one else to talk to, which was itself a kind of data point about his life. The rig was a 1987 Meridian Freightliner chassis that he'd rebuilt three times and rechristened *Dorothy's Revenge* on the door in hand-painted letters that were starting to peel at the R. Radar dome bolted to the roof rack, salvaged from a decommissioned weather station up north. Probe launchers welded along the bed. A hail cage of expanded steel mesh over every window, which made the cab feel like driving inside a birdcage and which had saved his life twice and his teeth once.

The anemometer read surface winds at thirty knots and climbing. The mesocyclone on his screen was rotating like something with intent.

"Come on, sweetheart," he said to the storm. "Show me what you've got."

It was a strange courtship, storm chasing. Most people thought it was about adrenaline, and Gideon let them think that because the truth was harder to explain: that a tornado was the only honest creditor he'd ever dealt with. It took exactly what it took, no interest, no collectors, no fine print. The sky didn't send men in gray suits to catalogue your belongings while you watched. The sky just came, and went, and left you standing in the wreckage doing arithmetic.

He'd been doing a lot of arithmetic lately.

The debt sat in him like ballast. Forty-one thousand marks owed to the Meridian Syndicate, accrued over six years of rig repairs and radar parts and one catastrophic season when the probes hadn't sold and the data contracts had dried up. Forty-one thousand marks, compounding monthly, and the Syndicate's patience — like the atmosphere this afternoon — had been going quiet in a way that meant something terrible was loading.

But that was a problem for after the storm. Right now there was a wall cloud lowering over the flats west of Harrow Bend, and it was beautiful, and beauty was the one currency Gideon had never managed to go into hock for.

The first funnel touched down at 4:17 p.m., a rope snaking out of the cloud base like the sky dipping a finger to test the temperature of the earth. Then it thickened. Then it *walked*.

"Oh," Gideon said softly, pulling off the road, killing the engine, grabbing his camera. "Oh, hello."

* * *

He got eleven minutes of perfect footage. Eleven minutes of the thing crossing open range two miles out, chewing a line through prairie grass and one abandoned grain silo, its debris column glowing amber where the low sun caught it. He was so absorbed in framing the shot — tracking the vortex through the viewfinder, calling wind speeds aloud to his recorder out of pure habit even though nobody would ever pay for them anymore — that he never heard the second vehicle come up behind him.

What he heard instead was a knock on the driver's side window.

Which was impossible. Nobody knocked on windows during a tornado warning. The knock was polite, measured, almost gentle — three raps, evenly spaced — and it was somehow more frightening than the half-mile-wide column of rotating death currently grinding its way across the basin behind him.

Gideon lowered the camera. A man in a charcoal-gray suit stood outside the hail cage, hands clasped behind his back, rain beading on his shoulders as though the water had agreed not to touch him. Behind the man, parked neatly on the shoulder of the farm road, sat a black transport van with the Meridian Syndicate's sigil stenciled on the door: a stylized river delta, silver on black, the mark that appeared on every contract Gideon had ever signed and every nightmare he'd had since.

The man gestured at the door lock. His expression suggested that locking it had been a charming formality and that they both knew how this would end.

Gideon weighed his options for approximately one second, glanced at the tornado — still two miles out, still moving parallel — and unlocked the door.

"Afternoon," he said, summoning the smile, the good one, the one that had talked his way out of repossessions and roadblocks and one memorable incident involving a sheriff, a bet, and a stolen weather balloon. "You picked a hell of a day for a house call. Can I offer you coffee? Fair warning, it's the instant kind. I'm told it doubles as rust remover."

The man in the suit did not smile. He climbed into the passenger seat with the unhurried precision of someone boarding a train he owned, set a leather folio on his knees, and said, "Mr. Whitlock. My name is Tallow. I'm going to need you to keep your hands where I can see them and your commentary to a minimum."

"Tallow," Gideon repeated. "Like the candle stuff?"

"Like the candle stuff." Tallow opened the folio. From the van behind them, four more figures emerged — gray suits all, moving with that same terrible calm — and began unloading equipment: scanners, inventory tablets, a portable scale. One of them popped the rear hatch of Dorothy's Revenge without asking, and Gideon felt it physically, somewhere under the sternum, like a hook going in.

"You're kidding me," he said. "You're doing this *now?* There's a category-four on the ground. You're going to get yourself killed."

"The Verrin office has been trying to schedule this appointment for five weeks, Mr. Whitlock. You have declined or evaded nine separate notices." Tallow turned a page. "We are instructed to conduct the assessment regardless of meteorological conditions. The conditions, frankly, were considered a feature. People don't run, during a tornado."

"That's not true. Everybody runs during a tornado. That's the whole—"

"Hands, Mr. Whitlock."

Gideon put his hands on the wheel. Outside, the collector with the scanner ran it along the probe launchers, and the tablet chirped with each pass — a small, bright, cheerful sound, like a songbird cataloguing a corpse.

* * *

They worked fast. That was the worst part, how fast. In forty minutes — while the storm crossed the basin and roped out and a new wall cloud began organizing to the northeast, because the sky, unlike the Syndicate, kept faith with its schedule — they inventoried his life.

The radar dome: assessed. The probe launchers: assessed. The winch, the spare tires, the fuel cans, the tool chest with the false bottom where he kept his mother's silver locket and four hundred marks in emergency cash: found, opened, assessed. The camera rig: assessed, and here Tallow paused longest, turning the body over in his gloved hands.

"This is a Kestrel-9," he said. "Broadcast grade."

"It's my livelihood."

"Your livelihood is collateral, Mr. Whitlock. Everything is collateral. That's what a debt is." Tallow set the camera down with exaggerated care, which was somehow worse than throwing it. "Current outstanding balance: forty-one thousand, two hundred and six marks, plus accrued penalties. Assessed asset value of vehicle and equipment, at recovery auction rates—" he consulted the tablet, and Gideon watched the number arrive in the man's face before he heard it, watched the tiny flicker of something that might have been professional satisfaction or might have been pity, "—eighteen thousand, four hundred."

The math landed in Gideon's stomach like swallowed gravel. Even if they took everything — the rig, the radar, the cameras, the tools, the locket, the cash — he'd still owe twenty-three thousand marks. They could strip his life down to the bolts and he'd still be drowning, just closer to the bottom.

"So that's it," he said. He heard his own voice and hated how steady it came out, how the charm held even now, grinning at the abyss like the abyss was a mark at a card table. "You take the truck, I walk to town, and I still owe you enough to buy a small house. That's a hell of a business model, Tallow. You guys ever consider robbery? Oh wait."

Tallow closed the folio. For a moment — just a moment — he looked tired. Not sympathetic; Gideon doubted the man had sympathy left in him, if he'd ever had any. Just tired, the way a levee looks tired.

"The Verrin office has authorized one alternative," Tallow said. "There is a retrieval contract. Northern territory. River work. Difficult, poorly documented, and the last two contractors assigned to it have... lapsed. If you accepted the assignment, the balance would be frozen during the term of service. Not forgiven. Frozen."

"What's the work?"

"Recovery of property. Documentation." Tallow's eyes went briefly, involuntarily, toward the northeast — toward the high country, the headwaters, though Gideon couldn't have said how he knew that. "You'd receive the details upon acceptance."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we load the vehicle at dawn, and you owe twenty-two thousand eight hundred marks against nothing but your name." Tallow stood, smoothing his coat. Rain dripped from the hem. "You have until dawn, Mr. Whitlock. We'll be at the Harrow Bend depot."

He stepped down out of the cab into the mud, and the collectors packed their instruments with the same birdlike chirping, and the van pulled away down the farm road with its wipers going, unhurried, a funeral procession for a man who wasn't dead yet.

Gideon sat alone in the hail cage with his hands still on the wheel, and behind him the new cell on the horizon flashed silent heat lightning, over and over, like something signaling.

* * *

He didn't go to the depot. He drove into Harrow Bend instead, because there was a woman there named Sera who ran the fuel stop and who had, over three years of storm seasons, become the closest thing to a friend that a man built out of exits could sustain, and because he needed diesel and he needed to hear a human voice say something that wasn't a figure.

Sera was restocking the ice cooler when he came in, and she took one look at him and said, "They came, didn't they."

"They came."

"Mid-run?"

"Mid-run. During the tornado, Sera. They boarded me during the tornado." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud in the little store. "I gotta hand it to them. That's showmanship."

She came around the cooler and looked at him the way she did when he was bleeding and pretending not to be — direct, unsentimental, her forearms still wet from the meltwater. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that they're offering me a job in the northern territory. Which, coming from the Syndicate, means either it kills you or it makes you into something they can use forever."

"Are you going to take it?"

"I don't know." And then, because lying to Sera had never once worked and he was too tired to try: "Yeah. Probably. What else am I going to do?"

She didn't answer right away. She wiped her hands on her apron and leaned against the counter, and outside the plate glass the green light was building again in the west, second show of the evening, the sky refusing to be done with him.

"My grandmother used to say," Sera said finally, "that good weather is heavier than bad. Bad weather, you brace. Good weather, you relax, and that's when the roof falls in." She tapped the counter once. "You've had good weather a long time, Gideon. Charming your way through. Everybody liking you, nobody knowing you. That's good weather."

"That's a terrible metaphor and I resent it."

"Mm." She filled his jerry cans without being asked, waved off his money, and pressed a paper sack of food into his hands — bread, hard cheese, the smoked fish she made herself. "For the road. Whatever road."

"Sera—"

"Don't." But she hugged him anyway, quick and fierce, flour and diesel smell in her hair, and then pushed him toward the door before either of them could make it into something worse. "Storm's turning. Go chase it. It's the only time you look honest."

* * *

He should have slept. He knew he should have slept. Instead he sat in the cab of Dorothy's Revenge in the lot behind the fuel stop, engine off, watching the lightning walk the western ridgeline, and turned the compass over in his hands.

It was the one thing the collectors hadn't taken, mostly because he'd kept it in his coat pocket and they'd never asked. Brass casing, dented on one side, older than he was. He'd won it off a drunk prospector in a card game seven years ago — or the prospector had let him win it, Gideon had sometimes suspected, though the old man had never said why. On the back, scratched deep with a knife point, someone had carved a single word:

*SILAS.*

The needle swung lazily, settling north, and Gideon stared at it the way you stare at a locked door when you're deciding whether to knock.

Twenty-two thousand marks. A retrieval contract in country where contractors *lapsed*. A life spent charming people just enough to keep them from asking what was underneath. He was thirty-four years old and everything he owned fit inside a truck that belonged to a bank in everything but name, and tomorrow at dawn the gray suits would come with their scales and their songbird tablets and weigh whatever was left.

Or.

The word arrived quietly, the way storms do, long after the signs have been stacking up all day.

*Or.*

He looked at the compass. He looked at the northern horizon, where the lightning had stopped and the dark was deepest — toward the high country, the headwaters, the place Tallow's eyes had flinched toward. Somewhere up there was a contract that froze debts. Somewhere up there was also, possibly, a way to make a debt unnecessary. Gideon Whitlock had spent his whole career reading skies that lied, and he knew the feeling of a system loading something enormous, and the north had that feeling tonight, dense and humming and patient.

"Ah, hell," he said softly, to the compass, to Silas, to nobody.

He started the engine. The headlights swept the empty lot, the rain-slick road, the dark line of the river valley beyond — the Verrin, running north out of sight, out of the basin, up into country no map in his cab bothered to detail. He topped off the tanks at the pump with Sera's cash hidden in his boot, left double what the diesel cost under the wiper blade, and rolled out of Harrow Bend at eleven minutes past midnight, heading upstream, heading north, heading anywhere that wasn't a depot at dawn.

Behind him the storm finally broke over the town he'd left, and he didn't look back. Ahead, the road followed the river into fog, and somewhere in that fog — though he couldn't have known it yet, though it would find him soaked and bleeding under a rail bridge by morning — a dying man was waiting with a map and a promise, and the compass in Gideon's pocket was already swinging, very slightly, very steadily, away from north.

Toward the source.

Toward everything.
