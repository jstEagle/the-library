## 2. A Trade in the Rain

The rain came down like it had a grudge.

Gideon Whitlock had been chased by weather his whole life — had driven toward the black-bellied clouds other people fled from, had stood in open fields with his arms out while the sky tried to kill him — but he'd never been chased by men before. Men were worse. Weather didn't hold a grudge. Weather didn't keep ledgers.

He'd left the rig in a ditch eleven miles back, engine drowned, tires sunk to the axles in red clay that ate anything heavier than a boot heel. Everything he owned was in that rig: the anemometers, the lightning triggers, the spare fuel cans, the photographs of a woman who'd stopped answering his letters three years ago. The Syndicate's collectors had catalogued all of it that afternoon, item by item, their styluses scratching against slates while the tornado chewed the horizon behind them like it was nothing, like the end of the world was just another line item.

*Item forty-one: one storm-rig, modified Ford chassis, hail-damaged.*

*Item forty-two: one barometer, brass, engraved G.W.*

*Item forty-three: one man.*

They hadn't said that last part out loud. They hadn't needed to. The way the lead collector had looked at him — patient, almost kind, the way you look at a horse you've already decided to sell — had said it plainly enough.

So Gideon ran. He told himself it wasn't running; it was *repositioning*. He told himself he'd double back for the rig once the collectors moved on. He told himself a lot of things, walking the rail line north through the dark with the rain hammering his shoulders flat, because lying to yourself was the one debt that never came due.

The rail bridge appeared out of the murk like the ribcage of something enormous and long dead. It spanned a cut where two hillsides pinched together, the old trestle work furred with moss, the rails above it rusted down to a dull brown shine. Underneath, in the dry triangle where the abutment met the earth, there was room enough for a man to sit out the worst of it.

There was already someone sitting there.

Gideon's hand went to the knife at his belt before his brain caught up. Then he saw the state of the man, and his hand came away empty.

* * *

He was old — sixty, maybe more, though it was hard to tell under the blood. His oilskin coat had gone gray with years and darker with rain, and beneath it his side was soaked through with something that wasn't rainwater. He sat propped against the stone abutment with his legs stretched out and his hat over his eyes, and for a moment Gideon thought he was dead, because nobody alive sat that still.

Then the hat tipped back, and a pair of pale eyes found him in the dark.

"You're not them," the old man said. His voice was gravel dragged through water.

"No," Gideon said. "Who's them?"

"Anyone wearing a badge or carrying a slate." The old man laughed, and the laugh turned into a wet cough that bent him forward. When he straightened, he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at what came away without surprise. "Well. That's that, then."

Gideon crouched at the edge of the shelter, rain streaming off his collar, weighing it. Every instinct he'd sharpened over ten years of chasing storms said *keep moving*. Strangers bleeding out under bridges were somebody else's problem, and Gideon Whitlock had no surplus of anything — not money, not time, not mercy. Mercy cost. He knew the price of everything now; the Syndicate had made sure of that, had taught him to calculate to the penny what every kindness would set him back.

But the old man's eyes were steady on him, and there was something in them Gideon recognized. He'd seen it in mirrors on bad mornings. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic on the time he had left.

"You're hit bad," Gideon said. Stupid thing to say. The man knew.

"Shot," the old man agreed, as if confirming the weather. "Three days ago. Up past Miller's Bend. They followed me down the valley." He tilted his head back against the stone. "Lost them at the ford. Or they lost interest. Hard to say which."

"Syndicate?"

"What else wears a slate these days?" The old man studied him. Rain drummed on the timbers overhead, filling the silence, and Gideon felt himself being weighed in return — measured against some standard the old man kept behind those pale eyes. "You're the chaser," he said at last. "Whitlock. I've seen your rig on the valley roads. You drive like a man who doesn't expect to arrive anywhere."

"Flattering."

"It's honest." Another cough took him, longer this time, and when it passed he breathed shallow and careful, like each lungful was borrowed. "You're in debt to them."

It wasn't a question, and Gideon didn't insult them both by denying it. "Doesn't everyone owe someone?"

"Everyone owes someone. But there's owing and there's *owing*." The old man's hand drifted inside his coat, slow, deliberate, and Gideon's knife was out before he'd decided to draw it. The old man didn't flinch. He brought out an oilcloth packet, soft-worn, tied with waxed cord, and held it between them like a card played face-up. "My name's Marsh. Elias Marsh. And I'm about to make you the only offer anyone's made you in years that isn't a trap."

"They're all traps," Gideon said. "Some are just slower than others."

"This one's fast." Marsh worked the knot loose with fingers gone clumsy from cold and blood loss. "I've spent thirty years prospecting this valley. Copper, silver, a little gold — enough to eat, never enough to matter. But nine years ago I found something that mattered. Something that isn't ore at all." He drew a breath that whistled. "I found where the Verrin is born."

Gideon lowered the knife an inch. "Every kid in the basin knows where the Verrin starts. Snowmelt off the high divide—"

"That's what the maps say. That's what *they* want the maps to say." Marsh's voice had found some iron underneath the weakness, and it surprised Gideon, the way a river surprises you with current under calm water. "Nine years ago I climbed past the divide following a warm spring, and I found a lake that steams in January. A caldera, boy. The mountain's hollow and it's *hot*, and the water coming off it runs down and becomes the Verrin. Every record says otherwise. Every survey, every chart, every schoolbook. Because the Syndicate found it first, decades back, and they've been erasing it ever since. Buying surveys. Burning records. Damming the lower river so nobody travels upstream anymore. There are towns under that reservoir, Whitlock. Whole towns. Callow's Ford is half-drowned and the maps still show it dry."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the canyon walls like furniture being dragged across a floor.

"Why?" Gideon asked. It came out quieter than he intended. "Why erase a hot spring?"

"Because it's not a hot spring. It's a furnace. Geothermal vent big enough to power a city — or a facility. Whatever they've built up there, they've built it on stolen heat, and the only way to keep it secret is to make sure no one living has ever seen the source." Marsh pressed the packet into the space between them. "This is the map. My map. Thirty years of walking folded into one sheet of paper."

"I don't want it." Gideon heard his own voice go flat and hard. "Whatever you think I am, I'm not your errand boy. I'm a man running from collectors, and the last thing I need is to be carrying whatever got you shot."

"Got me shot because I sent word ahead. One letter. To my daughter." Something moved across the old man's face, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "She'll come looking. She always comes looking, even when she hates me. Especially then." He coughed again, and this time it went on long enough that Gideon found himself leaning closer without deciding to, one hand hovering uselessly near the old man's shoulder. "Listen to me. I don't have time for pride, and neither do you. Your debt — how much?"

"That's none of your business."

"Everything's my business now. I'm dying; I get to be rude." The pale eyes glittered. "Is it the kind of debt they forgive? Or the kind they collect?"

Gideon said nothing, which was answer enough.

Marsh nodded slowly, as if Gideon had confirmed something he'd already calculated. "Then here's the trade. Take the map. Follow the river up. Find the source. And when you've seen it — tell no one, and burn this paper after. That's the whole of it. Three conditions. Take it to the source, tell no one, burn it after."

"And what do I get?"

"Nothing," Marsh said, and smiled with bloody teeth, and it was somehow the most honest expression Gideon had seen in years. "You get nothing. That's why you can trust it. Every offer that's ever been made to you came with something sweet attached, and every one of them was a hook. This one's just a job. A dead man's last job." He sagged back against the stone. "Or refuse it, and walk on, and let them find me here with it. They'll burn it. And then there will be nothing in the world outside that mountain that knows the truth, and the Verrin will belong to them forever, and every town on its banks will stay thirsty so a handful of men can stay rich."

Gideon stared at the packet. The oilcloth was dark in places, spotted with the old man's blood, and it lay there in the lamplight-less dark like something alive. He thought about the ledger. He could see it in his head — he saw it most nights — the columns of figures in the Syndicate's neat hand, the interest compounding like mold, the total that had stopped being a number years ago and become a kind of weather, something that hung over his whole life and rained on everything.

A man in his position should take nothing. Should carry nothing. Should be light, fast, invisible.

But he'd watched collectors catalogue his life that afternoon, item by item, and somewhere around *item twenty-eight* he'd understood that they weren't really collecting objects. They were collecting *him*, piece by piece, until there was nothing left that was his. And Elias Marsh — bleeding, freezing, three days from a bullet wound and still upright — was the first person in longer than Gideon could remember who was offering him something that wasn't a hook.

"Tell me about your daughter," Gideon said.

Marsh's breath caught — surprise, or pain, or both. "Why?"

"Because if she's coming after this map, I'll meet her on the river, and I'd rather know whether she'll shake my hand or shoot me."

"She'll decide when she sees you." The old man almost laughed. "Like her mother. God help you both." He closed his eyes for a moment, and when he opened them they were dimmer, the fire behind them banked low. "Her name's Wren. She'll be angry. Be angry with me, not her — I earned it. I chose the river over her when she was young, and then I chose the secret over her again, and a man only gets so many choices before the choosing's done." His hand found the packet and pushed it against Gideon's chest, insistent despite the tremor in it. "Take it. Please. I've carried it thirty years and I'd rather it die in a fire doing its work than rot in a Syndicate vault."

Gideon took it.

The oilcloth was warm from the old man's body, and lighter than it had any right to be. He untied the cord just enough to see the edge of what was inside: heavy paper, hand-ruled, dense with inked lines and marginal notes in a cramped, sure hand. Even in the dark he could tell it was the work of years. Decades.

"The source," Marsh said. "Tell no one. Burn it after. Say it back."

"Take it to the source. Tell no one. Burn it after." Gideon tied the cord off and slid the packet inside his coat, against his ribs, where his heartbeat made it feel like part of him. "And your daughter? If she finds me first?"

"Tell her—" Marsh started, and stopped. His jaw worked. Rain hammered the trestle above them, and the river in the cut below had risen with the storm, running loud and brown and impatient. "Tell her the compass points true. She'll know what it means. Maybe she won't. But say it anyway."

"What compass?"

Marsh's smile was fading now, going soft at the edges, the way light goes at dusk. "You'll find it. He left it for whoever came next. Silas always said—" The name caught in his throat like a fishbone. He swallowed. "Silas. My partner. Lost on the mountain, nine years back. The compass was his. It'll find you, or you'll find it. Either way, it points where it wants to go, and where it wants to go is home."

None of it made sense, and Gideon was too tired and too wet and too hunted to chase it. "Rest," he said. "I'll watch till morning. First light, I'll find help — there's a ferry landing downriver, Odile's, they say she'll take anyone—"

"There's no help for me, boy." Marsh said it gently, the way you'd correct a child about the moon. "But sit awhile. Company's company. It's been a long road with no one on it."

So Gideon sat. He sat with his back against the cold stone and the map warm against his ribs and the rain coming down like the sky meant to drown the whole world, and he talked when the old man wanted talking — about storms, mostly, because that was the only country Gideon had ever owned free and clear. Marsh had chased weather too, in his way; had read cloud and current the way Gideon did, had learned the valley's moods over thirty years of walking it. They talked about the river. About the towns going thirsty downstream while the Syndicate's dams held back the water and the Syndicate's meters ticked. About Callow's Ford, which Marsh remembered whole and bright, with a dance hall and a bell tower, before the water came up its streets like a tide that forgot to go out.

Somewhere in the small hours the old man's answers grew farther apart. Longer silences between them, like gaps widening in a fence. Gideon noticed, and pretended not to, because pretending was a skill he'd perfected, and because there was nothing else to do. You couldn't outrun this particular storm. He of all people knew that some systems you just stood under and waited out, and some you didn't wait out at all.

Near dawn, the searchlights came.

They swept the valley floor first — great white blades swinging slow from the ridgeline, the Syndicate's patrol craft riding the storm's trailing edge the way buzzards ride a thermos of warm air. Gideon pressed back into the shadow of the abutment and watched the light crawl across the flooded fields, across the drowned fences of Callow's Ford far downstream, across the rail bridge's rusted legs, close enough that the beams turned the falling rain into drifting veils of silver.

"Elias," he whispered. "Lights."

Marsh didn't answer.

Gideon turned. The old man's hat had slipped down over his eyes again, and this time there was no breath moving it, no rasp under the rain, no weight behind the stillness. Just a man made entirely of quiet, his hands folded in his lap like a laborer done with the day's work, his face turned slightly toward the sound of the river.

Gideon sat very still while the searchlights swept the bridge twice, three times, hunting. His heart banged against the map hidden under his coat. *Take it to the source. Tell no one. Burn it after.* The promise sat in him now alongside the debt, two weights on opposite ends of a scale, and for the first time in years the debt wasn't the heavier one.

The lights swung away, upriver, toward the high divide — toward the place where the maps lied and the water was born. The patrol craft droned on into the gray beginning of morning, and the rain thinned, and the river below the bridge ran loud and brown and certain, going where it was going regardless of who owned the paper that claimed it.

Gideon reached over and eased the old man's hat off his face, gently, the way you'd close a door on a lit room. He settled it instead on his own head. It fit badly. It fit fine.

"Well," he said to the river, to the rain, to the dead man and the living morning. "Compass points true, huh."

He stood, pulled the oilskin tight around the map, and turned upstream — toward the mountains, toward the lie at the top of every map, toward whatever Silas had left waiting and whatever Wren was already riding to meet him.

Behind him, the searchlights swept the empty valley one last time and found nothing but weather.

Ahead, the river ran home.
