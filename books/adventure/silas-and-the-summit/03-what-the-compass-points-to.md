## 3. What the Compass Points To

The rain had followed him out of the valley like a debt collector.

Gideon Whitlock noticed such things now — the way weather could shadow a man, patient and inevitable, the way collectors did. He'd spent eleven years reading storms for a living, learning their moods, their tells, the way a cumulonimbus stacked itself green-black over the plains before it decided to eat a town. He knew how to read the sky better than his own ledger. That was the trouble. The sky forgave nothing either.

He walked the river road at first light, when the mist came off the Verrin in long gray ribbons and the water ran the color of tarnished silver. Elias Marsh's map rode against his chest, folded into oilcloth inside his jacket, and with every mile Gideon became more aware of it — not its weight, which was nothing, but its *presence*, the way a loaded weapon has presence even holstered. A dead man's last wish pressed against his sternum. He'd buried Elias under the rail bridge with a cairn of ballast stones and no name, because he didn't know one yet, and that omission sat wrong in him too. You didn't leave a man anonymous. Not if you wanted to keep calling yourself anything at all.

The compass was in his left hand.

It had been Elias's — the old man had pressed it into his palm along with the map, closing Gideon's fingers around it with surprising strength for a man leaking his life out onto the gravel. *"It'll find the way,"* he'd rasped. *"Not north. Don't trust it to point north."* Gideon had assumed that was blood loss talking, the poetry of the dying. People said strange things when they were leaving. His mother had recited grocery lists for her final hour.

But this morning, on the river road, with the mist burning off and the sun finding a seam in the clouds, Gideon stopped walking and looked down at the needle, and understood that Elias Marsh had been entirely lucid.

The needle wasn't pointing north.

He knew north the way other men knew their own names. Eleven years of storm chasing will do that — you learn to orient by instinct, by the lean of the light, by which side of the road the moss gives up on. North was behind his left shoulder, upriver's west, where the ridgeline of the Coalcut Mountains made its long blue staircase against the sky. The needle didn't care. The needle swung lazily, deliberately, and settled pointing upriver — not just upriver, but *into* it, angled off the channel toward the high country where the Verrin was born, as if the little brass instrument had grown tired of pretending to be a compass and wanted to go home.

Gideon stood very still.

A modified compass. He turned it over. The case was heavy brass, old-fashioned, the kind prospectors carried before the war, before the Syndicate standardized everything worth standardizing. There was a screw at the base — small, flush, easy to miss unless you were looking. He'd have missed it. But now he saw the faint ring of disturbed metal around it, the ghost of a jeweler's wrench, and when he tilted the case against the light he caught it: a second needle. Thin as a hair, magnetized, mounted beneath the main one on a pivot so fine it moved like a thought. Someone had built a hidden magnet into the housing — or something stranger than a magnet — and balanced the whole assembly so the visible needle would ignore the poles of the earth entirely and point at one thing only.

The source. Or toward it. Close enough to die for.

"Okay, Elias," Gideon murmured. The mist took the words and gave back nothing. "Okay."

He should have felt cheated. A compass that lied about north was a compass that could lie about anything; a dead man's tool was a dead man's agenda. Gideon had built a whole life on trusting instruments — barometers, anemometers, the trembling pen of a seismograph — and here was an instrument built to deceive, handed to him by a stranger bleeding out under a bridge. Every sensible instinct said turn around, walk back downriver, sell the thing to a curiosity dealer in Meridian City and let the mystery drown with its maker.

Instead he closed his hand around the brass until it warmed, and kept walking upstream.

Because there was the other thing. The thing he hadn't said aloud, even to himself, even alone on an empty road where saying things aloud cost nothing. The Syndicate hadn't been searching for Elias Marsh. They'd been searching for what Elias Marsh *carried*. Gideon had watched them from the bridge abutment — three men in gray coats with lanterns and long-handled probes, sweeping the gravel bars like men panning for something worth more than gold. And when they'd found the blood, they'd gone quiet in a way that frightened him worse than shouting. Quiet meant orders. Orders meant someone above them wanted this found badly enough to spend professionals on it.

And Gideon Whitlock owed the Meridian Syndicate four thousand two hundred and sixty crowns, itemized, notarized, and accruing interest at a rate that had stopped feeling like arithmetic years ago and started feeling like weather. They owned his rig. They owned his instruments. By their ledgers, they very nearly owned him. If they wanted what Elias had carried — and they clearly did — then Gideon was holding the only object in the valley that might buy his way free.

Or the only object guaranteed to get him killed. With the Syndicate, those were usually the same purchase.

* * *

By midday the river road gave out.

It happened the way roads die in canyon country — gradually, then all at once. First the ruts filled with standing water, then the culverts rotted through, then a section of hillside simply slid away into the current and took the road's pretense of existing along with it. Beyond that point the Verrin narrowed and quickened, cutting deeper into red sandstone, and the going became boulder-hopping and ledge-walking, the kind of travel that punished hurry. Gideon climbed with the compass open in his hand, checking it every few minutes like a man checking a pulse.

The needle held true. Whatever Elias had built into the casing, it didn't waver with the river's bends. When the channel swung east, the needle stayed fixed on a point somewhere in the high country, patient as a nail. Twice Gideon tested it — turned in place, spun, covered the housing with his palm — and twice it swung back with the same lazy certainty. It wasn't pointing at the river. It was pointing past it, through it, at something the river knew about and he didn't.

The canyon changed character as he gained elevation. The sandstone darkened to iron-red, streaked with mineral weepings that looked like old wounds. There were ruins, too — small ones, easy to miss. A foundation half-swallowed by sage. A headframe leaning over a shaft plugged with decades of debris. A fence post with no fence, standing sentinel over nothing. This was mining country once, Gideon realized. Before the Syndicate dammed the lower river and drowned Callow's Ford, before the company towns went up like mushrooms after rain, this whole watershed had been worked claim by claim by men with mules and dynamite and dreams. And now it was empty. Not abandoned — *empty*, scoured, the way a room looks after someone has removed not just the furniture but the evidence that furniture was ever there. Even the rust seemed curated.

He ate cold beans on a ledge above the water and let the silence teach him what it could. It taught him one thing, eventually, and it taught it slowly:

The silence was wrong.

Canyons weren't silent. Canyons had ravens, at minimum — big black opportunists that haunted any route a traveler might take, hoping for spilled food or unattended death. Gideon had seen maybe forty miles of canyon since dawn and not one bird. No swallows working the cliff faces. No dippers bobbing on the midstream rocks. Nothing. Just the river's white noise and the wind worrying the junipers, and underneath it all a faint hum he kept almost hearing and kept dismissing — some trick of blood pressure, he told himself, or the river vibrating bedrock somewhere below.

He was repacking his satchel when he heard the click.

Small. Metallic. Upstream and across. The kind of sound a boot sole makes on loose shale when a man forgets, for half a second, to be careful.

Gideon didn't look up. That was the first rule of being followed, learned in eleven years of parking storm rigs in places where other chasers wanted your spot or your footage or occasionally your fuel: never confirm you've seen them. He finished buckling the satchel with fingers that wanted to shake and didn't allow it, stood, stretched like a bored man, and ambled on up the ledge — and while he ambled, he used the reflection in the compass's glass lid.

That was the beauty of a brass compass. Polished lid, wide angle. You could watch a whole hillside without turning your head.

Across the river, fifty yards up on the opposing bank, among the broken shadows of a collapsed adit: movement. Subtle. The kind of movement that was trying very hard to be geology. A shape detaching from darker shapes, low, deliberate, keeping the mine rubble between itself and the skyline.

Then the sun found a gap in the clouds and threw light across the far bank, and Gideon saw the figure plainly for perhaps three seconds.

Oilskins. Dark yellow, hooded, slicked with spray. Not Syndicate gray — the Syndicate dressed its people like accountants even in the field, gray coats, gray hats, gray souls. This was river gear, worn gear, patched at the elbows with stitches that caught the light. The figure was slight. Quick. It moved the way Gideon imagined water moving if water decided to hunt — flowing into cover, freezing, flowing again. And in the moment before it slid behind a fallen beam, the hood tipped, and he caught a flash of a face beneath: young. Sharp-boned. Watching him with an intensity he could feel across fifty yards of rushing water like a hand on the back of his neck.

Then gone. The bank was empty again, or looked empty, which amounted to the same thing.

Gideon walked another quarter mile before he let himself think.

His first instinct was Syndicate — but the coat argued against it, and so did the solo work. Corvus's people traveled in threes; he'd seen that pattern often enough to set his watch by it. A lone watcher in patched oilskins, tracking him along the far bank rather than crossing to close the distance, was something else. Someone who wanted to know where he was going before deciding whether to introduce themselves. Someone who'd known to be on this stretch of empty river on this particular morning, following the one man who'd sheltered under the rail bridge where a dying prospector had breathed his last.

*Elias's people,* Gideon thought. *If he had any.*

Or Elias's enemies. The list of people who might want a dead prospector's map was probably short, but every name on it would be dangerous, and Gideon had exactly one asset, one borrowed compass, and a talent for running that had never once impressed anyone he respected.

The hum was louder here. He noticed it again as the canyon walls pinched in — that sub-audible tremor in the rock, felt in the molars more than the ears, steady as machinery. He stopped on a flat shelf of stone and put his palm against the canyon wall, and the stone was warm. Not sun-warm. Sun had been behind cloud for an hour. Warm from within, faintly, persistently, like the flank of a sleeping animal. The Verrin ran cold off the high snows; every rational thing he knew about rivers and geology said this rock should be cool.

The compass needle trembled against its glass, straining upriver like a dog at the end of a chain.

Gideon looked across the water. On the far bank, at the edge of vision, the yellow oilskins flickered through a stand of dead alders — closer now, having gained on him while he dallied, patient as the rain.

"All right," he said to the canyon, to the compass, to the watcher, to nobody. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Let's see who wants it more."

He shouldered his satchel and climbed.

Behind him, downstream, so far off it might have been thunder, the sky was building storms over the plains he used to call home. He didn't look back at them. For the first time in eleven years, Gideon Whitlock was following something other than bad weather — and whatever waited at the head of the Verrin, he was beginning to suspect it had been waiting for him longer than he'd been walking.

The yellow oilskins paced him along the far bank, neither gaining nor falling behind, all the way into the gathering dusk.

And at dusk, when he made cold camp in the lee of a fallen slab, the figure crossed the river.
