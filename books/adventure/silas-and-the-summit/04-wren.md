## 4. Wren

Three days I'd followed him, and in three days he'd done nothing to earn the map.

That was my working theory, anyway, and I clung to it the way a drowning man clings to driftwood — not because it would hold, but because letting go meant admitting what I actually felt, which was something closer to shame. Shame that my father had died on a rail bridge with his blood soaking into the ties, and some stranger with a charming smile had walked away carrying the only thing Elias Marsh had ever chosen to give anyone.

I watched him from the far bank of the Verrin, where the willows grew thick enough to hide a woman in oilskins. He moved like a man who'd spent his life reading weather: always half-turned into the wind, eyes flicking upstream every few minutes, hands busy even when he was resting. He talked to himself, too. Low, constant, a running commentary of jokes nobody heard. At first I thought it was madness. By the second day I understood it was armor. Men who narrate their own lives don't have to listen to them.

He was camped where the river bent against a shale cliff, a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, and he'd rigged his tarp wrong — the low edge facing the water, so any wind coming down the gorge would fill it like a sail and peel it off the rocks by midnight. My father would have fixed it without comment. My father fixed things for strangers all the time. It was strangers he couldn't fix things *for* that got left behind.

Like me.

I crouched in the willows and went through the letter again, though I knew it by heart now, every smudged line of it. He'd written it from Callow's Ford, weeks before the end. *If you're reading this, Wren-girl, I've gone upriver one more time, and this time I don't expect to come down.* No apology in it. No explanation either, except the map itself, which he hadn't sent — which he'd apparently handed to the first luckless soul who tripped over him dying. *The man who carries it will be a stranger to you. That's the point. Strangers can be trusted exactly as far as they can be seen.*

I'd hated him for that sentence for eleven days straight. I expected to hate it for the rest of my life.

Below me, Gideon Whitlock fed a stick to his fire and said, conversationally, to no one: "You know what your problem is, Whitlock? You trust rivers. Rivers are just weather with ambitions."

I almost laughed. That was the worst part. Three days of hating him and he kept saying things that sounded like my father, and every time it landed somewhere under my ribs like a fishhook.

Enough. The river narrowed ahead — the gorge walls closing in where the current quickened, and after that there was a stretch I knew from my father's old surveys where the trail vanished into scree and a man alone could die stupidly. If I let him walk into it, I lost the map. If I followed him past it, I might as well be following a ghost. Tonight, then. Tonight I stopped being a shadow and became a problem.

I crossed at the shallows below his camp, boots around my neck, moving through water cold enough to make my teeth sing. The oilskins came off. Underneath I was just a woman in a canvas coat with a knife at her hip and her father's stubbornness in her blood, and I came up the bank behind him quiet as fog off the water.

He heard me at the last second. Credit where due — most men wouldn't have. He started to turn, and I put the knife's flat against his throat and my voice beside his ear.

"Evening, Mr. Whitlock."

He went very still. Then, because he was who he was, he said, "If this is about the tarp, I've been meaning to fix it."

"Hands where I can see them."

"Both of them? Because I need one to gesture. It's how I think."

"Whitlock."

"Right." He raised them, slow, palms out. "You've been following me since the bridge town. Oilskins. Far bank. You cross at the shallows because you don't trust the ferryman's dog not to remember you." A pause. "You're good. For what it's worth, I clocked you yesterday morning. Sun caught something metallic near the second bend."

"You didn't change course."

"Didn't want to spook you." And there it was — the smile, audible even without seeing it, the charm laid down like a card he didn't have to show. "So. Are we doing the part where you tell me what this is about, or do we skip to the part where I say something clever and regret it?"

"Turn around. Slowly."

He turned. Up close he looked worse than he had from across the river — hollowed, sleep-starved, a healing scrape along his jaw from the scree above the drowned town. His eyes went to my face and did a rapid, honest arithmetic: my father's gray in my eyes, my father's jaw, the family resemblance he had no way to name yet but recognized anyway, because men like him noticed patterns for a living.

"You look like someone," he said carefully.

"I'm going to ask you once," I said. "Where's the map?"

Something shifted behind his eyes — a door closing, then opening again a crack, deciding how much truth the moment could afford. "What map?"

I pressed the flat of the knife a fraction harder, not cutting, just reminding. "Elias Marsh drew it. On the back of a survey sheet, in pencil, because ink runs when a man is bleeding. He gave it to a storm chaser with debts, on a rail bridge, three days before the Syndicate's searchlights swept that valley looking for it." I watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out. "My father. My map. Where is it?"

"You're—" He exhaled. "You're Marsh's kid. Wren. He mentioned—"

"He mentioned nothing. He wrote me a letter and gave you everything else." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated it, hated that grief could ambush me even here, even now, with a knife in my hand and an enemy at its mercy. "Don't lie to me again. I've been lied to by professionals. You're an amateur."

"The inside pocket of my coat," he said quietly. "Left side. Wrapped in oilcloth."

I took it without taking the knife entirely away — a maneuver requiring more coordination than dignity allowed, and he had the grace not to comment. The oilcloth crackled open in my hands, and there it was: my father's pencil lines, his cramped surveyor's notation, the little crooked arrow he always drew north with, like it was pointing somewhere slightly better than north. I'd grown up watching him draw those arrows. I'd learned my letters tracing them.

Something in my chest folded closed, hard, like a lock throwing.

"It's mine," I said. "It was always going to be mine. You were just the courier."

"Maybe." He said it mildly, but he didn't move, didn't reach for it, and that mildness made me suspicious in a way aggression never could. "Can you read it?"

"Of course I can read it. I can read *him*. That's the whole—" I stopped.

Because I couldn't. Not entirely. The map wasn't a map, not the way ordinary maps were maps. The river was there, yes, and the gorge, and distances marked in my father's idiosyncratic paces. But threaded through it were symbols I half-knew — mining marks, yes, but also weather marks, barometric shorthand, the private language of a man who'd spent forty years thinking in pressure systems and ore veins simultaneously. And in the margins, tiny as insect tracks, ran notes in a cipher I'd seen exactly once, when I was nine, and been told never to ask about.

Gideon watched me struggle, and to his credit he didn't gloat. He just said, gently, "There's a key. Isn't there. Something he taught you, or something he hid, or something you two fought about. Cipher keys are always personal. That's what makes them safe."

"We didn't fight about anything," I said automatically, and then heard myself, and heard the lie in it, and Gideon heard it too, though he had the decency to pretend otherwise.

"Right," he said. "Well. Here's my situation, Wren Marsh. I've got Syndicate collectors who catalogued my entire life down to the spare socks, a debt with interest that compounds faster than floodwater, and a dead man's promise sitting on my conscience like a stone. What I don't have is the ability to read that map alone. So it seems to me we're both holding half a thing."

"And if I just take it and go?"

"Then you'll get as far as the narrows and die in the scree field, because the trail doesn't exist anymore unless you know where he rerouted it, and the reroute is in the cipher." He nodded at the map in my hands. "And I'll go back downstream and sell whatever I can salvage of this story to Corvus, who I imagine pays better than grief does."

The name hit like cold water. "You know Corvus?"

"Corvus knows everyone. It's his job." Gideon finally lowered his hands, slowly, testing the shape of the truce. "Look. I'm not going to pretend I'm a good man. Good men don't run up debts like mine. But I gave my word to a dying stranger, and it turns out my word is about the only asset I've got left that isn't mortgaged. So here's my offer: we read it together. We go upriver together. And when we find whatever your father found — you take it, whatever it is, and I take my freedom, and neither of us has to like the other."

I should have said no. Every instinct I'd inherited from a man who trusted strangers exactly as far as he could see them said *no, take the map, take the key, go alone, trust no one who smiles that easily.*

But my father had trusted *him*. Had bled out pressing a pencil-drawn map into the hands of a charming, drowning man rather than sending word to his own daughter. There was a reason in that reason, and I wanted it badly enough to stand in the firelight and bargain with the person holding it.

"One fire," I said finally. "We talk tonight. You tell me everything he said. Everything, in order, and don't polish it. In the morning we decide."

"Fair," he said. "Though I warn you, my memory improves dramatically when fed. Do you have food? I've been eating river trout and self-pity."

Against my will — genuinely against my will — I snorted.

* * *

We built the fire up against the shale cliff, where its light wouldn't carry. He produced a tin of coffee that tasted like bark and regret, and I produced dried fish wrapped in wax paper, and we ate across the flames from each other like two armies negotiating a border neither trusted.

"Start at the bridge," I said.

So he told it. All of it, unpolished, exactly as I'd demanded — the rain coming down in ropes, the prospector collapsed between the rails with his side opened up, the smell of blood and creosote. How Elias had grabbed his wrist with a grip that shouldn't have been possible from a dying man. How he'd said *take it upriver, tell no one, burn it after,* and how Gideon had promised, because what else do you promise a man who's already leaving?

"He asked about you," Gideon said, quieter now. The fire popped. "Near the end. He said — he said, 'Tell my girl the compass points true.' I didn't know what he meant. I still don't, entirely. But he said it twice, like it mattered."

The compass. I stared at him. "Show me."

He hesitated, then dug it out — a heavy brass thing, old, its case scarred nearly smooth, the needle settling with a faint shiver toward... not north. Toward upriver. Toward the source, wherever it lay. I turned it in my fingers and felt my breath go strange, because I knew this compass. I'd known it before I knew my own name. It had hung on a peg in our cabin my whole childhood, right beside the door, and my father had told me it belonged to a man named Silas, and when I'd asked who Silas was, my father had looked at me with an expression I'd spent twenty years learning not to ask about.

"This is Silas's," I said. "This was in our house. Why do you have—"

"He gave it to me. On the bridge. Said I'd need it more than he would." Gideon's voice had gone careful, the charm stripped off it, and underneath was something rawer. "Wren, I don't know what your father was into. But it wasn't prospecting. Prospectors don't get hunted by searchlights. Prospectors don't carry ciphers. Whatever's at the top of this river, he thought it was worth dying to keep out of the wrong hands — and he picked me, a stranger, a debtor, a man with no loyalties, to carry it. I've been asking myself why for three days and the best answer I've got is: because I had nothing to lose and no one to betray."

"That's not an answer. That's a diagnosis."

"Yeah," he said, and smiled, but it didn't reach anywhere important. "Welcome to my whole personality."

I sat with the compass in my palm and the fire painting us both orange, and I thought about my father writing that letter in Callow's Ford, choosing a stranger over me, and for the first time it occurred to me that maybe it hadn't been a choice about trust at all. Maybe it had been a choice about protection. Maybe Elias Marsh had loved me exactly enough to keep me out of it — and had failed, because I was his daughter, and his daughter had inherited the one trait he could never cure in himself.

We follow. Both of us. Right up the mountain, past sense, past safety.

"The cipher," I said. "There's a key. He used to set puzzles for me when I was small — riddles on scraps of paper, hidden around the cabin. The answers were always things from..." I stopped. Started again. "From the year my mother left. Everything he ever encoded came back to that. Her name, the date, the river stage the day she went. He built the lock out of the worst year of his life so no one would ever guess it."

Gideon was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had none of the performance left in it. "That's the saddest safe I ever heard of."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

"So we open it tomorrow."

"We open it tonight," I said, because sleep was a country I wasn't going to reach, and because every hour we sat still was an hour the Syndicate gained. I spread the map flat on a rock between us, weighted at the corners with stones, and pulled the letter from my coat, and began, number by number, date by date, feeding my father's worst year into his lock.

The first marginal note resolved at midnight: *THE RIVER REMEMBERS WHAT WAS TAKEN.* The second, near dawn: *NOT A MINE. NEVER WAS A MINE.*

Gideon read them upside down, and I watched the color drain out of his face a second time that night.

"Wren," he said slowly. "Your father wasn't leading us to treasure."

"No," I said, and the fire had burned down to coals, and the river ran loud and dark past our camp, and somewhere upstream the thing my father had died documenting was waiting with its secret held against its chest. "He was leading us to evidence."

Above the gorge, the sky was going gray at the edges, and far off — too far to be thunder, too rhythmic to be anything else — I heard engines.
