## 5. The Drowned Town

**GIDEON**

The river told them before the town did.

Gideon had learned to read water the way other men read faces — a skill born of necessity, honed through years of chasing storms across country that punished carelessness. And the Verrin was speaking in a voice he'd never heard from it before: slow, sullen, thick with silt that had nowhere to go. A river backing up. A river being held.

"Feel that?" Wren said from the bow of the borrowed skiff, her paddle resting across her knees. She didn't look back at him. She hadn't looked back at him much since the fire two nights ago, when they'd struck their uneasy truce — if truce was even the word for an agreement made by two people who each kept one hand near a weapon.

"The current's dead," Gideon said. "We're pushing against a wall we can't see."

"A dam."

"Downstream, maybe. Miles." He squinted at the water ahead, where the canyon walls fell away and the valley opened like a hand unclenching. "Or something older than a dam."

What they found, around the next bend, was neither.

Callow's Ford rose out of the water like a drowned man's hand.

The first thing he saw was the church steeple, tilted fifteen degrees off true, its shingled spire black with decades of standing water. Below it, rooflines broke the surface at odd intervals — some collapsed into themselves, some still stubbornly whole, their eaves furred with moss and trailing curtains of dead reeds. A main street ran beneath them all, visible in the strange clarity of the shallows: cobblestones, a hitching rail, the door of a general store standing open on darkness, silt drifting through it like smoke frozen mid-pour.

The town hadn't flooded. The town had been *drowned* — deliberately, slowly, the way you'd drown something you didn't want to hear anymore.

"God," Wren breathed. She shipped her paddle and let the skiff drift. "My father never mentioned this."

"He wouldn't have." Gideon kept his voice flat, but his chest had gone tight in a way he didn't like. He knew what this was. Everyone in the basin knew the shape of it, even if they'd never seen it: the Syndicate didn't destroy places with fire or dynamite when it could help it. It used paper. Water rights. Eminent domain writs signed by judges who owed their seats to Syndicate money. It turned towns into inconveniences and inconveniences into lakes, and then it wrote the lakes out of the maps, and twenty years later nobody remembered there had ever been anything under the water but water.

He'd catalogued enough of those papers himself, in another life, to know the procedure by heart.

"There." Wren pointed.

Past the steeple, where the flooded street widened into what must once have been the town square, a cluster of structures sat above the waterline — not ruins, but rafts. Platforms of salvaged timber lashed together with rope and wire, cabins built from doorframes and window sashes pried out of the drowned houses below, everything patched and canted and improbably alive. Laundry hung between poles. Smoke rose from a stovepipe. A rope ferry stretched from the shore to the largest platform, its pulley gleaming with fresh grease.

Somebody had stayed. Somebody had built a life on top of the grave.

"We need supplies," Gideon said. "And information. If the Syndicate dammed the lower river, there'll be patrols."

Wren was already paddling. "Then stop talking and row."

* * *

The woman who met them at the ferry landing was sixty if she was a day, broad as a barrel and weathered to the color of old rope, with a scarred wooden pipe clamped in the corner of her mouth and a boat hook held with the easy authority of someone who had pulled a great many fools out of a great many rivers.

"Ferry's a copper bit," she said, before either of them spoke. "Crossing back's another. Supplies cost extra. Trouble costs double." Her eyes — pale, sharp, the gray of river fog — went from Gideon's storm-worn jacket to Wren's oilskins to the wrapped bundle strapped across Gideon's back, and lingered there exactly half a second too long. "You're not from downstream."

"No," Gideon said. "We're headed up."

"Everyone's headed up these days." She spat over the side, unhurried. "That's the problem with upstream. Everything flows down eventually, and none of it's good."

"We can pay," Wren said flatly.

"I know you can. Question's what else you're carrying." The woman stepped aside, though, and waved them onto the platform with the boat hook. "Odile Vasseur. This is my crossing. Mind the gaps, mind the kids, and don't touch nothing that hums."

"Hums?" Gideon asked.

But she was already walking away, and the question died behind him as he got his first real look at the settlement.

It was bigger than it had seemed from the water. Three platforms, maybe four, connected by gangplanks and rope bridges, built upward in crooked tiers because there was nowhere left to build outward. And everywhere — *everywhere* — children. A girl of perhaps ten hauling a bucket of river water up a ladder with the grim competence of a dockhand. Two boys mending a net on the planks, arguing about knots. A small figure perched on the roof of the highest cabin, face turned toward the drowned steeple, watching the water with the stillness of a heron.

They weren't playing. That was what caught in Gideon's throat. Kids were supposed to play. These children worked the way adults work — without complaint, without pause, with the economy of people who had learned early that idleness was a luxury the river didn't stock.

"Refugees," Wren said quietly, beside him. Not a question.

"When they drowned the Ford, anybody who wouldn't sign the relocation papers lost everything." Odile had reappeared, silent as driftwood, pipe smoke curling around her head. "Most of 'em took the Syndicate's coin and moved to company towns downstream, which is just a slower way of drowning. Some of 'em wouldn't go. Some of 'em were already orphans, or near enough." She shrugged, a mountain range shifting. "Water finds the low places. So do I."

"You took them in," Gideon said.

"I put a roof where there wasn't one. They did the rest." Her eyes came back to him, and there was something in them now — not warmth, exactly. Assessment. The look of a woman counting a stranger's weight before deciding whether the plank would hold him. "You've got collector's hands, chaser. I've seen a hundred pairs. You handle paper for men who drown towns?"

The question landed harder than she could have known. Gideon felt Wren glance at him and made himself hold Odile's gaze.

"I used to," he said. "Not anymore."

"Used to is a wide country." She drew on her pipe. "Supplies are in the long shed. Rice, salt fish, lamp oil, rope. Pay what it's worth and no haggling, I haven't the patience. And chaser—" she caught his sleeve as he turned, her grip surprisingly strong, "—there's a man come through yesterday asking after travelers on the river. Well-dressed. Soft-spoken. Paid in new-minted scrip." She let that sit. "Syndicate scrip."

The cold moved through Gideon's gut like the river's dead current. Corvus. It had to be. The man didn't chase; he *arrived*, the way weather arrives, and you found yourself inside him before you knew the sky had changed.

"What did you tell him?" Wren asked.

Odile smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "I told him the truth. That I see everyone who passes and remember none of them." She released Gideon's sleeve. "I'm good at truth that lies. Now go buy your rice."

* * *

**WREN**

Wren bought the supplies because she didn't trust Gideon to haggle without charming someone, and charming was how trouble started. She counted out coins for salt fish and lamp oil while Odile's grandchild — or whatever the girl was; on this raft the word had probably stopped meaning anything specific — stacked tins with quick brown hands.

"You're going to the summit," the girl said. Not looking up. Stacking tins.

Wren went still. "Why would you say that?"

"'Cause everyone who comes through going up has that look." The girl glanced at her then, and her eyes were old, old eyes in a young face, and Wren thought: *this child has watched more strangers drown than most undertakers.* "Papa Elias had it too."

The world narrowed to the space between one heartbeat and the next.

"Elias Marsh," Wren said. Carefully. As carefully as she had ever said anything. "You knew him."

"Knew *of* him. He'd come down sometimes. Years back." The girl finished stacking and wiped her hands on her dress. "He'd trade maps for food. Drawed pictures for us kids of what the town looked like before. Mama Odile says he was the only one who ever wrote down our names like they mattered." She frowned, remembering. "He always sat facing the water. Like he was keeping watch on it. Or apologizing."

Wren's hands had gone cold around the coin purse. Her father. Her father, who had walked out of her life when she was nine and sent nothing for twenty years but a final letter and a map folded into a stranger's hands — her father had sat on this raft, drawing pictures of a drowned town for orphaned children, writing down names.

She had spent three weeks telling herself Elias Marsh had been a coward. A man who couldn't face his own daughter, so he'd handed his life's work to whoever happened along. It had been easier to hate him that way. It had given the anger somewhere to live.

Now she stood in a drowned town he had apparently loved, holding groceries, and the anger had nowhere to go.

"Did he say anything," she managed. "About where he was going. About why."

The girl shook her head. Then paused. "He gave Mama Odile a letter once. Said if a girl ever came asking — dark hair, mean look—" the girl's mouth twitched, entirely without apology, "—Mama should give it to her."

Wren crossed the platform in six strides.

Odile was coiling rope by the ferry landing, unhurried as ever, but when Wren stopped in front of her, the old woman set the rope down and reached into her coat without being asked. The envelope she produced was soft with age, water-stained at one corner, addressed in a cramped, sure hand: *For my Wren, if she comes.*

*If she comes.* Not *when*. Even at the end, he hadn't dared assume.

"Read it private or read it here," Odile said. "Makes no difference to me. But I'll tell you what he told me, the last time he passed through, because I think you ought to carry it with the paper." She leaned on the rail, looking out at the tilted steeple. "He said, *'Odile, I spent my whole life mapping what men wanted to take. Turns out the only map that matters is the one showing what they've already taken.'*" She tapped ash into the water. "Then he paid his ferry fare and went upriver, and that was the last anyone saw."

Wren opened the envelope with fingers that didn't feel like hers.

Inside was a single page. No apology — she'd braced for an apology and was almost grateful it wasn't there, because she might have torn it up. Instead, a list. Names, dates, figures. Payments. Signatures. A column of towns, eleven of them, each with a year beside it, each year marking when the water came. Callow's Ford was third from the top. At the bottom, in letters pressed hard enough to tear the page: *They aren't dams. They're weights. Ask yourself what needs holding down.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost gentle: *If you're reading this, you found your way here without me. I'm sorry for it, and proud of it, both. Don't trust the compass. Trust the river.*

"Don't trust the compass," Wren whispered.

Behind her, she heard Gideon's boots on the planks, and she folded the page away fast, into the pocket over her heart, where things lived that she wasn't ready to share.

* * *

**GIDEON**

He found her at the rail, staring at the water, and whatever she'd been reading she'd put away, but her face had the particular set of someone carrying new weight in an old pack. He'd worn that expression himself often enough to recognize it. He didn't ask. There was a time, not long ago, when he'd have asked — charm was mostly curiosity weaponized — but three weeks upriver had taught him that Wren's answers cost more than his questions were worth.

Instead he leaned on the rail beside her and looked down at the drowned street.

From above, in the late light, the water was clear enough to make out details: a child's wagon lying on its side in the doorway of a house. A piano, impossibly, sitting upright in a parlor, its keys furred green. A mailbox at the end of a submerged walk, still mounted on its post, patient as a dog.

"Somebody's mail's still coming," he said quietly.

"Nobody's written to Callow's Ford in thirty years," Wren said.

"That's what gets me." He turned a copper bit over his knuckles, an old habit, a gambler's fidget. "The Syndicate erases places for a living. I helped. Paperwork, mostly — valuations, liens, the arithmetic of making a town worth less than a reservoir. You tell yourself it's just numbers." The coin stopped moving. "Standing here, it doesn't feel like numbers."

"No," Wren said. "It doesn't."

Across the platform, the evening was assembling itself around them without any invitation. Odile had lit lanterns on poles, and their light lay gold on the black water. Someone had started a cookfire, and the smell of fish and wild onions drifted over the planks. The children were gathering — drawn by the fire, by habit, by some gravity of their own — and one of the boys, the knot-arguer, had spotted Gideon's jacket, with its faded storm-service patch, and was looking at it with naked, undisguised hunger.

"You're a chaser," the boy said. Accusatory. Awed.

"Was."

"Chasers die," the boy said, with relish. "Everybody knows. Lightning takes 'em, or the wind, or the hail. Papa said a chaser's just a man who's decided how he wants to be buried."

"Your papa sounds like a laugh at parties."

"He drowned." The boy said it the way he'd report the weather. "In the second flood. When they raised the dam level."

The evening went very quiet around that sentence. Gideon knelt, slowly, bringing himself down to the boy's eye level, and found the other children had gathered too, a silent semicircle of them, watching him the way they'd probably watched every stranger who ever crossed — measuring whether this one would leave something behind or only take.

"Do you know what a chaser actually does?" he asked.

"Runs from storms."

"Runs *at* storms. Reads them." He held out his palm, and when the boy hesitantly placed his small hand in it, Gideon pressed it flat, palm-up. "Feel that? The air's got weight tonight. Means the pressure's falling. Tomorrow, maybe the day after, the wind's going to swing around to the southeast and the clouds will stack up tall and gray-green, like dirty snow. And when the birds go quiet all at once — not flying away, just *quiet* — that's when you get off the water and tie everything down twice." He closed the boy's fingers around the knowledge like a coin. "A storm tells you everything it's going to do, hours before it does it. Most folks just don't listen."

The boy stared at his own fist as though it contained lightning.

"Teach us," said a girl behind him. The bucket-hauler, arms crossed. "All of it."

"Marisol," Odile called warningly from the fire.

"He offered!"

"I offered nothing—"

"You held out your *hand*."

And Gideon Whitlock, who had spent forty-one years calculating exits — from debts, from towns, from marriages, from every human arrangement heavier than a handshake — heard himself say: "First lesson's free. After that, you pay in fish. Chasers eat a lot."

The cheer that went up nearly knocked him off the platform.

Later — much later, when the fire had burned to embers and the children slept in heaps like puppies and Wren had vanished somewhere with her thoughts and her father's letter — Gideon sat at the rail with Odile and listened to the water lap against the pilings of a town that refused to be a memory.

"You could stay," Odile said, apropos of nothing, puffing her pipe. "Raft's always short a pair of hands that knows weather."

"I owe the Syndicate more than this raft is worth. Twice over."

"I know. Saw the way you flinched at the scrip." She tapped her pipe out. "Debt's just water, chaser. It rises, it falls, it finds cracks. Only question is whether you learn to swim or stand there counting how deep it is." She stood, joints cracking like kindling, and paused at the edge of the firelight. "Man came through yesterday — the soft-spoken one. Left a card. Said if travelers passed through, I should send word downstream and there'd be payment in it." She drew something from her coat and held it out: a heavy cream-colored card, embossed with the Meridian Syndicate's triple-tower seal.

Gideon took it. His stomach had dropped through the planks, through the drowned street, through thirty years of silt.

"And?" he said. "Are you sending word?"

Odile smiled her unfriendly smile and nodded at the card in his hand.

"Read the back."

He turned it over. In pencil, in a firm, economical hand: *Three days. Then I stop being polite. — C.*

Below that, in Odile's own charcoal, added sometime after: *Burned the messenger pigeon. Fed the bird to the kids. Best meal they've had all month.*

"Get your sleep, chaser," Odile said, and walked away into the dark, leaving Gideon alone with the card, the embers, and the sound of the river — patient, patient, patient — carrying everything downstream, toward the sea, past the weights that held it down.
