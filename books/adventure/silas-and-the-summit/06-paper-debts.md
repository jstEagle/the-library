## 6. Paper Debts

The rain found them an hour before Corvus did.

It came down the gorge in a gray wall, swallowing the ridgelines one by one, and Gideon watched it come the way he'd once watched storms on the plains — with that old animal patience, reading the shape of the cloud base, the greenish cast to the light, the way the river went suddenly quiet beneath its noise. Some part of him had never stopped being a man who stood in open country and waited for the sky to tell him what it intended.

"You're smiling," Wren said.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's a terrible day and you're smiling at it."

He tipped his head back and let the first drops hit his face, cold as coins. "Bad weather is good cover," he said. "Nobody follows anyone into weather like this unless they have to."

"Someone's been following us for two days," Wren said. She said it the way she said most things — flat, certain, already done arguing about it. "I lost the sign at the burned homestead, but I didn't lose the *feeling*. There's a difference between being followed and being hunted. We're being hunted."

Gideon didn't answer. He was watching the river instead, where the water had begun to rise along the stones at the margin, climbing them like something patient climbing stairs. Two days ago the figure in oilskins had tracked them along the far bank; two nights ago she had stepped out of the dark with a knife at his throat and turned out to be Elias Marsh's daughter, and now here they were, sharing a fire neither of them trusted, walking upstream together because the map needed both of them — her father's hand to read, Gideon's stubbornness to follow.

They made camp under an overhang of granite where the gorge pinched narrow, and Gideon built a small fire because cold was a slower killer than discovery and he was tired of choosing the faster death. The compass sat in his palm through all of it, as it had sat there every night since the bridge, since Elias Marsh had pressed it into his bleeding hands along with the map and a promise Gideon hadn't wanted to make.

*Take it to the source. Tell no one. Burn it after.*

The needle swung its slow wrong swing, pointing not north but upriver, toward whatever waited at the head of the Verrin. Modified, Elias had written in the margin, in handwriting gone thin near the end. *Silas always said north was a matter of opinion.*

Gideon wondered, not for the first time, who Silas had been, and why a dead man's name lived inside a dead man's compass, and why he himself felt like a man carrying letters addressed to other people.

The rain thickened. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled down the canyon walls like a barrel going downstairs.

Wren slept first — or pretended to, curled against the rock with her knife hand free, which was her version of sleeping. Gideon took first watch, which was his version of honesty: he would rather stay awake than be caught asleep beside someone who had disarmed him in four seconds flat.

That was when the lantern came around the bend downstream.

Not a searchlight. Not the white glare of Syndicate enforcement craft he remembered from the night collectors had boarded his rig mid-tornado and catalogued his life against his debt. This was a soft yellow glow, carried low and steady, moving at walking pace, unhurried as a man coming home. It stopped perhaps thirty yards off, on the far side of a curtain of rain, and a voice came through the water-sound, pitched to carry and no further.

"Gideon Whitlock."

Wren's eyes were open. Of course they were. Her hand found her knife without her body moving at all.

"You know my name," Gideon called back.

"Everyone worth knowing knows yours." A pause, and then, almost gently: "May I approach? I'm wet, I'm alone, and I've brought coffee. Actual coffee. You'll remember what that is."

Gideon looked at Wren. Wren looked back with an expression that said *your funeral*, which, given that the voice had used his full name, seemed fair.

"Come slow," he said. "Hands visible."

* * *

Corvus was not what Gideon expected, though he couldn't have said what he'd expected instead. Enforcement men were big and blunt and smelled of engine oil. This man was slight, silver-templed, dressed in a long coat of good wool gone dark with rain, and he moved like someone who had spent thirty years making himself unremarkable in rooms full of dangerous people. He set the lantern down, produced a battered enamel pot, and poured three cups of coffee without asking whether three cups were welcome.

"Marsh's daughter," he said to Wren, nodding. "You have his chin."

"You have thirty seconds before I decide you don't," Wren said.

Corvus smiled as if she'd paid him a compliment, and handed Gideon a cup. The coffee was real, bitter, black as the gorge at night. Gideon drank it and hated how much it steadied him.

"Here's the shape of things," Corvus said, settling onto a stone across the fire with the ease of a man who had sat by ten thousand fires that weren't his. "You owe the Meridian Syndicate eleven thousand, four hundred marks. Your rig was seized against it. Your chase license is suspended. Your accounts are frozen, your name is in the red book, and every port, ferry, and freight office between here and the coast has instructions to hold you. That's the bad news, and you already knew it, so let's skip to the good news."

"Is there any?"

"There's a great deal." Corvus produced a leather folio from inside his coat, dry despite the rain, and opened it on his knee. "Deliver the map to the Syndicate. That's all. One document, hand-drawn, worthless to anyone but us. In exchange: the debt dies. Not deferred — *dies*. Your license is restored. Your rig comes back out of impound, fueled and repaired. And there's a signing bonus sufficient to put you back in the air within the month, chasing weather, owing no one."

The fire popped. Rain drummed the granite overhang. Gideon became aware that he was holding his cup too tightly and made himself loosen his fingers one at a time.

"And if I say the map doesn't exist?"

"Then I'll be disappointed," Corvus said pleasantly, "because I've been walking this river for six days in the rain, and I'm too old for disappointment." He turned a page in the folio. "Elias Marsh came to us eight months ago offering to sell the source's location. We declined. Not because we doubted him — because we already know where it is. We've known for forty years. What we don't have is his documentation, and the man was careless enough to get himself stabbed over it in a rail yard." Something crossed his face then, brief and unreadable, gone before Gideon could name it. "The map is a formality. But formalities matter. My employers are very fond of paper."

"You're lying," Wren said.

"I'm frequently lying," Corvus agreed. "But not about this. Ask yourself, girl — why does a syndicate that owns half the river country care about one prospector's drawing? Because paper can be shown to courts. To journalists. To the kind of people who ask questions about drowned towns and dammed rivers." His eyes flicked to Wren, and there was nothing pleasant in them at all. "Callow's Ford wasn't an accident. Nothing is an accident. Everything is a ledger entry."

Gideon felt the words land somewhere below his ribs. He thought of Odile's ferry, the kids hauling lines, the rooftops standing in brown water like teeth in a drowned jaw.

"What happens to the map," he asked slowly, "after you have it?"

"It goes into an archive."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Corvus said. "It isn't." He closed the folio. "But here's what I can give you that is. Eleven thousand four hundred marks, Whitlock. I've read your ledger. All of it. The medical debt from your mother's last year — the interest compounding on that alone is criminal, and I use the word precisely. The rig loan. The season you lost everything chasing the Kestrel cell and still flew anyway, because people on the ground needed the warning and you were the only fool willing to give it." He paused. "You're not a debtor by nature. You're a debtor by grief. The Syndicate understands grief. It's built on it."

The rain filled the silence. Gideon stared into his cup and saw, absurdly, the numbers — he always saw the numbers, they lived behind his eyes like birds roosting on a wire. Eleven thousand four hundred. Fourteen years of his life, itemized. His mother's hospital bed. The rig. The compass — no, the compass hadn't been in the catalogue; the collectors had weighed it and laughed and tossed it back, worthless brass.

And now it pointed upriver, toward a thing the Syndicate had spent forty years keeping, and a dead man had trusted it to him, and a living woman was watching him across the fire with her father's chin and her father's stubbornness, waiting to see what kind of man she'd tied herself to.

"I'll consider it," Gideon heard himself say.

Corvus studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw, he apparently decided it was enough, because he nodded, rose, and tucked the folio away. "Three days," he said. "There's a marker cairn at the confluence where the Verrin takes the north fork. Leave word there — a ribbon, a stone, anything. If I hear nothing, I'll assume you chose the river over the arithmetic." He picked up his lantern, then paused, and for just a moment the pleasantness dropped away entirely, and Gideon saw the thing underneath, the thirty years of it. "For what it's worth, Whitlock — and it may not be worth much — I pulled men out of box canyons for these people for three decades. They never once thanked me. Think carefully about whose ledger you'd rather be a line in."

Then the lantern swung away downstream, and the rain folded over the place where he'd been, and it was as if no one had ever come.

* * *

Wren didn't speak until the coffee was cold.

"You're going to do it," she said.

"I said I'd consider it."

"That's what liars say when they haven't finished lying to themselves yet." She fed a stick to the fire without looking at him. "Eleven thousand marks. A new rig. A clean name. You'd sell my father's dying wish for a fresh start, and honestly? I understand it. That's the worst part. If someone offered me that price, I don't know what I'd say either."

"Wren—"

"But here's what you should sit with tonight." Now she did look at him, and the firelight made her eyes look like the river in flood. "If you sell it, you don't buy your freedom. You buy a job. The Syndicate doesn't erase debts, Gideon. It *moves* them. You'd walk out of the red book and straight into their pocket, and every storm you chased after that, you'd be chasing for them. My father figured that out. It cost him everything, including me." Her voice caught, barely, and she killed the catch like snuffing a match. "So consider it. Consider it all night. Just make sure you're considering the right thing."

She rolled into her bedroll and faced the wall of rock, and Gideon sat alone with the fire and the rain and the compass.

* * *

Sleep did not come.

He tried the usual doors — the breathing exercise, the counting, the old storm-chaser's trick of naming clouds until the mind went quiet — cumulus, cumulonimbus, wall cloud, shelf — but the names dissolved as soon as he spoke them, and what remained was the ledger. It spread itself out behind his eyelids in Corvus's neat columns, every line a year of his life: *Whitlock, G. — outstanding. Whitlock, G. — extended. Whitlock, G. — collateral seized.* Fourteen years of being a number that other people owned, and here was a man in a wet wool coat offering to strike it all with one signature, and all it would cost was a piece of paper.

A piece of paper. As if that were all it was.

He turned the compass over in his hands. The brass case was warm from his pocket, dented at one corner where some old impact had left its mark, and the needle swung its faithful wrong swing, pointing upriver, up-gorge, toward the source of everything. *North is a matter of opinion.* Elias Marsh had modified this instrument so it could find one place in the world and only that place, and then he had sent it out into the dark with a stranger, because he trusted a stranger more than he trusted the world.

Or because he had no one else.

No — Wren's face surfaced, furious and hurt — because he'd chosen not to trust his own daughter, and she would carry that wound to her grave unless somebody gave her a reason to put it down. Gideon could sell the map and buy his way clear, and Wren would lose her father twice: once to the rail yard, once to Gideon's arithmetic.

Or he could keep faith with a dead man, and spend the rest of his life in the red book, owned, catalogued, a line item.

*Everything is a ledger entry,* Corvus had said, and the horrible truth was that Gideon believed him. The Syndicate thought in ledgers. Debts and assets, costs and returns. Which meant the question was not whether Gideon could afford to refuse the offer. The question was what kind of creature he became if he accepted — whether a man could sell a dead friend's trust and still call himself a man, or whether he'd simply be a well-dressed line item walking around in boots.

He thought of the collectors boarding his rig in the tornado's outer bands, wind screaming, hail ticking off the hull like applause, while a bored clerk walked through his life with a clipboard. *Item: meteorological instruments, professional grade. Item: sleeping bag, worn. Item: photographs, personal, negligible value.* They had held up a photo of his mother, from the cab's sun visor, and asked if it had resale value, and Gideon had said *no* in a voice he didn't recognize, and the clerk had written *negligible* and moved on.

Negligible. That was what the Syndicate thought a mother was. A town. A river. A father's last wish.

The fire burned down to coals. The rain kept on, steady now, the storm spending itself somewhere over the high country, and Gideon sat with the compass open in his palm and watched the needle strain upriver like a dog against a leash, and understood, finally, why Elias had built it this way. Not to find the source. To make sure that whoever carried it could never pretend they were headed anywhere else. Every hour of every day, the little brass instrument asked the same question: *which way are you actually going?*

North was a matter of opinion. So was honor. So was the difference between a debt you owed and a debt you were.

Toward dawn, the rain thinned to mist, and the gorge came back into existence around them, dripping and gray and indifferent. Gideon closed the compass, put it away, and lay down without sleeping, staring at the stone overhead, doing the arithmetic one more time and hating himself for doing it at all.

Eleven thousand four hundred marks against a dead man's trust. Against a woman's second grief. Against a drowned town full of kids who ferried strangers because the water belonged to everyone once.

Against whatever hummed at the top of the river, behind forty years of erased records, that the Syndicate wanted kept secret badly enough to send a gentle man in a wool coat walking six days through the rain.

When the light came up, Wren was already packing, moving with her economical anger, not looking at him.

"The confluence is a day downstream," she said. "If you're leaving word for him, say it now and I'll walk out alone. If you're not—" she slung her pack, and for the first time since the fire, something in her face was almost soft, almost hopeful, and it was worse than the anger, "—then we've got whitewater ahead, and I'd rather not drown next to a man who's already sold me."

Gideon stood, shouldered his own pack, and looked up the gorge, where the river ran fast and loud over rocks the color of old bone, toward the source, toward the choice, toward whatever kind of man he was going to be by nightfall.

"I'm not leaving word," he said.

Wren held his gaze a moment longer, then nodded once and started upriver.

They reached the rapids by midmorning.
