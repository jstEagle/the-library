## 7. Rapids and Reckonings

The river told Wren things her father never had.

It told her when the canyon walls were closing in, because the current quickened and the water went from brown to a hard, glassy green. It told her where the rocks slept just beneath the surface, because the current wrinkled over them like skin over knuckles. It told her the sky's intentions hours before the sky acted on them — a yellowing in the west, a hush in the pines, the smell of ozone and wet stone rising out of the gorge like breath from an open grave.

She had learned all of this before she was ten years old, standing on the deck of her father's scow while Elias Marsh read the water aloud to her like scripture. *See how it piles up there, little wren? That's a hole waiting to swallow you. See how it smooths? That's a lie. Smooth water is the river holding its breath.*

Her father had taught her everything except how to stop loving him.

Now she stood on the bank above the narrows with a stranger's compass in her pocket and watched the Verrin come down through the gorge like something escaped from a cage, and she understood that the river was telling her one more thing: that the route on Elias's map ran straight through the worst water she had ever seen.

"You want to run *that*," Gideon said beside her. He had his hands jammed in his pockets and his chin tucked into his collar, and he was doing a poor job of hiding how pale he'd gone. "In a raft we borrowed from a woman who charges by the lie."

"It's the only way through." She didn't look at him. Looking at him made it worse — made her remember that this man, this charming, broke, debt-ridden stranger, was carrying the last thing her father ever made. "The map shows a portage trail on the left bank, but Elias drew it eleven years ago. The Syndicate flooded the lower canyon since then. If the trail still exists, it's under forty feet of water."

"So we run it."

"So we run it."

Gideon exhaled through his teeth. Downriver, the gorge narrowed to maybe thirty feet across, and the whole weight of the Verrin funneled into it with a sound like continuous thunder — not the sharp crack of storm thunder but a deep, rolling roar that Wren felt in her molars, in the soles of her boots, in the cage of her ribs. Spray hung above the entry chute in a permanent gray mist. Rainbows lived there and died there, over and over.

"I've run Class Fours," Gideon offered.

"This is a Five. Maybe worse, with the meltwater." She crouched and let her fingers trail in the current, reading its temperature the way another woman might read a letter. Cold enough to stop a heart in four minutes. Cold enough to make strong arms useless in ninety seconds. "If you go in, keep your feet up. Don't try to stand — foot entrapment kills more people than drowning does. Swim aggressive, angle for the eddies behind the boulders. And whatever you do—"

"Don't fight the river." He said it flatly, like reciting someone else's prayer. "Odile's kids told me. Twice."

"Then you might live."

* * *

They launched at midmorning from the shingle bar where Odile's people kept the big raft lashed under a tarp of stitched oilcloth. The raft was a monster — three inflated bladders of cured hide wrapped around a timber frame, patched in a dozen places, ugly as sin and buoyant as a promise. Wren took the stern oars. Gideon took the bow line and the bail bucket, which struck her as an accurate division of labor: she would steer them through hell, and he could scoop.

"Talk to me," she said as they pushed off. "The whole way down. If you go quiet, I'll think you're dead."

"What do you want me to talk about?"

"Anything. The weather. Your debts. How many times you've lied to me since Callow's Ford."

"That's a short list," he called back, and she heard the grin in it, that easy charm he wore like armor, and she hated how much she wanted to believe it. "I've only lied to you once. I told you I wasn't afraid of your father's map. I'm terrified of it. I dream about it. It sits there at night looking at me like it knows something."

The walls closed in. The roar swelled until speech became useless, until they were shouting without words, until the world reduced itself to green water and gray stone and the two of them small as insects in a crack between them.

The first mile was survivable. Waves the size of wagons came at them in sets of three, and Wren threaded the raft through the gaps with her arms burning and her teeth clenched, feeling each wave take the stern and lift it, hold it, drop it. Gideon bailed with the frantic rhythm of a man who had discovered religion. Once, a wave broke clean over the bow and filled the raft to the frame, and for one long second they were sitting in a churning pond suspended above a killing river, and then the self-bailing holes did their work and the water sighed out beneath them.

Wren laughed. She heard herself laugh — a wild, involuntary bark of joy — and hated herself for it, and laughed again anyway.

That was the moment the river stopped playing fair.

She saw it too late. A lateral wave off the right wall met the main current at the top of the next drop and folded into a haystack of standing waves, and behind it, half-hidden in the spray, a hole yawned open — a true keeper, the kind that took a boat and held it and turned it over and over like a hand worrying a stone. There was no left. There was no right. There was only through.

"Paddle!" she screamed, though he had no paddle, though screaming was useless, though the word was torn from her mouth before it finished forming. "HARD LEFT—"

The raft hit the trough and the world went white.

Water hit her like a building falling. The oars ripped sideways; one tore free of her grip and cartwheeled away. The raft stood on its edge, hung there — a horrible, weightless instant in which Wren saw the whole gray sky through the spray, saw Gideon's arm flung out, saw the compass case fly from his jacket pocket and skip across the water like a struck stone —

— and then they were over.

Cold took her the way a fist takes a jaw. Not cold — *annihilation*. The Verrin in spring melt was not water, it was a decision the mountain had made about her, and the decision was no. She tumbled. Up and down lost their meaning. Her lungs locked. Somewhere below her, or above her, the raft boomed against rock. Somewhere near her, a man was dying, and she knew it the way you know a thing in a dream, absolutely and without proof.

*Feet up,* she told herself. *Don't fight it. Swim aggressive.*

Her shoulder struck stone and the pain cut through the panic like a lamp through fog, and she used it — used the pain to find her body, find the surface, find *downstream*, and she surfaced gasping into a chaos of foam and roar and gray light. Twenty feet away, Gideon's head broke water once, twice. His arms windmilled. His mouth opened in a scream she couldn't hear over the thunder, and then the current took him sideways, straight toward the seam where two currents met and folded under — the place her father had called the wringer, the place where swimmers went down and did not come up.

He went under. He did not come up.

Later, Wren would not be able to reconstruct what she did. She would remember fragments: the boulder sliding past her hip, the rope — Odile's throw rope, coiled at her belt since dawn, thank God for a ferrywoman's habits — the loop she shook out with hands that belonged to someone else, the cast that unspooled across the current and settled over Gideon's flailing arm like a blessing.

She remembered the weight. God, the weight. The river pulling him and the rope pulling her and her boots skidding on wet stone, her body bent nearly double, her heels digging a trench in the shingle, every tendon in her shoulders shrieking. She remembered thinking, with absurd clarity: *This is what it costs. This is what everything costs.* She remembered her father's voice, clear as if he stood behind her: *Smooth water is a lie, little wren. Fight the lie, not the river.*

She leaned back and let her whole life be the pull.

Gideon came up coughing, hooked in the rope's loop, and together — river hauling him, Wren hauling back, the two forces canceling into something almost like mercy — they swung out of the main current and into the eddy behind the great split boulder, where the water moved in a slow, stunned circle and the roar fell away to a distant animal growl.

She dragged him onto the gravel bar and he vomited half the Verrin onto it, and then lay on his side shaking, lips gray-blue, eyes fixed on nothing.

Wren sat down hard beside him and shook too, and could not stop, and did not try.

* * *

They found the raft a quarter mile down, wrapped around a strainer of flood-dead alders, three-quarters deflated, one bladder torn open along a seam. The second oar was gone. The bail bucket floated in an eddy, patient as a dog. Of the compass, nothing — swallowed, gone, fifty miles downstream by now or lodged in some crevice of the gorge, keeping its secret company.

Gideon sat on the gravel staring at the wreck. Water dripped off his hair and his lashes and the end of his nose. He looked ten years older than he had that morning, and younger too, somehow — stripped of the charm the way a house is stripped by fire, down to the studs.

"The map," he said finally. His voice was a ruin. "Tell me the map—"

"In my jacket. Inside oilskin." She patted her chest, where the rolled tube rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "I don't trust you with it in the water."

Something moved across his face — hurt, quickly buried. "Right," he said. "Smart."

They salvaged what they could: the intact bladder, the frame timbers, the rope, a tin of matches that had miraculously stayed dry inside its sealed lid. There was no rebuilding the raft here, no portage, no trail. What remained was the thing Wren had been dreading since the narrows: they were committed. Committed to the river, committed to the gorge, committed to whatever came next, with one oar between them and a walk of nine days through country that had already tried to kill them once before lunch.

They got a fire going in the lee of the boulder, feeding it driftwood until the flames stood up tall and honest, and stripped to their underlayers, and sat steaming like a pair of plow horses. The sun found a seam in the clouds and laid a stripe of warmth across the gravel, and the gorge, having nearly murdered them, now pretended to be beautiful. The light came down gold and green. The spray off the rapids caught it and burned.

"Why?" Gideon said.

She looked at him.

"Why did you come after me." He wasn't being charming. That was the strange thing — the armor was off, and underneath it he looked exhausted and puzzled, like a man reading a bill he couldn't understand. "You've made it plain since the bridge that you think I stole from you. You could have taken the map any night this week. I sleep like a drunk. Instead you've walked every mile beside me, saved my hide from Corvus's scouts, mended my boot, taught Odile's kids my own card tricks wrong just to embarrass me. And today you put a rope on a drowning stranger in water that should have taken you instead." He turned to face her fully. "So why?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the river kept up its low argument with the mountains.

Wren fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch, and understood that the moment had arrived — the one she had been walking toward since the rail bridge, since the letter, since the day eleven years ago when her father's scow had come down the river empty and Elias Marsh had stepped off it alone and would not say where he'd been or why he never went back.

"My father chose you," she said.

Gideon went very still.

"He had a daughter. He had a daughter who could read water before she could read words, who spliced rope, who shot rapids he wouldn't run himself because he said the risk wasn't worth it with her aboard. He had me, and he taught me everything, and then one day he stopped coming home. Eleven years. Letters, sometimes — a few lines, no return address, always the same handwriting getting shakier. And then a final letter saying he was dying, and that he'd sent the only thing that mattered to a stranger. To a storm chaser who owed money to the Syndicate. To *you*." Her voice cracked and she let it. "He trusted you with his life's work. He didn't trust me with so much as the name of the river. So yes, Gideon. I need to know why. Because I have read that man's map until I can see it with my eyes shut, and there is nothing on it — nothing — that explains why a dying prospector would hand it to a stranger and leave his own blood standing on the bank."

The silence stretched. Gideon stared into the fire, and she watched him decide not to deflect, watched the charm fail to assemble itself, watched him sit there as just a tired, soaked, frightened man with someone else's inheritance in his keeping.

"I can't tell you why he chose me," he said at last. "I've asked myself the same question since the bridge. I was nobody to him. I gave him shelter from the rain and a promise, and he gave me everything he had. Maybe—" He stopped. Started again. "Maybe that was the point. Maybe he needed someone with nothing. Someone whose only reason to carry it forward was the promise itself. Somebody the Syndicate couldn't lean on through family, because he didn't have any left to lean on." He glanced at her, quick and guilty. "Or thought he didn't."

"He knew about me," Wren said. "He wrote to me. He knew."

"Then I don't know." He spread his hands, helpless, honest. "But I know this much. He talked about you. On the bridge, bleeding out, with searchlights sweeping the valley — he spent his last minutes talking about his daughter. How she could read water. How she'd hate the map's margins because he never labeled anything properly. He said—" Gideon's voice caught, and he cleared it roughly. "He said, *she'll follow it anyway. She's her father's girl. Tell her the river remembers her.*"

Wren hadn't known she was crying until the tears hit the backs of her hands, hot against the cold. She wiped her face with her sleeve, furious at herself, and the fury didn't help, and she cried a little more, and the fire cracked, and Gideon had the grace to look away and give her the shoreline of the moment to herself.

*The river remembers her.*

Eleven years she had carried the question like a stone in her chest — *why not me, why not me, why not me* — and the answer, when it finally came, was not an answer at all, only a message passed hand to hand through a dying man to a stranger to her, and somehow that was worse and better both. Her father had not chosen the stranger over her. He had chosen the stranger *because* of her — because a daughter would be hunted, because a daughter could be found, because the Syndicate erased towns and families alike and Elias Marsh had loved her too much to make her the map's keeper and too stubbornly to leave her with nothing.

It didn't forgive him. But it explained him, and explanation, Wren was discovering, was its own terrible gift.

"We're partners," she said finally. Her voice came out scraped raw and steady underneath. "Not allies of convenience. Not rivals. Partners. You carry the promise, I carry the reading. You don't make a move with that map without me, and I don't make a move without telling you. And when we reach the source—" She held his eyes across the fire. "We decide together what happens there. Both of us. Or neither."

Gideon nodded slowly. Then he reached into his ruined jacket and pulled out the compass — the brass case dented, the glass crazed, the needle swinging lazily — and set it on the gravel between them, halfway across the fire's warm circle.

"Then you'd better learn what this really does," he said. "Because it doesn't point north. It hasn't since the bridge. And this morning, when it flew out of my pocket—" He tapped the crazed glass. "Before it went in the water, the needle was spinning. Hard. Like it was trying to point at something close. Something ahead of us."

Wren picked it up. The needle steadied under her fingers, quivered, and settled — not north, not toward the summit, but bearing hard to the northeast, up a side draw where the map showed nothing at all except a single marginal note in her father's cramped, infuriating, unlabeled hand:

*A light in the mountain. Do not approach after dark.*

The sun was already going down behind the western wall.
