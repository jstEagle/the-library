## 8. Odile's Bargain

The river told us before we saw them.

Wren heard it first — she had ears like a heron, always tilted toward the water — and held up a fist without breaking stride. We crouched in the alder scrub above the bend where Callow's Ford used to be, where Odile's camp strung itself along the high bank like laundry on a line: tarps and rope bridges, the ferry landing with its blackened pilings, the cookfire smoke that smelled of pine resin and burnt fish.

But the smoke was wrong.

Too much of it. Too many fires burning at once, laid neat and deliberate in a row along the shingle, the way soldiers build fires when they want you to know they're there.

And there was a sound underneath the river-sound that didn't belong — a low diesel thrum, idling. Somewhere past the drowned church steeple, an airboat sat at anchor, its hull painted the flat gray of a storm front, its gunwale stenciled with a mark I knew better than my own heartbeat: a circle bisected by a vertical line. The Meridian Syndicate's seal. The mark that had been stamped on every bill, every notice, every threat that had followed me for six years.

"Corvus," Wren said. Not a question. She'd seen the man once, at distance, on the far side of the box canyon country, and apparently that had been enough. "He got here first."

"He always gets here first," I said. "That's the job. Arrive before hope does."

She gave me a look — the flat, measuring look she'd been giving me since the rapids, since the night she'd pulled me out of the white water by my collar and asked me why her father had chosen a stranger over his own blood. I still hadn't answered her. I wasn't sure I could. But something between us had changed in that churning water, some knot worked loose, and now when she looked at me it wasn't only suspicion. It was suspicion wearing patience like a coat.

"We could go around," she said. "Push upstream through the timber. Lose two days."

"Two days we don't have." I watched the fires. "And we need supplies. Rope, salt, lamp oil. Odile's the only game on this stretch of river, and Corvus knows it. That's why he's here. He isn't hunting us anymore — he's cutting off our supply line."

"Or he's buying the whole camp."

"Odile doesn't sell," I said. "She trades. There's a difference, and he's about to learn it."

I didn't believe that as much as I wanted to. Odile was a survivor, and survivors keep their options open the way other people keep spare keys. But I'd slept under her tarps, eaten her stew, taught her kids to read cloud faces, and I needed to believe that counted for something more than credit.

We came down through the willows single file, hands visible, moving slow. It didn't matter. Before we reached the edge of camp, a boy of maybe twelve stepped out of the reeds — Tam, the smallest of Odile's crew, quick as a water spider — and shook his head at us, eyes wide.

"They've been here since morning," he whispered. "Gray coats. Four of 'em, plus the talker. Odile sent us all to the boats. She said if anything goes wrong, take the little ones down to the fish weir and wait for the whistle."

"Where is she?"

"The landing. Talking to him." Tam's throat moved. "He brought a chest, mister Whitlock. A real strongbox. I seen him open it."

Of course he had. Corvus never traveled without the strongbox. I'd seen it once before, three years ago, in a hotel room in Meridian City, when he'd opened it on the bed and let me look at what six years of my life weighed in paper and coin. He liked people to see it. Debt was a language, and the strongbox was how he said *hello*.

* * *

We left Tam in the reeds and walked into camp the way you walk into a room where a card game has gone quiet — openly, unhurried, letting everyone get a good look at you.

The gray coats were exactly where Tam said they'd be: four of them arranged around the landing with the loose discipline of men who were bored but armed. Two by the airboat. One at the foot of the gangplank. And one standing near the cookfire, turning a spit that nobody had asked him to turn, roasting Odile's fish over Odile's fire as though ownership were a thing you could simply occupy.

Corvus stood at the end of the landing with Odile.

He hadn't changed. He never changed. A long man folded into a gray coat too fine for the river, silver at the temples, a face like a ledger page — every line ruled straight, nothing written on it that wasn't meant to be read. The strongbox sat open at his feet, and even from thirty yards I could see the gleam inside: not just coin. Paper. Promissory notes, water rights certificates, documents with ribbons and seals. He'd dressed the box for this audience. He knew what these people had lost.

"—restored by autumn," Corvus was saying, his voice carrying across the water with the smoothness of a thing rehearsed. "Not promises. Instruments. Signed, sealed, filed with the territorial office. The dam comes under new management, and the new management answers to different arithmetic than the old one. Your ferry runs free. Your children drink from a river that remembers their names."

"And all I have to do," Odile said, "is tell you where two travelers went."

Odile stood with her arms crossed and her weight on her back foot, the way she stood when she was winning. She was a broad woman built like a mooring post, gray-streaked hair bound in tarred cord, hands that had hauled a thousand ropes and would haul a thousand more. She looked at Corvus the way she looked at floodwater: without fear, without affection, with a professional assessment of how deep it was and how long until it receded.

"Not where they went," Corvus corrected gently. "Whether they passed. Whether they took supplies. Whether they mentioned where they were bound. You see how small a question it is. Small questions, small prices. Large questions cost large."

"I don't answer questions I haven't been paid to hear."

"Madame Odile." He inclined his head, and there it was — the charm, the terrible reasonable charm, the same voice that had talked me into signing my first note against my rig. "The Syndicate remembers its friends. When the water rises again, it will rise for the friends first."

"The water rose last time too," Odile said. "It rose right up over Callow's Ford and drowned eleven families while your Syndicate's engineers drank champagne on the dam. I buried neighbors in trees, Mr. Corvus. Their houses are still down there. On calm days you can see the weathervanes through the water." She uncrossed her arms. "So you'll forgive me if 'the Syndicate remembers' sounds less like a promise and more like a diagnosis."

For a moment — half a moment — something flickered behind Corvus's ruled-straight face. Not guilt. I'd have bet everything I had left that it wasn't guilt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing his own eulogy read aloud and finding it accurate.

Then he saw us.

He didn't startle. Men like Corvus don't startle; they *recalculate*. His gaze found mine across the water and settled there with something almost like warmth, the way a creditor looks at collateral walking in the door.

"Gideon Whitlock." He said my name the way you'd set a glass down carefully. "You're looking well. Prison agrees with some men; debt agrees with others."

"Corvus." I walked out onto the landing, Wren a half-step behind and slightly to my left, where she could watch his hands. "You're a long way from your office."

"My office is wherever the ledger requires." He glanced at Wren, catalogued her oilskins, her knife, the way she stood, and dismissed her — a mistake I noted with interest. "You made good time through the gorge. My compliments. Most men lose the river after the second canyon."

"Most men aren't motivated."

"No," he agreed. "Most men aren't." He stepped aside, gesturing at the strongbox with one open hand, a showman presenting the finale. "You've arrived at an interesting moment. Madame Odile and I were discussing the future. Perhaps you'd like to join us. After all—" and here his voice dropped into that register I knew from hotel rooms and collection notices, warm as banked coals, "—the future is rather more yours than hers."

* * *

They put us in the big tent — Odile's council tent, the canvas dark with age and river damp — and Corvus dismissed his coats to the perimeter and poured tea from Odile's own kettle as though hospitality were a weapon he'd mastered decades ago. Three cups. He'd known we'd come. Of course he had; the airboat hadn't been hunting us, it had been *bait*, sitting fat and loud at the drowned steeple because Corvus understood that a man running out of supplies eventually walks toward the only fire on the river.

"You burned my report," he said, settling onto a crate. "In the box canyon. My own incident report — you pulled me out of the rockfall, dug Chen out with your hands, and then you burned the documentation of the whole operation. Do you know what that did to my standing?"

"It bought you a life," Wren said. "Yours and Chen's. Seemed a fair trade."

"It bought me an audit." He sipped his tea. "Which, for a man in my position, is slower and considerably more painful. So you'll understand why I've had time to think about you, Whitlock. About what you want. About what you'd trade."

"I'm not trading."

"Everyone trades. It's the one law older than the Syndicate." He set down the cup. "Here is what I know. You have a map drawn by Elias Marsh. You are following the Verrin to its headwaters. And you believe, because Marsh believed, that there is something at the source worth dying for." He paused. "You're right. There is. That's precisely the problem."

The tent flap breathed in the wind. Outside, one of the gray coats laughed at something, and the sound was obscene against the river's murmur.

"Tell me what you think is up there," Corvus said. "Go on. Indulge me. Gold? A lost claim? Some prospector's fantasy of a mountain that bleeds silver?"

"A vent," I said, because lying to Corvus was like lying to a scale — it already knew your weight. "Geothermal. Something the Syndicate's been tapping for years and burying in paperwork."

Something moved in his face then. Not surprise — confirmation. The look of a man who has finally been dealt the card he was afraid of.

"Yes," he said quietly. "A vent. Do you know what it produces, Whitlock? Steam enough to run turbines the size of cathedrals. Power enough to light a territory. And do you know who holds the leases? Who owns the shafts, the roads, the transmission lines — all of it filed under shell companies so deep that even the directors' directors couldn't draw you the chart?" He leaned forward. "The source isn't hidden because it's worthless. It's hidden because it's *load-bearing*. Pull that thread and a hundred companies unravel. Fortunes unravel. Men very much more dangerous than I am find themselves asking very unpleasant questions of everyone who ever signed anything." He spread his hands. "Including a ferrywoman. Including a camp full of children. Including you."

"That's a threat."

"That's arithmetic." He opened the strongbox at last, tilting it toward me. Coin. Notes. And on top, a single document with a wax seal the color of dried blood. "Your ledger, Whitlock. Every entry. Six years of interest, penalties, the works — the number that's been riding your neck since the tornado took your rig. Here it is. And here—" he drew out a second paper, blank except for a signature line, "—is a release. Signed by an authorized officer. You walk away from the river today, hand over the map, and tomorrow morning you wake up owing nothing to anyone. A clean name. First one you've had since you were twenty-three."

The tent was very quiet. I could hear the river, patient and enormous, doing what it had always done regardless of who owned its paper.

And God help me, I looked at the papers. I looked at them the way a drowning man looks at a rope, and for three full seconds I was twenty-three again, signing my first note, certain I'd beat the odds because I'd always beaten the odds, because charm was a kind of luck and I'd never yet run out.

Then I thought of Tam in the reeds. Of Odile's kids learning to read clouds off my finger. Of Wren hauling me out of the white water, and the question she still hadn't gotten an answer to.

"What happens to them?" I asked. "The camp. If I walk away."

Corvus's pause was small, professional, and utterly damning.

"That," he said, "depends on the camp."

* * *

Odile found me at the woodpile an hour later, after the gray coats had withdrawn to their airboat to wait out the evening, after Corvus had left the strongbox — *a gesture of good faith*, he'd called it — sitting open in the tent like a trap left baited. I was splitting kindling because my hands needed something honest to do.

"Say what you came to say," I said, not looking up.

She picked up an axe, matched my rhythm, split wood beside me in silence for a while. The sun was going down behind the drowned town, and the flooded streets caught the light and threw it back copper, so that Callow's Ford seemed briefly to burn underwater — its rooftops, its weathervanes, its eleven drowned families all lit up like something holy.

"He offered you the same thing he offered me," she said finally. "Water rights. Legal instruments. A river that remembers their names." She spat to the side. "Pretty words. I'll give him this — he hires pretty words."

"Are you going to sell us?"

She stopped splitting. Looked at me with those mooring-post eyes, unblinking.

"Gideon Whitlock. You slept under my tarps. You taught Marius to read a shelf cloud, and that boy hasn't stopped talking about it since. You pulled my net lines at dawn without being asked, which is more than most men do who owe me nothing." She resumed splitting, harder. "No. I'm not going to sell you. But I am going to tell you something you need to hear, and you need to shut up and listen, because I've been trading on this river longer than the Syndicate's been filing leases."

I shut up.

"You think Corvus wants the source found," she said. "That's why he's chasing you, why he's offering deals, why he's spending Syndicate money floating an airboat up a dead river. You think it's a race — him versus you, first one to the prize." The axe bit deep and stuck, and she left it there, breathing hard. "It's not a race. Listen to me. The Syndicate has known where that source is for thirty years. They built the dam to hide the water levels. They drowned Callow's Ford because the town sat where their survey lines ran and the townsfolk asked too many questions. They erased Elias Marsh from every record because he *saw* it and wouldn't stop writing about it." She turned to face me fully. "They don't want the source found, boy. They want it *kept*. Kept quiet, kept buried, kept exactly as secret as the day they found it. And the only reason a man like Corvus is offering you a clean ledger—"

"—is that a clean ledger is cheaper than a body," I finished. My mouth had gone dry. "Or a search party. Or another drowned town."

"Now you're reading water." She pulled the axe free. "Whatever's up that mountain, it isn't treasure. It's a wound they've been bandaging with paperwork for thirty years, and they cannot afford to have it looked at. Not by prospectors. Not by daughters. Certainly not by charming, desperate storm-chasers who've got nothing left to lose." Her voice dropped. "Which means every mile you go upstream, you're not getting closer to a prize. You're getting closer to the thing they'll burn a river to protect. And everyone standing near you when it burns."

The copper light was fading off the drowned town. Down at the landing, the airboat's running lights came on, two green eyes in the dusk, watching the camp. Watching us.

"Odile," I said. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked me that question in the tent." She shouldered the axe. "'What happens to them.' Nobody's asked me that in eleven years, Gideon. Not one soul in a gray coat." She started toward the cookfire, then paused. "Corvus sleeps on the boat tonight. The strongbox stays in the tent. Third watch, the fog comes off the water thick enough to chew. My kids will have the raft loaded with supplies at the north landing — salt, oil, rope, and the good lamp. Don't thank me. Just don't make it a waste."

She walked away, and I stood alone at the woodpile with the strongbox glinting through the tent canvas behind me and the river running dark and endless toward the mountains.

Behind me, soft as breath, Wren spoke from the shadow of the tent pole, where she'd been standing long enough to have heard every word.

"Third watch," she said. "We go tonight. And Gideon—" her voice had an edge I hadn't heard since the rapids, sharp and bright as a fresh-ground blade, "—there's something in my father's map I've been trying to decipher. Marginalia. Symbols I couldn't make sense of." She stepped into the last of the light, and her eyes were wide with something between fear and revelation. "After what Odile just said — I think I finally understand what he wrote. And I think we've been reading this map wrong from the very beginning."
