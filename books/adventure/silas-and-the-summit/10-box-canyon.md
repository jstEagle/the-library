## 10. Box Canyon

The canyon had a name once, Wren was sure of it. Every place did, before the Syndicate got to it — before the maps went gray and the records burned and the towns drowned. But whatever the old-timers had called this slot in the earth was gone now, and so it was just the box: two walls of rust-colored stone leaning toward each other like conspirators, a floor of loose shale that shifted under every boot, and at its head, according to her father's map, nothing at all.

That was the point. That was the whole elegant, cruel point.

She crouched on the rim above it, wind pulling at her oilskin hood, and watched Corvus's team walk into the trap her father had drawn for them eleven years before he died.

*They'll take the left fork,* Elias had written in the margin, in his cramped surveyor's hand. *Men who chase always take the route that looks fastest. Give them a road and they'll believe it leads somewhere. Give them a wall and they'll start thinking.* Below that, almost as an afterthought, in ink gone brown with age: *I'm sorry to whoever has to be standing there when it happens.*

Wren had read those lines four times since dawn. She kept coming back to the apology. Her father had planned for betrayal the way other men planned for weather — not hoping it would come, only knowing it would, and leaving provisions against it. He had never once mentioned a daughter in those margins. Never wrote *if Wren comes*, or *tell my girl*. He had written *whoever*, and the whoever had turned out to be a stranger with a gambler's smile and a debt ledger, and she was still deciding how much that hurt.

Below, the Syndicate column moved up the false route in good order. Six of them, plus Corvus, roped into teams of three, their gear wrapped quiet, their boots finding the shale path Elias had drawn so carefully — a path that narrowed, and narrowed, and ended.

"They're committed," Gideon said beside her. He lay flat on his belly in the sage, binoculars he'd won off Odile in a card game pressed to his eyes. "Last bend. Another ten minutes and they're past the choke."

"Then we go the other way." She stood. "While they're buried, we move. That's the whole design, Gideon. My father didn't build this trap so we could stand around admiring it."

He didn't get up. That was the thing about him she'd learned to watch for — the stillness that came before his worst decisions, the way charm drained out of him and left something older and more stubborn underneath. "Six men," he said.

"Seven with Corvus."

"Six men who are going to have a mountain fall on them." He lowered the binoculars. In the flat morning light his face looked scraped clean of everything easy. "You knew that when you figured out the bait route. You've known for two days. You didn't say anything."

"I said the map had two routes. I didn't say I'd mourn which one they took."

"No," Gideon agreed quietly. "You didn't."

The wind came down the canyon then, carrying the sound of the column below — the clink of carabiners, a man's low laugh, the ordinary sounds of people who believed the day would end with them alive in it. Wren found she couldn't look away from the last bend, where the walls pinched together and the shale path vanished into shadow.

"My father spent thirty years documenting what they did," she said. "Towns bought and erased. Rivers dammed and renamed. Men like that one down there carried out all of it. If a few of them stop existing—"

"That's not how it works." Gideon sat up, brushing dirt from his sleeves, and for once there was no performance in him at all. "You don't get to write your father's truth with their blood in the margins. He wouldn't have wanted it. He apologized, Wren. In his own map. To whoever was standing there. He built the trap because he had to survive, and he hated it, and if you use it to kill instead of escape then the Syndicate wins twice — once today, and once forever, because now you're exactly what they say everyone outside their ledger is."

"And what's that?"

"Worth erasing."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted, worse, to agree with him, and that frightened her more. Below, the last rope team passed the choke point and disappeared into the throat of the false route, and the canyon held them the way a fist holds coins.

Then the mountain spoke.

It started as a sound Wren felt in her teeth before she heard it — a deep grinding note, tectonic, patient — and then the far wall let go. Not all at once; that was her father's craft in it too. The rockslide came in stages, shelves of sandstone peeling away in sequence, each collapse triggering the next, until the whole upper canyon was a brown roaring curtain and the air filled with dust that turned the sunlight the color of old brass. The sound went on and on, longer than any single slide should last, Elias's engineering compounding itself, and when it finally stopped the silence afterward was enormous.

Where the false route had been there was now a wall. Forty feet of it. Fifty. Fresh rock, raw-faced, seamless. No gap. No light from beyond it. No sound from anyone inside.

Wren exhaled slowly. "It's done," she said. "We move. Now, while they think we took the real route."

Gideon was already on his feet. But he wasn't looking at the wall. He was looking at the talus slope beneath it, where the dust was still sifting down through the light, and his jaw was set in the way she had learned meant he was about to do something she'd have to decide whether to forgive.

"Gideon."

"There'll be pockets." He was shouldering his pack as he talked, tightening straps with quick, practiced hands. "Rockslides don't seal clean. There's always voids, air channels, places the rubble bridges over. If even one of them is breathing—"

"They're Syndicate enforcers. They boarded your rig mid-tornado and catalogued your life like inventory. They drowned Callow's Ford. They put a bullet in my father—"

"We don't know that."

"—and you want to dig them out."

He turned to face her fully, and up close she could see he was afraid — genuinely, physically afraid, his hands not quite steady on the buckles — and that he was going anyway. "I know what kind of arithmetic you're doing," he said. "I've done it my whole life. Everything's a trade, everything's a ledger, you just carry the numbers until they bury you. I'm done carrying them. My father taught me storms. You know the first thing he ever told me? The storm doesn't care who you are. It kills the merciful and the merciless in the same order: first. So mercy isn't about what they deserve. It's about who you decide to be while the storm's here." He glanced back at the wall of fresh rock, already moving. "Come or don't. But I'm going down there."

Wren stood on the rim a moment longer, watching him descend into the dust, and understood with terrible clarity that her father had chosen this man for reasons she was only beginning to see — and that she had followed him into the canyon anyway.

* * *

The rock fought them for six hours.

Gideon worked by feel mostly, after the first hour, when the dust thickened until his own hands vanished at arm's length. They'd found the first void within twenty minutes — a hollow moan under the talus when Wren struck it with a pry bar, a pocket of compressed air breathing out through a crack no wider than a knife. Someone was alive behind it. Someone had answered when Gideon shouted, a thin, muffled voice reporting casualties in a clipped military cadence: two dead, three injured, one missing, air bad, water gone.

Three men alive in a stone throat. And Corvus among them, or not — the voice hadn't said, and Gideon hadn't asked, because it didn't matter, because he'd decided on the rim that it didn't matter, and he was holding onto that decision with both hands the way a drowning man holds a spar.

"Through here." Wren's voice came out of the murk, closer than he expected. She'd stopped arguing somewhere in the second hour — not agreed, he understood the difference, but stopped — and had thrown herself into the work with a ferocity that scared him a little. She dug like the rock owed her money. Maybe it did. "The bedding plane dips east. If we follow the seam we come at the pocket from underneath instead of through fifty feet of rubble."

"You've done this before."

"Mines, not slides. Same principle. My father made sure I could read rock before I could read words." A pause, and then, grudgingly, as if it cost her: "His margins were right. About the voids. He engineered the slide to leave air paths. He wanted survivors possible. He just didn't want them able to follow." Another pause, longer. "He really did hate this part of himself. I'm starting to understand that."

Gideon wedged the pry bar into the seam and leaned his whole weight into it. Stone ground against stone, a sound like teeth. "Your father," he said, working, "spent eleven years drawing a map designed to betray anyone who stole it, and then gave it to a stranger and asked him to burn it. You want to know why he chose someone like me? Because someone like me was the only person desperate enough to take it and decent enough to feel bad about it. He needed both."

"He needed a fool with a compass."

"He needed someone who'd go back." Gideon nodded at the wall of rock between them and the trapped men. "Think about it. He could've sent the map anywhere. He sent it downstream, into Syndicate country, to a debtor. He wasn't choosing a courier. He was choosing a test. And I keep wondering—" the bar slipped, he reset it, sweat cutting clean lines through the dust on his face "—whether I'm passing it."

They broke through at dusk.

Not dramatically — the seam simply widened enough to admit a body sideways, exhaling a breath of foul, warm air that smelled of crushed stone and blood. Gideon went first, sideways, one shoulder, then the other, into a chamber the size of a chapel with a ceiling of bridged slabs that groaned faintly when he breathed. His headlamp found them.

Three men. Two sitting propped against the wall, legs at wrong angles, faces gray with shock and powdered rock. The third lay flat, and it took Gideon a long moment to understand that the third man was digging — had been digging, bare-handed, at the base of the rubble pile, for hours, by the ruin of his fingers — and that the third man was Corvus.

He looked up into the light. For a moment neither of them spoke. Thirty years of the Syndicate looked out of that ruined face, and under it, newly cracked open, something else.

"Whitlock," Corvus said. His voice was gravel and blood. "Of course it's you. Nobody else is stupid enough."

"Two of yours didn't make it." Gideon knelt, checking the injured men with hands that remembered field triage from a hundred storm wrecks. Crushed leg, here; broken ribs, possible puncture, there; the third man concussed and shivering. "The rest can walk, barely. We've got a seam out. It's tight. Your big friend won't fit without we widen it, and widening it risks the bridge overhead."

"Then widen it slow." Corvus tried to stand and failed, and Gideon saw then that his leg was broken too, badly, the shin bowed where the rock had taken it. He'd been digging on a broken leg. Hours of it. "And Whitlock." He caught Gideon's sleeve with fingers stripped to the quick. "Before you decide what this costs. I want you to know I sent the report upstream this morning. False position, false heading. Bought you three days before they figure out I lied. Three days you wouldn't have had otherwise." He laughed, wetly, without humor. "So either you leave me here and I die having finally done one honest thing, or you pull me out and I spend the rest of my career owing a man I was sent to break. Either way the ledger balances strange. Get them out. Start with mine."

Gideon looked at him a long moment. Then he turned back to the seam and started widening it, slow, careful, stone grinding like teeth, and behind him Wren appeared in the gap with the rope coil already off her shoulder, saying nothing, doing the work, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They brought them out under the first stars.

It took four hours more — the seam widened inch by inch, the bridge overhead groaning its warnings, Wren propping the dangerous slabs with pitons and prayer while Gideon ferried men through one at a time, the injured half-carried, half-dragged, Corvus last, lashed to a makeshift litter of pack frames, biting down on a strap while they hauled him through the pinch. When they finally laid him on open ground under the open sky, he stared straight up at the stars for a long time, breathing, as if confirming they were still there.

The surviving enforcers huddled together ten yards off, watching their rescuers with the wary incomprehension of dogs that had been kicked their whole lives and been fed anyway. One of them, the young one, kept opening his mouth to say something and closing it again.

Corvus broke the silence first. "You should have left me."

"Yeah," Gideon said, sitting down heavily across from him. He was wringing out his hands; the work had opened blisters he hadn't noticed. "People keep telling me that lately. Wren, mostly. The river, occasionally."

"The Syndicate doesn't forgive debts, Whitlock. It collects them. What you did today — you think it makes us square?" Corvus shook his head slowly against the litter. "It makes it worse. Now I owe you. And the Syndicate knows I sent a false report. By tomorrow night there'll be a retrieval team with my name on it, and yours behind it, and they'll want us both in the same room so the accounting can be tidy." He turned his head, and in the firelight his eyes were clear and terribly calm. "So listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once. Three days. That's what I bought you, and I've maybe spent one. The director is already on the river — he doesn't wait for reports, he follows the money, and the money says the source gets tapped this season or the whole upstream operation goes dark. He'll be at the summit lake before you are. You can't outrun him. You can't outfight him." He paused. "But I've served him for thirty years, and I know what he's afraid of."

Wren crouched at the edge of the firelight, arms crossed, dust still ghosting her hair. "Which is?"

Corvus smiled, and it was the first expression Gideon had ever seen on him that wasn't armor. It looked almost like relief.

"Being witnessed," he said. "Your father understood that. It's why they killed him. And it's why, if you get to that lake before my replacement finds me—" he lifted his ruined hand, palm up, an offering or a surrender, Gideon couldn't tell which "—you take me with you. Not as your prisoner. As your witness. Thirty years of ledgers, routes, names, dates. Everything I collected for them, I can testify against them. But only if I live long enough to reach the summit." His hand dropped. "So. Chaser. Do we have a trade?"

Gideon looked at Wren. Wren looked at the sky, jaw tight, weighing her father's grave against a living archive of everything that had killed him.

Above the canyon rim, far to the north, thunder rolled — dry, distant, the season's first real storm clearing its throat. Gideon felt it in his chest the way he always did, the old pull, the old hunger. But this time it sounded less like a warning than a schedule.

"Three days," he said. "Then we climb."
