## 11. High Water

The gorge told us before the sky did.

I'd been listening to it for two days — that low, swallowed growl beneath the river's voice, like something turning over in its sleep — but on the third morning the sound changed key. Wren heard it too. She stopped mid-stride on the shale shelf above the water and tilted her head the way she did when her father's map was speaking to her, and I knew we had maybe hours, not days.

"It's coming down," she said.

Not a question. The spring melt had been building in the high country for a week, feeding off snowfields we couldn't see from the canyon floor, and now all of it was looking for a way down. The Verrin ran brown-green and swollen between its banks, carrying whole pine trees end over end like matchsticks, and where yesterday there'd been gravel shoals there was only moving water, deep and muscular and utterly indifferent to anything with a pulse.

We were halfway up the gorge wall when the first surge came through. It didn't look like water. It looked like a decision — the river simply rising, a hand lifting, swallowing the shelf we'd camped on not six hours earlier. Our fire ring went under without a hiss. My pack, which I'd stupidly left at the low edge of camp while I scouted ahead, went with it. I watched forty pounds of gear — rope, rations, my spare shirt, Wren's oilskin she'd lent me back at Callow's Ford — turn once in an eddy and vanish downstream.

"Well," Wren said beside me. "That's one way to travel light."

She didn't laugh. Neither did I. But something in the flatness of her voice kept me from panicking, and I understood then that this was what she did — she met catastrophe with bookkeeping. Her father's daughter down to the marrow.

The problem was simple and ugly: the melt had cut off the upstream route along the bank, and climbing out of the gorge meant days over the rimrock with no water and no cover, straight through country where Syndicate patrols still swept. Corvus's false report had bought us three days. We'd spent two getting here. The river itself was the only road left, and the river was trying to kill us.

That was when we heard the bell.

* * *

It came up the gorge thin and bright over the roar — a handbell, struck three times, a rhythm I recognized before I could name it. Odile's signal. Three rings meant *come to me*. Four meant *stay away*. Five meant someone had died. The kids at Callow's Ford had taught me the code one night around their cookfire, giggling, making me repeat it until I got it right, and I hadn't thought about it since. Now it came floating up through the flood-haze like a rope thrown across dark water.

We found them wedged into a side-canyon eddy half a mile down: Odile's raft, the big one, the *Maren-Louise*, lashed behind a spur of rock where the current curled back on itself. She stood at the stern with the bell in one fist and a boat hook in the other, gray braid plastered to her neck, looking exactly as unimpressed by the apocalypse as she'd been by Corvus.

"You," she called up, "are the luckiest fools on this river."

"Odile." I slid down the last few feet of rock. "How—"

"I read water, boy. Same as you. Water said *flood*, so I brought the raft up-gorge before the flood said *no*." She jerked her chin at the crew scrambling on deck — six of the Ford kids, older ones mostly, Pip and Marisol and the twins, faces I'd eaten fish stew with, played cards with, taught knots to. They waved like this was a holiday outing. "Get aboard or get dead. Those are the options."

Wren hesitated at the gunwale. I watched her do the arithmetic — the raft meant Odile, and Odile meant ties to Callow's Ford, and ties meant people who could be hurt by the choices we were dragging behind us like a wake. I'd done the same math a hundred times in my life and always arrived at the same answer: travel alone, owe nothing, endanger no one because you love no one.

Wren stepped aboard.

"Your father ferried with me two seasons," Odile said quietly, taking her elbow. "He'd have my hide if I let the river have you."

* * *

The Maren-Louise was built for water like this, though you wouldn't know it to look at her. Forty feet of lashed timber and tarred canvas, shallow-drafted, steered by two great sweep oars fore and aft, riding the flood the way a hawk rides wind — not fighting it, reading it. Odile handled her like an extension of her own arms. The kids worked the lines barefoot, calling depths and hazards to each other in a shorthand I only half followed, and the raft slipped down the drowned gorge at a speed that should have terrified me and instead felt like flight.

I stood at the bow for most of the first day, useless and aware of it, watching the water. That was the thing about high water — it wasn't chaos, whatever it looked like. It had grammar. Eddies behind every obstruction, seams where currents met, boils marking sunken rock. A flooded river was just a fast one with more to say. Once you stopped flinching, you could hear sentences in it.

Pip found me there near dusk. He was fourteen now, maybe fifteen — hard to tell with river kids, who aged in weather rather than years — and he planted himself at the rail beside me with the studied casualness of someone who has rehearsed a question.

"Gideon. When the water does that thing—" he pointed at a place where the surface dimpled in a long line ahead, "—the wrinkly part. Marisol says it's rocks. I say it's where two currents push. Who's right?"

"Both of you. Show me."

He pointed again. I made him describe it — the direction of the wrinkle, how it moved, whether debris tracked along it or piled against it. He got most of it right, and where he got it wrong, I didn't correct him so much as ask another question until he corrected himself. That was how Odile taught, I realized. That was how the whole crew taught. Nobody handed you answers; they handed you better questions.

"That seam," I said finally, "means the fast water and the slow water are shaking hands. Debris rides the seam. So if you ever fall in, you swim for the wrinkle, because that's where the boats will be looking."

He nodded solemnly, absorbing it, and then: "Teach me the storm-reading. The cloud thing. Odile says you can smell a twister two hours out."

"Odile exaggerates."

"Odile doesn't exaggerate," Pip said with the certainty of scripture. "She's Odile."

So I taught him. There wasn't much else to do, pinned to a raft in a flooded gorge with night coming on, and the truth was I wanted to. I showed him how green in the sky means the storm is eating light and getting serious; how a sudden dead calm after wind isn't mercy, it's the storm reloading; how hail means the updraft is strong enough to suspend stone, and a storm that can hold up stones can hold up a lot worse. He listened the way I used to listen — hungrily, like weather was a language the world spoke only to people willing to stand outside in it.

By full dark, half the crew had drifted over, and I was standing in the lamplight at the stern doing what I'd sworn off doing for years: being useful to people who might remember me.

* * *

The cookfire burned in a sand-filled iron tray amidships, because Odile would no sooner run a cold galley than run aground. Nights on the raft became the part of the day I stopped pretending I didn't live for.

There's a particular quality to firelight on open water — the flames lean with the current, shadows slide across the deck like fish, and everyone's face goes soft and honest at the edges. The kids took turns at the oars in pairs, and the off-watch gathered at the fire to eat Odile's river stew — catfish, cattail roots, wild onion, whatever the flood served up — and argue and sing and tell stories with the volume turned all the way up.

Marisol could imitate anyone, including Odile, including me, including Corvus, whose memory she performed with such venomous accuracy that even Wren laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she'd startled herself with it. The twins harmonized on old ferry songs with verses that were clearly invented and clearly obscene, and Odile pretended not to hear and tapped her foot anyway.

And Wren sat at the edge of the light with her father's map wrapped in oilcloth on her knees, and slowly, night by night, moved closer in.

The fourth night, she asked me about storms. Not the reading of them — the chasing of them. Why a man would drive toward the thing everyone else flees.

I gave her the easy answer first, the one I'd given collectors and barkeeps and women I never called again: the money, the data, the Syndicate contracts. She waited. She was good at waiting. And somewhere past midnight, with the fire down to coals and the gorge walls sliding black against the stars, I gave her the true one.

"When I was nine, a tornado took our house in eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Everything my family owned, gone faster than a breath. And I remember standing in the field afterward thinking — it came *here*. To *here*. Out of all the places in the world, it chose this square of dirt, and nobody knew, nobody could've known, and that felt like the cruelest thing I'd ever heard." I turned my cup in my hands. "Chasing is just spending your whole life trying to make sure it never surprises anybody again. You can't stop it. But you can see it coming. You can shout the warning. That's the whole religion, Wren. That's all of it."

She was quiet a long time. The river talked underneath us.

"Elias used to say the mountain speaks first," she said at last. "That everything dangerous announces itself, if you've learned its language. He learned the Syndicate's language. That's why they killed him." Her jaw tightened. "Why they'll kill us, if we're slow."

"Not if we're fast," Pip said sleepily from the deck, where he'd been pretending to be asleep for the last hour. "Gideon says fast beats strong. He said it about the seam."

"He did, did he?" Wren said.

"Go to sleep, spy-child," Odile called from the tiller.

But she was smiling when she said it, and so was I, and I caught myself sitting there in the warm circle of the firelight thinking: *this*. This is the thing I sold pieces of myself to avoid needing. Belonging. The terrible, wonderful weight of people who expect you to show up tomorrow.

* * *

On the fifth night I noticed what I hadn't been doing.

We tied up in a hollow of the bank below a leaning cottonwood, and Odile posted watches, and I lay in my bedroll under the tarp listening to the flood go by, and I realized I didn't know where my exits were.

That sounds like nothing. It wasn't nothing. For eleven years — since the Syndicate bought my debt, since every job and every town and every friendship came with a mental floor plan titled *ways out* — I had gone to sleep cataloguing doors. North road, two miles, passable. Raft hidden in the willows. Cash sewn in the coat lining. It was as automatic as breathing and twice as necessary, because men who owe the Meridian Syndicate don't get surprised; surprise is how they collect.

And tonight, for the first time since I signed my name in their ledger, my mind lay quiet. No exits. No floor plans. Just the river, the fire's dying tick, Marisol snoring like a small sawmill, and the absurd, terrifying peace of a man who has stopped calculating.

I should have been frightened. Some part of me was — the old part, the part that knew attachment was collateral and collateral was leverage. But the larger part of me, the part that had spent five nights teaching children to read water and watching Wren laugh at Marisol's impressions, just lay there and let the quiet happen.

*You're becoming a man with something to lose,* the old part whispered.

*I know,* I told it. *Finally.*

* * *

The dream came anyway, the way debts do. In it I stood in Elias Marsh's blood-soaked shelter under the rail bridge, and the old man pressed the map into my hands and said, *tell no one, burn it after* — and then his face changed, stretched, became Corvus's, became the director's, became mine. *Tell no one,* my own mouth said. *Burn it.* And behind me, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a handbell ringing five times.

I woke before dawn with my heart going like a paddle wheel. The gorge was gray and silent except for the water. I climbed to the stern where Odile stood her morning watch, alone with the river, and stood beside her without speaking for a while.

"You slept," she observed.

"Barely."

"You slept *here*," she said, meaning the raft, meaning among us, meaning something larger. "First time since the Ford, I'd wager. You've been sleeping like a man in a card game — one eye open, chair tipped against the door." She adjusted a sweep oar with two fingers, effortless. "River's dropping a foot already. Melt's peaked. Two days, maybe three, and we're past the gorge narrows. After that—" she glanced at me sideways, "—after that, the water stops deciding things, and people do. So. What's the plan, chaser? Where does the Maren-Louise deliver you?"

I opened my mouth to give her the answer I'd been carrying — the safe one, the vague one, *upstream, that's all you need to know* — and found I couldn't. Not to her. Not anymore.

"The source of the Verrin," I said. "What Elias Marsh found. What the Syndicate's been hiding in these mountains for thirty years."

Odile didn't startle. She was too old and too river-made for startling. But her hands went still on the oar, and the silence between us filled up with the sound of the flood.

"I know what they're hiding," she said finally. "I've known since they drowned Callow's Ford and called it a dam project. You don't flood four towns to water cattle, boy." She looked up the gorge, toward the white shoulders of the peaks showing above the canyon rim, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Question isn't whether I'll take you. Question is whether you understand what you're asking. Because if you carry that map to the source, you're not running from the Syndicate anymore. You're running *at* them. And everything on this raft — every child at that fire — becomes part of it."

I looked back at the deck. Pip was awake, pretending not to be, watching us. Marisol's head lifted. Wren stood at the rail in the gray light, and I knew from her face she'd heard every word, and I knew from her eyes that she wasn't going to let me decide this alone — and that she wasn't going to let me decide it wrong.

Behind us, far downstream, thin over the water, a sound reached us that none of us mistook for the river.

An engine. More than one. Coming up the gorge against the current, fast, and closing.

Odile rang the bell four times.
