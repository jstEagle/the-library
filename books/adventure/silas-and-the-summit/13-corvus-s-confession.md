## 13. Corvus's Confession

The canyon kept its own hours.

Corvus had learned that much in thirty years of working the Syndicate's long shadows — that every place a man could be sent to do ugly work had its own clock, indifferent to the one ticking in his breast pocket or the one ticking in the ledgers back at Meridian House. This canyon's hour was dusk, when the rock walls went the color of old rust and the wind dropped so completely that the silence had texture to it, a felt weight against the ears, like wool pressed over the world.

He sat on a flat stone near the mouth of the box canyon with his boots off and his left ankle wrapped in a strip of shirt, because the chaser — Whitlock, Gideon Whitlock, a name he'd had filed under *delinquent, nonviolent, low priority* for eleven months without once thinking he'd know the taste of it — had insisted the swelling needed wrapping before they walked out, and Corvus had been too tired, too grateful, and too ashamed to argue.

Behind him, sealed by forty tons of fallen rock, lay the bait route. Elias Marsh's last joke. Three of his team were still under there.

He did not let himself think about them yet. That was the discipline. Thirty years of it: you did not think about the men under the rock until the report was filed, until the names were entered into the appropriate columns, until the machinery had finished digesting them and moved on to the next town, the next river, the next ledger line. You thought about them later, if you thought about them at all, usually at night, usually in rooms with good locks, and you told yourself that this was what service meant — that someone had to be the hand, because if not him it would be someone worse, someone who enjoyed it, someone who wouldn't sit afterward with his boots off feeling the cold come up through the stone like a question.

*Someone worse.* It was the oldest sentence in his vocabulary. He'd built thirty years on it the way a man builds a house on a cliff edge, telling himself every morning that the view was worth it.

The wind came back for a moment, carrying the smell of wet granite and pine resin and, faintly, from far downstream, woodsmoke. Odile's camp. Or what was left of it after Corvus had passed through playing the Syndicate's silver tongue, buying loyalty with promises of water rights the Syndicate had never intended to honor. He'd made those promises the way he made all of them — fluently, warmly, with the particular sincerity of a man who has said the same lie so many times it has worn smooth as river stone.

And then Whitlock had pulled him out from under the rockslide anyway.

Corvus turned his ankle wrap tighter and looked at his hands. They were good hands, still. Steady. A field agent's hands, scarred across the knuckles from work he didn't discuss in polite company, capable now of holding a pen, a radio key, a cup of coffee, a drowning man's wrist. All four, in the space of two days. He almost laughed.

* * *

The radio was a Meridian field unit, compact as a hymnal, powered by a hand crank he'd wound for six minutes while the light died. It sat on the stone beside him like a small black god, patient, waiting to be spoken to.

Upstream protocol was simple. Daily position reports, encrypted, keyed to the regional relay on Mount Halden. His last true report had gone out the morning before the rockslide: *Unit Corvus, grid seven-echo, target acquired visually, closing.* After that — the box canyon, the roar, the dust that turned noon to twilight, and then the impossible sound of a man digging.

Not a crew. Not equipment. One man, with a crowbar and his bare hands and a fury that seemed to have nothing to do with debt collection and everything to do with something older and simpler, some law Whitlock carried around inside him the way other men carried a knife.

Corvus had been conscious through all of it. Pinned at the ankle, ribs singing, listening to the rock shift and groan above him, and through the dust he'd watched Gideon Whitlock — delinquent, nonviolent, low priority — wedge himself into a gap no sane man would enter and haul stones aside like a man arguing with God. And when the gap finally opened wide enough, Whitlock hadn't asked about the map. Hadn't asked where the rest of the team was, whether they'd copied anything, whether Corvus had transmitted their position. He'd just reached in with both hands and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"I've got you. Don't make me have done this for nothing."

*Don't make me have done this for nothing.*

Corvus had served under directors who spoke of assets and exposure and acceptable loss. He had written reports in which entire towns were described as *mitigated*. He had watched Callow's Ford drown on schedule and filed the paperwork with his lunch still warm on his desk. He knew exactly what kind of man he was. He had never, in thirty years, heard anyone say *I've got you* in quite that voice — the voice of a man who owed the world nothing and was paying it anyway.

That was the confession, if he was honest, and alone in the canyon with the dark coming down, honesty was the only company left to him. Not that he pitied Whitlock. Not that he'd suddenly grown a conscience like a new tooth. It was smaller than that and larger: the chaser had pulled him out, and now Corvus owed him, and debt — real debt, the kind measured in breath rather than currency — was the one thing the Syndicate had never taught him how to carry lightly.

They taught you how to collect it. Never how to owe it.

* * *

He thought about the first town he'd helped erase.

It had a name he could still say without flinching, which was itself a kind of indictment: Harrow Bend. A river town, smaller than Callow's Ford, forty-one households, a church with a bell nobody had ever gotten around to naming. The Syndicate wanted the bend for a reservoir that was ultimately never built — the project was cancelled two years later, the valley flooded for nothing, the maps redrawn. Corvus had been twenty-six. His job had been reassessment: walking door to door with a clipboard, cataloguing what each family owned against what the Syndicate would pay, which was to say, cataloguing everything twice and paying once.

An old woman had given him tea. He could still taste it — bitter, oversteeped, served in a cup with a chip on the rim that she turned toward herself so he'd get the smooth side. She'd told him about her husband, dead ten years, who'd built the porch they were sitting on board by board. She'd talked the way people talk when they don't yet know they're already gone, and Corvus had written *porch, cedar, fair condition* on his clipboard, and drunk her tea, and thanked her, and walked out, and the water came in the spring.

For years he'd told himself the tea story meant he was human. Look, he'd say to himself at 3 a.m., I remember the cup. I remember the chip. A monster wouldn't remember the chip.

But monsters remember plenty. That was the trick of it. Monsters remembered everything except how to stop.

Harrow Bend wasn't on maps anymore. Neither were eleven other places he could name, and three he couldn't, because those had been erased so thoroughly that even his own reports referred to them only by grid coordinates. The Syndicate didn't burn towns. Burning left ash, and ash left witnesses, and witnesses left stories. The Syndicate drowned them instead — quietly, legally, with compensation schedules and right-of-way easements — and then it drowned the memory, buying up the photographs, pensioning off the historians, letting the flood do what floods do best, which is make the past seem like weather rather than decision.

Thirty years. He ran the arithmetic the way he ran all arithmetic, automatically, and arrived at the figure he always arrived at and always refused to look at directly: somewhere north of nine thousand people, un-housed, un-mapped, unremembered, by his hand or under his signature or within his professional silence.

Nine thousand. And one chaser with bleeding hands, saying *I've got you.*

* * *

The crank radio waited.

Three days. That was what the geometry of the gorge allowed — three days between here and the summit lake for a party moving fast and light, longer if they took the raft, and the Syndicate's intercept crews, staging out of the Halden relay, would need his coordinates to close the net. Without Corvus's reports, the director would assume the worst and deploy blind, scattering teams across a hundred square miles of canyon country like seed. With a false report — *target lost, rockslide damage to tracking equipment, requesting aerial survey, grid seven-echo confirmed empty* — the net would close on empty air, and by the time anyone understood, Whitlock would be at the lake, and whatever happened at the lake would happen without an audience.

Three days. He turned the number over the way he'd once turned silver coins across his knuckles, back when he was young enough to think dexterity was a virtue.

And either way, it ended him. He saw that clearly, and the clarity was almost restful. If the deception held and Whitlock destroyed or escaped with the source, the audit trail would eventually find its way back to the last man who'd had eyes on the target — and Meridian's internal affairs did not ask questions so much as they removed the people who might have answered them. If the deception failed, if Whitlock was taken anyway and the timeline examined, same ending, faster. There was no version of tomorrow morning in which Silas Corvus — yes, Silas; his mother had named him for a riverman she'd loved and never married, a fact he had spent thirty years burying deeper than any town — remained employed by the Meridian Syndicate.

Either way, the career was over. The only choice left to him was what the career would have meant.

He thought about Wren Marsh, then. The daughter. He'd read the file on Elias Marsh a hundred times — prospector, surveyor, inconveniently thorough, flagged for surveillance in '09 — but the file hadn't mentioned a daughter, which meant either the file was incomplete or the girl had been careful, and Corvus suspected both. He'd seen her at the rockslide, at the edge of the dust, watching Whitlock dig with an expression he recognized because he'd worn it himself for thirty years: the expression of someone recalculating everything they believed about another person in real time.

She'd get her answers at the summit. They all would. That was the strange mercy of endings — they told you what everything had been for.

* * *

Full dark now. The stars came out in the hard, rinsed way they only appeared deep in canyon country, away from towns, away from the light pollution of the Syndicate's works — and Corvus noted, with a tired irony he didn't bother to suppress, that the sky was clearer here than anywhere he'd been in years, precisely because the Syndicate had emptied the land of everyone who might have lit it.

He set the radio on his knee. Wound the crank one last time. Keyed the encryptor and watched the small amber lamp wake.

There was a protocol phrase for this situation. He'd helped write it, years ago, in a committee room in Meridian House with walnut paneling and air that tasted of money: *Target location compromised beyond recovery. Recommend standing down active pursuit pending aerial reassessment.* Clean language. Language designed to make absence sound like diligence.

He tapped it out slowly, letter by letter, and found that his hands — the good hands, the steady hands — shook anyway. Not from fear. From the strangeness of it, the vertigo of a man who has spent three decades being the instrument suddenly choosing, freely, at the end, what note to play.

*Recommend standing down active pursuit.*

Then, because the encryption would hide it and because some things needed saying even into a machine that would never understand them, he added a second transmission, off-protocol, plain-keyed, addressed to no file:

*To the chaser. Debt acknowledged. Consider us even at the lake. Watch the water level below the dam — Marsh knew what he was doing, and so, I think, does the ferrywoman. — C.*

He sent it upstream to the relay knowing the odds it would ever reach Whitlock were poor, and knowing he'd sent it anyway, and understanding that this — the sending regardless — was the whole difference between the man he had been and whoever he was becoming on this cold stone with his boots off.

The lamp died. The canyon took the silence back.

Corvus sat a while longer, listening to the river move in the dark below — the Verrin, patient, older than every ledger, carrying its cold cargo of snowmelt toward the drowned towns and the living ones alike, asking nothing, forgetting nothing. Somewhere downstream, a cookfire. Somewhere upstream, a lake that hummed. Somewhere behind him, under forty tons of rock, three men whose names he would write tonight in his own private book, the one no auditor would ever see, the one that began with Harrow Bend and ended, if he had any courage left, here.

He put his boots back on. Wrapped the ankle tight. Stood, testing his weight, and began the long walk down-canyon — not toward the relay, not toward Meridian House, but toward the smoke, toward the ferrywoman's fire, toward whatever was left for a man with no side and one debt.

Behind him, the false report climbed the mountain at the speed of light, buying three days.

Ahead of him, the river ran dark and quick, as if it already knew how few of them there were left to spend.
