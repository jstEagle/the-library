## 14. The Staircase

The river died at noon.

Gideon felt it before he saw it — the current thinning under the raft's hull like a pulse going faint, the water going glassy and strange, carrying less and less of the mountain's cold authority. He shipped the oars and let them drift, listening. Upstream, where the gorge should have roared, there was only a trickle talking to itself over stones.

"That's not right," Wren said. She stood at the prow, one hand on the painter line, squinting into the narrowing defile. "A river this size doesn't just — stop."

"It doesn't stop." Gideon pointed. "It goes under."

Ahead the canyon pinched to a throat no wider than a wagon road, and out of that throat rose the rock itself — sheer, black-banded, streaked with mineral stain the colors of rust and old blood. And cut into it, half-swallowed by talus and fifty years of alder scrub, was the mouth of a mine.

Not a hole in the hillside. A doorway. Timbered, squared, deliberate, with iron rings driven into the rock above the lintel where a hoist cable had once run. Someone had built this to be entered, and then someone else had tried to make the mountain forget it.

Wren was over the side before the raft finished bumping stone, wading to the bank with her pack already shouldered. Gideon followed more slowly, and Odile's youngest, a wiry boy named Finch who had refused at every fork to turn back, scrambled ashore beside him, breathless with the particular joy of children entering places they've been told are dangerous.

"Wait here with the raft," Gideon told him.

"You said the raft's safe."

"It is. That's why you're staying with it."

Finch's face did the arithmetic of mutiny and lost, and Gideon turned to the mine mouth with the compass in his palm. The needle didn't waver. It lay flat and certain, pointing into the dark as though the dark were a place on a map, which, he supposed, it now was.

The air inside was cold and mineral, tasting of iron and something older — sulfur, faintly, like a struck match remembered rather than lit. Their headlamps carved cones out of the black. The adit ran straight for thirty paces and then the floor began to climb, rough-hewn steps cut into the bedrock, each one worn slick in the center by boots long gone to dust.

"His boots," Wren said quietly. "Some of them, anyway."

"You can know that?"

She crouched, ran her thumb along a step's worn hollow. "He had a stride like a stork. Forty inches, always. I used to have to run to keep up." She stood. "These steps are cut long."

They climbed.

The staircase went up in switchbacks through the mountain's own architecture — through veins of quartz that caught their lamplight and threw it back in cold fire, through galleries where the timbering had held for decades and galleries where it hadn't, the collapsed rock spilled across the way like a frozen avalanche they had to chimney over, packs scraping, breath loud in the closed dark. Twice Gideon stopped and put his ear to the rock and heard, beneath everything, a sound he couldn't name. Not water. Not wind. A hum, deep and steady, felt more in the teeth than the ears, as if the mountain were a struck bell that no one had ever allowed to finish ringing.

"You hear that?" he asked.

Wren's lamp swung to him. "I've heard it since the gorge. I thought it was my blood."

"It's not your blood."

They climbed another hour, and the steps ended at a landing where the tunnel opened into a chamber, and the chamber stopped Gideon's heart for one full, unprofessional beat.

It was a room. Someone had made a *room* of it, down here in the dark — a stove, its pipe run up through a shaft of daylight that fell from a ventilation raise somewhere far above; a cot with a bedroll still rolled; shelves built from dynamite crates, loaded with tinned food gone round with rust, with candles, with coils of rope, with tools hung on pegs in the exact order of a man who believed a place for everything was the difference between living and dying. And covering one entire wall, pinned and pasted and strung on wire like laundry, were maps.

Dozens of them. The Verrin's whole watershed, drawn and redrawn across what had to be years — the river in every season, the tributaries, the elevations annotated in a cramped, patient hand. Elias Marsh's hand. Wren's hand, Gideon realized, was her father's hand, and watched her understand it too, watched her go very still in the lamplight with her fingers half-raised toward the wall as if the maps might startle.

And beside the maps, on a long table under the shaft of daylight, sat the journals.

There were nineteen of them. Gideon counted twice because the number felt like a lie. Oilcloth-bound, dated in sequence across eleven years, their spines cracked and salt-stained and swollen with damp. The topmost one lay open, face down, as though its author had stepped out to check a gauge and meant to come back.

Wren didn't touch them at first. She stood in the middle of the room with the light shaking slightly, and Gideon, who had spent his whole adult life knowing exactly what to say to people and exactly when to say nothing, said nothing.

"He lived here," she finally said. "Eleven years. He wrote to me twice in eleven years."

"Maybe the writing was the hard part."

"Maybe the writing was the *coward* part." But there was no force in it. She pulled out the topmost journal at last, and opened it, and the daylight from the vent fell across the page, and Gideon watched his ally, his rival, his uneasy other-half-of-this-map read her father's last year in silence.

He gave her that and turned to the wall of maps instead. And it was there, cross-referencing the compass against Elias's annotations, that the shape of the thing began to come clear.

Wren's POV

Her father's handwriting changed in the last journal.

For ten years — she'd pulled volumes at random, middle years, the ones where the ink was brown and confident — he wrote like a surveyor. Elevations. Flow rates. Ore samples. The dry liturgy of a man in love with the earth's arithmetic. She read a page from the sixth year and heard his voice so clearly she had to set the book down: *Quartz diorite, fault-gouge along the eastern contact. Water at 40 feet. Good country. Miss you, bird. Tell you about the wrens when I see you.* Her nickname, folded into the rock report like a flower pressed in a ledger.

Then the last journal, dated three winters back, and the handwriting had gone small. Hurried. The surveyor's calm had cracked, and underneath it was someone else — someone frightened, and furious, and writing fast because he believed the mountain was listening.

*They came up-valley in June. Not miners. Suits and hard hats and instruments I don't have names for. I watched them from the high shelf for nine days. They aren't looking for gold. There's no gold. There never needed to be.*

She read the next pages twice, three times, her lips moving though she made no sound, and the hum she'd felt in the stairwell — the hum in her teeth, in her sternum, in the soles of her boots — gathered meaning around itself like a stone gathering moss.

*The vent runs hotter than anything in the Range. The lake sits on it like a lid on a pot. I measured 214 at the shoreline in February, air thirty below. The Syndicate found it in the war years and they never left. They built the dam on the lower river not for power and not for water — for cover. Flooded Callow's Ford so no one would wonder why the road went up-valley. Flooded a town to hide a road. I watched them do it. I drank with those people. I did nothing.*

*The cables run down the north shaft. Miles of them. Whatever they're feeding, it eats like a city. I've seen the trains come up at night with no lights. I've counted the men. I've drawn what I could from the high shelf. If I go down there I don't come back, and if I send this down I might not come back either, and the bird deserves a father more than the world deserves the truth, and I have been a coward about which of those I believe for eleven years.*

The page blurred. Wren pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and made herself stop shaking, because Gideon was watching, and because the shaking was grief and the grief could wait — the grief had waited eleven years, it could wait until they were down off this mountain — and what could not wait was the rest.

She read on. Her father had documented everything. Gauge readings, cable counts, the rotation of the night trains, sketches of the intake structures at the caldera's rim, a whole clandestine census of the Syndicate's secret, kept in pencil by a man who trusted paper the way other men trusted God. And on the final written page, dated four days before the letter that had taken eleven years and a dying stranger to reach her, one last entry:

*The compass was Silas's idea, God rest him. A needle that wants the vent, not the pole — the magnetite in the deep rock pulls it true. He said: if anything happens, the map is bait and the compass is the truth. Let them chase the paper. The paper burns. The truth doesn't.*

Wren looked up. Gideon had the compass out, turning it in the lamplight, and when he caught her eye he didn't smile. He turned the instrument over in his hands, and she watched him understand, the way she had just understood, that they had been carrying the real thing all along — that her father had hidden the exposé inside a trinket and handed the world a treasure map instead, because treasure maps get chased and evidence gets burned.

"He planned for them," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "Every step. The bait route in the canyon. The map. He planned for *them*. Eleven years of planning for them." She closed the journal. "Did he plan for me? Did he plan for the daughter he wrote to twice?"

Gideon set the compass down on the table, gently, between the journals and the daylight, like something returned to its owner. "Wren."

"Don't. Don't do the kind voice. I've watched you do the kind voice to Odile's kids and to Corvus, and I know what it's for, it's for getting people to do what you want, and I don't want it."

"Fine." He leaned against the table, arms folded, and looked at her with the charm stripped off, and what was underneath was tired and honest. "Then here's the plain version. Your father sent the map out because he knew he was dying and he knew the Syndicate would kill for what he'd written down, and he gave it to a stranger with a price on his head because a stranger was the only kind of man desperate enough to run it up a mountain and too broke to sell it. That's not a plan for you. That's a plan *against* everyone, including you, and it was a cold thing to do, and I'm not going to tell you it was love, because maybe it was and maybe it was cowardice wearing love's coat, and only he knew which." He paused. "But he wrote *tell you about the wrens when I see you* in the margin of a rock report in his sixth year alone in a hole in a mountain. So whatever else he was, he was yours the whole time. He was just too much of a coward to come be yours out loud."

The chamber hummed. Somewhere above, through the ventilation raise, a thread of daylight moved as the sun crossed the shaft, and the maps on the wall brightened and dimmed like something breathing.

Wren opened the last journal again. "Then we finish it," she said. "He documented it. We *deliver* it. Copies to every river town from here to the sea, faster than the Syndicate can burn them. Odile's crew. The ferry lines. Every kid who ever lost a town to that dam." She tapped the page. "He was one man with a pencil. We're a river."

Gideon was quiet a moment. She watched him do the thing he did — the calculating look, the exits, the odds — and then watched him stop doing it, which she had learned was the rarest thing he did.

"There's a problem," he said. "The north shaft. The cables. If the Syndicate's tapped the vent, they'll have a station at the caldera, and stations have radios, and radios have men." He glanced toward the tunnel mouth and the stairwell beyond, and his voice dropped. "And we've been climbing their mountain all day."

It was Finch's whistle, echoing down the ventilation raise from the raft below, that answered him — three short blasts, the river kids' signal, drilled into them on Odile's raft until it was reflex.

Trouble on the water.

Gideon was already moving, compass into his coat, journal into Wren's pack, lamp beam swinging toward the stairwell. "That'll be Corvus," he said, "or it'll be worse," and the hum in the rock rose around them, patient and enormous, like the mountain clearing its throat.
