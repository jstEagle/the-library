## 15. What Elias Knew

The journals were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was wrapped in grief.

Wren had known that before she touched them — had felt it in the way Gideon handed the bundle over without a word, the way his hands lingered a half-second too long on the knot, as if he understood that what he was passing to her was not paper but her father's spine, extracted and bound. They sat in Elias's old camp above the third shaft, in the room where he had lived out whatever years remained to him after he'd stopped being her father and started being something else: a man alone with a mountain full of secrets and no one left to tell.

The stove still held warmth from the night before. Someone — Gideon, she suspected, though he'd never admit it — had fed it coal from the seam Elias must have mined himself. The fire ticked and settled. Outside, the wind moved through the dead mine timbers with a sound like slow breathing, like the mountain itself inhaling and exhaling around the wound the Syndicate had cut into it.

She unwrapped the first journal at the table where her father had eaten, slept, drawn, and died by inches.

There were eleven of them. She counted twice, because counting was easier than feeling. Eleven journals spanning nineteen years, their covers swollen with damp and then dried stiff, their spines cracked into white seams like river ice in spring. Her father's handwriting covered every page — small, precise, the hand of a man who had learned drafting before he'd learned anything else. She knew that hand. She had seen it on grocery lists, on birthday cards, on the single letter that had reached her two months ago and set all of this in motion.

*If you're reading this, I'm sorry for the shape of it. Come find me or don't. But if you do, bring nothing you can't burn.*

She had thought it was the rambling of a dying man who'd spent too many years alone. She had thought *bring nothing you can't burn* meant the paranoia of an old prospector guarding a claim.

She opened the first journal and began to understand how wrong she had been.

* *

The early entries were almost ordinary. Survey notes. Water levels. Ore samples described in the dry shorthand of a man who had prospected half the territories between here and the coast. Wren read fast at first, skimming, looking for the thing — the vein, the strike, the reason he'd vanished up this river and stayed gone.

It wasn't there. That was the first wrongness. Nineteen years of prospecting journals, and not one entry celebrated a find. Not one page recorded a claim staked, a sample assayed, a payday. Instead the notes bent steadily away from ore and toward water. Temperature readings of the river itself, taken weekly, then daily. Notes on steam vents along the gorge walls. Sketches of the rock strata with annotations in a technical vocabulary she didn't recognize — *thermal gradient*, *conduit fracture*, *artesian head* — words that belonged to engineers, not prospectors.

And dates. He recorded dates obsessively. Not just the day but the hour, sometimes the minute, as though time itself were part of the survey.

She turned pages until the lamplight guttered, fed it more coal, kept turning. Somewhere behind her Gideon sat against the far wall with the compass open in his lap, saying nothing, watching her read the way a man watches weather come over a ridge. She was grateful for the silence. She would have bitten anyone who spoke.

The shift came in the fourth journal, seven years in.

*A crew came through yesterday. Not miners. They wore no dust. Twelve men, new equipment, surveying instruments better than anything the territorial office ever issued. They asked me what I'd found. I showed them pyrite and they laughed and paid me twenty dollars to keep my mouth shut about the steam. Twenty dollars. For a vent hot enough to scald a trout mid-current.*

*I took the money. Then I went back down and started measuring everything again, properly, because men don't pay twenty dollars for nothing, and rich men pay it only when they're afraid.*

*They're afraid.*

Wren's throat tightened. She read the entry three times. Her father's voice lived in these lines so completely that she could hear him — the dry humor, the wariness underneath it, the particular cadence he used when he was frightened and covering it with arithmetic. She had heard that cadence across a kitchen table when she was nine, when the Syndicate's dam notices first came to Callow's Ford, when he'd sat calculating figures late into the night and told her, *Nothing to worry about, birdie. Paper can't drown a river.*

Paper couldn't. Men could.

* *

By the sixth journal, Elias Marsh had stopped being a prospector entirely, though he never said so outright. The transformation happened in the margins, in the gaps between entries, in the slow accumulation of evidence he gathered the way other men gathered gold — patiently, obsessively, against all sense.

He documented the crews. Names where he could get them, descriptions where he couldn't. Rotation schedules deduced from supply trains. He counted the barges going downstream heavy and coming back light, and noted what the heavy holds might contain — *not ore; ore doesn't need armed escort* — and he drew the access tunnel's mouth from a ridge three miles off, hiding in the rocks with a spyglass like a man scouting an enemy position, which, Wren realized with a cold spreading under her ribs, was exactly what he was doing.

Because that was what the journals were. Not a prospector's log. A case file. Nineteen years of surveillance conducted by one man with a spyglass, a thermometer, and a grudge, building — entry by entry, winter by winter — the only complete record in existence of what the Meridian Syndicate had built inside the Verrin's headwaters.

*They've driven the main adit two hundred fathoms past the fault line,* he wrote in the eighth journal. *I can hear the machinery from the ridge on still nights. Not pumps. Pumps have a rhythm. This has a hum, low and steady, like a hive the size of a cathedral. Whatever they're feeding down there, it eats heat and gives back power, and the power goes somewhere by wire, and the wire goes downriver, and downriver is where the towns are that don't appear on maps anymore.*

*Callow's Ford drowned for this. Odile's ferry route drowned for this. Half the settlements on the lower Verrin are underwater because they needed the reservoir depth to cool whatever devil's engine they've got humming in my mountain. My mountain. As if I own it. As if anyone owns it. That's the whole sickness of them, isn't it — they think owning is the same as understanding.*

Wren stopped reading. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and sat very still while the stove ticked and the wind breathed through the timbers and nineteen years of her own anger rearranged itself into a new and terrible shape.

She had hated him for leaving. That hatred had been the load-bearing wall of her life for two decades — she had built rooms inside it, furnished it, kept it warm. He had chosen the river over her. He had chosen some glittering strike, some fool's treasure, over the daughter waiting in a rented room in a town whose name she changed every few years to stay ahead of the Syndicate's inquiries into Elias Marsh's next of kin.

But he hadn't chosen treasure. He had chosen witness. He had looked at what was being done to the river — to *her* river, to the water she'd grown up poling a flatboat through — and he had understood that someone had to stay and write it all down, and he had known what staying cost, and he had paid it, year after year, alone, in the cold, writing in a hand as neat as prayer so that someday, someone could read it.

*Someday, someone.* He had meant her. He had sent the map out because he knew he wouldn't survive long enough to carry the truth down himself.

She hadn't gotten to the proof of that yet. She could feel it waiting in the later journals like deep water under thin ice.

* *

Gideon stirred once, near midnight, and put another armful of coal within her reach without a word. When she glanced at him, he was looking at the compass in his hands with an expression she was learning to read — the look of a man doing sums he doesn't like. She almost spoke to him. Almost told him what she was finding.

She didn't. Not yet. Some things needed to be carried whole before they could be shared, or they broke into pieces and cut everyone handling them.

She read on.

The ninth journal was the year Callow's Ford went under. She knew the date; she'd been there, seventeen years old and furious at the world, watching the water climb the church steps while men in Syndicate grey stood on the levee with clipboards, checking boxes. Her father had been four years gone by then. She had cursed his name from the levee — *where are you now, you gutless ghost* — and the journal told her exactly where he was.

*Fifty feet above the floodline with a stopwatch,* he wrote. *Timing the release gates. They opened them at dawn and called it spring melt. Spring melt. I stood on the ridge and watched a town die on schedule and wrote down the exact minute, because that's what I am now. Not a prospector. A clerk for the prosecution. Every minute I record is a nail, and someday there will be enough nails to build a coffin big enough for the whole rotten company.*

*Birdie, if you ever read this — and I pray you never have reason to — forgive me the timing. I couldn't come down. Coming down meant being found, and being found meant the journals burn with me, and then the river drowns for nothing.*

Wren did not cry. She had used up her crying years ago, on the levee, in the rented rooms, in the long grey stretch of her twenties when she'd tracked rumors of him from territory to territory and always arrived a season late. What came instead was worse and better than tears: a clarity like the air above the snow line, cold and clean and merciless.

Her father had watched Callow's Ford drown and written down the minute. He had carried that minute for twelve more years, alone, adding to it, weighing it, keeping it safe the way she had once kept a flatboat safe through white water — with both hands, with total attention, with the knowledge that letting go meant death.

He had sent the map out because the cough in the last journal's final pages had gone from occasional to constant, because he'd felt the ground of himself giving way, because a dead man's evidence is worth nothing unless it reaches living hands.

*The map is the bait and the beacon both,* he had written, in the last entry with real strength in it. *Anyone who comes hunting treasure will follow it and be dealt with by the country itself. But someone who comes hunting truth will read the margins. I've hidden the rest where only the compass can find it. Silas would have known what I meant by that. I hope whoever carries it now is half the man Silas was, and twice as stubborn as me.*

*That should give them a fair chance.*

* *

Dawn found her at the last journal, the eleventh, its final pages written in a hand that deteriorated entry by entry — the letters leaning, the lines wavering, the numbers still precise even as the sentences shortened, because even dying, her father trusted figures more than feelings.

The last full entry was dated nine days before the night Gideon had found him under the rail bridge.

*Done now. Everything a body needs is in the books and the casing both. If the girl comes, tell her the margins were always hers. Tell her I measured the river every week for nineteen years and it never once ran backward, and neither did I, and neither, I'd wager, will she.*

*Tell her the last thing isn't the map. The map is ash the moment it's used. The last thing is the record. Burn the map. Keep the record. Make them read it in daylight.*

Wren closed the journal and held it against her chest and finally let herself feel the full weight of it — not grief, or not only grief, but inheritance. Nineteen years of solitary courage, pressed flat into paper, delivered into her hands by a stranger who owed her father nothing and had carried it anyway.

She looked up. Gideon was asleep sitting up, chin on chest, the compass still loose in his fingers, his face in sleep younger and less armored than waking ever allowed. Beyond him, through the camp's broken window, the sky was going pale over the summit ridge, and beneath that ridge, humming in its cathedral dark, the Syndicate's engine ate heat and gave back power, and wires ran downriver toward towns that had been erased from every map but her father's.

They wanted the source kept. Buried. Erased, the way they'd erased Callow's Ford, the way they'd erased Elias Marsh, the way they were erasing, right now, every record that might contradict their version of the world.

Well. She was her father's daughter, and the river never ran backward.

Wren stacked the eleven journals in order, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and tied the knot the way Gidian had — tight enough to survive a drowning. Then she woke him with a boot against his, and when he blinked up at her, she was already lacing her boots.

"Get up," she said. "We're not stopping at the lake. We're going to make the whole world read it in daylight."

Outside, far below in the gorge, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the machinery hummed on — and for the first time, it sounded less like a hive than like something that knew it was about to be stung.
