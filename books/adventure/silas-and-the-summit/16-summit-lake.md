## 16. Summit Lake

The river stopped being a river an hour before we reached it.

I noticed it first in the sound — or the absence of sound. For eleven days the Verrin had been our metronome, its voice changing with every mile: a mutter in the shallows, a growl through the narrows, that long cathedral roar in the flooded gorge. Now, climbing the last switchback of Elias's staircase, I realized the roar had thinned to a whisper, and then to nothing at all. The water was still moving. It just wasn't talking anymore.

"It's scared," Wren said beside me.

She'd been quiet since the mine shafts, since her father's journals had turned her grief into something with edges. She climbed like someone following instructions written into her bones, boots finding holds Elias must have mapped forty years ago. The compass hung from my neck on its cord, and even now, even here, I could feel it pulling — not north, never north, but up and inward, toward whatever waited above us.

"Rivers don't get scared," I said.

"This one's been held down so long it forgot how to run." She didn't look back at me. "You'd be quiet too."

* * *

The last stretch of trail cut through a stand of pines so old their roots had grown around the rocks like fingers around coins. The air changed as we climbed — warmer, wetter, carrying a smell I couldn't place at first. Sulfur, yes, but under it something else. Something mineral and alive, like the breath of a forge that had been burning since before anyone thought to name it.

Then the trees ended.

We came out onto a shelf of black rock, and I stopped walking because my legs decided for me.

The caldera opened below us like a bowl the earth had blown out of itself and never bothered to close. Two miles across, maybe more, ringed by cliffs streaked white with mineral stain. And filling it — the lake. Not blue, not gray, but a pale, milky turquoise, steaming along every shoreline where the hot ground met the water. Mist rose off it in slow columns and leaned against the wind, and where the wind caught the surface, the steam tore away in banners that streamed toward the east rim like flags surrendering.

But it was the sound that undid me. Under the hiss of the vents, under the lap of hot water against hot stone, there was a hum. Low, steady, felt in the sternum before the ears. A machine-sound. A heartbeat that didn't belong to anything living.

"Gideon." Wren's hand closed on my arm. "Look at the far shore."

I'd been looking. I hadn't understood what I was seeing.

Syndicate infrastructure ringed the caldera like a crown of thorns. Transmission towers marched along the eastern rim, their lines sagging heavy between them, humming with the same note as the lake. A road — a real road, graded and graveled, hidden up here at four thousand feet where no map admitted it existed — curved along the southern shore to a compound of prefab buildings and fuel tanks. And at the water's edge, half-sunk into the shallows, stood the intake works: a lattice of steel and concrete swallowing the lake itself, pipes thick as train tunnels running from the waterline into the cliff face and vanishing.

"They're not tapping a hot spring," Wren breathed. "They're drinking a volcano."

Elias's journals had told us. Reading about it was one thing. Standing above it, feeling the hum climb through my boot soles into my teeth, was another thing entirely. Every light in three counties, every Syndicate ledger line, every erased town downstream — Callow's Ford drowned, the water rights bought and broken, decades of records burned — all of it fed out of this bowl. The forbidden source wasn't forbidden because it was sacred. It was forbidden because it was profitable, and profit keeps best in the dark.

"We should mark the towers," Wren said, already crouching, pulling Elias's folded survey notes from her jacket. "Count the intakes. He documented everything — we finish it."

She was right, and I hated how calm she sounded, because my own hands wouldn't stay still. We worked our way along the western rim, Wren sketching, me counting, the compass swinging lazily now that it had delivered us — its long journey over, pointing at nothing but the ground beneath our feet. That was when I saw the smoke.

Not steam. Smoke. Thin and gray, rising from behind a ridge of talus on the northern shore, maybe a mile off. A campfire, banked low. Someone was here ahead of us.

Wren saw it too. Her hand went to the knife at her belt. "Syndicate?"

"Too small for a crew," I said. "And they'd not bother hiding it if they owned this whole valley." But my mouth had gone dry anyway, because there was only one other person I could think of who might have followed us this far, this fast, after a rockslide should have buried him.

* * *

We came down through the talus with the wind at our backs, and he heard us anyway — of course he did; thirty years of serving careful men teaches you to hear everything — because when we rounded the last boulder, Corvus was already standing, hands loose at his sides, watching us come.

He looked worse than the last time I'd seen him, which was saying something, given that the last time I'd seen him he'd been bleeding under a rockslide I'd dug him out of. Thinner. Gray-faced. His coat was gone and his shirt sleeves were rolled over forearms scarred with old rope burns, and he stood like a man who had set down something very heavy and wasn't sure yet whether his back would straighten.

"You took the bait route," I said. "Box canyon. Rockslide."

"I did." No apology in it. No excuse either. "You pulled me out anyway. I've spent three weeks deciding what that makes us."

"And?"

He looked past me, at the caldera, at the towers and the intake works and the road that shouldn't exist. Thirty years of looking at things like this, and his face did something I'd never seen it do. It broke, just slightly, around the eyes.

"I sent a false report upstream," he said quietly. "Told them you were dead in the gorge. Bought you three days. This is day four." He turned back to me. "I'm not here for the map, Whitlock. I'm here to see it. Before they decide I'm worth erasing too."

Wren hadn't relaxed. "Why should we believe you?"

"Because I'm alone." He spread his arms, slow, letting her read him. "No team. No radio. I smashed it on the way up. Because I owe this man a debt the Syndicate taught me to count, and because—" his voice caught, and he steadied it, "—because I helped bury towns, girl. Whole towns. I drew the perimeters. I filed the paperwork. I'm owed a look at what it was all for."

The hum filled the silence between us. Steam drifted past, warm as breath.

"The director knows we're coming," I said. "Day four means they've had a day to figure out your report was ash."

"Then you'd best stop sightseeing." Corvus nodded east, toward the compound, and my stomach dropped as I followed his gaze.

Down on the valley floor, far below the caldera rim where the access road wound up through the foothills, dust was moving. Not weather-dust. Vehicle dust — a column of it, crawling upward along the switchbacks. Trucks. Maybe six, maybe eight, and behind them, glinting when the sun caught it, something larger. Something that moved like it weighed as much as the mountain wanted it to.

"Crews," Corvus said. "Intercept teams. They'll seal the rim roads within the hour, and then they'll come up here, and everything the three of us know will go into the ground with us." He looked at me, and for the first time since I'd known him, Gideon Whitlock's charm would have been useless, because there was nothing left to charm. There was only arithmetic. "So. What's it going to be?"

Wren was already moving, folding Elias's notes, her jaw set. Below us, the trucks kept climbing, patient as debt. And the lake went on humming its one long note, like it had been waiting all this time for someone to finally listen — and like it already knew what we'd choose.

I put my hand on the compass at my throat and felt it pull, faint and steady, toward the water.

"First," I said, "we see what they've built down there. Up close."

The trucks were a mile below us and closing.
