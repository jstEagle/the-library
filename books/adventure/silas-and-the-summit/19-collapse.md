## 19. Collapse

The mountain screamed before it moved.

It was not a sound I had words for — not thunder, not rockfall, nothing so honest. It was the sound of a thing that had been held shut for thirty years finally letting go, a groan that came up through the soles of my boots and into my teeth, and I knew in the marrow of me that we had maybe ninety seconds to live if we were lucky, and less than that if we weren't.

"Run," Corvus said. Not shouted. Said, the way a man says *rain's coming*, because he had spent three decades inside the Syndicate's machinery and he understood better than any of us what it sounded like when the machinery broke.

We ran.

The shaft Elias had called the Staircase — his old works, timbered and rotten and older than any of us — was coming down behind us in sections, like a zipper being undone by a giant's hand. Dust came first, a brown wall of it, tasting of iron and old dynamite and the mineral breath of the vent itself. Then the timbers began to go, one after another, each crack a gunshot in the dark, each collapse a door slamming shut on the world above.

Wren was ahead of me with the lantern, her father's journals strapped tight against her chest in an oilskin wrap, and she ran the way she did everything — like the ground owed her something and she meant to collect. Behind her came Odile's kids: Pip, fourteen and fearless; the twins, Sable and Juno, who had grown up swimming floodwater and feared nothing that floated; little Anselm, eleven, whose legs were shorter than the rest but whose lungs, Odile swore, could outlast a steam engine. They had come up the mountain with us because they refused to be left behind, and now they were running for their lives through a mine their grandparents might have worked, and all I could think was: *I brought them here.*

No. That wasn't true, and I knew it even as I thought it. The Syndicate brought them here. Every drowned town, every stolen river, every rigged ledger — the whole long architecture of erasure that Elias Marsh had spent his last years documenting. We were just the ones carrying the proof out.

The tunnel forked at the old pump station, and my memory of the map — burned now, gone, living only in the compass casing against my ribs — split with it. Left was the haulage way, wide and graded, the route the Syndicate's crews used. Right was Elias's back passage, narrow, flooded at the bottom, dropping toward the river level where Odile's crew had rigged the floodgates downstream.

Corvus stopped at the fork. He had a miner's lamp in one hand and a look on his face I'd seen once before, in the box canyon, when he understood what my mercy was going to cost him.

"The haulage way comes out at their staging camp," he said. "There'll be forty men there by now."

"And the other way?" Wren called back, already three steps down the right-hand passage.

"Flooded. Half a mile of it, maybe more, before you reach the river gate." He looked at me, and in the lamplight his face was older than it had been that morning, older than it had been thirty years ago when he first signed on with men who erased towns. "And the water's rising."

* * *

We took the right-hand passage, of course. There was never really a question. Forty Syndicate men with orders to bury the truth along with us was its own kind of drowning, slower and more certain, and I had spent enough of my life doing arithmetic with other people's lives to know which equation I'd rather run.

But the water was rising, and it was rising fast.

The caldera above us had been tapped, plugged, harnessed — decades of pressure held behind valves and tunnels and the Syndicate's enormous secret appetite for power. When Wren blew the floodgates downstream and the vent opened to open sky, all that held-back pressure went looking for somewhere to be. And the somewhere, it turned out, was everywhere. Down every shaft. Through every seam. Into the low places, which is where we were, wading now, then swimming, the passage narrowing around us until there was nothing to do but let the water carry us.

I want to say I stayed calm. I want to tell you I read the current the way I'd taught the kids to read it, that I found the eddies and the slack water and rode them down like a professional, like a man who had made his living reading weather and water and knew exactly what the river wanted.

Here is what actually happened.

The surge hit us at the second timbering, where the passage dropped through what Elias's maps had marked as a winze — a vertical connection between levels — and the whole world became brown water and noise. I remember Pip's hand catching my sleeve and then not catching it. I remember the lantern going out, or being torn away, and the dark that followed wasn't darkness exactly but a kind of solid, grinding blindness, water full of silt and splinters and the taste of the mountain coming apart. I remember thinking, with a clarity that still shames me, *the compass* — reaching for it, the one thing Elias had trusted me with besides the map, the one thing that pointed not north but home —

And then the river took me.

I have been in whitewater my whole adult life. I have driven rigs into storms that peeled asphalt off highways. I have stood on ridgelines while tornadoes walked past close enough to touch and felt nothing but joy. None of it prepared me for the inside of a flooding mine, because a river, even at its worst, is honest. It has banks. It has a surface. It moves the way water moves, and if you pay attention it will tell you what it's going to do.

This was not a river. This was the mountain's own blood, released after thirty years, and it did not want anything except down.

I tumbled. I don't know how far or how long. I hit timber — felt it give, felt a splinter open my forearm from wrist to elbow — and then I was through, into a wider gallery where the water slowed just enough to form a whirl, a slow terrible carousel turning in the black, and I couldn't find the surface. I pushed up and found stone. I pushed another way and found the pull of the drain, the winze below, sucking everything down into deeper dark. My lungs were already burning. My boots were filling, dragging me, and some cold rational corner of my mind — the same corner that had catalogued my debts for six years, itemizing everything I owned against what I owed — noted, almost politely, that this was it, this was how it ended, not in a storm with a camera rolling but alone in the dark, owing nothing, owning nothing, my name about to belong to no one at all.

Then hands caught me.

Not one pair. Several.

They came out of the black the way river kids do everything — without hesitation, without ceremony, without any apparent understanding that what they were doing was remarkable. Sable had my collar. Juno had my arm. Anselm — small, wiry Anselm, whom I had taught three weeks ago on Odile's raft to feel the difference between a hole and a hydraulic, to read the V of a current the way other children read letters — got a grip on my belt and hauled, and the three of them together pulled me out of the whirl and into the slack water against the gallery wall, where the air was a thin, sweet pocket under the arched roof of the tunnel.

I came up coughing half the mountain out of my lungs, and the first thing I saw in the gloom was Anselm's face, eleven years old and utterly unimpressed.

"You fought it," he said. "You're supposed to float. You *taught* us that."

I laughed. It hurt, and water came out of me instead of sound, but I laughed, because there was no answer to that, none at all. Somewhere upstream, in whatever accounting the universe keeps, a man who had spent his life calculating exits had just been pulled out of the dark by children he'd taught to read water, and the lesson had been his all along.

"Where are the others?" I managed.

"Ahead," Sable said. "Wren's got the light. Pip's with her. Come on — the water's still rising."

* * *

We came out of the mountain into rain.

Actual rain, ordinary rain, storm-season rain falling soft and gray on the lower slopes, and I have never in my life been so glad to feel something so ordinary. The river gate — the old sluice Odile's crew had rigged — hung open beside us, its timbers blown outward, and beyond it the gorge fell away in white water, the Verrin running higher and wilder than I had ever seen it, carrying the mountain's release down toward the valleys, toward Callow's Ford, toward every town the Syndicate had dried up and starved.

Above us, the summit breathed.

That is the only word I have for it. The caldera vented in a great slow column of steam, straight up into the clouds, unharnessed for the first time in thirty years — no turbines drinking it, no cables dragging its power down into the Syndicate's secret works, just heat and vapor rising into open sky the way it must have risen before anyone ever thought to own it. It made a sound too, a deep harmonic hum, like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Elias had heard it, I realized. From his camp above the old works, he had listened to this for years, documenting, waiting, sending his map out into the world because he knew he wouldn't live to see it free.

Wren stood at the mouth of the gate with her father's journals clutched to her chest, soaked through, watching the steam rise. When I reached her she didn't turn, but she leaned — just slightly, just enough — until her shoulder touched mine. Neither of us said anything. There was nothing that needed saying that the mountain hadn't already said louder.

Pip counted heads twice, the way Odile had drilled into them. All present. All accounted for. Except one.

"Where's Corvus?" I asked.

Sable and Juno exchanged a look — the twins' private language, older than words. It was Pip who answered, jerking a thumb back toward the dark mouth of the gate.

"He didn't come out."

* * *

I went back in. Of course I went back in. Wren cursed me with her father's own vocabulary, which told me more about Elias Marsh than any journal could have, and then she came in after me, because she was her father's daughter and stubbornness was the family inheritance.

We found him eighty yards up the main passage, at the second timbering, where the winze dropped away and the water still churned. He was braced against the last standing set of timbers with a prop bar across his shoulders, holding up a section of roof that had been sagging since before the surge, holding it because beneath it the escape route lay open, and behind him the passage was folding down like a throat closing.

He was bleeding from a cut over his eye. His lamp was dead. In the gray seep of light from the gate behind us, he looked calm. That was the worst part. Thirty years inside the machine, and at the end of it he looked like a man doing his job.

"It's settling," he said when he saw us. "Another minute and it's done either way. Go back, Whitlock."

"Not how this works," I said. "You pulled people out of worse than this. I've read your file."

"My file's classified."

"So am I, apparently. Move your feet, old man."

He almost smiled. Then the timber above him cracked — a long, splintering report, louder than any gunshot — and the decision was taken out of everyone's hands, because the roof came down in a rush of dust and broken wood, and Corvus threw himself sideways, off the prop bar, into the gap, and Wren and I caught him by the arms as the passage behind him sealed itself with a finality like a book closing.

For a moment the three of us stood in the ringing dark, breathing dust, alive.

"You shouldn't have come back," Corvus said hoarsely.

"Yeah," I said. "People keep telling me that. I keep not listening. It's becoming a character flaw."

He looked at me for a long moment, and something passed between us that neither of us would ever put into words — not gratitude, exactly, and not forgiveness, because there were things on both sides that would never wash clean. Something more like recognition. Two men who had spent their lives in service to ledgers, standing in the wreckage of the last debt either of them would ever owe.

Then the water rose again — a second pulse from the depths, the caldera clearing its throat — and we ran, all three of us, stumbling and coughing, out through the blown gate, out into the rain, out onto the wet green slope where six river kids and a ferrywoman's raft waited, and behind us the mountain settled into silence, and above us the steam kept rising, free, into open sky.

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bar two miles downstream, because nobody had the strength for more, and because Odile — who had come upriver to meet us the moment the floodgates roared — took one look at all of us and started issuing orders like the general she had evidently been in some former life nobody talked about.

The kids slept in a heap by the cookfire, tangled together like puppies. Pip had a new gash on her shin and wore it like a medal. Anselm, before dropping off, mumbled that next time I should try floating, and the others laughed in their sleep.

Corvus sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, drying his one good coat. He had sent his last false report upstream from the summit that morning — three days bought, a career ended, a life's loyalty spent in a single transmission — and he sat with the particular stillness of a man watching the only structure he'd ever known burn down behind him and finding, to his own surprise, that he could breathe the smoke.

Wren sat beside me. She had the journals open on her knees, wrapped in oilskin, dry, safe, every page of her father's thirty-year testimony intact. Below us the Verrin ran loud and high and clean, carrying snowmelt and freed water down through the gorge, past the drowned foundations of Callow's Ford, past every town that had ever been told the river belonged to someone else.

"They'll send people," she said quietly. "After the report fails. After they realize what happened up there."

"I know."

"They'll come down the river looking for us."

"I know that too." I turned the compass over in my hands — scorched now, its glass cracked, its needle swinging lazily, pointing at nothing and everything. Inside its casing, sealed in wax paper, were the copies: Elias's documentation, the photographs, the figures, the names of every town the Syndicate had erased. "Let them come. By the time they get there, the truth will already be downstream."

Wren looked at me, firelight in her eyes, and for the first time since the rail bridge — since a dying man's blood and a stranger's promise — she smiled.

"Downstream," she said, "is where we're from."

The fire crackled. The river sang. And far above us, faint on the night air, the summit hummed its long unfinished note — free, for the first time in thirty years, and rising.
