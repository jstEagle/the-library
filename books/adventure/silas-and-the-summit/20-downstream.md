## 20. Downstream

The river forgave us before anyone else did.

That was Wren's first thought on the second morning of the descent, watching the water carry the raft through a stretch of canyon so green it seemed lit from below. The Verrin ran high and loud with the last of the melt, but it had gentled since the summit — as if the floodgates opening, the tunnel drowning, the whole rotten engine of the Syndicate choking on its own river had taken some weight out of the current too. Water didn't hold grudges. It only remembered which way was down.

She sat at the stern with her father's journals stacked in an oilcloth bundle against her knees, and she did not touch them. She'd spent three days touching them. Reading them aloud in firelight to Odile's kids until they could recite passages back like scripture. Copying pages by lantern while Gideon slept sitting up, one hand still curled around the compass he no longer needed.

The copies were done now. Forty-one towns marked on a map Odile had drawn from memory — ferry landings, fish camps, the drowned edges of Callow's Ford where people still lived in houses with their knees in the water. Forty-one places that would know, by week's end, what the Syndicate had built above them and what it had cost.

"You're doing the face again," Gideon said from mid-raft, where he was teaching Silas's knot to a nine-year-old named Finch who kept tying herself to the cargo instead of the cargo to anything useful.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're about to argue with a piece of paper."

"I'm not arguing." She turned the top journal over, studying Elias's cramped hand through the oilcloth. "I'm deciding how much of him to give away."

Gideon went quiet. Even Finch stopped fumbling the rope, sensing weather the way kids on the river always did.

Wren hadn't spoken plainly about her father since the summit. There hadn't been time — there had been rockfall and black water and Corvus holding a door with his shoulders until the mountain took the choice away from him — and since then there had been only the long work of going downriver, which left a person alone with her thoughts whether she wanted company or not.

But the first town was coming. Odile had said they'd raise the lanterns of Miller's Bend by dusk, and after Miller's Bend there would be another town, and another, and somewhere between them Wren was going to have to hand her father's grief to strangers and watch them carry it off.

* * *

They made Miller's Bend as the light went amber.

It wasn't much — a shelf of shacks above a gravel bar, drying nets, a church with its steeple patched in tin — but when Odile hailed from the bow, people came down to the water like it was a holiday. Word had outrun them somehow; word always did on the river, moving faster than any Syndicate courier, passed dock to dock in the old cant of the boatmen. *The chasers came down. The dam business is finished. The water's coming back.*

Wren stepped onto the gravel bar with the bundle under her arm and felt every eye find it.

An old woman with forearms like dock rope pushed through the crowd. "You're Marsh's girl."

Not a question. Wren nodded. "You knew him?"

"Knew his handwriting better than his face. He sent pages down for years. Little things. River heights. Where the fish went strange." The woman's eyes were pale and unblinking. "We never knew what he was building toward. We just kept the pieces."

The pieces. Wren thought of the journal in her pack, the final entry, the one she'd read forty times and still couldn't read without her throat closing: *If I don't come down, send it all. Let them see the shape of it.* Her father had been mailing letters into the dark for twenty years, and the dark had kept every one.

She untied the oilcloth. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. "These are copies," she said, loud enough to reach the back of the crowd. "The originals go to the territorial archive in Corvane, with witnesses, once we're sure nobody can burn them first. But these—" she held up the stack, "—these are yours. Read them. Copy them again. Send them further downstream than I can row. My father spent his life writing this so it couldn't be erased. Don't let it be."

The old woman took the bundle the way people take infants and hot coals, with both hands and a certain fear.

And that was it. That was the whole ceremony. No speech, no weeping, though a man near the church took off his hat and a boy asked his mother loudly what a geothermal vent was. Wren walked back to the raft feeling lighter and emptier in equal measure, the way she'd felt after burying her mother — the grief not gone, just finally given somewhere to sit besides her own chest.

Gideon was waiting at the gunwale. He didn't say anything clever, which was how she knew he understood.

* * *

They camped that night on a bend of white sand below Miller's Bend, and for once the crew let the fire burn low and went to sleep early, worn out by the excitement of being heroes in a town with one street.

Wren stayed up. So did Gideon. They ended up side by side on the sand with the embers making a small red country between them, and the river talking its endless talk, and neither of them speaking, which had become its own language over the weeks — a silence that meant *I'm here* rather than *go away.*

It was Gideon who broke it, eventually, in the careful voice he used when he'd been rehearsing.

"Your father chose me because I was nobody."

She'd known this was coming. She'd felt it building in him for days, the way you feel a storm stacking up over a ridge. "He chose you because you were desperate," she said. "There's a difference. Desperate men say yes."

"He could have sent it to you."

"He tried." The words came out flatter than she intended. "I told you what the letter said. He wrote me in the spring. Said he'd found something worth more than gold and he needed someone he could trust with it. And I wrote back that I was busy." She picked up a stick and drew a line in the sand, then another, crossing it out. "I was running a salvage claim on the lower Kess. Three months of hard work about to pay off. I told him to send it to someone with fewer obligations." A breath. "So he sent it to a stranger who owed money to the worst people on the continent. Because that stranger said yes, and his own daughter said *later.*"

Gideon was quiet a moment. "You don't know that's why."

"I know my father. He planned for everything. He planned bait routes and dead drops and thirty years of coded marginalia. You think he didn't plan for me saying later?" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I think maybe he even counted on it. I think maybe he knew if he put it in my hands, I'd come up the mountain after him, and he couldn't—" Her voice snagged. She hated that. She dragged the stick through the drawing, obliterating it. "He couldn't risk me. So he risked you instead. A man with nothing to lose."

"That's not what I was," Gideon said. "By the end."

"No," she agreed. "By the end you had something to lose. That was the point, I think. That was his real design." She looked at him across the ember-light. "He didn't need a courier. He needed someone the river could make into family fast enough to matter. You were the most alone man I'd ever met, Gideon Whitlock. You were practically begging for it."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Is that forgiveness? Being told I was a stray?"

"It's being told you were chosen twice." She said it before she could decide not to. "Once by him. Once by everyone else on this raft. I'm just the one rude enough to say it out loud."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, and the kids' breathing made its small tide-sound from under the tarps.

"Gideon." She turned the stick in her fingers. "I spent the whole climb hating you a little. For having the map. For having his trust. For being the son of the story I should have been in." She met his eyes. "I'm done with that. But I need you to tell me the truth about one thing, and I need it plain, because we've both been fluent in not-saying for too long. When Corvus offered you the debt clean — before the box canyon, before everything — what did you actually want to do?"

He didn't answer right away. She watched him be honest with himself first, which was more than most men managed, and then honest with her.

"I wanted to take it," he said. "For about six hours, I wanted it so bad I could taste it. A new name. A clean slate. I lay awake composing the letter I'd write to the Syndicate, and in the letter I was already somebody else." He turned the compass over in his hands — Silas's compass, Elias's compass, its brass worn bright by three owners now. "And then I realized whatever name they gave me, I'd still be the man who sold a river. Names don't fix that. Only walking away does. Only burning it does." He glanced at her. "That's the truth. Plain."

"Thank you." She meant it more than she could fit into the words.

"And you?" he asked. "Plain, since we're doing plain. What happens to you now? The claim on the Kess is gone. The Syndicate's done. Your father's avenged, or exposed, or whatever word makes it hurt less. What's next for Wren Marsh?"

She looked downriver, where the dark swallowed the bend, and found — for the first time in longer than she could measure — that the question didn't frighten her.

"There's a girl in Callow's Ford," she said, "about Finch's age, who asked me yesterday how the floodgates worked. Not why. How. She wants to build things. And Odile needs someone who can read water and read paper both, and the archive in Corvane will want testimony, and there are eleven more towns on Odile's map that haven't heard yet." She shrugged. "I'm going to be busy. It turns out I'm my father's daughter after all. I just do my prospecting in daylight."

"And me?" Gideon asked, and there it was — the old desperation flickering under the charm, the stray-dog question he'd never quite stop asking: *is there a place for me.*

Wren smiled and threw the stick into the embers.

"Storm season's starting," she said. "Odile says the upper runs will be wild once the Syndicate's dams open full. Somebody on this raft has to teach the kids which clouds mean run for shore." She stood, brushing sand from her knees, and offered him the last plain thing of the night. "You're on my crew now, Whitlock. Try to keep up."

Downstream, past the bend, the first grey hint of dawn touched the water — and far off, faint but unmistakable, came the long roll of thunder.
