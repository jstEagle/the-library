## 22. Silas and the Summit

The compass stopped pointing at the river in the spring of the year after everything changed.

I noticed it on the first warm morning at Callow's Ford, when Odile's raft was hauled up on the bank for recaulking and the kids were running barefoot through the shallows like the water had never been taken from them. I'd kept the compass in my breast pocket out of habit — a chaser's habit, an old man's habit now, though I was neither old nor, by any honest measure, a chaser anymore. I pulled it out to check the light on it, the way you'd check a scar, and the needle swung lazy circles and settled on nothing at all. Not north. Not upstream. Just a small trembling indecision, like a dog that has finally been let off its rope and doesn't know which way to run.

I sat with that a long time, my boots in the mud, the river talking to itself below me.

It took me until noon to understand what it meant. The needle had never pointed north. It had pointed at the source — at whatever Elias Marsh had tuned it to find, some lodestone of heat and iron buried under the summit lake. And the source was gone now. Vented into open sky, collapsed into stone, given back to the weather. There was nothing left in the world for the needle to want.

Except it didn't settle on nothing. That was the thing I kept turning over. It didn't spin wild or lie dead in its housing. Every time I checked it — that morning, and the next, and every morning for a week — it came to rest on the same bearing, gentle as a hand on a shoulder. Southeast. Downriver. Toward Callow's Ford. Toward home, if I was the kind of man who used that word.

I wasn't, yet. But I was learning.

* * *

Wren found me at the ferry landing that evening, where I was pretending to help Odile's crew patch the raft and mostly just watching Tess read the current the way I'd taught her, calling the eddies before they opened, reading the river's grammar in the lean of the foam. She was good. Better than I'd been at her age, and I'd been paid — badly — to do it.

"You felt it too," Wren said. She held out her palm, and there lay her father's compass — no. *The* compass. Mine, if a thing could be mine when it had already belonged to two men before me. Hers, if blood counted for anything. Ours, maybe, which was a word I was still getting the shape of in my mouth.

"Silas," she said.

I looked up at her. She had her mother's jaw and her father's eyes, and those eyes were doing something careful right now, the way they did when she was about to hand someone a truth and watch what they did with it.

"The name inside," she said. "You never asked me about it."

"I figured you'd tell me when you were ready."

"That's a lie. You didn't ask because you were afraid the answer would be something you'd have to carry." She said it without cruelty. That was the trick of Wren — she could cut you to the bone and make it feel like a kindness, because at least someone had finally bothered to look.

She sat down beside me on the landing planks, our legs hanging over the black water, and turned the compass over. She pried the brass back loose with her thumbnail — a trick she'd learned from her father's journals, from pages written in a hand gone shaky near the end — and tipped the casing toward the last of the light.

Engraved inside the lid, so fine you'd miss it unless the sun caught it exactly: **S. VANE — HE FOUND IT FIRST.**

"Elias didn't come to the mountains alone," Wren said. "Silas Vane was his partner. Twenty years in the high country together, before I was born. My father wrote about him the way other men write about brothers. Better, maybe." She paused. "They found the vent together. They mapped it together. And then the Syndicate came, and Silas went down into the works one winter to document what they were building, and he never came out."

The river moved beneath us. Somewhere across the water, one of Odile's kids laughed at something, bright and unguarded.

"My father spent thirty years finishing what Silas started," Wren said. "Every journal page. Every marginal note. He wasn't prospecting, Gideon. He was keeping a promise to a dead man. And the compass—" she turned it in her fingers, "—Elias tuned it himself. To point at the vent. Because he wanted whoever came after him to know that the first name on this story wasn't his."

I thought about all the miles I'd walked with that brass disc against my heart, following a needle that hadn't been leading me north or even leading me to safety, but leading me to a grave. Two graves, it turned out. One in the mountain, one in the man who'd bled out under a rail bridge and pressed a folded map into my hands because his daughter was a day too far away and strangers were all he had left.

"He chose you," Wren said quietly, "because you were the kind of man who stops for people bleeding in the rain. That's what the last letter says. Not brave. Not clever. Just — the stopping kind." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Don't let it go to your head."

"Too late," I said, and she almost smiled, which from Wren was practically a parade.

* * *

We went up the mountain in June, when the melt had gentled and the high trails were open. Odile provisioned us like we were going to war — hard bread, smoked fish, a jar of the fierce plum spirit she distilled that could strip paint or grief, whichever you needed stripped worse. The kids made us take a bundle of letters they'd written, "for the summit," Tess said solemnly, as though the mountain collected mail.

The climb was different without Syndicate searchlights raking the ridgelines. Different without the debt riding my shoulders like a second pack. We moved at the pace of the country instead of the pace of fear, and I learned that Wren sang when she thought she was alone — low, wordless, something her father must have hummed over a thousand campfires — and I learned that I slept better above the treeline than I ever had in any city bed, with the whole dark sky pressing down soft as a blanket.

The caldera, when we reached it, was quiet.

That was the strangest part. I'd carried the image of it through the whole descent from the summit — the steaming lake, the humming infrastructure, the great tapped artery of the earth wired into the Syndicate's secret machine. Now there was only the lake itself, steaming gently in the cold air like a cup of tea forgotten on a windowsill, ringed by raw rock where their works had been torn out. The vent breathed slow and free into the open sky, a white feather of vapor rising and unraveling, rising and unraveling. Nothing harnessing it. Nothing hiding it. Just the mountain, doing what mountains do.

"It's beautiful," Wren said, and her voice cracked on it, and I understood that she was seeing her father here, and Silas Vane, and thirty years of a promise finally kept.

We built a small cairn at the lake's edge, the way river folk do, and set the letters from the kids inside it, weighted with stones, because Tess had insisted the summit should know it was forgiven.

Then I took out my knife.

I'd been carrying a flat piece of copper salvaged from the ruined works — a plate off some Syndicate gauge, its stamped numbers filed smooth. I'd filed it for days on the journey up, though I'd told no one why. Now I braced it against my knee and carved, slow and deep, the way you carve a thing you mean to outlast you:

**ELIAS MARSH**
**SILAS VANE**

And beneath them, after a moment's hesitation that Wren pretended not to notice:

**GIDEON WHITLOCK**
**WREN MARSH**

Four names. The ones who found it first, the one who finished it, and the two fools who burned the map so it could never be owned again. I set the plate into the cairn's crown and stepped back, and the wind came off the lake warm and mineral-smelling and moved through the steam like something exhaling after a very long time underwater.

Wren put her hand on the cairn. "Tell him," she said — to the mountain, to her father, to both — "that we stopped."

I didn't say anything. Some prayers are better said with a knife and a steady hand.

* * *

Storm season came back in July, the way it always does, rolling up the valley in towering violet stacks with lightning walking between the cells like a giant taking long strides. I stood on Odile's porch the first evening of it with a cup of coffee going cold in my hands, and I'll be honest with you — the wanting hit me like it always did. That old pull in the chest. The sky opening its mouth and every animal instinct saying *run* while the older, stupider instinct says *follow.*

"You're doing the face," Odile said from behind me.

"What face?"

"The face of a man about to make a bad decision beautifully." She handed me a canvas satchel, packed without my asking: bread, cheese, the plum spirit, a coil of line. "Take the skiff. Take the boy — Tess has been insufferable since dawn, asking if the green sky means today. And Gideon." She fixed me with the look she used on river barons and children alike, and it worked equally well on both. "Come back before dark. I'm not raising another stray."

"I'm nobody's stray."

"Mm," she said, and shut the door on me, which is Odile's way of saying *be safe* without surrendering the dignity of either party.

Tess was already in the skiff, of course, sitting straight-backed at the tiller like a small admiral, wearing the oilskin jacket that had belonged to Corvus — because that was another story, how the man who'd hunted us up the whole length of the river ended up mending nets at Callow's Ford and teaching the kids card games with a house deck he always, always lost with. Thirty years serving men who erased towns, and now he spent his evenings being beaten at cards by twelve-year-olds, and by his own account it was the best work he'd ever done.

"Southwest cell," Tess said, pointing with her chin, all business. "Building fast. Wind's backing."

"Wind's backing," I agreed, and something in my chest went easy and wide. This was the inheritance I actually had to give — not maps, not debts, not secrets worth killing for. Just the reading. The sky's grammar, the water's grammar, passed hand to hand like a lantern. Elias had passed his to me in the rain under a bridge. I passed mine to a girl on a raft, and someday she'd pass it to somebody bleeding out somewhere, because that was how the river worked. Everything downstream of everything else.

We ran the storm the proper way that evening — not chasing it into ruin but running alongside it, reading its edges, letting the first fat drops hit the water around us like applause. Behind us, I heard the deeper knock of oars in locks, and I turned, and I shouldn't have been surprised, but I was, the way you're always surprised by the things you most hoped for.

The big raft was coming off the bank. Odile at the stern sweep, unapologetic. Wren mid-raft with a pole, hair whipping, grinning like the storm had personally invited her. Half the kids of Callow's Ford arrayed along the gunwales in their mismatched oilskins, hollering at the lightning like it was a horse race. Even Corvus, perched amidships with a thermos and an expression of profound resignation that fooled absolutely no one, because he'd rowed hard enough to catch us.

The storm towered over the western ridge, purple-black and magnificent, throwing shadows across the whole valley, and the river ran silver ahead of us into the heart of it, and the raft behind me was full — full of people who knew my worst ledger lines and had offered to pay them anyway, full of kids who could read water, full of a woman whose father's name I'd carved into copper so it would outlast us all.

"Where are we going?" Tess shouted over the wind.

I looked at the compass one last time — southeast, steady, pointing at nothing and everything, pointing at home — and then I put it away forever and took the yoke.

"Nowhere," I called back. "Just follow the weather."

And we did, all of us, into the rain.
