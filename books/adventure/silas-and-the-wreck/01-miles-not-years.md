## 1. Miles, Not Years

Casper Ravensworth measured his life in kilometers now. It was a discipline, like prayer or penance, and he kept it the way other men kept calendars.

Fourteen kilometers from the depot at Lowmarsh to the first bend. Nine more to the narrows where the valley pinched shut and the current went sullen and deep. Eleven after that to the survey staging ground at Tallow Flats, if the sandbar hadn't shifted again—it always shifted—and then a final four of dead water where the river spread wide and lazy and you rowed with your shoulders burning just to keep moving.

Thirty-eight kilometers, give or take the river's mood. He'd counted them so many times he could feel them in his arms like a second pulse.

It was better than counting years. Years had weight. Years had names in them—Marta, the expedition in the Karakoram, the inquiry, the second inquiry, the letter that began *the licensing authority regrets to inform*. Kilometers had no names. A kilometer was just water gone past the hull, and there was always more water.

He loaded the last of the survey crates at dawn, before the heat came up off the banks and turned the valley into a kettle. Aluminum cases stenciled GEODETIC SURVEY — PROPERTY OF THE PROVINCIAL MINISTRY, each one heavier than it needed to be, packed by clerks who had never carried anything farther than a parking lot. Fuel drums. Rice and tinned fish. A crate marked OPTICAL that he'd been told not to drop, as though he were the sort of man who dropped things.

He wasn't. That was rather the whole tragedy of him. Casper Ravensworth had spent nineteen years in mountains and on rivers being precisely the man you wanted when everything went wrong, and then one bad day in another country's high country had made him the man nobody would hire at all. Two governments had seen fit to erase him. The paperwork had been very thorough. He'd read it all during the appeal, every clause, and understood finally that the truth of what happened mattered far less than the tidiness of the account. Someone senior had needed a name to hang the disaster on, and his name had been the right length.

So now he ferried supplies for a subcontractor who paid cash and asked nothing, up a river valley so remote that the maps themselves seemed embarrassed by it, and he practiced invisibility the way he'd once practiced ice-climbing footwork: daily, deliberately, until it became instinct.

The rules were simple. Be competent enough that no one remembers you. Be quiet enough that no one looks twice. Never correct a client, even when the client is wrong about the river—especially then. Take the money. Don't sign anything. And never, under any circumstances, tell anyone who you used to be.

He pushed off from the mud at 5:40 by his father's old watch, which kept poor time but honest time, and the current took the boat like a hand taking a shoulder.

* * *

The morning was the kind the valley did best: mist standing in the flooded forest on both banks, herons stalking the shallows like patient creditors, the water the color of strong tea. Somewhere back in the trees a gibbon started its rising call and was answered by another, and then a third, the sound bouncing down the valley like laughter in an empty house.

Casper rowed and counted.

Kilometer six. The drowned acacia where the kingfishers nested, blue sparks in the gray light. Kilometer nine, the bend where the current swung wide and you hugged the cut bank or lost twenty minutes. He knew the river the way he'd once known a glacier—by its moods, its lies, its small betrayals. Rivers lied constantly. They told you they were slow when they were deep, told you they were safe when the bar beneath had moved in the night. You survived them by refusing to believe anything except what your hands told you through the shaft of an oar.

His hands told him this morning was ordinary, and he was grateful for it. Ordinary was the whole of his ambition now. Ordinary food, ordinary work, ordinary exhaustion at the end of the day. He had learned that grief doesn't leave; it just gets quieter, the way a river gets quieter when it goes underground. Marta had been gone five years this spring. Some mornings he still woke reaching across cold canvas for warmth that wasn't there, and those mornings he got on the water early and rowed until his arms shook and the counting took over everything.

Two hundred strokes to the kilometer, roughly, against the current. He'd worked it out the first month. Sometimes he did the arithmetic aloud, a muttering rosary: *two hundred here, two hundred there.* The depot man thought he was odd. The depot man was welcome to his opinion. Odd men got left alone, and being left alone was the closest thing to peace the world still sold.

At the narrows he rested, drifting, and ate a handful of rice and dried fish wrapped in banana leaf, and watched the mist burn off the water. That was when he saw the boat.

Not a real boat—a government skiff, aluminum, Ministry stencil on the bow, tied badly to a root on the near bank with a painter long enough to let it grind against the bark with every swell. Nobody aboard. Nobody on the bank. The mist was nearly gone and there was nowhere a person could have walked without showing, and there was no path anyway, not on that side. Just wall-to-wall flooded forest going back dark and endless toward hills that didn't appear on any map he'd been paid to know.

Government boats did not tie up badly. Government boats did not tie up empty. Every instinct he owned—the ones that had kept clients alive on three continents—stood up inside him and said *turn around.*

He almost did. That was the truth of it, and he'd have admitted it later only to Silas, much later, laughing: he put his oar in the water to swing the bow downstream, and the current, obliging thing, carried him instead along the bank close enough to see into the skiff.

Empty. But not stripped. There was a dry bag in it, a thermos on the thwart, a coil of rope someone had meant to use soon. Abandoned the way you abandon a thing when you mean to come back in ten minutes—not the way you abandon a thing when you're gone forever.

Except nobody was coming back. He could feel that the way you feel weather in a healed fracture. Ten minutes ago on this river had been hours ago at least, maybe a day, and whatever ten-minute errand had pulled whoever-it-was up that bank, it hadn't gone the way errands go.

Casper sat with his oar shipped and listened. Water. Birds. Far off, the gibbons again, unhurried. Nothing else.

"Hello the boat," he said, because protocol said to say it, because saying it made him a ferryman and not a ghost.

The valley said nothing back.

He should have left it there. He wants me to say he should have left it there, some future version of him thought, writing the memoir he'd never write. He tied his own line to the root beside the skiff, which was the single most visible thing he had done in five years, and stepped onto the mud.

* * *

The camp was forty minutes upstream on foot, which meant someone had come downriver from it, which meant the skiff was the camp's boat, which meant the camp was the survey staging ground at Tallow Flats—his destination, the place he was contracted to deliver these very crates to.

That was the argument he made to himself, poling through the flooded scrub with the water at his knees and rising: *I'm going there anyway. This is on the way. Looking costs nothing.*

Looking cost something. Looking always cost something. But he was already wet to the waist, and a man will justify almost anything once he's committed his boots.

The camp sat on the one patch of high ground in a kilometer of swamp, a shelf of clay under a stand of kapok trees, and it was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name. He stood at the edge of it for a full minute, letting his eyes do the work his mouth couldn't.

Three tents, pitched properly, guyed well—these were people who knew fieldcraft. A folding table with a map weighted at the corners by stones. A stove, cold. Mess kits laid out.

And meals half-eaten. That was the first wrongness. Rice congealed in two bowls, a tin of fish opened and abandoned with half its contents drying in the sun. People finishing a meal is routine. People leaving a meal mid-bite is an event. Fire alarm, snake in the tent, man overboard—one of those things brings you up out of your bowl, but it does not usually bring you up out of your boots.

Because the boots were there. All of them. Six pairs, lined up along the base of the tents with the laces tucked inside, neat as a shop display. Rubber sandals too. Every stitch of footwear in the camp, arranged in rows, while the owners had gone somewhere barefoot or booted-and-gone-in-a-hurry—but no, if they'd gone in a hurry they wouldn't have arranged the boots. If they'd gone slowly they'd have taken the boots.

Nobody walks into flooded forest barefoot. Nobody leaves six pairs of boots lined up neat as soldiers unless they've been made to, or unless—

Unless someone else arranged them. Someone who wanted the camp to look tidy. Looked-after. *Nothing to see here.*

Casper crouched at the edge of the clay shelf and studied the ground the way he'd once taught novices to study snow. Mud everywhere, and mud keeps records. The team's own prints crisscrossed the camp in a normal week's worth of traffic. At the upstream end, where a game trail climbed away toward higher ground, the prints simply stopped—stopped cleanly, as if the last walker had been lifted off the earth. No prints leaving. No prints arriving since. Downstream, toward the skiff, one set of fresh prints in the last day's rain-slick: a single person, heavy, deliberate, walking down to the boat and back up again. Government-issue tread. He'd hauled enough Ministry crates to know their soles the way you know a signature.

One man had come back for the boat. One man had taken the skiff—or a skiff—and left. And six people had vanished upward into forest that floods first and deepest when the monsoon comes, leaving their boots behind like a confession.

He walked the camp slowly, touching nothing, breathing through his mouth. On the table, under the stones, the map. Official chart of the valley, the kind printed by the same ministry whose crates filled his hold, and it showed the tributary entering the main river eight kilometers north as a dashed blue line labeled SEASONAL, NON-NAVIGABLE.

Except someone had torn the map. Torn it deliberately, a clean vertical rip, removing exactly the strip of paper where the dashed blue line ran up into the hills—and then, oddly, wedged the torn piece back into place, misaligned by a centimeter, like a page put back by someone in a hurry or someone who wanted it found slightly wrong.

Casper lifted the corner with one finger. Beneath the repositioned scrap, in pencil, faint, someone had written two words in a hurried engineer's hand:

*NOT SEASONAL.*

Underlined twice.

He stood there a long moment with the map corner between finger and thumb, feeling the shape of it settle over him like the mist had settled over the water that morning. A survey team charts a tributary that official maps insist doesn't really exist. The team reports something about it. The team disappears, leaving boots and half-eaten rice and a map defaced in a way that says *look here* and *don't look here* in the same breath.

And I am the only man on this river, thought Casper Ravensworth, invisible man, careful man, counter of kilometers. The only man close enough to matter, and the only man with no one who would notice if he turned his boat around and paddled until the world forgot the question.

Turn around. Load the crates at Tallow Flats—if there was anyone at Tallow Flats to receive them, which there was not, because Tallow Flats was this—deliver them back to the depot, collect his pay, mention to the depot man that the camp looked empty, let it become someone else's problem at the end of a telephone line that may or may not work. That was the play. That was the smart play. That was the play of a man who had learned, expensively, what happened when you inserted yourself into other people's disasters.

He turned around. He actually walked back down the trail toward the boats, mud sucking at his sandals, rehearsing the phone call in his head. *Camp appears abandoned. Recommend follow-up.* Four words. Neutral. Involving nothing.

Then the radio spoke.

* * *

It was a thin sound at first, buried under birdsong, and he stopped dead in the trail with his head cocked like a dog's. A crackle. Then a voice, tiny and looped and mechanical, coming from the big tent.

He found it on the camp table inside: a field transceiver, Ministry issue, running off a solar panel rigged outside the fly. Someone had left it powered on, volume low, tuned to the provincial emergency band. It wasn't receiving. It was transmitting—a recorded message, set to repeat, the way you set a message to repeat when you can't stay by the radio and you're praying someone, anyone, is listening.

A woman's voice. Tired, clipped, professional, fraying at the edges.

"—any station, any station, this is Survey Team Four, Osei leading. We are moving to high ground northeast of the staging camp. Repeat, northeast, along the unnamed tributary. Our position instruments are—" static, a breath, "—they've taken our boat and our radio spares. If any commercial traffic is on the river, we are six adults, minimal supplies, monsoon approaching. Any station, please acknowledge. Please acknowledge. Please—"

The recording looped. Started over. *Any station, any station.*

Casper stood in the doorway of the tent with the canvas cool against his shoulders and felt something crack open in his chest that had been sealed a long time.

Six people. Barefoot in a flooding forest, because someone had taken their boat. Their lead engineer broadcasting into the empty air on a loop, because she had nothing else, because she believed—she had to believe—that the river was full of traffic, that the world was watching, that someone would hear.

The river was not full of traffic. Casper knew that better than anyone alive. He had run this stretch eleven times this season and passed two fishing dugouts and a crocodile. The world was not watching. The world had specifically, formally, with great administrative care, stopped watching him. He knew exactly how it felt to broadcast into silence and get silence back. He had done it for five years—to inquiries, to appeals, to former colleagues who crossed the street when they saw him coming. *Any station, any station. Please acknowledge.* And the answer had always been static.

His instinct was still to paddle away. It was a good instinct. It had kept him alive and free and un-jailed through half a decade of careful nothing. Every scar on his judgment said: *this is not your disaster. Your disasters are finished. You paid for them in full.*

But the woman's voice kept looping, and the loop caught on something in him that he'd thought was dead. Some old obligation, some muscle of the trade—the one that said a person in trouble on your mountain, on your river, in your weather, is yours. Not legally. Legally he owed these people a delivery of rice and fuel drums. But the other law, the unwritten one, the one that had made him good once, before good got expensive—that law had just spoken up, in a tired woman's voice, from a solar-powered radio in an empty tent.

*Please acknowledge.*

He looked down at his hands, rough and steady on the doorframe of the tent, and understood that they had already decided. Hands decide faster than heads. His hands had been reaching for rivers since he was nine years old.

He keyed the transmit toggle, because answering cost nothing and changed nothing and was, at minimum, the decent thing.

"Survey Team Four, this is an independent vessel on the main channel." He kept his voice flat, uninflected, a stranger's voice. "I have found your camp. I have your message. I am—" and here the sentence snagged, because the next word was *coming*, and *coming* was a door he could not walk back through, and every careful rule he lived by stood in front of it with its arms out.

Static breathed in the tent. The loop waited to begin again.

Northeast, she'd said. Along the unnamed tributary. The one the map tore out. The one labeled *not seasonal* in pencil by a hand that would never write anything again, unless—

Monsoon season was eleven days out, maybe fewer. The tributary would flood first. Whatever high ground six barefoot people were walking toward would become an island, and then not even that.

Casper closed his eyes and counted, not kilometers this time. One breath. Two. Three.

Then he keyed the radio one more time, and heard himself say the word that ended the life he'd been living, quietly, at dawn, thirty-eight kilometers from anywhere.

"Acknowledged. I'm coming northeast."

Outside, the mist had burned off completely, and the river lay bright and indifferent in the sun, already carrying his boat's reflection downstream without him—as if it knew.
