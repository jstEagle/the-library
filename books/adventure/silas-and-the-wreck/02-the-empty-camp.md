## 2. The Empty Camp

The radio was still transmitting when Casper reached the bend where the survey team's last known position sat marked on his manifest. That was the first wrong thing. A team that had packed out—or been carried out—did not leave a base station running on battery reserve for three days, bleeding its charge into the wet air like a lighthouse nobody tended.

He tied off at a gravel shoal below the camp and sat in the boat for a long moment, paddle across his knees, listening. The river made its usual arguments—water over stone, wind combing the alders, a kingfisher somewhere upstream rattling like a loose bolt. Underneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, came the radio's carrier tone. Steady. Patient. Wrong.

Casper had built a life on knowing which wrong things to walk away from. Two countries had taught him the lesson at the cost of everything he'd owned: a name, a license, a set of climbing racks he'd left in a Chamonix locker because shipping them meant putting his real surname on a customs form. He'd learned to read a situation the way he read water—the eddy that looked safe and held a strainer, the calm reach that funneled into a hole you couldn't see until you were in it. And every instinct he had, honed by five years of careful invisibility, told him this camp was an eddy with something under the surface.

But he'd also taken the contract. Fourteen days ferrying survey supplies up the Vell, resupplying a government hydrology team working the upper gorge, and then back down before the monsoon closed the valley. The pay was unremarkable. The work was quiet. That was why he'd taken it—and why the quiet mattered now. If the team had simply moved camp without filing a notice, someone downstream would sort it out in a week. If they hadn't moved—

He got out of the boat.

* * *

The trail from the shoal climbed through fern and slick clay, maybe four hundred meters, switchbacking up a shelf above the river. Casper counted his steps without meaning to. It was an old habit, one of the small arithmetic tricks he used to keep grief in its box—counting miles instead of years, steps instead of anniversaries, rope lengths instead of names. Four hundred meters of counting gave him just enough silence in his own head to arrive at the treeline steady.

The camp sat on the flat shelf like something set down by a hand that had been interrupted mid-motion.

Two dome tents, guyed out properly, rainflies on. A mess table under a tarp, and on it, breakfast—or what had been breakfast, some days ago now. Three enamel plates. One still held half a portion of rice congealed under a skin of mold-fuzz. A mug of tea gone to black sludge, a spoon standing upright in it. Nobody eats half a meal and walks away clean. Nobody leaves a spoon standing in their tea unless they stood up fast, or were made to.

Casper crouched at the edge of the clearing and did what he'd been trained to do twenty years ago, back when training had still meant something and his name opened doors: he read the ground.

No scuffing. No drag marks. The clay here held prints the way wax holds seals, and the prints told a story in three parts. First, ordinary camp traffic—booted feet crossing and recrossing between tents and latrine trench and river path, days' worth, layered. Second, a period of stillness—the topmost older prints softened at their edges by at least two nights of dew, meaning no one had walked there since. Third, and this was the part that raised the hair on his arms: nothing leading out.

Every direction a person could leave that clearing on foot showed either virgin leaf litter or his own fresh prints. No trail beaten down toward the tributary mouth. No tracks toward the gorge. No sign of a scramble down the bluff to the main channel. Six people—at least six; he counted six sleeping rolls, six plates stacked beside the mess tarp, though only three had food on them—six people had stopped existing at that camp without walking out.

Boats, then. Except his manifest said the team's two inflatable survey craft were both accounted for at the lower depot, waiting on motor repairs. He'd ferried the parts himself, ten days ago, to a mechanic who'd complained the whole shipment was a week late because the team kept requesting gear be sent *down* to them instead of up.

Down. They'd been sending equipment downriver. Away from their work site.

Casper stood slowly and let his eyes do a second pass, wider this time. That was when he saw the boots.

They were paired. Neatly, deliberately paired, laces tucked inside, set in a row against the base of the big strangler fig at the clearing's north edge. Seven pairs. Hiking boots, rubber river shoes, a pair of leather approach shoes worn nearly through at the outer heel. Paired boots are what you do at the end of a day, or at the start of a swim. But seven pairs, arranged, aligned, toes all pointing the same direction—outward, toward the trees—that wasn't tidiness. That was arrangement. Someone had wanted whoever found this camp to see the boots. To count them. To understand: people were here, and people left without their boots, or people were made to leave without them, or—

Or someone wanted the camp to look abandoned in a particular way. Staged grief. A message written in footwear.

His mouth had gone dry. He made himself keep moving, methodical, hands in pockets where he could, touching nothing he didn't have to. In the lead tent he found sleeping bags rolled—not used-then-rolled but rolled tight, compression-strapped, the way gear is stored between deployments. A data case, empty, its foam cutouts bare. A satellite phone in its charging cradle, screen dark, battery dead—but the cradle's cable ran to a solar panel laid flat under the rainfly, angled wrong for the latitude, angled wrong for any sun that reached this shelf. It would trickle-charge nothing. Someone had set it up to fail, or set it up to look like it worked.

And clipped to the tent pole, still transmitting on battery reserve, the base station radio. Channel 6, the government working frequency. Every ninety seconds it squawked a location beacon—automated, he realized, checking the unit's settings panel. An automated distress beacon, armed, transmitting coordinates for a camp its owners had clearly not left voluntarily.

Someone had turned it on. Someone had wanted the team found.

* * *

Except.

Casper sat down hard on a supply crate near the fire ring and put his head in his hands for a moment, because the logic had split into two live wires and he could feel both of them.

If someone wanted the team found, fine. Distress beacon, staged camp, arranged boots—a search party comes upriver, finds evidence of disaster, concludes the team drowned or was taken, closes the file. That was a story. That was a cover story, dressed in enamel plates and paired boots.

But if someone didn't want the team found—if the point was that no one should ever come looking—then why arm a beacon? Why leave meals half-eaten in plain sight? You don't stage a scene you want nobody to visit.

Unless the staging wasn't for searchers. Unless it was for *him*. For whoever came next—for the resupply run, the contracted boatman, the expendable outsider who'd find the camp, draw the obvious conclusion, report it to the same officials who'd signed the manifests, and become part of the record. A witness who saw exactly what he was supposed to see and nothing else.

Casper had been that man before. Not this exact man, not in this exact valley, but the shape of it—he knew the shape. Five years ago, on a glacier highway in a country whose name he now avoided saying even to himself, a road crew had cut corners on avalanche control because a minister's brother-in-law owned the blasting contract, and when the slide came down and took eleven vehicles with it, the inquiry needed a mountain guide to blame. They found one. He'd given honest testimony about conditions he'd measured honestly, and his honesty had been repurposed, paragraph by paragraph, into negligence. Two countries' worth of licensing boards had passed the verdict along like a cold. He hadn't caused the disaster. He'd simply been the nearest man with a reputation, and reputations, he'd learned, exist to be spent.

So he knew what it felt like to stand in a place where the facts had been pre-arranged, where the scene itself was testimony, curated, and the curator was nowhere in sight.

He looked at the boots again. Seven pairs. His manifest listed a team of eight: team leader, two hydrologists, a geologist, a surveyor, a cook, a boat driver, and the engineer—Amara Osei, the name on the special-equipment requisitions, the sealed-sample permits, the correspondence he'd ferried in waterproof tubes without reading because reading other people's mail was one more form of visibility he couldn't afford.

Eight pairs of feet. Seven pairs of boots.

One pair missing. One person who'd walked out—or been taken out—shod.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when he found the map.

He'd gone through the lead tent a third time, slower, forcing himself past the dread the way you force yourself past a cramp on a long pull. Under the folded floor mat, in a sleeve of plastic intended for charts, he found a topographic sheet of the upper Vell gorge—the official government survey base map, the one printed from the national cartographic archive, the one whose authority was the whole point of printing it. It had been torn. Not damaged—torn, deliberately, along a ragged vertical line, and the piece that remained showed the main channel, the established camps, the benchmark stations.

The missing piece showed the east bank.

Along the tear line, in pencil, in a hurried hand, someone had written a single word and a bearing: *KETTLE — 064°.*

Casper knew the official chart of this valley better than most men knew their own kitchens. He'd spent eleven days running the Vell's length with the manifest open on his knee, and he'd spent three seasons before that running cargo for a timber outfit that had once tried, unsuccessfully, to get him to falsify draft readings. There was no tributary at bearing 064 from this camp on any chart he'd ever seen. The east bank was mapped as solid slope, forested, unbroken, draining via seeps too minor to name.

But rivers lie on paper. He'd learned that young, poling barges through delta country with an uncle who trusted the water and never the map. Slopes that looked solid from the main channel could hide a cut—a slot canyon, a spring-fed creek running deep and dark under canopy, navigable for a kilometer or ten at certain stages. Locals knew them. Charts didn't, because charts were drawn by men in boats who stayed in boats, and because sometimes charts weren't allowed to know things.

He remembered the mechanic's complaint. Gear being requested *downstream*, away from the work site. He remembered the empty sample case, sized for core tubes and sealed vials. He remembered the requisition tube he'd delivered two weeks back, addressed to Amara Osei, stamped with a classification band he'd pretended not to notice: *HYDROLOGICAL SURVEY — RESTRICTED DISTRIBUTION.*

A restricted hydrological survey, in a valley where the government had recently announced, with press releases and ribbon-cuttings, a new aggregate concession. Dredging rights. Gravel, sand, gold tailings rework—the unglamorous extractive industries that lived and died on one document: a water-quality baseline proving the river was already degraded, so whatever the operation added wouldn't legally matter. Baselines were done by survey teams. Survey teams took samples. Samples went into sealed cases.

The case in the tent was empty.

Casper stood in the failing light with the torn map in his hands and felt the dread in his chest change state—not cooling, not fading, but sharpening, condensing, the way fog turns to rain when the temperature drops. Dread was what you felt when you understood danger. This was something with a direction to it. This was resolve trying to be born, and he could feel it kicking.

Because here was the arithmetic, the blacklisted man's arithmetic, the only kind he trusted anymore:

Six people missing from a staged camp. Eight on the roster, seven pairs of boots. An empty evidence case. A beacon broadcasting a false story to anyone who came looking. A torn map pointing at water that officially did not exist. And a monsoon due in nineteen days that would flood every low channel in this valley, scour the banks, bury every track, dissolve every print, and turn the entire question into silt.

Whatever had happened here, the river was scheduled to erase it. That was how these things were done. He knew, because it had been done to him—time and weather and paperwork eroding the truth until only the official version stood above waterline. He had spent five years watching his own disaster disappear the same way, one expired record, one retired investigator, one flooded season at a time.

He could paddle away. Tomorrow morning, downstream, back to the depot, file a verbal report with the district officer—"camp found abandoned, recommend search"—and wash his hands of it, and be invisible again by Thursday. The report would go into a drawer. The drawer belonged to men whose signatures were on the dredging concession. The search, if it ever came, would come slow, and late, and after the rain.

Or he could go up that tributary at 064 degrees, alone, in a loaded boat, ahead of a monsoon, toward whatever had swallowed six people—and possibly toward the machinery that had ruined him once already, still running, still hungry.

Casper Ravensworth had gotten very good at surviving. Survival, he'd learned, was mostly the discipline of not looking at things. Don't read the classified bands. Don't ask why the timber company wanted false drafts. Don't wonder who signed the avalanche contract. Keep your head down, your name small, your wake narrow, and the years will pass over you like weather.

But the radio was still transmitting. Ninety seconds apart, patient, insistent, a machine doing the one honest thing anyone in this valley had done in weeks: telling the truth about where it was. Somebody had armed it. Maybe the team itself, in their last minutes—maybe the one who left shod, the eighth pair of feet, walking out barefoot or booted into the dark to trigger a rescue that never came. Maybe she'd run for the beacon the way you run for a lifeline, and someone had caught her, or hadn't, and the beacon had kept calling anyway, faithful and useless, for three days, to a valley full of people trained not to listen.

Casper listened.

He broke down his tarp half a kilometer downstream, in a hollow below the shoal where no light would show, and ate cold rice from his own stores, and checked the sky. High cirrus veiling from the west. The barometer needle he kept taped inside his dry bag had slipped two points since noon and was still falling. Nineteen days, the forecasters said. The river said less. Rivers always knew first.

In the morning he would lighten the boat—cache the nonessentials, the trade goods, the spare fuel drums, under a rock overhang he'd noted on the way up, marked on his own private chart in pencil no official would ever read. He'd keep the med kit, the rope, the tools, three days of food. He'd take the torn map, and the empty sample case, because evidence of absence was still evidence, and because if he vanished too, someone should find more than paired boots.

And at first light, he'd point the bow at bearing 064 and go find the water that wasn't on the map.

He fell asleep listening to the beacon, ninety seconds, ninety seconds, ninety seconds, a heartbeat in the dark. Somewhere out there, past the treeline, past the slope the charts refused to acknowledge, six people—or what remained of them—were waiting to be counted. And one pair of feet had walked toward the unmarked water, and Casper meant to follow.

The last thing he heard, half-dreaming, was the rain starting—not the monsoon, not yet, just the outriders, the first scouts of the season ticking against the leaves—and beneath it, fainter than faith, the beacon still calling. Still calling. Still calling.
