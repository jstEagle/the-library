## 3. A Stranger on the Water

Silas had three rules, and she'd made them herself, which meant they were the only rules in the world she could trust.

One: never sleep in the same place twice running. Two: never let anyone see how much food you have. Three: when a stranger comes upriver, watch from the reeds until you know what he's carrying — and I don't mean cargo.

The man in the patched green boat was breaking rule three for her, because he was carrying something she couldn't name from fifty meters of brown water and morning mist. Not supplies, or not only supplies. The crates were stacked wrong, too neat, like a man who'd learned that neatness kept things from shifting and hadn't yet learned that neatness also got you noticed. And he sat wrong for a ferryman. Ferrymen slouched. They leaned into the current like it was an old argument they'd stopped trying to win. This one sat forward, shoulders squared to the bow, reading the water the way Silas read it — not as a road but as a language.

That interested her. Interest was dangerous. She noted it and stayed in the reeds.

She'd been in the reeds since before dawn, since the light came up gray and the mist lay on the Kettle like a sheet over something sleeping. Her spot was good: a fallen fig tree half-sunk at the channel mouth, its roots braided into the bank, a curtain of cane behind it tall enough to hide a girl of twelve if the girl knew how to be still, which Silas did, better than she knew anything except water. Mosquitoes worked her neck and wrists. She let them. Rule four, unwritten: itching is information you can afford; being seen is not.

The boat came around Horseshoe Bend at half-paddle, and she heard it before she saw it — the particular knock of a shaft against a gunwale, once, twice, then corrected. A man learning his boat, or a boat learning its man. Then it slid into view, low in the water, riding heavy.

Supplies, then. Real ones. Rice tins by the look of the long crate, and something boxed and pale that might have been instruments, and a coil of rope new enough to still hold its store-bought shape. Government rope. Silas knew government rope because government rope ended up in the river after every survey run, cut loose or dropped or left, and she'd hauled enough of it out to lash her whole floating life together twice over.

But it was the man himself who kept her there past the point of sense.

He was big through the shoulders in the way of men who'd carried things for a living, with a face that looked like it had been arranged for a photograph nobody ever took — closed, careful, eyes doing all the work the mouth refused. Dark hair going gray at the temples. Hands on the paddle that were too sure for a beginner and too deliberate for a hired hand. He counted under his breath as he came through the bend. She couldn't hear the words but she could hear the rhythm, steady as a pump, and she understood it without wanting to: he wasn't counting strokes. He was counting distance. Some people prayed. Some people drank. This one measured.

Silas knew about measuring. You measured when the thing you were keeping out was bigger than the walls you had.

*Not my business,* she told herself, which was rule five, the one above all the others, the one that had kept her alive for two years since the church bell stopped meaning anyone would come. *Boats go upriver. Boats come downriver. None of them are yours.*

Then the boat turned toward the tributary.

She actually said it out loud, a breath of a word, *no*, before she could stop herself, and clapped a hand over her mouth as if the river might carry it upstream faster than the current ran down.

Nobody went up the tributary. That was the whole point of the tributary. It didn't exist — not on the charts, not on the lips of the ferry captains who ran the main channel, not in the memories of the officials whose boats came through twice a year with clean decks and clean boots and questions nobody answered twice. The maps showed reed marsh and dead ground where the tributary mouth opened like a held breath between two bluffs. Only the river people knew different, and the river people had agreed, without ever agreeing, without ever saying it in words, to keep it that way. Hidden channels were how the poor moved what the powerful had priced out of reach: salt, medicine, a cousin fleeing a debt, a body that needed to arrive somewhere without arriving from anywhere. The tributary was the valley's oldest lie, and Silas was one of maybe six living people who could read it end to end.

And this stranger, this counting man with his government rope, was pointing his bow straight at the mouth of it like it was printed on his chart.

Which meant either he had a chart nobody was supposed to have — or somebody had told him, and whoever told him wanted him to go in, and men who wanted other men to go into hidden water were never, in Silas's experience, wishing them well.

She should have let him go. Every rule she had said let him go. The tributary floods first — everyone river-born knew it, the way you knew your own pulse — because it ran shallow and narrow through country that drained slow, and when the monsoon came down off the highlands it filled like a throat before the main river even dreamed of rising. First rain had already come, the outriders ticking through the leaves last night. The season was turning. The water was about to become a liar.

She watched him work the boat toward the mouth anyway, and she saw the thing that decided her, though it took her years to understand why it did.

He stopped.

Fifty meters short of the mouth, in open current, with no marker and no reason any outsider could see, he backed water and just — stopped. Held position. Looked. He tilted his head the way her father used to, listening to the river instead of looking at it, and Silas realized he could hear it too: the difference in the sound where the hidden channel breathed out against the main current, a soft interference, a seam in the water's voice. Outsiders didn't hear the seam. Outsiders motored over it and called it nothing.

Her father had heard the seam. Her mother had heard the seam. Both of them were under it now, somewhere down in the cold silt where the survey boats had been working the night the river "took" them, and the word *accident* had come down from the capital wearing a suit and tie.

The man in the boat listened to the seam, and Silas, crouched in the reeds with mosquitoes drinking her blood, felt something crack open in her chest that she'd spent two years mortaring shut.

Because here was the arithmetic she lived by, laid bare: if strangers started finding the tributary, the tributary died. But if this stranger went up that channel alone, in monsoon season, in a boat riding that heavy — he died too, and whatever he carried died with him, and more to the point, whatever sent him would send another, and another, until the hidden water was hidden no longer. Or worse: he'd find what the surveys had been finding. He'd find the dredge lines. The spoil piles. The things her parents had seen that got them folded into the word *accident*.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited and stayed like a stray — he'd find the truth, and a man who could hear the seam might be a man who could carry it.

Rule six, invented on the spot, in defiance of rules one through five: sometimes you have to make yourself visible to keep something hidden.

Silas stepped out of the reeds.

* * *

She did it right, at least. She gave him time to see her coming — standing up slow on the sunken fig root, arms loose, empty hands turned outward, the universal grammar of *I'm not holding anything*. He spun fast for a big man, paddle coming up across the boat like a bar, and she noted that too: he held it like a weapon first and a tool second, and then, deliberately, she watched him make himself put it down. That cost him something. She respected costs.

"You're going to die up there," she called across the water.

The mist was burning off and the light came down the channel sideways, gold on brown, and she watched him take inventory of her the way she'd taken inventory of him: age, threat, exits. She stood where the bank fell away behind her into cane, so he'd know she had exits too. Fair's fair.

"That so," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow. Up close he was older than the river had made him look from the reeds — forty, maybe more, with lines around his eyes carved deep enough to lose coins in. "And you'd be?"

"The person who knows you're going to die up there." She jerked her chin at the tributary mouth, at the water lying flat and innocent between the bluffs. "That channel floods first. Monsoon's early this year. First rain came last night — you hear it? That's not rain, that's the scouts. Main river won't crest for nine days. That channel'll drown in three."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear, calculation, the same kind she did, and she hated how much it loosened something in her to see her own arithmetic running on someone else's face.

"You know the channel," he said. Not a question. Careful. "Officially, there's no channel."

"Officially," Silas said, "there's no me, either. Officially there's no stilt village past Kettle Bend, no fish traps above the falls, no funeral barges. Officially this whole valley is empty except for survey teams." She let that word sit out on the water between them and watched it land, watched his jaw tighten the way a man's does when a word hits a bruise he thought was healed. Oh, she thought. Oh, this one has history with surveys. "So if you're carrying official paper, mister, you're lost. And if you're not—"

"I'm ferrying supplies." Flat. Too flat. A door closing.

"To who? The team that's missing?" She saw the flinch, small, quickly killed, but she was Silas and she missed nothing on this river, nothing, it was the one inheritance her parents had left her besides their absence. "Word came down yesterday with the salt barge. Six people, no radio contact, camp found empty. Everyone downstream is saying drowned. Everyone downstream says drowned real easy these days." She took a step along the fig root, bare feet finding the wet wood without looking. "Except the beacon's still transmitting. Isn't it. You heard it."

Now he went very still, the stillness of a man caught, and Silas felt the strange hot pride of having caught him. "You've been listening," he said.

"Ninety-second interval, low band, weak signal." She shrugged. "It repeats. Beacons repeat. Dead men's beacons especially — they're set to go off on impact or immersion, so either it's broken and lying, or—" she stopped herself. Said the rest softer. "Or somebody left it running on purpose. Like a note."

The mist was nearly gone now. A kingfisher went down the channel like a thrown blue knife. The man looked at her a long moment, and she watched him do the thing she was doing, weigh the stranger on the other side of the water, and she understood suddenly that they were both doing the same sum with different numbers: *what will this person cost me, and what might they be worth.*

"What do you want?" he asked finally. Blunt. She approved of blunt. Blunt was honest currency.

"Passage," she said. "Up and back. I take you up the channel, get you past the shallows and the deadfalls and the snag field at the narrows where the water reads backwards, and I get you out again before it drowns. In exchange: food. Rice tins, two of them, and the box of tea if there is one. And you tell me what you find up there."

His eyebrows came down. "Why the last part?"

"Because if you find bodies, I need to know if I knew them." She said it level, and it cost her more than the mosquitoes had, and she watched him hear the cost in it, because his face did a thing — softened and sharpened at once, like he'd recognized something. "And if you find something that isn't bodies, I need to know that too. Because my parents worked the river, mister, and the last survey that came through took them, and the capital called it an accident, and I have spent two years being officially nobody on an officially empty river, and I am done pretending the water doesn't talk just because adults cover their ears."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the cane a dove called, three notes, the valley's oldest clock. The man looked at the tributary mouth, then at her, then at the sky where the outrider clouds were stacking up over the highlands like furniture being moved into an upstairs room.

"It'll be dangerous," he said.

"It's always dangerous," Silas said. "Dangerous is free. Being ignorant is what costs."

Almost — almost — he smiled. It didn't finish the journey, but it started, a movement in the beard like a fish rising and turning away. He shipped the paddle and considered her across the shrinking gap of water.

"How old are you?" he asked.

"Old enough to know the channel. Young enough to be cheap." She held out her hand, palm up, an old trader's gesture her mother had taught her before teaching her anything else. "Food first. Then I show you the mouth. You can't find it yourself — you'd have found the seam eventually, but the seam isn't the entrance, the seam is the decoy. There's a sandbar that lies about being deep, and behind it the real cut, and if you take the seam you'll hole your boat on stakes some clever grandfather drove sixty years ago to keep official boats official."

He stared at her. "Stakes."

"River keeps its own defenses," Silas said. "We just maintain them."

Another long look. Then he reached into the stern, came up with a rice tin, and tossed it underhand across the water. She caught it against her chest — heavier than she'd guessed, full, the good kind with the government stamp pried off — and the weight of it in her arms was so absurdly, stupidly wonderful that she had to look away at the channel for a second to keep her face in order. Two days since she'd eaten properly. Three since she'd eaten at all.

"Casper," he said. "Casper Ravensworth."

She weighed the tin in one hand, testing, and gave him back the only part of herself that mattered out here, the only name the river needed.

"Silas."

"Like the book?"

"Like the boy in it," she said. "My mother liked that he heard things in the night. Now — eat something and bail your bilge, your boat's riding two fingers low on the port side, and we go while the light's still high enough to read the water." She was already moving along the fig root toward her own hidden skiff, cached in the cane, heart hammering with a feeling she refused to name because naming it would make it breakable. Rule seven, newest of all, written in the wet ink of the moment: *you can steer a stranger, but you cannot steer what a stranger wakes up in you.*

Behind her, she heard him moving in the boat, and then his voice again, quieter, pitched across the water like a question he was asking himself as much as her:

"Three days, you said. Before it drowns."

Silas paused at the cane line and looked back at him — at the big careful man with the counting rhythm and the closed face and the beacon still whispering its ninety seconds somewhere in his gear, calling and calling to people who might be beyond answering.

"Two and a half," she said. "Monsoon's early. Everything this year is early."

She pushed her skiff out of the cane, and together — the patched green boat and the small gray one, the man who measured miles and the girl who measured everything else — they turned toward the mouth of the water that wasn't there.
