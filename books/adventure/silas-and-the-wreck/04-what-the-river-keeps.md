## 4. What the River Keeps

The tributary didn't appear on any chart Casper had ever bought, stolen, or memorized, and that was precisely how he knew it mattered.

Silas stood in the bow of his patched workboat with one bare foot hooked over the gunwale and a pole in her hands, reading the water the way other people read newspapers—quickly, dismissively, with occasional contempt. She was maybe eleven, maybe thirteen; river kids aged by competence rather than years. She'd bargained her way aboard for two tins of fish and the promise of a third, and she'd already eaten one tin with the unselfconscious efficiency of someone who had never once been certain where the next meal was coming from.

"There." She pointed the pole at what looked to Casper like an unbroken wall of green.

He saw nothing. Then he saw it: a seam in the vegetation, barely wider than the boat, where the current ran backward against itself—the telltale of a side channel pulling water out of the main river into somewhere hidden. The entrance was screened by a curtain of hanging bamboo so dense it looked grown on purpose.

"That's not on the survey maps," he said.

"Maps are for people who pay for them." Silas said it without bitterness, as if stating the temperature of the air. "Government men come up the big river, they go where the big river goes. Nobody comes here unless somebody showed them."

"Somebody showed the survey team."

She looked back at him then, and her eyes were old in a way that made him want to look away first. "Then we go quiet," she said. "This channel hears everything."

* * *

They poled through the bamboo curtain in single file silence, Casper at the stern, the boat's hull whispering along water the color of steeped tea. The tributary was narrower than it had seemed from outside—a throat of a river, canopied so completely that the light came down green and dappled, like being underwater and breathing anyway. Orchids hung from branches that leaned out over the current. A kingfisher detonated off a snag ahead of them and was gone.

Casper counted. He always counted. Kilometers since the last bend, strokes between obstacles, seconds of silence between birdcalls. Counting was how he kept the grief in its cage—because grief, he'd learned, was mostly just time that wouldn't stay organized. Five years since Kestrel Pass. Two countries' worth of blacklisting. Three thousand-odd kilometers of river since anyone had spoken his name without checking it against a list first.

"You count when you're scared," Silas observed, not turning around.

"I count when I'm thinking."

"You're counting your strokes. I can hear your lips moving. My mother did that too, before—" She stopped herself with visible effort, jaw tightening, and jabbed the pole at a submerged log. "Rocks there. Go left."

Left of the rocks the channel widened into a flooded gallery of trees, their trunks standing knee-deep in still water, and the quality of the silence changed. It wasn't empty silence. It was the held-breath silence of a place where something had happened and the forest hadn't finished deciding what to think about it.

Silas felt it too. He watched her shoulders drop half an inch, her grip shift on the pole from working hold to weapon hold. She was, he realized, afraid—and she was staying anyway, which told him something about her that no amount of bargaining over fish tins would have.

"The camp is past the split," she said quietly. "Where the water goes two ways. That's where the government boats turned back last month. They came up here looking, you know. Big aluminum boat, four men, all looking at their instruments instead of the water." Her mouth curled. "They went up the wrong fork. This one doesn't show on their machines because the canopy—"

"—breaks the satellite return," Casper finished. "Yeah."

"You know machines."

"I used to know mountains. Same principle. Everything hides if it wants to badly enough."

At the split, Silas chose without hesitating, and Casper followed her choice into a stretch of channel so narrow the branches combed his hair. And then the trees opened, and there it was.

* * *

The camp sat on a low shelf of ground above the water, and it was wrong in every particular.

Not destroyed—abandoned. Not ransacked—arranged. A dome tent stood zipped and staked, its fly still cinched tight against rain that hadn't come. A mess kit hung from a ridge line, cups inverted to drain, exactly as a careful field team would leave them for a short reconnaissance on foot. A pair of boots sat paired at the tent door, laces tucked in. Neat. Tidy. The kind of neatness that takes discipline, or takes staging.

Casper grounded the boat and stepped onto the bank, and Silas followed without being told to stay behind, which he'd learned in three days of her company was a waste of breath.

"No tracks," she said immediately, crouching at the edge of the camp's cleared ground. She spread her fingers above the leaf litter without touching it. "Look. No one walked out. No one walked in, even. The ground's been swept." She picked up a frond and demonstrated, dragging it lightly across the soil. "Like this. Somebody brushed it all smooth."

Casper crouched beside her. She was right. The entire clearing wore the same soft, featureless surface, like a bed made by someone hiding something under the mattress. His skin prickled. In the mountains, after Kestrel Pass, he'd spent weeks reconstructing a disaster from sign this faint—reading compression patterns in snow, the angle of broken brush—and the officials who'd ruined him had taken his careful readings and called them lies. *You claim there was no warning, Ravensworth. Yet you were the only one who failed to see it.* They hadn't wanted the truth. They'd wanted a shape they could pin a name to, and his name had been the right weight.

Someone had done to this clearing exactly what they'd tried to do to him: erased the record and left a story in its place.

Inside the tent, sleeping bags were rolled. A stove was cleaned. On the folding table, under a waterproof cover, lay the team's chart case—aluminum, government-stamped, locked—with a second, smaller case beside it, canvas, scuffed, unmarked. And on top of both, weighted down with a stone, a piece of paper folded once.

Silas read faster than he did. "It says: *If found, take everything downstream to the Provincial Water Authority. Do not trust regional offices. Do not use the radio.*" She frowned. "There's no radio here."

"It's transmitting," Casper said. "That's why I'm here."

He'd heard it two nights ago, moored downstream—a thin, patient electronic pulse on the emergency band, repeating coordinates, too weak to be a distress call, too regular to be an accident. He'd told himself he was paddling away from it. He'd told himself a lot of things. But the pulse had kept time with his strokes, and eventually he'd understood he wasn't going anywhere until he answered it.

Now he lifted the canvas case. It was heavier than it looked. The lock was a simple field latch, and it opened at the third try.

Field notebooks. Dozens of them, rubber-banded in bundles by date. Waterproof sample vials in padded racks, each labeled in small, disciplined handwriting. A sealed evidence envelope, official tape across the flap, signed and countersigned. And a journal—not a field notebook but a personal one, its cover soft with handling—that had been placed deliberately on top, open to a page near the end, as if whoever left it had wanted the finder to start exactly there.

Casper started exactly there.

*Day 41. Osei flagged the sediment cores again—benzene fractions way above anything the permits allow. We re-ran them twice to be sure. The dredging operation upstream isn't licensed for half of what they're pulling, and the discharge is going straight into the spawning channels. Hale's office signed the environmental clearance personally. I've seen the signature. It's not a stamp.*

*Day 44. Told the team we transmit our findings tonight. Rennick got a call on the sat phone an hour later. Afterward he wouldn't look at me. He said maybe we should consider that the regional office has "context" we don't. Context. As if poisoned water needs context.*

*Day 47. Someone came up the channel in the night. No engine. I counted three boats' worth of movement. I've moved the lead case to the cache and left this copy where a careful person might find it. If you're reading this, they were thorough, and I was slow. Take it downstream. Don't trust the radio—it's theirs now, broadcasting our position to whoever's listening. I'm sorry. We should have run sooner. Amara wanted to run. I told her the data would protect us.*

*I was wrong. The data is the reason. Nobody silences an empty report.*

The handwriting deteriorated across the last lines until the final sentence was more gouge than script. Below it, nothing. The journal simply stopped, mid-life, the way a clock stops.

Casper became aware that Silas was reading over his arm, and that she had gone very still, in the way of children who have learned that stillness is armor.

"Hale," she said. "Director Hale?"

"You know the name?"

"Everyone on the river knows the name." She said it flatly, but her knuckles were white on the gunwale. "He's the one who signs the papers that let the dredges come. My parents worked the barge season the year the 'survey accident' happened." She used the phrase like she was spitting out a seed. "That's what they called it. An accident. During a survey."

Casper looked at the journal, at the neat boots, at the smoothed earth, and felt the machinery of it turn over in his chest with a familiarity that was almost tender, the way an old scar aches in cold weather. He knew this machine. He had been fed into it once, gear by gear. It had a signature move: it didn't destroy the truth, it *relocated* it—into a closed file, into a blamed man, into a smoothed clearing—and then it posted guards around the emptiness and called the emptiness the official record.

Kestrel Pass had killed nine people. He had read the mountain correctly, filed the honest report, and woken up two countries later as the man who "failed to act on known hazards"—known, it turned out, to everyone except the report he'd written, which had been edited before it ever reached the inquiry. He'd spent five years learning to be invisible, ferrying supplies for men who paid cash and asked nothing, telling himself the valley was safer than the summit, that distance was peace.

And the whole time, the same machine had been running down here in the green dark, quieter, wetter, eating rivers instead of climbers, and orphaning girls who poled boats and counted other people's fear.

"They didn't get lost," Silas said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"They took them." Her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "Like they took my parents. Like they take anyone who sees the water straight."

Casper closed the journal carefully, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping. "We need to find the cache the engineer mentioned. The original samples. Copies can be disputed. Cores in sealed vials with chain-of-custody tape—" He stopped. Silas was staring at him.

"You talk different when you're working," she said. "Less counting. More sure."

"I'm not sure of anything."

"You are. You just haven't noticed yet." She pointed the pole downstream, toward the deepening green. "The cache. Engineers hide things near water they trust. There's a hollow bank past the dead fig tree—overhang, dry floor even in flood. If I were a scared scientist with boxes to hide, that's where I'd put them." A beat. "Also that's where the crocodiles sun themselves, so we go fast."

* * *

The dead fig tree was unmistakable—a silver skeleton leaning out over a cutbank, its roots exposed like the ribs of something long drowned. Beyond it, exactly as promised, the bank hollowed into a dry overhang floored with sand, shadowed, defensible, invisible from the channel unless you knew to look.

The crocodiles were absent. The cache was not.

Behind a screen of woven palm fronds—careful, professional, desperate hands—sat the lead case: aluminum, government-stamped, padlocked, and wrapped in a groundsheet tied with the kind of knots Casper had spent a decade teaching clients on glacier margins. Beside it, a second bundle: clothing, a cracked pair of eyeglasses, and a woman's field jacket with a name tape. *A. OSEI.* The jacket was stiff. Casper didn't let himself think about why.

Silas kept watch at the lip of the overhang while he broke the padlock with a pry bar from his repair kit—slowly, hating the noise, each click of the mechanism enormous in the green silence. Inside: the originals. Sealed core samples in foam racks, dozens of them, each vial taped, dated, initialed. Field notes under official seal. Photographs in waterproof sleeves—dredge barges working a tributary Casper recognized from yesterday's paddle, discharge pipes running brown plume into channels that should have run clear, and in the corner of one frame, caught accidentally, a man in a pressed shirt and clean boots standing on the barge deck reviewing paperwork while silt buried the spawning beds behind him.

Casper knew the face. Everyone who'd ever been processed through a regional permit office knew the face. Director Hale, smiling faintly at some function or other, in a photograph that had probably never been meant to exist.

"He signed the clearance," Casper said aloud. "Personally. And then he sent men up a hidden channel to erase the people who measured what he signed."

Silas crouched beside him and studied the photograph for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into something older than she was. "My mother said the river keeps everything. Every lie sinks eventually. You just have to know where to dig." She touched the edge of the photo, not quite covering Hale's face. "You dug."

"We dug." He repacked the case, hands steady, mind running arithmetic it didn't want to run. Because here was the ledger he'd been avoiding since the bamboo curtain: taking this downstream meant checkpoints, meant the regional offices the journal warned against, meant putting his name—blacklisted, discredited, pre-ruined—onto a document trail again. It meant becoming visible to the exact machine that had taught him invisibility was survival. Last time he'd told the truth, two countries had erased him for it. The arithmetic was simple and it had never once favored honesty.

But the journal's last line sat in him like a swallowed hook. *Nobody silences an empty report.* The team had died because they'd measured the water straight. Amara Osei—if she was alive, and the missing jacket argued she might not be—had trusted a stranger to find this. And a girl who traded passage for fish tins was crouched in the mud beside him, ready to pole straight into whatever came next, because her parents' names were somewhere in the same machine's gears.

"Casper." Silas's hand closed on his sleeve, and her eyes had gone to the channel beyond the overhang. Downstream, faint through the trees, came a sound he'd hoped not to hear for days yet: the low, unhurried thrum of an outboard motor, running quiet, running deliberate, coming up the hidden water where no chart said water existed.

Somebody had found the swept clearing. Somebody was following the only channel in or out.

Silas was already moving, silent as the current itself. "Kill your lantern," she breathed. "And pray your boat leaks slower than they search."
