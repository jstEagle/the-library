## 5. Blacklisted Man's Arithmetic

The fire was small enough to hide under his cupped hands, and that was the problem. A fire big enough to burn a sealed field case would be a fire big enough to see from the ridge line, and the ridge line was where men with clean boots liked to stand.

Casper crouched on the gravel bar below the deadfall where they'd made camp for the night, turning the lead case over in his lap. It was heavier than it looked—lead always was—and stamped with the provincial crest, the same crest that had appeared on the letterhead of two separate letters that had ended his life as he'd known it.

*Blacklisted from two countries.* People said it like a weather report when they thought he couldn't hear. The guide who walked away. The guide who let them die. Neither version true, neither version worth correcting, because correcting it would have required someone in authority to listen, and listening was the one thing authority had never done for him.

He set the case down and fed another stick to the flames.

Behind him, up on the dry shelf of rock under the deadfall's leaning trunk, Silas sat cross-legged with her knees drawn up, watching him the way she watched everything: without blinking enough. She'd eaten half the rice and given him the rest, which he suspected was less generosity than arithmetic—a boy who knew every hidden channel also knew exactly how many days of food remained, and had decided he needed the calories more than she did.

"You keep looking at it," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking since noon. You think like a man walking in circles." She picked a reed stem and stripped it between her teeth. "Burn it or don't."

"It's not that simple."

"Everything's that simple. People just don't like the answer."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was something in her bluntness that scraped against a place inside him he kept padded and dark, and the scraping hurt in a way that felt, faintly, like cleaning a wound.

* * *

Five years ago there had been a different fire, on a different river, in a country whose name he no longer said aloud.

They'd been four days into a descent of the Karst Gorge, six clients and a junior guide named Tomas, when Casper noticed the water. Not rising—that was expected, the snowmelt season—but *wrong*. Green-brown instead of glacier white. Warm. He'd put his hand in it at the lunch halt and known, the way a man knows a fever on a child's forehead, that something upstream had changed the river's nature.

He'd called it in. That was the part nobody believed afterward, the part that got sanded off every retelling until only the disaster remained. He'd climbed to the one spot with signal, stood on a ledge with the wind trying to take his hat, and reported an anomalous discharge upstream—possible dam test release, possible industrial outflow, requesting confirmation before proceeding through the narrows.

The voice on the radio had been calm and official and had told him, in words he could still recite, that hydrological data showed no anomaly, that the schedule was approved, that delays carried contractual penalties, and that Mr. Ravensworth should proceed.

So he proceeded. Because he was a professional, because professionals trust the chain of command, because the alternative was to tell six paying clients and his own junior guide that he knew better than the ministry's computers.

Eleven hours later the gorge ate three of them. Tomas among them. Twenty-three years old, a laugh like a door swinging open, gone into water that should have been cold and clean and wasn't.

The inquiry found what inquiries are built to find: operator error. His error. The discharge records were sealed. The ministry official who'd cleared him through the narrows testified with a straight face that no such call had been logged. Two countries' worth of licensing boards exchanged letters, and by winter Casper Ravensworth existed nowhere except on lists of people not to hire.

He'd tried telling the truth. He'd written letters, filed appeals, stood in a hearing room with his hands flat on a table while men in good suits explained to him, patiently, that his memory of the call was stress-induced confabulation. The truth had gone into the record and then out of the record, the way water goes into sand.

After that he'd learned the lesson this valley had already taught him: distance is safety. Miles, not years. Don't be seen. Don't be remembered. Ferry supplies, keep your head down, count kilometers instead of counting the dead.

And now here was a case full of paper that said the machinery hadn't changed. Had never changed. Just moved rivers.

* * *

"You knew them," Silas said. It wasn't quite a question.

He looked back at her. She'd stopped stripping the reed. In the firelight her face was all angles and patience, a face that had learned early not to waste motion.

"The survey team? No."

"Not them." She nodded at the case. "The ones who did this. You look at that box like you owe it money."

Casper turned back to the fire. Above them the deadfall creaked in a wind that was starting to carry weight in it—the first thickening of the air that meant the monsoon was gathering itself somewhere over the coast, counting down. They had maybe three days. Maybe less.

"There was an accident," he said. "Years ago. Before you were—" He stopped, recalculated. "A long time ago. I told the truth about it. Who I told didn't want to hear it. They took my license instead. Both countries I worked in."

Silas considered this with the gravity she gave everything. "So telling the truth got you nothing."

"It got me this boat and this valley."

"That's not nothing," she said, "but it's not the truth either. That's just where you went after."

From a child who'd lost whatever she'd lost—and he still didn't know the shape of it, only that she lived alone in the reeds and flinched from nothing except kindness—it landed harder than any tribunal ever had.

"I could burn it," he said, more to himself than to her. "Nobody would know. The team's gone. The notes describe dredging operations that aren't supposed to exist, signed off by officials who are supposed to prevent them. If I burn it, I go back to being nobody, and nobody is safe. Nobody drowns. Nobody gets followed."

"And if you don't?"

"Then I'm carrying evidence that powerful men have already killed for, down a river that's about to flood, in a boat that leaks, toward authorities who might be the same men." He laughed, short and without humor. "That's the arithmetic. Blacklisted man's arithmetic. Every column says burn it."

Silas was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "There was a kid."

He glanced up.

"Two years ago. Down near Kettle Bend." Her voice hadn't changed at all; that was the terrible thing about it. It was level as still water. "The dredging crews came through before anyone admitted they were there. Barges at night, no lights, running pumps into the channel. Changed the current. A boy from the stilt village—he was eleven—went out in a canoe he'd paddled his whole life, on water he knew better than his own hands. The channel had shifted. There was a snag where there'd never been a snag." She stripped the reed again, one long clean pull. "They pulled him out two days later, eight miles down. The officials said it was a tragic accident. Unsafe conditions. Parents warned. Same words they use every time."

"Silas—"

"I knew him." Still level. "His name was Oren. He used to trade me fishhooks for stories." She looked at Casper across the fire, and her eyes in the flame-light were older than her whole body. "So when you ask whether to burn papers that say those same men signed the papers that dug that channel—my arithmetic is shorter than yours. Why would you protect men who'd let a kid drown?"

"I'm not protecting them."

"You're deciding whether they matter more than the next kid. That's protecting them."

The wind came down the valley then, properly, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't arrived yet, and the deadfall groaned overhead like a ship. Casper looked at the case. The provincial crest caught the firelight, gold and smug.

He thought about Tomas. He thought about the hearing room, the flat of his hands on the table, the word *confabulation* spoken by a man who never once looked at the water. He thought about how five years of invisibility had felt like survival right up until the moment he'd found boots paired neatly outside empty tents, and understood that invisibility was not survival—it was just dying somewhere quieter.

"They erased me," he said. "Both countries. I lost my name. My work. Everything I was. And the men who did it are probably the same men whose signatures are in this case, because it's always the same men, it's one machine wearing different flags." His voice came out lower than he intended, roughened. "And the last time I told the truth, the truth cost three lives anyway, including a boy who trusted me, and it bought me nothing but a list."

"So that's why you'd burn it."

"That's why burning it is *rational*."

"Rational." She tasted the word like bad water. "Oren's mother is rational too. She stopped talking two years ago. Sits by the water every morning. Very rational."

"Silas."

"No. You asked your question and I'm answering it." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time since he'd met her she looked, briefly, like what she actually was—not a river spirit, not a survivor machine, but a kid who had buried someone and been left to do the arithmetic alone. "You want to know the real reason you're going to carry that box downstream? Not because it's smart. It isn't. It's because you already know what happens to the next Oren if you don't. You've seen it. You've *been* it. The man they erased so the digging could go on." She sat back. "You're not protecting them by carrying it. You're finally doing the thing you didn't do in the gorge. You're refusing the order."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the black water a night bird called twice and quit.

Casper found that his eyes were hot, and he was grateful—grateful—for the darkness and the angle of his face, because the first honest answer he'd given in five years deserved better than to arrive wet.

"You're right," he said. It came out hoarse. He cleared his throat and said it again, steadier, because it was true and true things should be said plainly. "You're right. I told myself I was weighing it. I wasn't. I was waiting for permission." He looked at her. "I'm not burning it."

"I know," Silas said. "You've been not burning it since noon."

Despite everything, despite the dread sitting in his chest like ballast, he laughed—a real one, startled out of him—and heard her answer it with the smallest huff, quickly suppressed, as though laughter were a resource to be rationed.

He picked up the case and stowed it deep in the supply locker, wrapped in oilcloth, lashed under the false floor panel he'd built years ago for smuggling things that needed smuggling—tobacco, mostly, once, and medicine during the quarantine year. Evidence fit the compartment fine. Evidence had always fit there. He'd just never had any worth putting in it.

"We run at first light," he said. "Downstream, fast, before the rain. Verity's village is two days if the channels hold. After that—" He stopped. He didn't actually know what came after that. Authorities, presumably. The word felt like a bridge with missing planks.

"After that we find someone who isn't them," Silas said, as if it were obvious. Maybe to her it was.

He banked the fire down to coals and lay back under the deadfall, the case's weight a presence in the boat even through the dark, and listened to the river move. It sounded different tonight. Heavier. Lower in its voice. The monsoon gathering its argument somewhere far upstream, patient as bureaucracy.

He was nearly asleep when Silas spoke again, quiet, from the edge of the firelight.

"Casper. The water's coming up."

He opened his eyes. She was standing at the gravel bar's edge, barefoot, pointing upstream into the black. And he saw it—felt it before he fully saw it, through the soles of his feet where they pressed the stones. A tremor. Faint, rhythmic, wrong.

Not rain. Not yet.

Something upstream, moving a lot of water.

The river had begun to rise, and it wasn't the sky doing it.
