## 6. First Rain

The rain did not come the way the old-timers said it would. It didn't build. It didn't warn. One moment the air was a wet wool blanket pressing down on the valley, and the next the sky simply opened, all at once, like a throat.

Silas felt it before she heard it—a change in the river's voice, a low note under the water that meant the hills upstream were drinking fast and would soon be spitting back everything they couldn't hold. She was standing in the bow of Casper's patched boat with her hand trailing in the current when the first drops hit, fat and warm as coins flung from a great height.

"Rain," she said.

"I noticed." Casper didn't look up from the outboard, which had been sulking since morning. "Forecast said three more days."

"The forecast is written by men who sit in offices." She watched the far bank, where the reeds had begun to shiver in a wind that hadn't existed a minute ago. "The river says now."

The river was right. Of course the river was right. The first real gust came down the valley like something chased, flattening the water into gray ripples, and then the rain arrived in earnest—not falling so much as arriving sideways, in sheets, in walls, until the world beyond ten meters stopped existing. Casper got the engine going on the third pull, coughing blue smoke into the deluge, and Silas steered them toward the only shelter she knew between here and the flooded forest: the church at Old Meridian, half-swallowed by the river these last six years, standing in two meters of brown water with its bell tower poking up like a drowned man's finger.

"It's not far," she shouted over the noise. "But you have to let me take us in. There's a wall under the surface. The roof fell through the west side years ago."

Casper looked at her for a long moment—rain streaming off his jaw, hands steady on the tiller—and then he stepped back from the controls and gave them to her without a word. That was still new, this thing he did. Most adults said they trusted her and then gripped the gunwale white-knuckled the whole way. Casper handed over the boat like handing over a fact.

She took them in through the broken west wall, threading the gap where a beam jutted just below the surface, and killed the engine as the hull slid into the church's flooded nave. The sound of the rain changed instantly—from a roar to a drumming, softer, muffled by the sagging roof that still covered most of the sanctuary. Inside, the water was calm. Pale light came down through holes in the ceiling in long slanted columns, and dust motes and rain-sparks drifted through them like the whole place was full of slow golden insects.

A wooden cross hung above what had been the altar, tilted fifteen degrees, patient as ever.

"Huh," Casper said quietly.

"People still come sometimes," Silas said, shipping the oars. "Old Meridian folk. They come by boat and light candles on the pulpit. The water never took the pulpit." She nodded toward it—a dark shape against the far wall, dry-topped, crowded with stubs of wax. "They say God moved into the river, so you might as well pray where He lives."

"Do you believe that?"

Silas considered it honestly, because Casper always asked questions like they deserved honest answers, which was another new thing. "I believe the river keeps what people give it," she said. "Whether that's prayers or bodies or money. So maybe that's the same thing."

* * *

They got the boat tied to a pillar that had once held up a balcony, spread their soaked blankets over pews whose seats sat just above the waterline, and ate cold rice and dried fish while the storm worked itself into a fury outside. Through the broken wall they could see the main channel churning, brown and muscular, carrying branches and whole sections of reed-bank past the church like a parade of refugees.

Silas found herself watching Casper instead of the flood. He'd gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet when his mind was doing arithmetic she couldn't see. He did it a lot. She'd decided it wasn't about her—it was about whatever had happened to him before the river, the thing that made him move like a man trying not to leave footprints even in mud. She'd decided she didn't care. Then she'd decided she cared very much. She went back and forth on it daily, the way the tide went back and forth, and had concluded that this was simply how thinking about Casper Ravensworth worked.

"You knew the survey team," she said. "Before they vanished."

He glanced up. "What makes you say that?"

"You carried their supplies. You knew the lead man's name without checking the manifest. And when we found their camp, you stood over those neat paired boots like you were at a funeral." She tore a piece of fish with her teeth. "I read people on the water. It's how I stay alive."

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the ruined roof, running off the eaves in silver ropes.

"I ferried for them twice," he said finally. "Good people. Osei—the engineer—she asked real questions. The kind you're not supposed to ask if you want the contract renewed." His thumb traced a scar on the pew's edge, back and forth. "I told myself it wasn't my business. I've gotten very good at telling myself things aren't my business. It's practically a profession."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a flooded church with a stolen case full of field notes that could hang half the provincial government." He almost smiled, but it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "So apparently my self-deception has a leak too. Like the boat."

Silas laughed despite herself—a short bark that echoed strangely off the water. Then the laugh caught on something in her chest and turned into something else, and she had to look away at the tilted cross until her face behaved.

Because he'd said *stolen*, and he'd said *hang*, and he'd said *half the provincial government*, and every one of those words was a door, and behind one of those doors was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she kept in the deepest channel of herself where even she only visited it in the dark hours when sleep refused her.

The rain hammered. The pulpit candles had all melted to pools. And Silas heard herself say:

"My parents died in a survey accident."

Casper went very still.

"Eleven years ago. Before you were blacklisted. Before any of this." Her voice came out flat, which was how she'd trained it, which was how she survived saying it at all. "That's what the report called it. A survey accident. Two river workers, a husband and wife, hired to guide an official vessel through the Kettle Bend channels during low season. The vessel hit a submerged cable—one nobody had marked, one that shouldn't have been there—and capsized. My parents were in the water helping the officials out." She picked at the fish, not eating it. "The officials all lived. They were wearing vests. My parents weren't given vests. Guides never are. We're considered part of the landscape."

"Silas—"

"The report said human error. My father's error, specifically. Wrong channel, wrong season, unmarked hazard he should have known." Now she did look at Casper, and she let him see all of it, the whole flooded country of it. "My father knew those channels better than anyone alive. Better than me, and everyone says I'm a freak of the water. He would sooner steer a boat into the sun than hit an unmarked cable. But dead men make convenient errors, and orphan girls make no witnesses at all."

The rain roared. Somewhere in the flooded nave, water lapped gently against a pew leg, patient, rhythmic, like something breathing.

"What was the survey for?" Casper asked. His voice had changed—lower, rougher, like a channel narrowing.

"Dredging feasibility. Kettle Bend sector." She said the words like spitting. "I memorized the report number when I was nine. I used to think if I learned enough—read enough, understood enough—I could undo it. Prove my father right posthumously. Then I grew up and understood reports don't undo anything. Reports are what they use to bury you."

Casper set down his food. He didn't speak for a long time, and Silas watched him do the arithmetic again, the invisible kind, but this time she could see the numbers landing on his face one by one like raindrops.

"Kettle Bend," he said. "Same sector as the current operation. Same dredging program." He rubbed a hand over his face. "The cable your parents' boat hit. Submerged, unmarked, shouldn't have been there."

"That's what I said."

"A cable like that doesn't drift into a channel on its own. Cables get laid. By crews. For reasons." He stared at the water column of light slanting through the roof hole. "Or they get laid to sink something. Or someone." He shook his head slowly. "Eleven years. The same signatures. The same pattern—hire local guides who know the water, blame them when the water kills them, bury the inquiry in a report nobody reads. I thought what happened to me was its own disease. It's not. It's endemic. It's been running in this valley longer than I've been alive."

Something loosened in Silas's chest when he said it—something that had been clenched for eleven years, some small hard knot of *it was only us, it was only our bad luck*. Hearing it named as a pattern didn't hurt less. It hurt differently. It hurt like a bone being set.

"They'll do it again," she said. "When the monsoon floods the tributary and washes away the work camp and the dredge and any evidence. They'll write a report. Flood damage. Unfortunate. Perhaps they'll even find a body or two to blame it on." She looked at him. "Maybe yours."

"Probably mine." He said it without drama, the way you'd note the weather. "Blacklisted guide, trespassing in a restricted zone, in possession of stolen government property, last seen heading into floodwater. I've already written my own report. It's very tidy."

"Then we don't let it be tidy."

He turned to look at her fully then, and she saw something shift in his face—the careful blankness cracking, not breaking, just opening a seam, the way ice opens before it becomes navigable water.

"We?" he said.

* * *

The storm peaked near dusk and held there, a sustained violence that made the whole church groan like a ship. Water crept up two more steps of the aisle. They moved the boat deeper inside and wedged it between pews, and Silas showed Casper how to rig a rain-catcher from the torn tarp using the church's old collection basket, and they drank sweet cold stormwater and pretended it was a feast.

Later, in the gray half-dark, wrapped in damp blankets on opposite pews, they talked the way people talk when the world outside has been canceled—slowly, in pieces, about nothing and everything. He told her about mountains, the other life, the one before. She made him describe snow three times because she couldn't make it real in her head; water that falls soft and stays? Stays frozen? For months? He laughed—actually laughed, a real one, rusty as an unused winch—and the sound bounced around the flooded nave and came back warmer.

She told him about the year after her parents died, when she'd lived three months alone in a hollowed-out barge, and how Aunt Verity had found her stealing fish from Verity's traps and, instead of chasing her off, had left a second portion out every morning until Silas worked up the courage to eat it in front of her. "That's how Verity adopts people," Silas said. "Through food and ambush."

"She sounds formidable."

"She terrifies the cartel. They leave her village alone mostly, because the last man who didn't—" She smiled thinly. "Let's say the river near her village has a reputation."

Casper studied her across the dark water. "You could have run, you know. Back at the reeds, when you first saw my boat. Any sane kid would have paddled the other way from a stranger hauling government crates."

"Sane kids starve." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Besides. I watched you handle the boat for a day before I showed myself. You read water like a foreigner who's studied hard but never dreamed in it. I figured you needed me more than I needed to be afraid of you." A pause. "And you gave me food without making me beg for it. That counted."

"It counted?"

"On the river it counts double."

The rain began, very slowly, to lose its fury—not stopping, but settling into the long steady fall that would last for days, the true monsoon rhythm. Through the broken wall, the channel ran high and fast and gleaming under a sky the color of wet slate. Somewhere upstream, Silas knew, the tributary was rising toward its banks, climbing toward the work camp, toward the dredge at Kettle Bend, toward whatever remained of the survey team and the truth they'd carried.

She thought about her father's hands on a tiller. She thought about the report number she'd memorized at nine. She thought about Casper saying *we* in his careful voice, like a man testing ice.

"Casper," she said.

"Mm."

"When the water drops—we're going to Kettle Bend. Both of us. Not you dragging me along, not me guiding you. Both of us. Because whoever wrote the report that buried my parents probably signed the ones that buried your team, and I have eleven years of reading I want to hand back to them personally." She kept her eyes on the tilted cross. "I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you how it's going to be."

The silence stretched. Rain dripped from the rafters into the nave, each drop ringing softly off the floodwater, a slow bell counting something.

"Both of us," he agreed at last, and his voice had that roughness in it again, the seam-of-ice voice. "Verity will skin us both for taking a child into a cartel zone."

"Verity will skin you. She'll feed me first."

That startled a laugh out of him again, and this time she joined it, and the laughter echoed through the drowned church and out through the broken wall and over the running water, two small defiant sounds in a valley full of rain.

Then the bell tower spoke.

Not the church's bell—that had been sold decades ago—but something else, a deep resonant thrum rolling down the valley from upstream, felt in the flooded floorboards more than heard. Silas went rigid. She knew that sound. Every river kid knew that sound.

"What is it?" Casper was already moving.

"Upstream blast." She scrambled to the broken wall, staring north into the rain-hammered dark. "Dynamite. Big charge. From the direction of—" Her stomach dropped. "The tributary mouth."

Casper appeared beside her, following her gaze. Far off through the sheets of rain, where the tributary met the main channel, a light was moving. Not a village light. Village lights burned yellow and stayed put. This one swept side to side, white and hard, cutting the rain like a blade.

A searchlight. On the water. Coming downstream.

"At night?" Casper said quietly. "In this?"

Silas's mouth had gone dry. "Nobody runs the main channel at night in the first monsoon storm. Nobody except somebody who doesn't care about losing a boat." She backed away from the wall, pulling him with her, her mind already sprinting through the channels. "And nobody searches unless they've lost something. Or they're looking for it."

The searchlight swung wider, closer, licking across the flooded trees a kilometer upstream.

"Kill the lantern," she whispered. "Now."
