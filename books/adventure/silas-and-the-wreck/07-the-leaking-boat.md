## 7. The Leaking Boat

The boat began to die at dawn, and it did it politely, the way old things do—one soft sound, one slow admission.

Casper heard it before he felt it: a change in the rhythm beneath the floorboards, the river's voice coming up through the hull a half-tone too clear. He shipped his paddle and knelt, pressing his palm flat against the planking near the stern. Water. Not the damp that lived in every seam of a patched boat, but a cold, deliberate pressure, like a finger testing the skin.

"Silas." He kept his voice low out of habit. On this river, loudness was a currency he couldn't afford. "Bail."

She was awake already—she was always awake first, though she pretended otherwise, curled in the bow under the tarp with her knees drawn up like a comma. She surfaced without complaint, found the dented tin pan they used for bailing, and started scooping in long, economical strokes, throwing the water over the gunwale without splashing herself. A child who had grown up on this water didn't waste motion. Casper had learned more about economy from watching her for three days than from five years of running rivers alone.

"How bad?" she asked.

"Bad enough that we're not making the bend at Kettle today."

She considered that, her eyes going narrow and dark, flicking downstream as if she could see the whole valley laid out like a chart. "There's a village," she said. "Stilt houses. Old woman named Verity keeps a repair shed. My mother used to say Verity could fix anything except gossip."

"Can we trust her?"

Silas gave him a look—the look she reserved for questions she considered beneath them both. "You can't trust anyone," she said. "But you can trust what people want. Verity wants the river quiet and her village fed. We're not worth trouble to her."

It was, Casper reflected, a more sophisticated theory of trust than anything the officials in two countries had ever managed. They'd trusted him with their lives on the ice and the rock, then erased him with a signature when it became convenient. At least Silas's cynicism was honest.

He turned the boat toward the east bank, where the forest thinned into flooded grassland and, beyond it, the grey shapes of houses lifted on poles above the water like herons standing in the shallows.

* * *

The leak was worse than he'd thought. By the time they crossed the last half-mile of open water, Silas was bailing continuously, and the boat sat two fingers lower in the river than it should have, dragging its belly, wallowing on each stroke. The patch job they'd done two nights ago—a slab of rubber sheeting and a fistful of tar borrowed from an abandoned fish camp—was peeling at one edge, and beneath it the original crack had widened into something less like a wound and more like a decision.

The boat had been dying since before he bought it. Everything Casper touched lately seemed to be held together by tar and denial.

The village resolved out of the morning mist gradually, the way places do when you approach them tired: first the smell—woodsmoke, drying fish, mud rich enough to taste—then the sound of hammers and a radio playing something tinny and cheerful, then the details. Forty-odd houses on ironwood stilts, walkways connecting them in a web above the brown water, nets hung like banners, chickens regarding him from railings with the flat judgment of creatures who had seen every kind of stranger arrive by every kind of boat.

A dozen faces appeared at rails and doorways as they came alongside the main walkway. Nobody spoke. Nobody waved. But nobody reached for anything sharp, either, and in Casper's experience that was as warm a welcome as the interior valleys offered.

An old woman came down the central walkway at a pace that suggested the walkway belonged to her personally and everyone else was renting. She was small and wide and moved like weather, and she stopped at the edge of the platform, hands on hips, and looked at the boat, then at Casper, then at Silas, in that order, and her face did something complicated.

"You're Ravensworth's boy," she said.

The name went through him like cold water through a cracked hull. Five years since anyone had said it. Five years of *the guide*, *that man*, *the one responsible*—a nameless thing shuffled between jurisdictions. He'd almost convinced himself the name had drowned with everything else.

"I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "Yes."

"Hm." Aunt Verity looked at the boat again, and whatever she saw in its listing, exhausted posture seemed to decide something for her. "That boat is a disgrace. Get it against the shed. Girl, there's rice on the third house, tell Marta I sent you. You—" the finger came up at Casper, blunt and certain as a dock cleat, "—you'll carry your own gear, because my back is seventy-two years old, and then you'll tell me why a blacklisted guide is running survey supplies up a dead tributary with a child and a leaking hull, and you'll tell it straight, because I knew your father and he lied badly and I'll not sit through it twice."

* * *

The repair shed stood at the village's downstream end, a cathedral of salvaged timber with a roof of corrugated tin and forty years of boat skeletons hanging from the rafters like the molts of enormous insects. Verity worked while she listened, which Casper understood was her way of listening properly—hands busy, eyes down, attention total.

He told it mostly straight. He left out the sealed case hidden under the false floor beneath the rice sacks, and he left out Amara Osei's name, and he left out the journal with its careful, terrified handwriting describing men who arrived by night and made promises shaped like threats. But he told her about the empty camp, the boots paired neatly on the ground, the map torn to show a tributary no official chart admitted existed. He told her about the dredging. He watched her hands the whole time—at the word *dredging* they paused, just once, over the caulking iron, and then resumed.

"So," she said, when he finished. She drove a final tack and sat back on her stool, studying him with eyes the color of tea left too long. "And you think carrying papers downstream will change anything. Papers." She said the word the way other people said *mud*.

"It changed things once," Casper said. "Before."

"Did it." Not a question. She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and walked the length of the hull, pressing here, sounding there, reading the boat the way Silas read water. "Your father came through here the year before he died. Did he ever tell you that?"

Casper went still. "No."

"He was running a load of medicine past a checkpoint that wasn't supposed to exist. Officially there was no checkpoint. Officially there was no dredge behind it, either." She tapped the hull twice, sharply. "He asked me the same question you're going to ask me, so I'll save us both the hour. No, I don't know who signed the orders. Yes, I know how the money moves. And yes—" she turned, and her face was terrible and calm, "—I've been writing it all down for thirty years, in a ledger your government would burn this village to collect. So don't look at me like I'm brave. Bravery is just fear that's run out of alternatives."

The rain started then—not monsoon rain, not yet, but a fine grey sifting that ticked on the tin roof like a clock counting down. Through the shed's open side Casper could see the river rising already, the water climbing the stilts in slow increments, patient as debt.

Verity followed his gaze. "Early this year," she said. "Two weeks early, maybe three. The tributary will flood first—it always does; your girl knows that—and when it floods, everything that's hidden in those channels comes floating out. Bodies. Equipment. Truths." She picked up the tar pot and thrust it at his chest. "So we work fast. You mix. I caulk. And while we work, I'll tell you the news you came here hoping I wouldn't have."

* * *

The news was this: two days ago, a pair of government boats had come up the main channel, white-hulled and clean, flying the provincial crest. They hadn't searched. Searching was for people who lacked confidence. They had *visited*—tied up at the trading post twelve miles downstream, shown photographs, asked polite questions of everyone who drifted through.

"They were asking about a guide," Verity said, working the caulking iron along a seam with short, vicious strokes. "A mountain man, they said. Foreign-sounding name. Said he might be injured, might be confused, might be in danger." Her mouth twisted around the word. "*In danger.* Thirty years I've watched these people work, and they always use the same words. The missing are never missing. They're *in danger*. The dead are never murdered. There was an *accident*. And the men asking are never hunters. They're *rescuers*." She spat over the side of the shed into the brown water. "They showed a photograph. I didn't see it. But Marta's boy described it to me, and from his description, it was taken five years ago, off a permit file. Your file."

Casper's hands stopped moving in the tar. The shed seemed very quiet suddenly, the rain ticking, the river breathing against the pilings below.

"They're not looking for the survey team anymore," he said.

"No. They found what they needed to find—or didn't find it, which amounts to the same thing. Now they're tidying." Verity glanced at him sideways. "You understand what that makes you."

"A loose end."

"It makes you the loose end. Singular. The team is handled. The evidence is a rumor. But a blacklisted guide with nothing left to lose, who was actually *there*, who can testify to the camp, the map, the tributary—" She shook her head. "If I were Director Hale, I would not be sending two boats next time. I'd be sending four, and I wouldn't be showing photographs."

The name landed in Casper's chest like a dropped anchor. Hale. He hadn't let himself say it aloud in five years, hadn't let himself think it as anything more than a shape in the dark—the man whose signature had ended him, whose report had called the disaster *operator error*, whose career had climbed steadily upward on the same slope where eleven climbers' ropes had been cut, figuratively speaking, by budget decisions and falsified safety assessments. Casper had survived. The report had needed someone to blame. He had been available.

"You know him," Verity said. It wasn't a question either. She seemed to deal entirely in statements.

"I worked a season under his directorate," Casper said. "Before. Everyone did. He signs everything."

"Mm. Then you know he doesn't send boats for a man he intends to arrest. Arrests make paperwork. Paperwork makes records." She drove the iron home along the final seam and sat back, wiping her hands on a rag that had been black longer than Silas had been alive. "Boats like that mean a man he intends to *not exist*."

* * *

They ate that evening in Verity's kitchen, up three flights of stairs worn smooth as river stone, at a table crowded with jars and a sewing machine and a sleeping cat who owned all of it. Silas ate the way she did everything—efficiently, watchfully, one ear always on the conversation—and Casper noticed that she had positioned herself between him and the window. He didn't comment on it. Some protections were better left unacknowledged, or they got shy and left.

"The boat will hold now," Verity said, ladling out fish stew thick enough to stand a spoon in. "Not pretty. But she'll hold to the capital if the river doesn't eat her first. Which brings us to the question of *when* you leave, and the answer is: not tomorrow."

Silas's head came up. "The rain—"

"The rain is exactly why not tomorrow." Verity fixed the girl with a look that had probably ended arguments before Silas's grandmother was born. "In this rain, every channel runs high and every eye stays indoors—which cuts both ways, child, so don't smirk. It also means any boat moving upstream is conspicuous, and theirs are. They'll run the tributary mouth tomorrow, maybe the day after, checking the channels while the water does their work for them. You move the day after that, low water, grey sky, mixed in with the market traffic going down to Halfway Lock." She pointed her spoon at Casper. "And you go as what you are. A guide taking a paying passenger downstream. Guides are common. Grief is common. Men poling leaky boats at dawn in monsoon season are *memorable*, and memorable is dead."

Afterward, Silas washed the bowls and Casper mended the tarp by lamplight, and the village settled around them in creaks and murmurs, dogs sighing, the river talking to itself beneath the floorboards. Verity sat in her chair by the window with a ledger open on her knee—not the ledger, Casper suspected, or maybe exactly the ledger, copied out in a hand so ordinary it could pass for accounts—and she wrote for a long time without speaking.

"Your father," she said finally, not looking up, "would have burned the papers and gone home."

"Probably."

"And you're not going to."

Casper fed the needle through canvas. Outside, the rain thickened, finding its true monsoon voice at last, a vast grey roar that swallowed the river and the forest and the distance between things. "No," he said.

"Hm." She wrote another line. "Good. Someone has to be difficult. It's the only thing they've never figured out how to flood."

He slept that night on the kitchen floor with the sealed case under his jacket and Silas breathing slow and even in the corner, and he dreamed, for the first time in years, not of ice and falling, but of water rising—clean water, honest water, carrying everything hidden up out of the channels into the light.

He woke before dawn to the sound of engines. Two of them. Upstream, and closing.

Beside the cold stove, Verity was already dressed, already listening, her face in the lamplight calm as carved wood.

"Well," she said. "They're early. Get the girl."
