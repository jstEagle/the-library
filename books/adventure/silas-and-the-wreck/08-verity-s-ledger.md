## 8. Verity's Ledger

Silas knew the sound of Verity thinking before she ever saw the ledger.

It was a particular silence, the kind that filled the back room of the stilt house like smoke. The rain ticked against the tin roof. Down below, Casper's voice rose and fell with the rhythm of a man learning the shape of a hull—asking about planking, about pitch, about where the water got in. He'd been at it since dawn, sleeves rolled, hands black with tar, and Silas had spent the morning pretending not to watch him work because watching felt too much like trusting and trusting was a thing she did with her whole body or not at all.

"Silas." Verity's voice came through the bead curtain. "Bring the lamp."

She brought it. The back room smelled of camphor and old paper and the river itself, that green mineral smell that never left anything that lived on the water long enough. Verity sat behind her writing desk—a real desk, mahogany, salvaged from some drowned official's office decades ago—with a book open before her that made Silas's breath catch.

It wasn't a ledger the way Silas had imagined ledgers, all neat columns and tidy sums. It was a fat, swollen thing bound in oilcloth, its pages soft as cloth from handling, stuffed with loose papers pressed between them like flowers: receipts, telegrams, carbon copies of letters, photographs gone amber at the edges. Some pages were written in Verity's own hand, small and fierce, the ink faded to brown in places but every word still legible, because Verity wrote like she spoke—as if she expected someone to try to erase it and wanted to make the erasing hard.

"What is it?" Silas asked, though she knew. She'd known since she was small enough to sit under this desk and be shooed out whenever the book came down from its hiding place behind the false panel above the stove.

"This," Verity said, "is thirty-one years of men believing nobody was counting."

* * *

Casper came up when Verity called him, drying his hands on a rag, and stopped just inside the doorway the way he always did—like a man checking exits even in a friend's house. Silas had started keeping track of his habits the way she kept track of channels. The way he stood sideways in doorways. The way he counted things under his breath when he thought no one could hear. The way his eyes went to Silas first, quick, then away, as if confirming she still existed were a chore he performed without admitting it.

"You wanted the boat finished by tomorrow," he said.

"The boat will keep. Sit down."

He sat. Verity turned the ledger around so it faced him, and Silas watched his face do something complicated—the stillness first, the guide's stillness, the face he wore over rapids—and then beneath it, slow as floodwater rising, something that looked like grief.

"That's a payment schedule," he said quietly. His finger hovered over a column of figures without touching the page. "Quarterly. To—" He stopped.

"Say it." Verity's voice was flat as a calm reach of river, which meant danger underneath. "Names don't bite unless you feed them."

"To the Provincial Water Resources Directorate," Casper said. "Office of the Director." He looked up. "Hale."

"Hale's predecessor. Hale took over the arrangement when the old man retired to a house by the sea that cost four times what a director earns in twenty years." Verity flipped forward through the swollen pages, past receipts and carbons, to a photograph: a dredge barge at Kettle Bend, taken from the far bank, its machinery half-hidden under camouflage netting. "The payments started the year they began cutting the channel deep. Officially for navigation. Unofficially for gravel—river gravel, the kind they sell to construction firms two provinces over, the kind worth more per ton than the fish this valley used to live on."

"And the survey teams?" Casper asked. "The ones that come through every few years?"

Verity turned more pages. Here the handwriting changed—different entries, different years, but the same structure. A team arrives. A team is paid off. Or:

"A team is not paid off," Verity said, tapping an entry dated eleven years back. "This one. Okafor's crew. They measured contamination in the sediment and wrote it down honestly, and three weeks later their boat was found burned downstream of the gorge with all five of them listed as drowned." She looked at Casper over the top of the page. "You remember that one. It was in the reports they used against you."

Silas saw it land. She saw it the way you see a stone go into deep water—not the splash but the rings spreading after. Casper's hand closed slowly on the edge of the desk.

"My disaster," he said. "The crossing I supposedly botched. The report that got me blacklisted—it cited Okafor's drowning as evidence of my poor judgment on that stretch of river. I never understood why they reached for something five years old. Why me at all."

"Because you were useful twice," Verity said. "Once when they needed a channel cleared of witnesses who might ask questions about a burned boat, and once now, if they can find you, when they need a blacklisted guide whose word counts for nothing standing next to another vanished survey team." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "They didn't ruin you by accident, Casper Ravensworth. They ruined you the way they ruin everything. On purpose, on schedule, and with paperwork."

* * *

Afterward Silas went down to the water because the room had gotten too full of other people's history and hers was pressing up against it, wanting in.

Her parents' names weren't in the ledger. She'd looked, years ago, sneaking into the back room while Verity napped, running her finger down entries until Verity caught her—not angry, never angry, just sad in that way that was worse. "Your mother and father aren't in here," Verity had told her, crouching to her level, "because what happened to them was so ordinary to these men that it didn't rate a receipt. Two river workers, a cable snapped, a boat swamped. An accident. Accidents are free."

Now she sat on the lowest step of the ladder with her feet in the brown water and watched the rain stitch holes in the current, and she thought about the word *organized*. Verity had said it like an accusation, but Silas heard it differently. Organized meant somebody had decided. Somebody had sat in a dry room and made lists and signed things. Her parents hadn't died of bad luck or a rotten cable. They'd died of arithmetic.

It should have made her feel smaller. Instead it made her feel, for the first time in six years, less alone inside the anger.

Footsteps on the ladder. Casper lowered himself onto the step beside her, careful of the space between them, always careful. Below them the river slid past, carrying leaves and a dead branch and, somewhere far upstream, she knew, carrying the dredge's tailings in clouds too fine to see.

"Verity says you've been reading her book since you were six," he said.

"I read it since I was six. Reading and reading aren't the same." Silas skipped a flat stone across the current; it went four jumps and sank. "She wouldn't give it to you."

"No."

"She won't give it to anyone. Not to the inspectors who came after the flood year. Not to the journalist who paddled through wearing a city coat and asking brave questions until someone bought his boat and he went home." Silas glanced at him. "Why do you think she won't give it to you?"

Casper was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Because giving it away hasn't worked. Everyone who's carried the truth downstream has been met at the landing by men with better papers and worse intentions. She's not refusing to share it. She's refusing to waste it."

"That's what she told you?"

"She told me something better. She told me how the money moves." He turned his palm up, tracing a line in the air, the shape of the valley's rivers. "Every quarter, cash goes downriver from the directorate to a fuel depot at Halfway Lock. From there it's split—one part stays to pay the lockmaster and the checkpoint men, one part goes on to the dredge crews at Kettle Bend, and one part keeps traveling, all the way to the capital, where it becomes donations and licenses and a director's pension. The same barges that carry gravel down carry the money. The same checkpoints that search travelers wave the barges through, because the checkpoint men are on the schedule too." He shook his head slowly. "I spent five years thinking I was the only person in this valley who knew what those men were. And the whole time there was a woman on stilts keeping accounts."

"There's more than a woman on stilts," Silas said. "There's the ferry families at Three Bends who log every barge that passes. There's the nun at the flooded church who keeps the baptism records of every child born to dredge workers—they sign real names on church papers and fake ones on the camp rosters. There's old Emory, who was a customs clerk before they pushed him out, and remembers which signatures are forged because he taught half those men to write." She hugged her knees. "Verity doesn't have a ledger. Verity has a *network*. The book is just the spine of it."

Casper looked at her then—really looked, the way he almost never let himself—and Silas saw something shift in his face, some load redistributing, some weight set down.

"I keep measuring everything in miles," he said. "Distance I can cover. Distance between me and trouble. It's how I survived the blacklist—stay moving, stay small, stay unmeasured." He looked back at the water. "But Verity's been standing still for thirty years and she knows more than I ever learned running."

"That's because she isn't hiding from it," Silas said. "She's *keeping* it. There's a difference." She stood, wrung out her hem, and looked down at him sitting on her step with tar still in the creases of his knuckles. "My parents are in that network too. Not in the book—in the people. In everyone who remembers them. That's why she won't hand the book over to any single man with a plan." She paused. "Even a plan I like."

* * *

That evening Verity cooked rice and dried fish and the three of them ate on the covered porch while the rain settled into its long monsoon patience. Lantern light made a small gold room out of the dark, and beyond it the river ran invisible and enormous, the way it always did, going where it was going regardless of who watched.

"Show him the last page," Silas said.

Verity's ladle paused over the pot. "That page isn't for showing."

"You showed me."

"You're family. He's cargo with good posture." But there was no edge in it, only tiredness, and after a moment she set the ladle down and went inside and came back with the ledger and opened it to the final leaf, holding it angled toward the lantern so Casper could see.

The last entry was recent—days old, the ink still sharp. It wasn't a payment. It was a list of names: the missing survey team, each one written out in full, with a line beside each name. Beside four of the lines, Verity had drawn a small stroke, like a tally. Beside the fifth name—*Osei, A., engineer*—she had written a single word: *kept.*

"They're alive," Casper said. His voice came out rough. "One of them is alive."

"One of them is *useful*," Verity corrected. "Which is the only reason anyone stays alive in this business. An engineer who'll falsify reports is worth more breathing than buried—for now. When the reports are filed and the season closes, usefulness expires." She closed the book. "The question, guide, is whether you intend to reach her before her usefulness does."

"How do you know that's what I intend?"

"Because you tarred my boat for eleven hours without being asked, and men who are running away don't fix things." Verity gathered the bowls, then stopped, and Silas saw her grandmother's jaw set in the old way, the way it set before decisions that changed the shape of seasons. "I won't give you the ledger. Understand that. If you're taken, it burns with me, and the network carries on, and we lose a battle but keep the war. But I'll teach you what the book knows. The routes the money takes. Which checkpoints are bought and which are merely lazy. Where the barges tie up and who owns the tie-ups." Her eyes went from Casper to Silas and back again. "And when you go downriver—and you will go downriver, because men like you always do—you'll move the way the money moves. Hidden in plain sight. Inside something so ordinary that looking at it twice would embarrass a busy official."

Downriver, the rain thickened, and somewhere in it—far off, at the limit of hearing—came the low diesel knock of an engine working against the current. Silas's head came up. She knew engines the way Verity knew ledgers, and this one was wrong: too heavy for a fishing boat, too steady for a trader, pushing upstream through weather no honest boat would push through.

Verity heard it too. She blew out the lantern without hurry, the way she did everything, and in the sudden dark her voice was quiet and very clear.

"Well," she said. "It seems the lesson begins tonight."
