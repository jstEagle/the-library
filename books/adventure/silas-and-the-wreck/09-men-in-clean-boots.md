## 9. Men in Clean Boots

Casper saw the boat before anyone else did, because Casper had spent five years learning to see boats the way other men learned to see faces.

It came around the downstream bend at mid-morning, riding too high in the water for a working vessel, its hull painted the flat institutional gray that no fisherman chose and no smuggler trusted. Government issue. Two figures stood in it, and even at three hundred meters he could read the details that mattered: they weren't poling, they weren't hauling nets, they weren't doing anything but standing. Men who rode rivers without touching them. The kind of men whose boots were still clean when they stepped ashore.

He was on Verity's dock, mending a net he had no intention of using, when the gray boat's engine dropped to an idle. He let his hands keep moving. Panic was a broadcast; stillness was camouflage. Around him the stilt village went about its morning—smoke rising from cook fires, kids slapping water off the dock planks, Aunt Verity herself sitting in her doorway shelling beans with the unhurried rhythm of a woman who had outlived every authority that ever tried to hurry her.

But Casper caught the small signals underneath: a mother drawing her son back from the rail with two fingers on his shoulder. A fisherman turning his boat away from the channel rather than past the strangers. The village had seen clean boots before.

The gray craft tied off at the far end of the dock. The two men climbed up with the careful, unpracticed steps of people who considered water a surface rather than a home. The taller one wore a khaki uniform without insignia—rank enough to matter, deniability enough to lie about. The shorter one wore civilian clothes so new they creased wrong, and carried a leather folder under his arm like a hymnal.

They walked the length of the dock toward him, because there was nowhere else to walk. Verity's house sat at the end of it like a full stop.

"Morning," said the tall one. His voice was pleasant, pitched to carry just far enough. "We're looking for a guide. Casper Ravensworth."

Casper kept his eyes on the net in his hands. "Ravensworth." He made the name sound like something he'd heard of once, distantly. "Don't know him."

"Big man," the civilian said, consulting nothing, reciting from memory. "Gray in the beard. Blacklisted out of two countries. Works the valley run alone." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm as banked coals. "You'd be him, then. The beard gives you away."

Casper looked up slowly, letting confusion settle onto his face like weather. "I'm Casper Ravensworth," he admitted, because denying it would be worse—a man who lied about his own name had something to hide, and these two had come to collect exactly what he was hiding. "Nobody's called me a guide in years, though. I haul diesel and rice. That's all anybody'll hire me for."

"That's all we need," the tall one said. He crouched, bringing himself to eye level with a seated man, a courtesy that was really a claim of dominance dressed as politeness. "There's been an incident upstream. A survey team, overdue. Their equipment may have drifted. Boats, cases, instruments—that sort of thing. If anything washes into your keeping, we're authorized to offer compensation."

"Compensation," Casper repeated.

"And more than that." The civilian opened the folder. Inside, Casper glimpsed paper stamped with seals he recognized—the same seals that had signed his blacklist, that had signed the disaster report blaming him for a flood he'd spent three years trying to warn them about. "Full amnesty. Restoration of certification. Your name cleared in both registries." The civilian let the folder hang open a moment longer than necessary, letting Casper read the words *reinstatement pending* in official type. "A man like you could work legal water again. Licensed. Respected. No more rice barges."

For one treacherous second, Casper felt it land.

That was the obscenity of it—the offer wasn't crude, it was *precise*. They knew what to dangle. Five years of eating cold food in boats, of watching his name travel ahead of him like a disease, of doors closing in towns he hadn't even reached yet. And here it was, printed on government stock: *your life back.* All he had to do was hand over a case of field notes that proved the men holding this folder had drowned a survey team and were drowning a river.

He thought of Amara Osei, if she was alive, held somewhere by men who valued her only as long as she falsified reports. He thought of Silas, asleep under Verity's roof after a night of teaching him channels that existed on no chart, a kid whose parents had died in a "survey accident" that shared a signature with this very folder. He thought of the journal entry he'd read by lamplight: *If anything happens to us, it wasn't the river.*

Amnesty meant a grave. He understood that now with the simple clarity of arithmetic. Men who offered pardons to blacklisted guides didn't want witnesses restored; they wanted evidence surrendered and the man who'd found it standing in one place, marked, until the monsoon or a rifle finished the job.

"No," Casper said.

The word hung there. The tall one's pleasant expression didn't move, but something behind it recalibrated.

"I haven't found any equipment," Casper went on, and here he gave them the performance of his life, slow and slightly resentful, the voice of a man tired of being suspected. "But I'll tell you what I know, since you're asking. There was a camp up past Kettle Bend. Abandoned. Half-eaten meals, boots paired neat as churchgoers'. No tracks out. That spooked me, so I turned around. I don't follow ghosts, and I especially don't follow them during monsoon season." He jerked his chin at the graying sky. "You can check the camp yourselves. It'll tell you the same story it told me: the river took them, or something did, and neither one is my business."

"You turned around," the civilian said softly.

"I turned around. I've got a boat that leaks and a season coming that kills fools. Whatever your team was chasing, it outran them, and I'm not paid enough to chase it." He let bitterness do the work truth couldn't. "And I'll tell you something else for free. If you're offering amnesty to every drifter on this river, you're wasting your time. Nobody up here keeps anything worth sealing in a folder. We keep rice dry and our mouths shut. That's the whole economy."

Verity, in her doorway, shelled another bean. The crack of it was tiny and enormous.

The tall one stood. He looked down the dock, at the village, at the water, unhurried, cataloguing. Then he looked back at Casper, and for a moment the pleasantness drained out of his face entirely, and what remained was worse—an accountant's face, weighing a figure.

"The tributary above Kettle Bend," he said. "You visited it."

"I looked at it. Water was high. I've got no business in water that high."

"Mm." The tall one produced a card—not paper, waxed cardstock, the kind that survived soaking—and set it on the dock boards between them. "If anything surfaces. Cases. Instruments. Papers. Anything at all." He tapped the card once. "The offer stands for seventy-two hours. After that, circumstances change. Rivers are dangerous places in monsoon season, Mr. Ravensworth. So many accidents."

"We'll hope you stay lucky," the civilian added pleasantly, and both of them smiled, and the smiles were the most honest thing either man had shown.

They walked back down the dock. The gray boat pulled away, riding high and empty, and Casper watched it until the bend swallowed it, counting his heartbeat the way he counted miles—one, steady; two, steady—because panic was a broadcast and someone might still be watching.

When the water closed behind the wake, Verity spoke without looking up from her beans.

"They'll leave a man behind."

"I know."

"They always leave a man behind. Twenty-two years I've watched this river, and the government never visits twice unless the first visit was a lie." She cracked another bean pod. "You bought a day, maybe two. That watcher will count your boats, your faces, your comings and goings. And when the seventy-two hours die, he'll send word, and then it won't be polite men in clean boots anymore."

Casper picked up the waxed card. Heavy stock, embossed seal, a number printed beneath it and nothing else—no name, no office. An invitation to disappear properly.

"Restoration of certification," he said quietly. "My name back. Both registries." He laughed, short and airless. "Five years I've wanted nothing else. I used to rehearse the argument. Standing in some hearing room, laying out the whole disaster minute by minute, proving I'd warned them, proving the report was a lie. I had it memorized, Verity. Every word."

"And now?"

"Now they've handed me the hearing room on a card, and all I have to do to win is help them bury a living woman and a dead team and a kid's parents." He tore the card in half, then in half again, and dropped the pieces through the dock boards into the brown water, where they floated a moment, seal-up, and sank. "Turns out the name was never mine to buy back. It was theirs to give away, which means it was never worth having."

Verity finally looked up. Her eyes were old and river-colored and missed nothing. "The girl," she said. "She heard all of it. She's under the floor with the ledger pages, has been since the boat rounded the bend."

Silas emerged from the hatch at Verity's feet, dusted with rice chaff, clutching a wrapped bundle against her chest. Her face was pale but level, and she looked at Casper with the flat assessing stare she used on water she didn't trust.

"You lied good," she said.

"It's the first useful skill my old career taught me."

"How long do we have?"

"A day. Maybe two, if the watcher's lazy." Casper glanced downstream, where the current ran brown and fast with the first melt-fed swell of the season. "Which means tonight we decide. Run the notes downstream through the checkpoint towns, or sink them deep and scatter, and let the river keep its secrets another year."

Silas hugged the bundle tighter. "The river doesn't keep secrets," she said. "It just holds them till somebody's brave enough to come back for them. My mama taught me that." She paused, and her voice dropped to something harder than her years. "Those men will come back whether we're here or not. I say we go tonight, while their watcher's still learning our faces."

Beyond the stilts, the sky over the western ridge had gone the color of wet slate, and the first fat drops of the season began ticking against the dock boards—one, then three, then too many to count.

* * *

The rain settled in by dusk, a soft persistent drumming on the tin roof, and Casper spent the last light doing what he did best: reading water and reading men.

The watcher was easy to find once he knew to look. A lone fisherman had anchored in the slack water below the village point, lines out, lantern lit, in a spot where no fisherman anchored because the current scoured the bottom bare. He'd been there since the gray boat left. Through Verity's borrowed spyglass Casper watched him eat from a tin, check his lines without interest, and glance—at regular intervals, every few minutes, like clockwork—up at the village lights. Counting boats. Counting faces.

"He'll sleep in shifts," Silas said beside him. She'd appeared without sound, the way she always did, and handed him a bowl of rice and dried fish. "Everyone sleeps in shifts on the water. Even liars."

"Then we don't leave by water."

"There's always the forest paths." She said it casually, chewing, eyes on the dark. "Flooded forest. Old hunting runs. The water's knee-deep and the way's ugly, but nobody watches a place boats can't go."

Casper looked at her. Thirteen years old, maybe fourteen, soaked to the knees half her life, proposing to lead a grown man and a satchel of state-secrets through drowned jungle at night, past a government watcher, on the strength of channels she'd mapped with her feet.

"You've done this before," he said.

"I've done everything before. That's what nobody understands about kids on the river." She scraped her bowl clean. "Question isn't whether I can get us out. Question's whether you can keep up."

He almost laughed. Five years of solitude, of measuring his life in miles to avoid measuring it in years, and here was a child telling him his legs were the weak link in her escape plan. And the strange thing—the thing he turned over later, lying awake to the rain—was how little fear he felt. Not none. But the dread had changed shape. For five years he'd been afraid of being seen, of the name traveling ahead of him, of doors closing. Tonight, hunted by men in clean boots, carrying evidence that could hang a director, he felt—for the first time—like he was moving toward something instead of merely away.

Downstream, the watcher's lantern bobbed at its anchor, patient as a spider.

Seventy-one hours left on their offer. One flooded forest. One leaking boat hidden in the reeds. One bundle of papers that weighed nothing and everything.

Thunder rolled up the valley from the coast, long and low, the monsoon clearing its throat. Silas was already lashing the ledger pages into oilcloth, quick and sure, and when she caught him watching she grinned—sharp, bright, unafraid.

"Tide turns at midnight," she said. "Be ready."
