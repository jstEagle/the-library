## 10. Night Channels

Silas felt the watcher before she saw him, the way you feel a leech on your ankle before the sting.

He was still in the village when the lamps went out—Aunt Verity's house dark, the cook-fire banked, everything arranged to look like sleep. But Silas had grown up reading absence the way other children read letters. The village had a rhythm at night: the creak of Verity's floorboards twice an hour because the old woman never slept a whole hour through; the dog at the Okonkwo end of the walkway that barked at fish and nothing else; the soft slap of moored boats adjusting themselves against their lines. Tonight there was an extra silence where a man should be breathing. Someone was lying still on purpose, and only one kind of person did that.

She found him eventually, or rather found his shape—a darker patch against the black of the boathouse wall, downwind of the cook-smoke so no dog would catch him. He'd settled in like a man prepared to be patient. Clean boots, even now. She could smell the leather oil from six meters across the water. Government men oiled their boots every day whether they needed it or not. River men oiled theirs when the leather cracked, and not before.

Silas slid back along the stilt path without a sound, barefoot, the boards warm from the day's stored heat under her soles. In Casper's room she didn't knock. She put her mouth close to his ear and said, "We leave now or we don't leave at all."

He came awake the way he always did—not with a start but with a slow gathering, like water finding its level. His eyes opened and found hers in the dark and he didn't ask questions, which was one of the things she had decided she liked about him, though she would have drowned before saying it out loud.

"The watcher," he whispered.

"Boathouse. Downwind side." She held up three fingers, then folded two. "One man. He'll sit till dawn unless we give him something to watch."

Casper nodded slowly. "So we give him something."

* * *

They gave him a boat.

It was Verity's idea, delivered in a voice like gravel poured slow while she packed dried fish and a coil of rope into a waterproof bag. "The young one from the capital, the polite one—he watches boats. So let him watch one." She jerked her chin toward the old punt tied at the far end of the village, the one with the rotten gunwale that had been dying for two seasons. "Send it downstream with the current and a lantern burning. He'll follow it all the way to the checkpoint at Two Sisters if you let him, and by the time he learns it's empty, you'll be somewhere he can't read a chart to find."

"And when they ask you what happened to your punt?" Casper asked.

Verity looked at him the way she looked at everyone, which was as if measuring them for a coffin. "I'll say river people lose boats the way government men lose documents. Constantly, and always to their advantage."

Silas almost smiled. Almost. Smiling felt wrong tonight, with the rain starting again above the canopy—a fine, needling rain, not the true monsoon yet, but its herald, its scout sent ahead to map them.

They launched the dead punt from the upstream side, gave it a shove, hung a shuttered lantern from its stern post so a moving eye would catch just enough light to believe in. Then Silas took Casper and the evidence bag out the other way, into the flooded forest, where the channels were.

"You can't paddle this," she told him, pushing off from Verity's walkway into black water threaded with tree trunks. "You steer with your eyes closed and your ears open. If you try to see, you'll hit something. If you try to think, you'll panic. Just do what I say."

"Understood," Casper said, and she believed him, and that frightened her more than the watcher did. Trusting someone was a kind of drowning too. Her parents had trusted the survey company's barges to run safe channels. The company had known about the sandbar shift for eleven days and said nothing, because reporting it would have delayed the dredging schedule and delayed money. That was the story Verity had finally told her last night, in the lamplight, with the ledger open between them—pages and pages of payments and threats and names, going back further than Silas had been alive. Her parents hadn't died in an accident. They had died in arithmetic.

So no more trusting. But Casper had pulled her out of the flood channel without hesitating, back before any of this, when she was just a reed-rat kid stealing passage off a stranger. And he'd chosen to carry the notes instead of burning them. Those were two data points. Silas worked with data points. Two wasn't proof. But it was enough to paddle with.

"Left," she whispered, and the boat swung left through a corridor of drowned acacia, branches combing overhead with a sound like fingers through hair. Behind them, far off through the trees, a lantern glow wobbled—their ghost punt doing its work, drawing the watcher's eyes downstream like a lure drawn over a pike.

The flooded forest at night was a country with no edges. Water everywhere, black as poured ink, and above it the trees standing hip-deep and higher, their roots lost, their crowns holding the last grey light of the sky. Frogs rang in the branches like small struck bells. Somewhere a nightjar called its three-note question and got no answer. The air smelled of rot and green life at once, thick enough to chew.

Silas navigated by things no official chart would ever hold: the way the current combed the duckweed into long grey lanes that pointed downstream; the hollow note a submerged log made when water moved over its end; the smell of clay banks versus sand; the particular silence around a deep hole where big fish held. She'd learned it all in the years after her parents died, when being invisible was the only safety a river girl had. Now invisibility was keeping two of them alive.

"Stop," she breathed, and Casper shipped his paddle instantly, no argument, and the boat drifted silent among the trunks.

Ahead, maybe sixty meters, a flashlight beam swept the water. Then another. Two men in a skiff, working the main channel that bordered the forest, sweeping their lights along the tree line.

"They're not following the punt," Casper murmured.

"No. They're smarter than the watcher." Silas eased the boat backward with one hand against a trunk, inch by inch, putting a curtain of acacia between them and the light. "They know nobody runs the open channel at night in this weather. So they're checking the forest edges. We go deeper."

"Deeper is—" Casper stopped himself. She knew what he'd been about to say. *Deeper is where the ground rises and the water shallows and you'll run aground.* Every guide thought in charts. Every guide thought in depth and draft and distance.

"Deeper is fine," she said, "if you stop thinking like a man with a license."

She felt him smile in the dark. She heard it more than saw it, a small exhale through the nose. "Yes, boss."

"Don't call me boss."

"Yes, boss," he said again, softer, and this time she did smile, briefly, in the dark where no one could tax her for it.

* * *

They went deeper.

For two hours Silas threaded them through the drowned wood by ways she had never once spoken aloud to another living person—ways that were hers, earned alone, kept like coins sewn into a hem. A gap between two buttress roots wide enough for a boat if you entered at exactly the angle the current wanted. A submerged fence line from some farm abandoned thirty years ago, its posts just kissing the surface, marked by a single line of bubbles on the downstream side. A channel that existed only when the water stood above the third root-flare of a great fig tree, which it did tonight, thanks to the early rains.

Casper learned fast. That surprised her. By the second hour he was anticipating her hand signals—paddle left, back water, hold—and by the third he'd stopped needing most of them. He poled when she pointed, paddled when she tapped his shoulder, and kept his eyes where she kept hers: on the water, not the map in his head.

Once, drifting through a cathedral of fig roots, he said quietly, "My father guided these valleys. Before the blacklistings. Before me."

Silas said nothing. Waiting was a tool; she used it.

"He'd have loved this," Casper went on. "This kind of country. No chart, no name, no one to report to." A pause. "That's what they took from me, you know. Not the work. The *being seen*. A guide who's good is a guide people point at. 'Him. Ask him.' I haven't been pointed at in five years."

"You're pointing at yourself right now," Silas said. "Coming with me. Carrying those notes."

"That's different."

"It's not." She shipped her paddle and let the boat drift, turning slowly among the roots, and made herself say the rest of it, because Verity had taught her that truths left unspoken went septic. "You keep waiting for someone to give your name back. Hale, or a court, or whoever. Like it's a coat somebody stole and might return. It's not a coat. You make a new one or you freeze."

The frogs rang. The water ticked against the hull. When Casper finally spoke, his voice was rough at the edges. "How old are you?"

"Old enough."

"Yeah," he said. "You are."

* * *

The trouble came at the seam, where the flooded forest met the old logging cut.

Silas knew the seam well: a place where the water funneled between a clay bluff and a stand of dead bamboo, sped up, and dropped half a meter into the cut below. In low water it was a riffle you walked across. Tonight, with the early rains stacked on the week's fall, it was a churning slot of brown water, loud enough to cover a boat, which was why she'd chosen it—it would drown the sound of their passage past the patrol route. Loud also meant violent. Everything useful was dangerous. That was the first law of the river and possibly of everything else.

"We line through," she said. "Rope on the bow, you on the bluff side, walk it down. I ride the boat and fend off the bamboo. The water wants to throw us left into the strainer—that deadfall jammed in the cut. We do not go left. Left is how people die here."

"Right side," Casper said. "Good."

They rigged the rope. The rain thickened, drumming the canopy, running off the leaves in ropes of water. Silas took her position amidships with a pole, and Casper took the line, boots slipping on wet clay, and they started down.

The first two-thirds went clean. The boat bucked and yawed in the chute, spray coming over the gunwale cold as ice water, but Silas fended the bamboo off and Casper paid out rope hand over hand, braced, strong, steady—and then the bluff gave way.

Not all of it. A slab of saturated clay the size of a door peeled off the bank beneath his feet with a sound like a giant lip smacking, and Casper went down with it, into the chute, the rope still wrapped around his forearm because a guide's instinct is to never let go of the boat.

The boat and the man met in the middle of the violence and became one problem.

Silas saw it happen in pieces, the way bad things come: the rope snapping taut, jerking the bow hard left—toward the strainer; Casper surfacing once, black-haired, eyes wide, mouth opening on nothing but water; the deadfall ahead, white branches down like the ribs of something enormous, the place the river kept its dead.

There was no time to decide. That was the thing she understood later, the thing she turned over for years afterward on nights she couldn't sleep: there was no moment where Casper weighed anything. The evidence bag sat in the boat, inches from her knee, sealed, irreplaceable, the whole reason for every mile since the empty camp. And Casper's hands were full of rope attached to a boat he could have climbed into.

He let go.

He let go of the boat—with the notes in it—in flood water, at night, and turned in the current and swam for her instead, one-armed, brutal, graceless, catching her collar as the bow swung into the strainer and the white branches came down at them like a closing hand.

Then the world was noise and cold and turning. Silas went under with his fist knotted in her collar, and the river rolled them together like clothes in a wash, and she did the only thing her body knew: she stopped fighting. Went limp, let the water take her weight, waited for the roll to bring her up the way rolls do. Casper's grip never loosened. Not once. Not when her boot caught a branch. Not when they went under a second time, longer, her lungs beginning to burn their red alarm. He held on the way drowning men hold flotsam, except he wasn't holding her to save himself. He was holding her instead of the mission, instead of the notes, instead of the name he wanted back so badly it leaked out of him at night like bilgewater.

They came up in the eddy behind the strainer, gasping, tangled in the rope, alive.

The boat was gone. They both heard it go—the crack of the hull meeting the deadfall, the brief scrape, then nothing, swallowed. Somewhere downstream, in the dark, the sealed field notes and Amara Osei's journal and the original samples were either wedged in the branches or sailing free toward the delta and the sea and no one.

Silas coughed river out of her lungs and laughed. She couldn't help it—a raw, shocked bark of laughter, hysterical at the edges.

"What," Casper wheezed, hauling her onto the clay shelf of the bluff, both of them shaking, "is funny."

"You picked me." She was shivering too hard to say it properly, teeth chattering through the words. "Over the notes. You didn't even—you didn't even *think*—"

"I thought," he said. "For about half a second. Which is longer than I'd like anyone to know."

But she'd seen his face underwater. There had been no half-second there. She filed it away with her other data points, and this one, she thought, might be proof.

* * *

They got out of the water and got a fire going—small, shielded, deep inside a hollow between root buttresses where the light couldn't leak. Casper stripped to skin and wrung his shirt dry; Silas kept her clothes on and steamed like a kettle, hugging her knees, watching the flames. The rain had gentled to a whisper on the canopy. Somewhere a long way off, an engine thumped—the patrols, hunting the empty channel, hunting a ghost punt, hunting two people who were, for this one hour, nowhere at all.

"The notes," Casper said at last. It wasn't despair in his voice. It was worse: inventory. "If they wedged in the strainer, they're retrievable. If they floated free—" He stopped. Started again. "Amara's journal. The samples. Everything we've bled for."

"Everything we've bled for is a bag," Silas said. "Bags sink. Bags snag. Bags get found." She looked at him across the fire. "You know what doesn't float away?"

He waited.

"Knowing the river." She jabbed a finger downstream, toward the black throat of the logging cut. "That strainer holds everything the flood brings it. Boats, logs, bodies, bags. It's been collecting at that bend for thirty years. Tomorrow, in daylight, at low slack, I can swim it. I know the eddies behind every branch. If the bag snagged—and it snagged, I heard it hit—I can get it back."

"And if it didn't snag?"

"Then we go to Kettle Bend anyway," Silas said, "and tell Amara Osei the truth: that her samples are feeding fish. And she gives us new ones. Because she's still alive, Casper. Alive means able. That's the whole secret nobody tells you." She fed a twig to the fire. "The paper was never the evidence. The woman is the evidence. Paper burns. Witnesses have to be silenced one at a time, and every time they try, somebody sees."

Casper stared at her for a long moment. Firelight moved on his face, and she watched something settle in it—not peace, exactly. Direction.

"When did you get wise?" he asked.

"About the same time everybody stopped protecting me," Silas said. "It happens fast."

He laughed then, quiet, careful of the dark, and it was the first time she'd heard him laugh all the way through instead of halfway. She decided not to count it as a data point. Some things you just let be true.

They slept in shifts, her first. Before she closed her eyes she made him repeat the plan until she was satisfied: dawn, the strainer, the swim, the bag—or without it, Kettle Bend, Amara, the living witness. He repeated it all, word for word, and added, "And you don't go into that strainer without a rope on you. Non-negotiable."

"Bossy," she said, and slept.

She woke once, deep in the small hours, to the sound of Casper sitting watch with his knees drawn up, looking downstream into the black. Not anxious. Listening. The way she listened. And she thought: *he's learning to read it.* Not the charts. The real thing underneath.

Far off toward Kettle Bend, faint under the rain-hush, came a sound she knew and hated—the rhythmic diesel cough of a dredge pump, working the night shift, chewing the riverbed into money for men in clean boots.

Tomorrow, Silas thought, pulling her blanket tighter. Tomorrow they'd go and look at the machine that had orphaned her, with the man who'd chosen her over the evidence sleeping three feet away.

The river carried the dredge's heartbeat to them all night, patient as a pulse.
