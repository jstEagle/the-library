## 11. The Dredge at Kettle Bend

The tributary narrowed the way a throat narrows before a scream. Casper felt it in the paddle first—the current quickening against his blade, pulling with an insistence that had nothing to do with rain and everything to do with elevation. Somewhere ahead, water was being held back and let go in quantities that no natural river managed on its own.

"Kettle Bend," Silas said from the bow, not turning around. She'd named it herself, he suspected; no chart carried the name. "You'll hear it before you see it."

She was right. A mile on, the sound reached them through the flooded forest—a low, rhythmic grinding, like millstones fed by patient hands, punctuated by a hydraulic hiss that rose and fell with a pulse too regular to be weather.

Casper backed the boat into the shadow of a strangler fig whose roots had grown around some long-dead kapok like fingers around a throat. He shipped the paddle and listened.

"You knew this was here," he said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. With Silas it never worked as one; she simply absorbed accusations the way the river absorbed silt and kept moving.

"I knew machines were here." She turned then, and her eyes—river-colored, river-cold—held his. "I didn't know what they were eating."

*The riverbed,* Casper thought. *They're eating the riverbed.* The dredging operation described in Amara Osei's field notes—the ones he'd pulled from the lead case in the drowned channel—had been clinical about it. Sediment extraction at volumes that would lower the valley floor by meters within a decade. Gold, or tin, or whatever lay locked in the alluvium; the notes hadn't cared about the mineral, only the tonnage, only the signatures of officials who had certified the operation as a *hydrological survey*.

He'd spent two days telling himself there might be another explanation. That the notes could be forged. That men who left boots paired neatly beside half-eaten meals might have walked out alive into some country the maps didn't admit to.

Now he could hear the truth grinding away upstream, and it sounded exactly like the machinery that had ruined him.

"We go the rest on foot," he said. "From here, if we take the boat around the bend, we're in their sightline."

Silas was already moving, gathering the bail bucket, the oilcloth, the coil of line they'd rigged for dragging the boat under branches. She did everything without wasted motion, the way she did everything, and Casper had learned over the past days that this economy wasn't training—it was hunger, refined into habit by a childhood that had offered no surplus of anything, least of all time.

They tied the boat into the fig roots, low, where the floodwater wouldn't take it, and Silas marked the spot with three cuts in the bark arranged like a bird's footprint. Then they went up the bank, into the green dark, toward the sound.

* * *

The camp revealed itself in pieces.

First: a sluice pipe as wide as a house, running from the cutbank down into the main channel, vomiting gray water that spread across the floodplain in a fan of dead silt. Everything the fan touched was dying in slow motion—ferns gone brown at the edges, saplings standing leafless though it was weeks before autumn, a heronry abandoned, nests still hanging in the stripped trees like empty fists.

Second: the dredge itself. It sat anchored mid-channel where the tributary made its bend—a barge-mounted machine the size of a chapel, its boom lowered into the water like a drinking animal, cables running down into the murk. Floodlights on its superstructure burned even now, in daylight, throwing hard white circles on the current. Behind it, the river ran cloudy for a hundred meters, carrying away the mountain's ground-up bones.

Third: the men.

Casper counted them from behind a fallen trunk, counting the way he counted miles—not because the number mattered but because the act kept something in him from unraveling. Fourteen workers in rubber aprons, moving gravel. Two supervisors under a corrugated shelter with a radio mast. One guard with a shotgun slung muzzle-down, walking a lazy circuit along the near bank.

And beyond the work area, set apart on a shelf of higher ground: a fenced compound. Three tents. A generator. And figures moving inside the fence—four of them, maybe five, wearing the wrong clothes for labor. Khaki shirts. Field trousers. The uniform of people who had come to measure a river and been measured instead.

Casper's mouth went dry.

"They're alive," Silas breathed beside him. She'd seen it too. Her voice held something Casper had never heard in it before—wonder, almost, as if survival itself were a thing she'd stopped expecting the world to permit.

*Not all dead.* The words arrived in him like light through a crack. For six days he had carried the camp's silence like a stone—boots paired, meals abandoned, no tracks—and beneath the stone, unexamined, the certainty that he would find bodies. That his job here was grief-work, witness-work, the closing of accounts. But the fence held living men. Living *surveyors*. Which meant—

Which meant someone wanted them alive. And there was only ever one reason to keep witnesses alive.

"Look at the tents," Silas whispered. "Look how they walk."

Casper looked. The figures moved slowly, deliberately, the way people move when they've learned that haste draws attention. One of them stood at the fence line, gripping the wire, staring downriver with an expression Casper could read even at this distance because he'd worn it himself for five years: the look of a person calculating whether the river would be kinder than the men behind them.

"The woman," Silas said. "At the fence. She's the one from the journal."

Amara Osei. Lead engineer. The journal—found wrapped in oilcloth inside the sealed case—had described her in its final entries with a bitterness that had curdled into pity: *Osei has agreed to sign. They showed her photographs of her daughters' school. She signed. God forgive her, she signed, and I understand why, and I hate that I understand.*

Alive because she'd agreed to falsify reports. Kept alive the way you keep a tool—cleaned, stored, used.

Casper felt the old machinery turn in his own chest, the gears he knew by heart. He had met these men before, or their brothers, in another country, in another life, on the day a bridge inspection became a disaster and his honest report became evidence of negligence while the contractor's falsified one became a career. He knew how it worked. Truth was negotiable; silence was currency; and the people who refused to pay were not killed, usually—they were *disappeared into blame*, made into the story's villain so the real story could sleep.

That was what waited for Osei. Sign enough false reports and eventually one of them would fail publicly, and she would become the engineer who lied, the scapegoat with a name and a face, while the men with clean boots moved upriver to the next bend.

Unless someone took her out first.

*There is a witness worth everything,* he thought, and the thought frightened him more than the guard's shotgun, because wanting something that badly in this valley was how people died.

* * *

They watched through the afternoon.

Casper made himself learn the camp the way he'd once learned mountains: patterns, intervals, blind spots. The guard's circuit took eleven minutes and hugged the near bank, avoiding the silt fan where the footing was treacherous—he didn't like getting his boots wet, which told Casper the man was new, or soft, or both. The supervisors changed shift at what Silas judged by the sun to be four o'clock, and for perhaps twenty minutes the radio mast went quiet while whoever ran the operation's communications ate. The dredge shut down at dusk—not from mercy but because the sediment barges came at night, running dark down the main channel, and the boom had to be raised to let them load.

"Barges," Silas murmured. "Every night?"

"If Verity's ledger is right." Casper kept his voice below the river's noise. "The money moves downstream the same way the gravel does. Night runs, dark boats, tolls paid at Halfway Lock." He'd spent an evening in Aunt Verity's stilt house watching her trace the network in candle smoke and pencil lines—payments, threats, disappearances, decades deep—and the ledger had shown him something worse than corruption. It had shown him *infrastructure*. Corruption was a man taking a bribe. Infrastructure was a schedule.

"So we go in at dusk," Silas said. "When the barges come. Everyone watches the water."

Casper turned to look at her. She was fourteen, maybe fifteen—she didn't know, and neither did anyone else—and she was planning an assault on a fortified worksite with the same flat practicality she brought to reading channels.

"No," he said.

"Yes."

"Silas—"

"You need someone in the water." She said it the way she said everything, without decoration. "The fence goes to the bank on the far side. There's a gap under it—I can see it from here, where the scour hole cut the mud away. Someone small goes under the wire and opens the gate end from inside. That's me. You're too big, and you swim like a man anyway—all arms and apology."

He wanted to tell her no again. He catalogued the reasons—her age, the risk, the fact that she was the best navigator on this stretch of river and losing her would mean losing the route downstream, the cache, the whole fragile architecture of the plan. All true. All beside the point.

The truth was simpler and worse: he could not bear the arithmetic of spending her. Not her. He'd spent five years refusing to spend anything of himself, hoarding his invisibility like a miser, and somewhere between the empty camp and the flooded church he had understood that Silas had quietly become the thing he was unwilling to lose. Telling her no would protect nothing. She'd simply go alone, later, without him.

"Dusk," he said finally. "We watch one more cycle first. If anything changes—if the pattern breaks—we pull out and come back tomorrow. Agreed?"

"Agreed." She spat in her palm and held it out, a river kid's oath, and he took it, her grip surprisingly strong, her palm cool as wet stone.

* * *

Dusk came up gold and bruised, and with it the barges.

Casper heard them before he saw them—diesel engines idling low, running without lights—and then two flat-decked vessels slid around Kettle Bend, riding heavy, and the whole camp's attention swung to the water exactly as Silas had predicted. Workers scrambled to the loading cradle. Supervisors shouted numbers. The guard abandoned his circuit to help swing the boom's hopper over the first barge's hold, and for eleven minutes—Casper counted, always counting—the entire near bank was unwatched.

Silas slipped into the water off the scour hole like a knife going into silk. No splash. He'd have missed her entirely except for the brief V of her wake and the reed stalk she'd taken with her, breathing through it, drifting under the fence line. He watched the spot where she'd gone until his eyes burned, forcing himself not to look away, as if attention were a rope he could hold from this side of the river.

Under the wire. Through. He saw the fence shiver once near the far post—something small passing beneath—and then nothing, and the longest minutes of his life began.

The barges loaded. The generators throbbed. Somewhere in the compound a dog began barking and was kicked silent. Casper pressed his cheek to the wet log and made himself breathe in counts of four, the way he'd taught himself after the blacklistings, on nights when the walls of cheap rooms felt like they were drawing in.

Then the gate latch clicked.

It was a small sound, lost instantly under the dredge's rhythm, but Casper had been listening for it the way drowning men listen for gulls. The compound's gate stood open a hand's width, and a shape detached itself from the fence line and moved toward it—not Silas; too tall—and behind the shape came Silas, ghosting low, pointing the way she'd come.

The shape reached the gate. Paused. Looked back at the tents, at the fence, at the river, and Casper saw her face in the last light: hollow-cheeked, close-cropped hair growing out unevenly, eyes that had the specific exhausted clarity of someone who has decided something terrible and final.

Amara Osei stepped through the gate and dropped flat in the mud beside them, and she was shaking—not with fear, Casper realized, but with the effort of not running, of holding herself to a crawl when every nerve screamed for flight.

"You're not rescue," she whispered. Her voice was rusted from disuse. "Rescue doesn't exist. So either you're thieves, or you're stupid, or—" Her eyes found his face in the gloom, and something shifted in them. "You're Ravensworth."

Casper went cold. "You know me?"

"I know your file." A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh, if laughter could bleed. "Hale keeps it. Shows it to new engineers. *This is what happens to guides who write honest reports.* You're a teaching aid, Mr. Ravensworth. You're the cautionary tale."

The words landed in him like cold water down the collar. Five years of careful invisibility, of ferrying supplies and counting miles, of being no one—and all the while he'd been a story told in offices, a warning shaped like a man. Even erased, he'd been *used*.

"Then you know why I'm here," he managed.

"I know why you should leave." She glanced back at the compound, where a tent flap had begun to move. "But I have three months of original samples buried in a core box under my cot, and a testimony in my head, and daughters in the provincial capital who think I'm dead. So." She looked at Casper, and behind the exhaustion something burned—steady, banked, the fire of a woman who had chosen which side of history to stand on and was waiting only for the door. "Either you're the man from Hale's file, or you're something better. Decide fast. Because in about ninety seconds, that guard finishes his cigarette, and I am not going back inside that fence."

Behind her, Silas rose out of the mud like something the river had dreamed, and grinned.

"He's something better," she said. "I checked."
