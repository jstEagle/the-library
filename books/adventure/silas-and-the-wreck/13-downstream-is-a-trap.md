## 13. Downstream Is a Trap

The map Verity spread across her table was older than the country that had drawn it, and more honest. She'd marked it in three colors over forty years: blue for water, red for money, black for men who never came back.

"Every road downstream," she said, tapping the red marks one by one, "belongs to them now. Kessel's Ferry. Marrow Bend. The tollhouse at Graven's Cut. You show your face at any of them with a bag on your shoulder, you're a dead man by supper."

Casper leaned over the table, Silas a warm weight against his side where she'd wedged herself to see. Outside, the monsoon drummed on the tin roof of Verity's stilt house, steady as a heartbeat that wouldn't quit.

"The river, then," he said. "Main channel. We run it fast."

"The main channel has checkpoints too." Verity's finger traced the brown line of the river as it widened toward the provincial capital, two hundred kilometers south. "Floating ones. They call them *inspection platforms*. Steel barges with canvas roofs and polite young men who ask to see your cargo manifest." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I've watched them work for six years. They don't ask twice."

Silas snorted. "Then we don't carry cargo."

"You always carry cargo," Verity said. "The trick is carrying the right kind."

Casper looked up from the map. There was something in the old woman's voice he hadn't heard before—a dry, buried amusement, like a stone rolled aside to show what lived underneath. "What kind is that?"

Verity straightened, pressed her knuckles into the small of her back, and looked at him the way she probably looked at every fool who thought he'd invented trouble.

"Grief," she said. "Nobody searches grief."

* * *

They laid it out that evening in the long room above her boathouse, where the village came when the river took someone. Casper had seen the room from outside on his first night—had assumed it was a storage loft, all those benches and shuttered windows. Now he understood it was an armory of another kind: a place where sorrow was staged, rehearsed, and sent downriver dressed in its best clothes.

There were eleven of them around the lamp. Fishers, net-menders, a woman named Odile who ran the fuel barge, two brothers who poled the timber rafts. Silas sat cross-legged on a bench between Casper and Verity, very still, very awake, the way she got when adults were saying things they thought children shouldn't hear. Nobody asked her to leave. That was new to him. In the survey camps and government offices of his old life, children were furniture—present, ignored, expected not to speak. Here Silas was simply part of the count.

Odile spoke first. "You want to move papers to the capital. Inspectors, journalists, whoever Verity's cousin trusts at the water board." She said it flatly, testing the shape of it. "And every checkpoint between here and there is paid for by the dredging company. So we put the papers where no man with clean boots will ever put his hands."

"On a body," said one of the raft brothers. His name was Tam; his brother was called Little Tam, though he was the larger of the two by a head. "We put them on a body, and we take the body home."

Casper felt the room shift around him—the particular hush of people arriving together at something both terrible and funny, which is the only kind of joke that survives hard places.

"There's a coffin in the boathouse," Verity said. "Cedar. Made it myself, four years ago, for old Mateo when the fever took him. He never used it." She shrugged. "His family wanted him burned on the sandbar, river custom. So the box waited. Cedar keeps."

"And the mourners?" Casper asked.

"We are the mourners," Odile said, and now she did smile, thin and sharp as a filleting knife. "Every family on this river has lost someone to that dredge or the flood or the fever. When one of ours goes home, the whole valley turns out. That's not theater, guide. That's just true. We've had so much practice grieving that grief is the one thing they'll never stop us for."

Tam nodded slowly. "Checkpoints wave funerals through. It's bad luck to search a coffin. Even the cartel boys know it—they grew up on this water same as us. Their mothers would rise out of it to slap them."

"It's not the searching I'm worried about," Casper said. He'd been turning the plan over since Verity first said *grief*, looking for the flaw the way he'd once read a river for the rock under the shine. "It's the questions. A funeral barge comes through with eleven strangers aboard, and some junior officer gets ambitious. Asks names. Asks where the deceased died. One wrong answer—"

"Then nobody gives wrong answers," Verity cut in, "because nobody answers anything. That's how funerals work. You bow your head. You look tired. You let the officer feel like a trespasser, because he is one. Grief doesn't explain itself to anyone." She fixed him with her pale eyes. "Can you do that? Look grieved?"

"I've had practice," Casper said quietly. "Five years of it."

Something passed across the old woman's face—not pity, exactly. Recognition. "Then you'll be our chief mourner," she said. "The brother of the deceased. Closest kin sits nearest the box and says nothing to anyone. Perfect casting."

"And me?" Silas said.

Everyone looked at her. She sat there with her chin up and her arms crossed, daring the room to tell her she was staying behind. Casper knew that look. He'd worn it in mirrors for years.

"You're the daughter," Verity said. "Kindest thing a child can be at a funeral is heartbroken. Sit by the coffin, hold the flowers, cry if you can manage it. If any soldier asks you a question, look at him like he kicked a dog and say nothing at all." She paused. "Can you cry on command?"

Silas considered this with the seriousness she gave everything. "I can think about the survey boat," she said. "The one that took my parents' crew. I can think about the sound it made."

The room went quiet. Casper's chest did something complicated and painful.

"That'll do," Verity said softly.

* * *

They built the deception over two days, and it was stranger than any expedition Casper had ever provisioned.

The cedar coffin came out of the boathouse smelling of resin and years. Inside, Verity and Odile constructed a false bottom from slats and waxed canvas—a shallow tray beneath the pillow-rest where Amara Osei's sealed samples, the field notes, the journal, and Verity's own ledger pages were wrapped in oilcloth and packed tight as a keel ballast. Above them they arranged a figure: bundled sacking shaped and bound into a human form, dressed in a dead man's shirt that Verity produced from a chest without explanation, face covered with a white cloth embroidered at the corners with river symbols Casper didn't recognize.

"He needs a name," Silas said, standing over the coffin with her hands on its rim.

The room stopped. Casper realized then that for the villagers this wasn't entirely a ruse. The coffin represented every person the river had given back empty-handed. A nameless dead man was an insult to all of them.

"Give him mine," Casper said.

They all turned. He kept his eyes on the sacking figure, on the white cloth, and found—to his surprise—that he meant it.

"My father's name was Elias Ravensworth," he went on. "He worked these rivers before I was born. Verity remembers him. He died owing nothing to anyone, and nobody ever made a monument of him. Let him ride downriver doing one last good turn. Better than most of us get."

Verity looked at him a long moment. Then she took a scrap of paper and wrote *ELIAS RAVENSWORTH* in careful letters, and tucked it inside the coffin lid where the dead could read their own names.

"That's either very brave or very foolish," she said.

"Usually the same thing on this river."

Silas touched the coffin rim once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Elias," she said, trying it out. "Okay. But he's my uncle now. Family sticks together."

That night Casper couldn't sleep. He climbed down to the boathouse and sat on the gunwale of the funeral barge—a broad, flat-bottomed craft with a canopy frame, currently stripped bare—and listened to the rain working the roof. After a while, footsteps on the dock planks. Silas dropped down beside him without asking, the way she did everything.

"Do you think it'll work?" she asked.

"No."

She glanced at him. "Then why are we doing it?"

He watched the black water slide past the pilings. Somewhere out there were men with clean boots and a director in a dry room who had spent five years believing Casper Ravensworth was finished, broken, safely erased. Every plan he'd ever trusted had been built on outrunning that belief. This one was different. This one walked straight through the front door wearing mourning clothes.

"Because hiding hasn't worked," he said. "I hid for five years. Changed countries, changed work, kept my head down and my name quiet. And it bought me nothing except being alone when it mattered." He turned to look at her—this fierce, unsentimental kid who'd lost her parents to the same machinery and come out swinging. "Your aunt Verity figured out something I'm still learning. You can't disappear your way to safety. Sometimes the safest place is right in the open, surrounded by people who'll stand with you."

Silas chewed on that. "So we're not hiding the evidence. We're hiding it in plain sight."

"In plain sight," he agreed. "Under a dead man's name."

"Elias's name." She smiled faintly. "He'd better be lucky."

* * *

They launched at dawn on the third day, under a sky the color of wet slate.

The barge rode low and slow, poled by Tam and Little Tam at the stern, Odile at the tiller, the rest arrayed along the benches in their borrowed sorrow. Verity stayed behind—too known, too recognizable—but she stood on her dock as they pulled away, and she raised one hand, palm out, and held it there until the current took them around the bend. Casper understood it was the closest thing to a blessing the river offered.

The coffin lay amidships under a woven cloth, banked with cut reeds and white flowers Silas had gathered before light. Casper sat beside it, chief mourner, hands folded, eyes lowered. It should have been an act. Somewhere in the second hour, poling down the swollen brown river past drowned fences and struggling trees, it stopped being one. He thought about Elias Ravensworth, whom he barely remembered—a big quiet man who'd smelled of engine oil and had once carried Casper on his shoulders through a flooded market so the boy could see over the crowd. He thought about everyone else the coffin stood in for. Amara Osei, caged at Kettle Bend. The survey team in the unmarked tributary. Silas's parents. Himself, in a way—the man he'd been before two countries decided he was a liability instead of a witness.

Grief didn't need performing. It needed only permission.

The first checkpoint appeared just after noon, where the river narrowed between stone bluffs: a steel inspection platform moored midstream, canvas-roofed, with a chain stretched across the channel and two skiffs riding at anchor. Men in gray uniforms moved along its deck. Casper heard Silas's breath change beside him and reached over without looking and took her hand. She gripped back hard enough to hurt.

"Funeral barge," Tam called out as they approached, unhurried, bored even, a man doing a chore he'd done a hundred times. "Make way for the dead."

A young officer stepped to the platform rail. Up close he couldn't have been more than twenty-two, rain beading on a cap a size too large, and Casper watched him wrestle visibly with procedure versus superstition. His eyes went to the coffin, to the flowers, to Silas's bowed head, to Casper's hollow stare.

"Manifest?" the officer said, but softly, already apologizing.

Tam handed over a folded paper—Verity's network had prepared it, a death certificate stamped by a village elder whose stamp was older than the cartel. The officer read it, glanced again at the coffin, and something in his face shifted. Casper had spent enough years reading clients on mountains to know the exact moment fear of superiors loses to fear of the dead.

"Who—" the officer began, then stopped himself. Cleared his throat. "Name of the deceased?"

Silence. Eleven people looking at the rain. Casper lifted his head just enough to give the boy a look he'd learned from Verity: not defiance, not pleading. Just the vast, patient indifference of a man standing over his brother's body, wondering why anyone was talking.

The officer folded the certificate and handed it back. "Pass," he said. "Condolences." He waved the chain down himself.

They slid through. No one spoke until the platform was a smudge behind them, and then Little Tam laughed—one short bark, quickly strangled—and Odile said, "One," and tapped the coffin lid twice, gently, like knocking on a neighbor's door.

Two more checkpoints followed that afternoon and evening, and each time the ritual held: the slow approach, the bowed heads, the certificate, the officer who wanted to ask and couldn't. At the third, a dog on the platform barked at the coffin and had to be dragged away yowling, and Silas's shoulders shook against Casper's arm, and he couldn't tell if she was crying or laughing, and decided it didn't matter.

They tied up at dusk in a blind channel Silas chose herself—a slot of black water behind a willow island, invisible from the main river. Cold rice, shared in silence, the canopy dripping. Tomorrow they'd pass Marrow Bend, the largest town on the route, the deepest into cartel territory, and everyone knew it.

It was Silas who broke the quiet, lying back against the coffin's flank with her hands laced behind her head.

"Elias is doing well," she said. "Three checkpoints and nobody even touched him."

"Don't jinx him," Little Tam said solemnly.

"He's not jinxed. He's family." She said it easily, tossing it off, but Casper caught the look she gave him afterward—quick, checking, the way she checked whether a channel was deep enough before committing the boat. Family. She'd said it before, but tonight, floating toward the worst water of their lives with a dead man's name between them, it landed differently. Not a claim. An anchor.

Casper lay back beside her and looked up through the canopy at the rain-blurred stars and thought: whatever happens at Marrow Bend, whatever waits downstream, this—these people, this ridiculous brave coffin, this kid teaching him to belong to something—is already worth what it costs.

From the stern, Tam's voice, low: "Marrow Bend tomorrow. They've got a proper garrison there. Real soldiers, real orders."

"Then we'd better be the saddest barge they've ever seen," Odile said.

In the dark, under the woven cloth, Elias Ravensworth rode on toward the capital, carrying other people's truths in his cedar chest—and at Marrow Bend, waiting in the rain, stood a young officer with a lantern and a list of names, and one of the names on that list was Casper's.
