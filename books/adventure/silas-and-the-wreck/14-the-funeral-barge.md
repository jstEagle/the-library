## 14. The Funeral Barge

The dead man in the coffin was called Tomas Bellwether, and he had never existed.

Silas knew this because she'd helped build him. Three nights in Verity's stilt house, sawing pine and planing it smooth while the rain drummed the tin roof like a thousand small hands demanding entry. Aunt Verity had insisted on real joinery—dovetails, no nails where a customs man's probing fingers might feel them—and Silas had learned more about coffin construction in those three nights than she'd ever wanted to know about anything.

"He has to be heavy," Verity had said, thumping the lid. "Dead men are heavy. Nobody questions a heavy box. It's the light ones they open."

So they'd weighted him with sandbags along the spine and lead sheeting under the shoulders, and packed the hollow of his chest—not his belly, because bellies were the first place searchers looked—with oilcloth-wrapped bundles: Amara's sealed samples, the survey notes, Casper's own account written out in a careful hand, and forty pages copied from Verity's ledger in her cramped, iron-fisted script. Forty years of payments and disappearances, every name that could be named.

Tomas Bellwether was going to be buried in the provincial capital, said the papers Verity's network had forged, by his grieving sister, who was traveling downriver with six mourners and a boatman.

Silas was the sister.

She stood at the rail of the barge now as it pulled away from Verity's landing, wearing a black dress that smelled of camphor and another woman's life, and watched the village shrink behind her. Verity had pressed something into her palm at the last moment—a river stone, flat and grey, worn smooth as a coin.

"For your pocket," the old woman had said. "When your nerve goes thin, hold it. Stone doesn't lie about being stone. Neither should you."

The barge was a funeral barge the way Tomas Bellwether was a dead man—which is to say, completely, if you didn't look too hard. Old Doss piloted it, a leathery man who had carried actual coffins on this same route for thirty years and knew every checkpoint officer's grandfather. The six mourners were river people: two women from the stilt village, a fisherman with a face like folded laundry, a boy of nineteen named Pell who kept forgetting to look sad, and old Marta, who did not need to pretend grief because she had buried a real son in a real flood and wore her mourning like skin.

They had practiced for two days. How to walk when carrying a coffin—slowly, always slowly, grief has no hurry. How to weep without sound, which was harder than weeping with it. Where to put your eyes: not on the officers, not on the ground, but on some middle distance where the dead live.

"And you," Verity had said to Silas on the last night, gripping her wrist hard enough to leave marks, "you're the worst liar on this whole river. So don't lie. Grief is true even when the dead man isn't. Find the true thing inside the false one and hold onto it like a rope."

Silas had thought she understood.

She did not understand until the first checkpoint.

* * *

It came an hour past noon, where the river narrowed between clay banks and a government platform jutted out on pilings like a rotten tooth. Two soldiers, a barrier chain, a table with a ledger. A sergeant with a mustache he clearly tended more carefully than his rifle.

Doss brought the barge in slow, letting the current do most of the work, and killed the engine with a cough of smoke. The mourners arranged themselves around the coffin, which sat amidships under a canvas awning strewn with frangipani—the flowers real, cut that morning, because Verity said no detail was too small to be true.

"Business?" the sergeant called.

"Burial," Doss answered. "Brother of the family. Going down to Greenfield Cemetery, capital side."

Papers changed hands. Silas stood at the head of the coffin with her hands folded, staring at the middle distance, holding the stone in her pocket so hard its edges printed her palm. She could hear everything—the scratch of the sergeant's pen, the soldiers' boots on wet planking, Pell's breathing going quick and shallow beside her. *Slow down,* she wanted to tell him. *Grief has no hurry.*

The sergeant came up the gangway. He looked tired. He looked bored. He looked at the coffin and made a small gesture—half salute, half apology—that men make around death everywhere, in every country, in every language. Something in Silas's chest unclenched.

Then he looked at her.

"Sister?" he asked.

She nodded. Her voice had gone somewhere and left no note saying when it would return.

"Condolences," he said, and stamped the paper, and waved them through, and the barrier chain rattled down, and the engine coughed back to life, and it was over.

It took Silas a full ten minutes downstream before her hands stopped shaking. Marta, sitting beside her, put one dry hand over hers without looking, without comment, the way river people do things.

"You held," Marta said.

"I didn't say anything."

"That's how you hold."

* * *

The second checkpoint was different.

Everyone on the barge felt it before they saw it—the way you feel weather change in your teeth. Doss slowed at the bend above Kettle Flats and squinted at the moored patrol boat, at the four soldiers instead of two, at the young lieutenant standing on the platform with a clipboard and the particular stiffness of a man who intended to find something.

"That's a new one," Doss murmured. "Lieutenant Voss. Transferred in three weeks ago. Eager." He said *eager* the way other people said *cancer*.

There was no choice about stopping. There was only choice about everything after.

"Same story," Doss said quietly, engine idling. "Nobody varies. Varying is what gets boxes opened." His eyes found Silas's. "Sister. If he pushes—he'll push at you. You're the paper's name. You ready?"

No, Silas thought. I am eleven years old and I am terrified and my hands are sweating through black cloth.

"Yes," she said.

The barge bumped the platform. Lieutenant Voss stepped aboard without asking, which was itself a message, and walked the length of the deck slowly, reading faces the way Silas read water—for the places where the surface lied about the depth.

He stopped at the coffin.

"My condolences," he said, to all of them and none of them. Then, pleasantly: "New regulations. All cargo manifests verified by physical inspection. You understand. Procedure."

The word *inspection* landed on the deck like a dropped anchor. Silas heard Pell's breath catch. She heard one of the women shift her weight, a small betraying sound. Under the canvas, under the frangipani, under the false floor of Tomas Bellwether's chest, forty pages of names lay waiting to hang everyone on this boat—including a girl with no surname and no papers older than a month.

*Procedure,* Silas thought. *Procedure is how they drowned the survey team. Procedure is how they erased Casper. Procedure is just a word men hide behind when they've already decided.*

Voss lifted the canvas corner. Frangipani petals slid off and scattered on the deck, white against wet wood, and Silas thought absurdly of the day her mother had strung flowers for the boat-blessing, before the *survey accident*, before the word *accident* became the river's cruelest joke.

"No," Silas said.

Her own voice surprised her. It came out low and cracked and absolutely certain.

Voss turned. "I beg your pardon?"

"You will not touch him." She stepped forward, putting herself between the lieutenant and the coffin, and something rose in her—not performance, not anymore, but the true thing inside the false one, the rope Verity told her to hold. "He was on the water forty years. He pulled drowning people out of the flood of '09 with these hands. He buried two children. And you want to open him up on a plank in front of strangers because of *procedure*?"

Every word was a lie. Every word was true. That was the trick of it, she realized, the thing Verity knew and she hadn't: grief doesn't check its facts. Grief only checks whether it's real.

"I have my orders—" Voss began.

"Then order me." She was crying now, and it wasn't acting; it was her mother, her father, the church with the waterline stain on its wall, Casper's face going under in the flooded forest when he chose her over the notes without a heartbeat's hesitation. It was all of it, everything the river had taken, pouring through the crack she'd been keeping sealed since Kettle Bend. "Order a sister to let strangers handle her brother's body like freight. Write it in your book. Sign your name to it. Go on."

The deck was very quiet. Even the soldiers on the platform had gone still.

Voss looked at her—at the tears cutting through the dust on her cheeks, at the fists knotted in black cloth, at the fury and sorrow standing together in one small soaked figure—and Silas watched him do the arithmetic that young officers do: that procedure was worth following until it became a scene, and scenes required reports, and reports required explaining why he'd humiliated a mourning family over a coffin full of nothing he could prove was anything.

He stepped back.

"Family only handles the deceased," he said stiffly, to no one in particular. "Standard exemption." He stamped the manifest without opening the coffin, without lifting the canvas again, and got off the barge like a man leaving a room where someone had died.

Doss waited until they were a full mile downstream, around the bend, out of sight of the platform. Then he began, very quietly, to laugh—a wheezing, disbelieving sound, like an engine turning over after years cold.

"Well," he said. "Well, well, well."

Marta squeezed Silas's shoulder. Pell stared at her with his mouth open. One of the women pressed a cup of tea into her hands, and Silas drank it because her body demanded something to do, and only then did she notice that her other hand had been in her pocket the whole time, closed around the river stone, holding on.

Stone doesn't lie about being stone.

Neither, it turned out, did she.

* * *

They ran dark that night, tied up in a backwater slough where the mosquitoes sang like small furious gods and the stars came down to the water in double file. No fires. Cold rice and dried fish. The coffin stayed lashed amidships under its canvas, and twice in the night Silas got up just to put her hand flat on the pine lid, feeling the weight beneath, the paper heart of a man who had never lived carrying the truth of everyone who had.

Casper would have done this part differently, she thought. He'd have planned the checkpoints down to the sergeant's bootlaces, counted the meters, run the arithmetic of risk the way he ran rivers. But he wasn't here. He'd gone into Halfway Lock with his hands up and his eyes finding hers across the water, and the last thing he'd mouthed—not said, *mouthed*, so no listening ear could catch it—was:

*You know the way.*

And she did. That was the terrible, magnificent gift of it. He hadn't given her the mission because she was expendable or because he expected to die. He'd given it to her because he'd spent weeks teaching her to read water, and somewhere in there he'd come to believe the same thing Verity believed, the same thing the whole river seemed to know except Silas herself:

That she was not cargo. Not ballast. Not a kid to be ferried to safety.

She was the current.

Toward dawn, Doss shook her awake. He crouched beside her in the grey light, and his face had lost its laughter.

"Lamp signal from the point," he said. "Two longs, one short." He paused. "That's Verity's code for trouble ahead. Checkpoint at Halfway Lock's been doubled. They're boarding everything." His jaw worked. "And there's worse, girl. Word coming down the net. They're saying the guide they took at the lock—Ravensworth—they're saying he's being moved upstream. To the capital. In chains."

Silas sat very still, the stone in her fist, the sky going pale over the water.

Upstream meant Hale. Upstream meant the dry room, the questions, the machinery that had eaten the survey team and spat out nothing but boots paired neatly in the mud.

Downstream—past the doubled checkpoint, past whatever waited at Halfway Lock—lay the capital, the inspectors, the journalists Verity's network had already begun whispering to. Lay the place where a coffin could finally stop pretending to be empty.

"They want him silent," Silas said. Her voice didn't shake. She noticed that, and marveled at it, the way you marvel at ice holding. "Then we'd better get loud."

Doss looked at her for a long moment—this scrap of a girl in a dead woman's dress, with river-mud still under her fingernails and a dead man's paper heart under her hand—and then he grinned, gap-toothed and fierce, and went forward to raise the anchor.

"We run the lock at dusk," he said. "In plain sight. Mourning barges don't hide, girl. We *arrive*."

Silas stood at the bow as the barge swung out into the main channel, the current taking hold, the capital lying somewhere ahead like a held breath. Behind her, upstream, a man she loved was walking into chains. Ahead of her, a checkpoint bristled with rifles.

She put the stone in her pocket, straightened her black dress, and set her face toward the middle distance where the dead live.

Let them look. Let them see a grieving sister, bowed and broken and harmless.

Let them learn, when the lid came off, exactly how wrong they'd been.
