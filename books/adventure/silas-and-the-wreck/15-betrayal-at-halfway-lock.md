## 15. Betrayal at Halfway Lock

The funeral barge moved at the speed of grief, which is to say slowly, and with a great deal of noise.

Casper sat beneath the woven canopy between two hired mourners—a grandmother named Odile who wept on schedule every twenty minutes, and her nephew, a broad-shouldered man who had been told only that he was being paid to look solemn and had committed to the role with unsettling thoroughness. The coffin rode amidships under a shroud of white cloth and frangipani garlands, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed in wax, lay four kilograms of documents, three sealed sample canisters, and one engineer's testimony that could bring down a directorate.

Silas crouched at the stern beside Verity's cousin Marn, learning the tiller. She'd been quiet all morning—not frightened quiet, but calculating quiet, the way she went when she was memorizing water. Casper had learned to read her silences the way she read channels: by what they bent around.

Ahead, Halfway Lock showed itself as a gray line across the green: two stone piers, a timber gate, a customs shed with a tin roof gone orange with rust. Smoke rose from behind it—breakfast fires, maybe. Or maybe not.

"You're staring," Silas said without turning around.

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring *at* the lock."

He let it go. The truth was he didn't like Halfway Lock, and hadn't since before any of this. It was the kind of place where the river narrowed and men got ideas about ownership. One gate in, one gate out, and a customs officer with a ledger and all the time in the world. Verity's plan had accounted for it—the mourning ritual was old law here, older than the cartel, older than the government that leased itself to it. A dead body traveling downstream passed unexamined. To open a coffin was to invite the death into your own house. Even clean-booted men respected that arithmetic.

Even so, Casper had counted the mourners twice. There should have been six. There were five.

"Odile," he said quietly. "Who was meant to sit where I'm sitting?"

The old woman dabbed her eyes, real tears or performed ones—he genuinely couldn't tell anymore. "Tomas. But his boy took fever in the night. He sent word he'd follow at noon."

"Word came how?"

"Runner." She shrugged, a small folding of her shoulders. "Why?"

Because runners could be bought, Casper thought. Because a sick child was exactly the kind of debt that broke a man. Because halfway down a river full of people who owed each other everything, someone had finally owed the wrong party too much.

He didn't say any of it. He looked at Silas instead, and she was already looking at him, and he saw that she had done the same arithmetic and arrived at the same ugly sum.

*Five mourners,* her eyes said. *One short.*

*Maybe nothing,* his answered.

*You don't believe that.*

No. He didn't.

* * *

They took the lock at midday, when the heat stood up off the water in visible columns and even the flies seemed to be grieving.

The lock keeper was a woman with a whistle on a lanyard and the bored authority of someone who opened and closed the same gate ten thousand times a year. She barely glanced at the coffin. "Name of the departed?"

"Anselm Root," Odile said, in a voice like wet gravel. "River worker. Drowned at Kettle Bend."

The lie was beautiful because it was nearly true; there had been an Anselm Root, drowned at Kettle Bend eleven years ago, and half the valley still claimed his ghost walked the reeds. Verity had chosen him deliberately. You did not search the coffin of a man whose ghost might be listening.

The keeper stamped the manifest. The great timber gate groaned and began to swing inward, and the barge slid into the lock chamber, walls rising on either side, water calm as held breath—

—and then the second boat came around the bend upstream.

Government gray. Outboard engine idling low. Four men aboard, and none of them wearing mourning, and one of them holding a clipboard the way other men hold weapons.

Casper's stomach dropped through the hull into the lock water.

"No," Silas breathed. "No, no—"

"Easy." He kept his voice below the engine note. "Nothing's wrong yet. They board, they ask questions, we are six heartbroken people and a dead dredger. That's all we are."

"They'll open it."

"They won't."

"How do you know?"

Because Verity had drilled them for two days, because the ritual held, because fear was a current and you didn't fight a current, you read it—but mostly because she was fourteen years old and looking at him like he was the last solid thing in a flooding world, and he would have said anything to keep her hands from shaking.

"I know," he said, "because I'm going to make sure they never get the chance."

* * *

The gray boat tied off above the gate. Three men dropped onto the lock wall and walked the length of the barge with the unhurried gait of people who had already been paid for whatever they found. The leader was young, soft-handed, with a customs badge pinned crooked over his heart. His boots were clean. Of course they were clean. Clean boots were practically a uniform now.

"Mourning passage," the man said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an apology either. "Standard inspection. Open the manifest."

Marn handed it over. The officer read it upside down first, which Casper noted with distant professional admiration—it was a trick Hale's people used, checking whether manifests were written to be read or written to survive being read. Then he looked at the coffin for a long moment.

"Drowned at Kettle Bend," he said.

"Eleven years back," Odile said, weeping on cue. "We only just found enough of him to bury."

Something flickered across the officer's face—doubt, or its cousin, greed. "Then it's a strange cargo for a strange route. And I'm told—" he consulted the clipboard, though Casper would have bet his remaining good hand there was nothing about coffins on it, "—that this barge may also be carrying recovered property. Survey equipment. Documents. Property of the provincial hydrographic office."

"Sir," Marn said carefully, "we carry a dead man and some flowers."

"And I'm told one of your passengers is a guide." The officer's eyes came up and found Casper's, and stayed there, and Casper understood with cold clarity that this had never been an inspection at all. This was a retrieval. Somewhere upstream, a runner had been paid in medicine, and somewhere downstream, a name had been sold. *A guide.* Not a description. A name. "Ravensworth. Is that right?"

The lock chamber was thirty meters long and eight wide, walled in cut stone, filling now as the keeper worked the sluice to raise them to the upper reach. Water climbed the hull. There was no current in here, no channel, no trick of the flood forest. There was one gate behind them, closing, and one ahead, shut, and four men with a clipboard and a warrant that probably didn't exist.

Casper had spent five years learning exactly one skill: how to leave. Every blacklist, every burned bridge, every border crossed at night had taught him the same lesson—that survival was a matter of deciding early, committing fully, and never looking back at the thing you sacrificed. Grief taught him the rest: that sometimes the sacrifice was the whole point.

"Ravensworth," he said pleasantly. "That's me. Terrible reputation. You've heard."

"I've heard you're carrying documents that belong to the hydrographic office."

"I'm carrying my fare and a headache." He spread his empty hands. "Search me."

The officer gestured, and two of the men came aboard. They were not gentle. They went through the mourners' bundles, tipped out Odile's basket of garlands, made the nephew turn out his pockets. One of them lifted the shroud at the head of the coffin, looked at the frangipani, looked at the wax seal showing beneath the cloth, and hesitated.

That hesitation was the ritual working. That hesitation was Anselm Root's ghost standing on his own lid. Casper watched the man's hand hover and thought, *hold, hold, hold*—

"Open it," the officer said.

And the arithmetic changed.

* * *

Later—much later, in a dry room with a barred window, Casper would replay those ninety seconds the way he replayed every disaster he'd ever failed to prevent. He would count the things he could have done differently and find, as always, almost nothing. The gate was shut. The boat was boarded. The officer's patience had a shorter life than the sluice needed to fill the chamber.

There was exactly one move left on the board, and it was a bad one, and it was the only one.

"Silas." He said her name quietly, in river-code cadence, the clipped syllables they'd built between them over a hundred miles of hidden water. *Listen. Don't argue. Don't look at me.*

She looked at him anyway. Of course she did.

"The bilge," he said. "Now."

Her eyes went wide. Then narrow. Then she understood—not the words, but the shape of them, the whole architecture of what he was about to do, because she was the sharpest reader of water he had ever known and he was, at that moment, water.

The bilge access lay beneath the stern boards, a shallow well where the patched hull wept its slow leak. Beneath the bilge plate, wedged against the keel where only a child who knew this exact class of river barge would think to look, was the cache tube—Verity's design, brass-capped, weighted, sealed. It had ridden there since the village. It had been there the whole time, under their feet, under the mourners, under the coffin, because the best hiding place on a funeral barge is the part everyone believes is broken.

Silas went to the stern. Knelt. Made a show of seasickness, of misery, of a child huddled over the boards—and her hands, invisible beneath the drape of her shirt, worked the plate free.

The officer was prying at the coffin's wax seal with a knife. Odile's wailing had risen to something operatic. The nephew had discovered genuine outrage and was spending it loudly. The lock keeper shouted something about protocol from the wall and was ignored.

Casper stepped between the officer and the coffin.

"That's far enough."

"Step aside, Ravensworth."

"It's a dead man, son. Whatever they're paying you, it isn't enough to pay for what comes after. Ask anyone on this river what happens to the man who opens Anselm Root's box."

"I don't believe in ghosts."

"No," Casper agreed. "But you believe in Director Hale, and I'm about to save you from a worse fate, which is meeting him having failed." He raised his voice—not shouting, just lifting it over the water, the way a guide lifts his voice across a gorge. "You want your recovered property? Fine. It's not in the coffin. It never was. It's in the lock."

Every head turned toward the water. It was the oldest misdirection in guiding—point the client at the ridge while the trail runs along the creek—and it worked exactly as well here as it ever had on rich men in expensive boots. For three full seconds, four trained men stared at brown lock water rising around a funeral barge.

Three seconds was plenty.

Under the stern boards, Silas slipped the cache tube into the water and pushed it down along the hull, deep, past the weed line, into the silt shelf at the lock's northwest corner where the masonry had crumbled and the river kept its own counsel. He saw her do it—or rather, he saw nothing at all, which was how he knew it was done. She surfaced the lie with a coughing fit, clutching the gunwale, and the nearest agent grabbed her arm, and she spat at him with such authentic fourteen-year-old venom that he let go.

"There's nothing in the water," the officer said, flushing.

"Then open the coffin," Casper said, "and explain to your director why you spent forty minutes desecrating a corpse and found flowers." He smiled, and put five years of blacklisted, erased, unseen loneliness into the smile, and made it a weapon. "Or take me. Just me. I'm the guide. I'm the one whose name you actually have. Everything else on this boat belongs to grief, and grief doesn't testify."

The officer looked at the coffin. At the water. At Casper.

At the clipboard.

"Take him," he said.

* * *

They bound his wrists with a zip tie and put him in the gray boat, and the whole time Casper kept his face bored and his heart hammering out a rhythm he hoped to God Silas could read: *northwest corner, silt shelf, below the weed line, wait for dark, wait for rain, trust no one but the family, go to Verity, finish the route.*

He looked at her exactly once, as they pulled away. She was kneeling at the stern again, arms wrapped around her knees, small and furious and utterly composed, and her eyes said what her mouth knew better than to say aloud.

*I'll find it.*

*I know,* he answered, and meant it more than he had ever meant anything, and then the lock gate opened and the barge fell behind and the gray boat carried him downstream toward whatever came next.

It occurred to him, somewhere in the middle of the longest hour of his life, that he had spent five years perfecting the art of disappearing, and had just used every ounce of that expertise to make himself impossible to miss. The irony would have made him laugh if his hands weren't tied. Instead he watched the banks slide past—reeds, stilt houses, children waving at the government boat without knowing what it carried—and did the only navigation left to him.

He trusted the river.

He trusted a girl who could read it blind.

And somewhere behind him, in the brown water of Halfway Lock, four kilograms of truth sat in the silt, waiting for the only person alive who knew exactly where to dive.
