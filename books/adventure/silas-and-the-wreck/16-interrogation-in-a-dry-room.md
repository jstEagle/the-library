## 16. Interrogation in a Dry Room

They took his boots first.

That was the detail Casper would remember afterward—not the zip ties, not the hood, not the long ride in a vehicle that smelled of diesel and wet canvas—but the boots. A man in a gray uniform crouched at his ankles with the unhurried competence of someone who had done this many times, unlaced them, drew them off, and set them side by side against the wall like a pair waiting for their owner to come back from a walk.

"Regulations," the man said, and there was almost an apology in it.

Casper said nothing. He had learned years ago that silence was a currency, and he was rich in it.

The hood came off in a room that was dry in a way nothing in the valley had been dry for weeks. Dry walls, dry floorboards, dry air that tasted faintly of paper and old varnish. A converted customs house—he knew the type. High windows barred with fresh steel over old ironwork, a counter once used for weighing cargo now pushed against the far wall, ledgers still shelved above it, their spines bleached by decades of river light. Someone had put a table in the middle of the room and two chairs facing each other across it, and the arrangement had the studied casualness of a stage set: *this is only a conversation.*

A lamp burned on the table. Outside, through the high windows, the sky was the color of wet slate, and the rain came down with the steady patience of something that intended to keep going long after everyone in this room was gone or dead.

His hands were free now. They wanted him comfortable. That told him more than the hood had.

He sat because sitting was what the scene required, and he made himself look around slowly, cataloguing the exits he couldn't use, the window he couldn't reach, the door behind which footsteps were pacing—two men, maybe three, rotating a watch. Old habits. Five years of being a man nobody looked twice at had taught him how to be looked at again.

The far door opened without ceremony.

Director Hale had aged well, which was somehow worse. The same silver at the temples Casper remembered from the inquiry—the official inquiry, the one that had eaten his name in two countries and handed out careers as consolation prizes. The same half-rimmed glasses. The same voice, low and reasonable, the voice of a man who had never once raised it because he had never once needed to.

"Casper Ravensworth." Hale said the name carefully, tasting it, the way you'd test a rope before trusting your weight to it. "You know, I've thought about this meeting more times than I can count."

"You'll forgive me if I haven't returned the favor."

Hale smiled as though Casper had said something charming, and took the chair opposite. Up close there were new lines around his eyes, fine ones, the kind that come from squinting at documents rather than weather. His hands were clean. His nails were trimmed. Somewhere under all that dryness, Casper thought, was a man who had signed orders that drowned a valley, and his hands had stayed clean the whole time.

"I want to begin," Hale said, "with an apology."

"Don't."

"Hear me out. You're owed one." Hale folded his hands on the table, a gesture of openness so precisely calibrated it could have been printed in a manual. "What happened at Kettle Gorge was a tragedy. The inquiry was rushed. Political pressure from two governments, and you—you were the convenient variable. A freelance guide, no institutional backing, no family with lawyers. You were the shape the story needed." He sighed, genuinely, or in a way indistinguishable from genuine. "I have spent five years regretting my part in that."

"Your part," Casper said. "Which part was yours. The rushing? Or the story?"

Something flickered behind the glasses—quick, then gone, smoothed over like water closing after a dropped stone.

"The past is complicated," Hale said gently. "The present doesn't have to be. I'm here to offer you a way out of it. Both of them, actually."

* * *

He laid it out the way a surveyor lays out a route: clearly, logically, with every hazard marked and every hazard, conveniently, surmountable.

The notes. The sealed field reports Amara Osei had been forced to falsify, the original samples, the journal pages—all of it, everything Casper had pulled out of the tributary's throat, everything Verity's network had been moving downriver inside a coffin. If Casper told him where it was—or better, sent word, made a call, wrote it down—then the machinery would reverse itself. Charges withdrawn. The blacklistings dissolved by ministerial order. His name restored in both countries, stamped and sealed, a man who could cross borders again, hold a license again, stand in a courtroom and say *my name is* without flinching.

"And Silas?" Casper asked.

Hale blinked, as if the question were slightly off-key. "The girl?"

"The girl I was ferrying when your men started shooting at boats."

"No one has shot at any boats." Hale's tone didn't change, but the temperature of the room did, a degree, the way a cellar cools when a door opens somewhere below. "There was an incident on the river involving smugglers. We responded appropriately. As for the child—she'd be looked after. There are programs. Educational placements. She'd want for nothing."

"She wants for nothing now."

"A river orphan running supplies for a blacklisted guide through flood country in monsoon season." Hale spread his hands. "Forgive me, Mr. Ravensworth, but that's not a life. That's a eulogy waiting for a date."

Casper looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked against the high windows. Somewhere below the floor, water moved—a drain, a culvert, the river itself whispering under the town like a prisoner pacing a cell.

"You've done your homework on me," Casper said. "So you already know how this goes. Last time somebody offered me a clean name in exchange for a quiet mouth, I took the deal. Did you know that? Not the version in the inquiry record. The real one. Before the hearing, a man came to my camp—polite, gray coat, same voice you've got—and told me that if I testified about what the equipment logs actually showed, I'd be ruined, and if I kept to the prepared statement, I'd be compensated. I was twenty-nine and tired and I believed that names could be given back." He turned his hands over, looked at the palms, the rope scars, the burn scar from a stove on a boat that no longer existed. "I gave them the statement. And they erased me anyway. Because the deal was never the deal. The deal was finding out whether I'd sell. Once they knew I would, I wasn't worth keeping."

Hale's face did not move. But his thumb, resting on the table, pressed a little harder against his forefinger, and Casper saw it, and knew he was right, and felt something in his chest that was not fear and not anger but a cold, clean clarity, like the first breath after surfacing.

"So here's what I know about your offer," Casper said. "It isn't a restoration. It's a purchase. And I'm not for sale twice."

* * *

Hale let the silence sit for a while. He was good at silence; he probably practiced it. Then he rose, walked to the barred window, and stood looking out at the rain with his hands clasped behind his back, a man admiring weather he had ordered.

"Do you know what the tributary is, Ravensworth?" he said, conversationally. "Not the river. The project. It's a ledger entry. A line item in a development portfolio worth more than every village on that water combined, multiplied by ten. When it's finished, there will be jobs, roads, a hospital in the district capital with my name on a plaque. Progress requires sediment control. Sediment control requires dredging. Dredging produces contamination, and contamination produces—" he waved a hand, plucking the word from the air— "*noise*. Letters. Surveys. Engineers with consciences. Noise is not evil, Mr. Ravensworth. Noise is simply unfinished business. My entire career has been the management of unfinished business."

"Amara Osei is unfinished business too."

"Engineer Osei is a guest of the program. She's being treated very well." Hale turned back from the window, and for the first time the reasonableness thinned, and Casper saw the thing underneath it, patient and bottomless. "Her family, however, lives in a coastal town with a storm season coming. Roads wash out. Ferries sink. Accidents happen constantly this time of year—it's remarkable, really, how often. So you understand my position. I don't need her testimony buried. I need it *authored correctly*, and she has already agreed to author it. All I need now is the physical evidence, which currently sits in the custody of a teenage girl and a network of river people who think a coffin is a filing cabinet." He smiled thinly. "Sentimental. But sentiment drowns like anything else."

"You're telling me you'll kill her family."

"I'm telling you the river is dangerous." Hale sat back down, unhurried, re-folding his hands. "And I'm telling you that you can prevent it. That's the difference between us, you and I. You've spent five years believing you were punished for the truth. You weren't. You were punished for being *disposable*. And I am offering, today, at some personal cost, to make you indispensable instead. Your name back. Your work back. A future. All you have to do is tell me where the notes are."

Casper looked at him across the lamp-lit table, and the strangest feeling came over him—not rage, not the old shame, but something quieter and more dangerous. Pity.

"That's the part you've never understood," he said. "In five years, you people have taken my licenses, my crossings, my name off every registry in two countries. You've made me a ghost with a paddle. And you think what I want—what I've *wanted*, all this time, lying awake in boat shelters listening to the rain—is to be un-erased. To be handed back." He shook his head slowly. "But I learned something on that river, Director. I learned that a name is just what other people write down. What I am is what I carry. And right now I'm carrying the only true account of what you did to that valley, and to that survey team, and to a girl's parents before either of us ever met. You can't offer me that. You can't take it either. It's not in a place. It's in motion. It's already downstream of you."

Hale's jaw tightened, a small mechanical failure in the smooth machinery of his face.

"Where is it."

"You're asking the wrong question."

"*Where is it.*"

"You should be asking," Casper said, leaning forward into the lamplight, "whether it's already arrived."

For a moment neither of them breathed. The rain filled the room with its sound, patient, immense, indifferent to both of them. And Casper watched Hale do the arithmetic—the barge, the checkpoints, the hours lost since Halfway Lock, the watcher who hadn't reported in—and watched him arrive, for the first time, at the edge of the possibility that the current had already beaten him.

Then Hale stood, buttoned his jacket, and became reasonable again, which was the most frightening thing he'd done all day.

"Keep him comfortable," he said to the door, to the men behind it. "He may feel differently in the morning."

At the threshold he paused, without turning. "For what it's worth, Ravensworth—I believe you. That it's moving. Which means we're done negotiating, and my colleagues downstream are not me. They don't offer deals. They offer *outcomes*." A beat. "I hope the girl can swim."

The door closed. The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Casper sat alone in the dry room, bootsless, nameless, smiling—actually smiling—for the first time in five years, because for the first time in five years he had refused, and refusal, he discovered, weighed nothing at all.

Outside, the rain thickened. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the river: engines. Many engines, running hard against the current, coming up fast toward the capital—

—and, farther off, lower, steadier, hidden under the rain's noise, the deep bell-note of a funeral barge's horn, one long blast, mournful as mourning, carrying something that was not grief.
