## 17. Silas Alone

The news came upriver the way bad news always did on the Kettle—fast, shapeless, and worse with every telling. A fisherman named Odo brought it to Verity's stilt village at dusk, poling his dugout in from the main channel with his hat pulled low and his voice even lower.

*They took a man off the funeral barge at Halfway Lock. Big fellow, foreign-looking. They put him in a boat and went down toward the customs house, and the barge burned to the waterline.*

Silas was mending a net when she heard it, her fingers working the knots without her, the way they did when her mind was somewhere else. She didn't stop mending. She didn't look up. She finished the row she was on, tied it off clean, and set the net down in the bottom of the skiff as carefully as if it were something that could break.

Then she walked to the end of Verity's walkway and stood there looking at the water until it got too dark to see it.

Casper Ravensworth had taught her exactly one thing about grief, though he'd never said it out loud. He'd taught it by counting. Miles, not years—that was how he measured everything, she'd realized early on. When the work was hard he counted strokes. When the night was long he counted stars through the canopy. He never counted what he'd lost; he counted what was left between him and the next thing that needed doing.

So Silas stood at the end of the walkway and counted. One: Casper was taken, not drowned. Two: men who wanted him dead would have shot him on the barge and saved themselves the fuel. Three: the cache was still where he'd sunk it, because only two people alive knew where that was, and one of them was standing on a creaking boardwalk breathing river air like it was the last thing keeping her upright.

Three things. That was enough. That was more than most people got.

Behind her, the boards announced Verity's weight before her voice arrived. The old woman came to stand beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was how Verity said most of the things she never bothered saying.

"You're doing arithmetic," Verity said.

"Casper does it."

"I know. I knew his father." Verity's hands folded over the rail, knuckles like river stones. "The same face when he's deciding something. Like the whole world is a sum and he's waiting for it to come out honest."

"It won't," Silas said. "It already came out wrong."

"Then you change the numbers." Verity turned and looked at her, and in the lamplight spilling from the kitchen window her eyes were pale and flat as a dead calm. "Girl. Listen to me once, all the way through. Those men took your mother and father and called it an accident. They took Amara Osei and called it employment. Today they took that guide and called it law. Every single thing they have ever done, they have done by naming it something else. You understand? The lie is not a wall around them. It is the only thing holding them up. Pull one stone out and the whole building comes down on their heads."

"The cache is under the water at Crooked Elbow," Silas said. "Under the leaning fig. In the old channel where the current runs backward at low water."

Verity nodded slowly. "And Hale's men will be dragging every tributary between here and Halfway Lock by morning, because they know the cargo went into the river somewhere, and they don't know that only you can find it again."

"They'll watch me."

"They'll watch everyone." Verity pushed off the rail. "Which is why we don't go tonight, and we don't go tomorrow, and we go the day after, when they're tired and bored and convinced the river has already eaten whatever they're looking for. Fear makes people fast, child. Patience makes them slow. We will be patient."

* * *

They were patient for two days, and it was the longest two days of Silas's life.

She worked the nets. She hauled water. She sat through meals and chewed and swallowed and tasted nothing. At night she lay in the loft above Verity's kitchen listening to the rain start—the true monsoon now, not the first shy试探 of it but the full-throated season—and she made herself count the sounds. Rain on thatch. Rain on water. The groan of the stilts taking the current. Somewhere below, Verity moving through her ledger room with a lamp, adding pages, closing books.

On the second night Silas climbed down and found the old woman sitting amid her papers like a spider in the center of thirty years of web. Payment records. Threat letters copied out in careful hand. Dates beside names, and beside some of the names, small crosses.

"My parents are in there," Silas said. It wasn't a question. She'd known since the church.

"Page forty-one." Verity didn't look up. "I could tell you what it says. I've read it so many times I could recite it. But you should read it yourself, someday, when this is finished. There's a difference between knowing a thing and carrying it, and you're carrying enough right now."

"Hale signed it?"

"Hale approved it. Someone smaller signed it. That's how he works—he keeps his hands so far from the ink that he could testify against himself and win." Verity closed the ledger. "Tomorrow we go for your cache. And after that, girl, you and I are going to have the conversation you've been avoiding, about what happens next."

"I'm finishing the route."

"That is the conversation."

* * *

They left before dawn on the third day, in Verity's own boat—a wide-beamed, ugly, dependable craft with a false bottom and a hull the color of the mud it loved. Verity steered. Silas rode the bow and read the water.

That was the part that surprised her, how much of Casper had gotten into her hands without her noticing. She found herself reading the surface the way he did—not just seeing the channels but hearing them, feeling the difference in the way the current combed through the reeds, the way a submerged log made the water wrinkle upstream of itself like a scar. He used to talk while he poled, half to himself, lessons disguised as muttering: *the river tells you where it's been, not where it's going; trust the eddy line, it never lies; when the water goes quiet, get worried.* She'd thought she was ignoring him. She hadn't been. Nobody ignores anything for a whole year without learning it.

Crooked Elbow came up gray-green through the drizzle, the leaning fig still holding its impossible angle over the old channel, its roots gripping the bank like fingers over a ledge. The main channel beyond it carried two government patrol boats, exactly as Verity had predicted—flat-bottomed, outboard-engined, their crews hunched under tarps and visibly bored. Bored was good. Bored meant three days of dragging hooks through silt had turned up nothing but old bicycles.

Verity killed the engine a quarter mile out and they drifted, silent, letting the current do the lying for them. To anyone watching, they were two women fishing a backwater in the rain. Verity even put a line out, because Verity understood theater the way other people understood breathing.

"The old channel fills on the flood tide and drains on the ebb," Silas whispered. "When it drains, the current near the fig runs backward—it curls around the root ball and pushes upstream along the south bank. The cache is weighted and sunk in the slack pocket behind the roots. If I go in on the ebb, the backward current will hold my scent trail downstream instead of up toward the cache. Anyone tracking the water later reads it wrong."

Verity looked at her for a long moment. "Who taught you that?"

"He did. He talked to himself constantly. I pretended not to listen." Silas checked the ebb against the stick she'd wedged in a root—water dropping, the curl beginning to show as a faint upstream ripple along the south bank. "Now. While it's running."

She stripped to her shift, strapped the waterproof oilskin roll tight across her chest, and slid over the side without a splash, because Casper had taught her that too—*enter the water like a leaf, not a stone.*

The river in the old channel was warm silk and cold knives, both at once, the monsoon runoff layering chill over the day-warmed shallows. She swam the eddy line with long easy strokes, let the backward curl carry her toward the fig, and dove.

The slack pocket behind the root ball was green-black and still, a held breath in the middle of the moving river. She felt for it the way Casper had made her practice blind—counting kicks, reading the pressure of the current against her body, trusting the geometry in her head. Four kicks past the lip of the drop-off. Down. Her fingers found silt, then rope, then the slick tarpaulin-wrapped mass of the cache, still lashed to the anchor stone where Casper had sunk it with his own hands two nights before everything went wrong.

She cut it free, hugged it to her chest, and kicked for the light.

When she broke the surface behind the fig, gasping, Verity was already there with the boat hooked close and a blanket ready, and the old woman's face wore an expression Silas had never seen on it—something raw and young underneath all those decades of armor.

"You came up on the first dive," Verity said.

"The current told me where it was."

"Hm." Verity hauled her aboard and wrapped her like cargo. "His father used to say that exact thing. Word for word." She turned the boat downstream before Silas could answer, and neither of them spoke for a long time, and the rain came harder, and the cache lay between them in the bilge like a sleeping animal.

* * *

The conversation happened that evening, in the kitchen, over rice neither of them ate.

"Run or fight," Verity said. "Those are the choices. Run, and we take the cache to the coast, find a foreign consulate, maybe see justice in ten years, maybe never. Fight, and we finish the route—the funeral barge, the capital, the inspectors Verity's network has already summoned. Fight means checkpoints. Fight means the watcher they left in the village might already be reporting our movements. Fight means—" she tapped the table, "—that if you're taken, no one is coming. Casper traded himself so the evidence would live. If you trade yourself too, there's nothing left to trade."

Silas looked at her hands. They were steady. That seemed like information worth having.

"When my parents died," she said slowly, "everyone told me the river took them. Everyone said it kindly, the way people do. *The river takes, the river gives, that's the bargain.* And I believed it, because believing it was easier than believing that men in an office decided my mother and father were cheaper drowned than paid." She looked up. "You showed me page forty-one. Now I know. And I can't unknow it, and I can't run from it, because the river is my home and I am not leaving it to the men who use it as a grave."

"So you fight."

"So I finish the route." She stood, and the steadiness in her hands moved up into her voice. "But not the way Casper planned it. His plan was built for a grown man with papers and a face nobody remembers. I'm a river kid. I don't need a funeral barge and a coffin and mourners in borrowed black. I need a channel nobody charts and a boat nobody looks at twice. I've been invisible my whole life. For once it's going to be useful."

Verity studied her across the lamp, and then, slowly, she smiled—the first real smile Silas had ever seen her spend. "Your plan is worse than his."

"My plan is better than his. His got him caught."

The old woman laughed then, short and rough, and got up to fetch paper and ink. "Then we build it tonight. And girl—one condition. You take the northern maze, the flooded forest, the paths only you can read. Not the main channel. Promise me."

"I promise."

"And when you reach the capital floodgates—" Verity paused, pen hovering. "There's a chance, small, that the guide is alive. Customs houses keep prisoners when prisoners are useful. If you get there first, you wait for word. You don't go looking. Promise me that too."

Silas promised. She was almost sure she meant it.

* * *

She left alone two hours before dawn, in the smallest boat in the village—a patched skiff that sat so low in the water it looked less like a vessel than like a piece of the river that had briefly developed opinions. No engine. A pole and a paddle. The cache lashed under the floorboards beneath a layer of fish and ice, because smugglers' wisdom was smugglers' wisdom no matter whose plan it traveled in.

At the mouth of the village channel she stopped and looked back once. Verity stood on the walkway, a lamp held high, a small yellow flame against all that dark water. Silas raised her paddle in salute, the way river people did, and turned north into the flooded forest.

The monsoon had transformed the world. Trees stood waist-deep, their trunks striped with new waterlines, and the channels between them were roads that existed for only a few weeks each year—roads only she could read. She poled through cathedrals of drowned kapok, past windows of light where the canopy opened, following a route she'd mapped in her head since she was six years old: the vein behind the twin buttress trees, the cut where the current split around a sunken shrine, the long dark gallery where the water ran glass-still and the fish rose all around her like questions.

Somewhere behind her, down the main channel, men were searching. Somewhere ahead, in the capital, a director named Hale believed the truth was at the bottom of a river he'd never learned to read. And somewhere—maybe—Casper Ravensworth was alive in a dry room, counting miles.

She poled into the current and let the forest take her, and for the first time since Odo's dugout had come up the channel with its terrible news, Silas felt something that wasn't grief.

It felt like being the river itself.

By midday she had passed the first checkpoint town without slowing—she took the reed cut behind the market, the one the cartographers missed because it dried out nine months of the year, and came out below the town where the patrol boats couldn't follow. By late afternoon she was through the second, hidden in a floating mat of hyacinth, drifting with the current while soldiers stamped about on the bank twenty feet away complaining about the rain.

And at dusk, as the light went copper and the drowned forest opened onto the long straight reach that led at last toward the capital's distant floodgates, Silas saw the thing that made her heart stop and then start again, harder.

A boat, coming upriver against the current. Small, fast, riding low. And standing in the bow, soaked to the skin, one arm bound in a strip of torn shirt, a figure she knew better than she knew her own heartbeat—

—waving.
