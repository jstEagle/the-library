## 18. The Monsoon Breaks

Amara Osei had learned to measure her captivity in small units: the number of steps from the barracks to the mess shed (forty-one), the number of times the generator coughed before it caught on damp mornings (three), the number of men at Kettle Bend who would not meet her eyes when she walked past (all of them, eventually). She had been an engineer once—still was, on paper, though the paper was a lie she'd signed—and engineers measured things. It was how they kept from drowning.

She had been at the work camp for nineteen days.

She knew because she scratched a mark into the underside of her cot each dawn with a bent nail, and because every morning the water in the tributary rose a little higher against the gauge posts she'd been ordered to install herself. The monsoon was not coming. It had arrived, early and angry, exactly as the river girl's people had said it would, and the men who ran Kettle Bend had stopped pretending their operation was about surveying anything.

Tonight they were going to open the gate.

* * *

She'd overheard it the way she overheard everything now—by being invisible, by being furniture, by being the woman who had agreed to falsify reports and therefore could not be trusted but also could not be spared. Two foremen under the awning of the pump shed, rain drumming the tarpaulin like applause for something terrible.

"—Hale wants it done before the crest hits Halfway Lock. Full release. The valley floods, the camp 'evacuates,' everything downstream gets scoured clean. Samples, stashes, whatever the guide took off the mountain."

"And the villages?"

The first man shrugged in a way that made Amara's hands curl into fists beneath her poncho. "Verity's people know the channels. Most of them will get out. That's the story anyway. Natural disaster. Tragic. Unavoidable." He smiled at his own phrasing, tasting it. "*Unavoidable.* You can't prosecute weather."

They laughed. Amara stood in the rain and let it run down her face so that no one could say later whether her eyes were wet.

She did the arithmetic that night on the underside of her cot, scratching numbers beside the tally marks. The release gate sat two kilometers up the tributary, a concrete throat choked into the mountainside, built three years ago under permits her own signature had helped legitimize. When it opened fully, the impounded water behind it—eleven meters deep at last reading, and rising—would come down the narrow channel at a speed no dugout could outrun. Verity's village sat in the flood path. So did the flooded church where Casper and Silas had sheltered. So did the hidden channels Silas navigated in the dark, the ones that only worked if you had time to read them.

Hours. She could buy them hours. Maybe not more than that.

An engineer's whole life was spent learning what structures forgave and what they didn't. A gate mechanism was a machine, and machines had opinions, and Amara had spent nineteen days hauling fuel drums past that gatehouse, memorizing the rhythm of the men who tended it, learning which bolts were decorative and which were load-bearing, which cables were tensioned and which were theater. She knew, with the cold certainty that had been the only honest thing left in her since the day she'd signed her name to a lie, exactly how to make that gate fail.

She also knew what it would cost. Men like Hale's foremen didn't investigate mechanical failures; they investigated people. And there was only one person at Kettle Bend with the expertise to sabotage a sluice gate and the motive to want the valley drowned in blood instead of rain.

Her name was already on enough documents. One more wouldn't matter—except that this one, finally, would be true.

* * *

The rain came down in ropes. Amara moved through it between the third watch change and the fourth, when the sentry in the gatehouse tower ducked inside to boil tea and the relief man was always six minutes late because he cut through the covered walkway rather than the open ground. Six minutes. She'd counted for four nights.

The gatehouse smelled of diesel and wet concrete. The control housing was a dented steel cabinet bolted to the wall, its padlock replaced last month with a hasp after the original key went missing—an irony Amara appreciated even now. Inside, the winch drum gleamed with fresh grease, and the brake band—the single component that held eleven meters of impounded water obedient—was accessible with a spanner and thirty seconds of nerve.

She didn't touch the brake. Too obvious. Too fast. Instead she knelt in the dark and went to work on the thing she'd been planning since the second night, when she'd understood what the camp really was: the drain cock on the hydraulic reservoir, the one that fed the gate's actuator system. She loosened it a quarter turn—not enough to leak visibly, just enough to weep. Then she cracked the seal on the pressure gauge and nudged its needle rest pin so it would read ten percent high. Any operator checking the system tonight would see healthy pressure while the actual fluid bled away into the gravel below the floor plates.

When the order came to open the gate, the hydraulics would stall partway. The gate would hang, groaning, half-raised—a partial release, a trickle where there should have been a torrent. Enough to look like an accident. Not enough to kill a valley.

It would buy hours. And when they found the fault—

They would find the fault. She made herself see it clearly, the way she'd once made herself see the falsified sample results clearly, pen hovering over the line that would protect her family and damn everyone downstream. They would trace the weeping fitting. They would check the duty roster. They would find the one engineer in camp who had reason, and skill, and a sister in the provincial capital whose address was known to every man in Hale's chain of command.

Amara tightened the last fitting and sat back on her heels in the dark, listening to the rain hammer the roof, listening to the water behind the dam press its patient weight against the concrete like something alive that had been promised.

For nineteen days she had told herself she was surviving. That compliance was a kind of armor. That a live witness was worth more than a dead martyr, and her family needed her breathing more than they needed her righteous. All of it true. All of it also the architecture of cowardice, load-bearing and well-greased, holding up a life she could barely recognize as hers.

Casper Ravensworth had let himself be taken off that barge so a child could carry the truth downstream. He hadn't hesitated. She'd heard the story from the guard Dossa, who talked too much on night shifts and had begun, lately, to talk in a different register—not gossip but confession, the way men do when they're deciding which side of a thing they stand on. *He just put his hands out. Like he was glad to be done running.*

Amara wiped her face with her sleeve and stood.

There was one more thing to do, and it was the dangerous thing, the unforgivable-by-design thing. In the gatehouse wall locker, among the maintenance logs she was required to initial, she opened the current logbook to the hydraulic inspection page and wrote, in her own careful engineer's hand:

*Reservoir fitting 4-C shows seepage consistent with manufacturing defect. Recommend full teardown before any gate operation. Failure under load likely. —A. Osei*

Not a confession. A warning, dated and signed, that would sit in the official record whether or not anyone ever read it honestly. If the gate failed tonight, the log would show the camp's own engineer had flagged the risk. If they came for her, the words would still exist, in her handwriting, in a government book, saying what she actually believed: *this structure is being used to murder people and I refuse to be the instrument.*

It wasn't testimony. Testimony would come later, in a room with lights and lawyers, if she lived to give it. This was the first honest sentence she'd written in three years, and her hand shook doing it, and she wrote it anyway.

* * *

They came for her an hour before dawn.

She'd expected it sooner, honestly—had lain on her cot rehearsing the moment—but the delay told her something useful: they'd tried the gate first. She heard the diesel generators surge, heard the distant metallic complaint of the actuator straining, heard it stall, and heard the shouting start. The gate hung at eighteen percent open, exactly as designed. Water thundered down the tributary in a brown rope—enough to raise the river, enough to frighten, nowhere near enough to scour.

Enough to make Silas's channels deadly instead of impossible. Enough to make every hour count.

Boots on the gravel outside. Lantern light through the slats. The door banged open and two foremen filled it, and behind them stood a thin man in a dry coat whom she recognized from photographs in files she'd once been shown as a warning: Director Hale's regional man, Voss, with his soft voice and his notebook.

"Engineer Osei." Voss smiled the way rain falls—everywhere at once, touching everything. "There's been a malfunction."

"Has there." She stood slowly, making them wait, making herself tall. Nineteen days of being furniture ended here.

"The gate won't open. Fully." He let the word *fully* hang there, obscene. "And your logbook contains an entry I find... interesting. Seepage at fitting 4-C. Manufacturing defect." He tilted his head. "Funny thing, Amara. That fitting passed factory certification. I've seen the certificate."

"Certificates describe fittings that haven't spent three years underwater in silt," she said. "I described the one in front of me. That's my job. Or it was, until I was ordered to stop doing it and start describing samples that don't exist."

The younger foreman shifted. Voss raised one finger, gentle as a schoolteacher, and the man went still.

"You understand your position," Voss said. "Your family is in the capital. Your sister teaches piano on Wednesdays at the municipal hall—it's a lovely little building, right on the floodplain, incidentally. Everything downstream is on the floodplain tonight." He closed the notebook. "Help us clear the fault quickly, and none of that needs to enter the conversation. Sabotage is such an ugly word. Accidents, on the other hand—" he gestured at the rain, the roaring dark, the whole drowning world outside, "—are everywhere."

Amara looked at him for a long moment. She thought about her sister's hands on piano keys. She thought about a coffin on a funeral barge carrying documents instead of a body, and a boy-faced officer backing away from a grieving child's performance. She thought about the nail-scratched marks under her cot, nineteen of them, and how she'd once believed counting was how you kept from drowning.

Counting wasn't how you kept from drowning. Choosing was.

"Here's what I know about your accident," she said, loud enough for the foremen, for the sentry drifting toward the doorway, for Dossa standing at the edge of the lantern light pretending to be on patrol. "I know your gate is stuck at eighteen percent because your hydraulics are bleeding out through a fitting your own maintenance crew ignored for a year. I know the impoundment is over capacity and rising, and that if you force that winch manually—which your orders say to do—you'll shear the drum shaft, drop the gate entirely, and send the whole reservoir down the valley at once. Every village. Your own camp. Your own men." She stepped closer, and Voss, despite himself, gave ground. "So the choice isn't mine anymore, Director's man. Fix it properly, per my log entry, and lose your window. Force it, and drown your own. Either way, the valley gets its warning, because I've already given it—in writing, signed, in your book."

Silence, except the rain and the water behind the dam, pressing.

Voss's smile stayed fixed but his eyes went somewhere else—to calculations, to phone calls he'd have to make, to a director who did not accept failure. "Take her to the pump shed," he said quietly. "Guard her. Nobody speaks to her." And then, to Amara, almost kindly: "You've bought them hours, Engineer. Hours aren't mercy. The water doesn't care whose side you're on."

"No," Amara said. "But I finally do."

They marched her out into the deluge. Through the sheeting rain she could see down-valley, a gray blur of drowned light, and she fixed her eyes on it and did the only engineering that mattered now: she estimated flow rates, channel capacities, arrival times. Eighteen percent discharge. Rising impoundment. If someone was coming—if the guide was loose, if the girl was moving, if Verity's network had ears in this camp the way it had ears everywhere—they had perhaps five hours before Voss ran out of patience and forced the winch.

Five hours to get a valley's worth of people off the floodplain. Five hours for a coffin's worth of evidence to reach the capital.

Amara lifted her face into the rain and let it wash the last three years off her, as much as water ever could.

Behind her, in the gatehouse, a radio crackled to life, and she caught one fragment of the transmission before the wind ate it—a voice reporting movement on the main channel, a boat running the flood crest toward the provincial capital, and a name that made her heart slam against her ribs like water against a failing gate.

Ravensworth. He was alive, and he was coming, and he was racing the same water she'd just bought him time to beat.
