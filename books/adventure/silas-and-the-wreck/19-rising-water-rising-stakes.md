## 19. Rising Water, Rising Stakes

The water came for the customs house the way debts come—slowly at first, then all at once.

Casper had been counting it for two days from the high window of his cell, the way he'd once counted miles. The river rose a hand's width in the morning, another by dusk, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past the window like battering rams with their branches stripped to bone. By the third dawn it had swallowed the dock below the converted customs house entirely, and the moored patrol boats knocked against their pilings with a sound like knuckles on a door no one wanted to answer.

He'd been counting other things too. Meals: eleven, all of them cold. Questions: none since Hale walked out of the room three days ago, satisfied that silence was a leash. And footsteps on the stairs: a pattern he'd learned the way Silas read water—one set heavy, one set light, changing at midnight.

The light set belonged to a guard named Denna Okoye, though she'd never told him her name. He knew it because she talked to herself when she thought no one was listening, low murmurs in a valley dialect that Verity would have understood word for word. He knew she was from upriver because she salted her food like river people did, heavily, against a humidity that never quite left. And he knew, by the way she lingered outside his door a half-second longer each night, that something was eating at her.

On the fourth day, the water ate first.

It came up through the floor drains in the interrogation room below him, welling black and greasy across the tile where they'd sat him under a bare bulb and asked him nothing. He heard shouting then—the particular pitch of men whose plans have been interrupted by weather—and boots splashing in corridors built for dry feet. The generator coughed twice and died. The lights went out.

In the dark, Casper did what he always did: he counted. Doors between his cell and the river side of the building: four. Guards on the night shift: three, plus Denna. Boats at the flooded dock: two, maybe three if the skiff Hale's people kept for errands hadn't been pulled out. Water depth in the corridor: rising past ankle height already, judging by the sound of the boots.

And time until the tributary release upstream hit this stretch of the main channel: hours. Maybe fewer. Amara Osei had bought them time with whatever she'd done to that gate, but bought time wasn't given time. It was a loan, and rivers collected.

A key turned in his lock.

Casper pressed back against the wall as the door swung open onto a wedge of gray light—a storm lantern held by a small woman in a rain slicker two sizes too big, water streaming off its hem into the cell.

"You're awake," Denna said. Not a question. She'd known he would be. "Good. Don't waste my courage standing there blinking."

"What is this?"

"This is me being stupid," she said. "Or honest. I haven't decided which." She thrust a bundle at him wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, heavier than it looked. "From the engineer woman. Before they moved her."

Casper's hands closed around it and his heart slammed once, hard, against his ribs. *Amara.* Alive. Still fighting. "She was here?"

"Two nights. They were taking her downriver to the work camp when the water turned. Her boat couldn't make it through—they brought her back, and she knew it might be her only chance, so she paid me." Denna's jaw tightened. "Not money. She paid me with the truth. My brother worked the dredge at Kettle Bend. Two seasons. Came home sick in ways the company doctor called malaria. It wasn't malaria." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands did, just slightly, around the lantern. "I've been carrying that for a year. She put a name to it. Contamination in the sediment. Documented. Sealed. Signed off by men whose portraits hang in the provincial hall."

"Why give it to me? Why not run with it yourself?"

"Because you're the one they want," she said simply. "Every eye in this valley is looking for Casper Ravensworth. Nobody looks at a guard. That's not a curse tonight—it's a route." She jerked her chin toward the corridor. "Water's through the ground floor. The east stair floods last; it's how the old customs officers moved cargo when the river ran high. There's a skiff tied at the loading arch. Second boat from the left. The others are watched."

"And after?"

"After, you're a dead man swimming or a live man rowing. That part's yours." She paused, and in the lantern light her face was young and old at once. "My brother's name is Tomas. If any of this ever sees daylight—I'd like his name in it. Not mine. His."

"Tomas Okoye," Casper said. "I'll carry it."

Something in her shoulders let go, a tension held so long it had become architecture. Then she heard what he heard—boots in the standing water below, more than one pair, moving fast—and the softness vanished.

"East stair. Go now. Don't thank me, don't look back, and if they ask, I'll tell them I came to check your cell and found it empty because the river took you." A ghost of grim humor crossed her face. "Which will even be true, more or less. Rivers take people. Everyone knows that."

*Everyone knows that.* How many disappearances had been filed under those exact words? How many Tomases?

Casper slung the oilcloth bundle across his chest, knotting the cord tight the way he'd tie down cargo before white water. At the doorway he stopped.

"Denna. When the flood peaks, this building won't hold. Get yourself and anyone else with a conscience to high ground. Promise me."

"I've survived worse than weather," she said. "Go."

He went.

* * *

The east stair was a throat of black water. Casper felt his way down by the rail, the bundle held above his head, the cold climbing his body rung by rung—knees, waist, chest—until his boots found the submerged landing and he pushed off into open water where the loading arch had been.

The customs house had been built to face the river with authority, stone pillars and a wide arch for inspecting barges. Now the river faced back. It poured through the arch in a smooth, silent flood, and beyond it the world was rain and brown current and the shapes of drowned trees sliding past like a funeral procession.

The skiff was where she'd said it, second from the left, still tied, half-swamped, its bow line groaning against the cleat. Casper cut it with the knife he'd been denied for six days—he'd palmed it off a food tray an hour before Denna came, gambling on exactly this—and hauled himself over the gunwale, bailing with his cupped hands as the current grabbed the hull and swung it out through the arch like a door opening.

For ten seconds there was only water, huge and indifferent, and Casper doing the oldest thing he knew: reading it.

The main channel ran along the far bank where the current piled highest—that was speed, but speed was death today, debris running at ramming speed in the flood crest. Near the near bank the water backed-eddied behind a warehouse wall, slower, tangled with floating wreckage but survivable. He chose the eddy. He'd spent five years choosing eddies. Invisibility wasn't cowardice; it was hydrology.

He got the oars down and dug in, and the skiff answered—not well, shipping water through a seam that flexed with every stroke, but enough. Enough was a word he'd built his whole life on.

Behind him, the customs house loomed, its lower story already gone to the river, lanterns bobbing in upper windows like confused fireflies. He saw figures on the balcony—two, three—pointing. A shout carried flat across the water, torn apart by rain. Then a whistle, three sharp notes, the signal he'd heard at Halfway Lock, the cartel's signature, and somewhere downstream an engine caught and turned over with a hungry diesel growl.

Hale's last loyal boat. Casper had known it would be. Men like Hale always kept one vessel that answered to nothing but them—no registry, no crew roster, no questions. He'd seen it before, in the other country, in the other life, when the disaster broke and the machinery of denial spun up overnight. Some things translated perfectly across borders. Corruption was fluent everywhere.

He rowed.

* * *

The river at flood was a different animal wearing the same skin. Every channel Silas had taught him was transformed—the hidden cutoffs now highways of brown water, the sandbars gone, the snags riding high enough to decapitate a careless boater. He ran the main stem close to the drowned willow line, using the trees as a shield against eyes from the bank, and let the crest do most of the work. Twelve kilometers an hour without trying. Fifteen when the channel narrowed past the old ferry crossing.

The bundle rode against his chest inside his jacket now, oilcloth and cord, and every time the skiff slammed through a wave he felt it knock against his sternum like a second heart. Amara's testimony. Original sealed samples. Names, dates, signatures—the whole architecture of the lie, written in the hands of the people who'd been ordered to build it. In that bundle was Tomas Okoye's sickness and Silas's parents' "survey accident" and Verity's decades of ledger entries, all of it suddenly holding hands. All of it pointing the same direction: downriver, to the capital, to whoever was left there who could still be shocked.

If he made it. If the seam in the hull held. If the engine behind him didn't close the gap.

He checked it by sound, the way you check a storm by the color of the sky. The diesel was maybe two kilometers back, holding steady—not gaining, which meant its pilot was being careful in the debris field, or meant it was herding him, keeping him centered in the channel while something waited ahead. Both possibilities were bad. Casper had learned long ago that pursuit came in two flavors: the kind that wants to catch you, and the kind that wants you to keep running into the trap. He couldn't tell which flavor this was yet, and not knowing made his hands ache on the oars.

The rain thickened. Somewhere ahead, thunder rolled along the valley floor like a barrel going downstairs, and beneath it—fainter, almost imagined—another sound. A horn. Long, low, mournful.

Casper knew that horn. Every river kid in the valley knew it.

Funeral barges sounded their horns at the halfway shrines, one blast for the dead, so the living could bow their heads as the barge passed. Which meant there was a barge on the water ahead of him, in this flood, running the gauntlet of checkpoints and debris—which meant either someone had lost their mind with grief, or Verity's network had put its plan back in motion despite everything, despite the betrayal, despite Casper being taken.

Despite Silas.

His chest did something complicated. He rowed harder.

* * *

The seam gave out twelve kilometers short of the capital, in a straightaway where the flood spread wide and shallow over a drowned field, and the skiff began drinking the river faster than Casper could bail it.

He read his options the way he read water, fast and without flattery. Back: pursuit. Shore: mudflats waist-deep, a man on foot with a bundle and no boat, easy meat. Ahead: the channel bent around a bluff, and past the bend—

Past the bend, riding high and slow in the middle distance, black bunting streaming wet from its rails, came the funeral barge.

Casper shipped the oars and stood, water to his shins, and did the arithmetic of desperation. Distance to the barge: four hundred meters, closing. Depth here: less than a meter over the field, deepening toward the channel. His own strength: finite. The bundle: waterproofed, buoyant if it came to that.

He stepped over the side.

The cold hit him like a fist and the current took him instantly—too fast, much too fast—but the flood that was killing his boat was also carrying him, and he angled his body with the skill of thirty years, feet up, bundle held clear, riding the crest like the debris he resembled. The barge grew. Details resolved out of the rain: the coffin lashed amidships, the cluster of figures in mourning white, and at the stern rail a small figure with a pole, poling the barge through water no hired pilot would attempt, reading the flood the way nobody else alive could read it.

Silas saw him before he could shout. He watched it happen—watched her go rigid, watch her mouth form his name without sound, watch her turn and scream something to the others and point.

The barge began to come about, slow as a turning season.

Behind him, around the bluff, the diesel engine opened its throat.

Casper swam.
