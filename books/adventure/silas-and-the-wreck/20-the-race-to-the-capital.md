## 20. The Race to the Capital

The water told her before the sky did.

Silas felt it in the way the current leaned against her paddle, heavy and impatient, like a animal straining at a rope. The hidden channels she'd run all her life were no longer channels at all—they were veins of a single vast creature waking up. The monsoon had broken over the high country two days ago, and everything it caught was coming downriver now, all at once, looking for the sea.

She was riding ahead of it.

That was the arithmetic Casper had taught her without ever meaning to: *the flood moves slower than a boat that knows where it's going.* She'd heard him say it once, half-asleep in the flooded church, and she'd turned it over like a stone in her pocket ever since. Now it was the only thing keeping her ahead of the water—and ahead of the men.

The cache rode against her chest in an oilskin wrap, strapped under her jacket where it made her look broad-shouldered and strange. Coffin documents, Verity called them. Dead man's papers. Forty pages of sealed samples and signed orders proving what the dredging cartel had done to the tributary, to the valley, to the people who'd drowned in "survey accidents" going back fifteen years. To her mother and father.

Verity's network had carried it this far—funeral barge, halfway lock, the sunken cache beneath the willow roots. But Halfway Lock had cost them. A betrayal, agents boarding, Casper stepping off the barge with his hands up so the truth could slip away underwater where only Silas could find it. That was three days ago. Nobody knew if he was alive. Aunt Verity had squeezed her shoulder and said, *Finish it, girl. That's how you keep him alive.* So Silas had finished it. Alone, through the night channels, past the checkpoint towns, sleeping in flooded tree forks with the oilskin wrapped around her like a second heart.

Now the capital rose out of the rain ahead of her—or rather, its edge did. The provincial capital didn't end so much as dissolve into the floodplain: stilt districts, warehouse roofs, the gray shoulders of the customs houses, and beyond them, catching what little light the storm allowed, the long concrete spine of the floodgates.

And below the gates, the weir.

Everyone on the river knew the capital weir. It was older than the gates, a stepped cascade built to bleed off floodwater before it ate the city. In dry season it was a staircase of white noise. Now, with the crest coming down, it would be a wall of moving mountain. No boat ran it. No boat was meant to.

Which was exactly why the inspectors were supposed to be there.

Verity's last message had come by river-post two nights back, folded into a fish seller's ice block like something smuggled inside a smuggle: *Journalists and inspectors summoned. Weir overlook, when the crest arrives. Bring it yourself if you can. Trust no one in uniform until you see the woman with the red umbrella.*

*If you can.* Silas could. That was the whole point of her.

She swung the boat into the last hidden channel—the one that threaded behind the tannery ruins, under a bridge so old the city had forgotten it owned one. The water here was already brown and muscular, carrying branches, a dead goat, somebody's roof thatch. She paddled with her arms screaming and her mind doing what Casper's mind did: counting distance instead of fear.

Two hundred meters of channel. Then the open basin above the weir. Then—

A boat engine, upstream behind her. Not the loose rattle of a work skiff. The flat, disciplined hum of a government launch.

They'd found the cache route after all. Or they'd simply guessed that whatever Casper had buried, the river girl would come dig it up. Either way, the engine sound was getting louder, and the channel had maybe ninety meters left before it opened into water with nowhere to hide.

Silas did the thing that had kept her alive for eleven years on this river: she stopped thinking like someone being chased and started thinking like the water.

There was a side sluice near the tannery, a maintenance cut barely wider than her boat, running along the base of the gate works. Adults ignored it because it looked like a drain. Silas had shot it a dozen times in low water. In this water it would be suicide—fast, black, roofed with rusted grating for half its length.

In this water it was also the only door.

She shipped the paddle, grabbed the gunwale, and let the current take the bow toward the cut. The engine sound rounded the bend behind her. A voice through a loudhailer, flattened by rain: *"Small craft, heave to. This is a security cordon."*

She went into the dark.

The sluice took her like a throat takes a pill. Water slammed her sideways; the first grating passed close enough overhead that she lay flat in the bilge and felt rust flakes rain onto her neck. The oilskin dug into her ribs. She thought—not for the first time, but more fiercely than ever—*Casper would hate this.* He'd have counted the meters, calculated the squeeze, refused it as bad odds. That was the difference between them. He measured risk. She just refused to stop.

Then the grating ended, the walls fell away, and she shot out of the sluice into the open basin above the weir, thirty meters from the gate works, in full view of anyone standing on the overlook stairs.

No one was standing there.

The overlook was empty. Rain hammered the concrete. The red umbrella was nowhere.

Silas's stomach dropped through the bottom of the world. Verity's message had been specific. *Trust no one in uniform until you see the woman with the red umbrella.* But there was no woman, no journalists, no inspectors—just the basin, the boiling lip of the weir downstream, and the government launch now sliding into the basin behind her, its wake folding out like a hand opening.

They hadn't been chasing her to catch her. They'd been chasing her to herd her. Here. To this exact piece of water, at this exact hour, where anything that happened next could be blamed on the flood.

*Hale,* she thought. *You're not stupid. You're worse than stupid.*

She put the boat hard across the basin toward the gate works, looking for any cover, any stairwell, any shadow—and that was when she saw the other boat.

It came down the main current from the north, riding the leading edge of the crest, and even at that distance Silas knew it the way you know a walk through a wall. Patched hull. Lopsided repair where the leak had been. Someone at the stern handling it with the economy of a man who'd stopped wasting motion years ago and never started again.

Casper.

He was alive. He was soaked, gaunt, moving like his ribs were broken, and he was alive, and he was running the main current straight toward the weir because there was nowhere else the evidence could go and nowhere else he could be seen. That was his whole plan, she realized, and it was beautiful and terrible: *being seen.* He wasn't hiding anymore. He was delivering himself.

The launch saw him too. Its engine note changed—deeper, urgent—and it swung between them, cutting the basin in half.

* * *

From the water, the geometry was simple and awful.

Casper in the main channel, closing on the weir's upper lip. Silas pinned near the gate works. The launch between them, and on its deck, four men in rain gear, and at the bow rail a figure who didn't wear rain gear at all—a long coat, dry-looking somehow even in the deluge, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring public works.

Director Hale.

Silas had never seen him. She knew him anyway, the way valley children know the shape of the thing that took their parents. He raised one hand, and the launch throttled down beside Casper's boat without touching it, matching speed, herding.

The loudhailer again, closer now, and this time she could hear the words clearly, pitched to carry:

*"Ravensworth! You are in violation of a security cordon. Surrender the recovered property and yourself, and I give you my word you'll be treated fairly."*

Silas heard Casper's answer across forty meters of roaring water. He didn't shout it. He said it, almost conversationally, and the rain carried it anyway because some voices are built to be heard when it matters:

*"Your word's worth about as much as your boots, Hale. And I've seen where those have been."*

The launch lurched. Grappling hooks came over the side.

Silas didn't think. She shoved off from the gate works and drove her boat into the gap, straight across the launch's bow, small and fast and insane, forcing the bigger vessel to sheer away or crush her. It sheered. For one hanging moment the two boats were parallel, ten meters apart, and she looked directly across the rain at Casper Ravensworth.

His face did something she'd never seen it do. All the careful invisibility fell off it at once. He looked at her the way a drowning man looks at shore—not with hope exactly, but with the sudden unbearable knowledge of what hope costs.

*"Silas!"* he roared. *"The overlook! Get to the—"*

The first grappling hook took his transom.

His boat slewed. Men in the launch hauled, practiced, brutal. And the crest arrived.

She felt it before she saw it—a shudder through the whole basin, a change in pressure like the river inhaling. Then the leading edge of the flood came down the main channel, a brown wall shouldering aside everything in front of it, and Casper's tethered boat was picked up like a leaf at the lip of a drain.

The grapnel line snapped. One of the hooks tore free and took a chunk of transom with it. Casper's boat swung broadside to the current, and Silas watched—with a clarity she would carry for the rest of her life—as the water began to turn it toward the weir.

Toward the cascade. Toward the stepped white mountain that no boat survived.

*"CASPER!"*

Her voice cracked across the basin. His head came up. Through the rain, through the roar, he looked at her—at the girl in the little boat, the orphan of the survey accident, the student of every channel he'd ever named—and he did the last thing she expected.

He smiled.

Then he pointed, sharp and certain, at the overlook stairs, and mouthed words she read as easily as water: *Go. Be seen.*

The launch was fighting the crest now, engines screaming, trying to hold position. Hale stood unmoved at the bow rail, watching Casper's boat go sideways toward the drop, and Silas understood with total, sickening certainty that the Director had planned for this too. Let the flood do it. Let the blacklist guide and the evidence and the whole miserable story go over the weir together, and by morning it would be *tragic accident, fourth this season, the river is dangerous, we warned everyone.*

Casper's boat hit the lip.

* * *

Later, Silas could never say how much time passed in the next minute. It might have been ten seconds. It might have been a year.

The boat went over stern-first, hung for a heartbeat on the crest like a thing deciding, and then the flood swallowed it whole. The launch backed water, turning away, mission accomplished. Hale's coat flared as he finally moved—turning, unhurried, toward the overlook stairs, toward the place where, according to Verity's message, witnesses were supposed to be waiting.

And Silas sat in her boat in the hammering rain, holding forty pages of proof against her chest, alone on the water above the weir, with the most dangerous man in the province walking toward the only exit.

She should run. Every instinct bred into her by eleven years of survival said *run*—down the sluice, into the reeds, gone, live to carry it another day.

But Casper had pointed at the overlook. *Be seen.* And she suddenly understood what he'd been teaching her since the flooded church, since the night channels, since the moment he'd chosen her over the notes in the black water: the evidence was never the point. The evidence was just paper. The point was a person standing in the open, saying *I was here, and this happened, and you will not drown it.*

The woman with the red umbrella was late. Fine. Silas would hold the door until she came.

She drove her boat for the overlook stairs, hauled it up on the lowest step, and climbed—small, soaked, oilskin-wrapped, eleven years old and older than the river—onto the concrete platform above the weir, directly into Director Hale's path.

He stopped. Up close he had a pleasant face, the kind of face that signed things. He looked at her the way men look at weather they've decided not to acknowledge.

"Young lady," he said mildly, over the roar of the flood, "this area is closed."

"I know," Silas said. "Everything's closed. That's sort of your whole job, isn't it?"

Something flickered behind the pleasantness. Downstream, at the base of the weir, the flood was churning white over the place where a patched boat had gone in—and then, impossibly, through the spray, she saw movement. A dark shape hauling itself onto the rocks at the weir's foot. One-armed. Crawling. Alive.

Alive.

Hale followed her gaze. And for the first time, the Director of the provincial water authority looked genuinely surprised—which meant, Silas thought with a fierce, rising joy, that for the first time, he was losing.

She pulled the oilskin package from under her jacket and held it high over her head, both arms, the way you hold up a lantern, the way you hold up a child, the way you hold up anything you refuse to let the water take.

"My name is Silas," she shouted at the rain, at the launch, at the pleasant-faced man, at the whole drowned valley. "And I've got the receipts!"

Downriver, on the wet rocks, Casper raised one bloody hand in answer.

And around the far bend of the basin, small and absurd and right on time, came a flash of red—an umbrella, bobbing nearer, carried by a woman walking very fast, with people hurrying behind her.

Hale turned to look. When he turned back, his face had gone smooth and cold as still water, and he took one step toward Silas, and the flood behind him rose another meter, and the weir began, very softly, to sing.
