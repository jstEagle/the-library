## 21. Weir

The flood had a sound before it had a shape.

Casper heard it from half a mile out—a low grinding roar, like the valley clearing its throat—and he knew what it meant before he saw it: the tributary was coming down all at once, Amara's sabotaged gate buying them hours instead of days, and every one of those hours was now spent water. He drove the stolen patrol skiff hard against the main current, engine coughing, hull riding low with river it had swallowed through a cracked seam he'd stuffed with a shirt and prayer.

Ahead, the provincial capital rose out of the rain like something drowned and refusing to admit it—cranes, rooftops, the grey shoulders of the customs district, and beyond them the great concrete spine of the floodgates, gates that would either hold or wouldn't, depending on men who were, at this moment, deciding whether Casper Ravensworth was worth more dead or quiet.

Behind him, running lights. One boat. Hale's last loyal crew, the ones who hadn't fled when the money stopped making sense.

He didn't look back. Looking back cost speed, and speed was the only currency he had left.

* * *

Silas was already there when he came around the last bend.

He saw her first as a smudge against the grey—the old stone weir below the floodgates, the one the charts marked as ruined, the one she'd told him about in Verity's kitchen weeks ago, her voice matter-of-fact over the map: *When the water gets high, everything funnels here. Everything.* She stood on the broken crest of it with the waterproof satchel strapped across her chest, hair plastered flat, small against a river gone enormous. The hidden channels had delivered her exactly where the main current had delivered him, two rivers of the same truth converging on a single point of stone.

And between her and the shore, blocking the only dry path off the weir, stood Director Hale.

Casper cut the engine and let the current carry him in. There was no point arriving loudly. The whole valley was loud now.

The skiff ground against the weir's lower apron and he went up the wet stone on hands and knees, the way you climb anything when the river is watching. Rain hit his face sideways. Twenty feet away, Hale stood with his coat buttoned and his shoes—clean even now, clean always—darkening at the toes where the spray reached. Two men flanked him, and one of them held a pistol pointed not at Casper but at Silas, which told Casper everything about how the morning intended to go.

"Ravensworth." Hale's voice carried easily over the water, unhurried, the voice of a man who had conducted a hundred conversations in rooms where no one could leave. "You've made this remarkably difficult for a man with nothing left to lose."

"That's the thing about nothing," Casper said, coming up onto the crest beside Silas. She shifted toward him without looking, shoulder finding shoulder, the way she'd learned to do in flooded forests in the dark. "It turns out I never actually had it."

"The notes." Hale extended one hand, palm up, patient as a banker. "The samples. Osei's testimony, wherever you've hidden it. Give them to me, walk down this weir, and by Friday you're a licensed guide again. Cleared record. Both countries. Your name restored."

There it was. The same offer, dressed in better weather. Casper thought of the dry room, the teacup, the file with his father's name in it that Hale had slid across the table like a man showing a card trick. He'd sat in that room and understood, finally, that his name had never been the thing being bought. His silence was the commodity. It had always been the commodity. Five years ago they'd taken his name so he'd spend the rest of his life trying to buy it back, and men like Hale collected on that desperation the way Verity's people collected rainwater—systematically, season after season, from everyone upstream.

"You know what I did this morning?" Casper said. "Before I launched?"

Hale's eyes narrowed a fraction.

"I stopped counting miles." He let that sit. "Five years I measured everything in distance. How far to the next job. How far from the last disaster. How far I could stay from anyone who might ask me a question I'd have to answer honestly. And then a kid asked me why I'd protect men who'd let her drown, and I didn't have an answer, and I've been paddling toward one ever since."

The gunman shifted his weight. The weir hummed under them; he could feel it through his boots, the whole structure taking the flood's argument into its bones.

"Hale," Casper said, "look downstream."

For the first time, the Director looked away from him.

Downstream, where the river widened toward the capital's docks, boats were coming out. Not patrol boats. Not cartel runners. A press launch with a dish antenna lashed to its rail, camera operators braced against the spray. Behind it a grey government cutter flying the inspectorate pennant, and behind *that*, absurdly, a funeral barge hung with white cloth, Aunt Verity standing at its rail like a figurehead, arms crossed, daring the sky to say something about it. More boats behind those—stilt village craft, fishing punts, the whole loose ragged fleet of the river people, come up through channels only they could read, summoned by a ledger older than the cartel and a network that had spent decades learning patience.

"They've been arriving since dawn," Casper said. "Verity sent word the night we scuttled the cargo. Every journalist who ever got a whiff of the dredging contracts. Two inspectors from the capital who owe her favors going back thirty years. A photographer who's been sitting in her village for a week pretending to be a birdwatcher." He almost smiled. "You organized the silence, Director. She just organized the opposite."

Hale turned back slowly. Rain ran off his jaw and he didn't wipe it, and Casper watched something move behind his face—the recalculation of a man discovering that the room he'd built had windows all along.

"This is insurrection," Hale said. "You understand that. Obstruction, theft of state property, harboring—" his gaze flicked to Silas, and Casper felt the kid go rigid beside him, felt her weight shift forward onto the balls of her feet like a cat deciding— "a minor. Whatever happens next, you'll be charged with—"

"Yeah," Casper said. "Probably."

And he stepped forward, out from behind the meager shelter of the broken crest, into full view of the water, the boats, the lenses.

He raised both hands—not surrendering to Hale, but presenting himself, the way you'd present a document. He kept them up.

"Gentlemen," he called toward the press launch, pitching his voice the way he'd once pitched it across canyons to clients who couldn't hear well over wind. "My name is Casper Ravensworth. Five years ago I was blamed for a bridge collapse on the upper Kessel that killed eleven people. I didn't cause it. The survey data that cleared me was buried by the office this man runs. Yesterday I recovered field notes, sealed contamination samples, and the sworn testimony of Engineer Amara Osei documenting illegal dredging operations authorized by Director Hale and his associates—operations responsible for at least four staged accidents, including the deaths of two river workers named Okonkwo."

Beside him, Silas made a sound—small, involuntary, the sound of a name spoken aloud for the first time as something other than a wound.

"The evidence is in this satchel," Casper went on, louder, over the rising roar of the flood. "I am handing it, publicly, to the inspectorate cutter and to the press simultaneously, so that no single person—no officer, no official, no director—can take possession of it alone. Copy everything. Verify everything. Bury it if you can." He looked straight down the river at the cameras. "But you'll have to bury all of us."

* * *

What happened next happened fast and slow at once.

The gunman moved first—of course he did, men with guns always moved first, that was their whole tragedy—and Casper was already turning, already putting himself between the muzzle and Silas, hands still up, body open, offering the largest possible target because that was the entire point. If there was going to be a shot, let it be seen. Let it be witnessed by forty lenses and a matriarch's cold grey eyes. Let the river people watch what the government did to an unarmed man on a weir in broad daylight.

"No," Hale said.

Just that. Quiet. But the gunman froze anyway, because Hale's voice had the particular authority of a man who understood optics even in his ruin.

Casper didn't lower his hands. "Take the satchel," he said to Silas, without turning his head. "Walk it down to the cutter. Don't run. Running looks like hiding."

She hesitated—one heartbeat, her hand finding the back of his jacket, gripping the wet canvas—and then she stepped past him, chin up, and started down the crest toward the water, the satchel held out slightly from her body like something alive. The gunman tracked her with the pistol and Casper tracked the gunman with his whole self, coiled, ready to do the arithmetic of violence if the numbers changed—

They didn't change. Because below, the inspectorate cutter's boarding party was already wading ashore, four officers in orange vests, and the press launch had swung close enough that he could see a reporter's mouth moving, narrating, narrating, and the funeral barge put out its own skiff with Verity in it, upright and terrible, and the mathematics of the morning inverted themselves in front of Hale's eyes.

The Director's men looked at him. Hale looked at the boats. And Casper watched a career end not with a gunshot but with a shrug—the gunman lowering the weapon, stepping back, suddenly very interested in being somewhere else, loyalty being the kind of cargo that jettisons fast when the hull starts taking water.

"Ravensworth." Hale's voice had lost its temperature entirely. Just a man's voice now, thin in the rain. "You had nothing. You were nothing. I gave you five years of—"

"You gave me five years of miles," Casper said. "Turns out that was the gift. I learned every channel in this valley. Met everyone worth knowing." He nodded downstream, at the ragged fleet, at Verity's barge, at the kid wading hip-deep toward the cutter with the truth held high. "You should try it sometime. Being known. It's not as bad as they tell you."

The inspectors took Hale's arms at the top of the weir. He didn't resist, exactly; he simply stopped performing resistance, which for a man like Hale was the same confession as a signature. As they walked him down, he passed close enough that Casper could smell his cologne fighting the rain, and for one moment the Director paused, and something almost human crossed his face—weariness, maybe, or the vertigo of a man seeing, at last, the size of the thing he'd been standing on.

"The bridge," Hale said quietly. Only for Casper. "The Kessel report. You want to know who signed the order to bury your clearance?"

"I know," Casper said. "That's the point. I finally know all of it. And none of it is mine to carry anymore."

Something flickered in Hale's expression—defeat, or perhaps the strange relief of defeat—and then the inspectors walked him onto the cutter, and the rain closed over the moment like water closing over a sunk cache.

* * *

The weir began to fail twenty minutes later.

It went the way Silas had always said it would—not explosively but decisively, a section of the old crest slumping with a groan like a tired animal lying down, and the flood found the gap and poured through, and everyone got off the stone because everyone on that weir knew water. Casper stood on the skiff and watched a hundred years of masonry give itself to the current, and he felt oddly unafraid. Structures that had been holding things back forever, he was learning, didn't shatter when they finally let go. They exhaled.

Silas came alongside in the cutter's tender, soaked to the skin, empty-handed and grinning like the river itself had gotten loose.

"They copied everything twice," she shouted over the roar. "The reporters fought over who got to film the satchel opening. One of them cried. An *adult*, Casper. A grown man with a camera cried."

"River does that to people."

"And Verity says—" she grabbed the gunwale as the two boats knocked together, leaned close, rain streaming off her chin, and her grin softened into something else, something younger and more careful, "—Verity says there's room at the village for a licensed guide. Once you're un-blacklisted. Which the inspectors say won't take long, given that the man who blacklisted you is currently sitting in handcuffs eating a government biscuit."

Casper looked at her—at this orphaned kid who'd taught him more about reading water than twenty years of guiding, who'd carried grief and evidence and a family's name down a flooded valley without dropping any of them—and he found that the thing rising in his chest wasn't the flood at all.

"We'll need a partner-in-navigation," he said. "Somebody who knows the channels adults ignore."

"I know somebody," Silas said.

Downstream, the water was already starting to fall.
