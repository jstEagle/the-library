## 22. Years, Not Miles

Silas had a rule about mornings: never trust a sky until the river told you what it meant. The river never lied. It only spoke in currents, in the smell of mud, in the way the reeds leaned before the wind arrived to push them. On the morning the inspectors came down from the capital to hear the last of the testimony, the river said *clear*, and Silas believed it, and she was right.

She stood on Verity's lowest landing with her feet bare and her toes hooked over the edge of the boards, the way she'd stood since she was small enough that Verity had to loop a rope around her waist. Below her, the water ran brown-green and patient, carrying leaf-shadow and the occasional startled fish. Upstream, past the bend where the drowned church still leaned into the floodplain like an old man dozing in a chair, the tributary ran free again. The dam was gone. Not all of it—the concrete bones of the release gate still jutted from the bank like a whale's rib cage—but the machinery was gone, hauled out on flat barges under the eyes of men with clipboards, and the water that had backed up behind it for six years had drained down into channels that had almost forgotten how to hold it.

Silas had watched the first of that water come through. She'd stood on this same landing and felt the river change its mind, felt the old channels wake up one by one under her feet like a sleeping animal remembering its legs. Verity had put a hand on her shoulder and said nothing, because Verity understood that some things you didn't need words for. The river had been caged, and now it wasn't. That was all. That was everything.

"Silas." Verity's voice came down through the floorboards, through the cooking smoke, through the smell of frying plantain and river fish. "Boat's coming. Your boat."

*Your boat.* Silas smiled at the water. Six months ago the words would have meant nothing. Now they meant a patched hull with a new keel, a two-stroke engine Casper had rebuilt twice, and a name stenciled on the bow in Verity's careful hand: *Wren*. Silas had chosen it. Wrens were small, loud, and impossible to make disappear. She'd told Casper that, dead serious, and he'd laughed for a full minute—the first real laugh she'd ever heard out of him—and then he'd said, "Perfect," and that had been that.

She took the steps two at a time, grabbed a heel of bread from the plate Verity held out without looking, and ran the length of the landing as the *Wren* came around the bend with the current behind it and Casper standing at the stern the way he always did, weight low, one hand on the tiller, reading the water like a man reading a letter from someone he'd missed.

He was different now. Silas had spent months deciding how to say it, and in the end she'd said it to Verity on the porch one night: *He stands like he has a shadow he's allowed to keep.* Before, Casper had moved the way men move when they're trying not to be seen—not crouched, exactly, not hiding, but folded somehow, all his edges turned inward. Now he stood at his full height, gray-eyed and weathered, and when people on the landing called out to him he called back. He had a license now, laminated and official, with his name spelled right: CASPER RAVENSWORTH, LICENSED RIVER GUIDE, KETTLE BEND TO THE CAPITAL. He kept it in a waterproof sleeve in the *Wren*'s lockbox, and Silas had seen him take it out and look at it more than once when he thought nobody was watching. She never said anything. Some things you didn't need words for.

The boat bumped the landing and she caught the rope, snubbed it around the post with two turns the way he'd taught her, and held it while he stepped up onto the boards.

"Passengers?" she asked.

"Two geologists, a very nervous junior inspector, and a cooler of someone's lunch that I have been expressly forbidden to eat." He handed her the mooring line's tail. "They're at the dredge site taking core samples. Amara's with them."

"Amara's *up*?"

"Amara insisted." Something moved through his face when he said it—not a smile exactly, but the place where smiles started in him now. "She said if she had to look at that bend one more time from a courtroom, she was going to lose her mind. She wanted to see it with water in it."

Silas understood that too. They'd all gone, in the weeks after the hearings, one by one, to look at Kettle Bend with water in it. Silas had gone with Verity and stood on the high bank while the river ran through the scar of the work camp, over the places where the barracks had been, where the generator had been, where the men in clean boots had walked. The water didn't cover it. Nothing covered it. But the water ran, and running was its own kind of answer.

"Did the samples come back?" she asked, though she already knew. Everyone in the valley knew. The contamination reports had been read aloud in the provincial capital in a room so quiet you could hear the ceiling fans, and the numbers had been so bad that one of the inspectors had asked for them to be read twice.

"Cadmium, mercury, sediment load through the roof." Casper ticked them off on his fingers, and Silas noticed his hands—steady now, scarred from the flood and the weir, steady the way a riverbed is steady. "The dredging consortium's charter is suspended. Two directors arrested last week. Hale's trial starts after the rains."

"Hale." She said the name flat, the way you'd spit a seed.

"Guilty on everything they've charged him with so far, and they keep finding more." Casper leaned on the landing post beside her and looked downriver, toward the capital, toward the customs house that had been a prison for him and was now just a building with bad paint. "You know what his lawyer tried last month?"

"What?"

"Said the evidence was inadmissible because it had been handled by, quote, 'an unlicensed minor operating an unregistered vessel.'" Casper's mouth twitched. "Verity produced a bill of sale. The *Wren*'s been registered since June. Under your name."

Silas blinked. "My name's on the registration?"

"Whose else's? You found half the channels we used. You ran the coffin barge through two checkpoints when grown men with guns were looking for it. You pulled the cache out of sixty feet of flooded forest by yourself." He shrugged, as if the logic were obvious, and maybe it was. "Verity doesn't put names on paper for nothing. She put yours down because it's yours."

Silas looked at her hands. They were small hands, river hands, nicked and calloused, and for a long time they had been hands that took things—food, scrap, whatever the current offered—because taking was how you stayed alive when nobody was coming to keep you. Now they were hands that held a rope for a licensed guide, that had held a coffin lid shut while a young officer with a flashlight looked at her face and saw grief instead of a girl, that had pulled a drowning man out of black water by his collar and not let go.

She'd pulled Casper out at Night Channels. He never talked about it. He didn't have to. The next morning he'd made her tea the way Verity made it, too sweet, and drunk it with her in silence, and that had been the whole conversation, and it had been enough.

* * *

The hearings had ended in the dry season, and Silas had gone to the capital for the last day of them, sitting in the back row between Verity and a stilt-village fisherman named Odo who'd never worn shoes indoors before and spent the whole day looking at his own feet in astonishment. She had watched Casper testify. He'd stood in the witness box with his hands flat on the rail, and when the lawyer asked him to state his name he'd said it clearly, *Casper Ravensworth*, and Silas had felt the whole room flinch a little, because everyone in that room knew the name from the disaster, from the blacklists, from the two countries that had erased him. He'd said it like a man laying a card down.

They'd asked him about the survey team. They'd asked about the sealed notes, the journal, the tributary that wasn't on any chart. They'd asked about the night at Halfway Lock, and Silas had watched his jaw tighten when they got to the part about the village ally who'd sold their route for his son's medical debt. Casper had told that part carefully, without anger, and then he'd added something Silas hadn't known: he'd paid the debt. Quietly, through Verity's network, out of the reward money the province had eventually—reluctantly, with newspaper photographers present—handed over. "The man made a terrible choice for a good reason," Casper had said. "I've made worse choices for worse reasons. The river doesn't get to decide who deserves a second chance. That's not its job. That's ours."

The room had gone quiet again. Silas had looked at Verity, and Verity had looked at the ceiling, and Silas had known that Verity was crying and would deny it until her dying day.

The charges had come down in a single afternoon: all of them dismissed. Casper Ravensworth, cleared. The word *cleared* had gone down the valley faster than floodwater, and by the time he'd gotten back to the stilt village the whole population was on the landings, and someone had set off firecrackers, and old Odo had shaken his hand with both of his own and said, "We always knew," which was a lie, because half the valley had spent five years believing the official story, but it was the kind of lie communities tell to heal themselves, and Casper had accepted it the way you accept a gift you know is imperfect, which is to say, completely.

That night there had been a fire on the sandbar and fish roasted on sticks and Verity's rice wine going around in a single dented cup, and Silas had sat with her back against a log and watched Casper at the edge of the firelight, and he hadn't been at the edge because he was hiding. He'd been at the edge because he was watching all of them—the way he watched water, the way he watched weather—and when Silas had caught his eye he hadn't looked away, and neither had she, and after a while he'd come and sat down next to her and said, "You know what I figured out?"

"What?"

"I spent five years counting miles. Miles upriver, miles to the border, miles between me and the last place I'd been seen. I thought if I kept moving the miles would turn into something. Safety, maybe. Or just distance." He'd turned the cup in his hands. "They don't. Miles are just miles. It's years that add up to anything. And I wasted five of them."

Silas had considered this with the seriousness she gave all important things. "You didn't waste them," she'd said finally. "You were on the river. If you'd been anywhere else, you wouldn't have been close enough to find the survey camp. You wouldn't have found me."

Casper had looked at her for a long moment. The fire had cracked and spat. "No," he'd said. "I suppose I wouldn't have." And he'd put his hand briefly on top of her head, the way you'd steady a small boat, and she had let him, and that had been the whole conversation, and it had been enough.

* * *

Now, six months later, the guide service had a sign. Verity had painted it herself on a plank of ironwood: RAVENSWORTH & SILAS, RIVER PASSAGE AND SURVEY SUPPORT, KETTLE BEND — ALL CHANNELS. The *ALL CHANNELS* was Silas's addition and she was proud of it. It hung from the landing post, and tourists and inspectors and the occasional lost geologist read it and understood that this was a real business with real credentials, and Silas understood something truer underneath: that it was a flag. It said *we are here*. It said *you can find us*. It said, in the language of two people who had once survived by being unfindable, *we are done hiding*.

The work was good. Casper ran the main channel trips—safe, licensed, boring, as he put it—and Silas ran the channels nobody else could, the flooded-forest shortcuts and the monsoon-season detours that saved clients a full day's travel. They'd taken a contract with the provincial water authority to ferry inspection crews, which meant that every few weeks Silas piloted government officials—actual officials, honest ones, the kind who took notes—down the same water where men in clean boots had once hunted her and Casper like animals. The first time she'd done it, she'd been so tense her shoulders ached for two days. The third time, she'd started telling them about the channels. By the tenth, she was correcting their maps. One of them had actually written her corrections down, and Silas had thought: *there. That's what it's for. That's what knowing things is for, when you finally have somewhere safe to know them in.*

Amara Osei came through every month or two now, still thin, still carrying the tiredness of the work camp in her bones the way a hull carries the memory of a collision, but upright, and testifying, always testifying—depositions, hearings, a civil suit against the consortium that her lawyers said would run for years. She'd stayed a night at Verity's in the last rains, and Silas had found her on the landing at dawn, watching the water.

"I keep waiting to feel safe," Amara had said, not looking at her. "Everyone keeps telling me it's over. It's not over, but it's—different. Do you know what I mean?"

Silas had thought about it honestly, because Amara deserved honesty. "I think safe isn't a feeling," she'd said. "I think it's a place with people in it. You're not safe everywhere. But you're safe here."

Amara had looked at her then, this river kid who said things like that the way other people said *pass the bread*, and she'd laughed, a small cracked laugh, and said, "You're right. God help me, you're right." And she'd eaten breakfast with them at Verity's table like family, because that was what the table was for.

* * *

On the evening of the day the geologists came down from the dredge site, Casper sat on the landing mending a net he had no need of, because mending things was how he thought, and Silas sat beside him with her feet in the water, and the sky over the western hills went the color of the inside of a shell.

"They're going to name the restored channel," Casper said. "The tributary. Put it on the official charts for the first time."

Silas turned her head. "Name it what?"

"They're taking submissions." He was very carefully not looking at her, working his needle through the mesh. "Verity submitted one already. I think you should too."

"What did Verity submit?"

"Osei Water."

Silas sat with that. Amara, who had signed the false reports to keep her family alive and hated herself for it and then, at the end, at cost of her own safety, had put her body between the floodgate and the valley. Osei Water. It was right. It was exactly right, and it would be on the charts forever, and children a hundred years from now would learn it the way Silas had learned every channel, as simply the name of a place where water ran.

"What would you submit?" she asked.

Casper pulled the needle through, considered. "Ravensworth Channel," he said, and Silas's head came around, and then he grinned—the rare one, the real one—and she threw a wet strand of netting at him and he caught it, laughing. "No. I'm done putting my name on things. I had my name taken away and I had it given back and I've learned it's not the important part." He looked out at the river, at the last light lying on it like a hand. "You know what I'd submit?"

"What?"

"*The Wren's Run.* For the channel you found. The one that wasn't on any chart." He said it lightly, but Silas heard what was under it, the way she heard what was under the river: that the hidden channel, the secret water, the passage that saved them all, would carry the name of a small loud bird nobody could make disappear.

She didn't say anything. She put her shoulder against his arm, briefly, the way you'd touch a hull to check it was sound, and it was, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

Downriver, the first lamp came on in Verity's window. Somewhere upstream, in the dark, Osei Water ran free through country that had a name now, on charts that told the truth. The monsoon would come again in a few months—it always did, early or late, honest in its violence—and the *Wren* would be ready, and the channels would be ready, and Silas would read the water the way she always had, except now, when she read it, she was reading it for a home.

"High water season starts in six weeks," Casper said. "We should run the northern channels before it comes, chart the new sandbars."

"Tomorrow," Silas said.

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

And the river went on saying what it had always said—*clear, clear, clear*—and for the first time in either of their lives, there was nowhere either of them needed to be but exactly where they were, and the miles could keep their distance, and the years could come on slow and full, like a river that finally, after everything, was allowed to run.
