## 1. The Thief Who Wasn't

The magistrate of Corren's Hollow had sweaty hands and a liar's posture, which in my experience meant he was about to offer me a job I'd regret.

"You come recommended," he said, not looking at me. He was looking at the window, at the salt flats shimmering white beyond the town's edge like the world had been scoured down to bone.

"By whom?" I asked. "I don't leave recommendations. Recommendations are how people find you."

He laughed, a thin dry sound, and finally turned to face me. "That's exactly why you come recommended, Ranger Halloran. People who don't leave traces are the only ones who can follow them."

I should have walked out then. Twenty years of moving through the desert had taught me to read the exits in every room, and every instinct I had was pointing at the door. But the purse on his desk was heavy — I could tell by the way it sat, the way it didn't slide when he shifted his elbow — and heavy purses buy a lot of distance.

"Tell me what was stolen," I said.

"A map fragment." He said it quickly, as if speed would make it small. "An old one. From my private collection. It's worthless, really. A curiosity."

"Magistrates don't hire desert rangers to recover curiosities."

"This one does." He pushed the purse an inch toward me. "The thief is named Odell Vask. He passed through three days ago. Bought supplies, asked questions about the flats, paid with coin that was too old. Find him. Find what he took. Bring it back to me and no one else, and we're finished with each other."

*No one else.* That phrase again. I've heard it from smugglers, from fugitives, from a bride once who paid me to find her groom and then begged me never to tell her what I found. *No one else* always means *someone else is looking.*

"What did the map show?" I asked.

"Nothing," he said. "Salt. It showed salt."

Nobody steals a map of nothing. But I took the purse, because regret is cheaper when it's paid for in advance, and I told him I'd send word within the month.

He shook my hand with those damp palms and held on a half-second too long, and I remember thinking: *this man is afraid of me finding something more than he's afraid of me failing.*

I was right. I usually am, about other people. It's myself I misread.

* * *

The salt flats in high summer are a lesson in humility. The horizon goes soft and white, the air above the crust shivers like water that isn't there, and everything you thought you knew about distance turns out to be a rumor. I traveled light — water, rope, a week of hardtack, and the rifle I keep oiled but mostly use to look impressive. Most of tracking is looking impressive. The rest is patience.

Vask left a trail a child could have followed, if the child were me. He'd bought a mule in Corren's Hollow that was lame on the near foreleg, and a lame mule writes its name across the salt in a script even the wind can't fully erase. Three days out, I found where he'd camped: a fire ring built wrong, stones stacked for warmth instead of concealment. A man in a hurry, or a man who didn't expect to be followed, or a man who'd never learned that the desert reads your fires like confession.

Four days out, the mule's prints stopped being alone. There were others — riders, two of them, keeping back a half-day behind Vask, patient as debt. They weren't following his trail so much as his *direction*, which told me they knew where he was going before he did. And their horses were shod in a pattern I recognized from nowhere and everywhere: a double-strike on the outer heel, old-fashioned, southern work.

Southern. Toward the coast nobody used anymore.

I marked it and kept moving, and I'll admit here, in the privacy of my own skull, that a feeling had started walking beside me. Not fear. Fear has a shape; you can put your hand on it. This was older and vaguer — the sense of a room you've been in before, a smell you know without knowing. The further south I rode, the heavier it got, like weather building behind mountains.

On the sixth day I found the riverbed.

It hadn't held water in forty years, maybe longer. The banks were cut deep into the salt crust, winding channels of pale clay where the old current had run, and the wind had polished everything smooth except what the wind wanted to keep. I came over the eastern lip just as the sun dropped low enough to turn the whole basin amber, and I saw the mule first — dead, folded neatly against the bank like it had lain down to sleep and been embarrassed about it afterward.

Then I saw Vask.

He was sitting up against the far bank with his legs stretched out in front of him, and for one absurd moment I thought he was resting. Then I saw the dark stain on his shirt, dried to rust, and the way his hands were arranged — deliberately, carefully — around a chest in his lap.

I waited a long time on the ridge. Old habit. You watch a body for movement, and you watch the country around the body for anything that moves better. The riders' tracks led into the basin and out again, no hurry in them now. Whatever they'd wanted, they'd decided Vask couldn't give it to them alive.

When I finally climbed down, the flies told me what the ridge couldn't: he'd been dead two days, maybe three. Shot through the chest, and then — this was the part that made my skin crawl — searched. Thoroughly. His pockets were pulled inside out, his pack gutted, his boots removed and replaced. Someone had gone through him like a clerk through a ledger.

Everything, apparently, except what was in his hands.

The chest was small, iron-banded, the kind of thing you'd trust to a courier rather than a wagon. And stamped into the lid, pressed deep enough to survive the weather, was a seal.

A tower. A wave breaking against it. A star above both.

I knew that seal the way you know your own heartbeat — instantly, totally, and with a sick drop beneath the ribs that arrives before any thought does. Because there are no maps with that crest on them anymore. There are no books, no flags, no gravestones. Forty years ago the nation of Vessia was erased — borders redrawn, records burned, names struck from registers by three neighboring governments who agreed, with the rare efficiency of men dividing a meal, that the place had never existed. Children born after the erasure learn the word only as a warning: *don't ask.*

I am not a child born after the erasure.

I stood in that dead riverbed with the sun going down and my hands shaking, and I understood two things at once, the way you understand lightning and thunder as a single event. First: Odell Vask had died carrying a piece of a country that officially never was. Second: I was the last living person who had ever called that country home — and I had spent twenty years making sure no one, including myself, said so out loud.

The seal looked up at me from the lid like an eye opening.

Here's what I should have done. A professional takes the chest to the magistrate, collects the second half of the purse, and disappears before anyone connects Halloran the ranger to whatever wakes up when that seal sees daylight. A professional burns the ledger inside without reading it, forgets the dead man's face, and files the whole business under *jobs that cost more than they paid.*

But leaving it meant someone else would open it. Those riders with their old-fashioned horseshoes would circle back eventually, or the magistrate's sweaty hands would find another tracker, and whoever opened that chest would be looking for treasure, or leverage, or proof — anything but what it actually was. Some things shouldn't be read by strangers. Some things shouldn't be read by anyone, and yet I already knew I wouldn't burn it unread, because I'm not the person I pretend to be either.

I knelt and worked the chest free of Vask's hands. His fingers had stiffened around the lid like he'd been trying to hold it shut from the inside. I pried them loose gently, one by one, and I said — quietly, stupidly, in a language I hadn't spoken aloud since I was six years old — "Forgive me."

The words came out whole. Unbroken. As if they'd been waiting in my mouth all along for something worth saying.

I wrapped the chest in my bedroll, loaded it on my mule, and gave Vask what burial the salt allows: stones over the shallow spot I dug, facing south, toward water that hasn't run in forty years. Then I turned north, away from the magistrate, away from the coast, toward the high ground where a woman can think.

Behind me the riverbed filled with dusk, and ahead of me the chest rode against my spine like a second heart, beating out a question I wouldn't let myself ask until morning:

What kind of thief steals nothing but a map of a country that doesn't exist — and dies smiling?
