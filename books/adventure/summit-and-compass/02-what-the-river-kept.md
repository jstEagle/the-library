## 2. What the River Kept

I made camp that night in the lee of the dead thief's own fire, because the desert teaches you thrift before it teaches you anything else. His bedroll was still there. His kettle, still full. Whatever had killed him had done it fast and without ceremony, and the sand had already begun its patient work of pretending he'd never existed.

I knew the feeling.

The chest sat between us like a third guest at a table for two. I hadn't opened it. I'd told myself that was caution — you don't crack a seal on a stranger's property in open country with a magistrate's coin in your pocket and a dead man's blood still tacky on the river stones. That was true. It just wasn't the whole truth.

The whole truth was that I was afraid of it.

Forty years I'd been nobody in particular. A ranger who took contracts, crossed borders that weren't borders anymore, and left towns before anyone could ask where I was from. I had a system for questions like that. A shrug for the friendly ones, a lie for the persistent ones, and a departure for the ones who wouldn't stop. It had worked since I was old enough to walk out of a burning country on my mother's hip and never once look back.

Except the seal on that chest wasn't a stranger's crest. It was a double-headed heron standing in water, and I knew it the way you know the smell of a house you can't remember living in. Not learned. Remembered. And that was the problem, because I had spent four decades being certain I remembered nothing at all.

I fed the fire and told myself I'd open it at dawn.

Dawn came, and I opened it at dusk instead, which tells you something about how the day went.

* * *

The lock was Vessian work — a brass mechanism with no keyhole, just a dial of nine glyphs that had probably been gibberish to the thief right up until it killed him. To me it was a children's rhyme. *Heron, river, hearth, harvest, hawk, hollow, home.* Seven of the nine I could name before I'd finished turning them. My hands knew the sequence. My hands had always known it, apparently, and had simply never been asked.

The lid came up on a breath of cold, dry air, like the chest had been holding it for forty years.

Inside: a ledger bound in blue leather gone grey at the edges. A photograph in a paper sleeve. A folded list. And beneath all of it, a false bottom I found because my thumb went looking for it without my permission, which was becoming a theme.

The ledger was an expedition log. I read the first entries by firelight, and I'm going to set some of them down here, because I've carried them long enough that they deserve to be somewhere other than my skull.

* * *

> **Day 3 — Upper Vessia, the Kestrel Route.** Morane insists the glacier route is passable in calving season and only in calving season, which she says like it's a comfort. The ice bridge holds for two weeks a year while the meltwater runs beneath it, and the rest of the year the crevasse field is a mouth with no bottom. We have two weeks. Ilse says we should have left in spring. Ilse says a great many things.
>
> The cargo goes to the cache at the Blue Stair. Not to be opened until Vessia is Vessia again. I have written the opening sequence into the lock and into my daughter, whichever of us survives longer.

* * *

*My daughter.*

I read that line four times. The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the flats, wind moved salt across salt, the sound of the desert erasing itself.

I didn't remember a mother named Morane. I didn't remember the word *Vessia* spoken aloud in a kitchen. But I remembered — and it arrived not as memory but as weather, pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron — I remembered a woman's hands. Chapped, ink-stained, turning a brass dial while a small child watched and was told *you are the second key, little bird, don't ever let them find the first.*

I had been four years old. I had thought it was a game.

> **Day 9.** The maps are being burned tonight, all of them, by order of the Provision. If a country can be unmade by cartography, we intend to be unmade slowly. Ilse has taken the eastern passes with half the cargo. I have the ledger, the list, and Hazel.

* * *

I put the ledger down.

Not gently. I set it down like it might go off, which, in a sense, it had.

*Hazel.* Not my name — I'd made this name myself at fourteen, out of a hedge plant and a sound I liked, a name with no country attached, a name that fit in any mouth. But there it was, in a dead woman's handwriting, forty years old, sitting in a list of names like a stone in a riverbed.

I told myself it was coincidence. I have told myself a great many things over campfires, and I am a professional, and even I didn't believe me.

The list, when I finally unfolded it, was fourteen names in two columns. Some had lines struck through them. Some had small marks beside them I eventually understood were dates of death. Eleven of the fourteen were dead. The thief's name was on the list — struck through, and beside it, in a different hand, a single word: *found.*

Two names remained unstruck. One belonged to a woman in a place called Corren's Ferry, three hundred miles north. The other was mine. The birth one. The one I had not spoken since I was small enough to be carried, the one that had gone into the ground with everything else, and I will not write it here, because writing it felt, that night, like opening a vein over an open flame, and I have some discipline left.

The photograph was the worst of it.

* * *

It was a family of three on the steps of a stone house with blue shutters. A woman with ink on her fingers and a smile that was trying very hard to be brave for the camera. A man with a heron feather in his hatband, laughing at something outside the frame. And a small child in the woman's arms, squinting against the sun, holding — I knew before I turned it over, and the back confirmed it — a carved wooden bird.

I did not remember them. That is the honest truth and I will keep telling it to myself until it takes: I did not remember them.

But my body remembered. My chest ached in the specific place an ache goes when you are four years old and being held by someone whose heartbeat you know. The man's laugh, outside the frame — I could *hear* it, a low rolling sound, and I could not have described my own laugh to you with any confidence. The house with the blue shutters smelled of bread and river water. I have never in my life smelled a photograph before. I don't recommend it.

The thief hadn't been hunting money. The magistrate had hired me to recover a *map fragment*, and the map fragment was a page of this ledger, and the thief had spent — what, years? A lifetime? — hunting a country that every atlas had agreed to forget. He'd found the chest. He'd found the lock. And the lock had found him, because he didn't know the rhyme.

I did.

Which meant whoever killed him — and someone had, because thieves who die in dry riverbeds with full kettles don't die of natural causes — whoever killed him now knew the chest was real, knew it had been opened or moved, and knew that a ranger had been paid to follow his trail. The Vessian descendant societies were a rumor I'd spent forty years not listening to. Collectors, zealots, people who kept the erasure alive by hoarding its scraps. One of them had gotten close enough to kill.

And I was sitting in the open with the whole of it in my lap.

* * *

> **Day 14 — the Blue Stair.** The cache is set. Ilse never arrived; the eastern passes fell a week ago. Morane says grief is a luxury of the settled and we will grieve when the country exists again. I have begun to doubt the country will exist again. I have begun to doubt anything except the child asleep against my back, who is the entire surviving population of a nation, as far as I can prove.
>
> If you are reading this and you are not Hazel: put it down. The lock will not open twice for the same hands.
>
> If you are Hazel: the calving window opens on the first thaw wind. Two weeks. The Blue Stair. You are the key, little bird. Forgive me for making you one.

* * *

I sat with that until the fire burned down to its red arithmetic.

Here is what I knew, laid out as plainly as I know how: there was a glacier three hundred miles north, past Corren's Ferry, past the woman whose name wasn't struck through. There was a cache that opened for two weeks a year, and the first thaw wind was — I did the math on my fingers, the way you do when you don't trust yourself — nineteen days away.

There was a killer somewhere behind me, moving along my trail at the speed of a man who already knows where he's going.

And there was a four-year-old inside me who had been told a rhyme by a woman with ink-stained hands, and who had kept it for forty years without being asked, the way you keep a key in a house you've burned down.

I could burn the ledger. I'd burned things before; it's a skill like any other. I could sell it — the collector who killed the thief would pay a magistrate's ransom and then some, and I'd be gone before the ink on the receipt dried. Both options were sensible. I have survived forty years on sensible.

Instead I sat in the dark and said the birth name out loud, once, very quietly, to no one, just to see if the desert would take it from me the way it takes everything.

It didn't. It just sat there in the cold air, mine, and for the first time in forty years I didn't flinch from it.

Then I packed the chest, kicked out the fire, and turned north — toward Corren's Ferry, toward a woman whose name wasn't struck through, toward a glacier that opened twice a year like a mouth, and toward whoever was following me, because I had decided that if someone wanted the last of Vessia, they were going to have to go through the last living citizen of it first.

I just needed a crew. And I had nineteen days to convince three strangers to walk onto a glacier with a woman who didn't technically exist.
