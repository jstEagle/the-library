## 3. Calving Season

You can't hire people for a lie and expect them to walk on ice with you. Ice finds out what you're hiding. It has a way of asking.

So I lied anyway — just not about the dangerous parts.

The charter I posted in three towns read like every salvage expedition ever scribbled on a tavern board: *Glacier crossing, calving window only, hazard pay triple, discretion required.* What I didn't write was that the route came out of a dead man's chest, or that the destination had been erased from every map in the civilized world, or that I was leading six strangers toward the last unburned piece of a country that no longer existed.

I told them the dangerous parts. The ice, the window, the odds. Those were true, and true things hold a rope together better than comfortable ones.

* * *

**Sefa**

The ranger who hired me looked at my hands before my face. That's how you know someone's serious — they check whether you've actually worked or just dressed like it.

"Two weeks' pay up front," she said. "And one rule. On the ice, nobody asks anybody where they're from."

"I'm from Marrow's Ferry," I said.

"Nobody asks," she repeated, and there was something under it, some weight she set down carefully between us, and I decided I'd carry my half of the silence if she carried hers. A person who pays triple to not be asked questions is a person whose questions would cost more than triple to answer.

I signed. My mother said never sign anything a stranger writes by lamplight. My mother also died of a fever she caught waiting for a better offer, so.

* * *

**Hazel**

Sefa I trusted immediately, which frightened me more than the glacier did. Trust is a crack in the hull. The other two took longer.

Dov Ashkar came recommended by a magistrate who owed me and hated me in equal measure — a route-finder who'd crossed the Verhault ice twice and come back both times with fewer companions than he'd left with, through no fault anyone could prove. He read my charter twice, looked at me once, and said, "You've done this before."

"No."

"Liar," he said pleasantly, and signed.

The third was the problem. Odile Vane found me, which should have been my first warning. She arrived at my camp on the flats' edge with her own gear already packed, quoting my charter back at me word for word, including the line I'd scratched out and rewritten. Nobody quotes your revisions back at you unless they've been reading over your shoulder.

"Discretion required," she said, smiling. "My favorite kind of work."

"Where did you see this?"

"A wall. In Corren." She said it too smoothly, the way people say rehearsed places. I hired her because turning her away meant letting her follow me ungoverned, and because — I'll admit it now, since the ice made liars of all of us — she had the look of someone who knew what a Vessian seal looked like and hadn't flinched.

That either made her the safest person on the expedition or the most dangerous one. Calving season doesn't wait for you to decide which.

* * *

**Odile**

They think I don't notice things. Rangers are like that — competent, watchful, and somehow convinced the world arranges itself around their blind spots.

I noticed the chest before she ever posted a charter. I'd been following it since Corren, since the Society's letter reached me: *A seal has surfaced. Find it. Find who carries it.* Forty years the Vessian Descendant Society has been picking through the wreckage of a country that exists now only in attics and accents, and forty years we've agreed on one thing — whatever the old expeditions cached in the north stays buried until we decide, together, what Vessia gets to be next. A chest surfacing in the hands of a stranger threatens that. A stranger who moves like someone running from her own shadow threatens it more.

I watched her recruit. I watched her lie beautifully — the charter was a masterpiece, every word load-bearing. And when she asked where I'd seen it, I gave her Corren, which is true in the way that a map is true: accurate, and missing everything that matters.

She knows I'm not what I claim. I'm certain of it. We are two women crossing a glacier, each pretending the other's silence is ignorance, and the ice beneath us is more honest than either of us.

Good. Honesty can wait until we're somewhere it won't kill anyone.

Probably.

* * *

**Hazel**

The pass closed behind us on the third day, exactly as the ledger promised — rockfall, deliberate and ancient, sealing the route like a door drawn shut. Dov studied it without comment. Sefa checked our rope twice. Odile smiled at the mountain the way you smile at an old acquaintance.

From there we walked on the river itself, the frozen artery that fed the calving face, and the glacier spoke to us constantly — groans, cracks, the long wet sigh of a thing shifting its weight. The ledger called these sounds *the counting*. The Vessian expedition had crossed during a calving season too, sixty years ago, eleven strong. Their entries got shorter as they went. By the final page it was just distances and temperatures, as though the writer had stopped believing words would survive the ice and started trusting numbers instead.

We found the cache on the sixth day, exactly where the numbers said: a cairn against the moraine, and beneath it, a door of stacked stone packed with sawdust and pitch, sealed with the care of people who expected to come back.

Inside the ice cave behind it, the cache sat whole. Crates stenciled with the crest. A strongbox. Documents wrapped in oilcloth, forty years dry.

And nailed to a beam above them, a second note, older than the ledger's last entry, written in a hand I almost recognized:

*We came back. We looked. We left it. Some countries are better carried than opened. — M.*

M. My chest went tight around a name I hadn't spoken in four decades. Merrin Blackwood. My mother.

She'd stood where I was standing. She'd read whatever I was about to read. And she'd nailed the door shut and walked away and never told me, and raised me to run from a country she had chosen, twice, to leave buried.

"Hazel," Sefa said quietly. She'd taken to using the charter name without being told. "The ice is talking different."

It was. The counting had changed key.

* * *

**Dov**

Here is what thirty years of ice teaches you: the mountain doesn't care about your discoveries. The moment those crates came into lantern light, the glacier began negotiating. A deep report from the calving face, then another, closer. The kind of sequence that ends with a serac letting go and everything below it learning to swim.

"We take the strongbox and the documents," the ranger said. "Leave the crates."

"The Society will want—" Odile started, and stopped herself one word too late.

Everyone heard it. Everyone looked at her.

"Society," Sefa repeated.

Odile didn't even bother looking guilty. "Vessian Descendant Society. Yes. I was sent to find the chest. I'm telling you now because the ice is going to kill us all in the next hour and I refuse to die mid-lie."

"Charming priorities," I said.

"There's a party of them at the pass mouth," she went on, fast. "Six, maybe seven. They followed the same trail I did. If we leave the cache intact, they'll seal it properly and no one else dies over it. If we strip it—"

The glacier answered her. Not a groan this time — a crack that ran under our feet like a struck bell, long and rising, the sound the ice makes just before it makes its decision.

The ranger stood very still over the open strongbox. I watched her face do something complicated — grief and relief fighting over the same ground. Whatever was in those documents, she recognized it. And whatever her mother wrote on that beam, part of her had been hoping to find permission in it.

"Take the ledger pages," she said finally. "The route log. Nothing else. We leave the cache sealed, we add our own nail to the beam, and we tell whoever comes next that some countries are better carried than opened."

"And the Society waiting at the pass?" Sefa asked.

The ranger looked at the closing light, then at the four of us, and I saw her make the calculation that separates leaders from corpses: the truth, the treasure, or the exit. You can carry two across a calving glacier. Never three.

"We go out fast," she said, "and we go out honest. All of it. Starting with why they'll want to talk to me before anyone else."

Above us, the serac began, very slowly, to lean.

* * *

**Hazel**

The ice route takes two hours to cross in good conditions. We had nine minutes and bad ones.

Behind us the cache settled back into the dark, my mother's nail holding her sentence shut for another forty years, maybe forever. Ahead, at the pass mouth, lanterns moved — the Society, patient as heirs. Between us, a mile of glacier deciding whether today was the day.

Odile ran beside me, breathing hard, and shouted over the cracking: "Whatever name you give them — make it the real one. They've waited forty years for someone to say it out loud."

The serac fell. The world turned white and loud, and I ran toward the lanterns carrying nothing but a ledger, a photograph, and — for the first time since I was six years old — my own name, held out in front of me like a lamp.
