## 4. A Name Returned

The pass started closing behind us the way a door closes when someone in the house has decided you've stayed long enough.

We came down through it single file, the glacier groaning its goodbyes above us — that deep, tectonic complaint that sounds like furniture being moved in a house the size of a god. Sera led, because Sera always led and had stopped pretending otherwise around day six. Behind her, Doss carried nothing but his own considerable weight and an opinion about every step of it. Wick brought up the rear with the rope coil, humming something tuneless that I'd learned was either a prayer or a habit of a man who couldn't stand silence, and I walked in the middle where I'd learned to walk forty years ago: close enough to be protected, far enough to run.

We were carrying one item out of everything the mountain had given us. The ledger. Everything else had gone into the fire we'd built on the ice — the route logs, the supply manifests, the letters in that careful Vessian hand, the cache itself, which had turned out to hold less treasure than testimony. Paper burns poorly at altitude. We fed it page by page and watched forty years of somebody's hope go up in smoke that smelled like ink and cold.

I told myself I felt nothing. I am very good at telling myself things. It's practically a profession.

But my hand had been in my coat pocket since the fire, closed around the photograph, and by the time we reached the tree line my fingers had gone stiff around it like the paper was trying to become part of me. Maybe it was.

* * *

The magistrate met us at the assay office in Corren's Hollow with the particular relief of a man who has paid for a problem to disappear and is checking whether it actually did.

"Ranger Halloran." He said it the way he'd said it the first time, testing the name against some memory he didn't know he had. "The thief?"

"Dead before I found him," I said. "Riverbed took him. Flash flood, most likely, weeks back."

"And the map fragment he stole?"

"Burned with him." I let a beat pass. "Nothing on it worth the ink. Old survey work. Wrong mountains entirely."

He wanted to believe me. That's the thing about hiring someone to find your secrets — you're praying they don't. He counted out the pay in heavy coin, heavier than the job deserved, and I watched his shoulders come down as I signed the receipt. *H. Halloran.* The pen moved without consulting me. Funny how a lie gets smooth with use, like a stone in a river. Funny how it still fits the hand.

"The chest," he said, as we were leaving. "The thief was carrying something. A witness saw—"

"A bedroll and a bottle," I said. "Whatever the witness saw, they saw it from far away and wanted to be part of the story."

He nodded slowly, and I understood that we had made a trade, he and I. His ignorance for my silence. Both of us walking out richer than we'd walked in, and neither of us saying so.

Outside, the crew was waiting in the kind of silence that follows a job nobody wants to talk about yet. Doss spat. Sera looked at me with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"You burned all of it," she said. Not a question.

"All but one thing."

She waited. She was good at waiting; it was half of why she was alive.

"A photograph," I said. "Of people I don't remember."

"And you kept it why?"

I looked east, toward the flats, toward the dry riverbed where a stranger had died holding my history like it was his own. "Because burning it felt like letting someone else decide who I was. And I'm done with that."

Sera considered this for exactly as long as it deserved, which was not long. "There's a tavern down the street," she said. "Doss owes me a drink and you owe us the truth eventually. Not tonight. But eventually."

"That's more patience than I've ever been offered."

"Don't get used to it." But she almost smiled, which from Sera was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

* * *

Here is what I have always known about staying: towns are patient traps. They let you sleep three nights, then they learn your face, then they learn your habits, then someone asks where you're from and the whole architecture starts to creak. Forty years I'd kept ahead of it. Forty years of leaving on the fourth morning, always the fourth, before the questions could put down roots.

On the fourth morning in Corren's Hollow, I woke at dawn out of habit, packed out of habit, and stood at the window of my rented room watching the light come up over the assay office roof, and did not leave.

It felt like standing on cracking ice. Which, granted, I'd done recently, so I knew the sensation well. The trick, I'd learned up on the glacier, is not to freeze. The trick is to keep moving, carefully, and trust that the ice holds more often than it doesn't.

Downstairs, Wick was already at the tavern table, eating eggs like eggs had personally wronged him. Doss was asleep sitting up. Sera was reading the ledger — our one salvaged thing — with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"There's a descendant society," she said without looking up. "Vessia's children. They've been asking after a stolen chest in every town this side of the flats. Paid informants. The works."

"I know. I met their advance man on the riverbed. He was the thief."

That got her eyes up. "You knew and you didn't say?"

"He was hunting my past, Sera. I figured the least I could do was let it stay buried with him."

"And now?"

"Now they'll keep looking. The chest is ash, but the society isn't going to accept ash for an answer." I sat down across from her. "They'll come here eventually. Asking about a ranger named Halloran."

"So we leave." Doss, awake now, or enough.

"No," I said, and heard how strange the word sounded in my mouth, like a coin from a country that no longer existed. "No. I'm done running from people who want to tell me what I am."

Wick stopped chewing. Somewhere outside, the town was waking up — cart wheels, a dog with opinions, the assay office shutters banging open. Ordinary sounds. The sound of a place deciding whether to let you belong to it.

"They erased Vessia from every map," I said. "Every register, every record. Forty years of pretending a nation never happened. And it worked, mostly, except for a chest in a riverbed and a photograph in my pocket and whatever's left of me." I took the photograph out and laid it on the table between the plates and the ledger. Four faces. Two of them mine, or had been, once, under other names. "I can't get them back. I can't even remember them honestly. But I can stop agreeing with the erasure."

Sera studied the faces a long moment. "What does that look like? Practically."

"Honestly? I don't know yet. I think it looks like staying past the point where staying gets dangerous. I think it looks like answering questions instead of outrunning them." I turned the photograph over. On the back, in faded pencil, in a child's uncertain hand — my hand, once — someone had written a name. Not Halloran. The other one. The one I'd spent four decades treating like a wound instead of a word.

* * *

The town register hung by the tavern door, as registers do — a ledger of transients, of names passing through, of people the town would forget by season's end. I'd signed it four mornings ago as H. Halloran, ranger, just passing through. The ink had dried into the paper like it belonged there, the way lies do.

I picked up the pen.

Below my old signature, I wrote the new one. The old one. Both true, finally, at the same time: *Hazel*, and then the family name, the Vessian one, the one that had been waiting in a dead thief's fist and a glacier's cold keeping and a photograph's pencil ghost for forty years to be written down somewhere permanent.

My hand shook. I let it. You can't choose a name with steady hands any more than you can cross a glacier without hearing the ice complain.

"There," Wick said softly, when I set the pen down. "Now you exist."

"I always existed," I said. "I just kept filing the paperwork in the wrong country."

Doss laughed, a big ruinous sound. Sera closed the ledger and slid it back into its oilcloth, and I caught her looking at the register, at the name, memorizing it — not to sell, I understood suddenly, but to keep. Some people collect your secrets to spend them. The rare ones collect them to guard.

Outside, morning was coming up gold over the flats, over the riverbed, over the mountains where a nation's last testament was melting back into the ice that had held it. Somewhere east, the descendant society was sharpening its questions. They'd arrive. There would be hard conversations, maybe worse, and I'd have to decide again tomorrow, and the day after, who I was — because that's the secret nobody tells you about identity. It isn't a thing you escape or reclaim. It's a chore. A daily one. Like water, like fire, like staying.

I touched the photograph in my pocket, once, the way you touch a talisman or a scar.

Then I went inside, and ordered breakfast, and stayed.
