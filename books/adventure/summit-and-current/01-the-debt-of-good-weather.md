## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The dry season's last weeks smelled like dust and money, and Ada Montrose had neither.

She sat on the roof of the customs house in Kessel's Landing with her boots hanging over the gutter, watching the sky do nothing. That was the trouble with good weather. It looked honest. Blue from horizon to horizon, not a cloud worth naming, and every farmer, freighter, and ferryman in the valley breathing easy because the river ran low and tame and predictable. Good weather was a debt. Somewhere upstream, all that withheld rain was stacking up like coins in a jar, and when it came down—and it would come down—it would come down on somebody who hadn't saved for it.

Ada had made a living off that arithmetic for eleven years. Storms for hire. You paid her, she told you where the sky would break, and you got your hay in, your barges tied, your tents staked before the first fat drop hit. She'd been good at it. Better than good. The farmers called her the Weather Widow behind her back, on account of the black coat she wore even in August, and they paid her rates without haggling because haggling with a woman who could read a cumulonimbus like a ledger felt like asking for trouble.

Then the spring floods took her gauges, her instruments, and most of her savings, and the winter after that took the rest, and now she sat on a hot tin roof counting the ways a person could disappear from a town of four thousand people.

Three, as it happened. The river, the road, or the paperwork.

"Montrose." A voice from below, flat as a ledger line. "You're up there. I can see your coat."

Verrick. Deputy collector, third-rate, first-rate at finding people who didn't want finding. Ada didn't move. "I'm working."

"You're sitting."

"Sitting is how the work gets done." She tipped her head back. Not a wisp. Not a feather of cloud. "Tell Harl I'll have his money by the first frost."

"He says frost is a story you tell children." Verrick's shadow shifted at the base of the ladder. "He also says he knows about the boat."

Ada's stomach went cold in a way no weather had ever managed. The *Kestrel*—half hull, half home, all she had left of anything—was moored two bends downstream under a tarp, in a slough where nobody fished because the current ate hooks. Nobody knew about that slough. She'd made sure of it the way she made sure of everything: quietly, carefully, invisibly.

"I don't have a boat," she said.

"Right," said Verrick. "Harl says the first frost. He says he's feeling generous this year."

His boots crunched away across the gravel. Ada stayed on the roof until the sun went down and the tin stopped cooking, and then she climbed down and walked to the slough through the dark with her heart going like a hailstone on a tin roof.

The *Kestrel* was still there. Still tarped. Still whole.

She almost laughed at herself. Almost. Then she went aboard, lit the lamp, and let the fear drain out of her shoulders one degree at a time. This was the arrangement she'd made with her life: keep it small, keep it dark, keep it moving. Owe nobody, need nobody, be nobody. Invisibility wasn't cowardice. It was engineering.

She believed that right up until the knock came at her hatch, three nights later, in the hour before dawn.

* * *

Corvin Hale did not look like a man who knocked on boat hatches in the dark. He looked like a man who owned the dock the boat was tied to, and the town behind it, and possibly the weather itself. Tall, silver at the temples, dressed in river-gray wool that probably cost more than the *Kestrel*, and smiling—smiling at four in the morning, which should have been disqualifying and somehow wasn't.

"Miss Montrose." He held up a lantern so she could see his face, which was considerate of him, and a leather folio, which was not. "Forgive the hour. I'm told you're difficult to find during daylight."

"Then you were told correctly." She didn't open the hatch wider than the chain allowed. "Whoever told you can tell you where the door is, too."

"They can. But the door I want isn't one you're standing behind." He set the folio on her deck, squared its corners to the planks with a precision that made her teeth itch. "Corvin Hale. Hale Survey and Mineral, out of the capital. Three weeks ago I lost an expedition in the high country past the Kessel headwaters. Eleven people. Instruments, pack train, radio—the works. They went silent on the ninth and haven't answered since."

"Then they're dead," Ada said. "Or deserting. High country eats both kinds this time of year. Hire a rescue outfit."

"I hired one. They came back." For the first time, the smile flickered—not gone, just banked, like a fire stepped away from. "They came back without going up. Something spooked them, and my men don't spook cheaply. So now I'm hiring someone who doesn't take a mountain's opinion into account." He glanced up at her, and the lamplight caught something in his face that wasn't charm. Hunger, maybe. Or grief wearing charm's coat. "I'm hiring you, Miss Montrose, because everyone between here and the pass says you're the only person alive who reads weather like scripture, and because I'm told you read people nearly as well."

"Everyone's generous with my reputation and stingy with their coin."

"I'm neither." He slid the folio through the gap in the hatch. It was heavy. Paper-heavy and coin-heavy both. "A thousand down. Two thousand more on delivery of my survey team, alive, to Kessel's Landing before the snows close the pass. Whatever else you find up there is yours to burn or bring back—I only want my people."

Ada looked at the folio and didn't touch it. "Find yourself a guide."

"I found one. Bram Ostergaard. You may have heard what he lost his license over."

"I've heard everything about Bram Ostergaard. That's rather the point of disgrace—it travels well."

"And a radio operator. Sefa Ilori, out of the telegraph office. Young. Good hands." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on hers, weighing. "And one more name on the manifest. Yours to refuse, of course. Theo Montrose. Surveyor, second chair. Your brother."

The river made its small noises against the hull. Somewhere downstream a night bird complained once and quit.

"No," Ada said.

Hale nodded, unsurprised, as if *no* were simply the first figure in a negotiation. "No," he agreed. "I expected that. Take the folio anyway. Consider it payment for the conversation." He straightened his cuffs. "The expedition carried survey data worth more than every boat in this town. Worth more than the town. There are men who'd cross that range barefoot for a look at it, and some of them know I know where it is. Which means, Miss Montrose, that knowing things has become a dangerous occupation in Kessel's Landing—for you especially, since the man who wrote those surveys shares your surname."

"That's not a threat. That's weather talk."

"It's both," Hale said pleasantly. "I find the best threats are. Get your hay in before the storm, Weather Widow."

He left the lantern burning on her deck, which she decided was either courtesy or a message, and his footsteps went away along the dock light as smoke.

Ada sat with the folio in her lap for a long time. She didn't open it. Opening it would be the first yes, and she had gotten very good at first-no. She put it under her bunk unopened, next to the emergency water, and told herself it was storage, not surrender.

* * *

Two nights later, the *Kestrel* burned.

She woke to orange light on the slough water and got as far as the deck before the heat drove her back. There was no saving it. Whoever had done it had done it clean—no alarm raised, no neighbors stumbling out, just the tarp gone first and then the pitch in the seams catching with a sound like applause. She stood on the cold mud of the bank in her black coat and watched eleven years of careful smallness go up in a column of sparks that the whole valley could probably see.

By morning there was nothing but a blackened hull sitting in the shallows like a burnt match, and Verrick standing on the bank with his hands in his pockets, looking professionally sympathetic.

"Terrible thing," he said. "Dry season. Sparks get everywhere."

"Sparks," Ada said.

"Harl wanted me to mention—he's flexible on the frost date now. Given your circumstances." Verrick nudged a charred plank with his boot. "Funny thing about fires. They make a person visible. Whole town will be talking about you by noon. Hard to hide after a thing like this."

Ada looked at the wreck for a long moment. And there it was, arriving late but unmistakable, the thought she should have had on her roof a week ago: invisibility had never been her engineering. It had been permission—granted by other people, revocable by other people, and revoked now, thoroughly, by whoever had struck the match. Harl, maybe. Hale, maybe. Some third party she hadn't met yet. It didn't matter. The small dark life was over; someone else had ended it for her, and the only question left was whether she spent what remained of it running from creditors or walking toward the mountains.

She turned to Verrick. "Tell Harl he'll be paid by the first snow. Tell him exactly that wording."

"The first snow's a long way off."

"No," Ada said, watching the smoke still rising off the slough, thin and white and climbing toward a sky that was finally, finally clouding over in the west. "It isn't."

That afternoon she opened Hale's folio, counted the thousand, and went to find Sefa Ilori.
