## 2. Upriver Silence

Ada found Sefa in the back room of the telegraph office, soldering a radio that had no business working. She was small, quick-handed, and had been fired from the river company for refusing to falsify a maintenance log—which, in Ada's experience, was the best kind of reference.

"I need someone who can keep a set alive in wet cold," Ada said.

Sefa didn't look up from the board. "I need someone who pays."

"Hale's money. Half up front."

That got her eyes. "The optimist with the missing survey." She set down the iron. "You know what he wants, right? Not people. Paper."

"I know what he wants. I want my brother."

Sefa studied her a long moment, then wiped her hands on her trousers and stood. "Then I'm in. But I'm coming back with all my fingers."

Bram was harder. She found him where she expected—drinking slowly at the Ferryman, the guide who'd lost two clients on the White Stair rapids three years ago and never guided again. He was broad as a door and moved like he was sorry about it.

"No," he said, before she finished her first sentence.

"The expedition went up the Kessel headwaters. Your water. Nobody else knows those camps."

"Nobody should be going to those camps. That's the point of them being gone."

"They're not all gone. Hale has a letter—last transmission said survivors, high camp, below the pass."

Bram turned his glass in a slow circle. "I buried two men because I read the river wrong. You want me to read it again?"

"I want you to read it right this time."

He laughed, ugly and short. Then he looked at her, and something in her face must have answered something in his, because he drained the glass and stood. "Boat leaves at dawn. If you're late, I leave."

She wasn't late.

* * *

The first abandoned camp was four days up, on a gravel shelf above the water. It told its story neatly: tent platforms struck and lashed, fire rings doused and scattered, garbage packed out or burned. A supply cache under oilskin, inventoried in pencil on a slate leaning against a stone. *Departed 14th, all well, making good time.*

"All well," Sefa read aloud, and spat over the edge of the bank. "Somebody wrote that the day before they stopped existing."

"Or somebody wanted us to think so," Bram said.

The second camp was stranger. Same orderliness—but the cache was untouched, and beside the slate someone had written *gone ahead, light load* and beneath it, smaller, almost scratched: *not everyone.* No name. No date.

Ada crouched over that second line for a long time. The handwriting was Theo's. She'd have known it anywhere; he'd written her letters like this for twenty years, the last words always smaller, always the true ones.

They made the third camp on the sixth day, and it was the one that ended the neatness.

The tents were still standing. Two of them, torn open along their seams—not cut from outside, but ripped, the way fabric goes when someone is dragged through a flap. There was blood on the gravel, old and rain-washed, enough of it. A cook pot lay in the shallows. And driven into a post at the camp's center, a survey stake with paper wrapped around it, weighted by a stone.

Sefa pulled the paper free. Coordinates, a bearing, and one line: *Do not follow past this point. We are handling it.*

"Handling what?" Sefa said.

Ada took the note and folded it into her coat, next to the map. "Whatever they found."

Bram stood at the treeline, looking upriver toward the country where the valley narrowed and the mountains began to mean it. "There's a storm building," he said. "Two days, maybe less. We shelter or we die stupid."

* * *

The river family lived in a bend where the current slowed and the willows grew thick—Olek and Marta Voss, three children, a smokehouse full of whitefish, and a dog the size of a yearling bear. They took the strangers in without questions, which itself was an answer: people who lived this far up already knew about the expedition, and had decided what they'd say about it to nobody.

Marta fed them fish and barley bread and watched Ada eat with the particular attention of a woman counting how much a person could carry.

"You're going to the high camp," Olek said. Not a question either.

"My brother's there."

Olek nodded slowly. He was a man built like the river—wide, patient, moving everything along. "Survey crew came through in spring. Bought eggs, paid fair. The leader talked too much. The quiet one—" he glanced at Ada, "—the quiet one asked us about the old mine roads. Asked if anyone still went up past the tree line after first snow."

"What did you tell him?"

"That nobody does. That's why we live here and not there."

The storm arrived that night like something owed. Rain first, hard enough to flatten the river to white noise, then wind that walked the willows flat, then—the first gift of the season—snow, wet and wrong, months early. It fell all night against the shutters while the youngest Voss child slept against Sefa's side without ever having been introduced to her, and Sefa sat very still so as not to wake her, and Ada understood she was watching something begin.

In the morning the world was white to the knees. Olek stood at the door with his arms crossed against the cold.

"Turn back," he said. "I'll say you tried. I'll even believe it."

"We can't," Ada said.

"I know." He didn't sound angry. He sounded tired, the way men get tired of watching other people learn things. "But hear me. The crew that came through in spring—there were eleven. The crew that came back down in summer, before the passes closed—there were four. They wouldn't look at us. One of them had rope burns on his wrists. Rope burns, going *down*river."

Sefa went pale. Bram said nothing, but his jaw worked once, twice.

"And the leader," Olek went on, "the talkative one—he wasn't among the four."

Marta pressed a sack of dried fish and a brick of tea into Ada's hands at the door, and held her wrist a moment longer than the handing required. "Come back down," she said. "All of you. My table seats more than three."

It was such a small sentence. Ada carried it upriver like a coal.

* * *

They climbed for two days out of the river country, into country that didn't care about rivers. The snow deepened. The radio, strapped to Sefa's pack, caught fragments between bursts of static—a voice, thin and repeating, half-swallowed by altitude.

Sefa froze on the switchback, hand raised. "Listen."

Through the hiss, threaded with interference: *"—any station—high camp—repeat, high camp—we are not alone up here—"*

Then static. Then, fainter, a second voice underneath the first, farther from the transmitter, saying something urgent that the mountain would not give up.

Bram looked at Ada. Sefa looked at Ada.

"That's a distress call," Sefa said. "And that's somebody trying to stop it."

Ada tightened the straps on her pack, felt the folded note and the map against her ribs, and started climbing.
