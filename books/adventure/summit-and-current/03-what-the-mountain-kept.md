## 3. What the Mountain Kept

Bram had guided this range for eleven years and had never once seen the high camp where it was supposed to be.

They came up out of the treeline on the fourth morning, Ada in front because she refused to be anywhere else, Sefa dragging the radio sled and complaining about it in three languages, Bram reading the mountain the way he'd learned to read rivers before either of them could walk. The wind had teeth now. Two days of clear sky left, maybe three, before the snows closed the pass like a book.

The camp sat in a granite bowl below the eastern shoulder — tents sagged under fresh snow, a cook fire burning low and disciplined, and figures moving among the wreckage of the survey expedition like survivors of a shipwreck who'd decided to keep calling the wreck home.

"Eleven people," Ada said. Her voice did something strange on the number. "There were nineteen."

"Counting's not done," Bram said, because he could see the cairn at the bowl's edge, stones stacked over ground too frozen to dig. He didn't say how many mounds. She saw anyway.

A man detached from the cook fire and came toward them with a rifle slung wrong — carried for show, or carried by someone who hadn't slept. He stopped ten feet out and looked at Ada for a long moment.

"You're Montrose," he said. "He said you wouldn't come."

"Theo talks about me?"

"He talks *around* you. There's a difference." The man almost smiled. It didn't survive the trip to his eyes. "I'm Okafor. I was the surveyor. Before."

"Before what?"

Okafor turned and looked up-slope, at a seam of exposed rock where the snow had slumped away from something that glittered even in flat grey light. Even from two hundred yards, even knowing nothing about ore, Bram felt his stomach drop. The rock face looked like someone had spilled money down the mountainside.

"That," Okafor said, "is before."

* * *

They told it in pieces around the stove that evening, the way people tell things they've told themselves too many times already, each retelling wearing the story smoother and less true.

Hale had wanted the strike kept quiet until his company controlled the claims. Their leader — Voss, a decent man gone rigid with purpose — had agreed, then wavered when two porters died in a crevasse field and the crew started asking why they were sitting on a fortune instead of going home. Hale's answer had come by radio: hold the site, say nothing, the second team arrives before the snows. Voss put it to a vote. The vote went against him. And then, Okafor said, looking into the stove, "it stopped being a vote."

"Mutiny," Bram said.

"Worse. A mutiny has a side you can join." Okafor's hands were steady; his voice wasn't. "We just stopped obeying him. He took four loyalists and tried to cross the pass early to reach Hale himself. We found two of them." He nodded toward the cairn. "The mountain found the other two first."

Ada hadn't spoken since the tents. Now she stood, and Bram watched her cross the camp to the far tent where Theo Montrose had been sitting apart from everyone since they arrived, visible through the canvas gap, a man performing solitude so hard it looked rehearsed.

Bram followed at a distance that would let him hear and pretend he hadn't.

"You knew I'd come," Ada said.

Theo laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old rope. "I said you wouldn't."

"You told them I wouldn't come. That's different. That's you being certain of me for once in your life." She stepped into the tent. Bram saw Theo's face then — the handsome ruin of it, frost-nipped, sleepless. "You sent word through Hale's office. You knew exactly which string to pull. So say it, Theo. Say why I'm standing on a mountain instead of hiding in Kessel's Landing like a sensible debtor."

Theo looked at his hands. When he finally spoke, it came out level, which was worse than weeping.

"Hale offered me passage out. First team down, ahead of the snows, debts cleared, name clean. All I had to do was confirm the strike and keep the crew calm until his people arrived." He raised his head. "I told him they'd hold. I told him they were mine."

"And they weren't."

"They were never mine. I just needed them to be, for one more week." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "Voss is dead because I lied to a man who pays liars. The porters are dead because I needed their silence more than their lives. You're here because—" He stopped.

"Because you sold me too," Ada said. "Just last."

Nobody moved. Outside, the wind changed pitch, and Bram, who had spent thirty years listening to mountains lie about weather, felt the hair rise on his arms.

That night the sky came down.

* * *

It wasn't snow at first. It was pressure — the whole bowl pressing its ears, the fire flattening, the horses screaming once and going silent against the picket line. Then the front hit like a door slammed by a giant, and the world went white sideways.

Bram had ridden out whiteouts before. This wasn't that. This was the mountain exhaling everything it had been holding all autumn, a blizzard with intent, and every hour of it stole a day off their margin before the pass sealed for winter.

"We go tomorrow," Ada shouted across the cook tent, morning of the second day, though morning was a rumor. "All of us. Eleven survivors, our three, one pass. We move as one group or we die as two."

"With the samples?" Okafor asked. The core boxes sat under oilcloth by the supply tent — forty pounds of glittering proof, the whole reason anyone was on this mountain, the thing Hale's second team was climbing toward right now.

Ada looked at the boxes a long time. Bram watched her do the arithmetic everyone else was pretending not to do: weight versus speed, proof versus people.

"They come," Okafor said. "Hale doesn't pay us otherwise. Doesn't *find* us otherwise—"

"Hale," Ada said, "is not the weather."

And that was when the second team walked into camp.

Six men, roped, armed, faces goggled and anonymous, materializing out of the white like the storm had produced them. Their leader pulled back his goggles and Bram recognized the type instantly — not a thug, worse, a clerk with a rifle, a man who processed consequences rather than felt them.

"Corvin Hale sends his regards," the clerk said. "We're here for the survey and the personnel. In that order, if everyone's reasonable."

The camp fractured exactly the way rotten things fracture — quietly, all at once. Two of Okafor's survivors drifted toward the armed men without being asked. Okafor put himself between the clerk and the core boxes. Theo stood in the middle of it doing nothing, which was the most honest thing he'd done all season.

Then Sefa, who had spent two days nursing the radio inside the supply tent, came out backward with her hands up, and behind her the clerk's own radio operator was holding a pistol on her, and the sled with the transmitter was tipping into the trampled snow between them.

"The frequency logs," the clerk said pleasantly. "Your technician has been very busy broadcasting. Mr. Hale would like those."

Sefa looked at Bram. Twenty years of disgrace sat on Bram's shoulders like wet rope, and in that moment he understood he was going to spend them all in the next sixty seconds.

He moved. Not toward the gun — toward the sled. He got a hand on the transmitter's case as the shot went wide in the wind, and then the world was fists and white and Sefa screaming *the antenna, burn the antenna*, and somewhere in it the radio operator went down hard and stayed down, and Bram came up bleeding from the eyebrow with the transmitter's guts in his hands and Sefa striking flint against the fuel tin—

The radio burned. The frequency logs burned with it. Every record of who had called whom, every confession Sefa had copied off the airwaves, every thread connecting Hale to this mountain — orange and gone in ninety seconds of storm-whipped flame.

Sefa wasn't standing next to him anymore.

"Sefa!" Ada's voice, torn flat by wind.

They found her tracks leading away from the fire, toward the supply tent, and then no tracks at all — just the collapsed wall of snow where the tent had been, and beneath it, faintly, the shape of the backup battery pack she'd gone back for, because Sefa did not leave equipment, Sefa had never in her life left equipment—

They dug for an hour. The storm filled the hole faster than their hands could empty it.

On the third hour, Ada stood, snow-crusted, hands shredded, and looked at the core boxes, and at the pass, and at the eleven survivors watching her with the particular hunger of people deciding whether hope was safe.

"Leave the ore," she said. "Everyone carries someone. We move in twenty minutes."

Bram shouldered the last coil of rope and thought: *she'll make it, but the mountain will take its toll.* Then he looked north, where the storm had briefly thinned, and saw, impossibly, lights — lantern-light, moving, descending toward the pass from the far side.

Someone was coming over the top. In this. Toward them.

And the only person Bram knew reckless enough to cross a closing pass in a killer storm was a charismatic optimist who'd just lost his radio link to the world.
