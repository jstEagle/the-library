## 4. Current

The river ran high and fast with the first meltwater of the season's last storms, and we rode it like thieves.

Bram had the tiller, Sefa had the radio, and I had the crate between my knees—waterproofed, strapped, and full of nothing anyone could spend. No ore. No maps. No coordinates. Just paper: forty pages of sworn accounts, dictated in a freezing tent over three nights while the survivors' voices shook and steadied and shook again. Theo had written the first page. He'd written it in pencil, then gone over every word in ink, like a man signing a confession he intended to survive.

"Company skiff behind us," Sefa said, not looking up from the set. "Half a mile. Maybe less."

"Let them look," Bram said. "They'll see a burned boat with a new hull and three fools running the wrong way with winter on their heels."

"They'll see the crate," I said.

Bram glanced back at me. In the gray light his face looked carved from something patient. "Then they'll chase us."

"Good," I said. "Let them chase us all the way to Kessel's Landing. Let the whole town watch Hale's men chase a debt down Main Street. Nothing ruins a man faster than being seen collecting."

Sefa laughed—short, surprised, the laugh of someone who'd spent three weeks learning to be afraid and had just remembered she could also be this. The skiff fell behind by the second bend. By the fourth it was gone, and the current took us the rest of the way like it had been paid to.

* * *

Kessel's Landing looked smaller from the water than it ever had from the shore. That was the thing about running: you left a place the size of your fear and came back to find it was only a town—four streets, a mill, a courthouse with a leaky roof, and a saloon where everybody knew your name and your balance.

I tied off at the old landing, the one with the burned pilings, and I did not flinch at them. That felt like progress.

The town square was already full. Word travels upriver faster than boats; someone had seen us coming and done the arithmetic. Hale's factor stood on the courthouse steps with a writ in one hand and a smile that wasn't, and behind him a dozen of the town's solid men, the kind who lent money and never borrowed it.

"Miss Montrose," the factor called. "We understand you're in possession of company property. The survey. Mr. Hale is prepared to be generous. Your debt—"

I set the crate on the courthouse steps and opened it in front of everyone. Let them see the paper. Let them see it wasn't ore.

"This is the expedition's testimony," I said, loud enough to reach the back. "Sworn accounts from eleven survivors of the Hale survey expedition. It names who ordered the strike kept secret. It names who armed the second team. It names the man who traded his crew's rations for his own passage—" and here I looked at Theo, standing at the edge of the crowd, gray-faced and steady, "—and it names him in his own hand, because he asked for it to be read aloud."

The square went quiet the way a river goes quiet before it drops.

"Eleven survivors," someone said. "Hale reported nine dead."

"Hale reported what was profitable," I said. "Read it yourselves. I'm not selling it. I'm not trading it. It belongs to the people who lived it." I stepped back from the crate. "And the mountain keeps the rest."

The factor reached for the papers and Bram's hand came down on the crate—not threatening, just there, the way a mountain is there. The factor looked at the crowd, at the faces, and did the only arithmetic that had ever mattered in Kessel's Landing: which side of this would the town rather be on.

By nightfall the testimony had been copied four times. By morning it was on a packet boat to the company's rivals in the coast cities, and Hale's factor was on the same boat, going home to explain to a charismatic optimist why optimism had stopped being an asset.

* * *

Hale's credit collapsed the way a snow bridge collapses: silently, then all at once. The banks called his paper. The rivals he'd tried to ruin with silence bought his debts for pennies out of pure spite. And somewhere in the middle of it, my debt—four hundred and sixty dollars, plus interest, the number I'd carried in my chest for two years like a stone—was sold to a man in the coast cities who read the testimony, read the name Montrose attached to the man who'd helped deliver it, and tore the paper up rather than be seen holding it.

Sefa brought me the news at the boatyard. She was wearing Bram's coat because the mornings had turned cold, and she was grinning like the radio had just picked up music.

"It's dead," she said. "Your debt. It's dead."

I kept planing the hull plank I was working. Long, even strokes. "Good," I said.

"That's it? *Good?*"

I looked at the plank. At the new keel beneath it, scarred where the fire had been, strong where we'd made it strong. "I spent two years being nobody so nobody could collect," I said. "Turns out I was never hiding from the creditors. I was hiding from the idea that I was only worth what I owed." I set the plane down. "It's dead. Good. Now help me with this plank."

* * *

Theo left on a Tuesday, on the downriver packet with a duffel bag and a list—eleven names, and beside each one a town, a family, a debt of a different kind. He was going to each of them. Not to be forgiven. He'd said that much at the dock, and he'd said it looking at me the way he used to look at storms when we were kids: like something you don't run from, you read.

"I'm not asking you to come," he said.

"I know."

"I'm not asking you to forgive me."

"I know that too."

He nodded. Then, at the gangplank, he stopped. "The mountain kept the ore," he said. "You kept the truth. I used to think those were the same thing." He didn't wait for an answer. The packet took him downriver, and I stood on the burned pilings and watched until the current bent and he was gone, and I found that what I felt wasn't grief or relief but something quieter—like a river reaching the sea and finally knowing which way it had been going all along.

* * *

We finished the boat in the first week of the dry season.

Bram laid the last plank. Sefa wired the cabin for light and hung her radio in the corner where the stove could keep it warm. I painted the name on the stern myself, in letters big enough to read from the far bank: *Current*. Not the expedition's name. Not Hale's. Ours—the thing that had carried us, the thing we'd chosen to be.

We weren't going anywhere, that was the joke of it. A storm chaser, a disgraced guide, and a radio tech with a boat and no debt and no destination. But on the first warm evening we took her out anyway, just past the burned pilings, just far enough that the town looked like a painting of itself, and Sefa put her feet up on the crate that had once held the truth and now held fishing tackle.

"Storm coming," Bram said, nodding west, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise.

I felt it before I saw it—the pressure drop, the old itch behind my teeth, the thing that had always told me to run. I stood at the tiller and let it come, and for the first time in my life I didn't run from it or chase it for hire.

I just turned the boat toward it, with my crew, and went to meet it.

The radio crackled. Sefa leaned in, listened, and looked up at me with an expression I couldn't read.

"Ada," she said. "You're going to want to hear this."
