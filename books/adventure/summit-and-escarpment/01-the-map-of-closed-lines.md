## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The tide table said the water would be out past the black rocks by four in the morning, and Silas Sterling had been awake since two, listening to the house tick and settle above the harbor like an old man turning over in bed.

She did not sleep well in October. The mountain took something from the air that month—some softness—and what remained was thin and metallic, a taste like licking a rail spike. Forty years she had lived in this valley, or near enough to forty, and every autumn her body remembered the same thing: winter was coming down the escarpment whether anyone was ready for it or not.

She rose without lighting a lamp. There was no need. She could have walked this cabin blindfolded, could have found the kettle and the stove and the tin of coffee with her eyes shut, and in truth she often did, because light invited attention and attention was the one thing Silas Sterling had spent her whole adult life refusing.

Her cabin sat a mile above the town of Harrow's Bend, up where the old wagon road gave out and the pines took over, close enough to see the town's lights at night—when there were lights to see—and far enough that no one came calling. From her porch you could look down on the rooftops, the fish sheds, the gray curl of harbor water, and beyond it all the great green shoulder of the escarpment rising into cloud. Most people in the Bend thought of the mountain as scenery. Silas thought of it as a document. Every cut, every fill, every sagging grade told you what men had tried to do here and how the mountain had answered.

She drank her coffee standing at the window, watching the last of the dark drain out of the sky, and then she went to work.

* * *

The workroom occupied the back half of the cabin, and it was the only room in the house that mattered. Two long tables under the north-facing windows, lit by daylight because daylight did not flicker. A drafting machine bolted to the first table, its arms oiled and true. Flat files beneath, steel drawers full of linen and vellum, each sheet sleeved in acid-free mylar, each drawer labeled in her small square hand: *Cascade & Northern, Abandoned 1931. Harrow Incline, Never Completed. Summit Line, Closed 1911.*

That last drawer she touched more than the others. She would have denied it if anyone had asked, but no one asked, because no one came.

On the wall above the tables hung the map—the map, though she owned dozens now, some bought, some traced, one or two frankly stolen from county archives that would never miss them. It was her grandfather's original survey of the summit line, drawn in 1909, ink gone brown as old blood, the paper soft as cloth along its folds. Samuel Sterling's name in the title block, in his beautiful engineer's lettering, letters like architecture: *Surveyed and Plotted by S. S. Sterling, Resident Engineer.*

She had grown up believing those letters were holy. That was the inheritance, before the maps themselves: the belief that her grandfather had been a man who drew straight lines through impossible country, and that the line he drew was good, and that the eleven men who died on it in the winter of 1911 had died building something worth dying for.

Silas was fifty-one years old and she no longer believed it, but she could not yet say what she believed instead, and so the map hung on the wall, and every morning she looked at it the way you look at a wound you are not ready to clean.

This morning she did not look at it. This morning she rolled out instead the working copy of the Cascade & Northern, the branch that had died in 1931 when the copper played out, and she began the day's cataloging.

It was strange work, and she knew it. There were perhaps six people alive who cared about the closed lines of this coast, and four of them were academics who had never smelled creosote or stood on a trestle with the wind trying to peel them off into the gorge. But someone had to remember where the rails went. Someone had to walk the grades while they still existed, before the slides ate them and the alder swallowed them, and record what was there: culvert here, collapsed, span twelve feet; retaining wall, dry-laid stone, failing at the east wing; tunnel portal, timbered, unsafe, do not enter. Her field books filled shelf after shelf, neat as scripture, and no one would ever read them, and this did not trouble her. Or she told herself it did not trouble her. There is a kind of person who mistakes being unnecessary for being free, and Silas had been that kind of person for a very long time.

At nine she heard the mail jeep laboring up the switchbacks below—not for her; the jeep never stopped for her; she had a box in town—and she listened to it fade and felt the particular satisfaction of another day in which no one knew where she was or what she was doing.

Loneliness, she called it safety. She had been calling it that so long the words had worn smooth, like stones in a creek bed, and she no longer noticed they were two different things.

* * *

In the afternoon she drove the truck down into the Bend for supplies, because even hermits needed diesel and flour and lamp oil, and because once a week she allowed herself the small theater of being seen.

Harrow's Bend had been dying since before she was born. First the mine, then the mill, then the railroad itself—the passenger service ended when she was a girl, and the freight hung on another twenty years like a man refusing to leave a party. Now the town ran on fishing and stubbornness and the summer tourists who came to photograph the escarpment and buy smoked salmon and ask, inevitably, about the ghost line. There was a mural on the feed store wall, painted by the school kids a few years back: a locomotive bursting through a ring of mountains, all smoke and glory, SURVEY PARTY 1911 lettered underneath in gold. Eleven names. The town kept the names like coins in a pocket, rubbed smooth with handling.

Silas parked behind the mercantile rather than in front of it. Old habit.

"Miss Sterling." Ruthie Kettle was behind the counter, as she had been behind that counter since the Nixon administration, a broad woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray curls and hands that had scaled a thousand fish and opened a thousand crates and were not fooled by anything. "You're low on everything, I expect."

"I made a list."

"You always make a list. List won't tell me you've been eating crackers and sardines for a week. I can see it in your face." Ruthie was already pulling goods off shelves without reference to the list. "Heard you were up on the old Cascade grade Tuesday. Marty saw your truck at the washout."

"Marty should mind his nets."

"Marty minds everybody's business, that's why God put him here." Ruthie rang the goods up without asking prices, because they both knew the prices. "You be careful up there. Those trestles are sixty years past dead."

"I test them."

"I know you test them. Test them twice." Ruthie slid the sack across the counter and looked at her over the glasses, and for a moment the banter dropped away, the way it sometimes did between two women who had known each other since childhood and had never once discussed anything that mattered. "You all right, Silas?"

"I'm fine."

"That's what fine sounds like, is it." Ruthie let it go, as she always did, and Silas carried her sack out into the gray afternoon feeling the familiar small ache of having been seen more clearly than she wished.

She stopped at the harbor on the way back, because the harbor was on the way back only if you made it on the way back, which she did, weekly, without admitting it to herself. The fleet rode at moorings, masts clinking. Down at the end of the float, old Tomas Reyes was mending net, and he lifted a hand to her, and she lifted one back, and neither of them spoke, and that was the correct amount of conversation for both of them.

Below the float, the water lay low and glassy, holding the mountains upside down. And below the water, below the black rocks at the base of the escarpment where nobody ever went because there was nothing there but kelp and broken granite—

Silas stood a moment longer than usual, looking at that stretch of shore, and did not know why.

* * *

That night the wind came around to the northeast, and the barometer on her wall fell like a stone, and by midnight the tide was running out of the bay as though the sea had changed its mind about the whole coast.

She woke at three and could not say why. The cabin was cold. The wind had a new sound in it, a dragging, sucking note, like breath drawn through teeth. She got up and stood at the dark window with her arms crossed against the chill, looking down at the water she could just see glinting far below, and she thought about the tide table pinned beside her desk—she checked it every month the way other people checked their horoscopes, a leftover compulsion from her surveying days, knowing exactly where the sea would be and when.

Extreme low water. The lowest series of the year, beginning tomorrow morning, four-ten a.m., minus three point one feet.

She had lived here thirty years and had never once gone down to look at what an extreme low tide uncovered. There was nothing down there. Everyone said so. Black rock, old logs, the bones of a wharf from before the earthquake.

But she was dressed before she finished deciding to be, boots laced, coat over flannels, flashlight in her pocket, and she was halfway down the dark mile of trail before the thought caught up with her body and asked what, exactly, she thought she was doing.

The answer, when it came, walking down through the wet pines with the sea booming somewhere below, was simple and terrible: she was going because the mountain had been talking to her all week. Because the cataloging had gone stale in her hands. Because for thirty years she had walked the closed lines recording what was there, and had never once walked the one line that mattered, the one that ran through her own name, and some part of her—some part buried deeper than any culvert—had begun to suspect that the sea might know things about her family that she did not.

The trail let out onto the shingle beach below the escarpment, and the sight stopped her cold.

The sea was gone. Not lowered—*gone*, withdrawn half a mile across bared gravel and reef, leaving pools steaming in the cold air and stranded fish flashing silver in her beam. The black rocks she had known all her life stood up like the ribs of something drowned, and beyond them, exposed for the first time in living memory, the base of the cliff itself: a face of wet granite streaked white with mineral stain, and cut into it—

A cave.

Not a hollow, not a shadow. A proper mouth, arched, ten feet high, breathing out cold air that smelled of stone and salt and something older, something like iron. The kind of opening that geology makes once and seals again, that might stand open tonight and be drowned forever by morning.

Silas stood at the edge of the bared reef with the wind pulling her hair and her heart going hard against her ribs, and she understood that she had a choice, and that she had already made it, sometime between the bed and the door, sometime in the last thirty years.

She crossed the reef.

Inside, the cave narrowed and turned, and her flashlight found walls furred with barnacle scar and the high tideline of centuries, and then, forty feet in, above the reach of any ordinary tide, on a ledge of dry gravel—

A strongbox. Rusted to lace, iron bands burst, lid sprung. Around it, scattered and sorted by decades of storms into their own quiet archaeology: a leather satchel gone to paste. Brass instruments fused into green coral. A boot sole. And stenciled faintly on the box's side, in paint that time had almost eaten entirely, letters she recognized the way you recognize your own name spoken by a stranger:

*SURVEY PARTY — 1911.*

Silas knelt on the gravel and did not touch it. For a long moment she did not touch it. The sea boomed somewhere out in the dark, patient, certain of its return.

Then she reached out, and her fingers closed on the frozen lid, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth that crumbled at her touch, she saw paper. Letters. A ledger. And on top, in handwriting she knew better than her own—her grandfather's handwriting, the beautiful architectural letters, but wrong, hurried, the letters of a frightened man—a single page that began:

*If whoever finds this is reading it, then the route we were given was never the route Samuel Sterling surveyed—*

The tide turned. Far off in the black, the first wave of the returning sea broke against the outer rocks, and the sound came down the cave throat like a door closing.
