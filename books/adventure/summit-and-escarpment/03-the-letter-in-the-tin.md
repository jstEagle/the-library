## 3. The Letter in the Tin

The lamp burned low over the kitchen table, and Silas Sterling sat with the strongbox open before her like a wound she had chosen to keep.

She had not slept. The tide had gone out at four in the morning and come back at ten, and she had climbed the escarpment path in the dark with the box wrapped in her oilskin coat against her chest, feeling its weight shift with every step—the weight of other people's last possessions, which is a heavier thing than lead. Now the afternoon was failing outside her windows, the light going thin and gray over the valley, and she had not moved from this chair in hours except to fetch the tweezers, the magnifying glass, the flat iron pan she used for developing prints, repurposed now to hold water for softening paper that had spent forty years learning to be stone.

She worked the way she did everything: slowly, precisely, without permitting herself to feel anything until the work was done.

The box itself was iron, riveted, its lock long since surrendered to rust and salt. Inside, layered like sediment, like geology: a canvas rucksack gone stiff as bark. A brass theodolite case, green with verdigris, the instrument inside seized solid but whole—her grandfather's make, W. & L. E. Gurley, Troy, New York. She knew it the way she knew her own hands. Three men had carried instruments like that up into the mountains in 1911, and only the mountain had come back down.

A leather notebook, fused shut. She set it aside; forcing it would destroy it, and whatever it held could wait until she had steam and patience and a steady heart, and she did not know when any of those would arrive.

And beneath the notebook, folded into eighths and pressed flat by decades of pressure and water, a letter.

* * *

The paper came apart in layers when she lifted it. The ink had run where the sea had reached it, but the sea had not reached all of it. Someone—she thought of the hands that must have done it, shaking or calm, she would never know—had folded the letter small and pushed it deep beneath the theodolite case, into the driest corner of the box, as if burying a seed. Or a accusation. Some things are both.

Silas filled the iron pan with water warmed on the stove, laid the letter flat, and waited. This was the part she knew how to do. Paper relaxed in water. Ink, once it has bitten into fiber, mostly stays. You unroll a document the way you talk down a frightened animal: gradually, with warmth, giving it time. Her grandfather had taught her that too—not this specifically, but the principle underneath it, the one he kept returning to in his lessons, the one she had built her whole life on without examining it:

*The mountain keeps what you give it. So give it nothing careless.*

She peeled the folds apart with tweezers and a bone folder, millimeter by millimeter, breathing through her mouth so her breath wouldn't tremble the water's surface. Outside, a raven called from the dead spruce at the property line, twice, and went silent, as if it too were waiting.

By full dark the letter lay open under the lamp, and she could read it.

* * *

The hand was cramped and slanting, a surveyor's hand, written fast and written badly, the letters of a man who had been trained to write numbers legibly and had never cared much about words. The salutation had dissolved. The date survived: *October 9th, 1911.* Nine days before the party was reported lost. Nine days, perhaps, before they died—though the sea cave said otherwise about the manner of it, said shelter, said waiting, said the slow arithmetic of cold and hunger rather than a single catastrophic slide. She made herself hold that thought without flinching. They had lived in that cave. For how long, she did not yet want to know.

*We have been on the wrong line since the second week,* the letter began, in its drowned, slanting script. *I am certain of it now and I was certain three days ago when I first ran the closure and it would not close. The grade does not match the field notes. It cannot match them. H—— says I have misread my own stadia and perhaps I have, I have misread things before, but not this. Not six percent on a shoulder that will not hold ballast. Not a curve radius out of all reason.*

*I checked against the original traverse sheets tonight while the others slept and there it is. The line we are cutting is not the line that was surveyed. Somewhere between the summit station and here the route was moved. Moved east. Into the scarp. Whoever moved it moved it on paper, in an office, a thousand miles from this rock, and signed the sheets with initials so no one would ever have to say his name aloud.*

*S.S.*

Silas read the initials four times. Then she set the tweezers down very carefully on the table, aligning them parallel to the letter's edge, because her hands wanted to do something else entirely and she would not let them.

S.S. Samuel Sterling. Her grandfather. The man whose portrait hung in the county historical society wearing the expression of a benevolent patriarch. The man after whom the Sterling Siding was named, though the siding had been torn up in '54 and the name was all that remained, a ghost of a ghost. The man who had taught her to read grade and curve on his knee, who had walked her along the old alignments with a hand on her shoulder saying *feel it, Silas, feel where the ground wants the road to go*, the man whose maps she catalogued every winter night in this same room, lovingly, obsessively, the way other people tend graves.

The letter went on.

*I do not understand why. The eastern line saves maybe two miles of tunnel but it crosses the escarpment face and everyone who has stood on that face knows what the face does in thaw. I have written to the company office asking for the original sheets and received back a form letter. I have asked H—— directly and he looks at me the way a man looks at weather coming. I think he knows. I think he has known since June and cannot say it either, because his name is on the reports too, and because there are men in the valley whose money depends on this line opening by spring.*

*Tomorrow we go up to fix a benchmark below the cornice and I am going to look at the face with my own eyes and if it is what I believe it is I will write again. If I do not write again, then let whoever finds this know: the fault is not in our instruments. The fault is in the map. The map lies. Find the man who drew it.*

*Find the man who drew it and ask him what the ground cost.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. She knew the handwriting's cousins on half the benchmark plaques in three counties. Elias Marsh, assistant surveyor, 1911 party. A name she had known all her life from her grandfather's stories—the good-humored one, the one who could whistle through his teeth and carry a transit up scree without spilling a level bubble. One of the five who walked into the mountains in September and did not walk out.

*Ask him what the ground cost.*

Silas sat in the lamplight and let the question settle over her like snowfall, silently, layer on layer, until she could not see the edges of anything.

* * *

She should burn it.

The thought arrived fully formed, reasonable, almost gentle, the way temptation dresses itself in your own voice. Burn the letter. Melt the tin. The sea gives back what it gives back and takes the rest; who would ever know? The box had lain in that cave forty years. It could lie in her stove for ten minutes and lie in the ash pit forever. Her grandfather was dead twenty-two years. The families had grieved and scattered. The legend was set in bronze on the historical society wall: *THE SUMMIT LINE SURVEY PARTY — LOST TO THE MOUNTAIN, SEPTEMBER–OCTOBER 1911 — THEIR SACRIFICE PAVED THE WAY.* Nobody wanted the bronze rewritten. Least of all her. She was the last Sterling. The name was hers to keep or spend, and burning a piece of salt-eaten paper was not even a crime—it was housekeeping. It was mercy, arguably. Mercy for the dead, who could not be hurt further. Mercy for the town, which lived on the pretty version. Mercy for herself, who had built a life on the load-bearing walls of her grandfather's genius and had never once checked the foundation.

She got up. She put another log on the stove. She stood in front of the open firebox with the letter in her hand and felt the heat push against her face, and the fire made the damp paper smell briefly, terribly, of the sea cave again—kelp and iron and old, old dark—and she closed the firebox without feeding it anything.

No.

Not because she was brave. She examined the motive honestly, standing there, because dishonesty with oneself was a kind of rot and she had seen what rot did to trestle bents. No, not because she was brave. Because burning evidence was what a guilty family did, and she had just learned she belonged to one, and she would not add her signature to his. Because Elias Marsh had written *find the man who drew it* with frozen fingers in a hole in the rock, and the least she owed him was to be findable. And because—this was the truest thing, the one she held farthest down—because she was an engineer, and an engineer does not hide a defect. An engineer tags it, red paint, loud as a scream, so that no one walks onto that span again believing it sound.

Her grandfather had hidden a defect. Forty years later, in a kitchen above a fading town, his granddaughter stood in front of a stove deciding whether to inherit the hiding or the truth.

She took the letter back to the table. She laid it flat between panes of glass, weighted the sandwich with two volumes of the Pacific Coast Survey, and wrote in her logbook, in her own cramped engineer's hand, the date, the location of the cave, the tide tables, the contents of the box. Documentation. Chain of custody. If this was going to exist, it was going to exist properly.

Then she banked the lamp, and in the dark she said, out loud, to a man twenty-two years dead, in a voice she did not recognize as her own:

"What did the ground cost?"

The house answered with the tick of cooling iron and the wind moving through the dead spruce, and nothing else.

* * *

She told no one.

For six days she told no one. She went about her life the way she had built it, deliberately, invisibly: down to the store before eight, when the parking area held only Ruthie Kettle's truck; fuel for the generator; the weekly call to the county road office about culvert inspections nobody cared about; home by dusk with the curtains drawn. She transcribed the letter twice, once verbatim and once annotated, cross-referencing it against her grandfather's surviving maps with the methodical fury of a woman auditing a stranger's books. The evidence assembled itself with terrible neatness. The original traverse sheets in his archive showed the western alignment—sound, sensible, through stable ground. The as-built drawings showed the eastern one. Between them, undated, unsigned except by those two initials, a supplementary sheet revising the route "per engineering review," with no engineering review attached anywhere in any file she owned.

He had moved the line. On paper. In an office. And five men had gone up to build the lie with their hands.

She learned other things too, in those six days, things the letter had started and her grandfather's papers finished. Small payments, recurring, coded in his ledgers as "consulting fees" to names she didn't recognize, continuing for thirty years, stopping only at his death. She stared at those entries a long time on the fourth night, because they looked like guilt and they also looked like penance, and she could not tell which, and she suspected the difference mattered enormously and that she was not yet ready to know.

But she told no one. That was the discipline. That was the inheritance, too, she realized—secrecy passed down like eye color. He had kept it. His ledgers kept it. And now she was keeping it, walking through the store and the post office and the diner with a burned secret folded against her ribs, nodding to people who had known her father and her grandfather, smiling the small closed smile she'd practiced since childhood, and discovering that the smile had gotten heavier. That invisibility, which she had always mistaken for safety, was starting to feel like complicity. Every "morning, Miss Sterling" from the storekeeper landed on her now like a small audit.

On the sixth day, near dusk, she heard the truck.

It came up the switchbacks below her place too fast for the grade, tires complaining, and stopped in her yard in a wash of gravel and dying light, and Tomas Reyes got out with his cap in his hands and the particular hunched posture of a man carrying something he wishes were lighter.

Silas stepped out onto her porch. Below the yard, far down, the sea was going gray and the tide was pulling back off the black rocks, exposing, inch by inch, the drowned architecture of the coast—and somewhere down there, under the next low water, the cave mouth waited with its patience.

"Tide's dropping," Tomas said, by way of hello. He looked at the escarpment, at her door, at his boots. "Big drop. Lowest I'll see till spring, likely."

"I know," Silas said. "I watch the tables."

"Yeah." He turned his cap in his hands. "I figured you did." He glanced up at her then, quickly, and away, and she saw it in him plainly, the way she could see a stress fracture in a rail head—something small, loaded, about to propagate. "You were down there," he said. "Morning of the big low. I was working the kelp lines off the point. Saw your light in the cliff."

The wind moved through the dead spruce. Silas stood very still on her porch and felt the fracture begin to run.

"I didn't say nothing," Tomas said. "Wouldn't. But there's a fella come into town, couple days back. Out-of-towner. Nice coat. Asking questions about 1911, about the lost survey outfit, buying drinks, real friendly." He swallowed. "He asked me had anyone been poking around the cliffs lately. And I—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "I might've mentioned. I don't know why I mentioned. He's got money, Silas, and the boat needs—"

"Tomas." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Underneath the level voice, something vast and cold was calving off inside her, some certainty she had been refusing since the night at the lamp. "What did you tell him?"

"That folks say the Sterling girl knows more about the old lines than anybody living." He put his cap on, finally, like a man finishing a confession. "That if anybody'd find what the tide gave up, it'd be you."

Far below, the sea drew back another foot from the rocks, quiet as a thief, revealing more of what it had kept.

Silas looked at the road winding down toward town, where a man in a nice coat was asking friendly questions, and understood that the six days of silence had just ended—not because she had spoken, but because the world had heard anyway. Mountains keep what you give them. So do towns.

"Thank you for telling me," she said, and meant it, and watched Tomas get back in his truck and grind away down the switchbacks with his debt riding beside him.

Then she went inside, and took her grandfather's maps of the summit line down from the shelf, and began, for the first time, to plan a climb.
