## 4. Word Travels Downhill

The herring came in late that October, late and thin, and Tomas Reyes spent three gray days hauling nets that came up lighter than they should have, the sea giving him its small cold shrug. By the fourth morning he'd given up on fish and gone instead to the tide.

It was habit more than hope. Forty years of working these waters had taught him that the sea kept a ledger and paid out its debts at odd hours—crab pots lost a decade ago returned barnacled and whole; a skiff belonging to a drowned man washed ashore two winters after his funeral, dry inside somehow, as if the water had been keeping it safe. So when the great low tide drained the bay below the escarpment farther than he'd ever seen it go, exposing black shelves of rock and weed-slick channels no living man had walked, Tomas took his lantern and went down to see what the ledger held.

He found the cave an hour before the water began to come back.

* * *

He told himself afterward that he'd only looked. That was the story he gave, first to himself and then, when the time came, to the stranger with the soft voice and the hard eyes: he'd only looked, only lifted the lid of the rusted strongbox because it was already loose, already opening like a mouth. Inside there had been tin cups fused together by salt. A leather satchel gone to paste. Papers, dozens of them, wadded and browned, and among them a survey map so fine and strange that even Tomas—who could not read a word of English beyond *NO TRESPASSING* and the prices chalked on Ruthie Kettle's window—could tell it was drawn by someone who loved lines the way he loved the water, every contour considered, every curve a kind of devotion.

And bones. He did not mention the bones right away. The bones sat in his chest for three days like swallowed ice before he finally spoke of them, and even then only sideways, only as "remains," only once, to a man who had come asking.

The man's name was Voss.

* * *

Edmund Voss arrived on the Thursday boat, which was itself unusual—the Thursday boat carried mail and mackerel and nothing else worth anyone's crossing—and he stepped off it dressed in a way that made the whole landing feel underdressed. Wool coat the color of wet slate. Boots that had never seen mud. A leather case slung across his back with the careful geometry of a man who carried instruments.

Tomas noticed him the way you notice weather changing—not all at once but as a pressure behind the ears. The stranger stood at the rail of the dock for a long while, looking up at the escarpment where it shouldered out over the town, gray and enormous, wearing its first dusting of snow like a man trying on a hat he wasn't sure suited him.

"You're the fisherman." It was not a question. Voss had crossed the dock without Tomas seeing him move, which was its own small alarm. "Tomas Reyes. They said at the store you were the one who found the cave."

"I found some rocks," Tomas said. "The tide found the cave."

"The tide." Voss smiled. He had a smile like a door left open in winter—inviting, and cold behind it. "Yes. They say this year's low was the lowest in living memory. Lowest since 1911, one old fellow told me. Interesting, that. Nineteen-eleven keeps coming up in this town."

"Does it."

"There was a survey party." Voss turned from the escarpment at last and looked at Tomas directly, and Tomas felt the look settle on him like a hand. "Seven men and a boy. Went up the mountain in June of 1911 to run a line for the Coast & Cascade, and never came down. The railroad called it a washout. The town called it bad luck. I've read both accounts, Mr. Reyes, and I don't believe either one." He paused. "I believe the mountain kept them. And now the mountain is giving them back."

The wind came off the water and Tomas pulled his collar up. Around them the gulls worked the gutting tables, screaming their idiot hunger, and the smell of fish blood lay over everything like a truth nobody could wash off.

"People find things," Tomas said carefully. "Old things. It doesn't mean anything."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "Usually it doesn't." And then, as if the thought had only just occurred to him, as if he hadn't crossed a hundred miles of coast to have exactly this conversation: "What did you find, Mr. Reyes?"

* * *

Here is what Tomas would remember later, turning it over in the dark of his cabin like a stone he couldn't stop rubbing: the moment stretched long enough that he could have said nothing. Could have laughed, shrugged, pointed at the empty horizon. The stranger had no proof. He had a coat and a voice and a way of standing too close, but he had nothing, and a lie costs nothing to tell, and Tomas had told lies before—small ones, to his wife about the drinking, to the cannery about the catch, the ordinary mortar of an ordinary life.

But the money was already in the conversation. He could feel it the way you feel a big fish on the line before you ever see it—a weight, a patience, something alive pulling gently at the far end of him.

"There was a box," he heard himself say. "Rusted shut mostly. Some papers. Water got into everything."

"What kind of papers?"

"Old ones. Drawings. Lines and numbers." He watched Voss's face do something very small and very controlled, a lock turning somewhere behind the eyes. "Maybe maps."

"And where are these papers now?"

"Gone." This was true, or true enough. After the third day of the ice sitting in his chest, Tomas had rowed out past the point at dawn and dropped the papers into deep water, all except the one map, which he had kept, folded into oilcloth, wrapped in a sock, buried under the floorboards beneath his bunk, because a man can only be honest up to a certain depth and no further. "Threw them back. Didn't seem right, keeping dead men's letters."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Somewhere behind that still face, Tomas understood, calculations were running—weights and measures, what a fisherman knew against what a fisherman would sell.

"No," Voss said at last. "I suppose it wouldn't seem right." Then, mildly, terribly: "Did you see anyone else down there? Anyone else who might have been to the cave?"

And there it was. The name rose in Tomas's throat like a bubble through dark water, and he could have swallowed it. God help him, he tried. But the stranger was watching him with those patient eyes, and the money was a weight on the line, and forty years of hauling nets had taught Tomas Reyes that what comes up must come up all the way.

"Sterling," he said. "Silas Sterling. She was down there before me. Lives up past the tree line, old company house. Keeps to herself."

Something moved through Voss then, quick as a shadow under a boat. "Sterling," he repeated, tasting it. "Any relation to the Sterling who ran the 1911 survey? S. Sterling, chief engineer—he signed the reports I've read. A legend in the trade, by reputation. The man who mapped the impossible grade."

"That's her grandfather," Tomas said, and heard how it sounded, how each word he gave away was another plank pried off his own floor. "She's got all his papers up there, they say. Boxes of them. Maps nobody's ever seen."

"Nobody's ever seen," Voss murmured. He reached into his coat and produced a card, heavy stock, embossed, and pressed it into Tomas's palm along with something else, folded paper, thick. Tomas didn't look at it. He didn't need to. "You've been very helpful, Mr. Reyes. If anything else occurs to you—if the sea gives up anything else—I'm staying at the Kettle house until the weather turns. Come find me."

He walked away up the dock road toward the town, unhurried, his boots silent on the planks, and Tomas stood in the fish-smell and the wind with the folded money in his fist and felt something in him settle and shift, the way scree shifts on a slope—one stone moving, then another, then the whole hillside deciding.

* * *

That night he drank half a bottle of the cheap rye he kept for occasions worse than celebrations, and the money sat on the table where he'd thrown it, and the map sat under the floorboards where it always sat, and the two of them seemed to hum at each other across the room like tuning forks.

His wife had been dead six years. His daughter wrote from the city twice a year and sent photographs of children he'd met once. The boat needed a new winch. The winter was coming on early—the escarpment had taken snow in October, which the old-timers called a bad omen, though the old-timers called everything an omen—and the herring had run thin, and the money on the table was more than he'd make in a season of good fishing.

None of it excused him. He knew that. Knowing it, he counted it anyway, and the counting was its own kind of drowning, quiet and slow.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told Voss, the thing he'd kept back like the biggest fish in the hold: Silas Sterling had stood at the mouth of that cave with the lantern shaking in her hand, and when she'd seen him on the rocks above she had not waved, had not called out, had only looked at him with an expression he'd seen once before—on a man watching his boat take water faster than he could bail. And she had said, in that flat quiet voice of hers, "This doesn't leave the beach, Tomas. Please."

Please. From Sterling, who never asked anyone for anything, who bought her flour and lamp oil and said maybe four words doing it, who had lived above the town like a lighthouse with the lamp turned off for twenty years. Please.

And he had nodded. He had sworn it, more or less, with a nod, the way men swear things they don't intend to be bound by.

Now the nod lay between him and the money like a body.

* * *

In the morning it was snowing—fine, dry, early snow, the kind that squeaks underfoot—and Tomas walked up through the town with his collar up and his guilt walking beside him like a dog he couldn't shake. Past Ruthie Kettle's store, where the lamplight was yellow and warm and somebody was laughing inside. Past the sheriff's dark office. Up the switchback road toward the tree line, telling himself he was going to warn her, that was all, just to warn her, a debt paid in the only coin he had left.

He stopped at the last fence post below her cabin. Smoke stood up from her chimney, straight in the still air. Through the uncurtained window he could see her bent over a table under a lamp, working, her hands moving over paper with that same devotion he'd seen in the dead man's map.

He stood there a long time. The snow came down between them, patient, accumulating.

He did not knock. He told himself it was mercy—better she hear it from no one than know her neighbor sold her—but the truth was simpler and colder: knocking meant confessing, and confessing meant giving the money back, and the winch on the boat was broken, and winter was coming, and he was the kind of man who was, and had always been, the kind of man who was.

He turned around and walked back down. Behind him the smoke rose straight from her chimney into the gray morning, a thin dark thread stitching her to the sky, and the snow kept falling on the town and the escarpment and the drowned cave below the tide line, falling without hurry, without malice, flake upon flake upon flake, the way small betrayals fall—each one weightless, each one nothing, until the roof groans.

At the bottom of the road, leaning against the fence by the store, waiting as if he'd known the exact hour, stood Edmund Voss.

"Mr. Reyes," he said, smiling his open-door smile. "I wonder if you might draw me a sketch. Of the coast. Just the shape of it—where the escarpment meets the water, and where a man might climb down."

Tomas looked at the snow, and at the money Voss was already taking from his coat, and at the smoke rising thin and straight above the tree line.

"Yeah," he heard himself say. "I can draw you that."
