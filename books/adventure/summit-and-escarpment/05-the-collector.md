## 5. The Collector

Edmund Voss had learned long ago that the fastest way into a town's confidence was to arrive slightly wrong.

Not badly wrong—wrongness invited correction, and people loved nothing so much as correcting a stranger. So he wore his good coat but not his best one, carried a leather case that looked expensive but not new, and let the ferryman at the landing see him fumble the coins for the crossing so that the man could wave them off with the rough generosity of someone who wanted a story to tell tonight. By the time Voss stepped onto the gravel shore of Kestrel Narrows, he was already becoming a character: the gentleman scholar who couldn't manage his own change. It was a role he had played in forty towns on three coasts, and it had never once failed him.

The town sat where the river bent away from the escarpment, a scatter of shingled roofs and salt-bleached porches under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Behind everything, always, rose the mountain—the great gray shoulder of it, seamed with the ghost-lines of grades and cuts, the old railroad's spine still visible where the forest hadn't swallowed it. Voss stood a moment on the shore and let himself look at it the way he imagined a man looks at a creditor.

*There you are,* he thought. *Forty years, and there you are.*

He had been nine years old when his grandfather stopped speaking of the mountains. He remembered the silence better than any explanation: the way the old man's hands would go still over his supper, the way he'd leave a room when the radio mentioned railroads, the way he died with a locked drawer in his desk that Voss's father had opened and emptied without ceremony, keeping only a photograph of seven men in oilskins standing before a tunnel mouth, their faces turned toward the camera with the particular blankness of men who did not yet know they were being photographed for posterity's sake—or for an epitaph's.

Six of those men had gone into the high country in October 1911 and never come out. The seventh had come down alone, half-frozen, with a story about weather and a washout, and had spent the rest of his life being quietly pitied by everyone who knew what pity cost.

Voss had spent thirty years learning that the story was a lie. Not the weather—the weather had been real enough, the storm of '11 was in every almanac on the coast—but the *shape* of it. The route they'd been sent up was wrong. He had the letters to prove fragments of it, the surveyor's widow's correspondence, a cancelled check from a mining concern that had paid for a line through country no competent engineer would have chosen. Someone had drawn that line. Someone had signed it.

And now, if the fisherman's story held, someone's granddaughter had pulled the proof out of the sea.

* * *

He began, as always, with the tavern, because a tavern is a town's confessional and its marketplace at once.

The Anchor Room smelled of creosote and fryer grease and the sweet rot of spilled beer gone into the floorboards. Voss took a stool at the end of the bar, ordered whatever the man beside him was drinking, and opened with the oldest key in his ring: he asked for help with something small. Directions, first. Then the name of the man who owned the chandlery. Then, folding a bill absently into his palm as though he'd forgotten it was there, he mentioned—he made it sound like an afterthought, a scholarly footnote—that he was researching the survey parties of the early century. The 1911 party especially. Such a tragedy. Such a *mystery*, really, when one considered the discrepancies in the official report.

The bartender's rag slowed. The man two stools down turned on his elbow.

"You're the second one this month asking after that," the bartender said.

"Am I?" Voss let surprise move across his face like weather. "Who else?"

"Fellow from the university, springtime." A lie, or a half-lie; Voss filed it. "And then there's Tomas Reyes been telling anyone who'll buy him a drink about a cave."

"Reyes," Voss repeated, tasting the name. "A fisherman?"

"Salmon, mostly. Crab when the salmon don't cooperate." The bartender leaned in, lowering his voice with the delicious reluctance of a man who has already decided to tell. "Says the tide went out farther than he's seen in twenty years, down under the escarpment, and there's a cave down there nobody's laid eyes on since his granddad was young. Says there's bones in it."

"Bones?" Voss let the word hang. He did not smile. Smiling here would be fatal; the room wanted gravity, wanted to feel that it was handing him something heavy rather than selling something cheap.

"Old ones. And a strongbox, he says, all rusted through. He wouldn't say what was in it." The bartender's eyes flicked toward the far end of the bar, where a heavyset man in a yellow slicker was working through a plate of eggs with the concentration of the recently paid. "But he came in Tuesday night and drank like a man who'd found religion or money, and I'd put my wages on money."

Voss thanked him gravely, bought the room a round—it cost him eleven dollars and purchased him the evening's goodwill—and left before curiosity could curdle into scrutiny. Outside, the wind off the water carried the smell of kelp and cold iron. He stood beneath the tavern's awning and watched the light fail along the escarpment, the mountain going from gray to violet to black, and permitted himself, briefly, alone, the expression he never wore in company: the thin, hungry satisfaction of a man who has found the seam in a wall.

*Strongbox.* Not scattered effects, not a camp midden, not the sad detritus of men who died slowly. A strongbox meant organization. It meant the party had cached their records deliberately—which meant the records were worth caching, which meant the original field map, the true route, the thing Voss had chased through archives and attics and two continents, might be sitting in a rusted tin not forty miles from where he stood.

Or it might already be gone. Carried off by a granddaughter with a family name to protect.

He turned his collar up against the wind and went to find Tomas Reyes.

* * *

Reyes proved easier to read than most. He met Voss at the chandlery door the next morning—word traveled fast in a town of four hundred—and sized him up with the frank arithmetic of a man who had sold things before and knew the difference between buyers and browsers.

"You're the one asking about the cave," Reyes said. Not a question.

"I'm the one asking about 1911," Voss said pleasantly. "The cave is your story. I wouldn't presume to it."

That landed well. Men like Reyes wanted ownership of their discoveries even as they wanted to be rid of the burden of them; Voss had learned to offer both at once. They walked down to the dock together, boots ringing on wet planks, gulls screaming their idiot liturgy overhead, and Voss listened—head tilted, notebook open, pencil moving in small respectful strokes—as Reyes told it: the freak low tide, the green light under the overhang, the smell of the cave like a closed room after fifty years, the ribs of rock, the rusted box pried open with a boat hook, the *things* inside.

"What things?" Voss asked softly.

Reyes hesitated. His hands, scarred and rope-burned, worked at each other. "A compass. Boots. Some papers, all stuck together, more mush than paper. A watch stopped at—" he frowned, trying to remember, and Voss watched the memory assemble itself under the pressure of being asked, watched the man build the detail because the detail was wanted. "Four-something. Four-forty, maybe."

"And you told no one but the tavern."

"Told the sheriff, too. He came down, looked at it, said it was a county matter and the county doesn't pay for matters." Reyes spat over the rail with practiced economy. "Then the tide came back and the sea took its room again. You can't get down there now. Won't be another tide like that till—" he shrugged. "Years. Maybe never. That was a once kind of tide."

"Then whoever wants what's in that cave needed to have been there Tuesday," Voss said, watching him.

Reyes's jaw worked. He looked out at the water, at the gray line of the escarpment, and Voss saw the exact moment the fisherman understood what he was being asked and how much it might be worth and how much trouble it might be worth less. Greed and fear, weighing themselves on the same scale.

"There was a woman down there," Reyes said finally. "Before me, or after—I don't know which. Saw her coming up the goat track with her trousers wet to the knee and something wrapped in oilcloth under her arm. Sterling woman. Lives up past the tree line in her grandfather's old place. Keeps to herself." He said the last part with the faint contempt of a gregarious man for a private one. "If she took something out of that cave, it'll be in that cabin of hers. But I'm not saying I know that. I'm saying I saw a woman on a trail."

"Of course," Voss agreed warmly. "You saw a woman on a trail."

He paid him fifty dollars for the sighting, pressed the bills into the rope-hardened hand with the gravity of a man settling a debt of honor, and asked, as an afterthought, whether the Sterling woman came into town often.

"Rarely. Buys flour and lamp oil at Kettle's store, keeps her business short. Ruthie Kettle's the only one she talks to, near enough." Reyes pocketed the money with a quickness that embarrassed them both. "She's a strange one. Polite, but like a fence is polite."

"Like a fence," Voss repeated, delighted. "Yes. I imagine she is."

* * *

He spent three days being charming, and he was very good at it.

He called on the sheriff with a letter of introduction from a historical society that existed mostly on his own stationery, and left with a copy of the 1911 inquest file and the sheriff's blessing to poke around anything above the high-tide line. He bought coffee for the postmistress and learned the mail routes, the freight schedules, which cabins got newspapers and which got only bills. He sat with the ancient woman who ran the museum—a converted schoolhouse with eleven visitors a month—and let her show him the display case with its photograph of the lost party, and he studied the faces of those six doomed men until he could have picked them out of a crowd, and behind them, slightly apart, taller, with his chin lifted as though the camera owed him something, Silas Sterling's grandfather: Samuel Sterling, S.S., the engineer whose signature was on the fatal route.

*You drew the line,* Voss thought at the photograph, with something that was not quite hatred and had never quite ripened into grief. *You drew the line and you walked down out of the snow and you lived fifty years on what it cost them.*

The museum woman caught him staring. "His granddaughter has the rest of his papers, they say," she offered, in the tone of a woman who had waited years for someone to ask. "Up at the house. Never let anyone see them. Ruthie Kettle says the girl's half her grandfather and half the mountain, and neither half friendly."

"How vivid," Voss said, and smiled his scholar's smile, and bought a postcard of the escarpment he did not want.

By the third evening he had assembled the town the way he assembled everything: as a mechanism. Kettle's store for supplies and gossip, open till six. The sheriff, amiable and underfunded, unlikely to climb anything. The telegraph remnant at the pass, dead these fifteen years, its wire run up the old right-of-way. And the road—the single road—ending at a gate below the Sterling cabin, beyond which only foot trails climbed into the closed country where the 1911 party had gone to die.

He spread his notes on the tavern table after closing, by lamplight, with a glass of the bartender's indifferent whisky, and drew the valley freehand: the town, the river, the escarpment, the ghost-lines of grade and cut climbing toward the summit depot that appeared on no current map but which he had found referenced in a 1909 construction bulletin. *Depot, Summit Station, mile 41.4.* If Sterling had cached anything—if he had kept the true notes anywhere—it would be there, at the top of the line he had falsified, in the place he could never bring himself to dynamite or burn. Guilty men keep their evidence close. Voss counted on it the way other men counted on sunrise.

And between him and that depot, up a trail that would have snow on it within the month, stood a cabin, and in that cabin a woman with wet trouser cuffs and an oilcloth bundle.

He considered her now, the way he considered all obstacles: with respect, and without mercy. She was an engineer, the museum woman had said—surveyed culverts, walked the old lines, knew the mountain the way Reyes knew the tide tables. She would be competent. Competence was fine; competence could be outrun, outfitted, outlasted. What troubled him—what he noted, honestly, in the margin of his notes, because self-deception was a luxury he had never allowed himself—was that she had gone to the cave *alone*, before the tide gave it up to anyone else. She had known where to look. Which meant she had known what to look for. Which meant she had grown up on the story of 1911 the same way he had, except from the other side of it, and a woman defending her family's name was a different animal entirely from a man pursuing his family's honor.

Different animals required different handling. He wrote, in his small precise hand: *Approach gently. Learn what she has. Do not alarm.*

Then he underlined *gently* twice, and smiled at the word, and finished his whisky.

Outside, the first hard wind of the season came down off the escarpment and rattled the tavern's shutters like a fist on a door, and somewhere up in the darkening heights, past the tree line, a single window burned yellow in the black shoulder of the mountain. Voss stood on the porch a long moment, looking at it, letting the cold sharpen him.

Tomorrow, he decided. Tomorrow he would call on Miss Sterling, with his best manners and his worst intentions, wearing the polite face the town had already learned to trust.

He turned up his collar and went in to sleep, and the mountain stood over the valley all night, silent, holding its snow.
