## 6. Questions at the Door

She saw him coming from the kitchen window, and her first instinct was to count the ways he could be turned around.

He came up the switchback road on foot, which told her something already. Men who drove stopped at the gate and honked; men who walked wanted to be seen arriving, wanted their approach to be a kind of courtesy. He wore a long coat of good wool, city-cut, and he carried a leather case under one arm like a hymnal. The morning was cold enough that his breath showed, a small white flag with every step.

Silas dried her hands on a rag and watched him come. She had three days since Tomas Reyes's boat had been seen tied at the wharf too long, three days since the fisherman's laugh had carried across the water in a way that meant money had changed hands, or was about to. She had spent those days telling herself that a story sold in a tavern was a coin that wore smooth before it reached the far bank. She had spent them also taking down the strongbox from the shelf above her desk, looking at it, and putting it back.

Now the coin had arrived at her door wearing a scholar's coat.

She opened the door before he knocked. It was better, she had learned, to be the one standing inside the frame.

"Miss Sterling." He removed his hat. He was younger than his coat suggested, fifty perhaps, silver at the temples, with the unlined hands of a man who had never held anything heavier than a fountain pen. "Edmund Voss. I hope the hour isn't inconvenient."

"Most hours are inconvenient," Silas said. "What is it?"

"A courtesy call." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced the way a surveyor practices reading contours—until you looked at the actual ground beneath. "I'm compiling a history of the coastal surveys. The expeditions of the early century. Yours is a valley with a remarkable memory, Miss Sterling. Most places forget their dead. This one seems to have misfiled them."

"Who told you we had dead to misfile?"

"Valleys talk." He said it lightly, but his eyes went past her shoulder, into the house, with the unhurried thoroughness of a man taking inventory. She saw them catch on the map rails above her desk, on the rolled sheets standing in the umbrella stand like kindling. "May I come in? I promise I'm more interesting than the weather."

The wind chose that moment to come down the escarpment and shove against them both, as if the mountain itself had an opinion. Silas stepped back. She would tell herself later that she did it because it was colder to keep a man on the step than to let him in and be done with him, and she would know even then that it was a lie.

* * *

He sat where she put him, on the kitchen chair with the short leg, and declined coffee in a way that made it clear he had noticed she'd offered it the way you offer a dog a bone you intend to keep. He set his leather case on the table but did not open it. He talked.

He talked about the coastal survey of 1909, about triangulation stations and the men who had carried glass and brass up scree slopes for a dollar a day. He talked about the escarpment line, the one her grandfather had run through the pass, and he pronounced the names of the dead men of 1911 with a fluency that made her spine go cold, because he had not learned those names this week. He had carried them a long way.

"Hollis. Marsh. the two Okafor brothers, Peter and Daniel. A boy, Eli Brandt, seventeen, who kept the party's journal." He recited it like a litany, then looked at her with mild, intelligent interest. "Your grandfather's name is conspicuously absent from the party roster, though his initials are on the route drawings. Silas Sterling. S.S. Curious, that."

"My grandfather was the chief engineer," Silas said. "He surveyed from the office. The field party did the field work."

"Of course." Voss turned his hat brim slowly in his hands. "And the field work is the interesting part, isn't it? Because the party went up the east fork in October of 1911, and the east fork in October is a grave with a view. Any engineer worth his transit would have known it. Your grandfather was worth his transit."

"The weather was freakish that year."

"The weather is always freakish," Voss said pleasantly, "after the route is chosen."

The kettle on the stove began its low complaint, and Silas got up to tend it because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't trembling. Behind her, Voss's voice followed, conversational, almost gentle.

"I'll tell you why I've come all this way, Miss Sterling. A fisherman in this town has been telling a story about a cave below the escarpment. A low tide, a rusted strongbox, a woman who came down the cliff path at dawn and left with something under her arm. The town thinks it's treasure. The town always thinks it's treasure." He paused. "I think it's a map."

Silas poured the water over the grounds and watched the bloom rise, dark and slow. "The town thinks a great many things."

"The town thinks you're a hermit who talks to her maps. The town thinks Tomas Reyes would sell his grandmother's teeth for a bottle of rye, and they're right about that. The town," Voss said, "is not what interests me. What interests me is that when I mentioned the name Sterling at the hotel last night, the room went quiet. Not hostile. Careful. As though the name were a load-bearing wall." He smiled again. "Load-bearing walls are the first thing you check, in my line of work. You never know what they're holding up."

She carried the coffee to the table. Her hand was steady. She had trained it to be steady through twenty years of bridge inspections and slope reports, and it obeyed her now, and she hated it for obeying her so well.

"I don't know what you want from me, Mr. Voss."

"Nothing," he said. "That's the beauty of it. I want nothing from you. I want something that was in that cave, and I'm given to understand it's no longer in the cave. Which means it's either in this house, or it's somewhere else, and either way, time is working for me and against you." He opened the leather case at last and drew out a photograph, laid it on the table between them with two fingers, like a man playing a card he'd been saving. "Do you recognize this?"

It was a survey party, posed on a rail flatcar, 1911 by the date pencilled in the corner. Seven men and a boy. Her grandfather stood at the center, younger than she had ever seen him in any photograph she owned, one hand on the shoulder of the boy—Eli Brandt, seventeen, keeper of the journal. Her grandfather was smiling. She had never seen him smile in a photograph. In every image she had of him, from the stiff portrait above her mantel to the grainy newspaper clipping of his ribbon-cutting, he had the face of a man holding a ledger closed.

"He looks happy here," Voss said, watching her.

"He looks young."

"Young men are happy before they know what they've done." Voss took the photograph back, squared it, and returned it to his case with a care that was almost tender. "I'm going to be honest with you, Miss Sterling, because I think you're a person who respects honesty, even when it's aimed at you. I believe the original route map of the 1911 survey survived. I believe it came out of that cave. And I believe it is the single most valuable document in the history of this valley—not for what it's worth in dollars, though it's worth a great deal in dollars, but for what it proves. Whoever holds it holds the truth about why seven men and a boy walked up a riverbed in October and never walked down."

"And if I had it," Silas said, "what would you do with it?"

"Preserve it. Publish it. The truth is a public good, Miss Sterling. I've made a career of it." He stood, settled his coat, set his hat at an angle that suggested a man with many more doors to knock on. "You should know that I've retained a guide. A woman named Brandt—no relation to the boy, or so she claims. She knows the escarpment the way you know your drafting table. If the map is up the mountain, in the old summit depot, say, in some engineer's cache—well. Snow comes early up there. It would be a shame for anyone to be caught in it."

He paused at the door, and for one moment the charm thinned and she saw the thing beneath it, the way you see bedrock where a stream has washed the soil away: something patient and hungry and very, very old in its grievance.

"The dead don't care who tells their story, Miss Sterling. Only the living do." He tipped his hat. "I'll see you again before the snow."

* * *

She stood in the doorway until he was a speck on the switchback road, and then a while longer, because her legs had opinions of their own.

The lie she had told sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *I don't know what you want from me.* Not a denial, exactly. Worse than a denial—a dodge, the verbal equivalent of a culvert too small for the spring runoff. He had seen it. He had catalogued it with those unlined hands and that hymnal case and gone down the mountain to tell his guide: she has it, or she knows where it is, and she's frightened, and frightened people run.

She went inside and took down the strongbox.

The letter inside was soft as moth wing now, from handling. *The line was moved. God forgive me, I watched him move it, and I said nothing.* The water had taken the signature but not the shape of the accusation. S.S. Two letters. Her whole inheritance in two letters, the same initials she signed on every drawing, every inspection report, every bridge certification of her own career. She had spent her life signing her grandfather's name over and over without knowing it.

She set the letter down and looked at the map rails above her desk. The escarpment line ran across all of them like a scar. And past the summit, past the last station, the depot—a square of ink no bigger than a thumbnail, with a notation in her grandfather's hand that she had read a thousand times without reading: *field files, 1908–1912, stored.*

She had always assumed the notation meant drafts. Carbon copies. The paper sediment that every engineering office produces and no one wants.

But her grandfather had been a man who paid anonymous settlements. She had found the bank drafts after his death, forty years of them, amounts that matched no invoice she could locate, mailed from a town three counties over. A man who paid in secret kept his proof in secret. And a man who falsified a route kept the true one—kept it the way you keep a loaded gun, where you can reach it and no one else can.

Voss thought the map was in the strongbox. The strongbox held only the accusation. The map—the true route, the field notes, the whole confession in ink and contour lines—was at the summit depot, forty miles up the closed line, through the washout gorge, across the high traverse, on a shoulder of the escarpment where the first snows were already forming, she could feel it in the old fracture in her knee that had never lied to her about weather.

And Voss had hired a guide. Brandt. He had said the name the way a man lays a card face-up to watch your eyes.

Silas sat down at her desk and did what she always did when the world became unbearable: she opened a map.

She measured the distance in the way engineers measure, honestly, with dividers and a scale and no mercy. Forty miles of dead rail. Eleven miles of it passable. Six miles of trestle that had not been walked since the line closed, and one gorge crossing where the 1936 flood had taken the bridge and left the abutments staring at each other across a hundred feet of white water. The high traverse above it, corniced, exposed, a place where the mountain had already demonstrated its opinion of the line by shrugging a mile of it into the sea.

Against that: Voss. His guide. Whatever men he could hire in a town where a bottle of rye bought a fisherman's silence. They would go up the east fork, the sane way, the trail way. Three days, maybe four, if the weather held.

The closed line was longer. Harder. Colder. And it was hers—every culvert, every grade, every curve of it catalogued in her own hand, in her own maps, the obsession the town laughed at and the mountain had been waiting for.

She thought of Ruthie Kettle and did not go to her. Not yet. There would be time for promises later, and lies before that, and she was already ashamed of both and going to do them anyway.

She pulled the drafting lamp close. Outside, the light was going copper and flat, the light that meant snow somewhere above the tree line, and the wind had begun to carry the smell of it, that mineral emptiness rolling down off the escarpment like a warning sent in advance of the army.

She unrolled the line profile and weighted its corners with the strongbox, the tin, the coffee can of her grandfather's brass weights. She began, in her small precise hand, to write a list.

Provisions. Rope. The good parka, the one she'd sworn at for twenty years and never replaced. Fuel. The flare pistol from the boat shed. And at the bottom, after a long pause, in letters pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*Beat them there.*

The lamp hissed. The first flakes came down the valley at dusk, fine and dry, the scouts of the storm, melting on the window glass like small dying stars. Silas Sterling watched them and felt the fear in her chest change its shape—cooling, contracting, setting hard, the way water sets when it becomes the thing you walk on.

By morning she would be a liar and a trespasser and a woman running up a dead railroad into winter. By morning it would be the only honest thing left to do.
