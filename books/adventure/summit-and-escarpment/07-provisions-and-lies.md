## 7. Provisions and Lies

The morning of her departure came in gray and quiet, the kind of light that made the town look like a photograph of itself—faded at the edges, true only in the center. Silas rose before the sun cleared the escarpment and laid everything out on her kitchen table the way she'd once laid out survey instruments: rope, sixty feet; pitons she'd ground sharp herself; the small brass spirit level that had been her grandfather's before it was hers; a canvas rucksack with one strap mended in waxed thread. She counted food twice. Hard bread, cheese wrapped in cloth, a tin of rendered fat, dried apples, salt pork, chocolate gone gray at the corners but still good. Nine days if the weather held. Four if it didn't.

She told herself she was going up to inspect culverts.

It was a good lie. It had the shape of truth, which is what makes a lie dangerous—the way a false bearing on a map will carry you confidently for miles before the country stops matching the paper. Culvert inspection was real work; she'd done it twice since spring, walking the lower grades with a notebook and a shovel, clearing debris from the stone throats that carried snowmelt under the old roadbed. If anyone asked, and someone would, she had an answer ready, plain and boring as gravel. Nobody followed an engineer up a mountain to watch her look at drainage.

But she packed the lie first, before the rope. That was the order of things now, whether she liked it or not. The strongbox sat hidden beneath her floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, and the letter inside it sat inside her head, word for word, though she'd only read it three times. *The line was moved. Ask S.S. why.* Forty years of grief in a fishing village below, forty years of a name worn like a medal above—and all of it resting on a route her grandfather had drawn with his own careful hand, in his own careful ink, knowing—or not knowing—what the change would cost. She still didn't know which. That was the worst of it. She couldn't even hate him cleanly.

She dressed in wool and leather, braided her hair tight against the wind, and looked at herself in the small mirror by the door. The woman there looked exactly like the woman who had walked down to Hargrove's store a dozen times this year. That was the point. Invisibility was a discipline, and she had practiced it for thirty years.

She locked the cabin, tucking the key into the loose mortar where it always went, and started down the switchback trail toward town with the rucksack riding high between her shoulders, heavier than culvert work required, lighter than what she was actually carrying.

* * *

Hargrove's General Store smelled the way it had smelled her whole life: coffee, kerosene, gun oil, the sweet dust of flour sacks, and underneath it all the cold stone breath of the building's foundation, which had been poured before anyone thought to ask whether the town would last. Abel Hargrove stood behind the counter the way he'd stood behind it for forty years, a long man folded over a ledger, spectacles low on his nose, reading glasses and reading face both suggesting that whatever you wanted, it was going to be an inconvenience.

"Morning, Miss Sterling."

"Morning, Abel." She set her list on the counter, written out the night before in her narrow engineer's hand, quantities precise, nothing sentimental. "I'll be needing these."

He read it top to bottom without changing expression, which was his gift. Rope. Salt pork. Bread. Candle stubs. A coil of safety fuse—not for blasting, he wouldn't wonder about that, because everyone knew the old lines needed occasional clearing. Matches. "Culverts again?" he said.

"Up past the second tunnel. The spring melt took a bite out of the approach embankment." She kept her voice level, unhurried, the voice of a woman discussing ditches. "County won't send anyone until the budget clears, and by then the roadbed'll be in the gorge."

"Mm." He began pulling goods, weighing pork on the brass scale, counting bread into paper. His hands were slow and exact, a bookkeeper's hands, and Silas watched them and felt the familiar comfort of transaction—money across wood, goods across wood, no questions that mattered. This was how she preferred to be known: as a name on a list, a customer, a quantity.

"You'll want the heavy rope," Abel said. "The light stuff's been sitting in damp."

"Heavy, then."

He weighed it out. Sixty feet coiled like something sleeping. He set it on the pile and then paused, spectacles catching the lamplight, and looked at her over the rims for the first time. "That's a lot of provisions for culverts."

"It's a lot of culverts."

He held her gaze a moment longer than commerce required. Then he shrugged, wrote the total in the ledger with his nib scratching like a beetle, and said, "Weather's turning. Barometer's been sliding since Tuesday. My knee says snow inside a week."

"Your knee's been saying snow since June."

"And it's been right every October." He pushed the sack toward her. "You want anything else?"

"No." She counted out bills. "Thank you, Abel."

"Miss Sterling." He said it as she turned, and she stopped, hand on the door. He was looking at her again, mild and unreadable behind the spectacles. "Tell the mountain I said hello."

She didn't trust her voice, so she nodded, and the bell over the door rang her out into the street.

* * *

The town was awake around her in its small ways: smoke standing straight up from chimneys in the windless cold, a dog trotting somewhere with purpose, the mill wheel turning its slow brown water. She kept her head down and her pace ordinary. Past the church with its leaning steeple. Past the sheriff's office, dark, Sheriff Dunn presumably still asleep or still drinking, either way no obstacle. Past the Kettle house, where—

Where Ruthie Kettle was sitting on her porch steps with a cup of coffee, watching her come, the way Ruthie had watched her come for thirty years, since they were girls throwing stones off the trestle into the river, since Ruthie had been the only person in town who never seemed to need Silas to be anything other than exactly what she was.

There was no avoiding the porch. There never had been.

"That bag's too full for culverts," Ruthie said, before Silas had both feet on the bottom step.

"Good morning to you too."

"I'm serious, Silas." Ruthie set her cup down on the step. She was a broad, weathered woman with gray coming in at her temples like frost finding the high ground first, and eyes that had missed nothing in this valley for half a century. "Abel just told me you bought enough food for a mining crew and sixty feet of heavy rope. Culvert inspection doesn't take rope. Culvert inspection takes a shovel and a bad attitude, and you've already got the second one."

Silas stood at the foot of the steps with the rucksack pressing its weight into her shoulders, and considered the geometry of escape. There wasn't any. There never was, with Ruthie. "I'm going up the old line," she said finally. "That's all you get."

"All I get," Ruthie repeated softly. She studied Silas the way she studied weather, and Silas felt herself read, front to back, the strongbox and the letter and the stranger asking questions in town, all of it, or maybe none of it, maybe just the simple fact of a friend lying badly. "How far up?"

"The summit depot."

Ruthie was quiet for a moment. A jay scolded from the birches. Down the street, the mill wheel kept its patient turning. "Alone," Ruthie said. Not a question.

"Alone."

"In October. On a closed line nobody's maintained since before we were born. With snow coming—" She gestured at the sky, at the barometer-fall of the whole gray morning. "Silas. Sit down."

"I can't. I want to be past the washout by noon."

"Sit *down*."

Silas sat. The step was cold through her trousers. Ruthie picked up her coffee, drank, set it down again, and when she spoke her voice had changed—lower, stripped of the porch-morning ease. "There's a man staying at the hotel. Voss. Been buying drinks and asking about 1911. About the cave. About *you*, specifically, and where your cabin is, and whether you've been seen on the beach below the escarpment." She let that sit. "Tomas Reyes has been telling stories for coin. You know how Tomas is. A fish finds him and by supper it's a whale."

Silas's stomach tightened. "What kind of questions?"

"The kind a man asks when he wants something and doesn't care who pays for it." Ruthie turned to face her fully. "So don't tell me it's culverts. Don't insult us both. Tell me why a polite stranger with money is hunting you up a mountain."

The truth pressed against the back of Silas's teeth. She had carried it alone for two weeks now, had slept beside it, eaten beside it, and the carrying had begun to feel like the only honest thing left in her possession. But Ruthie's eyes were on her, and they were the eyes of the girl who had once sat with her all night on the water tower after Silas's mother died, saying nothing, asking nothing, just being there in the dark like ballast.

"My grandfather," Silas said slowly, "surveyed the Summit Extension. The route that killed the '11 party."

"Everybody knows that. Your family built this valley."

"Everybody knows he *built* it." Silas looked down at her hands, at the rope-burn scars and the pencil stains that never washed fully out. "The letter in that box says he moved the line. Says the route they died on wasn't the route he surveyed first. Somebody changed it, and the somebody was him." Her voice came out flat, engineered, each word placed like rail on tie. "And there may be papers at the depot that prove it. And a man named Voss wants those papers very badly, and I intend to reach them first."

Ruthie said nothing for a long time. The jay gave up and flew off. Somewhere a door slammed, ordinary life continuing around the two of them like water around stones.

"Well," Ruthie said at last. "Hell, Silas."

"Yes."

"Is it true? What the letter says?"

"I don't know." And there it was, the raw place, and saying it aloud hurt more than she'd expected, like probing a tooth. "That's why I have to go. Whatever's up there, I can't—it can't just sit in some drawer waiting for him to find it. It's mine. It's my name too."

Ruthie nodded slowly. She reached over and put her hand flat on the rucksack between them, on the canvas over the rope and the bread and the lies, and left it there like a blessing or a restraint. "Two promises," she said. "You'll make me two promises before you go another step."

"Ruthie—"

"One." Ruthie's grip tightened. "You come back alive. Not mostly alive, not dragged back by searchers in a week—I'd know, I'd organize them—you walk back down that trail on your own feet. You turn around the minute the weather turns, do you hear me? No line, no letter, no dead man's name is worth your hide. The mountain forgives nothing and remembers everything, and it has been waiting forty years for somebody arrogant enough to think they own it."

Silas almost smiled. "And two?"

Ruthie's voice dropped, and gentled, which was worse. "You come back honest. Whatever you find up there—whatever your grandfather did or didn't do—you don't bury it, and you don't burn it, and you don't decide for the rest of us what we can bear to know. You've spent thirty years being invisible, Silas Sterling, and I've loved you anyway, but I'm tired of watching you carry things alone that were never yours to carry alone. So you bring the truth down that mountain and you put it on this porch, on this step, right here, and we'll figure out together what to do with it. Promise me."

The words went into Silas like a piton driven home—clean, deep, holding. She found that her throat had closed. She looked out at the street, at the smoke and the mill wheel and the escarpment rising blue-gray beyond the rooftops, vast and indifferent, wearing its glacier like a crown it had no intention of sharing.

"Come back alive," she said. "And come back honest."

"Both of them. Not one."

"Both." She met Ruthie's eyes. "I promise."

Ruthie exhaled, long and slow, and some of the iron went out of her shoulders. She stood, went inside, and came back with a wool scarf, deep green, hand-knitted, and stuffed it into the outer pocket of the rucksack without asking permission. "My sister made that. She'll want it back on your neck, so don't lose it in a crevasse." Then she hugged Silas hard, brief and fierce, coffee smell and wool and thirty years, and stepped back before either of them could make it into something unbearable.

"Go on, then," Ruthie said. "Before I talk sense into you."

"There's no such thing."

"No," Ruthie agreed, sitting back down and picking up her cup. "There isn't. That's always been your problem." But she was smiling when she said it, and Silas carried the smile up out of town like a coal banked against the cold.

* * *

The trail left the last fence post behind within the hour, and then it was her and the grade and the mountain. She climbed steadily, rationing her breath, testing each footing out of habit, and did not look back at the town shrinking below her—smoke, steeple, mill wheel, porch. She could feel the promise sitting in her chest alongside the dread, two weights that somehow balanced each other, and she understood, with the clarity that altitude sometimes grants, that she had just done the harder thing. Lying to Abel had cost nothing. Letting Ruthie see her had cost everything, and bought back more.

By midday she reached the mouth of the collapsed tunnel where the closed line began in earnest—a black arch of shattered timber and spilled rock, breathing cold air from the dark like something asleep. Beyond it lay twenty miles of dead railroad: rotting trestles, rusted rail shining in the grass like veins, culverts real and imagined, and at the end of it all, the depot, and whatever truth her grandfather had locked inside it.

Behind her, far below, she thought she saw movement on the valley road. Small. Deliberate. Heading for the trailhead.

She shouldered the pack, checked her watch against the sun, and stepped into the dark of the tunnel.
