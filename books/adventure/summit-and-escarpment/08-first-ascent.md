## 8. First Ascent

The tunnel mouth had been breathing stone and shadow for sixty years, and when Silas put her shoulder to the rubble at its lip, it exhaled a smell she knew the way other people knew their mothers' kitchens: cold rock, rusted iron, the mineral sweetness of water working patiently through limestone. She stood in the gray light before dawn with her pack settled on her hips and let herself feel the size of what she was about to do.

The Cascade & Northern had called this the Mile 14 Tunnel on the surveys. The locals had called it the Throat. It had swallowed the line in the slide of '49, and the company, already dying, had simply let it. No one had bothered to clear it because no one had bothered to care, and Silas had spent her whole life in the country of things no one bothered to care about. It suited her. It had always suited her.

She clicked on her headlamp and stepped in.

The collapse was not total. She had known it wouldn't be—she had studied the old photographs, the ones her grandfather kept in a drawer he thought she didn't know about, and in the last of them, taken a decade after the slide, a gap remained near the ceiling where the rockfall had bridged itself into an arch. The gap was still there, smaller now, furred with ice. She crawled through it on her belly with the pack dragging behind her like something alive and unwilling, her breath loud in the confined dark, and for the length of a minute she was a child again, wedged under her grandfather's drafting table while he worked above her, listening to the scratch of his pencil and the tick of the stove and feeling that the safest place in the world was the underside of a man's obsession.

Then she was through, and the tunnel opened around her, and she stood up in the dark with dust sifting off her shoulders and her heart going hard.

* * *

The air inside was still, and the stillness had texture. Her lamp picked out the tunnel's ribs—timber sets gone silver-gray, steel arches furred orange with rust—receding into a darkness her light could not finish. Water had found a seam somewhere and dripped with the regularity of a clock. Drip. Drip. Drip. She counted eleven between drips and made herself stop counting.

She walked the Mile 14 Tunnel the way she walked everything: measuring. The gauge of the old roadbed was still readable beneath the ballast spill, the rails themselves pulled up decades ago but their spiking scars left in the ties like a sentence with the words removed. She put her palm flat against a tie. Wet, but sound. The creosote had done its work and then stopped doing it, and the wood was holding its argument with rot at a stalemate.

Her grandfather had taught her to read a line the way a priest reads scripture—slowly, and with the understanding that everything meant something.

"Grade first," he'd said, when she was nine, standing on the abandoned spur above Kettle Creek with his hand heavy on her shoulder. "Grade tells you what the engineer feared. Curve tells you what he wanted. And the two of them together—" he'd crouched, tracing the arc of the roadbed with one blunt finger, "—the two of them together tell you what he was willing to spend to get it."

She was thinking of that now, in the tunnel dark, and she was thinking of it with a bitterness that surprised her by how fresh it was. Forty years the letter had lain in its tin, and only a week had passed since she'd read it, but already the words had worn channels in her, the way water wears channels in limestone: *the line was moved, and the man who moved it knew, and the man who moved it signed his initials same as scripture.*

S.S. Silas Sterling. Her grandfather, whose hands had taught hers to read grade and curve.

She walked on.

* * *

The tunnel ran nine hundred feet and gave her a mile of roadbed beyond it, cut into the escarpment's shoulder like a shelf, and the morning came up gray and windless as she emerged. Below her the valley held its fog like a bowl holds milk. She could see the town—or the idea of the town, rooftops and the church spire and the thin thread of the highway—smoke rising from Ruthie's kitchen chimney, a smudge she watched longer than she needed to.

*Come back alive, and come back honest.* Ruthie had made her say it twice, gripping her wrist across the counter of the store, flour on her hands and no humor in her face at all. Silas had said it. She had not decided yet whether she meant it. Honesty was a heavy pack to carry uphill, and she was already carrying enough.

She checked her watch. Six-forty. The weather window was what it was—three days, maybe four, before the pattern turned, and every old-timer in the valley had said the same thing in the same tone: *she'll close early this year.* Above her the escarpment went up and up, gray ramparts streaked with the white of old slides, and somewhere at the top of all that stone was the summit depot, and somewhere in the depot was her grandfather's cache, and somewhere in the cache was the truth entire, and she intended to reach it before Voss did, or before the snow did, whichever came first.

She walked.

* * *

The first trestle came at Mile 16, and it stopped her breath.

It crossed a gorge that the maps called Miller's Notch and the railroaders had called, with the flat cruelty of working men, the Coffin—two hundred feet of air over a creek that ran white and loud at the bottom. The trestle itself was timber, tall as a ten-story building, and sixty years of weather had turned it into something between a structure and a skeleton. Half the deck planking was gone. The stringers showed their hearts, gray and checked. Snow had melted and refrozen in the joints a thousand times and the thousandth time had split them.

Silas sat at the near end with her back against an abutment and ate half a biscuit and made herself look at it properly, the way her grandfather would have made her look at it.

"Never trust a bridge," he'd told her once, on this very line, when she was fourteen and he was already an old man with a tremor in his left hand he pretended not to have. "Trust the man who built it. And if you don't know the man, read his work. A builder tells you everything about himself in a bridge. Whether he was hurried. Whether he was honest. Whether he was afraid."

"Was he?" she'd asked. "Afraid?"

Her grandfather had been quiet for a moment, looking down the length of the trestle with an expression she'd had no tools, at fourteen, to read. Now, with the letter's words burned into her, she could read it fine.

"Every good one is," he'd said. "The fear is the craft. It's the man who isn't afraid you want to keep off your railroad."

She got up and tested the trestle.

She went out onto it the way she'd been taught—never on the deck, always on the stringers, feet on the lower chords where the timber was thickest, one hand on the cap above, testing each member before she gave it her weight. The wind came up the gorge in slow pulses, and each pulse moved the whole structure a fraction, a groan deep in the bents like a sleeping animal shifting. At the third bent she found the rot. A pile cap gone punky, soft as bread under her thumb, and the diagonal brace above it hanging by two spikes. She memorized it, marked her route to the sound side, and crossed.

At the middle, two hundred feet over the white water, she stopped and made herself look down.

This was the thing about her grandfather that the letter had not changed and could not change: he had taught her this. He had taught her to be afraid in the correct proportions, to let the fear read the structure for her, to put her life on timber some dead carpenter had spiked together in 1910 and know, in her bones, whether it would hold. Whatever else he had been—falsifier, coward, the man who moved the line—he had made her competent at staying alive, and he had done it with a patience and a tenderness that she could not reconcile with the initials in the tin, and the not-reconciling hurt worse than either thing alone.

She crossed the rest of the Coffin and did not look back.

* * *

She camped that first night in a cut below Mile 19, a place where the line had been blasted through a rib of granite and the walls still held the drill marks, fan after fan of them, the fingerprints of men long dead. She got her stove going behind a wind wall of stone, ate, checked her feet, and lay in her bag watching the stars wheel over the cut's black rim.

Her grandfather had been brilliant. That was the part no one argued, not even now, not even her. He had run his first survey at twenty-three and his party had come down off the mountain with numbers so clean the chief engineer had accused them of copying. He had found the pass everyone said didn't exist. He had been ambitious—everyone said that too, said it in the tone people used for weather—and he had been afraid. She knew that last part not from the letter but from a hundred small memories: the way he checked a lock twice, the way he never sat with his back to a door, the way, one night when she was eleven and couldn't sleep, she'd found him standing at his drafting window at three in the morning with a brandy he wasn't drinking, looking at nothing, and when she'd asked what was wrong he'd said, "Nothing, sprout," in a voice that was a lie so old it had gone smooth as river stone.

She had loved him. That was the fact underneath all the other facts, the bedrock the rest was cut into. She had loved him, and he had taught her everything she knew, and he had moved the line, and the men of the 1911 party had gone into that cave and not come out, and somewhere in her chest these truths lay against each other like broken glass, cutting whichever way she turned them.

*He was afraid,* she thought, in the dark of the cut. *That's what the letter is. That's what the settlements were, if they were. Fear, wearing the clothes of craft.*

She slept badly and woke before light and was glad of it.

* * *

The second day the line climbed in earnest, switchbacking up the escarpment's shoulder in long traverses, and the going slowed. In places the roadbed had slumped off its bench and she had to traverse the raw slope above it, testing each hold. In one place a slide had taken the bench entirely and she climbed forty feet of talus on all fours, the pack catching on the brush, the drop below her opening out into the kind of view she refused to enjoy.

At noon she came over a shoulder of rock and saw, three switchbacks below and across the face, movement.

She dropped flat without deciding to. Her body had learned this from somewhere—her grandfather, probably, who had the instincts of a man who expected to be found. She glassed it with the small binoculars she carried, elbows in the scree, breath held.

Two figures on the old roadbed. One large, moving with the heavy economy of a man who carried weight for a living. One smaller, moving like a soldier. They were a mile back along the line she had climbed yesterday, and they were moving fast, and they were not lost. Lost people stop. Lost people stand and turn. These two walked like a sentence being spoken.

Voss. He had found the tunnel, or bought the finding, and he had hired legs that could use it.

Silas lowered the glasses and lay against the rock with her heart going and the wind pulling at her collar, and she made herself think instead of feel. They were a day behind her, maybe less. They were heavier—more men, more gear—but they were faster, and they did not have to test every trestle because they did not care if the mountain kept them. The weather window was closing. The summit depot was a day and a half ahead of her, two days ahead of them, and the arithmetic of that was not comfortable.

She could go down. Down was the town, was Ruthie, was the sheriff and a report and a week of talk while Voss did whatever Voss intended to do with a head start.

She looked up at the escarpment, at the gray ramparts going white at their tops, at the thin dark seam of the old line stitching its way toward the pass.

Her grandfather had taught her to read grade and curve. The grade said what the engineer feared. The curve said what he wanted. She looked at her own route, laid out against the mountain, and read it the way he'd taught her, and what it said was that she was her grandfather's granddaughter after all: ambitious, and afraid, and going up.

She got her pack on and climbed.

* * *

She made the high camp at dusk, in the lee of a snow shed the company had built in 1912 and never removed—a timber tunnel roofed with earth, built to shrug off exactly the avalanches she was now camping beneath, which was either reassurance or irony depending on how she held it. Inside, the shed was dry and dark and smelled of creosote and mouse, and her stove made its small jet sound in the black, and she was warm for the first time all day.

That was when she saw the initials.

They were cut into the timber of the shed's far wall, low, at the height of a man sitting with his back against it. Old cuts, weathered gray, but legible in the stove's light: *E.V. — 1911*, and beside them, smaller, in a different hand: *H.M. was here, and H.M. was cold.*

E.V. Voss's grandfather. The party's leader, the man history had blamed. And H.M.—she turned the initials over in her mind and found no name in her grandfather's papers to fit them, which was itself a kind of answer. A man erased from the record leaves no initials in the record either. Only here, in timber, where no one was keeping score.

Silas sat with her back against the wall an arm's length from the cuts and drank her tea and felt the mountain settle around her, the snow shed creaking under the weight of the slope above, and she thought: *they sat here. They sat here in 1911, cold, and cut their names, and three weeks later they were in the cave.* And the man who moved the line was her blood, and the man they blamed was the blood of the man now climbing her mountain behind her, and the whole of it was a knot that had been tightening for forty years and had chosen her, of all people, to cut.

She banked the stove and lay down and did not sleep so much as wait out the night, and near dawn she dreamed of her grandfather at his drafting table, and in the dream he looked up at her and started to say her name, and she woke with the word unfinished in the cold air of the shed.

Outside, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had turned, and it was coming out of the north.

The snow was early this year. She had maybe a day. Maybe less.

She packed in the dark and stepped out onto the line, and far below her, tiny with distance, a light was moving up the switchbacks.
