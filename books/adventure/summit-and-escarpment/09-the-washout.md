## 9. The Washout

The mountain woke her before dawn with a sound like a door closing somewhere far below the world.

Silas lay in the lee of the old culvert wall, wrapped in canvas and wool, and listened to it come again—a deep, grinding exhalation from the gorge below the line, as though the earth had turned over in its sleep and settled into a new position. Frost furred the inside of her collar. Her breath hung above her face in a small gray cloud that rose and thinned and vanished, and she thought: *that is what I am doing to myself, breath by breath, rising and vanishing on this mountain where nobody knows to look.*

She had been three days on the closed line. Three days of trestles tested plank by plank, of ballast shifted by forty winters of freeze and thaw, of tunnels breathing cold air at her like the throats of dead animals. She knew this grade—knew it the way you know a scar on your own body, from maps she had traced a thousand times under lamplight—but knowing a thing on paper and knowing it under your boots were two different countries, and the second one did not forgive.

*The wilderness forgives nothing.* Her grandfather used to say that, standing at the window of the cabin with his coffee going cold, watching weather come down off the escarpment. She had thought it was wisdom when she was nine. Now she understood it was a confession. He had learned it in 1911, up here, with five men and a boy, and he had carried the lesson home and never once explained the tuition.

She ate half a biscuit and a strip of dried venison, chewing slowly because chewing slowly made the food last longer in the mind if not the stomach, and broke camp in the blue hour before sunrise. The line climbed eastward through a cut choked with alder, and beyond the cut, according to the survey sheets folded against her chest, the roadbed crossed a gorge on a fill—a man-made shoulder of rock and gravel hauled in by mule teams sixty years ago, wide enough for two rails and a walking crew.

Fills were honest things when they were built. They were liars after sixty years of water.

She heard the gorge before she saw it—the same sound that had woken her, amplified now into something continuous, a rushing and crumbling beneath the alder silence. When she came out of the cut onto the last solid bench of roadbed, she stopped so abruptly that her pack swung forward and nearly took her with it.

The fill was gone.

Not all of it. Not even most of it. But the middle third of the crossing had slumped away down into the gorge sometime in the night—one whole section of the shoulder simply *removed*, as if a giant hand had scooped it out, leaving a raw amphitheater of exposed strata and a gap of perhaps thirty feet between the near edge and the far. Below the gap, eighty feet down, the creek ran white over its boulders, loud and indifferent, carrying the mountain's bones away piece by piece toward the sea.

Silas stood at the edge for a long time without moving. The wind came up the gorge and pressed her clothes flat against her back and tried, gently, patiently, the way wind does, to persuade her off the edge.

She got down on her knees instead and looked.

The far edge was not clean. The slump had left a shelf of compacted fill jutting out over the void on the far side, maybe four feet of it, ragged, undercut, but real. Between the near lip and that shelf the gap narrowed at its top to perhaps twenty-two feet—and hanging across the gap, sagging almost to the level of the shelf, was a length of rusted rail. It had been part of the track, of course; when the fill went, the rails had gone with it, but this one had caught on something—a boulder, a buried tie—and now arched across the chasm like a bent rib, its web corroded through in places, its base flaking orange.

A rail is not a bridge. A rail is a dare.

She measured it with her eyes the way her grandfather had taught her to measure everything: twice, and then a third time to catch yourself lying. Twenty-two feet of span. The rail's deflection under its own weight already visible—a shallow bow, maybe eight inches of sag at center. Rail steel from that era, good steel, but steel that had spent six decades drinking rust-water. It would hold a hundred pounds. It might hold a hundred and thirty. It would not hold a hundred and thirty pounds swinging, or landing, or hesitating halfway across while the mind failed.

Her pack weighed forty-one pounds. She had weighed it at Ruthie's kitchen scale, and Ruthie had laughed and said, *You're carrying more than some men carry out of marriages,* and Silas had smiled because it was expected, and had not told her that every pound had been chosen the way a surgeon chooses instruments.

She sat back on her heels and let herself feel the fear properly, all of it, without flinching, because her grandfather had also taught her—though not in words, only in the way he sometimes went quiet mid-sentence—that fear ignored does not go away. It waits. It collects interest.

Then she unpacked.

* * *

She built the anchor first, because anchors are hope made physical, and hope should be the first thing you build and the last thing you spend. There was a sound tie still bedded in the near roadbed, black with creosote, sunk to its shoulders in ballast. She dug around it until she could pass her rope beneath, doubled it, cinched it with a timber hitch her hands tied without instruction from her, and hauled on it until her shoulders burned and the tie did not so much as creak. Good. Then the harness—two loops of rope around her thighs and waist, the way she'd learned from a climbing manual bought secondhand in a city she'd visited exactly once and never returned to.

The food was the hard part.

She spread it out on the tarp: biscuit, venison, the waxed cheese, the tin of condensed milk, the sugar, the salt pork, the chocolate she'd been saving against despair. Forty-one pounds total, and more than half of it food, and there was no way across that rail with any of it on her back. The arithmetic was simple and cruel. She could make two trips—cross, anchor, return, cross again—but the rail would not forgive being loaded twice, and daylight would not wait, and the sky to the northwest had begun assembling the gray argument that becomes snow.

One trip. One crossing. Half the food.

She chose with a coldness that frightened her a little: the fat and the sugar and the salt, because those killed you last and kept you alive longest; the chocolate, because despair was a danger as real as exposure; half the biscuits. She left the rest of the venison and the milk and the remaining biscuits bundled neatly on the tarp at the edge of the gap, weighted with a stone, because some superstition older than sense refused to let her simply abandon food on a mountain.

*If I come back this way,* she told herself, knowing she would not.

She coiled the rope across her chest, clipped the carabiner to the rail itself—not to her anchor, which would do no good past mid-span—and stood at the lip of the gap with the wind worrying her hood and the creek roaring its one note eighty feet below.

"Read it," her grandfather said, in memory, his hand heavy on her shoulder, both of them standing on a trestle long since burned for scrap. "The structure will tell you what it thinks of you. You just have to listen."

The rail said nothing. Rails never did. That was the difference between structures and people, and she had always preferred structures, and she stepped out onto it.

The first ten feet were almost easy. The rail lay close to the fill's shoulder, and she moved in a crouch, one hand sliding along the corroded web, the ballast gritting under her boots. Then the fill ended and the rail began to speak—she felt it before she heard it, a fine tremor coming up through her palm, a vibration like a plucked string that has not yet decided whether to break. At mid-span the bow deepened under her weight. She watched the far shelf rise and fall slightly with each of her steps, a slow nodding, the whole arc of rusted steel breathing with her.

Do not stop. Stopping is death's favorite invitation.

At fifteen feet the corrosion found her. Her right boot came down on a section where the base had flaked to lace, and the lace gave—not all at once, but with a soft wet crunch, like stepping through rotten ice—and her foot dropped six inches and her whole weight swung onto her hands and the rail screamed.

It was not a loud scream. It was worse than that: a high, thin, singing note, the voice of metal at the exact edge of its patience, and it went through her teeth and her sternum and down into the gorge where the creek swallowed it. She froze, arms locked, knees against the rail, twenty feet of air beneath her boots and eighty feet of white water beneath the air, and the wind pushed at her shoulders with the flat of its hand, saying *go on, go on, it's only a little way down, everyone goes eventually.*

"Not today," she said aloud. Her voice sounded absurdly small, a human sound in a place that had forgotten humans.

She crawled. There was no dignity in it and she did not want dignity; she wanted distance. Hand over hand along the web, knees finding the moments between vibrations, she covered the last seven feet in a hundred years, and then her hand slapped down on solid fill and her boots found the shelf and she rolled away from the edge and lay on her back on the far side of the gorge, chest heaving, staring up at a sky going the color of dishwater.

The rail sang behind her for a moment longer, then fell silent, still bowed, still holding, holding nothing now but its own regret.

She allowed herself ninety seconds of shaking. She counted them. Then she stood, shouldered the pack, and climbed.

* * *

Above the washout the line gained the shoulder of the escarpment proper, and the country opened into the long treeless pitch she had dreaded since the valley: miles of exposed grade stitched across slopes of scree and bunchgrass, switchbacking upward through country where a misstep meant a slide measured in hundreds of feet rather than dozens. The wind up here had teeth. It came off the high snowfields to the north, smelling of iron and distance, and it combed the grass flat and drove grit against her goggles until the world arrived in small stinging pieces.

She walked with her head down and her mind on grade and curve, the old litany. *Two percent here, easing to one and a quarter. Curve radius generous—they were proud of this stretch. He was proud of this stretch.* And then the litany snagged, as it always snagged now, on the word *proud*, and she thought about pride and falsified surveys and five men in a sea cave, and she walked faster, as if the thoughts were weather she could outpace.

Near noon she gained the ridge above the third switchback—a knife of a ridge, wind-scoured, offering the day's first true view back along the line.

She stopped to drink, squeezing water from her canteen in careful sips, and it was while she drank that she saw them.

At first she took them for shadows. Far below, back at the second switchback, where the line curved around a shoulder of rock, something moved that was not wind and not animal. She lowered the canteen. She took off her goggles and wiped them on her sleeve and put them back on, and looked again, and made herself count.

Four figures. Small with distance, antlike, but unmistakable in their order: a point man breaking trail, two in the middle, one trailing with the deliberate pace of a laden man. They were on her line. On the *closed* line, forty miles from the nearest road, moving with purpose, and there was only one direction they could be going, and only one reason anyone would come this way in the season's last kindness of weather.

They had found the cave. Or Tomas had sold the story whole, fisherman's exaggeration and all, and someone had believed it enough to buy guides and gear and a week of hard climbing. She thought of Voss's polite gray eyes, the scholarly stoop, the way he had held his teacup as though the cup were fortunate to be held. *Men like that don't climb mountains,* she had told herself. *Men like that send other men.*

She had been right, and being right felt like a hand closing around her ankle.

She watched them for ten minutes, motionless on the ridgeline, wind hammering her back. They moved well—not townsfolk, not treasure-hungry clerks. The point man placed his feet with a economy she recognized; she had seen it in army manuals and in the way certain men step off trains. Hired professionals. Mountaineers. Which meant they would move faster than her when the ground turned technical, and they did not have to be careful of their food, because whoever paid them had paid for plenty.

Her hand went to the map case against her chest, an instinct like shielding a wound. The field notes were still two days ahead, locked in the depot cache, and between her and them lay the high traverse and whatever weather the northwest sky was still assembling. Behind her, gaining, came four men who wanted what she carried in her head and would take apart her family name to get it.

She should feel afraid. She noted, distantly, that she mostly felt *clear*—the strange lucidity that arrives when a choice stops being hypothetical. Run and hide, and they would find the depot eventually, and find her grandfather's legend intact and her guilt unspoken, and the truth would rot in the dark another forty years. Or go on, faster, and reach the notes first, and decide herself what the truth was worth and how it should be spent.

Below her, the lead figure paused at the washout. Even at this distance she could read the posture: the man had reached the gap and found the rail and was making the same calculation she had made at dawn. She watched him test it, watch him send the lightest of them across—the same arithmetic, performed by other hands.

The wind gusted, hard, and flattened the grass, and when it eased, the figures had moved on, tiny and patient, following her tracks up the line like a sentence following its first word.

Silas turned east, toward the traverse, toward the storm she could now see building in earnest over the summit—a bruise-colored mass with a bright cold seam beneath it, the color the mountains wore when they intended to keep what they caught.

She walked. The wilderness forgave nothing, and she had stopped asking it to.

But at the head of the ridge, where the line bent toward the traverse and the last of the town's world dropped away behind her, she glanced back once more—and saw, glinting on the far rim of the washout, a flash of glass. Binoculars. Held steady. Pointed not at the line ahead of them, but up. At her.

They had seen her too.

She pulled her hood low, set her boots to the broken ground, and gave the mountain the only answer it had ever respected: she climbed.
