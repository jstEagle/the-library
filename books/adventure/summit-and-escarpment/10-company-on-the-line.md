## 10. Company on the Line

Ilsa Brandt had learned to read weather the way other people read faces, and by the third morning on the closed line she did not like what it was saying. The sky over the escarpment had gone the color of pewter, flat and heavy, and the wind that came down off the summit carried a smell like iron and old snow—the smell of a mountain holding its breath.

She stood at the mouth of the collapsed tunnel where their party had entered the line two days before, watching Voss consult a photocopied survey map with the reverence of a man reading scripture. Behind him, his other guide—Dane Kowalczyk, big through the shoulders and quiet in a way that had nothing to do with calm—was tightening the straps on a load of climbing gear they would not need if the weather turned. Two hired porters from the valley town hovered at the edge of the group, already regretting their wages.

"Brandt." Voss didn't look up. "You've been standing there five minutes."

"Checking the sky."

"And?"

"Snow inside three days. Maybe two." She let that sit. "We should reconsider the pace."

Voss folded the map along its creases, each fold precise as surgery. He was not a large man, but he occupied space the way a blade occupies a sheath—entirely, without apology. His voice, when he used it, was warm the way a stove is warm: useful, and dangerous to touch.

"The pace is set," he said. "The woman is a day ahead of us at most. She's fifty-three years old and carrying a pack she packed herself, which means she packed too much sentiment and not enough food. We cut the switchbacks below the shoulder and we take her at the traverse."

Ilsa said nothing. She had been a soldier long enough to know when an order was an order and when it was a test, and this was both.

* * *

The switchbacks were a hundred years of engineering laid down in switch after switch up the face of the escarpment—stone retaining walls furred with lichen, ties rotted to black lace, grade so gentle a loaded mule could have walked it blind. Ilsa had climbed in the Hindu Kush and the Chugach and the icefalls above Saas-Fee, and she still stopped at the first bend and looked back down the line with something close to respect. Whoever built this had understood the mountain. Had listened to it.

"You're dawdling," Dane said behind her.

"I'm looking."

"At what?"

"At work somebody did right." She started up again. "Which is more than I can say for what we're about to do to it."

Dane grunted. He was not stupid; that was the trouble with him. Stupid men you could manage. Dane simply did not let himself think past the next payday, and he had made a religion of not thinking, and he expected her to convert.

They caught the others at the second switchback, where Voss stood over a section of wall with a pry bar in his gloved hands.

"We're cutting here," he said. "Straight up the fall line. It saves four hours."

"It's loose scree under that snowfield," Ilsa said. "And the snowfield is sitting on a slope that runs out onto the roadbed. You kick that loose and everything above it comes down."

"That's why we're cutting now, while it's frozen." He said it patiently, the way you explain arithmetic to a child. "Four hours, Brandt. The storm buys us nothing and costs her everything. She'll hole up somewhere and wait it out, and then we lose her until spring, and spring is unacceptable."

"Cutting the switchbacks doesn't just save us time. It wrecks the line for anyone coming up behind. Rescue parties. Searchers."

"There are no rescue parties. No one knows she's up here except us and one sentimental storekeeper." He smiled, and the smile did not reach anything above his cheekbones. "Besides, that's rather the point. Come here. I want to show you the rest of it."

The rest of it was this: at each switchback where they cut across, they were to leave markers—cairns, strips of fluorescent tape tied to the old telegraph poles, arrows of stacked stone—pointing away from the true route. East, toward the high traverse, toward the corniced shoulder where even in summer the mountain shed its skin.

"If she doubles back," Voss said, "if anyone follows our trail, they follow the markers. And the markers lead them onto the traverse." He said it the way a man discusses a change of hotel reservations. "The traverse in early winter is not survivable for someone traveling alone and tired."

Ilsa felt something turn over in her chest, slow and cold, like a stone shifting in a streambed.

"You want them to die," she said.

"I want them *stopped*." Voss's voice stayed pleasant. That was the worst of it; it never rose, never cracked. "There is a difference between wanting a thing and accepting its cost, Brandt. I'd have thought a soldier would understand that better than anyone."

"I was a mountain trooper, not a butcher."

"And now you're a guide with eleven thousand euros of debt and a lien on your mother's house in Innsbruck, which I know because you told me yourself over dinner in the valley, because you wanted me to understand why you'd take a job like this." He tucked the map into his coat. "So. Understand the job."

He walked on. Dane followed. The two porters looked anywhere but at her, and then followed too, because following was what wages bought.

Ilsa stood alone at the switchback with the pry bar's fresh scars bright on the old stone wall, and the wind came down the escarpment and moved through her jacket like it owned her, and she thought: *this is how it starts.* Not with a decision. With a delay. With telling yourself you'll object later, at a better moment, when the ground is firmer. She had watched good soldiers go bad exactly this way—one compromise, then another, each one smaller than the last, until the man looking back at you from the mirror was a stranger wearing your rank.

She picked up her axe and went up after them.

* * *

They cut the first crossing in ninety minutes, and it went as badly as she'd promised. The frozen crust held until midday sun worked the surface, and then a slab the size of a dining table let go and ran out onto the roadbed forty meters below, burying a hundred-year-old tie wall under a ton of gray rubble. One of the porters—a boy named Anselm with a wispy attempt at a beard—went down to his hip and came up white-faced and shaking, and it took Ilsa twenty minutes and a rope to get him out.

Voss watched from above with his hands in his pockets.

"No harm done," he said.

"A meter to the left and he'd have gone into the gorge."

"Then we're fortunate." He checked his watch. "Two crossings left before dark. Tape on the pole here—east, remember, east—and cairn at the bend. Then we move."

Ilsa tied the tape herself. She made herself do it, made her hands do it, because refusing outright meant being sent down the mountain and replaced, and being replaced meant Silas Sterling—she'd gotten the name from the storekeeper's loose tongue, Sterling, like the silver people polished and put away—would face Voss and Dane and whatever they became with no one on the line who might still choose differently.

That was how she framed it, going up. *I stay so I can be the one who doesn't.* It was a good frame. It almost held her weight.

At the second crossing, Dane cut the tape too short and it flapped loose in the wind, and he cursed and retied it, and Ilsa saw that his hands were not steady either. Interesting. Not-steady hands on a man who didn't think. Somewhere under all that not-thinking, the thinking was happening anyway, the way water finds rock.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a ruined signal shed, walls of mortared stone half-collapsed, roof gone to sky. Voss allowed a small fire because the cold was now a tactical problem and not merely a discomfort, and they sat around it eating rehydrated food while the temperature fell through the floor of the world and the stars came out hard and pitiless above the ridge.

Anselm spoke first, because boys always speak first.

"The tape," he said. "The false markers. If someone follows those—" He stopped himself, but the sentence was already out, lying in the firelight between them like something dropped.

"Someone won't," Voss said.

"But if they did—"

"They won't." Still pleasant. Still warm. "Eat your dinner, Anselm."

The boy ate his dinner. Ilsa watched him eat it and saw the exact moment the question went underground in him, the same place hers had gone, and she hated Voss a little for it—not for the cruelty itself but for the efficiency of it, the way he buried doubt in living men and left it there to grow or die on its own.

Later, when the others had rolled into their bags, Dane sat up beside her at the edge of the firelight. He didn't look at her. He fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch.

"You cut the second crossing wide," he said. "Wider than it needed to be."

"Did I."

"You left the footing good. A person coming up behind could cross it clean, if they knew where to look." He turned the stick. "A person who knew the line."

"Dane—"

"I don't want to know anything," he said. "That's my whole strategy. But I'm telling you what I noticed, because noticing isn't knowing, and because if this goes wrong—if someone ends up dead on this mountain because of tape and stacked rocks—I'm not going to be the one who can say he didn't see it. I'm going to be the one who saw it and said nothing." He glanced at her then, briefly. His eyes in the firelight were older than the rest of him. "You were army. What do they call that?"

"Ancillary to the crime," Ilsa said. "It carries the same sentence."

"Huh." He considered this. "Good night, Brandt."

He rolled into his bag. Ilsa stayed up and kept the fire, and the mountain breathed around them, vast and indifferent, and somewhere above them on the dark shoulder of the escarpment a woman was moving alone through the cold with her grandfather's sins in her pack, and Ilsa found herself—unprofessionally, unforgivably—hoping the woman was fast.

* * *

Morning came gray and lower. The cloud deck had dropped overnight until it scraped the ridgelines, and the air had the wet wool weight of snow deciding. They broke camp in silence and reached the third switchback by nine, and there Voss called a halt and gathered them with a gesture, and Ilsa knew before he spoke what was coming, because the whole climb had been building toward it the way a wave builds toward a beach.

"She doubled back in the night," Voss said. He held up a scrap of paper, a corner torn from a ration wrapper, and on it in pencil, small and neat: coordinates, a bearing, a timestamp. "One of the porters I placed in the valley town before we came up. She passed the way station at the fourth mile and turned east." He smiled at all of them, generous, sharing. "Onto the traverse. Our markers worked beautifully."

"Or she read the terrain and chose her own route," Ilsa said, "because she's an engineer and engineers don't follow tape."

"Either way, she's on the traverse, and the traverse is ours to control." He rolled the paper away. "Here's the order of march. Dane takes the lead. Brandt, you're with me. Anselm, you carry the spare rope and keep up. We intercept her at the narrows below the cornice field by tomorrow noon, and we take the map, and we walk her down the mountain alive, because I am not a monster, whatever anyone's face suggests." He looked at Ilsa when he said it. Only at Ilsa. "But if she resists, or if the weather closes and she becomes a liability to the operation—"

"Define liability," Ilsa said.

"I define it however the mountain defines it." He shouldered his pack. "Move."

They moved. Up through the broken country above the last switchback, onto the shoulder where the trees gave out and the world became rock and wind and the long white rampart of the escarpment running north. Ilsa climbed with her head down and her mind working, turning the problem over the way she'd once turned over route problems in the army, looking for the line through it.

Intercept at the narrows. Take the map. Walk her down alive. Every word of it plausible, every word of it deniable, and underneath it all the tape fluttering on the telegraph poles pointing east toward the cornices, and Voss's pleasant voice saying *the traverse in early winter is not survivable.*

Unless someone warned her off it first.

Unless someone who knew the high country—who knew that the eastern bench above the second gorge offered a thread of a route, ugly but real, the kind of line only a desperate person or a mountaineer would find—got word to her before the narrows.

Ilsa turned the thought over and over, examining it from every side like a suspect coin, listening for the ring of truth in it. She owed Voss money. Her mother's house was papered against it. Eleven thousand euros, and a debt in this life was a rope around the waist, and you did not cut ropes on mountains.

But she had also spent nine years in uniform learning the difference between an order and an excuse, and she knew exactly which one Voss was handing her.

By noon the first flakes came down, fat and slow, melting on her sleeves. By one o'clock they weren't melting anymore. By two the shoulder had gone soft-edged and silent, the world shrinking to the radius of the rope team, and Voss called a halt under an overhang and studied the white curtain ahead with narrowed eyes.

"She'll shelter," he said. "Two days, maybe three, then she moves again, weaker. We hold here and let the storm do our work." He settled back against the stone, patient as winter itself. "Nothing to do but wait."

Ilsa sat with her back to the rock and watched the snow come down over the traverse, erasing the world one flake at a time, and somewhere out in all that white a woman was holing up alone, and the window for a warning was closing with the light.

She waited until the others slept. Then she checked her watch, checked the bearing, and began, very quietly, to plan.
