## 11. The Way Station

The storm that had been threatening all day finally committed itself an hour past noon, and Silas Sterling was still a mile short of the way station when the first flakes came down fat and slow, like the mountain deciding to be generous before it decided otherwise.

She had been counting trestle numbers in her head to keep her pace honest—Eleven, Twelve, the long fill at Thirteen where her grandfather's maps showed a culvert that surely no longer existed—and she was somewhere in the middle of Fourteen when the wind turned and began pushing her uphill instead of letting her climb. That was the mountain's way of saying *turn around*, but turning around meant descending into the gorge country with darkness coming on, and she had seen what the gorge did to roadbed. She kept climbing.

The way station appeared out of the weather the way such places always did: not gradually, but all at once, as if the fog had been holding it in its mouth and finally let go. A low stone building hunched against the cut bank, roofed in cedar shakes gone silver-black with sixty years of rain, its eaves furred with lichen. The old telegraph line still ran past it on leaning poles, most of them snapped at waist height by some winter decades ago, the wire itself lying in the grass like a snake that had given up.

Silas stood in the falling snow and looked at it for a long moment before going in.

She knew this station. Not from memory—from paper. It appeared on three of her grandfather's sheets, always marked the same way: *W.S. 7 — water & telegraph — 2 men winter post.* Two men had wintered here once, keeping the line open through months when the summit passes closed and everything above five thousand feet belonged to the snowplows and the avalanche crews. She had traced the little rectangle of its footprint so many times with her finger that seeing the actual building felt less like arrival than like confirmation—the map made flesh, or rather the flesh made map, weathered and sagging and real.

The door was gone. Only the frame remained, and half of that, the wood rotted back to a soft gray comb. She stepped through sideways over the sill, which stood a hand's height proud of the floor because the floor itself had sunk, and then she was inside, and the wind dropped away all at once, and the silence was enormous.

* * *

It took her an hour to make the place defensible, and she worked without thinking about it, the way her hands knew things her mind hadn't authorized. She swept the main room clear of debris with a broken section of plank. She found the old stove—a squat cast-iron thing, its door hanging open like a jaw—and checked the flue with a lit match held up into the throat of the pipe until the smoke drew clean. There was split cedar stacked against the north wall under the last intact corner of the eave, seasoned beyond seasoning, dry as bone, and she carried it in armload by armload while the snow thickened outside the empty doorway until the world beyond was nothing but gray motion.

By the time she had a fire going, the light through the doorway had gone the color of pewter, and the snow was coming down in earnest—not the fat indecisive flakes of early afternoon but small hard grains that hissed against the stones. Early snow. Weeks early. She fed the stove and told herself the pass would hold another week, and did not quite believe it.

The main room was perhaps twenty feet by thirty, once divided by a partition that had mostly fallen. A bunk frame rusted against one wall. A shelf held three glass insulators, blue as glacier ice, still seated on their wooden pegs as if the linemen had only stepped out and might come back. On the wall beside where the door had been, someone had nailed a board, and on the board, in faded paint, someone had written:

**W.S. 7 — CHECK IN ALL PASSING TRAINS — NO EXCEPTIONS**

Below it, smaller, in different handwriting: *No exceptions means you too, Aldous.*

Silas read that twice. Then she sat down slowly on her pack, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

Aldous Voss. She had known the name since she was six years old, when she'd found it in the back of her grandfather's survey ledger and asked who Aldous was, and her grandfather had gone very quiet and said, *a man I worked with*, and had not said anything else for the rest of the evening. Forty years of silence around a single name. And here it was, scrawled in a dead man's teasing hand on a board in a way station her grandfather had drawn a hundred times and never once mentioned visiting.

She got up and examined the board more closely. The paint was old, older than the second inscription. But the second inscription—the joke about Aldous—was carved, not painted, cut into the wood with a knife point in letters small and careful. Someone had sat where she was sitting and thought about Aldous Voss enough to carve him a joke.

There were other carvings. Once she started looking, she couldn't stop.

* * *

They covered the partition wall, the one that still stood, and the window frames, and the inside of what remained of the doorframe, and a stretch of the stone near the stove where generations of waiting men had evidently found themselves with knives and time. Initials, mostly. Dates. The whole taxonomy of boredom and endurance that workmen had been scratching into wood and stone since there were workmen.

*R.T. + M.H., 1898.* A heart around two sets of initials, which struck her as either very hopeful or very foolish for a winter telegraph post. *J.F. was here, God help him.* A crude drawing of a marmot. A tally of days scratched in rows—she counted them, forty-one rows, and tried not to think about what kind of winter required counting to forty-one.

And then, low on the partition wall, at a height that suggested someone kneeling or sitting on the floor, a cluster of carvings that stopped her breath.

Five names. Five sets of initials, each with a date beneath: **September 1911**.

*A.V.* — Aldous Voss.
*H.M.* — Henrik Madsen.
*T.O.C.* — Thomas Osgood Crane.
*W.P.R.* — Walter P. Reyes.
*E.B.* — Elias Brandt.

She knew every name. She had known them since childhood, the way children know the names of saints or storms—recited, unexamined, part of the furniture of family legend. The Lost Survey Party of 1911. Five men who went up the escarpment in September to run the final alignment for the Summit Extension and never came down. The story in town was that they'd been caught by an early storm on the high traverse, the same traverse she'd crossed yesterday, and that the mountain had simply taken them, the way mountains do, without malice or meaning.

Her grandfather had been on that survey. Her grandfather had come down.

He never spoke of it. He drew the route a dozen times afterward, corrected and re-corrected, and the final version—the one that became the built line, the one that ran trains for thirty years—differed from his field notes in ways Silas had spent her whole career telling herself were drafting errors, revisions, engineering judgment. She had told herself that story so long and so carefully that it had worn smooth as river stone.

But she had found the strongbox in the cave below the escarpment ten days ago, and the letter in the tin, and the letter named *S.S.*—Samuel Sterling—in a hand that shook with anger or cold or both. *The line has been moved. Ask S.S. why the grade he swore was four percent now reads six. Ask him why we are surveying a deadfall chute in September.*

Four of these five men had died in that chute, or near it. Their initials were carved into this wall three weeks, maybe less, before they died. They had knelt here—or sat here, tired, laughing probably, taking turns with the knife—and put their names into the wood the way people do when they believe the future is a place they will arrive in.

Silas crouched down in front of the carvings with the firelight moving on the wall, and she touched each set of initials in turn, lightly, the way you'd touch the faces in an old photograph.

"I'm sorry," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the empty room, too loud and too small at once. "I'm sorry. I don't even know what I'm apologizing for yet. But I'm sorry."

The fire popped. Outside, the snow hissed against the stones.

* * *

It was while she was circling the room with her lamp, unable to settle, that she found the whistle.

It was in the corner behind the fallen section of partition, wedged between the wall and a collapsed shelf, in the kind of gap that collects things the way driftwood fences collect the sea's leavings. She saw a flash of metal and thought at first it was a cartridge casing, and reached in expecting brass.

What came out was a tin whistle. A child's tin whistle, six inches long, its surface gone dull and spotted with oxidation, one of the finger holes crimped shut as if it had been stepped on. A cheap thing. The kind of toy sold from a general store bin for a nickel, the kind of thing a boy would carry everywhere and lose within a week.

Except this boy hadn't lost it. He had left it here, behind a partition, in a way station seven thousand feet up a closed mountain line, in September 1911.

Silas sat back on her heels with the whistle in her palm and felt the cold of it working into her skin.

There had been a sixth member of the party. This was the part of the legend nobody told straight, the detail that survived only in fragments, in the newspaper accounts she'd read in the historical society's files with white cotton gloves on her hands. The cook's boy. Eleven years old. Son of one of the men—Walter Reyes, the records said, though other accounts disagreed—and brought along on the summer survey because his mother had died in the spring and there was nowhere else for him. When the storm came down off the summit, the boy had been sent down ahead with the mules. He had walked out alone through three days of storm, following the telegraph wire, and he had lived.

He had lived, and then he had vanished from the record entirely. No follow-up stories. No interviews with the grown man. The newspapers of 1911 had wanted the dead, and they had gotten them, and the living boy had been folded away into the same silence that swallowed everything else about that September.

Silas turned the whistle over. The mouthpiece was dented. She raised it to her lips without deciding to, and blew, gently.

Nothing. The crimped hole killed it. What came out was a thin breathy ghost of a note, less than a sound, more like the memory of a sound—the whistle's intention rather than its voice.

But in the silent room, with the storm pressing at the walls, it was enough. She lowered the whistle and sat very still.

He had been here. The boy had stood in this room, in this exact room, with these four walls and that stove and that board with Aldous's name on it, and he had been eleven years old and his father was up the mountain in the weather, and he had played with his whistle behind the partition where the men wouldn't tell him to stop, and then the men had sent him down into the storm alone, and he had followed the wire down through three days of white, an eleven-year-old boy holding a telegraph line like a rope thrown from a drowning ship, walking toward a valley full of people who would tell him his father was dead.

And then he had grown up. Somewhere. With some name. He had become a man who maybe never climbed again, or climbed every day of his life; who maybe kept the whistle's twin in a drawer, or nothing at all; who maybe remembered this room, these carvings, the smell of cedar smoke and cold stone—and maybe had spent seventy years not knowing that anyone else in the world remembered it too.

Silas looked at the wall of initials. *W.P.R.*, Walter P. Reyes. And she thought of Tomas Reyes down in the valley, the fisherman, the man who had seen her at the cave and sold the sight of her to a stranger for money she didn't want to imagine the amount of. Reyes. She had thought nothing of the name when Ruthie first mentioned him—half the valley seemed to be Reyeses, Kettles, Osgoods, the old families tangled together like roots. But Walter P. Reyes had a son who would be in his seventies now, if he lived. If he'd stayed. If any thread of him ran down through the valley's generations into the present.

Or maybe the boy had left. Maybe he'd been taken by an aunt to some city and renamed by circumstance, scattered into the great anonymity of the twentieth century, and the whistle in her hand was the last trace of him anywhere on earth.

Either way, he was not a legend. He was not a paragraph in a historical society file. He was a boy who had blown a whistle behind a partition wall while his father carved his initials twenty feet away, and both of those facts had happened in this room, and one of those people had walked down the mountain alive and carried the whole of it, unwitnessed, into a life Silas could not see.

History, she realized, standing there with the dying note still in her ears, was not the strongbox. It was not the maps, not the ledgers, not even the letter naming her grandfather. History was this: a tin whistle behind a partition, and a man somewhere—old now, old as her grandfather would have been—who had been that boy, and who had never once been asked.

* * *

She did not sleep well, and she did not expect to.

She banked the fire and laid her bedroll near it, and set the whistle on the flat top of the stove where the firelight could reach it, propped upright against the kettle she'd carried up from the valley. It looked absurd there, domestic and small, a toy among tools. She left it anyway.

In the night the storm deepened. She woke twice to the sound of the wind reorganizing itself against the building, finding new angles, testing the roof shakes the way a burglar tests windows. Once she woke to silence so complete she lay listening for her own heartbeat to confirm the world hadn't ended. The snow outside the empty doorway had drifted halfway up the frame. By morning, if it kept on, the door would be gone entirely, and she would dig her way out into a world remade.

Somewhere in the smallest hours, half-dreaming, she heard a whistle. Thin and far away, down the line, the way sound carries in cold air. A child's note, high and wavering. She knew—she knew with the whole flat certainty of the exhausted—that it was the wind in the telegraph wire, singing through some break in the strand, and she knew it just as certainly as she also knew that it wasn't, that it was the boy, that he was down the mountain in the dark following the wire home, and that if she ran to the doorway she would see nothing, because seventy years stood between her and him, and no one could walk through that.

She stayed in her bedroll. She listened until the note faded. "I'll find out your name," she said to the dark. "Whoever you grew up to be. I'll find it, and I'll say it out loud, where everyone can hear."

The dark didn't answer. The dark never did. But the fire settled, and the wind eased by a degree, and she slept after that, fitfully, with her hand curled near the stove where the whistle stood.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the storm spent, the light through the doorway diffuse and sourceless, the way light is after heavy snow. Silas dug herself out into eighteen inches of fresh powder, pristine and blue-shadowed, the whole mountainside rewritten overnight. The telegraph poles stood capped in white. The line ran down the valley and up toward the summit, a single stitched seam across all that blankness.

She ate oatmeal with the last of her sugar, packed, and stood a moment in the doorway looking back at the room—at the stove, the board with Aldous's name, the wall of initials with their September dates.

Then she saw it.

Fresh tracks. Not hers. They came up the line from the east, from the direction of the washout and the gorge, pressed deep by heavy boots and a loaded pack, and they circled the way station once at a distance, wary, reading it the way she would have read it—before they came on to the doorway and stopped.

The snow in the doorway was undisturbed. Whoever it was had stood there in the night, while she slept ten feet away, and had looked in at her fire, and had chosen not to enter.

Beside the tracks, planted upright in the snowdrift like a flag, was a single red surveyor's stake—the kind her own crews used, the kind that meant *occupied*, the kind that meant *someone is here*. And tied to it, fluttering in the still air, a strip of torn cloth.

She crossed the drift and pulled the cloth free. It was a piece of orange flagging tape, the waterproof kind, and written on it in grease pencil, in a woman's square unhurried hand, were two words:

**GO EAST.**

Silas stood in the trampled snow with the tape in her glove, looking east along the line, where the tracks of whoever had come in the night went on up the mountain toward the switchbacks—and, fainter, beside them, the tracks of others. Many others. Fresh. Headed the same way.

Someone had climbed through the storm to warn her. And behind that someone, closing fast along the very line she'd meant to take, came everyone else.
