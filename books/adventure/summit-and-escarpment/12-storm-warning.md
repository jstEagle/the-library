## 12. Storm Warning

Ruthie Kettle had lived in Meridian Falls long enough to know that weather came from the west and trouble came from the east, and that the trouble always arrived by train, or what passed for one now—the twice-weekly bus that rattled up the valley road and deposited its passengers outside the Kettle House with the same wheezing sigh of a thing that had climbed too far and wanted very much to lie down.

She was wiping down the counter when the bus sighed, and she looked up through the front window at the man who stepped down from it, and she thought: *there he is. There's the trouble.*

Edmund Voss. She didn't know his name yet, but she knew the type the way a barkeeper knows a drunk before the first glass is poured. He wore a city coat too fine for the valley, gray wool that would be ruined by the first honest rain, and he carried a leather case the way some men carry a rifle—close to the body, angled, as if the whole world were something to be aimed at. He was fifty, maybe, silver at the temples, handsome in the way of a photograph of a handsome man. Everything about him was composed. That was the thing that put the cold in Ruthie's spine. Men who were composed in a place like this had composed themselves somewhere else and brought it with them, and composition like that was a kind of armor, and armor meant a man expected to be shot at.

"Mrs. Kettle?" He set the case on the counter. "Edmund Voss. I've taken the room upstairs. I telephoned ahead."

"I know what you telephoned," Ruthie said. "Room's made up. Supper's at six, and I don't cook past six, and I don't cook twice."

"A woman of firm hours." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the kind of smile that had been practiced on a mirror until the mirror gave up and surrendered it. "I'm a scholar, Mrs. Kettle. Historical geography. I'm researching the 1911 survey party."

Ruthie set down her rag with great care, the way she did when she wanted her hands to have something to do besides what they wanted to do. "That was a long time ago."

"All the best stories are." He opened the leather case and drew out a notebook, and she saw that the pages were already dense with his small, upright handwriting. "The party that vanished on the escarpment. Five men and a boy, I understand. The railroad hushed it up. The town hushed it up. Forty years, and nobody's written so much as a pamphlet." He looked around the Kettle House—at the mounted trout, at the photograph of the depot in its prime, at the long scar of the old rail grade visible through the window where it climbed the valley wall like a vein. "That's not forgetfulness, Mrs. Kettle. That's a decision. And decisions like that are the most interesting things in the world."

"Supper's at six," Ruthie said again.

* * *

By the third day he'd spoken to everyone, and by the fourth day everyone had spoken to everyone about him, which in a town the size of Meridian Falls amounted to the same thing as speaking to him twice. He bought coffee at the Kettle House counter and paid with bills too crisp and left tips too large. He asked after the cave. He asked after the tide. He asked, with the delicacy of a man lifting a stone to see what was underneath, after Silas Sterling.

Ruthie watched it happen the way you watch weather come over the pass—helpless to stop it, reduced to reading the signs and battening down what you could. She watched Tomas Reyes come into the Kettle House on the fourth evening with money in his pocket and a look on his face like a man who'd swallowed a fishhook and couldn't decide which way to turn.

Tomas had fished the valley water for thirty years. He was a liar in the small ways all fishermen are liars, about the size of trout and the depth of holes, and honest in every way that counted. Ruthie poured him a coffee he hadn't asked for and sat down across from him, which she almost never did.

"Tomas."

"Ruthie."

"You talked to the coat."

He turned his cup a quarter turn. "He pays for stories. I had one. That's all it was. I saw her down at the tide flats, that's all I saw, and I told him what I saw, which was a woman walking on the flats at low tide, which is not a secret, Ruthie, half the town saw her walking on the flats."

"Half the town saw her walking on the flats," Ruthie agreed. "What did the coat see?"

Tomas was quiet a long time. The stove ticked. Outside, the light was going the color of pewter, and there was a smell on the wind that Ruthie had been smelling for two days and didn't like—the green, metallic smell of snow that hasn't made up its mind.

"He asked me," Tomas said finally, "if I thought she'd found something down there. And I said a woman like Silas Sterling doesn't walk three miles of tide flats to look at starfish." He looked up, and his eyes were miserable. "He smiled at me, Ruthie. Like I'd done him a kindness. And he gave me twenty dollars, and I took it, and I've been sick about it since."

Ruthie reached across and put her hand over his, rough knuckles and fish smell and all. "Then un-sick yourself," she said. "You tell me everything he asked you. Everything. And then you don't talk to him again, and you tell the others not to, and if he asks you where her cabin is, you tell him you've got a bad memory for directions."

"He already knows where her cabin is."

Ruthie closed her eyes. "Of course he does."

* * *

Sheriff Amos Dill was sixty-one years old, elected unopposed eleven times, and built like the furniture in a church—sturdy, plain, and intended to last. Ruthie had known him since he was a boy who stole apples from her father's orchard and had the decency to look ashamed about it. He came into the Kettle House on the fifth morning with his hat in his hands, which was never a good sign, and Voss came in behind him like a man attending a signing ceremony.

"Mrs. Kettle," Amos said.

"Amos."

"Mr. Voss here has raised a concern."

"Has he." Ruthie kept wiping the counter. It was already clean. She wiped it anyway. There were certain kinds of conversation that went better if your hands were busy and your eyes were down, because then the other man couldn't see you deciding things.

"Mrs. Kettle," Voss began, and his voice was warm as bathwater, "I want to say first that I have nothing but respect for your community. It's a remarkable place. It's kept a remarkable secret."

"Meridian Falls has never kept a secret in its life," Ruthie said. "It's kept a silence. There's a difference. A secret you're sitting on. A silence you're standing next to, respectful, like at a grave."

"A grave," Voss said softly. "Yes. That's rather my point." He set the leather case on the counter and did not open it, and Ruthie understood that this was deliberate, that the case was a prop, that everything about this man was staged down to the angle of his hat. "Five men died on that escarpment in 1911. Possibly six, counting the boy. The railroad falsified its own records to avoid liability—I have correspondence, Mrs. Kettle, letters, affidavits. The families were paid off in secret. The route itself was falsified. And now it appears that evidence of all of this has surfaced in a sea cave below the escarpment, and that one resident of this town removed that evidence and has not reported it."

"That's an accusation," Ruthie said.

"It's a question," Voss said. "I'm a scholar. I ask questions. But the sheriff tells me that the old rail line above the valley is closed. Posted. And that Miss Sterling has gone up it."

Ruthie's rag stopped moving.

"When," she said.

"Yesterday morning," Amos said heavily. "Storekeeper says she bought provisions. Told him she was inspecting culverts." He said it flatly, without belief, and Ruthie could have kissed him for the flatness, because it meant Amos didn't believe it either and was saying so in front of Voss on purpose. "Ruthie, Mr. Voss wants me to declare her a trespasser. Wants a warrant, wants deputies, wants me to go up after her."

"And what do you want, Amos?"

Amos put his hat on the counter and rubbed the back of his neck. "I want it not to snow," he said. "I want it not to be my job. I want a lot of things." He looked at her, and there was something in his face she'd seen maybe three times in forty years—Amos Dill asking for help without the words for it. "There's no crime in walking an old grade, Ruthie. Not that anybody's ever prosecuted. It's not fenced. It's not signed. Half the boys in this town have been up to the way station to smoke cigarettes their mothers would've killed them for. If I declare her a trespasser because a stranger with a leather case asks me to, I'm doing that stranger's work, and I don't know yet what that work is."

"Scholarship," Voss said mildly. "My work is scholarship."

"Then do it at a library," Ruthie said.

The look Voss gave her then lasted perhaps a second and a half, and it was the first honest thing she'd seen him do. Underneath the warmth, underneath the composed surface, there was something with teeth. It looked at her the way winter looks at a late garden. Then the warmth came back down over it like a lid.

"Mrs. Kettle," he said, "I understand loyalty. I admire it, truly. But if that young woman is up on the escarpment when the snow comes—and the snow is coming, you can smell it as well as I can—then loyalty won't keep her alive, and neither will the sheriff's patience. I have guides. Experienced men. Mountain men. I intend to go up after her regardless of what the sheriff decides, because I believe there is a document up there worth more than this entire valley, and because I believe she is in danger. You can help me or you can watch. Those are the choices."

Ruthie set down the rag. She looked at him for a long moment, this composed, handsome, armored man, and she thought about Silas Sterling at eight years old sitting at this very counter with her grandfather's survey maps spread out among the coffee cups, tracing the closed lines with one small finger while her grandfather watched her with an expression Ruthie had never been able to read and had spent forty years trying to. She thought about the promise she'd made in the store two days ago—*come back alive, and come back honest*—and how a promise like that was a debt with two payments, and how she intended to collect on both.

"Mr. Voss," she said, "you want coffee?"

He blinked. "I—yes. Thank you."

She poured it slow, and she let it be terrible, because the coffee was always terrible when she wanted it to be, and while she poured she listened to the wind coming off the pass, and it had changed in the night. It had gone around to the northwest. It had gone cold and clean and hungry.

The snow was coming. Maybe two days. Maybe three. Maybe less.

* * *

There was a telegraph line once, in the days when the railroad lived. It ran up the valley on its own poles beside the grade, and when the railroad died, the line died with it—except that it didn't, quite. Amos Dill's father had been the last operator at the pass, and when the company pulled out he'd bought the instrument and a spool of wire and kept a single working circuit patched together out of stubbornness and spare parts, from the pass station down to a receiver in the sheriff's office, four miles of wire strung through trees that had grown around it like a vine around a fence. It was a toy, officially. A curiosity. Amos kept it oiled anyway, the way he kept his father's razor oiled, for reasons he'd never explained and Ruthie had never asked.

She went to the sheriff's office at dusk, when the streetlamps were just coming on and the mountains to the west had gone the color of a bruise. Amos was alone. She closed the door behind her.

"Amos. I need to send something up-line."

He looked at her across the desk for a long moment. "The pass station's been dark since 1938."

"Then it won't take much light to surprise whoever's up there." She set her hand flat on the desk. "Amos, that man Voss has hired guides. Tomas saw two of them at the hotel—big men, ropes and iron, city money. He's going up the line after her, warrant or no warrant, and he's got a day's start on any rescue you'd mount, and when the snow comes, every soul on that escarpment is going to be in God's hands, and I don't trust God and I trust Voss less."

Amos was quiet. Through the window, the first flakes were coming down—not the real snow yet, just the scouts, the few flakes a storm sends ahead to look at the ground.

"There's a man at the pass station," Amos said at last, slowly, as if admitting to something. "Has been for a month. Fire lookout, forest service, fellow named Aldous. Young. Bored. I sent him up a week ago to watch the line, on account of the cave business making folks restless." He rubbed his jaw. "You're telling me to telegraph him. To tell him a woman's coming up the grade, alone, ahead of a storm, with men behind her."

"I'm telling you to tell him to watch for her," Ruthie said. "To watch for all of them. And if she passes below the station—if she signals, if she takes shelter at the way station—then he's to help her. Quietly. And he's to keep his eyes on the men following her, and if they cut switchbacks or pull markers or do anything that would bring a rescue party to grief, he's to write down what he sees and send it down, and you're to keep it. Paper, Amos. Dated. Signed. Because men like that—" she nodded toward the window, toward the hotel where the lights of Voss's room burned—"men like that count on the mountain to do their work and leave no witnesses. We are going to be witnesses."

Amos looked at the telegraph key sitting on its shelf under a cloth, his father's key, brass gone brown with age, and Ruthie watched something move through his face—grief and duty and a kind of relief, the relief of a man who has been waiting forty years for the machine to matter again and is almost ashamed of how much he wants it.

"She'll be mad," he said. "Silas. If she finds out we've been watching her."

"Silas Sterling has been invisible for forty years," Ruthie said. "It's the only way she knows how to live. But invisible isn't the same as safe, and it never was, and it's time somebody told her so." She pulled the cloth off the key. "Send it, Amos."

He sat. He flexed his fingers the way a man does who learned a skill young and never lost it, only buried it. And then the key began to speak—that dry, insect click, click-click, click—the old language going up the wire through the darkening trees, four miles of copper singing into the teeth of the coming storm:

*STORM MOVING IN STOP TRAVELER ON LINE STOP ONE WOMAN AHEAD STOP THREE MEN BEHIND STOP WATCH THE MARKERS STOP*

* * *

Ruthie walked home through the first real snow. It came down soft and thick, filling the ruts in the street, hushing the town, laying its white hand over everything—the Kettle House, the sheriff's office, the dark grade climbing the valley wall where a woman she loved like a difficult daughter was somewhere above the clouds with a liar's map in her pack and the truth in her bones.

At her door she stopped and turned and looked up, though the snow had already taken the mountain. Somewhere up there was the way station. Somewhere up there, higher, was the summit depot, and whatever her grandfather had locked inside it. Somewhere up there, too, was Edmund Voss, and his guides, and his leather case, and whatever it was he really wanted, because Ruthie Kettle had poured a thousand cups of coffee for a thousand kinds of men and she knew in her water that scholarship was not what that man had come for.

"Come back alive," she said to the snow, to the dark, to the mountain that did not listen and did not care. "And come back honest. Both of you. I don't care which order."

Behind her, in the sheriff's office, the receiver ticked once in the dark—Aldous at the pass, acknowledging, the thin wire holding against the wind—and Ruthie Kettle went inside to wait, which was its own kind of fighting, and she had always been good at it.
