## 13. Whiteout

The snow came the way her grandfather had always said it would—not falling so much as arriving, a decision made by the sky all at once. One moment Silas was reading the shoulder of the escarpment for the old cut marks, the surveyor's bench scars still faintly legible in the rock after forty years, and the next the world had been taken from her like a sheet pulled off a table.

She had seen it building, of course. All morning the clouds had hung low and wrong-colored, a bruised pewter that meant moisture and cold stacked in the same column of air, waiting only for a trigger. She had counted on another six hours. The mountain gave her four minutes.

Wind hit first—a flat shove against her left side that staggered her into the cut bank—and then the flakes, not the gentle kind but grains, hard as salt, moving horizontally with a hiss she felt through her hood rather than heard. Visibility collapsed to an arm's length. Then to less.

*Whiteout,* she thought, and the word carried her grandfather's voice with it, unbidden: *When you can't see the mountain, stop being on the mountain.* He'd said it once, crouched beside her at fourteen on the slopes above Kestrel Pass, drawing in the snow with a stick—the diagram of a snow cave, the angle of the windward wall, where to dig, where never to dig. She had memorized it the way she memorized everything he taught her, faithfully and without understanding that one day she would hate how well she remembered.

Silas turned her back to the wind and began, methodically, to look for the place she would spend what might be the rest of her life.

* * *

The shoulder of the escarpment was no place to be caught; she had known that when she chose this line, and chosen it anyway because the alternative—the main grade through the switchbacks—was the route Voss's hired men would be watching. So she had traded safety for stealth, and now the bill was coming due.

She found what she needed two hundred yards on, where the roadbed curved inside a rib of granite and an old culvert mouth opened beneath it, half-collapsed, breathing out air that was warmer than the storm. Not ideal. A culvert could fill with drift, could funnel wind into a scream, could bury her without her noticing until it was too late. But it was stone, and stone did not care about weather, and above it the mass of the roadbed would hold the drifting snow off—at least until the weight grew too great, which was a calculation she declined to perform just then.

She dug. That was the whole of the next hour: digging, packing, digging again, carving a hollow behind the culvert's throat with the shovel blade and her gloved hands, banking the walls, wedging her pack and the tarp from her kit across the entrance to break the wind. Snow is the best insulator in the world and the most patient killer; the trick, as her grandfather's stick-drawing had shown, was to make it work for you before it worked against you. She made the chamber small—small spaces hold heat—and smoothed the floor and sat down in the dark with her back against cold stone and listened to the storm try to take the mountain apart.

Her breath frosted the tarp edge. Through the gap she watched the light go from gray to nothing at all.

She ate three strips of dried venison and let herself want a fourth, and did not eat it. In her mind she laid out the arithmetic the way she laid out a grade profile: seven miles to the summit depot over the high traverse, two days lost here if the storm held, food for five days if she was strict, six if she was foolish. Fuel enough to melt snow twice a day. Matches: eleven. She wrote none of it down. There was no need. Numbers were the one language that had never lied to her, unlike maps, unlike men, unlike grandfathers.

The wind rose through the night into something that sounded less like weather than like argument—a vast, continuous quarrel between the ridge and the sky, with the escarpment losing. Once something heavy came down the slope above her, a slide or a falling boulder loosed by freeze-thaw, and the whole culvert shuddered and sifted fine snow onto her shoulders, and she lay awake afterward listening to the mountain's bones settle, thinking: *I am inside you now. Do what you like.*

* * *

On the second day the storm still owned everything, and the hallucination began sometime past noon, though noon was only a guess; the light behind the tarp never changed, and her watch had stopped at some point she couldn't name, its hands frozen at 4:17 like a monument to a moment that hadn't mattered.

He started with the sound of the stick on snow. *Tap. Tap-tap.* The way he'd tapped out points when he was teaching, marking each fact as if driving it into the ground like a stake.

"You're not real," Silas said. Her voice came out rusted. She hadn't spoken aloud in two days except to count matches.

"Never said I was," her grandfather said, from somewhere just outside the tarp gap, in the white roar. "You did. You've been carrying me up this mountain since the tide went out."

"I've been carrying your maps."

"Same thing." A pause, and the wind filled it. "You always were a careful girl. Careful girls don't get caught in whiteouts on the wrong side of an escarpment."

"Careful girls stay home." She pulled her knees up. The cold had gotten into the chamber despite everything; it lived in the stone and radiated outward like a slow confession. "Careful girls don't find strongboxes. Careful girls don't read letters addressed to dead men."

"Tell me about the letter."

"No."

"Silas."

"*No.*" It came out louder than she meant, cracking in the small space, and for a moment she was nine years old again in his drafting room, having knocked over the ink, watching his face do the thing it did—not anger exactly, but a closing, a door shutting partway. He had never once raised his voice at her. That had been the worst of him, somehow. Everything important had happened below the surface, like rail bed under ballast, unseen and load-bearing.

But there was no one here to perform restraint for. The storm didn't care about dignity. And so, alone in a hole in a mountain with a man who had been dead eleven years, Silas Sterling finally said the thing.

"You knew."

The wind screamed. The tap-tap of the stick had stopped.

"You knew what you'd done, and you spent fifty years—" Her voice caught. She pushed through it. "Fifty years teaching me to read contour lines. Fifty years of *measure twice*, of *the mountain doesn't forgive*, of *a surveyor's word is the only true thing he owns*. You stood in that drafting room and told me a falsified line was the one unforgivable sin, and you knew. You *knew* you'd done it. Eleven men, Grandfather. Eleven men walked your moved route into a chute that you knew would run full in spring melt, and you sat at home and drew pretty maps of closed lines and paid money to their widows like settling a debt for a broken window."

"They were my friends," the voice said, and it sounded smaller now, older, the way he had actually sounded at the end, thin as tracing paper. "Aldous was my friend. I carried his compass for thirty years after."

"That's not grief. That's inventory."

The silence that followed had texture. She pressed her mittened hand against her mouth and felt her own breath hot and ragged, and understood distantly that she was crying, that she had perhaps been crying since the cave, since the tin, since the water-blurred ink that spelled out *S.S. has moved the line* in a dead man's handwriting—and that the crying changed nothing, fixed nothing, melted no snow. Grief given weather, she thought. This was what it was. Not the tidy mourning she'd performed at his funeral, standing straight-backed while the town praised him. This was the raw version, the field version, the version with the errors left in.

"Why?" she asked, quieter. "Why move it? You weren't a coward. You climbed things I'll never climb."

A long time passed. Long enough that she thought the hallucination had abandoned her, that her mind had spent its last coin. Then:

"The investors wanted the summit by October. The northern alignment added eleven weeks. They had a man in the party—Harrower—who reported to them. If I refused, they'd have replaced me with someone who wouldn't refuse, someone who'd have moved it worse. I told myself that." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "I was good at telling myself things. It's the family talent. You're doing it right now—you told yourself you could beat Voss to the depot alone, in October, on a closed line."

"Someone has to reach those notes before he does."

"And then what? Burn them?"

"Yes."

"And the families? The ones he's already found? The ones who've waited forty years for someone to say *this is what happened, and here is whose fault it was*?" The voice was gentle now, which was unbearable. "You came up this mountain to protect our name. Ask yourself whose name, Silas. Yours or mine. Because mine is already written. It was written in 1911 in a chute above Miller's Bend. All you can choose is whether it gets read."

"Stop it." She was shaking, and not only from cold. "Stop talking like Ruthie. Stop talking like—"

"Like someone who loves you?"

"Like someone who *knows* me." She wiped her face with the back of her glove, rough wool against raw skin. "You never knew me. You trained me. There's a difference."

Another silence. When the voice came back it was very quiet, nearly lost under the wind, and it said only: "Then why is it my voice you brought up here, and not your own?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the whole question, the one she'd been circling since the tide went out and the sea gave up its dead. She had spent her life becoming her grandfather's instrument—reading his grades, walking his lines, keeping his secrets even the ones she hadn't known she was keeping. She had mistaken inheritance for identity. She had mistaken solitude for safety, and silence for strength, and now she sat in a snow hole arguing with a ghost about whether to burn the truth or carry it, and the ghost was winning, because the ghost was right.

"I'm tired," she said finally. It was the truest sentence she'd spoken in years.

"Eat," the voice said. "Sleep. Morning comes whether you forgive me or not."

"It isn't morning. It's a whiteout."

"It's always a whiteout," he said, "right up until it isn't," and the tap-tap of the stick faded into the wind, and did not come back.

* * *

She slept in fragments, waking each time to check the tarp seal and the depth of drift against the entrance and the small blue pilot flame of her stove. Somewhere in the deepest part of the night—or what she chose to call night—the wind dropped. Not ceased; dropped, the way a fever breaks, all at once, leaving a ringing quiet so complete she could hear the snow itself settling, grain by grain, the mountain exhaling.

Near dawn the light behind the tarp turned the color of pearl. She dug out.

The world had been remade. The escarpment's shoulder was a single smooth rampart of wind-carved snow, corniced along the ridgeline in long curling waves like frozen surf, beautiful and treacherous in equal measure. The sky above was hard, clean blue, rinsed. Cold of a different order had arrived with the clearing—the deep, ringing cold that follows a front—and every breath crackled in her chest. Far below and east, the valley lay white and featureless, the town invisible, smoke invisible, everything human erased under a foot and a half of new snow.

Two days gone. Five days of food, minus what the storm had cost her in fuel and effort. Seven miles of high traverse ahead, much of it now buried under fresh, unstable load, the cornices fat and hungry along every leeward edge.

She checked her gear with numb fingers, counted the matches again—nine now, two spent melting drinking water—and stood a long moment looking north along the line toward the summit, invisible beyond the shoulder's curve but present in her body the way a route always was, a pull more than a direction.

Then she saw them. Or rather, she saw what they'd left: far down-slope, at the margin where the new snow met the dark rock of the lower benches, a line of dots. Too regular for animals. Too spaced for a fallen fence. Tracks—days old, half-filled with drift, but unmistakable to a woman who had spent her life reading ground. A party of three, maybe four, moving up the main grade. Moving toward the switchbacks.

They'd sheltered through the storm too, then. Which meant they were rested, provisioned, and led by someone who could afford to wait her out.

And beside the track-line, planted upright in the snow where any watcher with a glass would find it, a single wands-stick with a strip of red cloth snapping in the wind. A marker. Not a rescue marker. A message, left deliberately on the route she would be expected to take.

*We know where you're going. Come talk.*

Silas looked at it for a long time, her breath streaming, the cold working into her seams. Then she turned her back on the main line, shouldered her pack, and started east, onto the high traverse, where the cornices waited and no honest person had any business being.

Behind her, faint on the wind, she thought she heard the tap of a stick on snow—but it was only ice shedding from the rocks, only the mountain, saying what it always said.

*Measure twice. Move once. And tell the truth about what you measured.*
