## 14. Ambush at the Switchback

The storm came down out of the north two days early, and Ilsa Brandt felt it in the old fracture in her left femur before she saw a single cloud. By dawn the light had gone the color of dishwater, and the wind had begun to speak in the register she had learned, years ago, on a glacier in the Karakoram, meant one thing only: *get off the exposed ground or die on it.*

Voss stood at the mouth of the ruined tunnel with his field glasses raised, his coat beaded with the first grains of snow, and he looked, Ilsa thought, like a man attending a christening. He had that expression. He'd had it since the pass, since the telegraph line, since whatever word had come down to him from the valley. He believed they were close now. He believed the woman was close.

"She'll take the main line," he said. "She has to. The traverse east of the switchback is a death trap this time of year, and she's an engineer—engineers don't gamble with ground they haven't measured." He lowered the glasses and turned, and his eyes had the bright, wet shine of a man who has stopped seeing other people as people. "We'll take her at the switchback. Gently. I want her unafrighted, Brandt. Frightened people do stupid things with documents."

*Unafrighted.* He said it the way he said everything, in that soft, cultured voice that made cruelty sound like scholarship. Ilsa had been three weeks in his employ and had learned to hear the thing underneath the voice the way you learn to hear water moving under ice.

"And if she won't come gently?"

"Then Halloran persuades her." He nodded toward the other guide, who was crouched by the cook fire sharpening a knife he had no practical use for at this altitude. Halloran was a big man gone soft at the edges, with small eyes and a way of smiling that never involved anything above the mouth. "Nothing that leaves a mark the doctors would ask about. Just—convincement. She's one woman alone on a mountain. This is not a complicated problem."

Ilsa said nothing. She checked the straps on her pack. She looked east, where the escarpment's shoulder rose into the cloud deck, and she thought about debts—fourteen thousand of them, in euros, held by a bank in Innsbruck that did not care about her conscience or her knees—and she thought about the marker Halloran had driven into the snow two days ago at Voss's instruction, a false cairn set to steer any rescue party away from their actual route, and how she had watched him do it and said nothing, and how the saying-nothing had a weight now, a mass, like a stone carried in the chest.

Three weeks of saying nothing. The stone was getting heavy.

* * *

They reached the switchback by midmorning, moving fast on the old grade, the snow squeaking dry under their boots. The line here was a marvel, Ilsa had to admit—her grandfather had run trains through country like this, she knew what the grade meant, what the curve radius meant—and it was a marvel built by dead men, some of whom had died because of the man whose map they were chasing. Voss talked about it constantly now, the 1911 party, as if they were colleagues of his. *My grandfather's men. My grandfather's route.* He said *my* the way a dog says *mine* over a bone.

The switchback itself was a hairpin of stone and rotting timber where the grade reversed against the cliff face. Above it the main line climbed toward a shelf of exposed rock—a kilometer of it, maybe more—completely open to the north wind. Below it, the line dropped into a gully choked with slide debris. And east, cutting away from both, the high traverse: a ledge the width of a coffin, blasted out of the escarpment's face in 1909 and abandoned in 1913, running two hundred meters above the valley floor, corniced, unmapped in any living survey.

Voss positioned them like pieces on a board. Halloran below, in the gully, to catch her if she ran down. Ilsa above, on the shelf, to turn her back. Voss himself at the apex of the switchback, in the open, hands visible, voice ready. *Unafrighted.*

The snow thickened. The wind found the shelf and began working at it, lifting spindrift off the cornice above in long white banners that streamed east like smoke. Ilsa crouched behind a granite block and waited, and in the waiting she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for three weeks.

Fourteen thousand euros. That was what her silence had cost, priced out. Her flat in Innsbruck, her mother's care home outside Brixen, the physiotherapy for the knee. Fourteen thousand. And against it: a woman alone on a mountain, hunted for a piece of paper. And against that: Halloran's false cairn, and the word Voss had used at the way station two nights ago, quietly, over the stove, when he thought she was asleep—*loose ends*—and the particular way he had looked at the boy's tin whistle when Silas Sterling's camp had been described to him. Not the map. The whistle. As though the map were only ever half of what he wanted, and the other half was making sure no one else ever told the story.

The wind rose. Somewhere above the cloud deck, the mountain was deciding things.

* * *

She heard Silas Sterling before she saw her—the small sounds, the disciplined sounds, of someone moving carefully through bad ground. A boot on stone. The click of an ice axe. Then the woman herself, coming up the main line through the thickening snow, small and wiry, gray-streaked hair plastered under a wool cap, moving with the economy of a person who has been alone with her own judgment for a long time and trusts it.

Ilsa stood up out of the shelter of the rock.

The effect was instant. Silas stopped as if she'd hit glass. Her hand went to the axe at her belt—not drawing it, just finding it, the way you find a doorframe in a dark house—and her eyes did a fast, professional circuit: Ilsa, the slope, the cornice, the switchback below, the gully. Reading the ground. Reading the trap. Ilsa watched her understand it, all of it, in perhaps four seconds, and watched something settle in her face—not fear exactly. Recalculation.

"Silas Sterling," Ilsa said. "I'm not going to touch you."

"No," Silas said. Her voice was rough from cold and wind and disuse. "He's below."

"Halloran's in the gully. Voss is at the switchback. You're boxed."

"I can see that." The woman's breath came in white rags. Her eyes were the color of the rock she stood on, and they did not leave Ilsa's face. "Then why are you talking to me instead of grabbing me?"

Because, Ilsa thought. Because of the cairn. Because of *loose ends*. Because fourteen thousand euros had a price and she had just now, standing in the snow with her heart going like a trip-hammer, finally seen the invoice all the way to the bottom.

"Because I've been paid to be one kind of person," she said, "and I'm finding out I'm not that kind."

* * *

Below them, faint through the wind, a whistle—two notes, Voss's signal. *Do you have her?* Ilsa didn't answer it. She stepped closer, boots scraping on the iced bedrock, and lowered her voice to the register you used in avalanche country, the one that carried three meters and no farther.

"Listen to me. The main line is what he expects, but it doesn't matter, because the main line is dead anyway—look at the shelf, look at the spindrift coming off that cornice. By tonight there'll be thirty centimeters on it and the wind loading will double. You cross that shelf, you die. You go down into the gully, Halloran takes the map off you and we find out what Voss does with witnesses. There's one way out of this box."

Silas's eyes went east. To the traverse.

"That's not a way out," she said. "That's a way to fall."

"It's a way to fall *free*." Ilsa pulled the map tube from inside her jacket—the one she'd copied from Voss's research, the old survey sheets, the 1909 blast records—and held it out. "The traverse was engineered. Badly, but engineered. There's a deadman anchor every forty meters, iron rings set into the face. The first three are gone—I checked, from below, with glasses. From the fourth on, they're sound. Rusted, but sound. You rope up. You move in storm conditions, which means you move slow, which means you don't reach the depot tonight, which means you spend a night belayed to a rail over a two-hundred-meter drop." She heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it was. "I've done worse. So have you, from the look of you."

"Why?" Silas said. Just that. Just the word, naked, in the wind.

Ilsa looked east at the traverse, at the white banners streaming off the cornice, and told the truth because it was the only thing left in her pack that wasn't counterfeit.

"Because he told a man to drive a false cairn to mislead rescuers, and the man did it, and I watched. Because last night he said the words *loose ends* about a woman he's never met, and he said them the way you say a line item. Because my grandfather ran trains in country like this and I know what it costs to build a line, and I know that whatever happened in 1911, it wasn't a treasure. It was people. And people are what he's actually hunting—not the map. The last person who can contradict the story he wants to tell." She pushed the map tube into Silas's hands. "And because I've been someone's loose end before, and someone looked away, and I promised myself—"

Below, the whistle again. Three notes now. *Report.*

Silas had taken the tube. Her fingers were blue at the tips. She looked at Ilsa for a long moment, wind screaming off the cornice above them, and something moved behind the rock-colored eyes—assessment, yes, but under it, something rawer.

"The fourth ring," she said. "Forty meters in."

"Count your paces. Don't trust the drift—probe every step. And Sterling—" Ilsa glanced down the line, toward the switchback, where Voss was surely already climbing. "When you write whatever it is you're going to write about all this. Put my name out of it."

"I don't even know your name."

"Good," Ilsa said. "Keep it that way. Go."

* * *

Silas went. Not down the main line, not into the gully—east, over the lip of rock at the shelf's end, onto the traverse, small against the escarpment's face, swallowed by the snow in under a minute. Ilsa stood and watched the place where she'd vanished until the wind filled it in, and felt the stone in her chest change weight. Lighter. No. Not lighter. *Placed down.* There was a difference.

Then Halloran came up out of the gully.

She heard him before she saw him—too heavy a tread for the ground, no discipline in it—and turned to find him filling the switchback below her, snow to his knees, the small-eyed smile already in place. Voss was behind him, climbing, and Voss's face had gone very still, which was worse than any anger.

"Where is she," Voss said.

"Gone," Ilsa said.

"Gone *where*." Not a question. He had seen the direction of her gaze, or he had read the whole of it in her stance, in the empty place at her jacket where the map tube had been. Voss stopped three meters away, and the snow fell between them, and for a moment the only sound was the wind working the cornice like a saw.

"You gave her the route."

"I gave her a chance. There's a difference, and it's the difference between us."

Voss sighed—the sigh of a professor with a disappointing student—and nodded, almost gently, to Halloran. "Brandt. I want you to understand that this is not personal. This is arithmetic. I have invested a very great deal in reaching that depot, and you have just moved the cost considerably higher. Halloran is going to express that to you in a language you'll understand. And then we are going east, onto that ridiculous ledge, after her. And you—" the bright wet eyes fixed on hers, and there was nothing scholarly in them at all now, nothing polite, "—you are going to walk in front, and break trail, and if she reaches that depot ahead of us because you're slow, then when we come down off this mountain, the bank in Innsbruck will learn a great many interesting things about the discrepancies in your last two expedition ledgers. Do we understand each other?"

The first blow from Halloran took her in the kidney and put her on one knee. The second caught her across the mouth, and the snow went red, and the old fracture sang its old song. She did not fight back. She knelt in the falling snow with blood in her mouth and made herself think: *the fourth ring, forty meters in, probe every step.* She made herself think it the way you hold a rope.

"Understood," she said, when they let her stand.

Voss turned east. "Then we go. And Brandt—pray she's as good as you seem to think. Because if she dies on that ledge, everything you just spent was spent for nothing."

They moved out onto the traverse into the teeth of the storm, Voss first, Halloran behind, Ilsa last, breaking trail with blood freezing in her collar and her heart, for the first time in three weeks, entirely quiet. Ahead of them, somewhere in the white roaring dark, a woman was counting her paces toward the fourth iron ring.

And above them all, unheard under the wind, the cornice began, very softly, to crack.
