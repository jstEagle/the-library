## 15. The High Traverse

The east traverse began where the main line ended—at a wall of shattered rock where the 1911 crews had given up on tunnels and simply clung to the mountain's shoulder like ivy.

Silas stood at the threshold for a long time before stepping onto it. Below her the escarpment fell away in gray terraces, each one stitched with the bones of the old roadbed: rotting ties, a rusted rail here and there surfacing from scree like a rib through skin. Wind came off the summit fields above in long, flat gusts that smelled of stone and coming snow. She had crossed this country once as a girl, riding her grandfather's shoulders while he pointed out grade and curve and called the mountain *cooperative* when it suited him. It was not cooperative now. It was indifferent, which was worse, and she had learned in the last three days that indifference was the only honest thing up here.

She checked her anchors out of habit rather than hope. Rope: forty meters, good. Pitons: six, two of them questionable. The deadman kit—folded steel plates and wire cable salvaged from a culvert cache below the washout—heavier than she liked but worth its weight in prayer. Food: half of what she'd started with, the rest feeding the gorge. Water: enough, if she ate snow and paid the body's tax in warmth.

She stepped onto the traverse.

The first hour was arithmetic. Every step had three questions attached: Will it hold? If it doesn't, what catches me? And what does falling cost? She moved the way her grandfather had taught her to read a map—never trusting the line itself, only the evidence around it. A slab of granite tilted outward meant frost-heave; she skirted it. A drift of fine scree fanning from a seam meant water moving underneath; she gave it room. Where the roadbed narrowed to nothing she climbed above it, hands finding holds worn smooth by sixty years of freeze and thaw, and lowered herself back down onto the far side like a woman wading a river.

Twice she stopped at cornices—the wind-carved overhangs of packed snow that curled off the ridge above the traverse like the lips of something sleeping. From beneath they looked solid, beautiful even, blue-veined and lit from within by the sun. She knew better. A cornice was a promise the mountain made and broke on its own schedule. She timed her crossings for the lulls between gusts, moved fast and light, and did not look up at the mass of it hanging over her, because looking up was how men froze mid-step.

By late afternoon she reached the first of the old anchor points—a ring of iron bolts driven into the living rock by the survey crews, spaced along the worst section like stitches in a wound. She ran her fingers over them. Someone had drilled these by hand, hanging off this same shoulder, in weather no better than hers, for wages she could guess and reasons she was only beginning to understand.

"They were brave," she said aloud, and heard how thin the words sounded against the wind. "And he sent them anyway."

That was the thought she'd been circling for two days, the way she circled cornices: not running from it anymore, but walking its perimeter, measuring. Her grandfather had altered the route. The letter in the tin said so plainly enough. But the letter said *what*, not *why*, and Silas had spent a lifetime believing that engineering was a moral profession—that a line drawn on paper was a promise made to every soul who would ever ride it. If he had broken that promise, she needed to know whether he'd broken it carelessly or under weight. Whether he was a fraud or a man crushed between investors and gravity. Whether the settlements she'd found traces of in his ledgers—the anonymous payments, year after year, to families whose names never appeared anywhere else—were guilt or penance or both.

She couldn't know from down below. She couldn't know from the cave. The answer was in the field notes, locked in the depot cache, and the depot was one traverse and one storm away.

So she kept moving.

* * *

Night caught her at the worst pitch—a section where the roadbed had sheared away entirely, leaving a hundred meters of bare cliff face threaded only by the old handline: a chain of iron eyes bolted into the rock, through which the 1911 crews had once run rope. Most of the rope was gone. What remained was black and brittle as burnt hair.

She considered going around. There was no around. Above, the cliff rose sheer into darkness; below, the terraces dropped four hundred feet into a basin of boulders that even in daylight looked like teeth.

"Cooperative," she muttered, and laughed—a short, ugly sound—and got to work.

The last workable rail lay twenty meters back along the roadbed, half-buried, its base still spiked to a tie that had somehow refused to rot. She dug it free, tested it with her whole weight, then hung from it with her whole weight plus a violent bounce. It groaned but held. She rigged her rope through the rail base with a tensionless hitch, paid out line, and traversed sideways across the blank face using the iron eyes for hands, the rope singing softly through her belay device every time her boots skated on verglas.

It took an hour to cross a hundred meters. At the far end she set a second anchor—a piton driven into a crack behind a flake of granite, tested until her arms shook—and then faced the problem she'd been avoiding all evening: she could not go back, and she could not go forward in the dark, and there was no ledge wide enough to sleep on.

So she slept hanging.

She built her seat from the rope itself, a loop passed around the piton and knotted under her thighs, her back against the rock, her boots braced against a nub of stone. She clipped herself twice. Then she ate a handful of cold oats and a strip of dried venison, pulled her hood down, and watched the stars come out over the escarpment in their terrible, indifferent thousands.

Sleep came in fragments. Each time she sagged, the rope woke her with a gentle, intimate tug, like a hand on the shoulder, and she surfaced into the wind and the dark and the memory of her grandfather's kitchen, the smell of coal oil and pencil shavings, his big cracked hands turning a map toward the lamp.

*"Grade is a promise, Silas,"* he'd told her, age nine, tracing the contour lines with one blunt finger. *"You promise the mountain you'll climb it gently, and you promise the people you'll bring them home. Break either promise and the other one breaks too."*

"You knew," she said to the dark. Her voice came out flat, stripped of anger, which frightened her more than anger would have. "You knew exactly what you'd done. You spent fifty years paying for it in secret and teaching me to love the thing you'd used to kill them."

The wind answered. Somewhere above, a cornice calved with a sound like a door closing, and the powder-snow drifted down past her face, soft as ash.

But lying there, strung on a rusted piton over four hundred feet of nothing, she felt something shift—not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but a kind of settling. Like ballast finding its place in a hull. She had spent nine days running: from the cave, from Voss, from the town, from the letter. Running had kept her alive. But it had also kept the truth small, a private shame to be buried or burned. And buried things didn't stay buried—they slid, like roadbeds, like snowfields. They took out whatever was below them.

The truth would have to come down whole, or not at all. All of it: the falsified route, the investors who'd demanded it, the settlements bought in silence, the boy with the tin whistle who had crawled out of that cave while grown men died around him. Not destroyed. Published. Named. Her grandfather's name among the names, written in her own hand, because a lie protected by silence was just a lie with longer legs.

"I'm not erasing you," she told the dark, and meant her grandfather, and meant the dead, and maybe meant herself. "I'm carrying you. All of it. That's the only way anything survives up here."

She slept then, really slept, an hour at a stretch, waking to check her knots and finding them true.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter. She descended from her anchor stiff and slow, hands clawed open one finger at a time, and stood on the first solid ground in twelve hours feeling the strange gratitude of the merely alive.

The traverse continued. Through the morning she worked its full vocabulary: cornices crossed in the lulls, scree slopes descended in long deliberate slides, two more deadman anchors buried in a snowfield she did not trust, each plate tamped into the slope and backed up with a piton because she had stopped believing in single answers. Once she found a cairn—old, moss-furred, built by human hands—and beside it, wedged in the stones, a rusted lantern with its glass fogged white from the inside. She left it where it lay. Some markers weren't hers to move.

At noon she gained the notch below the summit shoulder and allowed herself the luxury of looking back.

The traverse unspooled behind her like a sentence she'd survived reading. Far below and eastward, small as punctuation, she could see movement on the lower line: figures. Two of them, maybe three, working along the roadbed she'd abandoned, moving fast and low, roped together. Voss's guides. They'd found the high route after all—or been driven onto it—but the shear and the night climb had cost them, and they were a good half-day behind.

Half a day. In clear weather, that was nothing. And the weather was not going to stay clear. The clouds building over the western summits had the flat, bruised undersides she'd learned to read before she'd learned to read maps, and the wind had begun carrying that particular mineral smell that meant snow was being manufactured somewhere above her.

She should push hard. Every instinct said push hard, beat them to the depot, lock the door, burn the notes—

No. That was the old plan, the running plan, and she had killed it on the cliff in the night. The new plan required her to be there when Voss arrived, with everything open, everything read, everything true. Which meant she needed the storm less than she feared it and more than she wanted it: enough snow to slow him, not enough to bury her.

She studied the sky again, professionally, the way she'd study any variable in a design. Six hours of grace, perhaps eight. Then weather.

"Then we go," she said—to the mountain, to the dead, to the boy with the whistle, to whatever part of her grandfather still walked these grades—and shouldered her pack, and turned up toward the summit shoulder, where the old depot waited with its stone vault and its locked drawer full of the truth entire.

Behind her, far below, a figure on the lower line raised one arm—not waving, she thought. Signaling. Calling the others forward.

Silas climbed.
