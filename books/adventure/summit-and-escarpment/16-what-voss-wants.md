## 16. What Voss Wants

The storm had been building all day behind the escarpment like a debt coming due, and Edmund Voss felt it in his teeth the way he felt every change in pressure, every shift of wind, every small treachery of altitude. He stood at the mouth of the ruined way station with his hands clasped behind his back and watched the clouds come over the summit ridge in long gray banners, and he thought: *she is up there somewhere, and she is running out of mountain.*

Behind him, inside the station, Ilsa Brandt was building a fire from the splintered remains of a bench that had probably been old when his grandfather sat on it. The other guide—Kessler, a slab of a man with a beard like frozen tar—was checking the rope and pretending not to listen. They both pretended not to listen. It was one of Ilsa's virtues that she pretended less well than Kessler did.

"Two days behind her," Kessler said. "Maybe three if this snow closes the traverse."

"She won't take the traverse." Voss turned back into the station's dim interior. "Not unless someone forces her to."

Ilsa looked up from the fire. Her face was weathered into something older than her years, and there was a cut on her cheekbone from two nights ago that she had not explained and he had not asked about. "And if we force her," she said, "and she dies on it?"

"Then she dies on it." He said it mildly, the way one might discuss the weather, because mildness was a tool he had sharpened over forty years of use. "Engineers die on mountains all the time. It's practically a tradition."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer the mountain gives, Frau Brandt. Everything else is sentiment." He lowered himself onto a crate near the fire, careful of his knees, which had begun their own quiet mutiny against him somewhere around the fifth decade. "But you misunderstand me if you think I want her dead. Dead women can't testify. Dead women can't hand over what they're carrying. I want her *arriving*—exhausted, cornered, and reasonable."

"And if she isn't reasonable?"

Voss smiled. He knew what the smile did; he had spent a lifetime watching it open doors that should have stayed shut. "Everyone is reasonable at the top of a mountain in a snowstorm. It's the great equalizer. Better than money."

He let the silence sit while the fire caught and began to throw heat against the cold leaking through the boarded windows. The way station smelled of creosote and mouse droppings and old iron—the smell, he thought, of everything his family had lost. He had been smelling it for six days now, since they'd come up off the valley floor and onto the closed line, and each day the smell had grown more intimate, more accusatory.

Ilsa fed the fire another board. "You've never told us why," she said. Not looking at him. Looking at the flames. "The money I understand. There are men who'd pay a fortune for a map that old, drawn by a hand that famous. But you don't handle it like merchandise. You handle it like—" She searched for the word. "Like evidence."

Kessler snorted softly by the door. Voss ignored him.

"You want to know why I climb," Voss said. "At my age. In this weather. Chasing a woman half my weight across rock that wants us all dead."

"I want to know what happens when we catch her."

"Ah." He nodded slowly, as though she'd asked something profound. Perhaps she had. "Then I'll tell you. But not because you've earned it. Because you'll need it, in the next two days, when things get ugly—and they will get ugly, Frau Brandt, I promise you that—and a man who knows *why* he's doing something ugly can live with himself afterward. That's the whole secret of my profession. Motive is absolution, if you hold it correctly."

The wind rose outside, a long moan through the broken eaves. Somewhere above them, snow was beginning to fall on the high shoulder of the escarpment, and he imagined Silas Sterling hearing it too—a lean, solitary woman with her grandfather's stubbornness written all over her face. He had seen the resemblance the moment she opened her door. It had been like looking at a photograph that had learned to breathe.

"1911," Voss said. "You know the story as the valley tells it. A survey party went up to find a route over the escarpment for the Coast & Northern extension. Nine men and a boy. The route they chose crossed a slope that failed the following spring—killed three tracklayers during construction, buried a camp, ended the extension forever. The party itself vanished on the descent. Storm, they said. Exposure. The mountain took them." He paused. "And one man came home alive. One man walked down out of that white hell with frostbitten feet and a story, and the story was accepted, and the man was my grandfather. Heinrich Voss. Party leader. The finest route surveyor on the coast, they said, before."

Ilsa had stopped moving entirely. Even Kessler had drifted in from the door.

"The valley needed someone to blame for the tracklayer deaths," Voss continued, "and the company needed someone to blame for the lost extension, and my grandfather needed someone to blame for the eight men he'd led up there and failed to bring home. So they found one. A junior surveyor on the party. Silas Sterling's grandfather. Samuel Sterling. The story they settled on—the story that's been told in that town for forty years—is that young Sterling miscalculated the grade on the descent route. Led them onto a slope no one could survive. That Heinrich Voss fought him over it, was overruled, and barely lived to tell the tale."

"And it wasn't true," Ilsa said quietly.

"It was a lie so complete it became geography." Voss held his hands toward the fire. They were steady. His hands were always steady; it was one of the few inheritances he valued. "My grandfather came down that mountain a hero and spent the rest of his life dying of it. Drink first. Then the silences. Then the night in '29 when he wrote eleven pages in a hand like a spider drowning and burned nine of them and put a shotgun in his mouth with the other two on the table beside him. My father found him. My father was fourteen." He said it without inflection, the way you recite coordinates. "My father spent his life trying to clear the name and died trying—heart, at fifty-one, in a records office in the county seat, surrounded by boxes of company paper that had been water-damaged and misfiled past anyone's patience. And I inherited the boxes. All of them. Forty years of my life going through other men's paperwork, looking for the truth about 1911, and do you know what I found?"

"What?" Ilsa asked.

"Nothing. That's the joke. Nothing. The company covered its tracks better than the mountain covered its dead. Every ledger that mattered was destroyed in the '23 reorganization. Every witness was dead or scattered or bought. For forty years the only truth available was the valley's version, and the valley's version has Samuel Sterling's name on it like a brand." He looked up, and let her see—not the charm, not the mildness, but the thing underneath, the thing he kept banked like a coal. "Until a fisherman with loose morals and a boat saw a woman climbing out of a sea cave with a strongbox under her arm."

Ilsa was silent a moment. "You think the box holds proof. Proof that Sterling altered the route."

"I think it holds a letter. The fisherman said papers. Water-swollen, mostly gone—but papers, in a tin, in a cave below the escarpment, hidden where only the tide could reach it. Someone in that party survived long enough to hide evidence, Frau Brandt. Evidence against whoever moved the line." He let the words land. "And I think the original field map survived too. Maps were drawn on linen in those days. Linen doesn't rot. If the map exists—if I can hold the map that shows *whose hand* moved that route onto the killing slope—then forty years of lies end in a single afternoon. My grandfather's name comes clean. My family stops being the family that produced a coward and a suicide. And the Sterlings become what they have always actually been."

"Which is?"

"Liars," Voss said. "Respected, beloved, monument-having liars. There's a plaque in that town square, did you know? *In memory of S. S. Sterling, who gave his life to the mountain.* They named a creek after him. They tell children his story at the school. And every word of it is built on the corpse of my family's good name." He heard his own voice rise and brought it back down, gently, deliberately, the way you ease a sled back onto its runners. "I don't want her money. I don't want the map to sell. Let me be plain, since we're being plain: I will burn the map before I sell it. What I want is the truth published. Corrected. In the newspapers, in the historical society, carved into that obscene little plaque if it comes to it. I want the record set right while I'm still alive to read it."

* * *

Kessler went out to check the anchors before full dark, and the station shrank to the fire and the two of them, and Voss found that the telling had loosened something in him, as telling always did, and that he resented it.

Ilsa sat across the flames with her forearms on her knees. She had the particular stillness of people who had learned to wait out worse things than conversation. "There's something you're not saying," she said.

"There's a great deal I'm not saying. I'm a dealer in antiquities; omission is my native language."

"About her." Ilsa nodded toward the dark window, toward the mountain. "Sterling. You talk about her like a target. You don't talk about her like a target. I've watched you do targets."

Voss considered lying to her. He was good at it, and she was tired, and it would simplify the next two days considerably. But there was a quality in Ilsa Brandt's attention that made lying feel like a waste of good material—she would find the seam eventually, and then he'd have spent the lie for nothing.

"Her grandfather and mine were friends once," he said. "Did you know that? Before 1911. Young men together on the Coast & Northern surveys. There's a photograph—my father kept it—in front of a tunnel portal, arms around each other's shoulders, laughing at some joke forty years dead. Heinrich Voss and Samuel Sterling. Two brilliant boys who believed the mountain could be argued with." He turned his hands over, studying the palms as though the answer might be written there. "And then something happened up there in the autumn of 1911, and one of them came down and let the other one be blamed for it. Whether it was cowardice or calculation or simple self-preservation, I don't know. That's what the box will tell me. That's what the map will tell me. Which of them lied."

"And if it's your grandfather?" Ilsa asked. Flat. Direct. The question of a woman who checked her own knots twice.

The fire popped. Outside, the wind had found its full voice, a rising harmonic through the escarpment's throat.

"Then I'll publish that too," Voss said.

She didn't blink. "You expect me to believe that."

"No," he admitted. "I expect you to believe that I believe it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole tragedy of human affairs." He leaned forward. "But hear me, Frau Brandt, because this is the part you need. Whatever the truth turns out to be—his guilt or hers or both—I am done letting other people curate it. Forty years of curated grief. My father curated his into an early grave. I will not curate mine. I will have the documents, all of them, and the world will have the documents, and whatever they say, they will say it *finally*, in full, with names attached. That is the only vindication worth having. Anything less is just another lie with better lighting."

Isla studied him for a long time. The firelight moved on her face and made her look, briefly, like someone deciding something.

"And Sterling?" she said. "When you catch up to her. When you take what she's carrying. What happens to her?"

"She walks down the mountain," Voss said, "and watches her family's legend come apart in the newspapers, and lives with it, the way my father lived with it, the way I've lived with it. That's not cruelty, Frau Brandt. That's symmetry. Her family has had forty years of the good version. Mine has had forty years of the true one. I'm simply proposing an exchange."

"Symmetry," Ilsa repeated. She rose, stiffly, and stood a moment working feeling back into her hands. "My grandfather was a soldier too," she said, at the fire, not turning around. "East Prussia. He spent thirty years telling everyone the war had honored him. It hadn't. It had eaten him. And the family myth was the last thing standing between him and the truth, and we all protected it, and none of us ever loved him honestly because of it." Now she turned, and her eyes in the firelight were very level. "So I know what a family wound looks like when it starts giving orders. Be careful, Herr Voss. Wounds like that don't want vindication. They want the other family to hurt. Those aren't the same thing, and the second one never stops."

She rolled into her bag with her back to him, and the conversation was over, and Voss sat alone by the fire for a long time, listening to the storm assemble itself over the summit.

*Wounds like that don't want vindication,* he thought. *They want the other family to hurt.*

He did not sleep. Instead he took out the photograph—the tunnel portal, the two laughing boys—and held it near the coals until the light was right, and looked at his grandfather's face, young and unbroken and certain, and tried to remember that he loved him. It was harder up here than it was in the city. Up here the mountain kept insisting on its own account of things, and the mountain's account had no names in it at all.

Toward dawn the snow began in earnest—fat, silent, businesslike flakes falling past the broken eaves—and Voss stood at the doorway and looked up the black line of the grade toward the summit depot, invisible now behind the white curtain, and thought of Silas Sterling climbing toward it with her grandfather's secrets in her pack.

Three hours' hard climb ahead of them. Two, if the storm slowed her and not them.

"Wake up," he said to the darkness behind him. "We're going up."
