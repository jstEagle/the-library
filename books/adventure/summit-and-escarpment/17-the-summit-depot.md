## 17. The Summit Depot

The last mile was the worst of it.

The high traverse had spent her by degrees—her hands cracked and stiff inside ruined gloves, her shoulders carrying a pack that felt like it had been filled with wet stone somewhere in the night—but the final approach to the summit depot climbed through a bowl of scree where every step slid backward half as far as it advanced. The wind came off the escarpment in long, flat gusts that pressed her sideways against the slope, and she leaned into them the way she'd learned to lean into grade: not fighting the mountain's will, only negotiating with it.

She had stopped talking to her grandfather two days ago, in the whiteout, when she'd finally said everything there was to say to a dead man. But his voice had stayed with her anyway, quieter now, less an accusation than a habit—the way he used to murmur figures under his breath when he checked a curve, *rise over run, rise over run*, as if arithmetic were a prayer. She caught herself murmuring it too, on the traverse, when she set her deadman anchors in the crusted snow. She wondered if that was what inheritance really was. Not land, not name. Just a man's habits living on in your hands after his mouth had stopped.

Then the bowl opened, and there it was.

The summit depot sat on its shelf of leveled ground like something the mountain had tried to swallow and failed. It was smaller than she remembered from the photographs—a low stone vault with a timber superstructure above it, the wood gone silver-gray, the roof sagging in the middle like a tired back. The old water tower stood behind it on three legs, one leg buckled, the tank listing but holding. And beyond both, running along the shelf and then simply stopping at the lip of the escarpment, lay the line itself: rails brown with forty years of rust, ties rotted to lace, disappearing into cloud.

This was the end of the closed line. This was where her grandfather's survey had ended too—in more ways than one.

Silas stood at the edge of the bowl for a full minute before moving, because some part of her understood that once she crossed that distance, nothing would be the same again. The cave below the tide line had given her the accusation. The way station had given her the boy's tin whistle, which rode now in her breast pocket like a small cold heart. But this place held the answer, whatever the answer was, and answers were heavier than questions. Questions you could carry. Answers carried you.

She crossed the bowl.

* * *

The depot door was swollen shut with decades of weather, and she had to put her shoulder to it three times before it gave with a groan of splitting paint. Inside, the air was dry and mineral-cold, smelling of stone and creosote and the ghost of lamp oil. Dust lay over everything in a fine gray velvet, undisturbed except by the small archaeology of mice.

The main room had been an office once. A desk stood against the far wall beneath a window fogged nearly opaque with grime; a filing cabinet listed against the wall like a drunk leaning on a friend; a wall map still hung from two nails, curled at the corners, and Silas made herself look at it.

It was the route. The 1911 route, drawn in ink on linen paper, the survey party's own working copy—and even from across the room she could see the alteration, because she knew what to look for now. The line came up the valley, swung east around the shoulder of Kestrel Point, and then cut directly across the face of the escarpment on a grade no engineer should have signed. She measured it with her eyes out of pure professional reflex and felt her stomach drop all over again. Four point seven percent. Maybe five. On exposed roadbed, above a gorge, in country that avalanched before Christmas most years.

No wonder they hadn't come down. No wonder the washout had taken them. The line hadn't failed them. The line had been built to fail them—or if not built to fail, then built carelessly enough, arrogantly enough, that failure was only a matter of weather.

And her grandfather had drawn it.

She turned away from the map. Her breath was loud in the stone room. Behind the desk, half hidden by the filing cabinet, was what she had come four days through winter country to find: a hatch in the floor, iron-ringed, and beside it a padlock whose case had rusted to a reddish fur but whose heart, she was willing to bet, was still sound. Her grandfather had trusted locks the way other men trusted God.

Her key ring held eleven keys. She had carried them from the cabin without fully admitting why—eleven keys to doors in towns she no longer lived in, boxes she no longer rented, plus one heavy brass key stamped with no number at all that had hung on a nail in her grandfather's workshop for as long as she could remember, and that no lock in her memory had ever fit.

It fit.

The padlock opened with a click so small and so final that she sat back on her heels afterward, the key still in her fingers, and laughed—one short, ugly note with no humor in it. Forty years. He had kept the key hanging in plain sight all those years, in a house full of grandchildren, and none of them had ever found its door. Perhaps he'd told himself that was innocence. Perhaps he'd told himself anything at all. The dead are convenient that way; they never have to finish their explanations.

She lifted the hatch.

Stone steps went down into the vault, and cold air rose to meet her, colder than the day, older than the day. She took the lantern from her pack, lit it with hands that were not quite steady, and went down.

* * *

The vault was small—not much bigger than her cabin's kitchen—and dry, mercifully dry, the stone walls sweating nothing. Someone had lined it well. Along one wall stood a steel document case, army surplus, its latches bright with oil. Oil. Recently oiled. Her breath caught. He had come here. Near the end, maybe—he had died the winter she was nineteen—old Silas Sterling had climbed this same bowl, on legs that must have been failing him, and oiled the latches on the box that held his guilt.

Or perhaps he had come to burn it, and couldn't. She would never know which. That was the tax the dead levied: you paid forever for their unfinished sentences.

She knelt and opened the case.

Field notebooks first—seven of them, canvas-bound, their spines soft with handling. She knew the format instantly because she owned others like them, inherited, benign: the standard surveyor's log, columns for chainage and elevation, remarks in the margin. These were dated June through October 1911. The last one stopped on October 9th. The washout had been the 11th.

She read.

The early entries were ordinary, almost cheerful. *Good going above the falls. Alder thick but passable. R. sings in the evenings, drives the men mad.* R.—Rudolf Voss, the party's leader, the man whose name had become a curse in the valley, blamed for forty years for leading nine people onto a roadbed that killed them. Silas had grown up on that curse. It had been the background radiation of her childhood: the Voss disaster, the Voss folly, poor devils who followed a fool up the mountain.

The August entries changed.

*Investors' committee visited the 14th. They do not accept the western alignment. Too costly, too slow. They speak of capital already sunk and cannot be sunk further. Mr. Hargreaves used the word "vacillating" and looked at me when he said it.*

Then, three pages later, in handwriting that pressed harder into the page:

*S.S. proposes the eastern grade. Says it can be held at 4.5 if we cut deep at the shoulder. I have told him what I told the committee: the drainage alone will kill it within five years. He says there will be no five years if the money walks. I believe him. God forgive me, I believe him.*

Silas sat very still. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself in air that had no business moving.

S.S. Not her grandfather's initials—his were E.M., Edwin Marsh Sterling, a fact she had known since childhood and never once thought strange until this moment, until the letters S.S. surfaced in another man's notebook like a body coming up through ice. S.S. was the name the drowned letter in the strongbox had named. S.S. was the man who moved the line.

But the entry didn't read like an accusation. It read like a confession wearing someone else's initials. *I have told him what I told the committee... I believe him. God forgive me, I believe him.*

She turned pages with fingers gone clumsy. September confirmed it, entry after entry, Rudolf Voss documenting in real time the thing the valley had never known: that he had opposed the eastern grade, formally, in writing, and been overruled—not by ignorance, not by vanity, but by money and by the quiet ambition of the man the investors preferred to deal with. The man whose family name the valley would carry forward instead of his.

*I have filed my objection with the company. Copy enclosed. If the line fails, let it be remembered that I said so, and when, and to whom.*

There was no copy enclosed. There was no company anymore. There was only a stone vault and a dead man's careful hand and a girl with a lantern reading the truth forty years too late to matter to anyone but the living.

Except—it did matter. Because the last notebook was not a survey log at all.

* * *

The final volume was smaller, bound in black cloth, and it was a ledger.

She recognized the format immediately, because accounting is accounting whether the columns hold dollars or degrees. Dates. Amounts. Initials of recipients rendered as codes—*K.F., Alder Creek; M.T., widow, the Falls; H.V.*, and her eye snagged on H.V. and refused to move.

H.V. Helena Voss? Hedwig? She didn't know the family's given names; nobody in the valley did; the Vossses had scattered after the disaster like seed on wind. But the amounts told their own story. Payments beginning in 1913, continuing annually, growing modestly with the years, sent through a law office in the city, marked in her grandfather's later hand with a single recurring notation:

*Settlement, per agreement. Anonymity maintained per terms.*

He had paid them. Every year, for thirty years, he had paid the families of the dead—and the survivor, the boy, the whistle in her pocket—out of his own pocket, quietly, anonymously, while the valley cursed the wrong man and honored the right one. While they put his photograph in the bank and named a scholarship after him at the school. While they told his granddaughter she had his gift for lines.

Silas set the ledger down on the stone floor and pressed both palms flat against the cold until the shaking stopped.

Because here was the weight of it, the whole unbearable arithmetic: he had known. He had known from the beginning what he had done, and he had spent his entire life paying for it—not in confession, which costs a moment, but in installments, which cost everything. Every anonymous draft was a sentence he chose over the truth. Every settlement was a year the Voss name stayed blackened and his stayed clean. He had bought silence the way investors buy railroads: on credit, against future earnings, and the future earnings had been his granddaughter's inheritance, her good name, her careful invisible life built on ground that was never solid.

And yet.

And yet he had kept the notes. Hadn't burned them. Had climbed this mountain near the end of his life and oiled the latches and left the truth intact for whoever found the door—which meant some part of him, some part, had wanted it found. Had wanted, at the end, to stop paying and start telling. Had maybe even hoped his granddaughter, with her maps of closed lines, would be the one to open it.

*You keep what you're afraid to lose,* he used to say, teaching her to read grade and curve, his hand warm on her shoulder. *And you lose what you're afraid to keep.*

She had thought it was a lesson about bridges.

* * *

She came up out of the vault into light that had changed. The afternoon was dying; the clouds had lowered until they scraped the shelf of the depot, and the wind had a new graininess to it, a smell like struck flint. Snow. Not the whiteout's scattered flurries but real snow, gathering itself somewhere west of the escarpment and heading this way with the patience of something that has never once been late.

She should descend. Every instinct she owned said so: storm building, daylight failing, a body already spent past its margin. She inventoried what remained of her food—half what she'd started with, thanks to the gorge—and calculated, the way her grandfather had taught her, rise over run, supply over distance. She could make the way station tomorrow if the weather held. If it didn't, she could shelter here. The stone vault would stand through anything. It had stood for forty years.

She was repacking the notebooks when she saw it: tucked inside the black ledger's back cover, an envelope, unsealed, addressed in her grandfather's hand.

*To whoever finds this.*

Her heart performed a slow, painful revolution. She drew out the single sheet within, unfolded it, and read.

*If you are reading this, then either I have failed to destroy these papers or I have failed to want to. Both failures are mine. Know that I altered the route of the Summit & Escarpment extension in the autumn of 1911, under pressure I will not insult you by calling excuse, and that nine people died because of it, and one lived. I have paid what money can pay. Money is not enough. I know that. I have always known that. I tell myself the payments are penance. They are cowardice with a ledger book. If my name means anything to you—if the name Sterling means anything to you—then take these papers down the mountain and let the truth do what I never had the courage to do myself.*

Below the signature, in fresher ink, added years later in a shakier hand, a single line:

*Tell them all of it. Even the payments. Especially the payments.*

Silas folded the letter along its old creases and put it inside her coat, against her chest, next to the tin whistle. Then she stood in the doorway of the depot and watched the snow come.

It arrived the way weather arrives at altitude—not gradually but all at once, a gray wall advancing up the bowl, erasing the water tower, then the treeline, then the far lip of the escarpment, until the world contracted to the width of the lamplight behind her and the width of her own breathing. The temperature fell so fast the metal of the doorframe crackled. Somewhere above, wind found the cornices and began, patiently, to work.

She thought about Voss. Edmund Voss, with his polite voice and his patient questions, who had walked into her valley asking after the 1911 party as if he were a scholar, and who was somewhere behind her on the mountain even now—she had seen his hired guides' smoke from the traverse, two days ago, a smudge against the sky that she had pretended not to understand. He wanted the map. He wanted the records. And if he reached this depot and found the vault empty, he would follow her down, and he would take the truth and bend it to whatever purpose he carried, and she suddenly understood—with the clarity that exhaustion sometimes grants—that she did not know what that purpose was. A collector collects. But Voss had asked questions no collector asks. He had asked about the families. He had asked who received the settlements, as if he already suspected settlements existed.

As if he had a reason to.

The snow thickened. The roof timbers overhead groaned once, a long wooden complaint, and went quiet.

Silas banked the lantern, sat down against the warm stone above the vault with the notebooks stacked in her lap and the letter over her heart, and made herself say it aloud, to the empty room, to the storm, to the man who wasn't there:

"I'm going to tell all of it. Even the payments."

The wind answered. And beneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, from somewhere down the darkening line—too far to be certain, too rhythmic to be weather—came the sound of boots on frozen ballast, climbing toward the depot.
