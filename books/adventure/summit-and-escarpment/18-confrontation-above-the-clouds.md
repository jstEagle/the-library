## 18. Confrontation Above the Clouds

The storm found the depot before Voss did.

Silas heard it coming for an hour—a sound like a freight train that never arrived, building in the passes to the west until the whole escarpment seemed to hold its breath. Then the snow came sideways, white on white, and the world beyond the depot's windows ceased to exist. She had the stove going, the last of her fuel committed now, no arithmetic left to save. The field notes lay open on the surveyor's table where she'd spread them, drying by degrees, her grandfather's hand marching across the pages in that confident vertical script she'd spent a childhood admiring and two weeks learning to hate.

*Line relocated 40 feet north at Mile 14.2 per instruction of H.V. Sterling, resident engineer. Original alignment rejected as "excessively conservative."*

Forty feet. The width of a parlor. She had measured it out on the floor of the depot three times with a length of cord, pacing it heel to toe, trying to make her body understand what forty feet had cost. Eleven lives. One boy who walked down off the mountain alone.

Outside, the wind found the loose sheeting on the roof and worried it like a dog with a bone. The old structure groaned—long, structural groans that she felt through her boot soles—and snow slid from the eaves in soft avalanches of its own. The summit depot had stood sixty years against weather like this. It would stand tonight too. She told herself that the way her grandfather must have told himself things: firmly, repeatedly, in the voice of a man persuading rather than believing.

She was banking the stove when she saw the light.

It came up the line from the west switchback, bobbing, wrong somehow—too steady for a lantern carried by a man climbing in a gale, too bright. Headlamp. Of course. A man like Edmund Voss would not fumble through a whiteout with a flame he had to shield. He'd have bought his way up the mountain with the best equipment money could find, and money had found him guides to carry it.

She watched the light grow. There was no point hiding; there was nowhere to hide to. The depot sat alone above the cloud deck like a lighthouse that had forgotten the sea, and every approach was open ground. So instead of hiding she did something that surprised her: she put the kettle on. Whatever else happened in the next hour, it would happen between two people who were warm, because she refused—refused—to let this mountain take her manners along with everything else.

The door banged open on a column of swirling white and a man shaped like a question mark, bent double under his pack, ice crusted in his eyebrows so thoroughly he seemed to be wearing frost as a disguise. Behind him the storm swallowed everything. He straightened slowly, blinking, and when his eyes cleared and found hers across the lamplit room, he smiled.

"Miss Sterling." His voice was hoarse from wind but the charm in it was intact, worn smooth as river stone. "You cannot imagine how glad I am to see a roof."

"I can imagine it exactly," Silas said. "Come in before you die on my doorstep. I'd have to bury you, and the ground's all rock."

* * *

Voss took the room in the way he took in everything—quickly, completely, cataloguing. Silas watched him do it. His gaze touched the stove, the cot, the spread of papers on the table, the tin cup she'd set out for him, and lingered longest on the papers, though he pretended otherwise. He unwound himself from his gear with the efficiency of a man who had slept rough before and did not romanticize it, and when he finally lowered himself into the chair opposite her, he looked almost respectable again: gray at the temples, fine-boned hands, the face of a university lecturer or a well-fed wolf.

"You came alone?" she asked.

"My guide is making camp below the shoulder." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't move from her face. "Ilsa Brandt. You may have met her work already—the cairns on the east traverse? No? A pity. She's very good."

*Ilsa.* The name went into Silas's ledger of debts, another entry in a column that had been growing since the tide went out. "And the others?"

"Dismissed." He accepted the tea she poured and wrapped both hands around the cup without drinking. "They wanted to turn back at the way station. Storm like this, sensible men want to turn back. I don't employ sensible men anymore. It simplifies things." He smiled again, and this time the smile had edges. "You've read them, then. The notes."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't insult them both by pretending it was. Outside, the wind rose to a shriek and dropped, rose and dropped, the mountain breathing. Somewhere overhead a beam shifted under its load of snow, and both of them glanced up at the same instant—an involuntary, human flinch that made them, briefly, two people in a small room instead of what they actually were.

"They're not what you think they are," Silas said.

"No?" Voss set down the cup. For the first time since the door had opened, the polish cracked, and underneath it she saw something raw and old and hungry. "Then tell me what they are, Miss Sterling. Tell me why my grandfather died in a cave at the bottom of the world while your grandfather died in a bed with a pension and a street named after him. Tell me why I've spent thirty years collecting every scrap of paper from that disaster—every deposition, every coroner's report, every lying newspaper account—and every single one of them says *act of God*. Weather. Terrain. Fate." His voice stayed level, which made it worse. "I don't believe in fate. I believe in men. And I believe one of them moved that line."

"He did." Silas said it plainly, and watched the words land on Voss like blows he'd waited decades to receive. "My grandfather moved the line. Forty feet north at Mile 14.2. The original alignment followed the shelf; the new one cut across a slope he'd been warned about. He knew. It's all here—" she turned the pages toward him, "—in his own hand. The investor pressure, the deadline, the memo from the company telling him the conservative route would cost a season. He wrote it all down afterward, every detail, and then he hid it, and then he spent the rest of his life paying for it in secret."

Voss had gone very still. Stillness, she thought, was his native language; the charm was the translation. "Paying how?"

"Settlements. Anonymous ones. Money orders drawn through a bank in Denver, sent to the families of the dead. Every year, on the anniversary. My grandmother thought he was gambling. He wasn't gambling." Silas's throat tightened around the next part, and she made herself say it anyway. "There's a ledger. Names and amounts, thirty years of them. Your family's name is in it. Every March, forty dollars, until the payments stopped in 1949."

"Why did they stop?"

"Because he died." She met his eyes. "Because he died, Mr. Voss, and the money stopped, and nobody ever told you why it had been coming at all."

* * *

For a long moment Voss did not move. The storm pressed against the windows like something enormous leaning in to listen. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost its polish entirely, and what was underneath was younger than his face—thirty years younger, a boy's voice asking a boy's question.

"We were poor," he said. "After my grandfather died, my mother received an envelope every spring. Cash. No note. She never told me where it came from. She'd go quiet for days after it came, and once—I remember this, I couldn't have been more than seven—once I asked her if it was from God, and she laughed in a way that frightened me, and said, 'No, darling. It's from a coward.'" He looked down at his hands. "I built my whole life on finding out who that coward was. I assumed it was the company. Some clerk assuaging a conscience. I never—" He stopped. Started again. "I never imagined it was the man himself. Hand-addressed. Year after year. That's not cowardice, Miss Sterling. That's penance."

"It's both," Silas said. "That's what I've been learning on this mountain. They're not different things. He was a coward and he was sorry and neither one undoes the other. Forty feet of rail doesn't care how sorry you are."

"And the map." Voss leaned forward. The hunger was back, but transfigured now, aimed at something other than acquisition. "The original route map. The one showing the true alignment. Is it here?"

"Yes."

"Then give it to me." The words came fast now, tumbling. "Not sell—give, I'm past buying. Give it to me and I'll publish it. My name carries weight in the historical societies; they'll listen. We'll correct the record together. Your grandfather's guilt, my grandfather's vindication, the eleven names restored to their proper story. Everything you'd want, everything I'd want. We sign our names side by side to the account and let the world sort it out." He reached across the table, palm open. "Miss Sterling. Silas. We are the only two people alive who know what happened in that cave and on that slope and in this room. Everyone else who could testify is dead or was never born. It has to be us."

Silas looked at his open hand for a long time.

She had climbed four days to reach this room. She had crossed a gorge on a rotted stringer and buried half her food in a washout and spent two days in a drift talking to a dead man's ghost. She had come here intending, she admitted to herself now, to burn these notes—to feed sixty years of family legend into a stove and stand guard over the ashes, because the truth would ruin the name, and the name was all she had ever been given. Somewhere on the high traverse, belayed to a rusted rail over a mile of empty air, that intention had quietly died, and she had not yet said so out loud to anyone, including herself.

So she said it now.

"I'm not giving you anything, Mr. Voss. And I'm not keeping anything either." She drew the whole stack toward her—the notes, the ledger copy, the letter from the strongbox, water-stained and brown as autumn. "This isn't yours and it isn't mine. It belongs to eleven people who can't claim it, and to one boy who walked down this mountain alone and grew old somewhere without knowing why the envelopes stopped. If we publish it, we publish all of it. Not your grandfather vindicated and mine condemned—that's just vengeance wearing a better coat than the one you wore into this valley. All of it. The falsified route *and* the settlements. The ambition *and* the penance. The whole man, not the half that suits each of us." She pushed the stack to the center of the table, equidistant between them. "We write it together or we don't write it at all. Those are my terms."

"That's—" Voss began, and stopped, because the roof spoke.

It was not a groan this time. It was a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, somewhere above the ceiling plaster, and both of them froze with their heads tipped back like startled birds. Snowload. Four days of storm stacked on sixty years of rot, and the old trusses were announcing their opinion of the arrangement. A fine rain of dust sifted down through the lamplight and settled on the field notes, on the tea, on the open palm Voss still hadn't withdrawn.

"Your terms," he said quietly, "would take months. The historical societies move at the speed of glaciers. And we may not have until spring to—" Another crack, longer this time, a splintering run that traveled the length of the ceiling and back. He stood up abruptly, chair scraping. "How thick is the vault wall?"

"Stone. Two feet. It held in '31 when the east wing burned." Silas was already moving, sweeping the notes into their oilcloth, her hands steady in the way they only became when everything else was failing. "But the vault door is in the east wing, and the east wing hasn't held anything since '31 except weather."

"Then we have perhaps an hour," Voss said, "and a decision to make in it."

He looked at her across the table, and the lamplight caught the frost still melting in his hair, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like a collector than like a man standing at the edge of something he'd chased so long he'd forgotten what falling would feel like.

"The whole truth," he said slowly, testing the weight of it. "Both halves. Side by side."

"Both halves," Silas said. "Or nothing."

Above them, the roof groaned like a living thing, and the first long fracture opened across the ceiling plaster, white as a lightning scar, running toward the dark doorway of the east wing—and beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, the mountain itself began to hum.
