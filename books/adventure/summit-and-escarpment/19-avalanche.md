## 19. Avalanche

The roof went first.

It did not fail all at once the way a thing fails in a town, with warning and negotiation—a sag, a groan, a man sent up to patch it. It failed the way the mountain does everything: completely, and without apology. One moment the depot's rafters were a dark lattice above us, snow-packed and singing under the wind; the next they were a sound like the whole world being torn open along a seam.

Silas Sterling had spent her life reading structures, and even now, even with the ceiling coming down, part of her mind registered the engineering of it—the snow load that had been accumulating since the storm began, the ridge beam rotted through at the joint where the 1911 carpenters had spliced green timber, the way the failure propagated outward from that single point like a crack running through ice. The depot had been dying for sixty years. The mountain had simply decided tonight was the night.

"Vault," she said. "Now. *Now.*"

Voss was already moving. Whatever else he was—a liar, a collector, a man who had climbed a mountain to take something from her—he was not slow. They had been standing at the table with the field notes spread between them, and Silas grabbed for the oilcloth bundle even as the first rafter came down where her head had been, a beam the size of a mast splitting the table into kindling. Splinters flew like shrapnel. The lantern went out. Then the second lantern, knocked from its hook, rolled flaming across the floorboards and lit the destruction in strobing orange: the map drawers bursting open, sixty years of survey sheets fanning into the air like a flock of startled birds, the great plate-glass window bowing inward and then not existing at all.

The vault door was iron, set into the stone foundation the railroad had built to last forever, because the railroad had believed in forever in those days. Silas hit the wheel with her shoulder and spun it. It fought her—rusted, frozen, the mechanism shrieking—and behind her the roof came down in sections, and the cold poured in like water through a broken dam.

"Go," Voss said, and put his hands on the wheel beside hers, and together they broke it loose.

The vault was a stone room perhaps three meters square, built into the hillside at the depot's back, meant for strongboxes and payrolls and the survey originals of a line that had never been finished. Silas threw the oilcloth bundle in, spun back for the lantern—still burning, still rolling, a fool thing to reach for and she reached for it anyway because the dark in that room would be the dark of the grave—and Voss caught her arm and hauled her through the doorway as the lintel above it cracked.

They got the door shut to within a hand's width. Through the gap they could see the depot dying: the roof peeling back in sheets, the snow of the storm pouring through, the walls racking sideways as the avalanche above—the whole shoulder of the slope, loosened by the wind and the weight of days—came down through the ruin like a sea coming through a seawall.

Then the gap closed. Not by their hands. By weight. The outer door, its iron frame deformed, simply fell shut, and the sound of the mountain arrived all at once, everywhere, through the stone, through the floor, through their bones.

* * *

Silas had been in an avalanche before. Once, at twenty-three, on a closed line in the northern pass, a sluff that caught a section crew and buried two men, one of whom they dug out alive and one of whom they did not. She remembered the sound. People thought it would be like thunder, and it was not; it was like the world being erased. A white silence so total that her own heartbeat was the loudest thing left, and after that her own breathing, and after that the terrible arithmetic of how long a person could go on breathing in a space that was no longer a space.

The vault held. She knew that with the part of her mind that would not stop working, the engineer's part, the part her grandfather had built in her the way he'd built bridges—load paths, shear, the stone walls keyed into bedrock, the iron door, the ceiling of dressed granite slabs that had carried the mountain's weight for sixty years and would carry it tonight. The depot had been timber and shingles and hope. The vault had been certainty. The men of 1911 had built it to protect the truth, and it was protecting the truth still, and her, and the man she had every reason to hate.

"Silas." Voss's voice in the black. Steady. Too steady. "Are you hurt?"

She took inventory. Her left shoulder where the beam's edge had clipped her—bruised deep, maybe cracked, she'd know later if there was a later. Ribs on the right, where he'd hauled her through the doorway. Cold already, a deep cold, the cold of stone that had been drinking winter for months. "No," she said, and then, because the dark made lying feel obscene, "Yes. Shoulder. Nothing that kills me tonight."

The lantern had not made it. It lay somewhere in the depot under forty tons of snow and timber. But Voss had a matchcase in his coat—the man traveled prepared, the man always traveled prepared—and the scrape and flare of it lit a bubble of light the size of a held breath. In it she saw his face, and he saw hers, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because the face of a person who has just survived a thing is a naked thing, and neither of them wanted to be seen that way, and both of them were.

The match died. He struck another. In the third match's light Silas found the oilcloth bundle where she'd thrown it, against the vault's back wall, and pulled it to her, and held it.

"You were going to destroy them," Voss said. It was not a question. He had seen the kerosene in her pack at the depot door; she'd stopped hiding it hours ago, when hiding had stopped mattering.

"Yes."

"And now?"

She looked at the bundle. Under the stone, under the snow, with the mountain's whole weight pressing down and her breath making a small fog in the match-light, the answer came to her with the simplicity of a grade on a survey map.

"Now they're the only thing in the world I'm sure of."

* * *

They had air. She made herself calculate it, because calculating was how she kept the fear in its cage. The vault was not airtight—nothing built by human hands was—and the stone would breathe, slowly, through joints and seams. But slowly. She had heard men talk, men who had been buried and dug out, and the talk was always about the same things: the cold, the thirst, the way time went strange. Two people, this volume, stone that leaked like a stone wall leaks. A day. Maybe two. The town knew where she'd gone; Ruthie knew; the telegraph remnant at the pass had carried her message if it had carried anything. Someone would come. Someone would have to dig through an avalanche to do it, which was not the same as coming.

She did not say any of this aloud. Voss was an intelligent man. He was doing the same arithmetic. She could see it behind his eyes, the same numbers arriving at the same answer, and for a long time they sat in the dark on opposite sides of the vault, the oilcloth bundle between them like a border, like a treaty line, like the only lamp.

The cold was the enemy now. Not the mountain—the mountain had finished with them, had shrugged them off its shoulder the way a man shrugs off snow, without malice and without interest. The cold was patient and it was inside with them. Silas got her sleeping roll out of her pack, such of it as had survived, and after a silence Voss did the same, and they spread them against the stone wall, and the silence after that had a different quality. It was the silence of two people who have run out of reasons to pretend.

"My grandfather built this room," Silas said.

"I know."

"He built it to keep the payrolls safe from robbers. And then he used it to keep the truth safe from everyone. Including himself, I think. Including me." She turned the bundle over in her hands. "I came up this mountain to burn them, Voss. Do you understand that? Not to read them. Not to publish them. To burn them. Because the name on my door is his name, and I thought—I thought if the papers were gone, the name would be safe. I thought the mountain would keep the secret the way it kept the depot. Quietly. Forever."

"And the families," Voss said. His voice had changed. It had been a collector's voice on the climb, a lawyer's voice at the table, precise and acquisitive. Now it was just a man's voice, in the dark, in the cold. "The families of the dead. You thought of them?"

"No," Silas said. "That's the truth of it. I thought of myself. I thought of the legend. I stood in that cave with a dead man's letter in my hand and all I could think was *not my name, not my name.*" She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Forty years of settlements, Voss. He paid them. Anonymously. Every year, to every family, from the day he got down off this mountain until the day he died. My grandmother thought we were poor because he gambled. He didn't gamble. He paid. And he never told a soul, and he never told *me*, and I spent my whole life loving a ghost I didn't know was a debtor."

The matchcase scraped again. Voss lit a match and held it, and in its small burning she saw that his eyes were wet, and that he was not ashamed of it, and that ashamed or not he did not look away from her.

"Edmund Voss," he said. "That isn't my name. My name is Voss the way the depot is a depot—something built on top of something older. My grandfather was Henrik Aldringen. Survey chief. 1911." He said it the way a man says a word he has carried so long it has worn a groove in him. "They blamed him. The inquiry blamed him. The company blamed him. He came down off this mountain a broken man and died one, and my father grew up with that, and I grew up with that, and I have spent thirty years and most of my money trying to prove that the route was altered—that the line was moved, that the men died on a grade my grandfather never surveyed." He looked at the bundle. "And you're telling me he was right. The route *was* altered. By your grandfather. Under orders."

"Under pressure," Silas said. "From the investors. From the company. He wrote it all down. He wrote down the date and the order and the name of the man who gave it, and then he spent the rest of his life paying for it in the dark." She held the bundle out, across the space between them, across the treaty line. "Read it. All of it. Not the parts that clear your grandfather. The parts that damn mine. Both. The whole truth, or none of it is worth the paper."

Voss did not move for a long time. The match burned down to his fingers and he did not seem to feel it, and then he did, and shook it out, and in the dark Silas heard him breathing, and heard, beneath his breathing, the mountain settling its great weight around them, patient as a creditor.

"If I read it," he said, "I can't unread it."

"No," Silas said. "That's how truth works. I learned that this week. It's the only thing I've learned that I'd keep."

* * *

They took turns sleeping, or pretending to. The cold worked into them like water into stone, finding every crack. Silas dozed against the wall with the bundle inside her coat, against her chest, and dreamed in fragments: the cave, the tide, the rusted strongbox opening like a mouth, her grandfather's voice reading grade and curve to a girl of eight on a summer roadbed, *feel it, Silas, the land tells you what it wants, you just have to shut up long enough to hear it.* She woke with frost on her eyelashes and Voss sitting across from her, awake, watching the place where the door was as if he could see through iron.

"Tell me about the boy," she said, because it was the thing she had carried up the mountain along with everything else. "The way station. The initials carved in the beam—E.A., and a smaller set beside them, J.V. And a tin whistle. Someone got off that mountain alive who wasn't on the survivor list. A child."

Voss was quiet for so long she thought he hadn't heard.

"Johan," he said at last. "Johan Voss. My grandfather's son. Eleven years old. He was on the survey that summer because my grandmother had died in the spring and there was nowhere to leave him. He was in the way station when the party went up to the high camp. When they didn't come back, he—" The voice stopped. Started again, lower. "He walked down. Eleven years old, alone, in October, down a closed line, following the rails. It took him four days. He never spoke of it. He never spoke much at all, after. He was my father. I have his whistle in a case in my study. I've had it since he died."

Silas closed her eyes. The tin whistle in the way station, lying in the dust of sixty years, waiting. The boy who had walked down the mountain and grown into a man and fathered a man who had climbed back up it with hate in his pack.

"Then we've both been carrying the dead up this mountain," she said. "You for your grandfather's honor. Me for my grandfather's name. And they've been up here the whole time, Voss. In the vault. In the stone. Waiting for somebody to stop carrying and start telling."

The mountain settled. Somewhere far above, wind moved over the new snow, and the sound of it came down through the rock like breath through a whistle, small and clear and endless.

* * *

It was Voss who heard it first, in what Silas judged was the nineteenth or twentieth hour—time went strange in the stone, and hunger had begun to speak in both of them in a low, reasonable voice. He raised his hand. Silas went still.

A sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Not the wind. Not the mountain's settling.

Shovels.

Or a pick. Or an ice axe. Something human, striking snow and then stone, striking and pausing and striking again, and beneath it—she pressed her ear to the vault door and there it was, unmistakable, impossible, beautiful—a voice. Muffled, distorted, calling down through two meters of avalanche debris, calling a name.

Not her name.

"*Aldringen!* Henrik Aldringen! Can you hear me!"

Voss made a sound she had never heard a man make, something between a laugh and a wound opening. He put both palms flat against the iron door.

"Here," he called, and his voice cracked on it. "Here! We're here! *We're alive!*"

The striking stopped. Then it resumed, faster, harder, wilder, and Silas Sterling sat back against the stone with the oilcloth bundle against her chest and the frost melting on her face, and found that she was laughing too, and crying too, and that the two had stopped being different things.

The voice above called again, closer now, and this time she knew it—rough, warm, furious with relief.

It was Ruthie Kettle's voice, and it was saying: "Hold on, you stubborn fool. Hold on. I'm coming through."
