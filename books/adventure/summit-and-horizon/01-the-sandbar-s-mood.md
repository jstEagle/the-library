## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The fog came off the water at dusk the way a liar comes to dinner—smiling, patient, and full of bad intentions.

Ada Eriksen stood at the wheel of the *Maren Kade* with one hand on the brass and his chin tipped toward the river, listening. That was the first lesson of the lower reaches, older than any chart: in fog you don't steer by your eyes. You steer by your ears and your knuckles and that low animal place behind the sternum where a man keeps the things he's afraid of. The river spoke if you let it. It spoke in the hush of current sliding over a shoal, in the hollow knock of water folding back on itself where a snag lay drowned, in the particular silence of a sandbar deciding whether it would be kind tonight or not.

"Talk to me," he murmured, though there was no one at his shoulder to hear it. There hadn't been for ten years.

The *Maren Kade* was forty-one years old and moved like an old woman crossing ice—careful, deliberate, with a creak in her joints that Toma swore he could fix if Ada would ever give him two uninterrupted days and money they didn't have. She was a sternwheeler of the old school, oak bones and iron skin, her paddlewheel turning with a sound like slow applause. Her paint had gone the color of weak tea. Her name on the bow had faded until only *MAREN* remained, and even that was more memory than lettering now.

But she floated. On this river, in this season, floating was a kind of wealth.

Ahead, the fog banked against the bluffs like snow against a fence. Ada could smell the land through it—mud and rotting reeds and somewhere far off the coal smoke of Halloway Landing, which meant the landing was awake, which meant the landing's creditors were awake too, and one of them in particular would be standing on the dock with his hat in his hands and his arithmetic in his mouth.

Ada turned the wheel two spokes to starboard and felt the boat answer through the deck plates, a long shudder running forward like a word passed down a line of soldiers.

*There,* said the river, in the voice of water combing over gravel. *Here is the channel. Here I am.*

He'd learned to read it as a boy, kneeling beside his brother in the bow of their father's ferry while old Eriksen called out the signs like scripture. *V-vee of ripples means rock. Smooth tongue of water means deep. Where the current wrinkles without wind, something underneath is breathing.* Tor had been seven and Ada nine, and they'd recited it back like psalms, competing to see who could name a sign fastest, and their father had laughed from the wheel and said the river was the only book worth memorizing because it rewrote itself every day and never lied about what it was.

Their father had been wrong about the last part. Or maybe he'd meant it differently than Ada had taken it. The river didn't lie about what it was—but it lied plenty about what it would do.

The bell buoy off Gannet Point should have been close now. Ada closed his eyes for three seconds—not longer, never longer—and listened past the wheel's groan, past the steady thump-thump-thump of the paddles, past the tick of the engine gauges behind him. Nothing. No bell. Either the buoy had dragged its anchor in the spring floods, or he'd drifted further south than he liked, or—

There. A faint iron moan, off the port bow, muffled by fog the way a voice is muffled by a pillow. Closer than comfortable. He eased the throttle back a notch and swung wide, giving the point the respect it deserved, and the bell's sound slid past them like a stranger in a corridor.

"Miles," he said to himself, because saying it made it real. "Four miles to Halloway."

That was how he kept time now. Not years—years were a country he refused to visit. Miles. Miles since the wreck. Miles upriver and down, seasons measured in charter contracts and fuel receipts, the whole geography of his life reduced to distance covered and distance remaining. A decade ago tonight—or near enough; he kept the date the way you keep a stone in your pocket, unexamined but always there—Tor had gone into this same river eleven miles downstream of where Ada now stood, and the river had closed over him in four seconds, and Ada had spent every mile since trying to put those four seconds behind him instead of ahead.

It didn't work. He knew it didn't work. You can't outrun water. Water is patient, and water holds everything eventually—the silt, the secrets, the drowned boys who trusted their brother at the wheel.

He pushed the thought down where he kept it, under the floorboards of himself, and rang the engine-order telegraph to half ahead.

* * *

Halloway Landing materialized out of the fog all at once, the way places did on nights like this—one moment gray nothing, the next a row of lamplit pilings and the yellow windows of the Fuel & Feed, and the silhouette of a man on the dock's end who had the unmistakable posture of someone who has been waiting a long time and intends to make it everyone's problem.

"That'll be Pell," Ada said.

Toma appeared at the head of the stairs from the engine room, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime around midsummer. Toma was a broad, quiet man with burn-scarred forearms and the permanent squint of someone who spent his life looking into the guts of machines. "Pell from the bank?"

"Pell from the bank."

"He's got papers again?"

"He's always got papers." Ada brought the *Maren Kade* in against the current, gentle as setting down a sleeping child, and Junie—sixteen, quick, all elbows and watchfulness—was already at the rail with the bow line coiled over her shoulder before he'd finished the thought. Good girl. She read his hands the way he read the water. "Junie. When we tie off, you stay aboard."

Her chin came up. "I can carry fuel chits."

"You can carry fuel chits tomorrow. Tonight you stay aboard." He held her eyes until she looked away, muttering, and only then did he turn to the gangway. "Toma, keep steam up. We're not staying past the tide window."

"The tide window's six hours."

"Then we have six hours to be somewhere else."

Pell met him at the top of the dock steps with a lantern held too high, so that its light caught Ada full in the face like an accusation. Pell was a small man made mostly of ledger, with ink-stained fingers and a smile that arrived a half-second after the rest of his expressions, as though it had to be fetched from another room.

"Captain Eriksen." The smile, belated. "Foggy night to be moving a boat."

"Foggy night to be standing on a dock." Ada stopped an arm's length away, close enough to be polite, far enough to be clear. "You've got something for me, Pell, or you just enjoy the weather?"

"I have a schedule of arrears." Pell produced it from inside his coat with the tenderness of a man unveiling a relic. "Fourteen months outstanding on the refit loan. The bank's patience—"

"The bank doesn't have patience. Banks have interest." Ada didn't take the paper. Taking the paper meant reading the paper, and reading the paper meant admitting the numbers existed, and the numbers existed the way the fog existed—as a condition of the world he intended to pilot through rather than dwell in. "I'll have a contract inside the month. Big one, maybe. Then you'll get your fourteen months and your interest and your apology, all in one envelope."

Pell's lantern dipped. In the new angle of light his face did something complicated—pity and greed wrestling for the same ground. "Captain. I'll say this plain, because I've watched you run this river ten years and I don't wish you harm. The bank's hired eyes now. There's a man named Corvo Vane buying paper up and down this valley—boat notes, wharf leases, salvage claims. He bought yours in September."

The fog seemed to move closer.

"Bought mine," Ada repeated.

"Your debt. Your note. Which makes Vane's people your creditor, and Vane's people are not like the bank. The bank wants money." Pell folded the schedule away, back into the dark of his coat. "Vane wants boats. And crews. And rivers." He glanced past Ada's shoulder at the dim bulk of the *Maren Kade*, her single lit wheelhouse window hanging in the murk like a low star. "There's a man coming to see you tomorrow, I heard. Government clerk, down from the capital. Asking after captains who know the upper gorge."

Ada felt something shift in his chest—small, unwelcome, familiar. It was the feeling he got when the river wrinkled without wind. Something underneath, breathing.

"I don't know the upper gorge," he said.

"Everyone says you do." Pell picked up his lantern. "Sixty days, Captain. That's what's left on the note before Vane's people can move on the boat. Sixty days, and then she's theirs, and you're standing on a dock watching somebody else's smoke go upriver." He paused at the top of the steps. "For what it's worth—I'm sorry it's come to this. You're the best reader of water I've ever seen work this landing."

"Then you know why I'm still afloat," Ada said, "and why I intend to stay that way."

He walked back down the dock without looking at the schedule, without looking at the fog, without looking at anything except the next plank and the plank after that. Miles, he told himself. One plank at a time. It was the same principle. Everything was the same principle if you were tired enough.

* * *

Aboard, the lamp in the main cabin threw warm circles on the scarred table where the crew ate, and the smell of Junie's attempt at stew—onions, river fish, too much pepper—filled the boat with something almost like a home's noise. Almost. Ada sat across from Toma and told him about Vane, and watched the engineer's face go through the stages: disbelief, calculation, and finally a flat, hard calm that Ada recognized because he wore it himself most mornings.

"Sixty days," Toma said.

"Sixty days."

"And this clerk?"

"Coming tomorrow, according to Pell. Government business. Upper gorge." Ada turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the lamplight swim in his tea. "Nobody goes to the upper gorge for government business. The government surveyed it twice and gave up twice. The charts end at the narrows with a note that says *impassable* in three languages."

"Maybe they found a fourth language."

From the galley doorway, Junie said nothing, but she was holding the ladle like a weapon and looking at Ada with an expression he'd learned to dread—the expression of a young person about to volunteer for something. He cut it off before it could become words.

"Eat your stew," he said. "Both of you. Tomorrow we'll see what the government wants, and then we'll see what it pays, and then—" He stopped. The sentence had been going to end with *then we decide*, but the truth was there was no decision left in it. Sixty days. A clerk with a contract. The upper gorge, where the charts ended.

Where the water ran fast and deep and held whatever it held.

Toma studied him across the table with those machine-squinted eyes, and after a moment he nodded once, slowly, the way he nodded when an engine told him something true and terrible. "You're going to take it," he said. "Whatever it is. Whatever fool thing it is."

Ada looked down into his tea. Outside, the fog pressed against the cabin windows, and beneath the hull he could feel the river moving—always moving, patient, carrying its cold cargo of miles and years toward a sea that kept everything.

"I'm going to listen to what it pays," he said.

And far off, through the fog, through the dark, through the hull and the floorboards and the floorboards of himself, the river wrinkled without wind.

Something underneath was breathing.
