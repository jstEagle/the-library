## 2. A Clerk with Empty Pockets

The woman was waiting at the landing before the fog had finished lifting, standing on the rotted planks of the customs dock with a leather satchel clutched to her chest like a child she wasn't sure she wanted. Ada saw her from the wheelhouse of the *Maren*, marking her the way he marked everything that came down the gangplank: by what the river would do to her.

She had the wrong boots for the landing—city leather, thin-soled, already dark with river water where the planks wept between the boards. She had no luggage beyond the satchel. And she stood too still. People waiting for a boat fidgeted, paced, checked the sky. This one held herself like someone who had rehearsed the waiting and intended to see it through.

Ada brought the *Maren* in against the last of the ebb, easing her portside to the pilings with two touches of reverse and nothing wasted. Ten years of running this stretch had taught his hands the boat's moods the way a man learns a wife's breathing in the dark. He killed the engine and the silence came down over the landing, thick as the fog remnant, broken only by the creak of the hull kissing the tires hung along the dock.

"Captain Eriksen?" The woman's voice carried clean across the water. Not loud. Just certain.

"Depends who's asking."

"Miriam Sato." She produced a folded paper from her coat—a government commission, the seal of the Interior Survey Office stamped in blue wax—and held it up without letting him take it. "I'm told you run cargo upriver. As far as the gorge, some say."

"Some say a lot of things." He stepped down onto the dock, keeping the gap between them that habit demanded. Up close she was younger than her voice—thirty, maybe thirty-two—with ink stains on her right middle finger and the hollow-cheeked look of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. Her eyes did something most eyes didn't: they went past him to the boat itself, reading it. The patched rail. The engine housing Toma had rebuilt twice. The name faded to a ghost on the bow.

"You need work," she said.

"I need paying work."

"It's the same thing, from what I hear."

Ada almost smiled. Almost. "What is it you hear, exactly?"

"That your rates are honest and your silences are professional." She tucked the commission away. "That you don't ask questions about cargo, and you don't drink before noon, and you got a boat that can make the upper channel if the pilot knows what he's doing."

"The pilot's me."

"Then I'm hiring the right man."

He looked at her a long moment. The fog was burning off in rags now, and the morning light came down the valley flat and gray, turning the river into hammered tin. Somewhere upstream, a kingfisher rattled off a piling. Downstream, at the fuel depot, he could see the debt collectors' skiff tied up where it had been tied up for three days, waiting for him to be fool enough to cast off toward it. Harlan Voss and his brother, working collections for the Meridian Bank. They'd smile at him across a table and talk about installments, and behind the smiles would be the arithmetic of the *Maren*'s mortgage, four months behind, and the arithmetic always ended the same way: the boat taken, Ada put ashore, ten years of miles amounting to nothing.

"Come aboard," he said. "I'll charge you for the coffee."

* * *

His galley was six feet of scarred cedar and a stove that lit on the third try if you spoke kindly to it. Miriam Sato sat on the bench with her satchel in her lap and declined the coffee, which told Ada she expected to need her hands steady. He poured his own and sat across from her, and for a while neither of them said anything, because he'd learned that whoever talked first in a negotiation usually paid for the privilege.

She lasted four minutes. Better than most.

"A survey team went missing," she said. "Six people. Hydrographic engineers, mineral surveyors, a cartographer. They went upriver eleven weeks ago under government charter to complete a geological assessment of the canyon country above the gorge—the drowned canyon, the flooded towns, all of it."

"I know where it is."

"Of course you do." Something flickered across her face—not quite apology, but adjacent to it. "The team established a base camp at Miller's Bend. Their last scheduled transmission came eight weeks ago. Since then: nothing. No radio contact, no runner sent downstream, no sign of movement at any of the relay stations. The Office sent a patrol boat up to check. The patrol found the camp intact. Equipment secured, instruments stowed, provisions left behind. Six bedrolls. No boats. No tracks leading out except one set going north into the canyon, and those stopped at the waterline."

"Stopped how?"

"Stopped." She met his eyes. "As in, the man walking made no decision to turn. The prints just end where the water begins."

Ada drank his coffee. Outside, the current worried at the pilings, that endless patient conversation the river had with everything man-made. He'd heard stories come down with the timber crews all season—strange ones, half-formed, the kind men tell quietly and then laugh at to take the weight off. Lights in the flooded canyon at night. A whistle heard above the gorge where no boat could run. He'd filed them where he filed all such things, which was nowhere.

"And the Office wants them found," he said.

"The Office wants their equipment found." She said it carefully, the way a person steps between stones in a crossing. "There are instruments in that camp worth more than this boat. There are records. Field notebooks, plates, sealed cases of documentation. The assessment they were conducting has—" she paused, recalibrated— "implications. For the towns downstream. For land claims. For a great many interests."

"Interests." He set the cup down. "Madam Clerk, I've hauled gunpowder, smuggled medicine, moved a magistrate fleeing his own wedding, and once carried forty crates of live chickens through the narrows in a storm. I know what 'implications' means. It means somebody rich doesn't want something seen, or somebody poor does. Which is it?"

For the first time, she looked away from him. Out the galley's salt-fogged window, at the gray water, at nothing.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the truth, Captain, and I'd thank you not to make me repeat it. I'm a clerk. Third grade. I process requisitions and file assessments and I have done so for nine years without anyone telling me anything. Three weeks ago I was assigned to inventory the survey team's return manifest. Except there was no return. And when I started asking why, when I pulled the original charter and read the scope of the assignment—" her hands tightened on the satchel— "I found that the team's findings were pre-classified. Before they'd found anything. Do you understand? You don't classify results that haven't happened unless you already know what they'll be."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below decks, the bilge pump coughed and settled.

"So you're here off the books," Ada said.

"I'm here on money I borrowed against my own salary, because if I filed a request through channels it would reach the desk of the man who signed the classification, and then the survey team would stay lost forever, and whatever they found would belong to whoever he answers to." She opened the satchel and began laying things on the table with the deliberate neatness of a lifelong filing hand. A chart of the upper river, annotated in pencil. A copy of the camp inventory. And a drawstring pouch that hit the cedar with the particular dense, sweet sound Ada knew better than his own heartbeat.

Coin. Gold-weight coin, by the sound.

"Three hundred now," she said. "Seven hundred on delivery of the team or their remains, or their records—whichever comes first. Plus provisions, plus a bonus if we beat the tide windows on the way up. I can pay half the balance in advance on trust at the halfway station; the rest in the capital when we're done."

Ada looked at the pouch and did not touch it. Touching it would be deciding, and he wasn't ready to decide, because the number was wrong. Not too low. Too high. Three hundred was what he'd make in two months of honest hauling, and seven hundred more was the *Maren*'s entire mortgage with enough left over to recaulk her hull and replace the boiler fittings Toma had been nursing along on prayer and baling wire. It was freedom, sitting on his table in a drawstring bag. And freedom, in his experience, was the most expensive thing a man could buy, because nobody ever showed you the true price until after.

"Why me?" he asked. "There are pilots who've run the gorge more recently than I have. There are boats faster than mine."

"Faster boats burn fuel I can't afford. Recent pilots don't exist—the gorge passage has been closed since the flood, and everyone who ran it before either died in it or forgot it." She paused. "And you came recommended by a man named Ruth Calloway, who says you listen to water better than anyone she's known, and Ruth Calloway does not say things she doesn't mean."

That landed somewhere under his ribs. Old Ruth, up in the stilt village past the bend, who hadn't spoken to him in two years, not since the night he'd refused to run her up to the gorge cemetery in flood season and she'd called him a coward in front of half the landing. That she'd spoken his name to a stranger—that she'd spent whatever credit she had on him—was a stone dropped into water he'd thought was still.

"There's something else," Miriam said. "You should hear it before you decide, because after you decide it becomes leverage, and I won't have that."

"Go on."

"You're not the only one looking for the survey team. Corvo Vane's salvage fleet cleared the capital locks nine days ago. Two steam tugs and an iron-hulled recovery barge, the *Palmyre*. They filed a salvage claim on the abandoned equipment—government property, claimed as derelict, which is legal the way a knife is legal. Vane's crews will reach Miller's Bend inside the week. Whatever's in that camp, he means to have it first, and he has thirty men and guns to do it with."

Ada sat back. The name meant something even this far downstream. Corvo Vane had built a reputation the way rivers built canyons—slowly, relentlessly, and by removing everything soft. Salvage, dredging rights, tow contracts, insurance fraud dressed as rescue. Men who worked for Vane were said to sign papers they couldn't read and couldn't leave. His fleet ran black smoke and blacker books, and the last independent captain who'd contested a claim with him had sold his boat within the year and left the river entirely, and some said the leaving hadn't been voluntary.

"He'll overtake us," Ada said. "Whatever we find, he finds it first, unless—"

"Unless we're fast, and quiet, and lucky." Miriam closed the satchel over the chart but left the coin pouch on the table. "I won't lie to you, Captain. I've thought about this every night for three weeks and I can't see the whole path. I know the Office buried this survey before it started. I know Vane wants what it found. I know six people walked into the canyon and didn't walk out, and one of them may still be alive, because the patrol found fresh-cut firewood at the second campsite dated after the team vanished. Someone was there. Someone is possibly there now."

"Or was, and is dead."

"Or was, and is dead," she agreed quietly. "I've considered that too. Every night."

The stove ticked. The river talked to the pilings. Ada looked at the coin pouch and felt the old arithmetic start up in him, the one that measured everything in miles because miles could be counted and years could not. Ten years since the ferry went down at the gorge gate with his brother at the rail and Ada at the wheel, and in ten years he had not once gone back above the narrows, and every mile he'd logged since had been a mile chosen precisely because it wasn't that one. The river above the gorge was the one place on earth shaped like his worst hour. And this woman with her empty pockets and her borrowed coin was asking him to steam straight into it.

He should refuse. Any sensible man would refuse. Take the three hundred as a consulting fee, point her at Ruth Calloway, and let the whole rotten business sink or swim without him.

But Ruth had given his name. Ruth, who never gave anything.

And there was the other thing, the thing he didn't say, the thing that sat in him like ballast: six people had walked barefoot into the flooded canyon and vanished, and the river kept what it took, and Ada had spent ten years telling himself that what the river kept stayed kept. If there was even a chance—one in a hundred—that someone had come back out of that water alive, then the river could be beaten. And if the river could be beaten, then—

He stopped the thought where it stood. Some doors you didn't open in daylight.

"The terms," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Half the balance at the halfway station, in writing, witnessed. My crew signs on free—I hire who I need and you pay their wages, no argument about who they are. We provision today and cast off on tomorrow's dawn tide, and we don't stop for anything that isn't fuel, water, or dying. And if at any point I judge the boat or the people on her are lost, I turn around, and you keep your remaining coin and your silence. Those are my terms. Take them or row."

Miriam Sato looked at him for a long moment, and he watched something shift behind her eyes—the look of a person who has been carrying a weight alone for weeks and has just, cautiously, begun to set it down.

"Agreed," she said. "All of it."

She pushed the pouch across the table. He counted it out on the cedar—three hundred in heavy gold, real, not clipped—and the sound of it was the sound of the *Maren*'s mortgage burning, of Voss and his brother's smiles going slack, of a future that suddenly had more than one shape. It was too much money. It was far too much money, and it was strange, and strange money was the kind that cost the most.

He swept it into his coat anyway.

"We'll need a pilot who knows the gorge," he said, standing. "And an engineer who can keep a dying boiler alive on spite. And a deckhand who isn't afraid of work or questions. I know where to find all three, though none of them will thank me for it."

"Then we cast off tomorrow."

"Then we cast off tomorrow." He held the galley door open for her, and as she stepped out into the washed gray morning, he added, because fairness demanded it: "Clerk Sato. Whatever your Office buried up that canyon—you'd best hope it stays buried. Because if it doesn't, we're not the ones who'll have to answer for it."

She paused on the gangplank, the fog fully burned away now, the whole bright hard river laid out before her.

"I'm counting on it staying buried," she said. "Right up until the moment I dig it up."

She went down the plank and up the landing road toward the provisioning houses, thin-soled boots striking the boards, and Ada stood in his doorway watching her go, feeling the coin's weight against his ribs and the older, heavier weight beneath it—the one shaped like a gorge, and a gate, and a wheel held in two young hands on the worst morning of his life.

Tomorrow's dawn tide. He had one day to find a crew willing to follow him into the one place he'd sworn never to go again.

He went to find Ruth.
