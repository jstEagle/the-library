## 3. Six Tides

The *Kestrel's Wake* had not carried a full crew in three years, and Ada felt the emptiness of her the way a man feels a missing tooth—always with his tongue, always at the worst moments. He walked her decks that first morning after Miriam Sato's coin sat heavy in his strongbox, and he counted what he had: himself, forty years of river in his hands; an engine that coughed like a consumptive; and a boat built for six souls that had known only one for too long.

He needed three more. The clerk's papers said six tides to reach the upper survey country before Corvo Vane's salvage fleet could pick the bones clean, and no man alive could run the *Wake* alone through water that far and that mean. So Ada did what rivermen do when they need something they can't buy. He went looking among the people the river had spat out.

* * *

Toma Vasic found him, or let himself be found, which amounted to the same thing. Ada tracked him to the boiler-works at Gray's Landing, where the engineer had been blacklisted from every respectable boat on the river for reasons nobody would say aloud and Toma himself refused to confirm. He was a broad, slow-moving man with burn-scarred forearms and eyes the color of stove ash, and when Ada climbed down into the works' shadow and said his name, Toma didn't look up from the valve assembly he was rebuilding.

"Heard you were dead," Toma said.

"Disappointed?"

"Curious." He set down his wrench and finally looked up, wiping his hands on a rag already more grease than cloth. "They say you don't take passengers anymore. They say you don't take crew. They say you run cargo up and down the lower stretch and talk to nobody but the sandbars."

"The sandbars are better company."

"They are." Something moved behind the ash-colored eyes—not quite a smile, but its cousin. "So why are you here?"

Ada gave him the truth, or most of it. A government job, six tides upstream, past the gorge, into country where the charts went thin and the water went strange. Good pay, half up front. And a salvage fleet already moving, which meant speed mattered more than comfort and danger was a line item in the contract.

Toma listened without expression, then stood and rolled his sleeves down over the burns. "I'll want to see her engine first."

"You'll want to rebuild it first."

"I know. I'm saying I want to see how bad it is so I can grieve properly." He picked up his tool roll, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the care of a priest handling relics. "Three days' work if she's as sick as they say. Two if I hate her."

"She's been alone a long time."

"So have I." Toma shouldered his kit and fell into step beside Ada, and that was all the negotiation there was. Some men signed articles; others just started walking toward the same boat.

* * *

Junie came aboard the way smoke comes under a door—quietly, sideways, when nobody was watching.

Ada found her two days later, the morning before departure, curled in the forward hold behind a stack of spare canvas, asleep with a bilge knife still gripped in one small fist and a knapsack under her head for a pillow. She couldn't have been more than sixteen. Her hair was cropped short and ragged, done with a knife by someone who hadn't cared about the result, and her boots were worn through at the soles. She woke the instant his shadow crossed her, came up off the deck in a crouch, knife out, eyes wild as a snared animal's.

"Easy," Ada said, and didn't move. He'd dealt with feral dogs and feral men, and the trick was the same: stand still, speak low, mean it. "You've been eating my stores. Biscuits gone Tuesday night. Cheese Wednesday. That's theft."

"I'll pay it back." Her voice cracked in the middle of the word, and she hated it—he could see her hating it. "Work. I can work. Deckhand work, galley work, whatever. I'm strong."

"You're starving."

"I'm *strong.*"

He looked at her a long moment. There was a callus on her palm in the wrong place for anything but rope-burn, and rope-burn meant she'd handled lines before, probably on some mining-town barge where children did the work of grown men and got paid in company scrip worth less than the paper it was printed on. Upstream towns like that dotted the middle reaches—company towns, ore towns, places that existed at the pleasure of men who owned the ground beneath them.

"Where from?" he asked.

"None of your business."

"That's fair." He turned and climbed back up onto the main deck, and after a long moment he heard her follow, keeping six feet between them, knife put away but hand never far from it. "Here's how it is. We cast off tomorrow on the morning tide. Crew of four besides me. If you're aboard when we cast off, you're crew, and crew eats at my table and gets paid at the end of the run. If you're not aboard, I don't go looking. Either way, I don't ask questions you don't want answered."

She stared at him like he'd offered her something in a language she almost remembered. "That's it? No beating? You're not going to drag me to the magistrate at the next landing?"

"Do I look like a man who wants to explain a stowaway to a magistrate?"

Something happened to her face then—a flicker, quickly killed, that might have been the beginning of a laugh. "No," she admitted. "You look like a man magistrates don't like either."

"Smart girl." He turned away, then stopped. "Junie."

"What?"

"It's Junie, isn't it? You talk in your sleep."

For a heartbeat she looked genuinely alarmed, and then she saw the dry corner of his mouth and realized he was having a joke at her expense, and the alarm turned to outrage, and the outrage turned—finally, grudgingly—into that laugh she'd been strangling since the hold. It lasted about a second. But Ada heard it, and thought: *there*, and said nothing, because hope was a thing you didn't name aloud on a riverboat. Named things sank.

* * *

Ruth Okafor was the hardest, and the last.

She lived in a houseboat chained to the pilings below the old ferry landing at Cutter's Bend, a shack on a raft that had been patched so many times it was less a vessel than an argument held together with tar. She was seventy if she was a day, small and wiry as driftwood, with white braids shot through with river-clay beads and hands that trembled until they touched a wheel—and then didn't. Ada had heard of her the way every pilot on the river had heard of her: Ruth Okafor, who ran the upper gorge before the flood, who knew channels that had drowned twenty years ago and might still remember where their bones lay.

He brought whiskey, because that was courtesy, and the chart of the upper reaches, because that was business. She made him sit on her porch while she read the chart in silence for the better part of an hour, running one trembling finger along contour lines like a blind woman reading a face.

"This is wrong," she said at last, tapping the flooded canyon. "This whole stretch. They drew it from the old surveys, before the dam backed the water up. Nobody's charted it since. The river's had twenty years to rearrange herself in there, and she rearranges herself the way a snake does—slow, and always meaning something by it."

"That's why I need you."

"Mm." She poured two fingers of whiskey, drank half, set the glass down. "I knew a boy who drowned up there. Before the flood. Good pilot, better than me in the narrows. The river took him and I helped bury what she left." Her finger came to rest on the gorge gate, the narrows where the old canyon walls pinched together. "You want me to take a boat through there at night, against the tide schedule, chasing a ghost story about missing surveyors."

"Yes."

"And Vane's fleet is behind you."

"Two days behind. Maybe less now."

She was quiet a while. Gulls worked the shallows below the houseboat, screaming over something dead. When she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a woman reading water rather than remembering grief.

"Toma Vasic is engineering for you."

"You know him?"

"Everybody knows Toma. He rebuilt my sternwheel twice and only cheated me once, and he apologized for that." She drained the whiskey and stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked at Ada with eyes that had seen the river eat everything she loved and had decided, apparently, to keep watching anyway. "I'll pilot your boat through hell, Captain Eriksen. But I'll tell you this for free, and you can believe it or not. That river up there doesn't want your money or your cargo or your redemption. She doesn't want anything. That's what makes her dangerous. Everything else on this earth wants something, so you can bargain with it. The river just *is*. You go into that gorge thinking you've got a claim on her, she'll teach you different."

"I don't have a claim on anything," Ada said. "That's rather the point."

Ruth studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her, or at least amuse her. "Load your boat, Captain. I'll be aboard by dawn."

* * *

They cast off on the wrong tide, and everyone knew it.

The tide tables said wait eighteen hours for the ebb that would carry them upriver cheap and easy. But Miriam Sato's intelligence said Vane's steamer had coaled at Blackrock two days prior, and coal became steam, steam became distance, and distance was the only currency that mattered now. So Ada stood at the wheel with the morning fog burning off the water and gave the order against every rule of good seamanship, and Toma opened the throttle on an engine he'd spent three days cursing back to life, and the *Kestrel's Wake* leaned into the current like an old dog taking a hill it used to run.

It shouldn't have felt like anything. It was just a boat moving on water, the oldest motion in the world. But Ada stood at the wheel and felt the difference in her trim—the weight of Toma in the engine room, Junie coiling lines on the foredeck with a fierce, show-off precision, Ruth at the starboard rail reading the water with her lips moving silently, naming the currents like old friends and old enemies. Four strangers and himself, bound for the drowned country with a stolen schedule and a clerk's desperate coin.

The boat felt less empty. That was the thing he hadn't prepared for. Three years of silence had settled into the *Wake*'s timbers the way silt settles in a channel, and he'd told himself the silence was peace, and maybe it had been, the way a numb limb is peace. Now there were footfalls on his decks that weren't his own, voices carrying up from the engine room, the smell of someone else's coffee, and every one of those small intrusions pressed on a bruise he'd forgotten he had. He caught himself listening for a fifth set of footsteps, a voice from ten years ago calling up from the foredeck, and the bruise ached, and he let it ache, and he steered.

"Captain." Junie appeared at his elbow, breathless, pointing aft. "Miz Ruth says you'll want to see this."

At the stern rail, Ruth stood with the tide table crushed in her fist, staring back downriver at a smudge on the horizon that wasn't cloud. Too low, too steady, too dark. Smoke from a hard-driven stack, hanging over the water like a signature.

Ada took the glass from its bracket and raised it. The smudge resolved, sharpened, and his chest tightened around something cold. Iron hull. Twin stacks. A salvage steamer making better time than any honest boat had a right to make.

Vane. Not two days behind. Not even one.

"Mr. Vasic!" Ada called down the engine hatch, and his voice came out steadier than he felt. "How much more does she have?"

From below, muffled by iron and steam, Toma's answer rose like a challenge thrown down at his feet:

"How fast do you need?"
