## 4. What the Gear Said

The camp announced itself before they saw it. A thread of woodsmoke smell, days cold, drifted down through the morning fog, and then the fog thinned and there it was: a shelf of gravel and clay on the north bank, terraced by the flood's old moods, with a canvas lean-to sagging against a stand of dead alders.

Ada brought the *Kestrel* in slow, backing water long before the bank, the way he did everything—like a man who had learned that rivers punished haste twice as hard as they rewarded it. Miriam Sato stood at the rail with her hands folded too tightly, watching the shore come closer, and she found herself cataloguing details the way she'd been trained to: number of structures, state of disarray, evidence of fire, evidence of struggle. It was a clerk's habit. She had spent eleven years turning other people's field reports into tidy summaries for men who never left their offices, and somewhere in those eleven years she had stopped thinking of the reports as lives.

She was thinking of them as lives now. Four of them. Vanished.

"Nobody's home," Junie said from the bow, her voice pitched carefully flat—the voice of a girl who had learned not to sound excited about anything, because excitement could be taken from you.

"Nobody's been home for a while," Toma said. He had climbed halfway up the pilothouse ladder to look over Ada's shoulder, which he did whenever he wanted a better view and didn't want to admit the engine could spare him. "Smoke stain on that tarp's been rained on. Ten days, maybe twelve."

"Twelve," Ruth said. She hadn't moved from her chair at the stern, where she sat wrapped in a coat older than Miriam, hands resting on a walking stick that had once been a boat hook. Her eyes were half-closed, but Miriam had already learned that this was how Ruth listened—to the water, mostly, and sometimes to everything else. "River came up two weeks back. Spring pulse off the high snows. Anything light went downstream then."

Miriam stepped onto the gravel first. She told herself it was because the survey team had worked for her agency, technically, on paper, under budget line 44-C, and therefore the site was her responsibility. She told herself a great many things like that. They were getting harder to believe with every mile upstream.

The camp was orderly. That was the first thing wrong with it.

She had imagined wreckage—overturned cook pots, scattered instruments, the chaos of people running. Instead she found a camp packed down by professionals who had expected to come back. The lean-to was staked properly. The fire ring was stacked with unburned wood, laid ready against an evening no one had stayed to see. A kettle hung from a tripod over the cold stones. Everything in its place, everything waiting.

Except the people.

"Boots," Ada said.

He was crouched at the edge of the sleeping area, where four bedrolls lay in a neat row beneath the lean-to's drip line. He touched one of them without picking it up, the way a man touches something he suspects might be a wound.

"Gone," Miriam said. She had seen it too. "All four pairs."

"And the coats are still here." Ada stood, and his face did something complicated and brief—a flicker, quickly smoothed away, like current over a submerged rock. "Who walks out of the mountains barefoot and leaves their coat?"

"Nobody," said Ruth, from the boat. She hadn't come ashore. She rarely did anymore; Miriam had noticed that, and noticed also that Ada never asked her to. "Nobody does that unless the ground's warm enough not to need them. Or unless they weren't walking far."

"The ground isn't warm," Toma said. He had wandered to the fire ring and was crouching over it now, rubbing ash between his fingers with an engineer's absent tenderness, as though the fire were a machine he was diagnosing. "This burned down clean. Nobody kicked it apart. You kick a fire when you're leaving in a hurry or when you're hiding that you left. This is a fire somebody meant to relight."

Miriam moved toward the survey instruments, set up on a folding table at the camp's center, and the closer she got, the slower she walked.

There were three of them: a theodolite still locked on its last bearing, a barometer chained to its case, and the survey ledger itself—a fat, oilcloth-wrapped book lying open on the table, weighted at both corners with stones. Not closed. Not hidden. Open, as if Dr. Brandt—or whoever kept the records—had been writing when something happened.

She turned back the pages with fingertips gone clumsy. The entries were precise, beautiful even, in a small slanted hand that identified itself as I. BRANDT on the flyleaf. Elevation readings. Flow rates. Sediment samples logged by station number. The handwriting marched up the page day after day, station after station, confident as a metronome.

Then, two-thirds down the final written page, it stopped.

Not at the end of a line. Not at the end of a column. Mid-entry. The last line read: *Station 19 — flow anomaly persists, gradient inconsistent with bed profile, recommend re-survey of the canyon reach before—*

And nothing. The sentence simply ended, the pen lifted, the ink dry for days.

Miriam stood very still over the book, and the guilt she had been carrying since the harbor came up in her like floodwater finding a crack in a foundation.

Because she knew what Station 19 was. She had signed the requisition for the extra instruments that made measuring it possible. She had filed the travel authorizations. And she had also, six months ago, filed a different document—one that reclassified the entire survey's findings as preliminary, sealed pending review, copies restricted to a distribution list of five names. Five names, none of them Dr. Brandt. None of them anyone who lived downstream of the flooded canyon.

She had told herself it was procedure. Bureaucracies ran on such self-tellings the way engines ran on coal, and hers had run smoothly for years. But standing here in the cold ashes of a camp whose occupants had walked into the wilderness without their boots, looking at a sentence that ended mid-word, she understood—with the terrible clarity that only comes too late—that she had helped build the machine that swallowed these people. She had stamped the forms. She had been the stamp.

"They found something," she said aloud. She hadn't meant to say it aloud.

Ada looked up from the bedrolls. "You know that already, don't you?"

It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse. His voice was level, tired, the voice of a man confirming a bill he'd already agreed to pay.

"I know the ministry sealed the findings," Miriam said. "I know the review board that was supposed to publish them was dissolved before it ever convened. I know—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "I know that when a government pays for a survey and then buries the survey, it's usually because the survey says something the government doesn't want carved in stone. Either the vein isn't there and they've promised money it can't back. Or it is there, and what sits on top of it matters more than what lies underneath."

"Which is it?" Junie had come ashore without being told to, drawn by the gravity of the table the way young things are drawn to danger. She was looking at the ledger with an expression Miriam couldn't read—hunger and dread sharing the same face.

"I don't know," Miriam said. And then, because these strangers had given her three days of their lives and their boat's bottom against the river's teeth, she gave them the truer thing: "But I know the order came from above the ministry. From the Confluence Commission itself. And I know that Corvo Vane's fleet left port nine days before we did, which means someone told him where to go before any of us were told anything. Governments don't leak to salvage men by accident."

Toma whistled low. "So we're chasing a secret that was sold before it was found."

"We're chasing people who may have been silenced because of it," Miriam said. The words tasted like iron filings. "That's why I hired a riverboat instead of reporting it. That's why I paid you with my own savings instead of ministry funds—ministry funds leave receipts, and receipts get read." She looked down at the open ledger, at the sentence that ended mid-thought, and felt the full weight of her own small signature pressing on her chest. "Dr. Brandt wrote that entry believing her work would be published. Believing it would matter. Someone in my chain of command decided otherwise, and I carried the order down without asking what it cost. So yes. I know things. And most of them shame me."

The silence that followed was not unfriendly, exactly. It was the silence of people recalculating.

Ada broke it by crouching again, this time at the soft clay at the camp's northern edge, where the shelf narrowed toward a game trail climbing away from the river. "Here," he said. "Come look at this."

They gathered. Even Ruth rose from her chair and came ashore, leaning on the old boat-hook stick, and Miriam understood that whatever Ada had found was worth the old woman's rare effort.

Footprints. Bare footprints, pressed deep into the clay—deeper than body weight alone explained, as if the walkers had been hurrying, driving off their heels. Two sets, side by side, then a third following. All pointing upstream. Toward the gorge. Toward the flooded canyon.

"No boots," Ada murmured. "Four bedrolls, four coats hanging, four sets of instruments—and three sets of naked feet heading into the high country in early spring." He glanced up at Miriam. "Your survey team was six."

"Six," she confirmed. "Brandt, two technicians, a logistics officer, a guide, and a junior cartographer."

"So either three of them walked out and three of them didn't"—he let that hang in the cold air—"or some of them left twice."

Ruth had lowered herself carefully to one knee beside the prints. She spread her hand above them without touching, reading depth and spacing the way she read current. "They left at night," she said. "Toes dug in on the push-off. People leaving in daylight watch where they step. People leaving at night just go." She looked up, and her eyes in the seamed brown of her face were like two chips of river ice. "And they left quiet. No prints going to the latrine trench after dark, no prints at the fire. Whoever stayed behind kept the camp looking lived-in. Kept it looking like they'd all be back for supper."

"A decoy," Miriam said slowly. The shape of it assembled itself in her mind, ugly and elegant at once. "Someone wanted the camp to look abandoned casually. Wanted whoever came looking to think the team had simply... moved on. But three of them ran. At night. In bare feet, so they wouldn't leave boot prints a tracker could follow."

"Or so they could feel the ground," Ruth said. "Old trick. You can feel a follower through your feet before you ever hear him. Bare feet in the dark hear the earth talking."

Junie hugged her arms around herself. "Where's the fourth? Six minus three is three, and there's three sets of prints. Where'd the others go?"

No one answered, because there was nothing in the clay to answer with. Miriam looked around the camp again, and now she saw it differently—not as an orderly site but as a stage set, dressed by someone who understood exactly what searchers expect to find. The coats on their pegs. The kettle on its tripod. The ledger open to a page that stopped mid-sentence, as if snatched away by catastrophe rather than closed by choice.

Except catastrophes scatter things. Choice arranges them.

"Brandt's pen," Toma said suddenly. He was holding up a fountain pen, found tucked into the ledger's spine. He uncapped it and touched the nib to his tongue, grimacing. "Ink's still wet enough to taste. Days old, not weeks. Somebody wrote that last line recently, Miriam. Recently enough that the river hasn't washed the taste out of it."

"Or the page was written to be found," Miriam said. The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, certain. "A message disguised as an interruption. Read the line again."

Toma read it aloud: "*Flow anomaly persists, gradient inconsistent with bed profile, recommend re-survey of the canyon reach before—*"

"Before what?" Junie whispered.

"Before whatever comes next," Ada said quietly. He was staring upstream, past the dead alders, to where the river narrowed between walls of gray stone and the fog lay on the water like a held breath. "Recommend re-survey of the canyon reach. That's not a data point. That's a direction. She's telling whoever reads this where to go. Or warning them."

"Or leading them," Ruth said, "into whatever took the others."

Miriam closed the ledger at last and wrapped it in its oilcloth, and the weight of it in her satchel was heavier than paper had any right to be. She thought of the five names on the distribution list. She thought of Vane's fleet, nine days ahead, iron smoke on the horizon of her imagination. She thought of three sets of bare footprints climbing into country where spring still wore its winter clothes.

She had spent eleven years believing that papers were safe things, that information properly filed was information properly contained. Standing in the arranged ruins of Station 18's camp, she finally understood that papers were never safe—they were only ever arrows, and someone, somewhere, always drew the bow.

"We make camp here tonight?" Toma asked Ada.

Ada was still looking upstream. When he answered, his voice had changed—lower, more certain, the weariness burned off it like mist off morning water.

"No. We take on water and cast off within the hour. Those prints are ten days old at most, and bare feet don't get far in cold country—but they get farther than boots if they're desperate." He turned, and for a moment his eyes found Miriam's, and she saw in them not the guarded boatman from the harbor but something harder and more awake. "Whatever your government buried, clerk, your surveyors tried to dig it up and run with it. And somebody with better boots than theirs is ahead of us, doing the same math we are."

He raised his voice for the boat. "Toma—steam her up. Junie—take the lines aboard. Ruth—" he paused, and the old woman was already moving back down the bank, stick striking stone in a rhythm like a clock deciding to run again, "—you know what's upriver. Take us to it."

Miriam stood a moment longer at the edge of the camp, looking back at the lean-to, the coats on their pegs, the kettle waiting on its tripod for a supper no one would eat. Then she followed the others down to the water, and behind them the camp settled back into its long patience, keeping its arrangement, keeping its lie, keeping—for those clever enough and desperate enough to read it—the truest record any of them would find:

that the survey team had not vanished at all.

They had scattered, like startled birds, and something upstream was still calling them home.
