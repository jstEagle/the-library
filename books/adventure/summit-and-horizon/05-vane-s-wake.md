## 5. Vane's Wake

The iron came upriver on the second morning out of Sallow Landing, and Ada felt it before he saw it—a tremor in the deck plates under his boots, a wrongness in the water that had nothing to do with tide or wind.

He was at the wheel with a mug of chicory going cold in his fist, running the *Maren* easy through the long bend below Cutter's Reach, when the river itself told him. The current stuttered. Not slowed—stuttered, like a heartbeat skipping, the way water does when something big and hard displaces it far upstream and sends its argument rolling down through every channel and eddy. Ten years of reading this river had taught him its grammar, and this was a word he knew: *something heavy is coming.*

He set down the mug and climbed to the roof of the pilothouse, one hand on the rail, and looked back the way they'd come.

The fog had burned off early, leaving a sky the color of old tin, and against it—low, black, and patient as a coffin lid—the steamer sat on the water two miles astern and closing.

She was everything the *Maren* was not. Where his boat was wood and patch and stubbornness, ninety feet of patched hull and a boiler Toma coaxed along like a sick animal, the steamer was iron from keel to stack, broad-beamed, twin-screwed, her deck crowded with cranes and winches and the blunt geometry of salvage gear. Her wake spread behind her in a flat white fan, and she ate the distance between them with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had never once been in a hurry because nothing on the river could outrun it.

"Captain." Ruth's voice from the pilothouse doorway, low and dry as driftwood. She'd seen it too. Old pilots always did; the river spoke to them first. "That'll be Vane."

"You know her?"

"Know the class." Ruth came out onto the roof, squinting against the glare off the tin sky. She was seventy if she was a day, hands like knotted rope, eyes that had watched forty years of water go by and forgotten none of it. "*Iron Meridian.* Corvo Vane's flagship. I ran supply barges past her fitting-out at Grayharbor, back before the flood took the yard. She don't chase, Ada. She arrives."

Ada watched the black shape grow. Behind him he heard Junie come up from the deck, heard her sharp intake of breath, heard Toma's boots on the ladder—his engineer moved heavy for a small man, all shoulders and grievance.

"Salvage fleet," Toma said, stating what they all could see. He spat over the rail. "That's not a boat, that's a floating claim office. They'll have papers for everything they take and guns for everyone who argues."

"Miriam below?" Ada asked.

"Asleep, or pretending to be. She hasn't slept proper since we cast off. Clerk keeps her case strapped to her wrist even in the bunk." Toma glanced at him. "You want me to wake her?"

"Not yet." Ada kept his eyes on the steamer. "Let's see what she wants first. Boats that size don't waste coal chasing wood boats for nothing."

"She's wasting it on us," Junie said quietly.

And she was right, which was the part that sat cold in Ada's stomach. The *Iron Meridian* wasn't passing them. She was coming straight up their wake, dead on their line, and she was doing it fast enough now that he could pick out details: the men at her rail, small as ants; the crane arm folded flat along her deckhouse; the name picked out in flaking gilt across her bow. She'd been built for hauling drowned things up out of deep water. She'd been built, Ada thought, for exactly the kind of work that left other people's griefs stacked on her deck like cordwood.

"Ruth, take the wheel. Hold this line, steady as church. Nobody changes course until I say."

"And if she doesn't slow?"

"Then she means to run us down," Ada said, "and changing course won't matter much anyway."

He went down to the main deck. The *Maren*'s engine room hatch stood open, breathing heat and oil-smell, and Toma dropped down into it without being asked, because Toma always knew when his engine might be needed to do something stupid. Ada stood at the stern rail with his arms crossed and waited, watching the iron ship swell in their wake like a storm front made of rivets.

She slowed a quarter mile off. Close enough to read the faces at her rail. Far enough that her wash wouldn't shove them around—which meant whoever commanded her wanted to talk, and wanted them to know he could just as easily not.

A voice came across the water through a brass speaking-trumpet, amplified and flattened by distance into something almost gentle.

"*Wooden vessel, paddle-wheel, name Maren. This is Captain Corvo Vane aboard Iron Meridian. I'd admire a word with your master.*"

Ada looked at Ruth up on the roof. She gave him a look back that said, plainly, *don't*. Then she shrugged, because she'd known him three days and already knew how that would go.

He stepped to the stern rail and cupped his hands. "You're speaking to him."

"Captain Eriksen." A pause, and then the trumpet again, and there was something in the voice now that hadn't been there before—something almost warm, which was worse than any threat. "I've heard your name. You ran cargo through the lower marshes during the quarantine year. Alone, they say, when nobody else would take the contracts."

"The fees were good."

"They usually are, for men willing to be alone." A soft sound across the water that might have been a laugh. "I'll make this brief, Captain, because I respect your time and my own. You're carrying a government clerk and a sealed case upriver toward a survey camp that no longer exists. I'm asking you to turn around. I'm prepared to pay you triple whatever she promised you—and I know what government clerks pay, so I know that's not a fortune, but it's more than she has. Triple, in coin, today, at any landing you name. And you keep your boat, your crew, and your health."

The offer hung over the water. On the *Maren*'s deck, Junie had gone very still by the cargo netting, and Ada could feel her looking at him, and could feel, beneath that, the shape of the question she couldn't ask aloud: *what's my town worth to you?*

Triple pay. Miriam had paid him half upfront in coin that was real but thin, scraped together from some clerk's discretionary fund, and the rest promised against a treasury voucher that might take months to honor—if the treasury still existed in any form that honored vouchers by the time they got back. Triple would clear the *Maren*'s debt at Sallow Landing. Triple would buy a new boiler, real planking, food for a winter. Triple was the difference between the boat he had and the boat he'd stopped letting himself want.

Ten years ago, Ada thought, he'd have taken it. Ten years ago he'd have taken it without even calling it cowardice. He'd have called it sense. He'd have called it keeping his head down, minding his own water, staying alive—the whole litany of small surrenders a man recites while he drowns by inches instead of all at once. He'd been reciting it the day his brother went under. He'd been standing right there, at the wheel, close enough to hear the rope go slack, and he had done nothing fast enough, and every day since he had told himself the same story: *nothing you could have done, no way to know, the river takes what the river takes.*

The river took what the river took. But men like Corvo Vane helped it along.

"No," Ada called back.

The trumpet was quiet a moment. When Vane spoke again, the warmth was gone, and what remained was worse—not anger, but the mild patience of a man discussing freight rates.

"I haven't named my figure yet, Captain. Triple was the courtesy. I can go higher. There isn't a number on this river I can't meet."

"It's not the number."

"Then what is it? Sentiment?" A pause, and Ada could picture him up there on that iron deck—could picture the kind of face that went with that voice, smooth and reasonable, a man who had never once raised his voice because he'd never once needed to. "You've known the clerk four days. The crew less. Whatever's in that case, it isn't yours, Captain. It never was. You're risking your neck and that fine old boat for other people's business, and other people's business sinks ships. I've pulled enough of them off the bottom to know."

Ada looked down at his own hands on the rail. Scarred knuckles, rope-burned palms, the hands of a man who had spent ten years hauling other people's cargo up and down a river because it was easier than stopping moving, because miles were easier to measure than years, and years held nothing but a rope going slack and a brother's name he hadn't spoken aloud since the funeral.

Other people's business. That was all it had ever been, he'd told himself. His brother's ferry run wasn't his. The overloaded manifest wasn't his to argue with. The storm warning wasn't his to insist on. And so he'd stood at the wheel and let it happen, and had spent a decade calling the standing-still a kind of innocence.

"No deal, Captain Vane," he said, and heard his own voice come out level, and was surprised by it. "We're chartered through to the survey camp. We finish the job or we don't, but we don't quit for coin."

"Then let me speak plainly about the alternative." The trumpet's voice dropped, and somehow carried better for it. "This river is mine from Grayharbor to the gorge gate. Every landing, every fueling station, every chandlery and coal yard—I own the paper on them or I own the men who do. Your boat will need wood, water, oil, and food before you're another three days upriver. You'll find none of it sold to you. And should you manage to reach your survey camp and find whatever it is you're looking for—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table, "—you'll still have to bring it back down. Through me. Rivers only run one way, Captain Eriksen. Everything comes downstream eventually. Everything. I can wait. I'm very good at waiting. Ask anyone I've waited for."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"One last courtesy." The voice softened again, and this time there was something underneath it, something that made the hair on Ada's forearms rise. "Your pilot. The old woman. Ruth Aldous, formerly of the upper gorge service. Tell her Corvo Vane remembers her. Tell her the gorge gate is closed, Captain. Has been closed eleven years, since the flood, and every chart says so, and every chart is mine. If she takes you in there, she takes you in blind, off memory, at night if she knows what's good for her—and memory is a poor chart. I'd hate to read about a wooden boat on the rocks at the narrows. I'd hate it the way a man hates hearing about weather that ruined someone else's crop. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you're threatening an old woman."

"I'm describing geography," Vane said mildly. "Good day, Captain. I do hope we meet again under better circumstances."

The trumpet clicked off. The *Iron Meridian*'s bells rang twice, thin and bright across the water, and the great black ship began to fall away astern—but slowly, without urgency, her twin screws turning at an easy walk, as if to demonstrate that she could keep this pace forever, that she had coal enough to shadow them to the end of the world and back, and that the race, such as it was, had already been decided in her favor.

Ada stood at the rail until the iron shape shrank to a smudge against the tin-colored water, and then a suggestion, and then nothing at all except the flat white scar of her wake lying across the river like a healed wound.

* * *

They gathered in the pilothouse, all five of them, which was more bodies than the little house had ever comfortably held. Miriam had come up at last, the case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, her clerk's composure cracked just enough at the edges to show the fear beneath. Junie sat on the chart bench with her knees drawn up. Toma leaned in the doorway smelling of engine oil and fury. Ruth stood at the wheel, saying nothing, her jaw working like she was chewing on words she refused to swallow.

"Well," Miriam said finally. "Triple pay."

"Don't," Ada said.

"I'm not suggesting—"

"You're a clerk. You costed it out in your head the moment he said it. I watched your face." Ada turned from the window. "I didn't refuse for you, so don't thank me, and don't apologize either. I refused for me."

Miriam blinked. For a moment the bureaucratic armor slipped entirely, and what was underneath looked young and tired and grateful in a way she clearly didn't know how to carry. "That's—"

"He threatened Ruth," Junie said. Her voice was thin but steady. "He knew her name. How did he know her name?"

"Because I'm worth knowing," Ruth said dryly. But her hands stayed tight on the wheel spokes, and Ada saw her knuckles whiten, and understood that the old woman was more rattled than she'd ever admit. Vane had reached back eleven years to pull her name out of the flood. Men did that when they meant to collect a debt—or settle one.

"He'll choke our fueling," Toma said. "Every landing from here to the gorge. He wasn't bluffing about owning the paper. Half those stations fly his colors and the other half owe him money. We'll make maybe two more days on what's in the bunkers, three if I lean on her."

"So we don't stop at landings," Ada said.

Toma stared at him. "You want to run six tides upriver without taking on wood or water."

"I want to think about it tonight. All of us. Ruth—" Ada turned to the old pilot. "What he said about the gorge gate. The charts. Is it true?"

Ruth was quiet a long moment. Outside, the river slid by, brown and patient, bending north toward the high country where the flooded canyon waited with whatever it held.

"The charts are true," she said at last. "Officially, the gate's been closed since the flood. Unofficially—" her mouth bent into something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't, "—the river don't read charts. Never has. I ran that narrows twenty years, before the water rose, and the water rising didn't move the stone. It just moved the floor." She looked at Ada, and her eyes in the dim pilothouse light were pale and clear as river glass. "I can take us through. Night run, no lights, off memory and the sound of the water against the rock. But I'll tell you the same thing I'd have told you twenty years ago, Captain: the gorge forgives nothing, and it remembers everybody."

Ada nodded slowly. He went back to the window and looked astern one more time—at the empty water, at the fading scar of the wake, at the whole downstream world where Corvo Vane's paper owned every dock and every scale and every man who weighed things for a living.

Three days of fuel. A closed gate. An iron ship somewhere behind them, in no hurry at all.

It should have felt like a trap closing. Instead—and this was the strange thing, the thing he turned over in his chest like a stone found in a dredge bucket—it felt like the first time in ten years he'd chosen a heading instead of drifting into one. Refusing Vane had cost him nothing yet. But refusing him had felt, for one clean moment on that stern rail, like standing at a wheel and actually turning it.

"Junie," he said. "Get the lead line out. I want soundings every half mile from here on—this stretch shoals on the east side and I don't trust the marks anymore. Toma, give me everything the boiler's got and keep her fed. Ruth, hold us north of the channel mark."

"And where exactly are we going in such a hurry?" Miriam asked.

Ada watched the river bend ahead of them, toward higher country, toward the gorge, toward whatever the survey team had found and whatever had been left behind in bare feet.

"Away from him," he said. "Fast."

Behind them, far down the long brown reach, a smudge of black smoke lifted above the tree line—patient, unhurried, and still coming.
