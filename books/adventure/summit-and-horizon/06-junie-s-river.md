## 6. Junie's River

Junie had been aboard the *Kestrel's Wake* for four days before anyone asked her name.

Not that she'd hidden it, exactly. She'd scrubbed decks, coiled lines, hauled coal up from the hold until her shoulders screamed and her palms went raw and shiny with new skin. She'd learned the boat the way you learn a strange dog—slowly, sideways, never reaching out first. Captain Eriksen watched her work without comment. Toma the engineer grunted approval in the language of grease and gaskets. Old Ruth, who slept more than she spoke, had looked at Junie exactly once on the first morning, a long look from under the brim of an oilskin hat, and said nothing at all.

It was Miriam Sato who finally asked, on the fourth evening, as the river widened into a brown mirror and the sun went down behind them like a coin dropped into a well.

"Junie," the clerk said, settling onto the crate beside her with the careful politeness of someone who'd rehearsed this. "Short for something?"

"June-iper." She kept her eyes on the water. "My ma liked flowers she'd never seen."

"And where does a girl named after a flower she's never seen come from?"

The question was gentle. That was what made it dangerous. Junie had prepared herself for suspicion, for interrogation, for the captain to change course mid-river and put her ashore at the next landing with a lecture about stowaways and the law. She had not prepared herself for kindness.

"Copperline," she said.

The word dropped between them like a stone into still water. She felt Miriam go very still beside her. Down the deck, near the wheelhouse, Ada Eriksen's hands paused on the spokes—not stopping, not turning, just pausing, the way a man pauses when he hears a sound he's been half-expecting.

"You know it," Junie said.

"I know the name." Miriam's voice was careful now too. "It's one of the towns in the survey corridor. Upper reach. Why do you ask?"

*Because I grew up in the shadow of its smokestacks. Because my father coughs black every winter and my mother mends the same three dresses until the fabric gives out. Because when the company store closed last spring, half the town ate garden vegetables and pride, and pride runs out faster.* But what she said was: "It's my river. The one we're going up. I wanted to see how far you'd get before somebody told you what's waiting there."

Nobody answered that. The paddle wheels turned. Somewhere below, Toma's engine kept its steady iron heartbeat, and the *Wake* pushed on against the current, which was the only direction it seemed to know.

* * *

She hadn't meant to tell them. She'd meant to work the whole run, earn her keep, maybe earn a place—people did that on rivers, she'd heard; boats collected strays the way driftwood collects barnacles—and then slip away at whatever landing sat closest to Copperline and walk home through the hills with news nobody would believe.

But that night, anchored in a slack-water eddy while the tide turned, the fear caught up with her.

It came the way it always came, in the dark, when there was nothing left to do with her hands. It came as the sound of the company man's voice reading the notice off the church steps, his boots polished, his voice bored: *relocation assessment pending final mineral survey, all residents advised to maintain records of property claims.* It came as her mother's face going gray, not angry, not even surprised—just gray, the way faces went in Copperline when the mine took another lung or another son. It came as her father saying, quietly, over the lamp: *They'll move us if the numbers say so, June-iper. And if the numbers say stay, it'll be because they want something worse than our houses.*

And it came as the memory of the survey team itself—four strangers with brass instruments and government letterhead, eating dinner at the Copperline boarding house, asking polite questions about water tables and land titles while everyone smiled the smile of people who have learned that smiling is cheaper than fighting.

She found the captain alone at the stern rail, smoking a pipe he mostly forgot to light.

"I need to tell you why I'm really here," Junie said.

Ada didn't startle. He took the unlit pipe out of his mouth and looked at her, and up close his face was older than it seemed from across a deck—lined like riverbank clay, with eyes the flat gray-green of deep water. "I figured there was a why," he said. "Stowaways running from debt don't work as hard as you do. They hide."

"I'm not hiding." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "That's the problem. I'm doing the opposite of hiding and it might get us all killed."

He gestured at the rail beside him. She leaned there, forearms on wet wood, and looked down at the black water sliding past, carrying their reflected lantern-light away downstream like something spilled.

"There's a vein under the valley," she said. "Under Copperline and the six towns around it. Everyone's always known there was *something*—the old-timers used to say the mountain was hollow and full of silver. But nobody could prove it, and proof costs money, so we just kept scraping copper out of the hillside and calling it a living." She swallowed. "Then the government sent a survey team. Official. Sealed orders. And everybody in town got excited, because official means fair, right? Official means they'll measure what's there and write it down and the writing will protect us."

"And instead?"

"Instead the team stayed eleven days and left in the night. No goodbyes. Left their camp standing, left food in the pans." She turned to look at him. "You're going up to find them. I've heard Miriam say it. Well, I'm telling you what I think you'll find. You'll find men who saw the numbers and got scared. Not scared of the mountain. Scared of what people do when there's that much money sitting under a town full of poor folks."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. The current muttered against the hull. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, thunder rolled along the gorge.

"What happens to Copperline if the survey says the vein's real?" he asked finally.

"Depends who reads it." Junie's hands had closed into fists against the rail without her permission. "If it goes to the government proper, maybe—*maybe*—we get compensated fairly. New town built somewhere safe, jobs, all the things they promise. But there's a man named Corvo Vane who owns salvage rights on half this river, and my father says Vane's people have been buying up Copperline land claims for two years, pennies on the dollar, from families too broke to say no. If Vane gets those papers—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "If Vane gets those papers, the survey won't say there's a vein under our town. It'll say there's a vein and no town worth mentioning. It'll say relocation is a formality. And there are nine hundred people in Copperline, Captain, and most of them can't read, and every one of them will be gone inside a year, scattered like chaff, and nobody will even write down where they went."

The words ran out of her and left her shaking. She hadn't said any of it aloud since she'd crawled into the coal bunker at Millhaven Landing three days before Miriam Sato ever set foot on this boat. Saying it made it real, and real was heavier than she'd budgeted for.

Ada knocked his dead pipe against the rail, twice, slow.

"Why'd you run instead of staying to fight?" he asked. "Nine hundred people. Seems like they could use every pair of hands."

"They don't know I'm here!" It burst out of her louder than she meant, and she dragged it back down to a whisper. "They don't know anything. My pa thinks I'm working the cannery season at Estuary Point. If I'd stayed—if I'd stood up at the meeting and said *I'll go find the truth*—they'd have locked me in the house for my own safety, and they'd have been right, because what can I do? I'm sixteen. I can read, though. I can read better than anybody in Copperline except the preacher. So I came to find the papers myself." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Which sounds insane when I say it out loud. A girl and a stolen look at a government document, versus Corvo Vane and his iron steamboat."

"It does sound insane," Ada agreed.

Her stomach dropped. Here it came—the turn-around, the next landing, the lecture. She gripped the rail and waited for it.

"But," he said.

She looked up.

"But insane and impossible aren't the same thing." He put the pipe away in his coat pocket, and when he spoke again his voice was lower, almost to himself. "Ten years ago I'd have told you the smart play. Cut your losses, go home, let the big machines decide. Smart keeps you alive." His eyes were on the dark water, but Junie had the sudden, certain feeling he wasn't seeing the river at all. "Smart also leaves you standing on a bank somewhere counting everything you failed to save. I've done a lot of counting."

"Captain—"

"I'm not promising you anything." He turned and looked at her straight on, and there was no softness in it, and somehow that was the kindest thing anyone had said to her in months. "I don't know what's up that river. I don't know if your papers exist, or if they do, whether they say what you hope. I'm not going to swear an oath over a rail at midnight by a girl I've known four days. That's not how I keep faith."

Junie's chest tightened. "Then—"

"Then hear the rest of it." He held up one scarred hand. "We're not turning around. Whatever's up there—Vane, the survey, the whole rotten arithmetic of it—we're already committed past the point where turning back costs less than going on. And while I'm going anyway, I'd rather go knowing what I'm going for." He nodded at her, once, deliberate. "A town of nine hundred. That's as good a reason as any man ever had. Better than most."

The relief hit her so hard her knees nearly went. She turned back to the rail fast, before he could see her eyes, and blinked hard at the water until the world steadied.

"Thank you," she managed.

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me in Copperline, if we make it." He pushed off the rail. "Get some sleep. Tide turns at four, and Ruth wants to run the shallows in the dark, which means I want everyone awake and holding something solid."

He was three steps toward the wheelhouse when Junie made herself say it—the thing she'd been carrying under her tongue since Millhaven, the thing that had finally tipped her from stowing away to speaking.

"Captain. There's something else. About the survey team."

He stopped.

"The night they left Copperline, I saw them. All four, coming down from the ridge trail after dark, moving fast. And they weren't together." She wrapped her arms around herself against the river chill. "Two of them were arguing—I heard *it has to go back downriver*, that's all, just that phrase over and over. And the other two were walking ahead like they couldn't stand to be near them. Like a family after bad news." She met his eyes. "Whatever they found up there, Captain, they didn't all agree on what to do with it. And only some of them were headed downstream."

For a moment Ada Eriksen stood absolutely still, and the lantern light moved on his face, and Junie thought she saw something pass through his expression that had nothing to do with surveys or salvage fleets—something older and more private, surfacing and sinking again like a body under brown water.

"Bare feet leave tracks," he said quietly, mostly to himself. Then, louder: "Four bells, Miss Junie. Be on deck."

He went into the wheelhouse and the door clicked shut, and a moment later lamplight bloomed behind its glass, and Junie stood alone at the stern rail with her heart hammering and the gorge thunder rolling closer out of the north.

Above the black line of the hills, clouds were stacking up against the stars. The river smelled like rain coming, and iron, and something else—something mineral and ancient, rising up out of the flooded canyon ahead as if the mountain itself had begun, at last, to speak.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She tried—curled in the bow locker among the spare canvas, listening to Toma banking the engine fires below—but every time she closed her eyes she saw the church steps and the polished boots and her mother's gray face, and finally she gave up and went topside to watch the dark.

That was how she came to be standing at the rail when Ruth emerged from the pilot cabin at half past three, moving with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had never once needed to hurry in her life. The old pilot climbed to the wheelhouse, said something low to Ada, and then paused in the doorway and looked back—at Junie, at the bow, at the north.

"Gorge gate tomorrow night," Ruth said, to no one in particular, in a voice like gravel poured slow. "Water's higher than it was in my day. Higher than it ought to be." She squinted at the distant thunderheads, and then at Junie, and something in her seamed old face shifted—recognition, or pity, or both. "You're Copperline stock. I can hear it in your accent. Valley girl."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then pray your river's in a forgiving mood." Ruth tapped the doorframe twice, the way superstitious pilots did, and went inside. "Because I've seen what it does when it isn't."

An hour later, at the turn of the tide, the *Kestrel's Wake* cast off her lines and swung her nose north into the current, and the river rose up to meet them—dark, swollen, and full of everything it was carrying toward the sea.
