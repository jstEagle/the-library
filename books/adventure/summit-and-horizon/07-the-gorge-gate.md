## 7. The Gorge Gate

Ruth had not spoken the gorge's name aloud in thirty-one years.

She kept it the way a widow keeps a wedding ring—turned over in private, polished by memory, never shown to company. The maps called it the Kestrel Narrows now, when they called it anything at all, and the maps were liars. The maps had been drawn after the flood, by men who measured from the new waterline and never knew what lay beneath: the old towpath, the drowned ferry landings, the church steeple that had stood at the mouth of the gorge like a finger raised in warning until the river took it whole. The maps showed a channel. Ruth remembered a river.

And it was the remembering that frightened her, standing at the wheel of the *Marrow's Grace* with night coming down and Ada Eriksen watching her with those patient drowned-man eyes.

"You're certain," he said. Not a question. He'd asked it three times since noon and each time she'd answered with silence, and silence, between people who worked water, was its own kind of yes.

"I'm certain it was there," she said. "Whether it's still there is between me and the river."

Below them, Toma's engine turned over with a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something difficult. The boat rode low—too low, Ruth thought, with all the coal and chain and Junie's endless appetite for canned peaches—and the tide was already turning against them at the mouth. Behind, somewhere downriver and gaining or not gaining, Corvo Vane's iron steamer ground its way north like a sin looking for a place to happen.

Ada waited. That was the thing about him she'd learned in six days of working his deck: he could wait like stone waits. She'd piloted for forty years and known a hundred captains, and most of them filled silence with orders because orders made them feel like captains. Ada let the silence do the work.

"Before the flood," she said at last, "there was a gate."

* * *

The story came out of her in pieces, the way old stories do, worn smooth at the edges from handling.

Before the dam, before the government's engineers decided the valley would make a fine lake and the towns would make a fine floor for it, the Kestrel Gorge had been the only road north. A mile of granite with the river threaded through it like wire through wax. The current ran fast there even in high summer, and the channel wound through standing rock in three bends the boatmen called the Needle, the Coffin, and the Widow's Turn. Every pilot on the river learned them by daylight first, then by moonlight, then—if they lived long enough to be arrogant—by nothing but feel.

"There was a gatehouse at the mouth," Ruth said. "Stone tower, signal lamp. The keeper would light it green when the water was honest and red when it lied. My father kept that lamp for nineteen years." She looked out at the darkening water. "I lit it myself the last night. Red. I knew what was coming—the engineers had told us, told everyone, *the water will rise, you must go*—and I lit it red anyway, as if a lamp could stop a flood. As if anybody was left to read it."

"And the channel?" Ada said. "The bends?"

"The bends are cut in stone. Stone doesn't drown." She tapped the rail with one knuckle, an old habit, knocking on wood to keep the river listening politely. "The Needle should be where it always was. The Coffin too. But the depth's changed—forty feet of new water sitting on forty feet of old, and the two of them don't move alike. The surface current will tell you one lie and the undercurrent another, and if you steer by the wrong one—" She shrugged. "The Coffin earned its name twice over, back when it was only sixty feet deep."

Junie had drifted up during the telling, the girl's bare feet silent on the deck plates, peach syrup still sticky at the corner of her mouth. Miriam Sato stood further off by the cargo netting, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Even Toma had climbed halfway up from the engine well, grease to his elbows, to listen.

"So nobody's run it," Junie said. "Since the flood. Nobody at all?"

"Nobody foolish enough to need to. The long way around adds four days through the lowland channels, and Vane's steamer can't take those shallows any more than we can take this gorge—or so I'm betting." Ruth felt the old pride rise in her chest, hot and unwelcome, like heartburn. "I ran the Narrows two hundred times before the water came. I was seventeen the first time, and my father stood behind me at the wheel and never once touched my hands. When we came out the far side he said, *Now you know what your ears are for.*"

"What are they for?" Junie asked.

"Listening to water you can't see yet."

* * *

Full dark found them at the mouth of the gorge, and the gorge did not welcome them.

It announced itself first as a change in the air—a coolness falling over the deck like a sheet drawn up over a body—and then as sound, a deep hollow booming off stone that Ruth felt in her back teeth before she heard it with her ears. The cliffs rose on either side, blacker than the sky, and the river between them moved with a smooth muscular indifference, the way a sleeping man turns over without waking.

Ruth took the wheel.

Ada stood at her right shoulder—not touching, never touching, just present, the way her father had been. Toma crouched at the engine telegraph with his hand resting on the lever like a rider's hand on a horse's neck. Junie hung a lantern at the bow, hooded, throwing only a coin of light on the water ten feet ahead. Miriam held the stern. Nobody spoke. The engine idled down to a heartbeat.

"Green means honest," Ruth murmured, mostly to herself, mostly to a dead man's ghost. "There's no lamp anymore. So we'll be our own lamp."

She closed her eyes. Just for a breath. Just long enough to let the wheel speak to her palms.

Then she opened them and steered into the dark.

The Needle came first. In the old days it had been a bend so sharp the boat seemed to turn back on itself, the current piling against the outside wall and boiling up in a slick black hump that could spin a lighter craft like a leaf. Ruth read it now by the sound—the boom off the left wall arriving a half-beat before the right, telling her the channel hugged starboard—and she brought the *Grace* around with the current instead of against it, letting the river throw them into the turn the way you'd swing a child by the arms, trusting the grip.

The hull groaned. Something vast slid past the port side close enough that the lantern-light caught wet stone, streaming and black, close enough to touch if a man were fool enough to lean.

"Depth's good," Toma called softly from the well, reading his sounding line. "Deeper than the charts say. The flood silted the inside of the bend, but the main channel scoured clean."

"It would," Ruth said. "The river keeps its roads better than governments keep their promises."

The Needle gave them passage, and for a hundred yards the water opened into something almost gentle, a pool between the bends where the current slackened and the stars found room enough to double themselves on the surface. Ruth allowed herself one long breath. Her hands ached. She realized she'd been strangling the wheel spokes and made herself loosen, finger by finger.

"My father used to say the gorge was the river's church," she said. The words came out quieter than she intended. "You didn't talk in it. You didn't hurry in it. You came in humble or you didn't come out."

"What happened to him?" Junie asked, from the bow. The girl had no sense of what questions cost. Or maybe she did, and asked anyway, which was worse and braver both.

"He stayed," Ruth said. "When the water came. Somebody had to keep the lamp lit till the last boats were out, and he decided it was him." She turned the wheel a spoke, feeling for the pull of the next bend. "They found the tower itself three miles downstream, floated whole off its foundation. The lamp was still bolted to the top. Still burning, they said. I've always doubted that part. But I've always hoped."

Nobody answered. The river filled the silence with its booming, and Ruth understood that the silence was the correct response, the only one. Some inheritances you carry. Some you steer by.

* * *

The Coffin came at midnight.

She heard it before anything else—a wrongness in the water's voice, the steady boom of the walls interrupted by a churning, sucking hiss, like breath drawn through teeth. Her stomach dropped through forty years and landed in the body of a seventeen-year-old girl with her father's hand hovering near her shoulder.

"Kill the lantern," she said.

Junie snuffed it. Darkness fell complete, absolute, the kind of dark that has weight, and Ruth steered into it by ear alone.

The Coffin had been a short, vicious stretch where the channel split around a midstream spire of rock—the Devil's Tooth, the old boatmen called it—and rejoined in a trough of standing waves. By day it had been a matter of reading the white water. At night, before the flood, pilots had run it by the sound of the two channels: the eastern passage sang high and quick over shallow gravel, the western boomed low and slow over deep stone. You chose west unless the water level told you otherwise, and the water level—

The water level had changed. Forty feet of it. Everything she remembered was true and useless at once, and the realization arrived in her chest like a hand closing.

"Ruth." Ada's voice, low, at her shoulder. Not questioning. Offering.

"I know what I'm doing," she snapped, and immediately regretted it, because it was half a lie and he surely knew it. What she was doing was listening harder than she had ever listened in her life, every nerve stripped and straining, while the boat drifted toward a hissing dark that wanted to eat them.

High song to the east. Low boom to the west. But underneath both, something new—a third voice, a deep grinding groan that hadn't existed before the flood, coming from the west passage, from the deep channel, from the place that was supposed to be safe.

Undercurrent. The new water lying on the old, moving against it, dragging along the drowned canyon floor like a second river wearing the first as a coat. She'd heard about such things from salt-water men. She'd never heard one. It sounded like a millstone turning.

"West passage is lying," she breathed. "Something's down there. Something big, moving wrong." Her mind raced ahead of her mouth, running the arithmetic of the east side—shallow gravel, singing water, but how shallow now? Silted? Scoured? No way to know except—

Except the way her father would have known. Except the way the river tells you, if you ask it properly.

"Toma. Give me half ahead, no more. Junie—lantern, unhooded, straight down over the bow rail, close to the water. I want to see the surface skin, nothing else."

The girl obeyed without question, and the little flame bloomed at their feet, and Ruth leaned out over the rail into the cold breath of the gorge and watched the water directly beneath the light.

The surface told its lie beautifully—smooth, glassy, sliding westward with a gentle confidence. But where the lamplight struck it, she saw the skin shiver. Small wrinkles crossing the grain of the current, moving upstream. Ripples flowing backward. The undercurrent below, grinding past the Devil's Tooth, reaching up like a drowning man's hand to drag the surface down with it.

East, then. East, where the water sang.

"Hard to starboard. Half ahead. Trust me and don't touch anything."

She took them across the face of the Coffin's approach with the current pushing wide and the undercurrent pulling deep, and for eleven seconds—she counted them, she would count them for the rest of her life—the *Marrow's Grace* sat on two disagreeing rivers at once, her hull trembling like a plucked string, the lantern light showing the black hump of the Devil's Tooth sliding by to port close enough that spray off its flanks dusted the deck.

Then the eastern passage took them, and the water began to sing.

It was exactly as she remembered: a bright rushing note, gravel under the keel, shallow but honest, the channel scoured clean by forty years of new current finding the old path the way water always finds the old path eventually. The song rose around them, wild and clear, and Ruth felt something crack open in her chest—not fear, not anymore, or not only fear. Pride, fierce and unreasonable, the pride of a woman proving to a drowned valley that its knowledge had not drowned with it.

Her father's hands had never touched hers. Her hands, now, on the wheel, alone, in the dark, in a passage no living soul had run since the world ended here—they were his hands, and they were her own, and the river knew the difference and forgave it.

They came out of the Coffin into open black water, and Junie whooped once, sharp and young, and clapped both hands over her own mouth.

* * *

The Widow's Turn was almost gentle after that, a long lazy bend where the gorge widened and the current spread out, and Ruth took it slowly, letting the boat breathe, letting herself breathe. The cliffs fell back. Stars multiplied overhead. Somewhere ahead, the far gate waited—the place where the gorge spat its travelers back into the ordinary world.

Miriam came forward and stood beside her at the wheel, and for a while the clerk said nothing, which Ruth appreciated. Then:

"You knew the third voice was there. Before you heard it."

"No," Ruth said. "I knew the river doesn't waste a flood. New water finds work to do. I just listened for what work." She glanced sideways at the younger woman. "That's the whole trade, miss. Everybody thinks piloting is knowing the river. It isn't. It's believing the river knows itself, and getting out of its way politely."

"And Vane's captain? His charts won't have any of this."

"His charts have the flood survey. Depth lines drawn by lead line from a steam launch, done in daylight, done in a hurry." Ruth smiled, and felt her face make the smile, an unfamiliar sensation, like wearing someone else's coat. "He'll come to the mouth of this gorge, look at his pretty map, and decide the lowland route costs him less than the dark does. We've bought ourselves a tide. Maybe two."

Ahead, the walls fell away entirely, and the gorge released them with one last long boom of farewell. The river widened, slowed, went silver under the stars. Ordinary water. Honest water, for now.

Ruth stepped back from the wheel and found her knees shaking, found that her shirt was soaked through with sweat despite the cold, found that she was laughing—quietly, helplessly, an old woman's cackle she hadn't heard from herself in years. Ada looked at her with an eyebrow raised.

"Two hundred runs before the flood," she said, wiping her eyes, "and that was the finest one. Don't tell my father."

"I wouldn't presume."

Junie appeared at her elbow and pressed something into her hand: a can of peaches, the syrup-sweet peace offering of the young. Ruth took it, absurdly moved.

Then Toma came up from the engine well two steps at a time, and the look on his face killed the laughter where it stood.

"Captain," he said. "You'll want to hear this. There's a boat behind us in the gorge. Running dark. And it's not Vane's steamer—it's small, it's quiet, and it's been following us since the Needle."
