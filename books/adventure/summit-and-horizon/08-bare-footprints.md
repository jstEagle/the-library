## 8. Bare Footprints

The river changed its mind about them an hour past dawn.

Ada felt it before he saw it—the way the *Maren* sat in the water, her bow riding a half-hand higher than she should, the current beneath them gone from a steady pull to something sullen and shifting. Ten years of reading water had taught him that rivers spoke first in the hull and only later in the eye. He eased back on the throttle and stood at the wheel, watching the banks slide by: red clay giving way to pale stone, willow scrub thinning into stands of dead pine drowned to their knees, their silver skeletons rising out of the flood like the masts of a fleet that had sunk standing up.

"Water's wrong," Ruth said. She had come up without being called, as she always did now, her bare feet silent on the deck planks, her hands wrapped around a tin mug of coffee she never seemed to drink. "Current's braiding. Three channels where there was one on my last run."

"When was your last run?"

"Nineteen years." She sipped the coffee anyway, cold or not. "Before the dam backed this whole valley up. It wasn't a river then. It was a canyon with a river in it, and the canyon had opinions."

Ada looked at the drowned pines, at the ghostly fence posts standing in six feet of water off the eastern bank—the tops of somebody's pasture, somebody's life, bought cheap and flooded deep. Somewhere under all that flat brown water were roads, orchards, church steeples. The government had drawn a line on a map and turned three hundred square miles of other people's memories into a lake, and now it wanted the mineral survey that would decide what happened to everything still above the waterline downstream.

He thought of Junie, who hadn't slept well since they'd passed the last mining town's smoke on the horizon two days back. He thought of Miriam Sato up in the pilothouse with him, hunched over her charts with the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

"How far to the second camp?" he asked.

Miriam checked her notes. "By Brandt's field log—assuming they made the same speed we did—another four miles. There's a shelf of rock on the north bank where the old wagon road used to climb out of the canyon. The log calls it the Larder Shelf. They camped there the night before the baseboat went down."

"The night before," Ada repeated. "And the morning after, five surveyors became one survivor."

Nobody answered that. The engine ticked beneath them, patient as a heartbeat. Toma had the watch below and would have heard every word through the deck grating; Ada could picture him down there with his ear cocked and his wrench in his lap, listening the way he listened to everything, cataloguing.

Four miles. The fog had burned off clean for once, and the light lay hard and white on the water, and Ada found himself wishing for the fog back. Clear days showed you too much. Clear days let you see exactly how far you'd come and exactly how little cover there'd be when whoever had hunted Dr. Ilsa Brandt through these hills came hunting again.

* * *

They found the Larder Shelf an hour before noon, and Ada brought the *Maren* alongside it with the tenderness of a man setting down something breakable.

The shelf thrust out of the north bank like a lower lip, forty feet of flat limestone above the present waterline, scarred with the ruts of a wagon road that hadn't carried a wagon in twenty years. Behind it the bank rose in terraces to the rimrock, and the drowned forest stood off the shelf's edge in a black lattice. A natural landing. Anybody running boats up this drowned canyon would have seen it from a mile off, same as Ada had.

Which meant anybody else would have seen it too.

"Toma, with me. Junie, stay on the boat and keep her hot. Ruth—" he hesitated.

"I'm coming." Ruth set down her mug. "You'll want eyes that know this ground before it drowned. And don't tell me to mind the boat. I've been minding boats since your father was in short pants, boy, and I mind them better from shore."

He didn't argue. He'd learned that much about Ruth already.

Junie watched them go from the rail, one hand on the mooring line, the other resting near the boat hook she'd taken to keeping close since Vane's steamer had passed them going the other way. She was sixteen and trying very hard to look like someone who had done this before. "If anyone comes," she said, "I'll ring the bell twice."

"If anyone comes," Ada said, "you ring it once and you cast off. Don't wait for us. We'll swim."

Her jaw set. "And leave you?"

"Leave us. That's not a request, Junie. That's the whole job." He held her eyes until she nodded, and hated himself a little for how much it cost her, and knew it was right anyway. A boat is a promise you make to everyone aboard. He'd broken that promise once, ten years ago, and the river had kept the receipt.

The climb up the shelf was easy going, the old road bedded in gravel and goatweed. Toma ranged ahead with a boat hook he'd sharpened into something between a tool and a weapon, moving with the deliberate care of a man who trusted nothing that hadn't been tested. Ruth walked behind Ada, and Ada noticed she was counting under her breath—not steps, he realized, but landmarks. *Road bend. Culvert. Blister of rock shaped like a loaf.* Mapping the drowned world against the world that had replaced it.

The camp announced itself by smell before sight: cold ash, and under it something sweeter and worse.

"Meat left out," Toma said quietly. "Days ago."

The survey team's second camp sat in the lee of a granite blister, sheltered from the wind off the water. Ada took it in the way he took in any strange landing—slowly, in quadrants, letting the details arrange themselves.

Two tents, both collapsed but neither cut down. A fire ring with ash gone gray and rained-on. Crates stacked with military neatness, stenciled with the survey bureau's mark, some opened, some still sealed. A theodolite on its tripod, abandoned mid-sight, its brass telescope pointed at nothing across the water. Cooking gear. A lantern with a cracked globe. And everywhere, everywhere, the particular disorder of people who had left in a hurry—or been made to.

"Nobody packed," Toma said. He crouched by the crates. "Look. These are sealed. You don't seal crates you're carrying out. You seal crates you're leaving for someone to find, or crates you expect to come back to." He glanced up. "Or crates you're hiding things inside."

Ruth had drifted toward the fire ring. She stopped there, very still, and Ada came to stand beside her.

The ash held things. A tin cup, flattened as if stepped on hard. A button, bone-colored. And a dark stain soaked deep into the gravel where the rain hadn't reached—a stain the size of a man folded small, with a drag mark leading away from it toward the shelf edge.

"Blood," Ruth said. "Old. Week, maybe more." She looked up at Ada, and her eyes in the flat noon light were the color of the water. "This is where it went bad. Not at the first camp. Here."

Ada crouched at the edge of the stain. He'd seen blood enough times on a working river—gaff wounds, winch accidents, the year the *Maren*'s old owner had fallen into her own propeller. This was different. This was a lot of blood, in one place, and no body.

"They fought here," he said. "Or one of them died here. Or—" He stopped, because his eye had caught something at the margin of the camp, and the rest of the sentence died in his throat.

Footprints.

The ground beyond the fire ring, where the gravel gave way to a seam of fine gray silt the floods had deposited years ago, was printed with tracks. Boot prints first—he counted four distinct sets, overlapping, crossing, some deep with urgency, some wandering—and among them, unmistakable, a fifth set.

Bare feet.

Small. Narrow. A woman's, or a slight man's. Printed deep at the heel and ball, the toes splayed slightly, the gait uneven—one stride longer than the next, favoring the left side. They came out of the tent area, crossed the silt, and went north along the shelf, away from the water, toward the terraces and the rimrock and the black mouths of the canyon caves that pocked the cliff face above the old road.

"They walked out barefoot," Toma said. He'd come to stand over the prints, and his voice had gone careful, the way it did around machinery he didn't understand. "In this country. On this stone. Nobody does that unless they can't make noise, or can't afford to leave boots behind, or—" he paused, "—or they're following something they don't want to lose track of."

"Or being followed," Ruth said, "and don't want to be heard coming."

Ada crouched and put his own hand beside one of the prints. Small. The stride favored the left. He thought of Dr. Ilsa Brandt's field log, which Miriam had read aloud to them twice on the long cold evenings—Brandt's precise, dry entries, the readings, the depths, the mineral assays recorded in a hand so neat it looked typeset. The last entry, mid-sentence, unfinished:

*Assay confirms primary vein extends beneath the settlement grid at concentrations exceeding—*

Exceeding what? The sentence had simply stopped, the way a clock stops.

"Brandt," he said. "It has to be. She's the one who wrote the log. She's the one who survived." He looked north, at the caves. "She walked out of here barefoot, hurt, alone, and went up into the rocks."

"Or something walked out of here," Toma said, "wearing her feet."

Nobody laughed. The drowned pines ticked in the breeze off the water, and somewhere far upstream a bird cried once and quit.

* * *

They followed the footprints because there was nothing else to follow.

The trail climbed the first terrace, and Ada understood quickly why bare feet had been the choice. The old road was paved in places with slabs of limestone that rang hollow under boot heels—you could hear a shod man coming fifty yards off in this quiet—but bare feet on stone made almost nothing. Whoever had walked this trail had known the country, or learned it fast, and had wanted silence more than comfort.

The prints told a story if you bent low enough to read them. Ada read them the way he read water, patiently, letting the details accumulate. At the second terrace the stride shortened—the walker had slowed, hurting. A smear of blood on a slab, thumb-width, days old, brown as rust. Below a ledge, a place where someone had pressed against the rock and waited—two prints planted close together, weight on the outside edges, the stance of a person making themselves small. Above that ledge, on the rimrock itself, fresh scratches. Not animal. Tool marks, recent, deliberate—someone had chiseled or pried at the stone up there within the last few days.

"Somebody else has been up here," Toma whispered. "After her."

"Vane's men?"

"Could be. Could be the ones who did the killing down at camp." Toma spat. "Survey teams don't gut each other over assay numbers. Somebody paid for this."

They climbed on. The caves began at the third terrace—dark seams in the cliff face, some no wider than a hand, some yawning. The footprints passed the first two without pausing. At the third, the largest, a cavern mouth tall as a barn door breathing cool air, the prints stopped.

Stopped dead, at the threshold, pointing in.

And there, just inside the shadow line, Ada saw what the walker had left: a boot. One boot, upright, placed deliberately on a flat stone like an offering or a marker. Beside it, weighted down with a pebble, a scrap of paper gone soft with damp.

Toma reached for it; Ruth caught his wrist.

"Traps come in all sizes," she said.

Ada knelt instead, and lifted the paper without touching the stone beneath it. The ink had run but held. A single line, in that same precise typeset hand:

*If you are reading this, do not follow me in. Tell no one what we found. Burn the log.*

Below the signature—there was no signature, only a small drawn symbol, a circle bisected by a wavy line, the survey bureau's mark for classified hydrographic data—something else had been scratched into the paper with a pin or a nail, hurried, almost illegible:

*They are not all dead. One of them works for him.*

Ada sat back on his heels. The cave breathed its cold breath over him, smelling of wet stone and old water and, faintly, impossibly, of lamp oil.

Lamp oil. Fresh.

"She's alive," he said. "And she's been burning through oil down there in the dark, waiting for someone stupid enough to come find her." He stood, and his knees cracked, and he found that his hands were steady in a way they hadn't been since they'd left the first camp. "One of her own team sold them to Vane. Maybe more than one. And that one knows these caves, or knows someone who does."

"So we go in blind," Toma said, "after a woman who's telling us not to, hunted by a killer who's had days to pick his ground."

"Yes," Ada said.

Ruth snorted softly. "That's pilot logic for you. Straight channel, straight answer."

But she was already uncoiling the lantern line from her belt, and Toma was already checking the oil in his lamp, and Ada stood at the mouth of the cave looking back down at the *Maren*, small and brown and stubborn on the bright water far below, with Junie a speck at her rail.

Down there, the bloodstain dried in the gravel. Up here, the boot stood its lonely watch. And somewhere in the cold throat of the mountain, a woman who had mapped the death of towns was hiding from the men who meant to profit by it, listening—for water, for footsteps, for the sound of rescue or the sound of the end.

Ada struck a match and lit the lantern.

"We go in quiet," he said. "Barefoot, if we have to."

The dark swallowed the light three steps in, and somewhere ahead—deep, deep in the stone—they all heard it at the same moment.

A voice. Thin, cracked, human, singing something old and wordless to keep the dark company.

And underneath it, closer than the singing, closer than their own breathing: the scrape of a boot heel on stone, moving fast, moving toward them out of the black.
