## 9. Tide Against Them

Toma Vasiliev had spent thirty years listening to engines the way priests listened to confession, and in all that time he had never once heard a machine lie.

The *Marrow's Grace* was telling him the truth now, and the truth was ugly. He lay flat on his back in the engine room with a stethoscope of his own devising—a length of copper pipe pressed to his ear and to the valve housing—and listened to the third cylinder knock. Not a tap. A knock. Deep and wet, like a fist on a coffin lid. The bearing was going. It had been going since the gorge, when Ruth had threaded them through the narrows with six inches of water under the keel and the river screaming at them from both banks, and Toma had pushed the boiler to a pressure he still hadn't written in the log because no inspector would believe it and no engineer should survive it.

They had survived it. That was the problem with surviving things. It made you think you could survive more.

He slid out from under the engine, wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth, and climbed the ladder into daylight. The river ran wide and brown-green here, swollen with snowmelt off the high country, carrying whole trees like matchsticks. Ada stood at the wheel with his shoulders set in that particular way Toma had learned to read over two weeks of shared water—not tense, exactly, but coiled, the way a man stands when he is doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Third bearing," Toma said. "She'll hold for another day at cruising speed. Maybe."

"And past cruising speed?"

Toma looked at him. Ada Eriksen was not a man who asked questions he didn't want answered, which meant this one had been eating him for hours.

"Past cruising speed," Toma said slowly, "she'll hold until she doesn't. And when she doesn't, she won't fail quiet. She'll throw a rod through the hull or crack the block, and we'll be a very well-furnished reef."

Ada nodded once, as if Toma had told him the weather was fine, and turned back to the channel. That was the thing about the captain that Toma had come to respect and resent in equal measure: he absorbed bad news the way sand absorbed rain. No puddles. No runoff. Just gone, down into some deep place where he kept everything—his brother, his debts, whatever ghosts had made a man buy a boat named after grief and live alone on it for ten years.

Toma went forward to check the tide tables spread under a lantern in the pilothouse, because Miriam Sato insisted on keeping them there even though the pilothouse smelled of tar and Junie's contraband tobacco. The clerk had changed in the last hundred miles. When she'd hired them she'd been all ink-stained fingers and apologies; now she stood over those tables like a general over maps, and her pen moved fast.

"We have tomorrow's high tide at the second narrows," she said without looking up. "After that, the window closes for nine days. Nine days, and Vane's steamer reaches the wreck first."

"The window holds if the river holds," Toma said.

The pen stopped.

That was how they learned, an hour later, what the river intended to do about it.

* * *

It came up from downstream first, as sound—the deep, flat *whump* of explosive charge rolling along the water like thunder looking for its storm. Then the birds went up off the banks in clouds, hundreds of them, herons and ducks and things Toma couldn't name, fleeing upstream past the boat in a panicked gray river of their own.

Then the water itself changed.

Toma felt it through his boots before anyone called it out. A shudder in the deck plates, a wrongness in the vibration coming up from the hull. He was down the ladder and at the stern rail before Junie's voice cracked across the deck—"What was that? What *was* that?"—and he looked back the way they'd come and saw nothing, and then looked ahead, because the sound had come from ahead, upstream, around the long bend where the valley pinched between two bluffs of red shale—

And the bluff was moving.

Not falling. Moving. A whole face of it, forty feet of rock and root and ten thousand years of standing still, sliding into the river with a groan that Toma felt in his teeth. The splash when it hit threw spray higher than their mast. The wave came downriver at them minutes later, not large by ocean standards but wrong for a river, a single long hump of displaced water that lifted the *Grace*, rolled her fifteen degrees, and set her down again while cargo shifted below and Junie swore with a fluency that would have made a dockhand blush.

When the water settled, Toma understood what he was looking at, and understanding it made him sick.

A bluff had come down across half the channel. Not enough to dam the river—the water still poured through, white and furious—but enough to choke it. Enough to back the river up behind the slide like a thumb over a hose. And somewhere up there, hidden beyond the bend, men with dynamite and a steam launch were waiting for the pool behind that choke to rise high enough to burst through all at once: a flood-wave that would tear down the narrows, scour the low channels, and turn every carefully calculated tide window on Miriam's tables into so much waste paper.

"He's not chasing us," Ruth said quietly. She had come up from below without Toma hearing, which she did, always, moving like a woman half her weight. Her eyes were fixed on the broken bluff, and there was something old and cold in her face. "He's drowning the clock. Corvo Vane doesn't need to catch us. He just needs us to be late."

"Can we get through?" Miriam asked. "Before the flood-wave comes down?"

Ruth shook her head once. "Nobody outruns a river that's been given a reason."

Toma looked at the choked channel, at the white water forcing itself through the gap, and then—he couldn't help it—back at his engine room hatch, at the boat's heart beating under his feet with its bad bearing and its tired boiler and its thirty years of honest service.

Ada's voice came from the pilothouse, level as a spirit gauge.

"Toma. How much does she have?"

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He went below instead, because a question like that deserved an honest answer and honest answers required looking.

The engine room was hot and close and smelled of oil and iron and, faintly, of the river seeping through seams that had been caulked twice already this trip. Toma knelt by the third cylinder and put his palm flat against the housing. Warm. Too warm. He thought about the bearing inside, the babbitt metal worn to a shine, the crankshaft running a hair out of true with every revolution, each revolution a small wager, thousands of wagers a minute, and the house always winning eventually.

He thought about other boats he'd killed. There had been three, over the years. Not through carelessness—through necessity, or what he'd told himself was necessity, war and blockade and one terrible winter on the lower delta when the choice had been push the engine or watch a town run out of medicine. He remembered the sound each one made at the end. The first had screamed. The second had simply stopped, mid-beat, like a man struck dumb. The third had knocked, exactly like this, for eleven hours, and he had pushed it anyway, and it had carried them home and died at the dock with its rods bent and its block split open like a chest.

He had sworn after the third one. Standing in the wreckage of it, oil to his elbows, he had sworn he'd never again ask a machine to die for him.

But machines didn't die for him. That was the lie in the vow. Machines died *with* him, for whoever was aboard, for the people above decks who didn't know a bearing from a blessing. And right now above decks there was a runaway girl whose whole village hung on a case of papers, a clerk who had chosen treason over career somewhere in the last hundred miles and hadn't told anyone yet but wore it plain as a bruise, an old pilot who had given the river back its own gorge, and a captain who measured his life in miles because years held nothing.

Toma took his hand off the warm housing.

"All right," he said to the engine, quietly, the way you speak to something that can hear intention. "I'm sorry. I need everything."

Then he went to work.

* * *

The next six hours passed in a blur of heat and profanity and arithmetic done in chalk directly on the boiler casing.

The plan, such as it was, had three parts, and Toma hated all of them.

First: pressure. The *Grace*'s boiler was stamped for 120 pounds and had lived its whole life at 90. Toma meant to run her at 150. He stripped the safety valves, weighted them himself with iron scavenged from the ballast, and wired the governor open so the throttle could draw full steam. Every joint in the system would be living above its rating. He went over them twice with fresh packing and a torque wrench, and then a third time by hand, feeling each flange like a physician taking pulses.

Second: the bearing. You couldn't fix a worn bearing in six hours on a riverboat. But you could feed it. He mixed a lubricant of tallow, graphite, and the last of his private store of heavy cylinder oil—the good stuff, the stuff he'd been hoarding against some future emergency without ever admitting that's what he was doing—and rigged a drip-feeder straight onto the third journal, gravity-fed, adjustable, so the bearing would drown in lubricant and maybe, maybe, the hydrodynamic film would keep metal from touching metal. It was a gamble dressed as engineering. He knew it. He did it anyway.

Third: fire. The boiler needed heat, and heat needed draft, and draft meant opening the firebox beyond its design and burning fuel faster than any sane stoker would allow. They had coal, wood, and—after Junie volunteered the contents of her secret locker with a grimace—one barrel of pitch-pine kindling that burned hot and mean. Toma showed her how to lay a fire for maximum heat, and Junie, who turned out to have stoked boilers illegally since she was twelve, took to the shovel like she'd been born holding it.

"You know this will kill her," Junie said, wiping soot from her chin, nodding at the engine. "Eventually."

"Everything kills everyone eventually," Toma said. "We're negotiating the date."

She grinned at that—fierce, soot-smeared, seventeen years old and already harder than he'd ever been at seventeen—and shoveled again.

Miriam came down once, stood in the heat with her sleeves rolled and her ledger clutched to her chest, and asked, "What do you need from me?"

"Numbers," Toma said. "Every reading, every five minutes. Pressure, vacuum, temperature, revolutions. Write them all down." She blinked at him. "If she blows, I want to know exactly when and why, so the next fool who tries this lives longer than I do."

Something moved across her face—horror, and under it a strange respect—and she sat down on the coal scuttle with her book and began to write.

Ada came down last. He stood in the doorway a long moment, watching the engine turn over under its load of improvised hope, and Toma knew what he was seeing: the heart of his boat being asked to beat double-time, and the man responsible for it standing in the wreckage-to-be.

"How long?" Ada asked.

"At full press? Six hours to the narrows, if the bearing holds, if the boiler holds, if the river gives us the channel." Toma met his eyes. "Captain—I can promise you speed. I can't promise you arrival."

"I'm not asking for promises." Ada's voice was quiet. "I'm asking if you'll hate me for this."

Toma almost laughed. Thirty years of engineers hating captains, and this one asked permission.

"I'll hate you if we stop," he said. "Get us to the narrows."

* * *

They ran.

God help them, they ran.

Toma lived in the engine room's orange light while the world outside became a smear of green banks and white water. The needle on the pressure gauge trembled at 148 and he watched it the way other men watched a sleeping snake. The third bearing drank its tallow and graphite and sang a note he'd never heard from it before—a thin, rising whine above the knock, the sound of metal deciding, minute by minute, whether to forgive him.

At hour two the starboard gland packing began to weep, and he repacked it hot, wrapping his hands in wet rags while Junie fed the fire and Miriam read the numbers aloud in a clear clerk's voice, as steady as a lighthouse. At hour four the knock deepened, and Toma opened the drip-feeder another quarter-turn and whispered to the bearing like a man bargaining with God: *not yet, not yet, not yet.*

At hour five, Ruth's voice came down the speaking tube, dry as driftwood:

"Flood-wave's loose. Water's coming down the narrows ahead of schedule. Captain says give him everything."

Toma looked at the gauge. 152 pounds. Past anything the plate allowed, past anything the boiler had known in its long obedient life. He thought of the three dead engines of his career. He thought of a girl's village underwater on some surveyor's map, condemned in ink.

"Everything," he repeated, and opened the throttle to the stop.

The *Marrow's Grace* surged like a creature waking angry. The deck plates buzzed under Toma's boots. Steam shrieked from a joint he'd have to fix someday, if there was a someday, and the bearing's whine climbed toward a pitch that made his fillings ache—and held. Held. Held.

Above him, muffled through steel and timber, he heard the river rise to meet them, heard Ruth calling lines in a voice like a bell, heard the whole improbable crew of strays become one animal with a single purpose.

Two days had become one day. One day was becoming one hour.

And somewhere in the roar and the heat, with his hand on the throbbing iron and his chalk numbers climbing past sanity, Toma Vasiliev felt something he hadn't felt since the war ended: not fear, exactly, and not hope either, but the fierce, terrible joy of a man spending everything he has on the only bet that matters.

The bearing whined. The boiler groaned. The narrows opened ahead, white and waiting.

"Hold," Tama whispered to the heart in his hands. "Just hold."
