## 10. The Survivor

Ilsa Brandt had learned to count in small numbers.

One candle stub, half-burned. Two tins of condensed milk, hoarded past their expiration into something closer to glue than food. Three days since she'd last heard footsteps on the ledge outside — and those had been an animal's, or she'd told herself so. Eleven weeks since the *Meridian* went down with four of her colleagues aboard and her own conscience somewhere in the flooded dark below them.

She counted because counting kept her from listening. Listening was dangerous. Listening was how you heard things that weren't there, and worse, how you stopped hearing the things that were.

The cave mouth gave her a view of perhaps forty meters of the drowned canyon's eastern wall: black basalt streaked with mineral bloom, the river far below running green-gray and patient through a channel it had stolen from the world when the dam went in. She'd chosen this cave for its sightlines and its height above the waterline — six meters up a talus slope that no one climbed quickly or quietly. In eleven weeks no one had climbed it at all.

She almost didn't see them coming anyway.

It was the smoke that betrayed them first — a thin gray thread rising from the bend downstream where the old survey camp lay gutted, and Ilsa felt her whole body go cold and then hot, because that camp had been dead for two months and dead camps did not make smoke unless someone had come back to them. Then the voices, faint, carried up the canyon wall on the morning thermal the way sound traveled here, bouncing off stone like rumor off tongues.

She snuffed the candle with wet fingers and pressed herself to the rock beside the entrance, the case pulled against her chest, her arms wrapped around it the way she'd once held her daughter through thunderstorms. The case was cold. It was always cold. Brass corners, steel bands, government seals she'd broken and resealed twice with wax bought at terrible cost from a fisherman who hadn't asked questions because she'd paid him not to.

*They've come for it.* The thought arrived with total clarity, the way the worst thoughts did. *Vane found the camp. Vane found the trail. And now they're climbing.*

Footsteps on the talus. Not quiet ones — whoever it was moved like someone who had never been hunted, boots scraping loose stone, breathing loud enough that she could map his position by sound alone. Two of them. Maybe three. A woman's voice, low, saying something about handholds.

Ilsa's hand closed around the iron pry bar she'd kept within reach since week three, when she'd started hearing Vane's name in her sleep.

*You are a doctor,* she reminded herself. *You took oaths. You mapped a vein of ore worth more than every town on this river and you watched men die over it and you have eaten candle wax when the milk ran out, and if they take this case everything those people downstream are will be decided by men in an office who have never smelled this water.*

The shadow reached the cave mouth.

She swung before she could think better of it — a short, ugly arc, all shoulder and desperation — and the pry bar cracked against something solid. A man's forearm, raised too late. He grunted, staggered back onto the slope, and for one suspended moment the three of them regarded each other across a meter of nothing: Ilsa crouched in the dark like something the canyon had grown, wild-haired, twenty kilos lighter than the woman in the survey photographs, and beyond her a tall weathered man with river written all over him, and behind him a girl whose eyes went wide as coins.

"Dr. Brandt." The tall man said it quietly. Not a question. He'd raised his hands, palms out, and he did not come forward. "Miriam Sato sent us. Government clerk. She's down at the camp now."

"I don't know any Miriam Sato." Her voice came out rusted. When had she last spoken aloud? To the fisherman, maybe. Weeks.

"You do," he said. "You just don't know you do. She signed your expedition orders. She's the one who's been trying to find you since spring."

"Liar." The word cracked in half. "Nobody's been looking. Nobody looks. That's the whole point of what happened to us — nobody was ever supposed to look." She shifted the pry bar, keeping it between them, and her arm trembled with the effort of holding it up. Eleven weeks of rationed food lived in that tremble, and she hated it, hated that these strangers could read her condition off her body like a chart. "Vane has clerks too. Vane has people who say exactly that. *We're here to help you, Doctor. Just come down off the rocks.*"

The girl spoke then, from behind the tall man's shoulder. "We ran his steamer off our stern two days ago. He tried to buy our captain and then he threatened to sink us. If we were his people, would he have done that?"

Logic. Actual logic, delivered by someone young enough that Ilsa's chest ached to hear it. She didn't lower the bar. But she didn't swing again either.

"The arm," the tall man said. "Let me look at it. I'm Ada Eriksen. I captain the boat down there." He tilted his head toward the river, where — she craned despite herself — a battered paddle-steamer sat at anchor in the channel, patched hull, mismatched paint, smoke rising from a stack that had been repaired with what looked like hammered tin. It was the least impressive vessel she'd ever seen flying a charter pennant, and something in her unknotted slightly at the sight of it, because Corvo Vane did not send unimpressive vessels. Vane sent iron.

"My arm is fine."

"It's bleeding on my boot, Doctor."

She looked down. A thin line of red traced her forearm where the pry bar's rough edge had opened skin during the swing. She stared at it with distant surprise, the way she'd once stared at patients' wounds, professionally detached, and realized distantly how long it had been since she'd been the professional anything.

"Come inside slowly," she said finally. "Both of you. The girl stays where she is."

"She's my deckhand."

"The girl stays where she is, Captain Eriksen, or this conversation ends with one of us going off this slope, and I promise you it won't be me. I've had eleven weeks to learn every rock on it."

He studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw — and she knew what he saw, a starved animal guarding a box — seemed to satisfy some private arithmetic, because he nodded and stepped carefully over the threshold into the dimness, hands still visible, moving the way a man moves through a house where a dog has gone mad with grief.

* * *

Inside, the cave was hers the way a wound is yours.

She watched him take it in: the bedroll of woven reeds, the rain-catchment sheet angled beneath a ceiling seep, the wall where she'd scratched a calendar of eleven weeks' marks and then, further along, calculations she couldn't stop making even now — flow rates, tide windows, the depth of the channel below, all of it useless knowledge she'd carved into stone because her hands needed work or they'd start shaking. The case sat where she always kept it, between her body and the wall.

"You've been living on condensed milk and rainwater," Ada said. It wasn't a question either. He was reading the tins, stacked and emptied, forty-one of them arranged in rows with a precision that embarrassed her now that another human being stood inside it.

"There were fish, early on. Before the cold set in." She wrapped a strip of cloth around her forearm, tying it with her teeth. "What do you want, Captain? Say it plainly. I've got no patience left for the long way around anything."

"Miriam Sato wants the survey. The findings. What your team mapped." He crouched down across from her, unhurried, forearms on knees, and up close she could see the tiredness banked in him like coal — a man carrying something heavy for a long time. "And I want to know why four trained surveyors ended up dead in a canyon, and why the fifth one is living in a hole eating candle wax instead of walking out to any of thirty landings and telling her story."

"Because there's nowhere to tell it." The words came out of her before she could stop them, eleven weeks of silence splitting open like a seam under pressure. "Because the story ends with *and then everyone who heard it died*, Captain. Because the vein we found runs directly beneath nine towns, and depending on which office reads our report, those towns get bought out and flooded for the extraction works, or strip-mined down to their foundations, or — best case, the very best case — condemned as unsafe ground and evacuated with whatever compensation a budget committee sees fit to approve. There is no version of that document that doesn't end with thousands of people losing everything they have. And Corvo Vane knows it. And your government clerk knows it. And I am the only person alive who knows exactly what's in this case, and I will die on this rock before I hand it to anyone."

Silence. The river murmured far below, indifferent.

"That's not quite true," Ada said at last, gently. "Your team knew. Four of them are dead. Somebody else knows."

And there it was — the thing she'd spent eleven weeks not thinking about, arriving on schedule, the way it did every time she let her guard down. Ilsa closed her eyes.

"They didn't die of the river," she said. "That's what you want to ask. Everyone would want to ask. The *Meridian* didn't sink. She was scuttled — opened at the seams with a cutting torch while most of us slept at the upstream camp. Halvard and Osei and the twins went down with her because they were sleeping aboard." Her voice stayed level, clinical; she'd practiced this part, delivered it to the empty cave a hundred times, and the practice held, mostly. "Petra cut the hull. Petra and Deshi. They'd been in contact with Vane since the second month — I found the correspondence in Deshi's kit. Triple pay, salvage rights, a percentage of the extraction concession. All they had to do was lose the original survey and deliver a doctored one showing the vein running east, away from the towns, onto land Vane had already quietly optioned through three shell companies."

"And you?"

"I refused." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "I refused, and I made copies, and I hid them, and I told them I'd testify. So they decided I'd go down with the boat too. Except I wasn't aboard that night, because I'd gone up to check a benchmark station, and when I came back down at dawn the *Meridian* was a slick of diesel and debris and Petra was standing on the shore with a rifle, waiting for me to come into range." Her hands had begun to shake. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "We played cat and mouse through this canyon for six days, Captain. Six days. I watched Deshi fall from the rim on day four — whether he slipped or Petra helped him, I don't know, I only heard him go. On day six Petra cornered me at the falls and I put a rock against her head and I ran, and I have not stopped running since, and I don't expect you to understand what that makes me."

"A survivor," Ada said simply. "It makes you a survivor. This river's full of them. Most of us are less honest about what it cost."

She looked at him then, really looked, and saw that he meant it — saw the specific weight in him that recognized hers, the way one drowning man recognizes another across a wide stretch of water. It undid something in her that the fear hadn't touched. Her eyes stung and she turned her face away, furious at the weakness, furious at the kindness more.

"The case," she managed. "You want to know what's in the case."

"If you want to tell me."

"No one wants to tell me. Everyone wants to *take* it. That's the difference between you and everyone else, Captain, and I notice you haven't denied wanting it." She drew a breath, and made the decision she'd been circling for eleven weeks without knowing it — the decision to trust, not because trust was safe but because the alternative was dying alone in a hole with a secret no one would ever use. "Then I'll tell you. Not because you asked. Because I'm so tired, and because your boat is the first thing in three months that Vane hasn't owned."

She pulled the case into the thin light and broke her own careful reseal, wax cracking under her thumbnail.

"Stratigraphic cores. Spectrometer readings. The full bathymetric map of the pre-flood canyon — which, incidentally, your government already had and buried, because they commissioned our survey hoping we'd find nothing, and we found everything." She lifted out the top folio, pages soft with damp at the edges despite her care. "The Aldervik Vein. Copper, silver, and native gold in a single continuous lode running nine kilometers beneath the river towns — Marrow Bend, Tarn Hollow, Juniper Landing, all of them. Shallow enough to work with 1890s equipment. Rich enough to fund a war. Every report, every assay, every core sample logged and witnessed. And this—" she drew out a slimmer folder, oilcloth-wrapped, handled like something venomous, "—is the correspondence. Vane's letters to Petra and Deshi. Payment schedules. The shell company registrations. And a memo from the Ministry of Interior authorizing our expedition with instructions to find the ground *geologically unsuitable*. Signed before we ever launched. They sent us to bury it, Captain. We dug up a graveyard instead."

Ada was quiet for a long time. Outside, the girl's voice called down something to the camp, ordinary and alive, and the sound of it drifted up the slope like news from another country.

"Juniper Landing," he said finally. "That's where my deckhand's from."

"Then your deckhand's town is sitting on the richest ore body on the continent, and every powerful man on this river is about to decide what her family is worth per ton." Ilsa closed the folio. Her hands had steadied, strangely, now that the secret was out — as though the case had been heavier from the inside. "Now you understand why I couldn't give it to a clerk. Why I can't give it to anyone. Whoever holds this holds nine towns by the throat. I held it for eleven weeks and all it made me was a wild animal in a cave."

"Maybe that's not the only thing it can make you." Ada rose, joints cracking, and looked toward the cave mouth and the river beyond it with an expression she couldn't yet read. "There's a woman at the camp below who says she came to find you. Says her agency sent her. You'll want to hear what she has to say about that — and I suspect she'll want to hear what you just told me. Whether you believe her after is between the two of you."

"And if she's lying? If she's Vane's?"

"Then we're all in the same boat," Ada said, and there was something almost like humor in it, dry as riverbed bone. "Literally. Come down when you're ready, Doctor. We've got real food, and an engineer who'll want to argue about your tide calculations, and—" he paused at the threshold, silhouetted against the white sky, "—and nobody on board who'll ask you to hand over that case. Not even me. Though I won't pretend I'm not curious what a person decides to do with a thing like that, once she stops running."

He went down the talus carefully, testing each hold, and Ilsa sat alone in the cave with the open case in her lap and the wind coming up off the water, and for the first time in eleven weeks she let herself cry — briefly, furiously, the way you bleed from a wound you've finally allowed to be cleaned.

Then she repacked the folios, sealed the case, and began the climb down toward the smoke and the voices and the battered little boat, because staying on the rock had never actually been living, and she was done mistaking the two.

She was halfway down the slope when the girl appeared at the bottom, shading her eyes, and shouted up the canyon wall with all the force of her young lungs:

"Ma'am! Steam smoke on the water! Coming upriver fast!"

Ilsa froze, one hand on the stone, and looked downstream to where a smudge was gathering on the horizon — iron-colored, deliberate, and closing.

Corvo Vane had found her at last.
