## 11. What the Case Holds

Miriam Sato had spent eleven years learning how to lie with documents, and she had gotten very good at it. She could shade a figure until it meant the opposite of what it said. She could bury a finding in a footnote so dense that no one would ever dig it out. She could write a cover memorandum so bland that it made the eye slide off it like rain off oilcloth. These were the skills the Ministry of Interior Resources had hired her for, though the posting announcement had used gentler words: *records administration, senior grade.*

She sat in the cramped aft cabin of the *Kestrel's Wake* with the sealed case between her knees, and for the first time in eleven years she could not make the lie sit still.

The boat rocked. Above her, through the thin deck planking, she could hear the crew moving—Toma's boots on the engine housing, the rhythmic scrape of Junie's brush against the hull where the girl was chipping river mud from the seams, Ada's low voice at the wheel, though the boat was anchored and there was nothing to steer. He talked to the wheel the way other men talked to God. She had noticed that on the first day and had filed it away as a curiosity, the way she filed everything away, everything that wasn't a document, everything that wasn't her problem.

The case was iron-bound cedar, the kind used for survey instruments, with a lock that wanted a key Ilsa Brandt did not have. Brandt had carried it out of the flooded canyon on her back, half-dead, guarding it for—Miriam did the arithmetic and flinched—nine days. Nine days alone in the dark above the waterline, eating river mussels and whatever the cave walls wept, waiting for rescue or for the men who had killed her colleagues to come back and finish the work.

And now it sat in Miriam's lap, and the whole boat seemed to lean toward it, and she understood that whatever she did in the next hour would follow her the rest of her life. She had always assumed her life would be long and quiet and gray, a corridor of filing cabinets. She had not planned on this.

The hatch opened and Ada came down the ladder without asking, the way he did everything, as if permission were a formality the river had already granted. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a face like a riverbank—worn down, but you could read the strata if you looked. He looked now. At her. At the case.

"Brandt's asleep," he said. "Toma gave her something for the fever. She'll talk straighter in the morning, if she talks at all." He lowered himself onto the bench across from her, knees nearly touching the case. "But you already know what's in there. Don't you."

It wasn't a question. Miriam had watched him read the river for six days—reading water like scripture, Ruth called it—and she recognized the same attention turned on her now. He read people the way he read currents: not the surface, but the pull underneath.

"I have a hypothesis," she said.

Ada almost smiled. "Clerks."

"Analysts," she corrected, and heard how thin it sounded, and hated herself for the correction, and hated herself for the hating. "Captain, before I open this, I need to tell you something. Not because you've earned it. Because if I don't say it now, I never will, and you'll all be carrying my secret into whatever comes next without knowing its weight."

He folded his hands and waited. That was the thing about him, she was learning. He could wait like the river waited.

* * *

"I didn't hire you to find the survey team," Miriam said. "I told you the Ministry wanted them recovered. That was true in the narrowest sense. But the Ministry didn't send the survey to find anything. It sent them to lose something."

The lantern swung gently with the boat's motion, and shadows moved across the cabin like slow fish.

"Three years ago the Interior Resources office commissioned a mineral survey of the upper gorge and the drowned canyon. Officially: assessing post-flood geological stability for resettlement planning. That was the language in the charter. *Stability. Resettlement. Planning.*" She recited the words the way you'd recite a confession. "The real charter was verbal. I wrote the written one myself. I was told what to leave out, and I left it out, and I told myself it was because the funding would die if I didn't, and the funding mattered, and eleven towns downstream mattered, and someone had to keep the office running so that *someone* was watching. I was very good at telling myself things."

"What was left out?" Ada asked.

"The purpose. The survey wasn't meant to publish findings. It was meant to *contain* them. If the gorge was geologically worthless—which is what the office expected—then the resettlement plan would proceed. The plan that moves eleven river towns off the floodplain and onto government land. Land the government has already promised, in other documents I have also filed, to the mining consortiums. The same consortiums that fund Corvo Vane's salvage fleet." She watched his face for the flinch and saw it, small, around the eyes. "You begin to see the shape of it. If the survey found something valuable—"

"Then the towns don't get moved for safety," Ada said slowly. "They get moved for *access*. And the government doesn't have to pay relocation rates for a hazard. It pays extraction rates for a resource. Or it doesn't pay at all, because the land was condemned first."

"You read ledgers better than you let on."

"My brother kept our books." Something crossed his face and was gone, a current under ice. "Go on."

"The survey team was competent. That was the mistake. The office wanted competent enough to be credible and not so competent they'd ask questions. Dr. Brandt asked questions. And then, apparently, she answered them." Miriam looked down at the case. The iron binding was cold under her fingers. "They stopped transmitting eleven weeks ago. The office's position—my director's position, delivered to me in a meeting where the tea was better than it had any right to be—was that the team had gone rogue, possibly sold their findings to private interests, possibly Vane himself, and that recovery of government property was now a matter of fiscal integrity. That's the phrase. *Fiscal integrity.* I was sent to retrieve the survey's records before Vane's fleet reached them. Not to rescue anyone. The rescue was my idea. The money I paid you with is my own savings, and it is most of what I have, and when I get back I will almost certainly be dismissed, or worse."

Ada was quiet a long moment. The river murmured against the hull.

"Why tell me now?" he said at last. "You could've waited. Opened it, seen what's inside, decided what version of the truth we needed."

"Because I've been deciding what version of the truth people needed for eleven years," Miriam said, "and it turns out I'm not able to do it anymore. Something about watching a woman guard a box with her life for nine days. Something about a girl on your deck scrubbing hull seams who stowed away from one of the towns on the condemnation list." She hadn't meant to mention Junie; the words came out on their own, and she let them. "I keep a list, Captain. Every town, every household, every name on every condemnation notice that has crossed my desk. Four thousand one hundred and six names. I used to tell myself the list was just data. Now I've seen one of them. She has a scar on her chin from a fall she took when she was small, and she hums when she works, and she is on my list, and I signed the routing slip that started the process."

"You signed a routing slip," Ada said. "Not the order."

"I've been telling myself that for three years too." Her voice cracked and she let it, because she was done sanding the edges off things. "The order doesn't get signed without the slip, Captain. That's how it works. That's how it always works. Nobody pushes the whole weight; everybody pushes a little, and the little adds up to a canyon, and the canyon floods, and four thousand people are displaced so that men in clean offices can divide a vein of ore they'll never have to look at."

* * *

She opened the case with a cold chisel from Toma's kit and a mallet wrapped in cloth, because there was no key and no time to be gentle. The cedar split along the seam and the lid came up and the smell of oilcloth and machine grease rose out of it, and beneath that, faintly, the river.

Inside: three bound survey folios, wax-sealed. A leather roll of instruments. A strongbox of assay samples in glass vials, labeled in Brandt's small precise hand. And at the bottom, wrapped separately in oilcloth, a folder of correspondence that was not survey correspondence at all.

Miriam took the folios first and opened them on the bench, and the lantern light lay across the pages, and she read, and as she read the cabin seemed to grow colder.

The numbers were impossible. She knew the geology of the gorge the way she knew the filing system of her office—thoroughly, and without love—and the numbers Brandt's team had produced were an order of magnitude beyond anything the office's own consultants had projected. A copper-iron lode running the length of the drowned canyon and up under the gorge walls, high-grade, shallow, *accessible from the towns themselves*. The vein didn't run beneath empty wilderness. It ran beneath cellars and cisterns and church foundations and the buried roots of orchards older than the flood.

"They found it under them," Ada said, reading over her shoulder. His voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was steering through something dangerous. "Under the towns. All of them."

"Every one." Miriam turned a page. Cross-sections, elevation drawings, Brandt's annotations climbing the margins in a hand that grew less precise as the entries went on, as if the writing itself had been running. "The resettlement plan was never about the flood. The flood was the opportunity. Move the towns, condemn the land at hazard rates, and the consortium strips the gorge with open cuts. The survey was sent to confirm the vein was there. It was. It is. And then the team was supposed to come home and hand it to me, and I was supposed to file it, and the filing was supposed to be the end of it."

"But Brandt didn't file it."

"Brandt copied it." Miriam lifted the oilcloth folder from the bottom of the case, and her hands, which had never shaken over a document in eleven years, shook now. "And she took proof of something else with her. Look."

The folder held letters. Copies—flimsy, second-generation copies, the kind made on a scribe-press when no one was meant to know the original existed. Miriam read the first one and the floor of her professional world tilted.

They were letters between Vane's salvage company and the Deputy Director of her own office. Terms. Schedules. Payments, with amounts, with dates, with the dry courtesy of men who had done this before. The Deputy Director—her director's superior, the man who had approved her charter, the man whose signature sat at the bottom of her own commission—had sold the survey's route and schedule to Vane before the team ever left the capital. Vane's fleet hadn't been hunting the survey by accident. It had been hunting it by *appointment*.

And there, in the last letter, in a paragraph that Miriam read three times because her mind kept refusing it: an arrangement for the disposition of the findings. Vane would take the survey. The office would declare the team lost. The towns would be condemned on the original geological pretext, which the office's consultants had already prepared, and the extraction leases would be granted to a consortium that Vane controlled through three layers of holding companies, and the Deputy Director's retirement portfolio would be very comfortable indeed.

"They weren't going to bury the findings," Miriam said. Her voice came out strange, small, like someone else's. "They were going to bury the *team*. And then dig."

Ada took the letter from her hand and read it without expression, which somehow was worse than if he'd sworn. When he finished he set it down on the bench with great care, the way he did everything, as if even a piece of paper deserved the courtesy of not being thrown.

"How many copies of this exist?" he asked.

"Brandt made these. If she made others—" Miriam stopped. Thought. The analyst waking up inside her, the part of her that had kept her alive in offices full of knives. "If she made others, they're hidden, and she'll tell us where when she trusts us, and she doesn't yet, and she's right not to. This folder is the only proof I can hold in my hands. Which means that from this moment, everyone who wants this buried wants *us* buried with it. Not just Vane. The office. The Ministry. Men with warrants and men without them."

"And if you take it back to the capital," Ada said. "File it. Do it the proper way."

"Then it goes into a records system that the Deputy Director effectively controls, and it disappears, and so do I, and so does Brandt, and so does every one of you who helped me." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the sound a person makes when a wall they've been leaning on all their life turns out to be painted canvas. "Eleven years I believed in the filing system, Captain. The system is the crime. The filing *is* how they bury things. I know, because I'm the one who buries them."

* * *

She went up on deck after that, because the cabin had become too small to hold what she knew, and the night was wide and the river was there, black and moving, indifferent and honest. Honest was the thing about the river. It never pretended to be anything but what it was.

Junie was at the rail, wrapped in a coat too big for her, watching the water. She didn't look around when Miriam came to stand beside her. Up close, in the lantern light from the wheelhouse, Miriam could see the scar on the girl's chin, the one from the file, from the notice, from the routing slip with her own signature at the bottom.

"Your town," Miriam said. "Millbrook Landing. Population three hundred and forty."

Junie turned and looked at her, wary, the wariness of someone who had learned young that adults with documents were weather to be survived.

"You know it?"

"I know everything about it except what it looks like." Miriam kept her eyes on the river. "That's the trouble with my work. You learn the numbers and never the faces. Three hundred and forty. Forty-one households with children under ten. A schoolhouse. A ferry landing." She paused. "I signed the slip that started your town's condemnation. I didn't know I was signing it. That's not an excuse. It's just what happened. I signed a routing slip, and the slip became a notice, and the notice became a process, and the process would have become a barge at your landing and men with clipboards telling you that your home was now a resource."

Junie was quiet for a long time. The river ran.

"Ada said you might be trouble," the girl said at last. "He said clerks were trouble. But he also said you paid in real coin and asked real questions, and liars don't usually do both." She shifted, and her voice dropped. "Is it true? What's in the case? About the vein?"

"It's true. And it's worse than the vein." Miriam told her. Not all of it—not the letters, not yet, those belonged in front of the whole crew, in daylight, with Brandt awake to confirm—but the shape of it. The condemnation as pretext. The sale. The burial. Junie listened the way young people listen when they are learning that the world is larger and crueler than they'd guessed and that they are not, in fact, the center of its cruelty, only one of its targets.

When Miriam finished, Junie was silent, and then she said, "So what do we do?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Miriam had spent eleven years being the person who *processed* what other people decided to do. She had never once been the person who decided.

"I don't know," she said, and it was the most honest sentence she had spoken in a decade, and it felt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. "I know what I was sent to do. I know what my career requires. I know that the safe thing, the smart thing, the thing that keeps me employable and alive and gray, is to take this case and hand it to people who will make it vanish." She looked down at the girl, at the scar, at the humming girl on her list of four thousand one hundred and six. "And I find that I would rather be dismissed. I would rather be *hunted*. I would rather stand in front of a tribunal with nothing but these folios and my testimony and go down telling the truth than spend one more year being the hand that files the order."

"You'd lose everything," Junie said. Not accusing. Just checking, the way you check a knot.

"I've already lost it," Miriam said. "The moment Brandt opened her eyes in that cave and I saw what eleven weeks of being hunted does to a person, I lost it. Everything before that was just—inventory." She straightened, and to her own surprise her voice came out steady. "Here's what I know how to do, and it's not nothing. I know the office's procedures better than the men who abuse them. I know which warrants are real and which are theater. I know the Deputy Director's signature well enough to spot a forgery and his habits well enough to prove the letters are genuine. I know where the original condemnation files sit and how to get them copied before anyone knows they're gone. I can build a case, Captain. A real one. It won't be a routing slip. It'll be a hammer."

From the wheelhouse behind them came Ada's voice, low, talking to the wheel or to himself or to a brother ten years drowned, and then his footsteps on the deck, and then his shadow falling across them both.

"Then build it," he said.

Miriam turned. In the dark she could see the river in his eyes, moving.

"But hear me," he went on. "A hammer needs a hand it trusts. You walk into the capital waving those letters and you're a dead clerk with a dead crew behind her. You build it *here*, with us, with Brandt's testimony and Junie's town and every landing between here and the confluence, and you build it where Vane can't burn it." He nodded at the case. "That proof doesn't go back to your office. Not yet. Maybe not ever. It stays on this boat, with this crew, until we're somewhere it can't be buried."

"The boat can be sunk," Miriam said. "Vane has proven he'll try."

"Then we'd best not let him," said Ada, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before—not the weariness, not the grief, but the thing underneath them both, the thing he steered by.

Miriam looked at the case sitting on the deck between them, iron-bound and split, its secrets open to the river air, and she understood that she had already chosen. She had chosen somewhere back in the fog on the first morning, when she'd paid a stranger her savings to chase a lie she'd written herself. Everything since had only been catching up to it.

Below, faint through the deck, Ilsa Brandt cried out in her fever, a name—*Tomas, Tomas, the readings, take the readings*—and went quiet again.

And it was then that Junie, who had the sharpest eyes on the river and the least sense of when not to use them, went still at the rail and said, in a voice gone flat and young:

"Captain. There's a light on the water. Two lights. Running dark except when they think we can't see—and they're coming up the channel fast."
