## 12. Ambush at the Landing

Junie felt the landing before she saw it—a change in the way the deck hummed under her boots, the engine dropping from its fevered roar to a grumbling idle, Toma's voice carrying up from below: *fuel in twenty minutes, keep the line clear.* She had been scrubbing the rail for an hour, working at the rust stains with a wire brush until her knuckles burned, because Ada had said the boat needed it and because work was the only prayer she knew how to say.

Kessel Landing was not much of anything. A coal-blackened fuel dock jutting off a shelf of mud and gravel, two storage sheds with their paint gone gray as driftwood, a single lamp on a leaning pole that buzzed like something trapped inside it. Behind the sheds, hills rose up dark and treeless, terraced by old mining cuts that even the flood hadn't managed to erase. Junie knew those cuts. She'd grown up in the shadow of them, in a town called Bracken Hollow, forty miles downstream, where every man's lungs were half stone dust and every woman knew the sound of the pit whistle better than her own child's voice.

The survey papers in Miriam Sato's sealed case would decide whether Bracken Hollow stood or drowned. That was why Junie had stowed away in the first place, folded into the lifeboat canvas like a bad secret. It was why she scrubbed rails now instead of sleeping. The boat was not just a boat anymore. It was the only thing between her home and a decision made by men who had never smelled the Hollow's air.

"Fueling stop," Ruth said, coming up beside her at the rail. The old pilot's hands were wrapped around a tin cup of something steaming, and her eyes—pale as river ice—moved over the landing the way they moved over water. Reading it. "Twenty minutes. Nobody leaves the boat."

"Nobody said we were stopping here at all," Junie said.

"Nobody ever says. Rivers make you stop whether you like it or not." Ruth sipped. Her gaze snagged on the far end of the dock, where a tarp covered something long and low, and stayed there a beat too long. Then she moved away toward the wheelhouse without another word, and Junie told herself the old woman was just being careful, the way old pilots were careful about everything.

The fog was coming down off the hills in ropes. That should have been the first warning. Fog on the upper river came off the *water*, rising, not descending—and when it fell instead of climbed, it meant cold air moving fast behind it, pushing down the gorge like a hand closing. Junie had watched weather all her life from the tailings piles above Bracken Hollow. She knew what falling fog meant.

She went looking for Ada anyway, and found him amidships, checking the mooring lines with Miriam standing close, both of them bent over the case between them like it was an altar. Their voices were low. They stopped talking when she got near, which stung more than she wanted to admit—she was crew now, wasn't she? She'd bled for nothing yet, but she'd scrubbed and hauled and kept watch through three nights of tide, and still they talked over her head like she was cargo.

"Ruth thinks the fog's wrong," Junie said.

Ada looked up. He had a face built out of weather, all of it bad, but his eyes did a thing just then—flicked past her shoulder to the landing, to the tarp at the dock's end, back again. "Toma needs eight minutes to take fuel," he said. "We'll be gone in ten."

"That's not what I—"

"I heard you." He turned to Miriam. "Get below. Take the case."

And that was when the lamp on the leaning pole went out.

Not flickered. Went out, all at once, like a held breath released, and the darkness that came down over Kessel Landing was total and wet and full of sound—the sound of boots on planking, many boots, moving fast, and the flat iron clatter of something heavy dropped against the dock.

Junie's body understood before her mind did. She had grown up around pit bosses and company men; she knew what violence sounded like when it was organized. She grabbed the boathook off its brackets and turned toward the gangway just as shapes resolved out of the fog—four of them, five, six, big men in oilskins with cloth over the lower halves of their faces, and the one in front carried a boarding axe with the confidence of a man who had used it before.

"*Boarders!*" she screamed, and her voice cracked on it, high and girl-ish, and she hated herself for the crack even as she swung the boathook into the throat of the first man who set foot on the deck.

He went down gurgling. The hook tore free with a sound Junie would hear for the rest of her life, wet and wrong, and then everything happened at once.

* * *

Later—much later, lying on a bunk with her side packed in linen and her whole body one continuous scream—she would try to put the fight in order, and fail. Memory broke it into pieces, each one lit too bright:

Ada coming out of the wheelhouse doorway like a door slamming, a fire axe in both hands, meeting two men at once and driving them back step by step down the deck.

Miriam at the companionway with the case strapped across her chest, swinging a lantern by its handle in a wide desperate arc, glass bursting, flame licking up an attacker's sleeve before he beat it out and lunged through.

Ruth's voice from the wheelhouse, calm as Sunday: *"Lines cut. We're drifting."*

Toma bursting up from the engine hatch with a wrench in one fist and steam pouring off him, taking a knife slash along the forearm without a sound, putting the man down with a blow that Junie felt through the deck plates.

And herself—herself with the boathook, backing along the rail, keeping two of them away from the companionway, away from Miriam, away from the case, her arms burning, her breath sawing, thinking with a strange floating clarity: *this is what it costs, this is the price, pay it.*

One of them feinted left and came right. She was too slow. The boarding axe caught the boathook midshaft and snapped it, and the backswing took her across the ribs—not the edge, thank God, the flat, but a flat swung by a strong man is still a train—and the world went white and sideways and she was on her knees on the deck with blood in her mouth and no memory of falling.

Through the ringing in her skull she heard the other sound. The one underneath the shouting. A rhythmic knocking from below the waterline, hollow and getting louder, and she understood it the way you understand things in nightmares: they weren't just here for the case. Someone had gone under the dock with tools. Someone was drilling holes in her—*their*—boat.

"They're holing us!" she shouted, and her voice came out broken and bloody and true. "Under the hull—they're sinking us!"

Ada heard. Whatever else the night took from him, he heard that. He roared something wordless and drove the last boarder back over the rail with the flat of the axe, then turned and dove down the companionway toward the bilge, and Junie, kneeling in her own blood, watched the remaining attackers retreat down the gangway into the fog like smoke pulled backward into a chimney.

It was over in less time than it took to tell. That was the obscene part. All of it—her ribs, the man's throat, the holes in the hull—had cost maybe ninety seconds of the world's attention.

* * *

They found the drill marks at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the fog: six punctures along the starboard side below the waterline, clean-bored, plugged now with tapered softwood pegs and slathered pitch, Toma's emergency surgery performed by lamplight while the river tried to come in. Two of the plugs wept anyway. Thin threads of water crept along the bilge stringers and gathered in the pump well, and the pump ran constantly, a patient mechanical heartbeat that everyone aboard pretended not to listen to.

"We've lost the lead," Toma said. He sat on an overturned crate with his arm bandaged to the elbow, gray-faced, refusing to lie down. "Patched hull can't take full pressure. I have to run her at half steam or the plugs pop. Vane's steamer will round the bend by tomorrow's tide, and we'll be doing six knots against his twelve."

Nobody argued. There was nothing to argue with. The arithmetic was the arithmetic.

Junie sat propped against the wheelhouse bulkhead because lying down made her ribs grind like millstones. The ship's doctor was a book, and the book said *bind the chest, rest, avoid shock*, and so Miriam had bound her chest with strips of sailcloth and given her willow bark tea that tasted like punishment, and now Junie sat and hurt and listened to the pump and hated.

Not the men who had hit her—she found, to her surprise, that she couldn't quite hate them. They were paid men, pit men probably, men like her father, swinging axes for someone else's money. What she hated was bigger and colder than that. She hated the shape of the world that had sent them. The same shape that had always sent them: the company men at Bracken Hollow, the assayers, the men with clipboards who walked through town writing down numbers that decided who ate. Somewhere upstream, Corvo Vane was spending coin like water to put holes in boats and hooks in children, all to own a piece of paper that would let him own the river, and the towns, and her mother's house, and the churchyard where her father was buried under a stone that read *KILLED IN THE PIT, AGED 34.*

"Hey." Miriam crouched beside her, careful of the ribs. Up close the clerk looked older than her years, smudged under the eyes, the case still strapped to her body like a second spine. "You saved the companionway. If they'd gotten below—" She stopped. Started again. "You saved it."

"I screamed and flailed with a stick."

"You held two armed men off a doorway for ninety seconds with a boathook. I counted." Miriam's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I'm a government clerk. Counting is my whole profession. Trust me."

Junie looked out at the fog-bound river. The pump thumped. Somewhere below, Toma was already rebuilding, already planning, because that was what Toma did with fear—he fed it to the engine. And Ada stood at the bow rail with his back to all of them, watching the water like it owed him an answer.

"It costs," Junie said quietly. Not really to Miriam. To the river, maybe. "Everyone talks about surviving like it's free. Like you just *get through*. But it costs. Every time. You pay and pay and nobody gives it back."

"No," Miriam agreed. "They don't give it back." She settled onto the deck beside Junie, shoulder to shoulder, careful of the ribs. "But you know what I figured out? The people who never pay anything don't get to be anyone. They just get to be *safe*, and safe is the smallest room in the world." She touched the case at her chest. "I lived in that room for eleven years. You paid ninety seconds' worth today, and you're already more out of it than I ever was."

Junie didn't answer. But somewhere under the pain, under the fury, something shifted its weight and lay back down—some small animal part of her that had been braced since Bracken Hollow, since the lifeboat canvas, since the first night she'd lain awake listening to strangers breathe and wondered if she'd made the worst mistake of her life. She had paid. She was paying. And the boat was still going upstream.

That mattered. She held onto it like a rail in rough water.

* * *

Near noon the fog lifted just enough to show them the gorge walls, and Ruth came down from the wheelhouse wearing the expression of a woman who has read a letter she didn't like.

"Vane's not behind us," she said.

Every head turned. "What?" Toma said. "He has to be. The math—"

"The math is fine. Your math assumes he wants to catch us on open water." Ruth's pale eyes swept them all. "I've been listening. No smoke on the horizon, no whistle, no wake. He's not chasing." She paused, and the pause was worse than any threat. "He's ahead. Has been since the landing, I'd guess. Those boarders weren't trying to take the case—they were trying to slow us. Hole the hull, cost us a day, let him run upriver clean and pick the ground where he meets us." She spat over the rail. "Man doesn't chase what he can ambush. He's choosing his spot."

Silence, except the pump. Thump. Thump. Thump. The boat's wounded heart.

Junie looked at Ada. He hadn't moved from the bow rail, but his shoulders had changed—set lower, squared, the way a man sets himself when the weight arrives and he decides to carry it rather than put it down. She was learning to read him the way Ruth read water. Small signs. Big meanings.

"How much lead does he have?" Ada asked.

"At half steam?" Ruth calculated with her eyes on the current, lips moving. "A tide. Maybe less."

"A tide." Ada turned. His face was still all bad weather, but underneath it, for the first time since Junie had known him, something was burning. "Then we don't take the main channel. Toma—how's the hull at quarter pressure?"

Toma blinked. "Quarter? She'd hold. Why—"

"Because Ruth knows a passage through the gorge that isn't on any chart," Ada said, "and I'm asking if the boat can survive it."

Ruth went very still. "That passage hasn't been run since before the flood. Half of it's underwater now. Nobody knows what the water's done in there."

"Vane doesn't either," Ada said. "That's the point."

Junie pressed her palm flat against her bound ribs, against the deep hot ache, and thought about her mother's house, and the churchyard, and the pit whistle, and the paper in the case that would decide all of it. Ninety seconds had bought her a lifetime of pain and she'd pay it again tomorrow without blinking.

"Do it," she said.

Everyone looked at her. She stared back, sixteen years old, bleeding, furious, certain.

"Whatever it costs," Junie said. "Pay it."
