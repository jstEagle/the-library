## 13. The Wreck Below

The canyon announced itself before they saw it.

Ada felt it first in the wheel—a tremor through the spokes, the current below them turning strange and layered, like a river trying to remember two different courses at once. Then came the sound: a low, hollow resonance rising out of the water itself, the deep-throated hum of current pouring over drowned stone. The old charts called this reach the Sorrow Narrows. The boatmen who worked it had another name for it, one they only said after dark and never twice.

The flooded canyon opened ahead of them like a wound that had healed wrong.

Where the river should have narrowed between granite shoulders, it spread instead into a wide, still sheet of green-black water, smooth as poured glass, hiding everything beneath it. Ten years ago the government's engineers had raised the dam downstream and let the water climb the canyon walls, drowning the rapids, the fishing camps, the ferry landing—drowning all of it under forty feet of cold, patient silence. Ada remembered standing on a bluff above the rising water with his brother's hat in his hands, watching the last dry stone disappear.

He had not come back since.

"Depth," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended.

Toma read the lead line and frowned at it. "Forty-one feet on the channel. Forty-three." He looked up. "That's deep for salvage work, Captain. That's deep for anything."

"It's where their baseboat went down." Miriam stood at the rail with the survey notes open against her chest, though she wasn't reading them. She'd been staring at the water for an hour with the expression of someone approaching a grave. "Brandt's logs put the sinking here. The last coordinates she recorded are inside this reach."

"And Vane?"

Ruth spat over the side, an old habit that meant she was thinking. "His steamer draws too much to come in close. He'll anchor off the mouth and send divers down in a cage, slow as church. We've got a day on him, maybe less."

"A day," Junie repeated. She was coiling line near the bow, moving carefully; her ribs were still strapped from the ambush at the fueling stop, and every motion cost her something she tried not to show. "Then we dive fast and get out faster."

Ada brought the *Maren* in slow, easing her toward the eastern wall where the water ran deepest and the current was kindest. The canyon rose around them now, sheer walls streaked white with mineral stain, their tops lost in morning haze. The water below was so clear in places that he could see the ghost shapes of drowned trees still standing, their branches reaching up like hands that had given up waving.

He cut the engine. The silence that followed was enormous.

* * *

They dove in shifts, because Toma insisted and because Ada knew he was right. Cold water at depth took a man's judgment before it took his breath, and they had exactly one suit between four divers, patched twice and smelling of vulcanized rubber and old fear.

Toma went first, working the grid they'd laid from Brandt's coordinates, paying out the guide rope hand over hand while Ruth watched the bubbles and called his drift. He came up after twenty minutes blue-lipped and shaking his head. "Debris field. Timbers, plating, a stove door. It's scattered—the baseboat broke apart when she went down. But there's a hull section down there, maybe thirty feet of her, sitting upright on the shelf. I saw brass fittings. Could be the survey locker."

Miriam's breath caught. "The instruments would be in the locker. The master plates."

"Could be," Toma said again, and rubbed feeling back into his hands. "Water's black down there past thirty feet. I had the lamp going and it was like reading by a match in a cellar."

Junie wanted to go next. Ada told her no, and she argued, and he let her argue until she ran out of argument, which didn't take long with her ribs. Ruth went instead, slow and methodical, an old woman's patience in the water, and came up with a surveyor's tripod clutched in one fist like a trophy. The brass was green with ten years of river. Stenciled on the leg, half worn away: *G.S.S. MERIDIAN — SURVEY DIVISION.*

"That's them," Miriam whispered. She held the tripod across her knees like a sleeping child. "This is their camp. They were right here."

Ada went down fourth.

* * *

The cold hit him through the suit like a hand closing around his chest. He equalized, checked his air, and followed the guide rope down into the green dark, the lamp on his helmet cutting a trembling cone through water thick with suspended silt. His own breath roared in his ears—in, out, in out—the loudest thing in the world, louder than the river, louder than thought.

At thirty feet the light began to fail. At thirty-five he switched on the lamp and the drowned world assembled itself around him out of nothing: the canyon floor, silted pale gray, strewn with the bones of trees. A fish—some blind-pale thing that had never seen sun—slid away from his light without hurry. And ahead, on the shelf where Toma had marked it, the broken hull of the *Meridian*'s baseboat lay like a rib cage picked clean.

He swam to it.

Up close the wreck was worse than bones. The hull had been torn open along its length—not holed, *opened*, the plating peeled back in a way that spoke of explosion rather than grounding. Ada ran his gloved hand along the torn edge and felt the metal curled outward. Something had gone off inside her. Something before the water ever touched her.

*They found something worth killing for,* he thought, and the thought sank through him colder than the water did.

The survey locker was amidships, half buried in silt, its brass fittings furred green. He dug with both hands, working blind more than not, and the lid came free with a groan of corroded hinges. Inside: mud, and the shape of things beneath the mud. He cleared it away and found the instrument cases, cracked but sealed, and beneath them a flat tin box lashed shut with wire—the kind of box built to keep paper dry in any weather a river could offer.

He lifted it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

And then the current shifted.

It happened the way it always happened in drowned canyons—the deep water turning over, some pressure far below letting go—and the silt clouded up around him in a blinding brown bloom. Ada hung on the guide rope, blind, breathing hard, waiting for it to settle. And as the murmur of the current faded and his lamp carved a weak circle through the thinning cloud, he saw what the shifting water had uncovered beside the wreck.

A hull. Another hull.

Smaller than the *Meridian*. Wooden, not iron. A shallow-draft workboat with a squared stern and a wheelhouse whose windows had long since gone to soft green glass. Painted letters showed faintly through the silt film on the transom, and Ada's heart stopped knowing them before his mind did, the way you know a face in a crowd before you know why.

*MAREN II.*

His brother's ferry.

* * *

He didn't remember crossing the distance. One moment he was hanging on the rope with the survey case in his grip; the next he was kneeling on the canyon floor beside the wreck of the little ferry, his lamp held close, his breath coming too fast, too loud, the gauge needle already sliding. The *Maren II*. He had helped build her—he and Eirik both, three winters of evenings in the boathouse, arguing about the curve of the stern boards. Eirik had named her after their mother. Eirik had run her on this same channel, back when it was a river and not a grave, carrying mail and freight and once, memorably, a prize hog to the county fair.

Ten years ago she had gone down in the spring flood with Eirik at the wheel, and they had never found her. Never found him. The river had taken the boat whole and given back nothing—not a plank, not a boot, not a body to bury. Ada had spent ten years telling himself the flood had swept her downstream, out into the lowland channels, miles away, anywhere but here.

She hadn't gone anywhere. She had been here the whole time, forty feet down, in the dark, in the cold, in the same channel where the survey team had moored their baseboat and found whatever it was they'd found. The river had kept him. All these years, the river had kept his brother's bones, and Ada had been running up and down this water measuring his life in miles, and his brother had been underneath him the entire time.

The lamp flickered. Or his vision did. He couldn't tell.

He reached out and put his palm flat against the hull, and the wood was soft with rot, giving slightly under his glove like skin. Through the broken wheelhouse window he could see the wheel itself, still mounted, still turned hard to port—the last thing Eirik had done, fighting for the bank, fighting for the channel, fighting the same water Ada had fought his whole life and lost to, once, in the worst way a man can lose.

"Eirik," he said, and the word left him in a burst of bubbles, meaningless, enormous.

The gauge said nine minutes of air. He had come down for a tin box and he was kneeling on a grave, and somewhere above him Ruth was watching his bubble line and starting to count.

He should have gone up. Every part of him that was captain, that was survivor, that was the man who had kept himself alive for ten years by never looking down—that man said *go up, go up now.*

Instead he pulled himself along the hull to the wheelhouse opening, and he went inside his brother's last room.

* * *

It was small in there. Smaller than he remembered building it. The bench seats had rotted to lace. A lantern hung from its hook, green with age. The wheel stood at its post, and beside the wheel, wedged in the corner where a man would put it so it wouldn't slide in rough water, was a sea chest—Eirik's sea chest, iron-banded, the lid warped but shut.

Ada got his fingers under the lid and pulled. The wood parted with a sigh, releasing a small avalanche of silt, and inside, perfectly preserved in the cold and the dark, lay the ordinary archaeology of his brother's life: a wool coat folded with care. A tin cup. A deck of cards rubber-banded together. A ledger book swollen fat with water, its pages fused to brick. And on top of it all, settled in the corner as if it had rolled there in the last terrible minute and stayed—

A compass. Brass cased, glass cracked, the needle frozen at a bearing that pointed nowhere anymore. Their father's compass, handed to Eirik the day he got his pilot's papers, the one Ada had secretly always believed should have been handed to him.

He took it. He didn't decide to take it; his hand simply closed around it, the way a drowning man's hand closes around anything.

The gauge said six minutes. The silt outside the window was stirring again, the current turning over, and through the murk he could see—could have sworn he saw—the pale shape of the channel stretching away downstream, deeper, toward the dam, toward the drowned town, toward everything the water had swallowed and kept. Eirik was here. Eirik had been here. There was no one to tell, no one to bring home, no wrong to undo. The river had finished its work a decade ago and had simply been waiting, patient as silt, for Ada to stop running long enough to look down.

His chest was tight. Not the cold this time. Something behind the cold, something cracking.

He surfaced into noise and daylight and Ruth's voice saying his name in the flat tone she used for counting, and Toma hauling the rope, and Junie leaning so far over the rail that Miriam had her by the belt. Hands pulled him aboard. Someone stripped the helmet off. The sky was shockingly bright, absurdly blue, and Ada lay on the deck of the *Maren* with the compass locked in his fist and shook, silently, the way a man shakes when a fever breaks or when something in him finally does.

Nobody asked. That was the mercy of the crew he'd gathered—they knew what it looked like when a man met the river's dead, and they gave him the deck and the silence to do it in.

* * *

Later, when the light had gone long and gold across the canyon walls, they gathered in the wheelhouse around the tin box. Toma had cut the wire. Miriam's hands hovered over the lid without touching it, the way you approach a thing that might be a cure or a curse.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as the day they were sealed: survey plates. Dozens of them, negatives of the canyon's geology, annotated in a fine draftsman's hand. And beneath the plates, a bound report, its cover stamped with the government seal Miriam served, its pages listing assays, tonnage estimates, projected yields—and, in the appendix, a table of towns. Names Ada knew. Names Junie knew. Each with a column beside it, filled or empty, and the meaning of those columns sat in the wheelhouse with them like a third passenger.

"The vein runs under all of it," Miriam said quietly. "Under the towns. Under the channel." She looked up at Ada, and her eyes went to his fist, to the compass he still hadn't set down. "Captain—what did you find down there? You were down four minutes past your mark."

Ada opened his hand. The brass compass lay in his palm, needle swinging slowly, finding some bearing none of them could follow.

"My brother," he said.

No one spoke. Outside, the evening wind moved down the canyon and stirred the green water, and far off—so far it might have been imagination, might have been the drowned trees knocking together in the current—came the sound of an engine. Deep-throated. Iron-hulled. Running against the tide.

Ruth went to the window and stood there a long moment, listening the way she listened, with her whole body.

"He's coming," she said. "Vane's coming. And we've still got the case, and he knows it."
