## 14. Brother Water

The drowned canyon did not look like a place where men had died. It looked like a cathedral.

Limestone walls rose sheer on either side of the channel, streaked with mineral bloom—rust and copper and a green so deep it seemed lit from within—and the water between them was the color of old glass, still enough that Ada could see forty feet down before the light gave out. The survey's baseboat lay somewhere in that dark, folded around a rock shelf like a bird that had flown into a window at speed. Toma's sounding line had found it an hour after dawn: thirty-one fathoms, give or take, resting on a ledge where the current couldn't quite reach it.

Thirty-one fathoms was too deep. Ada knew it the way he knew weather coming. The air hoses they'd salvaged from the wreck camp would buy a man maybe six minutes at that depth, and the cold would take half his thinking before he ever touched bottom. Ruth had said as much over breakfast, not unkindly, the way she said everything. "River keeps what it takes, boy. You want to go robbing graves, bring a priest."

But it wasn't only the baseboat down there.

Toma's line had snagged twice more on the way up—a second shape, smaller, sitting in the silt off the channel's eastern wall. When they'd hauled the grapnel close enough for the glass-bottom box to show them anything at all, Junie had gone quiet in a way Ada had never heard from her, and Miriam had made a small sound behind her hand, and Ada had looked down through thirty fathoms of green water at the broken spine of a river ferry he would have known anywhere in the world, even stripped to its ribs, even drowned ten years.

*The Kestrel.* His father's boat. His brother's last command. And for one hour on a night in the spring flood ten years ago, *his* wheel.

He hadn't spoken since. He knew he hadn't because his throat had been closed the whole time, and no one had asked him anything, which meant the crew had watched him go silent and decided, together, without a word among themselves, to let him be. That was the kind of crew he had somehow collected. Strays who could read a silence better than most pilots read water.

Now it was late afternoon, the light going amber along the canyon rim, and Ada stood on the foredeck in his diving dress while Toma checked the valves for the third time and pretended not to be checking them for the third time.

"You don't have to," Toma said finally. "Ilsa says the case is almost certainly aboard the baseboat. I can run the line. Junie can run the line. Anybody can run the line."

"It's my boat we're standing on."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know what you mean." Ada rolled his shoulders inside the canvas suit. The weight of the breastplate settled across him like a hand. "Nobody else goes down there. Not to that hull."

"Ada—"

"Toma." He made himself look at the engineer, made his voice come out level. "You've lost people. You know there's a door you walk through or you don't. I walked past mine every day for ten years. Today the door's open. If I don't go through it now, I never will."

Toma held his gaze a long moment. Then he nodded once, the way he nodded when a weld held under pressure, and turned to the air pump without another word, because that was Toma's whole language: *understood, proceeding.*

Ruth came up from the pilothouse and stood at the rail beside him. She didn't offer luck or scripture or any of the small superstitions river people traded like coin. She just put her old brown hand flat against the breastplate, over his heart, and left it there for three breaths, and then took it away.

"The current turns at slack water," she said. "You'll feel it let you go about the time you're ready to come up. Listen for it."

"And if I don't come up?"

"Then I'll dive after you myself, and I'm seventy-one years old, so think about how that'll look in the telling." She turned back toward the wheel. "Go on, boy. The river's been waiting to talk to you longer than I have."

* * *

The water took him the way cold always does—at first like a blow, then like an embrace he hadn't asked for.

Six fathoms down the amber light went gray. Twelve fathoms and it went green. By twenty he was descending through a color that had no name, holding the shot line with both mittened hands, watching his own bubbles rise past his faceplate in silver strings and wondering distantly if each one was a thought leaving him. The pressure squeezed the suit against his legs and arms. His ears had popped twice on the way down and now ached with a deep, drumlike fullness. Somewhere far above, muffled through thirty fathoms and a hull and a lifetime, Toma's air pump thumped its slow heartbeat.

And below him, resolving out of the green like something surfacing from sleep rather than sinking into it, lay the *Kestrel*.

She lay on her port side on the ledge, half-buried in silt, her starboard rail pointing upriver like a finger giving directions to nowhere. Ten years of river had dressed her in weed and rust and a soft grey fur of silt, but the lines were still her lines—the sweet sheer of his father's design, the wheelhouse with its windows gone, the name board hanging by one bolt and swinging gently, gently, in a current too faint to feel but strong enough to move a dead boat's name back and forth, back and forth, like a metronome counting out a decade.

*KESTREL*, the board said, in paint his mother had mixed herself from lampblack and linseed.

Ada stopped on the line three fathoms above her and hung there in the cold, breathing the tin-and-rubber taste of compressed air, and let himself look.

He had imagined this a thousand times and never once seen it clearly. In his dreams the wreck was always burning, or whole, or empty of meaning—his mind refusing, decade after decade, to render the actual image. Now here it was, ordinary and terrible. A boat. A sunk boat. Wood gone soft, iron gone to lace. Fish moved through her wheelhouse window openings with the unhurried indifference of tenants who had long since stopped expecting the landlord.

He had been at the wheel.

That thought arrived the way it always arrived—not gradually but all at once, complete, the whole night reassembling itself in his chest in the space between two breaths.

Spring flood. The *Kestrel* running mail and freight up to the gorge landings because the regular boats were all contracted and his brother had said yes to the job the way Eriksen men said yes to work—with their hands already moving. Alder at the engine telegraph, laughing, twenty-six years old and immortal. Their father ashore with his bad hip, trusting his sons with his boat and his route and, though none of them said it, his whole life's meaning. Night coming down hard on the water. Fog off the tributary mouths. And Ada—twenty-two, licensed eight months, hungry to prove the license wasn't paper—at the wheel, because Alder had said *take her a stretch, little brother, learn the flood while I watch.*

He had taken her a stretch. He had learned the flood.

The eddy off Miller's Point had set wrong that year—the whole channel shifted by the high water, the way channels do, the way the river quietly redraws its own map every spring and dares you to keep up. Every pilot on the river knew this. Every pilot respected it. Ada had known it too, in the part of his mind that knew things. But the other part—that young, that eager, that desperate to make his brother say *well steered*—had carried the summer channel in his head instead, and when the fog closed and the point came at them out of the dark, he had put the wheel over for water that wasn't there anymore.

The rock hadn't been new. The rock had probably sat under that eddy for ten thousand years, patient as stone is, waiting for exactly one young man on exactly one night to steer by an old map.

Alder had been in the engine room when she hit. That was the fact Ada had built his entire life around avoiding: Alder never had a chance to reach the deck. The hull opened along the starboard garboard strake, the river came in the way rivers do—all at once, total, without negotiation—and the *Kestrel* went down off the point in four minutes with the fog swallowing her whistle, and Ada stood on the upturned starboard rail in the freezing water and screamed his brother's name into the fog until dawn, and they never found him, and the river kept him, and Ada had spent ten years steering everything—boats, debts, grief, himself—away from that point on the map.

Not once back to Miller's landing. Not once up the gorge in flood season. Not once saying his brother's name aloud where anyone could hear it break.

And now here he hung, thirty fathoms down, ten years and two hundred miles from that night, staring at the same river's work in a different canyon, and understood with a clarity that felt almost like cruelty: he had not come up this river for the survey team. He had not come for Miriam's coin or Junie's town or even, if he was honest in the deep honest place, for redemption. He had come because some part of him had known, from the moment Toma's line snagged, that the river had been keeping both his dead—one in the baseboat's hold, one in the *Kestrel*'s ribs—and that a man cannot keep running a river that holds his brother without the river eventually collecting the fare.

His chest was tight. He noted it distantly, professionally, the way he'd note a fouled propeller. Too fast. Too shallow. The gauge on his breastplate showed pressure fine, air flowing fine, everything mechanical fine—it was only the man inside the machine who was coming apart at thirty fathoms in the dark.

*Alder,* he thought, and the name went out of him through the faceplate like heat through glass.

*I was at the wheel.*

Ten years he had arranged the story every other way. Bad luck. Bad chart. Bad night. The fog, the flood, the rock, God. Anyone's fault but the hands on the wheel, because the hands on the wheel were his, and if they were guilty then everything after—the drinking year, the debt, the slow hollowing-out, the decade of measuring life in miles because miles didn't ask questions—if all of that was not misfortune but penance, self-imposed, endless, and entirely his to end.

The bubbles rose. The pump thumped. The name board swung.

Ada Eriksen, alone in the green dark with his brother's grave, did the thing he had not done in ten years.

He forgave the pilot.

It didn't come as words. It came as a loosening—physical, actual, a release in the chest and throat like a knot cut. Because he saw it suddenly from outside himself, from the height of these ten hard years, from the wheel of his own command with his own stray crew sleeping below decks trusting his hands: he saw a twenty-two-year-old boy in fog doing his absolute best with an old map, and he saw that boy spend the rest of his life paying for four minutes, and he thought—*if Junie had been at that wheel, if Toma, if any soul I love had made that mistake in that fog—I would tell them the truth. I would tell them the river shifts its channels and the fog comes down and sometimes the best hands in the world are on the wheel when it happens, and it is not a verdict on their souls.*

He had never once said it to himself. He said it now, silently, in the water, in the language he trusted most:

*Well steered, little brother. You did your best. Come home.*

His vision blurred inside the faceplate and he realized distantly that he was crying at thirty fathoms, which was absurd, which was impossible to distinguish from the surrounding ocean anyway, and he let it happen, let the grief move through him like a freshet through a sluice, violent and cleansing and finite. His breathing ragged. The gauge needle trembled. Somewhere in his body an old alarm began to ring—too fast, too shallow, too deep, too long—and he made himself grip the shot line and count, slow, the way Ruth had taught him to count through panic in the gorge narrows, and slowly, slowly, the alarm faded and there was only the water, and the wreck, and the strange enormous peace of a man who has finally set down a weight he forgot he was carrying.

Then he swam down to the *Kestrel*.

* * *

The wheelhouse was a cave of silt and shadow. He pulled himself through the window opening feet-first, careful of the rotten frame, and stood—or rather floated, boots finding the deck with a soft silty kiss—in the place where he had last stood alive with his brother nearby. The wheel itself was gone, torn away in the sinking or salvaged after; only the pedestal remained, a stub of pipe wearing ten years of moss. He put his glove on it anyway.

"Alder," he said aloud, and heard his own voice boom strangely in the helmet, huge and small at once. "I'm taking something. You'd have let me."

The cabin below was where the river kept its gifts. He worked his way down through the collapsed companionway, along the port side where the deck sloped into the silt, feeling rather than seeing, his lamp a weak gold coin of light in the blackness. Bunk frames. A stove, tipped. And there, wedged beneath the fallen drawer of the chart table where the current had washed everything loose except what had lodged tight—

A brass housing, crusted green, heavy as a fist.

He worked it free with both hands, heart hammering, and brought it up to the lamp, and rubbed the crust away with his thumb, and the letters came up out of the green like faces out of fog:

*STANMOOR & SONS — BRISTOL.*

His father's compass. Alder's compass—the one their father had given him at sixteen with both hands and the words *a pilot who won't ask the water where it's going will follow it somewhere bad.* Ada had assumed it went down with Alder, or been sold in the bad year, or lost. It had been here the whole time. Ten years, thirty fathoms, four miles of silt from where anyone had thought to look—and the river had kept it safe the way the river keeps things, patiently, without sentiment, until the one person it was meant for came back down into the dark to collect.

Ada held it against his breastplate, over his heart, the way Ruth had touched him on the deck, and he felt the weight of it go through the canvas and the wool and the skin and the bone, all the way in.

"I've got it," he told his brother. "I've got it, Alder. Go on, now. Rest."

The current turned. He felt it exactly as she'd promised—a softening, a letting-go, the faint pull of the river changing its mind—and he knew without looking at any gauge that his time was spent and his air was spending faster than it should and the surface was a very long way up. He kicked free of the wheelhouse, past the swinging nameboard, and caught the shot line, and began the long climb toward the light with his brother's compass lashed inside his suit and his brother's forgiveness carried somewhere no gauge could measure.

Halfway up, the cold and the depth and the emotion collected their tax all at once. The green dimmed. The pump's thump seemed to recede. His hands went clumsy on the line and a grey static crept in at the edges of his mind, and for one long suspended moment the river offered him the other door—the easy one, the warm one, the one where a tired man simply stops kicking and lets the water finish its long conversation.

Ada looked at that door. And chose the other one.

He kicked. Again. Again. Counting, climbing, furious and alive, hauling himself up the line hand over hand through the gathering dark, and the green grew pale, and the pale grew silver, and then his helmet broke the surface into the roaring ordinary evening air and the sky was going bruise-colored over the canyon rim and Toma and Junie were hauling on the lifeline and shouting his name, and Ada Eriksen came up over the rail of his own boat coughing and laughing and crying, all three at once, alive on purpose.

They got the helmet off and he sat on the deck planks dripping, compass in his bare hands, and Junie crouched in front of him with her eyes wide and wet and asked nothing, and Miriam wrapped a blanket around his shoulders like he was something worth keeping, and Ruth stood at the wheel and said only, "Told you she'd talk."

Ada looked down at the compass in his palms, the needle swinging, settling, finding north the way it always had, the way his brother's hands always had, steady and true.

"We're taking her home someday," he said hoarsely. "Miller's Point. I'm going to stand on that bank and say goodbye properly. But not yet." He closed his fingers around the brass. "Because we're not done—"

Junie's shout cut him off. She was at the stern rail, glass raised, and her voice came back flat and fast as a slap:

"Smokey astern! Steamer, making smoke—she's big, Ada, she's *big*—and she's coming up the channel like she owns it!"

Ada was on his feet before the blanket hit the deck, compass thrust inside his shirt against his heart, turning toward the wake of the horizon where Corvo Vane's iron was bearing down on them with the tide turning and six hours of river left between the rapids and ruin.
