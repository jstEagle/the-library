## 15. Six Hours Short

Toma Vasiliev had spent thirty years listening to engines the way priests listened to confession, and he knew every sin his own machine was capable of committing.

The *Kestrel's* heart lay open before him in the engine room's amber gloom, and it was confessing plenty. He knelt on the greased deck plates with a stethoscope of braided hose pressed to the crankcase, one ear to the brass, and what he heard made him close his eyes. A knock. Faint, rhythmic, down at the bottom end where no knock should ever live. Bearing number three, or maybe four—the vibration blurred them together now—wearing itself into a shape that would soon be a hole, and a hole would soon be a seized shaft, and a seized shaft would be the end of everything, six tides upriver from anywhere that sold spare parts.

He'd known since the ambush. Of course he'd known. When the hull took that splinter below the waterline and they'd limped into the willow shallows with the river pouring through them like guilt through a liar, Toma had done things to this engine that no manual sanctioned and no engineer should survive admitting. He'd patched the planking with canvas, pitch, and a copper sheet torn from the still's roof. He'd shimmed the bearing with leather from his own boot sole because there was nothing else aboard soft enough and tough enough both. He'd run her hot for two days straight, past the line marked in red paint that some long-dead chief engineer had painted with the certainty of a man writing his own commandment: *THOU SHALT NOT*.

The red line was three inches behind them now, in the mirror of the gauge glass, and gaining.

Above him, through the deck, he could hear the boat talking in her other language—the slap and drag of water against patched planking, slower than she ought to be. The *Kestrel* had always answered the wheel like a thought; now she answered like an old woman wading. Every hour the tide turned against them, the river thickened and slowed, and every hour the drag of that sodden patch pulled another half-knot out of her, and Toma sat in the belly of his dying machine doing arithmetic that kept coming up short.

Six hours. That was the number. Ruth had stood at the chart table that morning with her cracked forefinger planted on the gorge mouth and said, "We make the narrows on this tide or we wait a full day for the next, and a full day is a full day Vane doesn't have to spend." Six hours of tide left. Fourteen miles of river between them and the gate. At their best speed before the ambush, that was comfortable. At the speed the *Kestrel* could honestly manage now, hauling her wounded hull against the last of the ebb—

Toma did the division again, hoping the numbers had changed.

They hadn't. They were eight miles short. Eight miles, six hours, and somewhere behind them, closing, an iron steamer that didn't care about arithmetic because Corvo Vane had never once in his life needed more time than he could take by force.

Toma wiped his hands on a rag that had been black so long it was less a rag than a philosophy, and climbed the ladder to tell them.

* * *

The pilothouse smelled of coffee gone bitter and Junie's cheap tobacco, and everyone was already looking at him when he came through the door, which meant Ada had heard the engine too. Of course he had. The man read water like scripture and machinery like a face across a crowded room; there wasn't much about this boat that got past him, which was convenient and occasionally unbearable.

"Tell me," Ada said. He stood at the wheel with his sleeves rolled, forearms scarred and steady, eyes forward on the brown water sliding past. Behind him the whole crew had arranged itself without anyone arranging it: Miriam at the chart table with her clerk's neatness even now, papers squared despite everything; Junie folded onto the settee with her arm bound and her chin up, pretending the wound didn't pull when she breathed; Ruth in the corner chair like a figurehead that had learned to sit, her hands quiet in her lap, her eyes closed—not sleeping, Toma knew, but listening to the river through the hull the way he listened to the engine.

"Bearing three is going," Toma said. "Maybe four. I've got it shimmed with boot leather, which is a sentence I'd like struck from the record of my life." He held up a hand before Ada could speak. "It'll hold. I can make it hold. But it holds at seven knots, maybe seven and a half if I insult her further, and we need nine to make the gate on this tide. We're eight miles short."

Silence, except the river.

Miriam spoke first, because clerks always do the math aloud. "Then we miss the window."

"Then we wait a day," Junie said, "and Vane's steam comes over that horizon while we're sitting on our hands, and everything we—" She stopped herself, jaw tight, hand going unconsciously to the bandage on her arm. Everything we bled for. Nobody finished it for her. It hung in the pilothouse like smoke.

Ada hadn't moved. "How much does she have left, Toma? Honestly. Not engineer-honestly. Honestly."

Toma looked at his hands. Thirty years of grease worked into the creases, scars from a hundred small violence of metal and heat, and beneath all of it the memory of another engine, another boat, another time he'd been asked how much a machine had left and had lied to keep hope alive and watched the lie kill a fireman named Polaski when the boiler let go. He did not lie anymore. It was the only vow he'd ever managed to keep.

"At full insult—everything I can wring, running her past every line ever painted—she has maybe ten hours of honest life in her. After that the bearing goes, and it goes hard, and we're dead in the water wherever we are when it happens." He met Ada's eyes. "Ten hours. The tide gives us six. The gap is three hours wide and eight miles long, and I don't have a bridge to build across it."

"Then we don't cross it," Miriam said quietly. "We find a landing, we hide, we—"

"There's no landing," Ruth said.

Everyone turned. The old woman hadn't opened her eyes. Her voice came out dry as driftwood, unhurried, the voice of someone who had been waiting for this conversation the way the river waits for rain.

"There's no landing between here and the gate. There's the channel and there's the walls and there's the water, and then there's the gate." She opened her eyes at last, pale as river fog, and looked at Toma. "You said nine knots."

"I said we need nine."

"No." Ruth leaned forward, and something moved in her face that Toma had never seen there before—not fear exactly, though fear lived under it, but the thing fear becomes in a person who has already survived the worst day of her life and knows its exact weight. "You said the boat needs nine. I'm asking what the pilot needs. Because there's a stretch above the bend—two miles, maybe three—where the channel narrows and drops, and the current runs eight knots on the ebb, nine on the spring. No pilot runs it. Not because it can't be done. Because the ones who tried are part of the scenery now."

"Ruth," Ada said, low.

"The water does most of the work." She ignored him, or rather she spoke *through* him, to Toma, engineer to pilot, machine to river. "A boat doesn't need to make nine knots through the water in that reach. She needs to make five, and let the river give her the rest. Five knots your engine can do. Five knots your engine can do for ten hours." Her finger rose, pointing at him across the pilothouse, thin and steady as a needle. "The question isn't whether your machine survives. The question is whether you trust it to carry us while I steer us through water that eats boats, on an engine held together with boot leather, with a patched hull that might peel open like a ripe fruit the first time we hit a standing wave."

The silence after that had texture. Toma felt it press against his eardrums along with the knock of the failing bearing, which he could hear even up here, faint through the deck, patient as a clock.

Junie broke it. "What happens if we don't?"

"We wait a day," Miriam said. "We lose the lead. Vane takes the case, takes Miriam back, probably takes the boat, possibly takes us. And Junie's town gets condemned by a survey nobody will ever get to argue with." She said it flatly, a clerk reading findings, and Toma saw her hands were not flat at all—they were fists on her neat papers.

"And if we do," Ada said, "and the hull lets go in the rapids, we drown in a canyon with no witnesses except the fish." He turned from the wheel at last and looked at each of them in turn, and Toma recognized the look, because he'd seen it once before, ten years ago, on a different boat, on a different man's face, right before a different impossible decision went the wrong way. It was the look of a captain measuring his crew against the water and hating the scale.

"It's not your decision alone," Toma said.

Ada's eyes came to him.

"You're the captain. You steer. But that engine is mine, and I'm telling you what I'm telling Ruth: she'll hold. Five knots, three hours, through the worst water on this river." His voice surprised him by being steady. Inside, nothing was steady; inside, he was a boy again watching Polaski walk into the stokehold on Toma's assurance that the gauge was wrong, the gauge had to be wrong. "I have spent ten years not making promises to machines or people, Ada. So hear me. This is the first one. She'll hold, or I'll die in the engine room trying to make her, and either way you'll have your nine knots at the gate."

"Toma—"

"That's not courage," Toma said. "I want that understood. Courage is what Junie did when she got boarded. Courage is what Ilsa did alone in a cave for a year. This is just arithmetic with my life as the remainder, and I've been the remainder before, and I'd rather spend myself on something than sit at anchor waiting for Vane to spend me on nothing." He looked around the pilothouse—at the runaway, the clerk, the old pilot, the haunted captain—and found, to his astonishment, that his hands had stopped shaking. "Run it."

Ruth smiled. It transformed her face into something ancient and fierce, a carving on a prow.

Ada looked at the river for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the smallest motion, and put his hands back on the wheel.

"Ruth, call the lines. Toma, give me everything she has and nothing she doesn't. Junie, watch the patch from aft—if that canvas starts to breathe, I want to know before the river does. Miriam—" a ghost of grim humor crossed his face, "—pray, if you know how. If not, take notes. Someone should record how stupid this was."

* * *

The engine room at full insult was a church of noise and heat, and Toma its only congregation.

He ran her at a throttle setting he had never written down because he never intended to defend it to any board of inquiry, living or dead. The gauges climbed. The red line fell behind in the glass like a shoreline receding. The knock at the bottom end deepened from a tap to a knock to something with the cadence of a fist on a door, and Toma talked to it the way he talked to all his machines, low and constant, an unbroken mutter of encouragement and threat: *not yet, not yet, hold, you beautiful ugly old girl, hold, I know, I know it hurts, hold.*

Sweat ran off his chin and sizzled on the exhaust manifold. The firebox roared. He fed it coal in measured shovelfuls, watching the flame color the way a doctor watches pulse—too white and she'd burn the tubes, too dull and she'd fall off the power curve and every knot mattered now, every single revolution of the shaft was a yard of river they wouldn't have to beg from the tide. Through the skin of the hull he could feel the current change as they came up on the bend, feel the *Kestrel* begin to tremble in a new register, the deep hum of fast water gripping her keel.

The voice tube from the pilothouse crackled. Ruth's voice, stripped down to iron: "Engine room. Stand by. Water's standing up ahead."

"Standing by," Toma said, and put both hands on the throttle, and waited.

The first wave hit like a whale shouldering the hull. The whole boat lurched, tools leapt from their racks, and Toma rode the deck plates with his knees bent and his palms flat on the engine block, feeling through his bones what no gauge could tell him: the shaft loading, unloading, loading again as the stern lifted and slammed. The knock stuttered—changed—came back worse. Somewhere below the waterline the canvas patch was taking the river's argument personally.

*Hold.*

He gave her more. Against every instinct, against thirty years of doctrine, he opened the throttle another quarter turn as the boat climbed the back of a standing wave, because Ruth needed rudder authority and rudder authority was water over the prop and water over the prop was revolutions, and revolutions were the only prayer he knew how to say. The engine screamed a note he'd never heard from her, high and ragged over the knock, and the temperature needle swung past the last sensible mark and kept going, and Toma Vasiliev laughed—a wild, cracked sound torn out of him by the roar, terrified and exhilarated in equal measure, the purest moment of his entire careful, guarded, unforgiven life.

The voice tube again, and this time it was Ada, calm as Sunday: "Toma. Whatever you're doing. Keep doing it."

Through the engine room's single porthole, as the *Kestrel* plunged down the wave's far face and the canyon walls closed in green and thundering, Toma caught one glimpse astern—just a flash between swells—of the horizon behind them, and on it, small and black and patient, a smudge of smoke that had not been there an hour ago.

Vane. Coming on with the tide.

Toma turned back to his gauges, to his knocking, bleeding, magnificent machine, and shoveled the last of the good coal into the firebox.

"Hold," he told her. "Just to the gate. Just to the gate, old girl, and I'll never ask you for anything again."

She knocked twice, like a promise—or a countdown—and drove on into the roar.
