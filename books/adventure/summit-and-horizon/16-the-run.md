## 16. The Run

Ruth Okafor had run the gorge twice in her life, and both times the river had let her live out of what she could only call indifference. Now, standing at the bow rail of the *Marrow's Grace* with the patched hull groaning beneath her feet like an old man climbing stairs, she understood that this third time would be different. The river was not indifferent. The river was paying attention.

She could feel it in the water.

Fifty years on these rivers had taught her to read current the way a priest reads scripture—not the words on the surface but the meaning underneath, the grammar of eddies, the punctuation of rocks breaking white. And what the river was saying now, in the last grey hour before dawn, with Vane's steamer a smudge of coal smoke on the horizon behind them and the tide already turning against them, was simple: *you are not welcome here.*

"Tell me again," Ada said beside her. His hands were on the wheel, knuckles pale, and his voice had the flat calm she'd learned to distrust—the calm of a man who has decided to be afraid later. "The lines."

"The lines." Ruth closed her eyes and let the map unroll behind them, the one that existed nowhere but in her memory, drawn by a pilot named Corrigan forty years before the flood drowned his maps and his town together. "First line is the Widow's Teeth—three rocks just under at this water level, maybe four inches of green over the middle one. We go left of all three, hard left, and we don't correct until I say. The river will try to push us right. Let it think it's winning."

"And after?"

"After the Teeth the channel dog-legs right around the Old Church spire—that's the stone steeple sticking up midstream, you'll see it, God help us—and then it drops. Nine feet in a hundred yards. That's the Staircase. You don't steer the Staircase, Ada. You point the boat true and you hold her straight and you pray to whatever's listening that Toma's engine doesn't quit."

Behind them, Toma's voice floated up from the engine hatch, hoarse and feverish. "Engine's listening! Engine heard that!"

Junie laughed—a short, startled sound, the laugh of someone surprised to find laughter still living in her body. She was at the stern rail with a boat hook, her bandaged shoulder darkening where the wound had seeped through. Miriam stood next to her, gripping the rail with both hands, and Dr. Brandt crouched low near the cargo hatch where the empty case had been, watching the water ahead with the flat stare of a woman who had already decided how she would die and found it acceptable.

Ruth looked at them all—this ragged collection of strays and debtors and runaways that had somehow become, without her noticing the exact moment, *hers*—and felt something crack open in her chest that was not fear.

Fear she knew. Fear was an old companion, older than her marriage, older than her piloting license, older than the day the flood took the gorge towns and turned every channel she'd memorized into a guessing game played against a drowned world. This was something else. This was the feeling of standing in front of people who trusted you with their lives, and finding that instead of crushing you, it made you larger.

"Ada," she said. "When I call the lines, you repeat them back. Not because I doubt your ears. Because if I go quiet—if the water takes my voice—you'll know the lines yourself, and you can finish the run without me."

Ada looked at her for a long moment. "Nothing's taking your voice, Ruth."

"Everything takes everything, eventually. That's the first lesson of the river." She turned back to the bow. "Second lesson is: sometimes it gives things back. Hold on to that one today."

* * *

The gorge swallowed them at first light.

It came up around the boat like a throat closing—walls of black basalt rising sheer on either side, streaked with the mineral stains of the old flood line, hung with vines that trailed in the water like the hair of drowned women. The air went cold and wet and echoed strangely, so that the engine's labor came back to them doubled, tripled, as if other boats were running the narrows alongside them in some ghost fleet.

"There." Ruth pointed. "Widow's Teeth."

They were barely visible—three dark shadows beneath the surface, marked only by a faint wrinkling of the current above them, like the skin of a sleeping thing. To starboard, the main channel stretched wide and inviting, deep and smooth.

"That's the trap," Ruth said. "Wide water's the lie. It funnels down to nothing half a mile in—there's a sill across it, submerged, no way through. Corrigan lost two barges proving that. Left. Hard left. Now."

Ada put the wheel over. The *Marrow's Grace* heeled, her patched hull complaining, and swung toward the narrow slot between the Teeth and the left-hand wall—a gap that looked, from this distance, exactly wide enough for grief.

"Left of the Teeth," Ada said, repeating it back, his voice steady. "Don't correct till you say."

"Don't correct." Ruth watched the middle rock slide closer, its four inches of green water gleaming. Fifty feet. Thirty. She could see the rock now through the surface, barnacled and patient. "Hold... hold... hold—"

The boat threaded the gap with a hand's width on either side. The current grabbed them the moment they were through, shoving them sideways toward the wall, and every instinct in Ada's arms must have screamed to correct, but he held, held, held, until Ruth said "Now!" and he brought the bow around smooth as a dancer's turn into the dog-leg.

Ahead, rising out of the mist like a monument to a drowned parish, stood the Old Church spire.

"Right of the spire," Ruth called. "Give it room—it's got a ledge underwater, runs out eight feet. Right of the spire, then square up for the Staircase."

"Right of the spire, square for the Staircase."

They cleared the ledge close enough that Junie could have touched it with the boat hook. Then the river dropped away in front of them.

The Staircase. Nine feet of fall in a hundred yards, broken into three steps, each one a shelf of submerged rock with a tongue of white water pouring over it. In the old days there had been a lock here, Ruth remembered—a stone lock, hand-cranked gates, pilots waiting hours for their turn. The flood had taken the lock and left only the raw descent, and no pilot had run it since, because no pilot had been fool enough or desperate enough.

"We're fools enough," she murmured. "Are we desperate enough?"

"Square," Ada said. "What do I do?"

"You do nothing. That's the hardest work you'll ever do as a steersman—nothing. Point her true. Both hands. If she starts to broach, give her left rudder, quarter-turn, no more. And Ada—" she gripped his shoulder, felt him trembling under her hand, fine as a plucked string, "—whatever happens, don't shut your eyes. Pilots die with their eyes shut. Live with them open."

He nodded once.

The bow crossed the lip of the first step, and the world became noise.

* * *

Later, Ruth would try to describe those ninety seconds and fail every time.

There was the drop itself—the stomach-lifting plunge as the boat fell down the first step, the bow burying itself in the foam below, green water coming over the rails in a cold slap that knocked Junie to her knees. There was the second step, where the channel kinked without warning and the hull struck something glancing—a glancing blow, thank God, a kiss instead of a bite—and the whole boat spun ten degrees off true before Ada wrestled her back with that quarter-turn of left rudder, exactly as instructed, no more.

And there was the third step, where Toma's engine—the engine rigged past every safe limit, the engine gambling its heart for their time—screamed like a living thing and then, at the worst possible moment, coughed.

One cylinder died. The boat, half-broached in the churning water below the final drop, began to swing broadside toward the right-hand wall, toward the seam of white water that would roll them like a drum.

"LEFT!" Ruth screamed, and Ada was already turning, throwing his whole weight on the wheel, and Toma's voice rose from below in a wordless roar of effort, and the dead cylinder caught—caught, caught, CAUGHT—with a bang that shook the deck plates, and the propeller bit, and the *Marrow's Grace* came around through the foam with her rails nearly kissing the water, and shot out the bottom of the Staircase into the long, deep, gentle pool beyond.

Silence, except for the engine settling back into its rhythm and the water dripping off everything.

Nobody moved. Then Junie started laughing—really laughing, wild and gasping, holding her wounded shoulder—and Miriam sat down flat on the deck and put her face in her hands, and Dr. Brandt said something in her own language that needed no translation, and Toma appeared at the engine hatch black with grease and grinning like a skull.

Ruth looked at Ada. He was still holding the wheel, still pointing her true, eyes open, eyes wide open, and tears were cutting tracks through the spray on his face, though she doubted he knew it.

"You did nothing," she said. "Perfectly."

He exhaled, a sound like a man surfacing from deep water. "What's next?"

"Next?" Ruth turned forward. Below the Staircase the gorge opened its arms—the walls falling back, the channel widening, morning light finally reaching the water and turning it from black to a deep moving bronze. Six miles of clean running water to the confluence, and the tide, if she read it right, still in their favor. "Next is easy. Next is just the river being kind to us for a while."

She didn't say the rest. Didn't need to. They all felt it—the shadow behind them, the coal smoke, the iron ship that would reach the foot of the Staircase and stop, because Corvo Vane's salvage steamer drew too much water to follow, and Vane would not risk her, not even for the case. He would have to take the long route around, through the lower locks, and that cost him a full tide.

A full tide. One tide ahead.

Ruth leaned on the bow rail and let the wind of their going dry her face, and listened to her strange family making noise behind her—Toma singing to his engine, Junie laughing, Miriam's shaky breath steadying into something like resolve—and thought about Corrigan, dead forty years, whose map lived on in her head alone. Some inheritances you carry in paper. Some you carry in blood.

And some, she thought, watching Ada wipe his face and square his shoulders and bring the boat home through the light, some you carry in people.

Then she saw it—far ahead, where the gorge mouth met the confluence, a thin thread of smoke rising from the fueling landing that should have been empty.

Someone was there first.
