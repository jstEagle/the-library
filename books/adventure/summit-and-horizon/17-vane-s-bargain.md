## 17. Vane's Bargain

The confluence announced itself before they saw it—a widening in the water's voice, two rivers arguing over which would carry them forward. Ada stood at the wheel with his hands light on the spokes, feeling the *Kestrel* shudder as the currents met beneath her hull, and understood that this was where the river had decided to end their running.

Vane's steamer came out of the downstream haze like a verdict.

She was called the *Iron Mercy*, though no one on the water had ever mistaken her for mercy—two hundred feet of black plate and riveted muscle, paddle wheels churning the brown water to lace, her deck guns covered with canvas that fooled no one. She had been behind them since dawn, a smudge of smoke on the horizon that Toma had named aloud an hour ago with the flat calm of a man reading his own death warrant. Now she filled the channel ahead, angled across the confluence like a door closing, and there was nowhere left to go but into her shadow or onto her guns.

"Ruth," Ada said quietly. "Options."

Ruth stood beside him at the wheel, one weathered hand resting on the rail, squinting at the water ahead with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Upstream's against us and we've got nothing left in the boiler to fight it. The east fork narrows too shallow for our draft by tomorrow's tide. West fork—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "West fork is Vane's water. He owns the towage rights clear to the head of navigation. We'd be sailing up his throat."

"And straight?"

"Straight is him." She said it without drama, the way she said everything about the river. "He's been herding us for three days, Ada. This ain't an ambush. It's a parlor."

Ada looked back along his deck. Junie sat propped against the cargo hatch with her arm bound and slung, her face still gray from blood loss but her eyes sharp as ever, tracking the steamer the way a small animal tracks a hawk. Miriam stood at the rail clutching the sealed case to her chest like a child she'd promised to protect, and Ilsa Brandt crouched beside her, hollow-cheeked and fever-bright, watching the iron ship approach with the particular stillness of someone who had already survived one betrayal and did not intend to be surprised by another. Toma was below, keeping the wounded engine alive by willpower and blasphemy.

His crew. His strange, battered, borrowed family. All of it gathered now in the palm of Corvo Vane's hand.

"He's signaling," Junie called. "Wants to parley. Single boat, unarmed, alongside his starboard quarter."

"Of course he does." Ada cut the engine to half and felt the *Kestrel* settle, obedient even now, even here. "Ruth, hold us steady. If I'm not back in an hour, you run the west fork anyway and let Vane's lawyers sort out the wreckage."

"You'll be back." Ruth didn't look at him. "Men who owe their crew a homecoming don't drown easy. I've seen it."

He took the gig alone. He told himself it was because a single man looked less like a threat, less like a declaration of war—but the truth, which he carried down the ladder and into the boat with him, heavier than any weapon, was that whatever bargain was coming, he wanted to be the only one of his people who had to carry its weight afterward.

* * *

Corvo Vane received him on the steamer's aft deck, beneath an awning of striped canvas, seated in a wicker chair as though the whole arrangement were a garden party and not the end of a chase that had cost men their lives. He was smaller than Ada remembered from the landing at Harrow Bend—compact, silver-templed, dressed in river linen that probably cost more than the *Kestrel*'s hull. Two guards flanked him with carbines held low and casual. A third man stood apart, taking notes in a leather ledger, and Ada marked him: clerks recorded things, and things written down became leverage later.

"Captain Eriksen." Vane gestured to the chair opposite. "You look tired. The gorge does that to people. Sit. Drink?" He lifted a decanter of something amber without waiting for an answer, poured two glasses, and pushed one across the small table between them. "I'll come directly to it, because I respect your time and I suspect you don't trust anything I serve you."

Ada remained standing. He didn't touch the glass. "Then say it."

"The case." Vane folded his hands. "Dr. Brandt's survey. You have it aboard your boat—I know this the way I know my own pulse; I have known what you carry since before you found her, Captain, which is a thing you should sit with for a moment. Every mile you've run, every rapid, every wound your people have taken—you were carrying my property toward a government office that will bury it in a drawer for thirty years. That's all it was ever going to be. Paper in a drawer. And for paper in a drawer, you've lost a hull patch, a fueling stop, and I'm told a girl took a knife through the arm."

Ada kept his face still, though the words went into him like hooks. *They knew.* They had known about Brandt before the *Kestrel* ever reached the second camp. Which meant the ambush at the landing hadn't been opportunistic. It had been scheduled.

"What's your offer," Ada said.

"My offer." Vane smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, the smile of a man who had bought entire towns and never once had to raise his voice. "Here is what the survey says, Captain, so we can speak honestly. There is ore under that valley. A great deal of it. Enough that extracting it requires flooding the lower canyon—which means condemning six river towns, including a little mining settlement called Graywater Landing, population four hundred and eleven, if my figures are current." He sipped his drink. "Your government commissioned that survey intending to suppress it. My fleet intends to act on it. Those are the only two futures that paper has, and one of them includes me paying you very well."

"And the other part of the offer. The part you led with."

Vane set down his glass. For just a moment the warmth drained out of his face, and what remained was older and colder and far more honest—the face of a man doing sums.

"The trade is this. Give me the case, and I give you my word—witnessed, contracted, enforceable—that Graywater Landing is removed from the condemnation schedule. Not flooded. Not bought out at ruinous rates. Spared. Your runaway deckhand's village keeps its church and its graveyard and its name on the maps. In exchange, the ore comes out of the ground somewhere else, the salvage fleet does what salvage fleets do, and you and your crew sail home rich enough that none of you ever have to read a tide table again unless you want to." He spread his hands. "Everyone lives, Captain. That's the offer. I'm told you're a man who's spent ten years learning what it costs when everyone doesn't."

The river spoke beneath them both, patient and enormous, and Ada heard in it his brother's voice, though his brother had been dead a decade and the hearing of it was a thing he'd stopped fighting somewhere around the gorge gate.

Because here was the trap, and he saw it plain, the way Ruth saw a snaggle under brown water—not by looking at it but by reading everything around it. Vane's word was worth exactly what it cost him to break. A contract witnessed by Vane's own clerk was a rope with one end tied to nothing. And even if Vane honored it—even if every clause held—Graywater spared meant some other town condemned instead, some other four hundred souls shifted down the ledger to make room, because the ore was real and the hunger for it was real and Vane was merely the sharpest tooth on a jaw that had many. The deal didn't save anyone. It just chose who drowned, and made Ada the one who chose.

And yet.

And yet here was the truth underneath, the cold stone under the current: *not choosing* also chose. Run for the government office and maybe—maybe—Miriam's agency grew a spine and published the findings and fought the fleet in courts for twenty years while the towns rotted in uncertainty, or more likely buried the survey as planned and Vane dredged it out of some clerk's filing cabinet for the price of a dinner. Keep the case and keep running and eventually the *Iron Mercy* caught them, because iron catches wood, always, everywhere, forever. There was no clean road off this sandbar. Every path was paved with somebody's bones, and the only question—the whole of the question—was whose, and whether he had the right to pick.

"I need to understand something first," Ada said. "Harrow Bend. The men who boarded us. Were they yours before we ever loaded fuel?"

Vane considered him, then nodded once, unbothered by being caught. "I put two men aboard your crew's orbit in Harrow Bend, yes. One of them is still aboard your boat, I expect. The other got stabbed, which was untidy." He said it the way another man might report the weather. "I won't insult you by apologizing. This river runs on money, Captain, and money doesn't apologize either. But hear me on one point, because it matters to what happens next: I gave orders that no one was to be killed. Wounded, frightened, delayed—yes. Killed, no. Dead crew draw coroners, and coroners ask about salvage rights. I am ruthless, not stupid, and I am certainly not a butcher." His eyes went briefly, genuinely hard. "The man who burned your hold acted past his instructions, and he's been paid off and put ashore. That's the truth of it, take it or leave it."

Ada thought of Junie's arm, wrapped and useless. Of Toma's engine screaming below decks. Of Miriam memorizing routes in the dark of a stolen cabin. *Ruthless, not stupid.* Both things could be true. That was the horror of men like Vane—he really might spare the town. He really might keep his word. And he would still be a man who fed on rivers, and the feeding would go on long after this bargain was signed, town after town after town, forever, with Ada's signature on the first meal.

"You'd honor it," Ada said slowly. "The Graywater clause. You actually would."

"I would." No hesitation. "I want the ore, Captain, not a massacre. Condemning that village costs me nothing—it was going to be flooded regardless of whose plan wins. Giving it to you costs me almost nothing and buys me the one thing money can't." He leaned forward. "It buys me a man who spent ten years running from a drowning, walking away clean. Do you know how rare that is? How valuable? Men who've paid grief don't double-cross. Grief makes them honest. I'd rather have your word than your government's seal."

There it was. Not the trap Ada had expected—the trap inside the trap. Vane wasn't buying the case. Vane was buying *him*: his guilt, his grief, his decade of measured miles, all of it rendered down into a guarantee of good behavior. Take the bargain and become, forever, the man whose redemption had been purchased by Corvo Vane. Refuse it and become the man who let four hundred people drown rather than shake a devil's hand.

Every choice cost. The river had been trying to teach him that for ten years, and he had finally, finally learned it, here in the shadow of the iron ship, with the water muttering its one eternal lesson beneath his boots.

"Give me until dark," Ada said.

"Until dark." Vane rose, extended his hand, and when Ada didn't take it, withdrew it gracefully, without offense. "A wise captain thinks. A hasty one sinks. My launch will see you back." Then, as Ada turned: "Captain. The girl with the arm—Junie, is it? When you speak to your crew, remember that she's the one who pays either way. That's not cruelty. That's just arithmetic. Someone always pays. All any of us decide is whether we look at them while we do it."

* * *

The gig crossed back through the steamer's long wake, and Ada sat in the stern with the oars shipped, letting the current carry the last hundred yards, because his arms weren't steady and he wanted them steady before his people saw his face.

They were all waiting at the rail. Of course they were. Ruth with her arms crossed, Toma up from the engine room streaked with grease, Ilsa pressed against Miriam's side like a shadow, and Junie front and center with her sling and her burning eyes, because she knew—somehow she already knew, the way the young always know when the grown world is dividing up their future like a pie.

"Well?" Ruth said.

"He wants the case. In exchange, Graywater goes free. Contracted, witnessed, his word of honor." Ada climbed aboard and stood among them, and told them all of it, every clause, every hook he'd found in it, because they deserved the whole ugly shape of it and not a captain's filtered version. When he finished, the deck was quiet except for the water slapping the hull and the distant churn of the *Iron Mercy*'s wheels.

"It's a lie," Miriam said at last, but her voice shook. "It has to be a lie. He can't promise a town's survival—he doesn't control the condemnation schedule, the ministry does—"

"He controls the ministry," Ilsa said flatly. "Or enough of it. My team sold to him because he could deliver what he promised. Believe me, Doctor Sato, I spent three months learning exactly which of Vane's promises are real." She hugged the case tighter. "The Graywater clause would hold. That's what makes it monstrous."

"Then take it." Everyone turned. Junie had spoken, and her voice cracked down the middle but held. "Take the deal. It's my town. It's my—my mother's grave is there, and Aunt Bess, and the schoolhouse, and—" Her breath hitched. "Four hundred people, Ada. Against papers. Against *ore*. You said yourself there's no version where nobody bleeds. So bleed me. I'm already bleeding. Take the deal."

"No." Toma, quiet, from the engine room hatch, grease-black hands hanging at his sides. "You don't spend a town to buy a case, girl, and you don't spend yourself neither. That's not how family works." He looked at Ada. "But she's right that it's four hundred against everything else. I won't pretend the math is easy. I'm just saying we don't make it easy by making *her* carry it."

Ada looked at each of them in turn—Ruth unreadable and steady, Toma grim, Miriam white-knuckled around the case, Ilsa watching him with the terrible clarity of the twice-betrayed, Junie with tears cutting clean lines through the grime on her face—and felt the vertigo open beneath him like the deep channel under the wreck. Whatever he chose, he would stand on this deck tonight and watch it cost someone. That was the inheritance of command. That was the inheritance of being the one at the wheel when the ferry went down. You chose, and then you lived in the choosing, every day, for the rest of your miles.

"There's a third number in the arithmetic," Ada said slowly, and the thought arrived even as he spoke it, rising from somewhere below thought, from the place where the river kept its truths. "Vane said the buyers are coming. He said it at Harrow Bend and he said it today—his rendezvous, his sale, the fleet's whole season riding on one exchange." He turned to Miriam. "When they took you aboard. The route. You memorized it."

Miriam blinked. Then, slowly, understanding kindled in her face—not hope yet, but the ground hope stands on. "The Narrows Reach. Tomorrow's morning tide, the old customs anchorage. He can't sell the survey without showing the vein charts, and he can't show the charts without opening the case, and he can't open the case without—" She stopped. "Without all his attention in one place."

"So the case isn't the prize," Ada said. "It's the bait. His and ours." He looked west, where the sun was going down red behind the drowned canyon, and beyond it, downstream, toward the anchorage where an iron ship would open a box worth killing for in front of every buyer and rival and vulture on the river. "We don't sell the town. We don't surrender the case. And we don't fight him at the confluence where he's strongest." His hands had gone steady again. "We follow him to his own wedding and rob it."

Ruth began, very quietly, to laugh.

Below them, unseen, in the dark of the hold where the fire had been, the infiltrator Vane had claimed to have paid off and put ashore knelt among the tarred cordage with a fresh fuse coiled in his fist like a sleeping snake, and waited for full dark.
