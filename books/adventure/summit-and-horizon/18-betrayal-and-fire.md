## 18. Betrayal and Fire

Miriam Sato had learned long ago that the worst news never arrived looking like news. It arrived looking like weather. Like a clerk's errand. Like a man asking to borrow a lamp.

So when the deckhand named Pell came up from the hold at dusk, wiping his hands on a rag and saying the bilge pump was running sluggish, some cold and ancient part of her sat up and took notice. She had spent eleven years in the Ministry of Rivers and Reclamation, and in that time she had catalogued a hundred small betrayals, and every one of them had begun with a man wiping his hands and mentioning something mundane.

Pell had come aboard at the fueling landing, three days back. He was broad, quiet, competent with a caulking iron, and he had asked no questions about pay. Ada had hired him because they needed hands and because the ambush at the landing had left them short-handed and holed and afraid. Ruth had squinted at him the whole first day. Junie had said, flatly, *he smells like coal smoke and nothing else*, which Miriam had taken for a child's prejudice against strangers.

Now she watched him fold the rag with unnecessary neatness and said, "Sluggish how?"

"Slow to draw." He shrugged, an easy shrug, the shrug of a man with nothing behind his teeth. "Toma says maybe a fouled intake. We'll clear it at morning."

Toma said no such thing. Miriam had spent the evening in the wheelhouse going over tide tables with Ada, and Toma had been with them, grease to the elbows, complaining about what the patched hull did to his engine's appetite. There had been no talk of the bilge pump. There had been no problem with the bilge pump.

She said none of this. She smiled the small, apologetic smile that had gotten her through a decade of ministry politics, and she said, "Well, I'm sure Toma knows his engine," and she walked aft, unhurried, and she found Ada at the rail.

"Your new deckhand is lying," she said quietly.

Ada didn't look at her. He was watching the water the way he always watched it, as if it were a text he was only halfway through and expected to be tested on. "Everyone on this boat is lying about something," he said. "I'm lying about how much I sleep. Ruth's lying about her knees. What's his lie?"

"He says the bilge pump is fouled. Toma never said anything of the kind. And he folded his rag like a man putting away evidence."

Ada turned then. In the last light his face was all planes and old weather, and Miriam had come, over these weeks, to read it the way he read the river — not easily, but truly. What she saw now was not surprise. It was the grim confirmation of a man who had been waiting for the other shoe, and had heard it drop.

"Junie," he called, without raising his voice.

She came out of the galley with a tin plate in her hands, fourteen years old and all elbows and watchfulness. "Yeah?"

"Go tell Toma to check the bilge pump. Right now. Don't say why. Don't say I said. Just say check it."

Junie set the plate down and went, quick and silent as a heron.

* * *

They never got the chance to be grateful for the warning.

The explosion, when it came, was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. There was no gout of flame, no cinematic roar. There was a *whump* from below, a pressure change that Miriam felt in her back teeth, and then a thin gray ribbon of smoke curling up through the deck boards near the cargo hatch, and then the smell — oil and burning canvas and something chemical and sweet that she would remember for the rest of her life.

"Fire in the hold," Toma bellowed, and the boat became a different animal.

Miriam had been on riverboats her whole career and had never once seen a crew actually fight a fire. She had read the reports. Reports were her native country. But nothing in the ministry's incident filings had prepared her for the violence of it — Toma tearing open the hatch and the heat coming out like a struck match the size of a room; Ada shouting for the pump lines; Ruth at the wheel, swinging the boat broadside to the wind with an old woman's precision, buying them the angle that would keep the flames off the fuel store; Junie hauling buckets with her face black and her eyes enormous and dry, because she was too frightened even to cry.

And Pell was gone.

She understood it in pieces, the way one understands any catastrophe: not whole, but in shards, each one arriving with its own small horror. Pell was gone from the deck. The skiff was missing from its davits. And the case — Ilsa Brandt's sealed case, the case with the vein maps and the bribe ledgers and the whole rotten future of six river towns folded inside it — the case was gone from the cabin where it had been lashed beneath Miriam's bunk.

She had put it there herself. She had lashed it with a bowline she had tied herself. She had checked it at noon.

*He was in the cabin,* she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *He was in the cabin while we ate dinner. While Junie laughed at something Ruth said. He was three feet from me in the dark and I never—*

"Miriam!" Ada's voice cut through the smoke. "The case!"

"Gone. He took the skiff. He—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "He was one of ours for three days."

"Later." Ada's hand closed on her shoulder, brief and hard, and then he was gone into the smoke, and Miriam stood alone at the rail with the fire roaring behind her and the river sliding black and indifferent past the hull, and she understood, with the same terrible clarity, that this was not the end of it.

Pell had not taken the skiff to escape. Pell had taken the skiff to *deliver*.

* * *

They saved the boat. That was the miracle Toma would retell for years, though he would tell it as an engineering story rather than a survival one — how the fire had found the tarred canvas stores and would have eaten through to the fuel locker in another ten minutes, and how he had flooded the aft compartment on a gamble that the patched hull would hold the water, and how the patch had held by, in his words, *the thickness of a prayer and a half inch of pine*. By full dark the hold was a charred, steaming cave, and the *Maren Lee* — Ada's boat, though Miriam had never once heard him call it by name — was alive, listing two degrees, smelling like a burnt offering.

They found Pell's bunk stripped. His pay left behind, all of it, counted out neat as an insult. In the seam of the mattress Junie found a brass whistle of the kind salvage fleet bosuns used, and that told them everything the whistle needed to tell.

"He signaled them," Ruth said. She sat at the galley table with her hands around a mug of tea she wasn't drinking. "Three days aboard. Long enough to learn our schedule, our draft, our watch order. Long enough to know where the case slept." She looked at Ada. "Vane didn't send that man to burn us, boy. Burning was the *diversion*. He sent him for the case, and the fire was to keep us busy while the skiff ran."

"And it worked," Miriam said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "It worked perfectly."

Ada was standing at the wheelhouse window with his back to them, and Miriam watched the set of his shoulders and understood that he was doing what he always did with pain, which was converting it to distance. Measuring. She had learned that about him — that he measured things because measuring was the only grief he could carry without drowning.

"Steamer's downstream," he said at last. "Pell's skiff will have run with the ebb. Vane will have the case inside two hours."

"Then we've lost," Toma said. It wasn't despair; it was inventory. Toma spoke of disaster the way he spoke of engine parts. "No case, no proof, no survey. We've got a holed boat and a wounded—"

"I'm *fine*," Junie said, from the corner, where she was sitting with her bandaged arm held carefully against her chest, daring anyone to make an issue of it.

"—a holed boat," Toma repeated, "and nothing to trade."

Miriam looked at her own hands. They were steady. That surprised her. She had imagined, in the abstract, that the moment of total failure would feel like panic. It did not. It felt like a door opening onto a very cold, very clear morning.

"No," she said.

They all looked at her.

"You're thinking of it as a thing we carried. A case. Papers." She stood. Her legs were steady too. "Vane doesn't understand what he took, and neither, until this moment, did I. He thinks he's bought the vein. He thinks the papers *are* the mineral rights, that whoever holds them owns the future of the river." She shook her head. "I wrote the ministry's position papers on this. I know exactly how Vane's buyers will read those documents, because I helped draft the legal framework they'll read them under. And I know something else." She drew a breath. "I know the rendezvous."

Ruth's eyes narrowed. "You what?"

"The buyers aren't coming to Vane. Vane's going to them. It's in the bribe ledgers — the ones Ilsa copied, the ones we still have photographs of, the ones in my head because I spent two nights memorizing them before I understood why." Miriam heard her own voice and was faintly astonished by it: calm, precise, the voice of a woman giving testimony. "Corvo Vane is not a free agent. He's a contractor. The salvage fleet is the visible hand; the hand that feeds it is a consortium out of the capital, and they are meeting him at the confluence of the Sere and the mother river, on the night of the high tide, because the high tide lets their barge come up the Sere without lightening load. That's in the ledger. Dates, bearings, the draft of their barge, the name it's traveling under. I memorized all of it. I memorized it because some part of me already knew my agency would burn me the moment this was over, and I wanted—" She stopped. Started again. "I wanted to be worth more than the fire they'd make of me."

The galley was very quiet. Somewhere below, water lapped in the flooded compartment, a soft patient sound.

"So we know where he's going," Junie said slowly. "And he doesn't know we know."

"He knows we have a fast boat and a pilot who knows the gorge," Ada said. He had turned from the window. In the lamplight his face was unreadable, but his voice had changed — Miriam had heard that change before, at the ambush, at the rapids. It was the voice of a man who had finished grieving in advance and moved on to the work. "He'll assume we're done. Crippled, beaten, nothing to trade. That's the one gift in this." He looked at Miriam. "Can you navigate by that ledger in your head? Bearings, tide times, all of it?"

"I can do better than that. I can get us there before he's finished counting his money."

Ada held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something in him. "Then we run the river tonight. Toma — can she make speed on that patch?"

Toma snorted. "She can make speed on a *wish*. Question is whether the wish holds."

"Then wish loudly," Ada said, "and let's find out."

* * *

They came up on the steamer's lights near midnight, and Miriam understood immediately that God or the river or simple arithmetic was not finished with them.

The *Corvo's Pride* — Vane's iron ship, all black stack and running lights — lay anchored off a mudbar two miles short of the confluence, riding at easy anchor as if she had all the time in the world. Which she did. The high tide was still six hours out. Vane was early. Vane was *careful*. And between the *Maren Lee* and the steamer lay three miles of open water with no cover at all.

They drifted, engine idled to a whisper, in the lee of a willow island, and looked at the lights.

"He'll have a watch posted," Ruth murmured. "And he'll know your boat's silhouette if he knows nothing else."

"He knows more than that," Miriam said. Her heart was beating very fast and very evenly, the way it had beat through every bad meeting of her career, and she had learned to trust it: fear at the right tempo was just attention. "Pell was aboard three days. He knows your watch order, your draft, your crew count. Vane isn't anchored there because he's cautious. He's anchored there because he's *baiting* us. He wants us to come. He wants to end this tonight, before the buyers arrive, so there's no loose thread." She looked at Ada. "He's not guarding the case. He's hunting the witnesses."

Ada was silent a long moment. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere in the willows a night bird made its one dry note.

"Then we don't come to him," he said finally. "We let him come to us. Toma — how quiet can she run?"

"Quiet as a coffin. For about twenty minutes."

"Then here's the shape of it." And Ada laid it out, low and fast, the way he must once have laid out cargo manifests: the tide, the mudbar, the steamer's anchor watch, the skiff they still had, the fog that was already beginning to gather on the water the way fog did on this stretch before a high tide, Ruth had told them, every time. Fog was coming. The river was handing them its one mercy, and Ada meant to spend it.

Miriam listened, and nodded in the right places, and felt almost nothing — because some part of her, the clerk's part, the part that had spent eleven years watching powerful men divide up rivers on paper, had already done the arithmetic and arrived at the number that mattered.

The plan required her to be aboard the skiff. The plan required her to point out the cabin where Vane would keep the case, the layout of the *Pride's* aft deck, the watch rotations she could infer from the ledger. The plan required Miriam Sato, because she was the only one aboard who knew the enemy's paperwork the way Ada knew the water.

Which meant the plan required her to walk, willingly, into the reach of a man who burned boats and bought people.

She found Ada alone at the rail afterward, checking the skiff's lines by lantern light.

"I know what you're about to say," he said, without looking up.

"Do you? Because I'm not sure I know." She came and stood beside him. The fog was coming in properly now, softening the steamer's lights into smears of gold. "I've spent my whole life being the person in the room who takes the minutes. Who records what other people risk. And I have told myself that was prudence, and some nights I have even believed it." She watched the lights waver. "I'm not asking your permission, Captain. I'm telling you I'm going, and asking you to make it count."

Ada set down the lantern. When he looked at her, there was something in his face she had not seen before — not pity, not admiration, but a kind of recognition, one drowning person acknowledging another.

"Vane takes hostages," he said quietly. "You know that. It's the one move he has left that's worth more than the case."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway." She managed something that was almost a smile. "You of all people should understand, Captain. There are things a person carries that are heavier than fear. I've been carrying my agency's rot for eleven years. Tonight I'd like to set some of it down."

For a moment he said nothing. Then he reached out and gripped her forearm, hard, the grip of a man who did not waste words on blessings.

"Junie stays aboard," he said. "You come back. Those are the only two orders I have left."

* * *

The fog took them the last mile, and Miriam learned what it was to cross open water toward an enemy ship with no sound but the muffled knock of the skiff's oars and her own blood in her ears.

The *Corvo's Pride* rose out of the fog in pieces — first the anchor light, a blind gold eye; then the black shoulder of the hull; then the stack, breathing its low heat-smell into the wet air. Ada brought the skiff in under the stern quarter where the plating was rusted and the watch lanterns didn't reach, and Ruth held the skiff against the current with one oar and the patience of a stone, and Miriam went up the ladder that Ada's hook found for her, into the iron dark, with her heart going like a clock that had finally been wound after years of running slow.

She knew the ship. That was the terrible advantage of paperwork — she had read the *Pride's* registry filings, her deck plans, her tonnage, her refit history. She had read them as an analyst. Now she walked them as a trespasser, and every bulkhead was where the filing said it would be.

She found the aft cabin by counting doors. She found the case by its lashing — Vane's people had re-secured it to a chart table, neat as a museum piece, and beside it lay Pell's folded rag, kept like a trophy. And she found Vane himself standing at the cabin's aft windows, looking out into the fog, and she understood one full second too late that the cabin was not a prize she had reached.

It was a room he had been sitting in, waiting.

"Ms. Sato." He did not turn around. His voice was mild, almost courteous, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "I wondered which of them they'd send. The engineer's too useful, the pilot's too old, the girl's too young, and the captain—" he paused, and something in the pause was colder than the fog outside, "—the captain doesn't leave his boat. But you. You're the one who memorizes things. Pell told me all about you."

Miriam stood very still in the doorway. Behind her, somewhere above, she heard the faintest sound — a boot on iron, a breath, the beginning of Ada's quiet war on the deck above. Too far. Too late.

"Corvo Vane," she said, and was proud of how level her voice came out. "Your buyers arrive on the high tide. I know the barge's name. I know its draft, its bearings, and the names of the three men aboard it. I have known them since before your man ever set foot on our deck. Kill the case, burn the boat, sink the river itself — and I will still be the most dangerous thing on this water, because everything I know is *here*—" she touched her own temple, "—and it does not burn."

Vane turned then. In the lamplight his face was composed and patient and utterly without mercy, and he looked at her the way she had seen men look at a ledger entry that had finally, inconveniently, come due.

"Well," he said. "Then we'll just have to make sure you're on hand to *check my figures*."

The door closed behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence, and above her, faint through the iron deck, the quiet fight went on — and Miriam Sato, hostage, stood in the belly of the enemy's ship and began, methodically, to memorize the route to the rendezvous: the channel markers, the tide windows, the turning bearings, the mudbar that would ground a barge running heavy.

Whatever happened next, the river's future would not be decided in a case or a cabin or a buyer's signature.

It would be decided by what she carried out of this room — one way or the other.
