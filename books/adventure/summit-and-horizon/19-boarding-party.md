## 19. Boarding Party

The fog came off the water at dusk like a held breath finally released.

Ada stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Grace* and watched it swallow the far bank, the confluence, the black silhouette of Vane's steamer anchored mid-channel with her running lights burning yellow through the murk. The fog was a mercy and he intended to spend every coin of it.

Behind him, his crew moved in silence that was itself a kind of language now. Toma had spent the afternoon wrapping the engine's exhaust in wet canvas to kill its rumble, and when they'd drifted downriver on the ebb tide—engine cold, current doing the work—the steamer's watchmen had never marked them. Now they lay at anchor two hundred yards downstream of Vane's iron hull, hidden in a white world where sound traveled strange and sight barely reached past an oar's length.

Junie sat on the deck boards winding a length of rope around her forearm, over and over, the way she did when she couldn't sleep. Her shoulder wound had been dressed twice since the ambush; Miriam's needlework was clumsy but honest, and the girl hadn't complained once, which worried Ada more than crying would have.

"You don't have to come," he said quietly.

She looked up. In the fog-dimmed lantern light her face was all sharp angles and stubbornness. "Miriam taught me my letters. Did she tell you that? Three nights running, upriver, when I couldn't sleep and she couldn't either." She tied off the rope. "You're not leaving me behind."

Ada didn't argue. Arguing with Junie was like arguing with the tide—you could do it, but you'd lose, and you'd look foolish doing it.

Ruth came aft last, moving stiff as an old gate hinge, and pressed something into Ada's hand: a boathook, its iron head wrapped in cloth so it wouldn't ring against the steamer's plating. "Your brother's," she said. "It was in the lazarette under the spare anchor line. Been there ten years, by the look of it."

Ada turned the hook over. The wood was worn smooth in exactly the places a grip wears smooth—in the places *his* hands wore things smooth—and for a moment the fog seemed to close in around him like water closing over a sinking man. Then he closed his fist around the handle and the moment passed, or rather it didn't pass, it simply went where all his grief went now: down deep, into ballast.

"Listen," he said, and his crew leaned in, four faces in the fog. "We're not soldiers. We're not going over the side swinging. We go quiet, we go slow, and we take back what's ours—Miriam and the case. Nobody fights unless fighting finds them first. If it goes wrong, we swim for the *Grace* and we run, and we grieve later. Understood?"

Toma nodded. Junie nodded. Ruth said, "Understood," in the flat voice of a woman who had buried enough people to know that promises made before a fight are just prayers wearing boots.

* * *

They crossed the two hundred yards in the ship's dinghy, paddling, no oarlocks to creak. The fog was so thick Ada could see only the backs of the heads in front of him and the pale smear of his own hands on the gunwale. Somewhere ahead, the steamer's generator throbbed like a second heartbeat, and voices carried across the water in fragments—a laugh, a curse, the clank of a pot from the galley.

Vane's ship was called the *Iron Meridian*, and she was everything the *Grace* was not: steel instead of cedar, steam instead of diesel, forty men instead of five. She sat low in the water with salvage cranes folded along her deck like sleeping insects, and her hull rose out of the fog beside them, black and barnacled, tall as a chapel wall.

Toma found the chain plates first—he always found the metal first, the way some men find water—and they hung there in the dark, four bodies pressed against the hull while a watchman's boots crossed the deck six feet above their heads. Ada counted the footsteps. Out, pause, back. Out, pause, back. A man who walked a circuit because walking it was easier than thinking about what he guarded.

On the third circuit, they went up.

Ada went first, hook in one hand, knife in the other, and the years fell away from him as he climbed—not the years of debt and drift, but the older years, the ones from before the drowning, when he and Tam had climbed rigging like this for the pure joy of it, racing, laughing, their father shouting from the wheelhouse that they'd break their necks. He had spent ten years trying to forget those years too. Tonight he needed them.

He came over the rail into a shadow between a winch housing and a stack of cable drums, and froze, listening. The watchman's boots were receding toward the bow. Behind him, Toma came aboard silent as spilled oil, then Junie, then Ruth, who moved slower than the rest but made less noise than any of them, which was its own kind of terrifying.

The deck plan unfolded in Ada's mind the way river charts used to. Salvage steamers kept their officers aft, their crew forward, and their valuables wherever the captain could hear anyone coming after them—which meant Miriam would be near Vane's cabin, under his eye, or locked in the hold below it. The case would be with Vane himself. Men like Corvo Vane did not entrust their futures to safes; they wore them.

They moved aft along the starboard side, single file, hugging the bulwark. The fog had followed them aboard, pooling between the deck machinery, and it swallowed their shapes whole. Once they passed within ten feet of a pair of crewmen sharing a cigarette, close enough to smell the tobacco, and the men talked on about pay and a woman in Port Calloway and never knew death had walked between them.

Then a door opened directly ahead, spilling lamplight across the deck, and everyone stopped breathing.

A man stepped out—officer's coat, unshaven, carrying a mug—and stood not eight feet from Ruth, looking straight at the fog bank where she stood. Ada saw the old woman's hand slide, slow as a heron's strike, into her coat. But the officer only stretched, yawned, emptied his mug over the rail—so close to Junie's head that Ada heard the splash below—and went back inside, pulling the door shut behind him.

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Junie exhaled through her teeth, soundless, and they went on.

* * *

Vane's cabin occupied the stern, and they found Miriam before they found Vane—but not the way they'd hoped.

She was being moved. That was the shape of it as Ada pieced it together from the shadows: two men flanking her on the quarterdeck, her wrists bound in front of her, a third man ahead with a lantern, heading for the companionway down to the hold. Vane was relocating his leverage before his buyers arrived. Of course he was. A hostage in a cabin is a risk; a hostage in a hold is inventory.

Ada put his mouth close to Toma's ear. "The lantern man and the escort. Take them quiet. I'll get Miriam."

Toma nodded once and peeled away with Junie, and what happened next happened in the space of four heartbeats, so fast and so quiet that afterward Ada could hardly believe he'd seen it at all. Toma came out of the fog behind the lantern man and put him down with a forearm across the throat and a knee behind his knee, lowering him to the deck like a man setting down something fragile. Junie took the nearer escort with the boat gaff reversed, the crook of it catching his ankle and hauling his feet from under him, and she was on his chest before he finished falling, her knife flat against his jaw, her weight and her eyes both daring him to make a sound. He didn't. The second escort turned, opened his mouth—

—and Ruth's hand closed over his face from behind, and whatever she did with her other arm, she did it with the economy of a woman who had done it before, in some earlier life Ada had never asked about. The man folded. The lantern rolled, guttered, and died, and the fog took the quarterdeck back.

Miriam stood alone in the dark, bound and blinking, and when Ada stepped in front of her she made a sound that was half sob and half laugh, strangled small in her throat.

"Captain Eriksen," she whispered.

"Quietly, Clerk Sato." He cut her wrists free with two strokes. "Can you run?"

"I can run." Her hands found his sleeve and gripped it hard. "Ada—the case. Vane keeps it in his cabin, under his bunk, chained. I've seen him open it. And Ada—" her voice dropped lower still, urgent, "—his buyers come tomorrow at the narrows buoy. Two boats. He told me tonight, because he wanted me to know what happens if you fail. He wanted me to *tell* you."

"He wants us to trade."

"He wants us to think we have to."

Ada looked at her—at this dry, frightened, stubborn clerk who had hired him with money she barely had and had since become something he did not have a word for—and felt the cold courage settle over him like a coat that fit. Not rage. Rage was for men who fought for themselves. This was colder and steadier than rage: these people were his. The engineer who gambled engines, the runaway who learned letters by lamplight, the old pilot who killed with her hands and never spoke of it, the clerk who chose towns over career. His to bring home.

"Junie," he breathed. "With me. We're getting the case. Toma—take Miriam and Ruth, back the way we came, into the dinghy. If we're not at the rail in ten minutes, cast off."

"Ada—" Toma started.

"That's not a request."

"It wasn't an argument. It was a prayer." Toma gripped his shoulder once, hard, and melted into the fog with the women.

* * *

Vane's cabin door was unlocked, because men like Corvo Vane trusted locks less than they trusted fear. Ada eased it open and the warmth of the room rolled out over them—lamplight, cigar smoke, the smell of good brandy bought with bad money. Charts covered the desk. A decanter sat half-empty beside a glass with the captain's lip-mark on the rim.

And Corvo Vane was not there.

Ada's stomach dropped through the deck. He crossed to the bunk in three strides and dropped to his knees, and there it was: the case, steel-banded, chained through the bunk frame to a hasp bolted into the deck itself. A padlock the size of his fist. Beside it, a key hanging on a nail, almost insultingly casual—or a decoy. With Vane, everything was a decoy until proven otherwise.

Junie knelt opposite him, working the chain with her knife, whispering curses at it. "It's not giving, Captain. It's not—"

The lamp behind them went out.

Not the lamp—the match. Someone in the doorway had pinched the wick, and the cabin plunged into darkness, and Ada rolled left off pure animal instinct as something heavy swung through the space where his skull had been. Wood cracked against the bunk frame. He came up swinging the boathook blind, felt it connect with an arm, a grunt, a body shifting in the dark.

"Captain!" Junie's voice, sharp with terror. Scuffling. A body hitting the deck.

"Junie—down! Get down!"

He couldn't see. That was the whole horror of it—two strangers and a girl in a black box, blades finding flesh by luck instead of aim. Ada backed against the desk, hook forward, listening the way he listened to water: for the change in texture, the false note, the thing that didn't belong. Breathing, left side. Another breath, right. Junie's, fast and shallow, near the floor by the bunk.

The left-side breathing moved. Ada struck—not a swing but a thrust, short and mean, the way you spear an eel—and felt the hook's point bite through cloth and into the meat of a shoulder. The man screamed, high and involuntary, and the scream did what screams do: it ended the quiet war and started the loud one.

Boots on the deck outside. Voices. The door banged wide and lamplight flooded in, and Ada got his first real look: two of Vane's men with cudgels, and behind them, filling the doorway, Corvo Vane himself—big, silver-templed, unhurried, watching his own cabin like a man watching cards turn over.

"There he is," Vane said mildly. "The riverboat romantic. Do you know what I admire about you, Eriksen? Nothing. But I'm told admiration is customary."

"Junie," Ada said quietly. "The key. Nail, left of the frame."

Her hand shot out. One of the cudgel-men lunged; Ada met him with the boathook's shaft across the knees and heard something give that made the man sit down hard and stay there. The other raised his club over Junie's back—

—and the lamp went out again. All of them. Every lamp on the quarterdeck, all at once, plunging the stern into perfect dark, and through the sudden ringing silence came a sound Ada knew better than his own heartbeat: canvas and rope, the wet slap of a boarding hook biting home, and Ruth's voice, low and rough, carrying across the deck like scripture:

"*Over the side, Captain. Now.*"

Ada grabbed a fistful of Junie's collar, found the case—she had it, God knew how, chain and lock and all, torn bodily from the bunk frame with a strength that would become a story later—and ran for the window. Vane's cabin had stern windows, decorative, painted shut for years. They went through them anyway, glass and frame and all, into the night air and the fog, and hit the quarterdeck rail beyond, and then the black water took them both.

The river was cold enough to stop thought. Ada surfaced with the case strap clenched in his teeth, found Junie bobbing beside him, coughing, clutching the case with both arms like a child with a doll. Above them the *Iron Meridian* blazed suddenly with lanterns, shouts boiling across her decks, searchlights sweeping fog that gave nothing back. Somewhere close, oars dipped—Toma, Ruth, Miriam, the dinghy sliding out of the white blindness like a fish through weed.

Hands pulled them over the gunwale. Ruth rowed. Nobody spoke until the steamer's lights were smears behind them and the *Grace*'s dark hull rose ahead, and even then the only words spoken for a long time were Toma's, counting heads under his breath like a man counting coins he couldn't believe he still had.

"Five," he said. "Five. Five."

* * *

They cast off cold and let the ebb carry them, engine silent, the case dripping seawater onto the deck boards between them all. Miriam knelt beside it, working the padlock open with the key Junie had torn from its nail—it had been the real key after all; Vane's arrogance ran deeper than his caution—and when the lid came up, the lamplight inside showed oilcloth packets, ledger pages, survey charts sealed in wax.

And photographs. Miriam lifted them one at a time, and her face changed with each one, and the last one she held for a long moment before passing it to Ada without a word.

It showed a table. Around the table, men shaking hands over documents. Corvo Vane on one side. On the other, three men whose faces Ada didn't know—but the fourth man signing, the fourth man taking Vane's purse, was the deputy minister whose signature appeared on Miriam's own commission papers. The government hadn't sent a survey team to bury the findings. It had sent one to sell them.

"They were never going to condemn the towns," Miriam said softly. "Or save them. They were going to auction them."

Ada looked at the photograph a long time. Then he looked up—at Junie wrapped in a blanket with Ruth's arm around her, at Toma already kneeling to check the patched hull seams, at Miriam holding proof that could hang a ministry or start a war depending on whose hands it landed in.

Ahead, through the thinning fog, the narrows buoy blinked its slow red blink, and out of the dark upstream came the sound Ada had been dreading since sundown: two engines, running easy, running patient, coming down to meet a buyer.

"Get below, all of you," Ada said, and his voice was very calm, the way it went calm in the worst water. "Whatever comes alongside next—we're not here."
