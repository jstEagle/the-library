## 20. Give It to the River

The fog came off the confluence at dusk the way it always did—slow, then all at once, like a held breath finally let go. Ada stood at the wheel of the *Meridian* and watched it swallow the far bank, the burned hulk of Vane's launch still smoldering somewhere in that gray, sending up a thin black thread that the mist bent and broke apart.

Behind him, the deck was quiet in a way he had never heard it quiet. No hammering from Toma's engine room. No Junie arguing with the rigging. Just the soft slap of water against patched planking, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, the groan of Vane's great iron steamer drifting dead in the channel, its fires drowned, its crew scattered into the reeds or chained below in their own hold.

They had won. Ada turned the phrase over in his mind like a coin he didn't recognize. It didn't spend right.

Miriam Sato came up the companionway with the case under her arm. She carried it the way she'd carried it since the cave—two-handed, close to her chest, like an infant or a bomb. The brass latches caught the last of the light.

"The buyers are still coming," she said. "Vane's wireless went out before we took the bridge, but his second sent the confirmation hours ago. Three vessels, maybe four. They'll be here by tomorrow's tide."

"I know."

"Ada." She set the case down on the chart table with a click that sounded louder than it should have. "We beat him. The case is ours. That's what we bled for. That's what Junie—" She stopped herself, but not fast enough.

Junie's wound was bound and tight under her jacket. She'd refused the bunk, refused the laudanum, sat through the boarding fight propped against the wheelhouse door with a boat hook across her knees like she was guarding a gate only she could see. Right now she was below, sleeping at last, or pretending to, one hand pressed flat over the bandage even in sleep, as if the pain were something she could hold down by weight alone.

"That's what she paid for," Miriam finished quietly. "I know what I'm asking you to consider. But if we hand this to the government now—if we run it downriver to the capital—it's done. Her town gets its reprieve in writing. We get paid. It's over."

Ada looked at the case. Sealed steel, government stamp, and inside it—the way Ilsa Brandt had described it with her cracked lips and fever-bright eyes—a vein of ore running beneath six river towns like a silver root under a garden. Enough wealth to lift every one of them out of poverty. Enough cause to condemn every one of them to the excavators, the dredges, the removals. Both truths in one box, and neither truth caring which hand opened it.

"It's never over," Ada said. "That's the thing about paper, Miriam. Paper doesn't drown. Paper doesn't rust. You put this in a ministry drawer and in twenty years some clerk with Vane's ambitions pulls it out, and there's no crew, no boat, nobody standing between it and the towns. We don't end anything tonight. We just pass it on."

"And sinking it ends it?"

"Sinking it means nobody owns it." He heard how it sounded even as he said it—the words of a man who had once thrown a compass into a river rather than look at what it told him. He almost laughed. Ten years running from the water's memory, and here he stood proposing to feed it again.

But this was different. He knew it was different the way you know a current from a riptide. One takes you where you were already going. The other just takes you.

* * *

They argued until full dark. Toma came up from the engine room with grease to his elbows and took Miriam's side first—that they'd earned the salvage rights, that the *Meridian* needed a new boiler and sentiment wouldn't cast one. Ruth said nothing for a long time, sitting on her coil of line with her hands folded, watching the fog the way she watched everything: as if the river might say it better than any of them.

Then Ruth spoke. "You know what's under us here?"

"The confluence," Ada said. "Deepest channel on the whole reach. Old riverbed before the flood."

"Older than that." Ruth's voice was dry as driftwood. "My father ran barges here when I was a girl. There's a hole below the meeting of the waters that no lead line ever found bottom on. The surveyors called it a fault. My father called it the River's Pocket. Everything the current takes and doesn't want to give back goes down there." She looked at Ada. "Your brother knew it too. He used to say it was the one place on the river where a man could keep a secret from God."

The name landed the way it always did—not like a blow anymore, but like weather. Something Ada had learned to stand in.

"Weigh anchor at slack water," Ada said. "I'll take her down alone."

"You'll take me," Toma said. "If you're putting a case on a chain over that hole, you want someone who can rig the release so it actually lets go. Sentiment sinks things all the time, Captain. Steel needs engineering."

Ada looked around the wheelhouse—at Miriam's exhausted defiance, at Toma's stubborn practicality, at old Ruth already turning back to the fog as though the matter were settled because the river had settled it first—and felt something shift in his chest that he would spend years trying to name. Not relief. Not grief. Something like the moment a knot comes free: the sudden memory that your hands were made for more than holding tight.

* * *

Slack water came at two in the morning, a half hour of held breath between the tides when the confluence went glass-still and the fog lay on it like a sheet over furniture in a closed house.

They ran dark. No lamps, no engine—Toma had rigged the winch to the capstan so they could work it by hand, and the four of them turned the bars in silence while the *Meridian* drifted out over the deep water. Somewhere downstream, past the bend, the buyers' lights would be coming up the channel, three or four constellations low on the water, and every mile of river between here and there was a race they no longer intended to win.

The case hung from the boom on a length of anchor chain, swinging gently. Miriam had opened it one last time on the chart table—not to take anything out, she said, but to look at what she was giving away. The maps were there, the assays, the letters naming names: Vane's bribes, the ministry's orders to bury the findings, the survey team's signatures selling themselves one by one. Ilsa Brandt's signature last, crossed out, and beside it in different ink: *refused.*

"She tried," Miriam had said, closing the lid. "She was the only one who tried."

Now Miriam stood at the rail with her hands flat on the wood, watching the chain pay out, and Ada saw that she was crying without any sound at all, the way people cry when they've finally stopped fighting something.

"Fifty fathoms of chain," Toma murmured, counting the links through his gloved hands. "Release pin's set. When it hits, the shackle opens and the case drops free. Nothing on the surface will ever mark the spot."

"Nothing on the surface will ever need to," Ruth said.

The winch slowed. The chain hung straight down now, trembling faintly, and the fog pressed in so close that the *Meridian* seemed to float in a room with no walls. Ada put his hand on the release lever.

He thought of his brother at the wheel of the ferry, ten years ago, laughing at something Ada had said, the last ordinary minute of both their lives. He thought of the compass in his coat pocket, recovered from the wreck, its needle still faithful to a north that had nothing to do with any of them. He thought of Junie asleep below with her hand pressing down her own pain, of a mining town upstream that would wake tomorrow not knowing how close it had come to being erased, of Ilsa Brandt in the infirmary at the last landing, alive because a clerk had chosen towns over a career.

Every inheritance he'd ever been given, he had either fled or clutched until it cut him. This one he could set down.

"River's Pocket," Ada said quietly. "Keep it."

He pulled the lever.

The shackle opened with a sound too small to hear over the water, and the case went down—no splash, no ceremony, just the chain whispering through the block and then going slack, and then nothing at all. Fifty fathoms of dark water closing over the maps and the money and the war, taking them the way the river took everything it was given and kept: patiently, completely, without malice and without mercy.

Nobody spoke. The fog held them. Somewhere far below, in a hole no lead line had ever sounded, steel came to rest among the bones of the old riverbed, and the secret belonged to the water again.

Miriam wiped her face with the heel of her hand and said, in a voice that was almost steady, "They're going to be furious. Four ships' worth of furious."

"They'll search," Ada agreed. "Vane's buyers will drag this confluence for a month. Then they'll drag the next likely spot, and the next. And eventually they'll decide the case never existed, or that somebody else got to it first, and they'll go hunt each other instead of the towns." He looked at Ruth. "How long to clear the reach before dawn?"

"With the tide turning and no lights?" Ruth smiled, and in the gray it made her look fifty years younger. "If we run the back channel past the drowned orchard—tight, but I've taken worse through tighter—we're over the bar and gone before they've finished their first sounding."

"Toma?"

"Engine's patched. She'll do twelve knots if I ask nicely and fourteen if I insult her." Toma spat over the side, an engineer's benediction. "Ask nicely."

Ada rang the telegraph to slow ahead, and the *Meridian* woke beneath his feet, the old boat answering the way she always had—imperfectly, loyally, alive.

* * *

They cleared the confluence as the sky went from coal to ash. Behind them, faint in the lifting fog, the first of the buyers' lights appeared exactly where Miriam had predicted, and behind those lights, the sound of Vane's abandoned steamer settling deeper into the shallows, a long iron sigh, like something enormous finally lying down.

Junie came up on deck as they entered the back channel, moving stiff, one arm braced against the rail, refusing help with a glare that dared anyone to offer it twice. She looked at Ada. She looked at the empty spot on the deck where the case had been. She understood immediately—he watched it happen, watched her understand—and he braced for the fury.

Instead she leaned on the rail beside him and watched the drowned orchard slide past, dead trees rising out of the floodwater like the masts of a fleet that had sunk standing up.

"My grandmother used to say the river keeps what matters and gives back what doesn't," Junie said. "I thought that was just old-people talk." She was quiet a while. "Town's going to wake up and nothing will have changed. They'll never know how close it came."

"No," Ada said. "They won't."

"Is that the good ending or the bad one?"

Ada thought about it honestly, the way he was learning to think about everything now—not fleeing it, not clutching it, just looking at it straight on. "It's the one where everybody's still here tomorrow," he said. "Ask me again in ten years."

Ahead, the channel narrowed toward the gorge gate, and beyond it the long open reach ran south toward home landings, toward repairs, toward the slow unglamorous work of living. Ruth climbed to the pilot's perch without being asked. Toma went below to argue with the engine. Miriam stood at the stern rail for a long time, watching the fog close over the place where the secret slept, and when she finally came forward her shoulders were different—lighter, or maybe just carrying the weight in a new place, a place that could bear it.

Ada put the wheel over and steered for the gap in the hills, where the morning was coming up gold behind the stone, and the river ran on ahead of them, wide and deep and keeping, as it had always kept, its own counsel.

Behind them, faint and fading, came the first angry whistle of the buyers' lead ship, arriving to find the water empty and the fog full of nothing at all.

Ada smiled, and gave her more steam.
