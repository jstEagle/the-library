## 21. Tide's End

Junie felt the tide change before she heard it—the way the *Marrow's Grace* stopped shuddering against her mooring lines and settled, like a dog lying down at last.

She was in the hold, third morning of repairs, a rivet gun heavy in both hands and Toma shouting instructions over her shoulder that were half engineering and half prayer. The hole Vane's men had punched below the waterline gaped like a missing tooth, and they'd been filling it plank by plank, plate by plate, for three days while the confluence went on about its business around them—barges sliding past, gulls arguing over fish guts, the far bank green and indifferent as ever.

"Again," Toma said. "Hold it square this time."

"I held it square last time."

"You held it *almost* square, which is how boats become reefs." He crouched beside her, gray-streaked hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a bootlace, and put his hand over hers on the gun. "Feel that? The hull talks to you through the steel. When it's seated right, you'll feel it stop complaining."

Junie pressed the rivet home and listened. And there it was—a small stubborn resistance, then nothing, a silence like a word finally said right. She grinned before she could stop herself.

"There," Toma said, satisfied. "You're a shipwright now. Don't tell your mother."

"My mother would faint." Junie sat back on her heels, flexing her bandaged forearm where the knife had opened it. It ached less each day, though the ache had changed character—from something sharp and frightening to something almost companionable, a reminder she'd paid for her place aboard and the receipt had been accepted. "She thinks I'm apprenticed to a dressmaker in Corvane City."

"You could be. You'd be terrible at it."

"I'd be *fine* at it."

"You'd sew your sleeve to the dress and set fire to the shop within a week." Toma tossed her the next rivet. "You're a river rat now, girl. Own it."

Outside, through the open loading hatch, she could see Ada on the foredeck talking to Miriam Sato. The clerk had changed since the boarding fight—since the steamer, since the fog, since everything. She stood straighter now, and she'd stopped wearing her government coat, trading it for one of Ruth's old oilskins two sizes too big, sleeves rolled four times. She looked, Junie thought, like someone who had walked out of one life and hadn't yet decided what the next one was made of, and was in no hurry to decide.

They were looking at the same thing Junie had been watching all morning: the horizon downstream, where the iron silhouette of Vane's salvage steamer had been anchored for two days.

This morning, it was gone.

* * *

The news came up the gangplank with the fish seller's boy, who charged them a copper for it and thought he was robbing them blind.

"They ran in the night," he said, delighted. "Full steam, no lights, straight down the middle channel like the devil himself was rowing after them. Old man Pell saw them go from his porch. Says Captain Vane didn't even wait to load his barges—left half his salvage gear sitting on the dock at Graywater, just *left* it."

"Left it," Ruth repeated, from her chair by the wheelhouse, where she seemed permanently installed now, tea in hand, as if the boat might wander off without her anchoring it. "Man spends a fortune chasing a paper and leaves his own tools behind. That's not running scared. That's running *ruined*."

"It's the testimony," Miriam said quietly. She came down the deck with a folder under her arm—her old habit, folders, though this one held nothing official anymore. "I sent copies ahead on the mail packet. Three ministries, two newspapers, and the harbor commission at Corvane City. His buyers' names, his bribes, the survey team he bought and then buried. By tonight there won't be a port from here to the sea that will sell him fuel or let him tie up."

"And the survey itself?" Toma asked. "The vein? The papers that were going to condemn half the towns on this river?"

Miriam looked at Ada. Ada looked at the water.

"The river has them," he said.

That was all he'd say, and Junie understood by now that it was all anyone would get. She'd been there at the confluence—had watched from the rail, arm still bleeding, as Ada took the sealed case to the stern of the *Grace*, weighed it with chain from the anchor locker, and gave it to the deepest channel with less ceremony than most men give a dead fish. No speech. No hesitation, or none visible. Just the splash, and the bubbles, and then Ada standing at the rail a long time with his hands empty, looking like a man who had set down something so heavy he'd forgotten what it felt like to stand upright.

She'd hated him a little, that day. Her whole village's future had been in that case—every farm, every family, every name she'd grown up with, all of it hanging on whether some ministry clerk stamped CONDEMNED or SPARED. And Ada had thrown the answer into the water.

It had taken her two days of thinking to understand, and the understanding had arrived not as an argument but as a feeling, the way most true things did: if the papers existed, someone would always be coming for them. Vane. The next Vane. The ministry itself. Her town would spend its whole life under a knife that never fell, waiting. But with the papers gone—truly gone, beyond any diver, any fleet, any bribe—there was nothing to steal, nothing to trade, nothing to condemn anyone *with*. The fate of the towns went back to the towns. Back to people like her mother, arguing in meeting halls, making their own mistakes instead of having them mailed downriver in a government envelope.

Her mother would have hated it. Her mother believed in papers, in stamps, in the power of being right on record.

But her mother had never watched a man bleed on a deck to protect an envelope either.

"They'll rebuild the case," Miriam said now, echoing her thoughts. "Re-survey. Someone will map that vein again someday—it's too big to stay hidden forever. But it'll take years, and when it happens, it'll happen in daylight, with hearings, with the towns in the room." She hugged the folder to her chest. "I burned my career for that much. I've decided it was a fair price."

"You'll get work," Ruth said. "Woman who can bring down a salvage baron with the mail packet? Every shipping concern on this river will want you."

"I wasn't fishing for compliments."

"No," Ruth agreed placidly, sipping her tea. "But you'll get them anyway."

Junie laughed, and the sound surprised her—bright and easy, bouncing off the wheelhouse glass. When had she last laughed like that? Before the stowaway crate. Before the gorge. Before the night she'd learned what a knife felt like and what her own screaming sounded like and what it meant to have three near-strangers come running across a dark deck because *she* was theirs now, and that was simply a fact of the world, like weather.

Ada turned at the sound of her laugh. For a moment their eyes met, and something passed between them that needed no words—something that had started the day she'd confessed her village's name in the chart room, expecting to be put ashore, and instead had watched him check the tide tables and say, "Then we're not turning around."

He nodded at her bandaged arm. "How is it?"

"Healing. Toma says I can go back on rivets tomorrow."

"Toma says you're a menace with a rivet gun."

"Toma says a lot of things." She grinned at the engineer, who raised both hands in surrender.

Ada almost smiled. On his face it was a rare weather event, and everyone on deck pretended not to notice so as not to scare it off. Then he turned back to the rail, to the empty horizon where the steamer had been, and Junie saw the other thing cross his face—the thing that never fully left him, the thing that lived behind his eyes like a stone at the bottom of clear water. Ten years of it. A brother in the channel. A wheel in his hands.

But it was lighter now. She could see that too. Whatever he'd found down there in the drowned canyon, in the wreck of the old ferry, he'd brought some of it back with him, and it wasn't only grief.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the work was the best part—that was the thing nobody told you about nearly dying together. Afterward, everything ordinary became luminous.

Toma had the engine room torn open to its bones, and by evening he had Junie and Miriam elbow-deep in it with him, passing tools, learning names of parts that sounded like insults (*the stuffing box, the bilge strum box, the damn fool governor*). Ruth supervised from a stool she'd carried down herself, offering commentary that was forty percent useful and sixty percent stories about pilots dead before Junie was born. The engine came apart and went back together better than it had been in years, and when Toma finally threw the switch and the great heart of the boat turned over—clean, strong, no rattle, no complaint—the whole crew cheered like they'd won a war.

Which, Junie supposed, they had.

They ate on deck because the galley stove was still being resealed and because the evening was warm enough to allow it. Stew from the fish seller's boy's mother, bread from the landing's bakery, tea brewed strong enough to strip paint. They ate off mismatched plates with the lanterns lit, and the talk went where talk goes among people who have survived something: sideways, into smallness, into jokes.

"—and then," Ruth was saying, "Hollis Brand turns to me, white as a sheet, and says, 'Ruth, are we aground?' And I says, 'No, Hollis, we're *sinking*, aground would be an improvement,' and the man *faints*, right onto the wheel—" She slapped her knee. "Forty years I piloted that stretch and never once hit anything, but Hollis Brand fainted on my deck six separate times."

"What happened to him?" Miriam asked.

"Became a banker. Fainted there too, presumably, but onto softer surfaces."

Junie found herself telling her own story without quite deciding to—the mining town, the meeting where the surveyor's map had gone up on the wall with its red line drawn through the valley, her father's face, the crate she'd hidden in among the salted fish. She'd told pieces of it before, in bad moments, in fear. This was the first time she told it as a story, and the crew received it as one, groaning in the right places, laughing when the crate's owner came aboard and she'd had to share his lunch to keep him from opening it.

"That's how you got your sea legs," Toma said. "Three days in a fish crate. Most people pay for lessons."

"Most people don't get the smell as a souvenir."

"Oh, we noticed," Ada said mildly, and the table went off again, and Junie laughed until her ribs hurt, and realized somewhere in the middle of it that her eyes were wet and that it wasn't entirely from laughing.

Later, when the plates were cleared and Ruth had gone to her bunk and Toma was showing Miriam how to read the tide gauge by lamplight, Junie found herself alone at the stern rail beside Ada. Below them the black water slid past the hull, carrying the lantern light away in long trembling ribbons.

"My arm doesn't hurt much anymore," she said. "Except when it rains. Toma says that means it's becoming a story."

"He'd know. Man's got scars enough to write a library."

"Does yours do that? Hurt when it rains?"

Ada was quiet a moment. "Mine doesn't rain," he said. "Mine's more like... a tide. Comes in, goes out. Some days it's way out and I forget it's there. Some days it's all the way in and I can't remember anything else." He turned his hands over on the rail, studying them as if they belonged to someone else. "Used to be it never went out at all. Ten years, permanent high water." He glanced at her. "It's lower lately. I don't know why."

"I think you do."

"Maybe." He almost smiled again. "Maybe I do."

Downstream, the current pulled steady toward the sea, toward the towns, toward everything they'd fought a war over and given away. Somewhere down there, in meeting halls lit by oil lamps, her mother's neighbors were arguing about their own future—loudly, imperfectly, free. Junie breathed the river air, coal smoke and mud and fish, the smell of home even though home was a hundred miles upstream, and thought: *this is what safe feels like.* Not walls. Not papers. Just a deck, a crew, a boat that talked to you through the steel when you held the rivet square.

"When do we cast off?" she asked.

"Day after tomorrow. Tide's right at dawn." Ada looked at her sidewise. "Unless you want putting ashore. There's a packet at noon. Your mother's probably got a dressmaker's apprenticeship waiting."

Junie considered it, theatrically, for exactly one second. "And leave this floating disaster in the hands of you lot? You'd sink inside a week."

"Probably," Ada agreed solemnly. "Toma alone is good for two fires."

"I heard that," came Toma's voice from the engine hatch.

"You were meant to."

Junie laughed and leaned her arms on the rail, and the *Marrow's Grace* rocked gently beneath her, patched and scarred and seaworthy, holding its little family against the dark the way it had held them against everything else.

Above the gorge, far upstream, the first stars were coming out over the gate where the river squeezed through stone—and Junie noticed, without knowing why it mattered, that Ada was looking at them too, at that particular bend of dark hills, with an expression she hadn't seen on him before.

Not grief. Not fear.

Something more like a decision.
