## 22. Horizon Line

The gorge gate had not changed. That was the first thing Ada noticed, standing on the shelf of black stone where the river squeezed itself between the old cliffs, and it was the last thing he expected to feel about a place that had taken so much: recognition without dread.

The water ran high with the spring melt, green-gold in the morning light, folding over the submerged teeth of rock that Ruth had threaded them through in the dark all those weeks ago. From up here, on the ledge where the surveyors' cairn still stood, the narrows looked almost gentle—a lie the river told in daylight, the same way grief told its own lies at night, whispering that the past was survivable if you just kept moving fast enough to outrun it.

Ada knelt at the base of the cairn and took the compass from his coat pocket.

It was a small thing, heavier than it looked, brass worn to bare metal at the edges where his brother's thumb had worried it for years. The glass was clouded now, fogged from its decade underwater, and the needle swung lazy and drunk when he tilted it, catching on something inside that no longer worked as it should. He'd cleaned it as best he could in the boat's galley, three nights running, with vinegar and a jeweler's cloth Toma had produced from some engineer's hoard of impossible tools. But some things don't come back from the deep clean. Some things carry their drowning with them.

Like brothers. Like captains who were at the wheel.

He turned the compass over in his hands. On the back, the engraving his brother had paid a dockside jeweler two coins for, laughing the whole time: *FOR THE MAN WHO ALWAYS KNOWS THE WAY.* Ada had hated those words for ten years. He'd hated them because they weren't true—because on the night the ferry went down, he hadn't known the way at all, hadn't seen the snag until it was under the hull, hadn't been able to do anything but watch the water take everything while he stood at the wheel of a boat that floated.

He had spent a decade measuring his life in miles because miles could be counted, and years could not, and what happened in years—the drowning, the silence after, the slow erosion of everyone who had once called him friend—what happened in years had no arithmetic that made sense.

But the river had taught him different arithmetic this season. Six tides upriver. A clerk with empty pockets and a spine of iron. An engineer who gambled a boat's heart and won. A deckhand who stowed away carrying a whole town's future in her thin shoulders. An old pilot who proved the water still answered to those who listened. And a doctor in a cave, guarding truth like an ember in cupped hands.

And a ferry at the bottom of a drowned canyon, holding his brother's bones the way the river holds everything—not cruelly, not kindly, but completely.

*Brother Water*, Ruth had called it, watching him come up from that last dive with the compass clenched in his fist, coughing half the channel out of his lungs. She'd said it the way she said most true things: plainly, without ceremony. *The river keeps what we give it. Sometimes it gives back what we need.*

Ada set the compass on the flat stone at the center of the cairn.

"I'm not keeping it," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the gorge, small against the water's constant speech. "I know you'd tell me that's foolish. You always said a working man doesn't bury his tools." He almost smiled. Almost. "But this one was never mine. It was yours. And I've been carrying it like a debt instead of a gift."

The needle swung, settled, swung again. Pointing nowhere useful. Pointing everywhere at once.

Below him, the *Kestrel's Wake* sat at anchor in the eddy below the gate, patched and re-patched, her hull scarred from Vane's boarding hooks and her engine rebuilt twice by a man who talked to her like a lover. She rode low in the water but she rode true. Toma had pronounced her sound three days ago with the gravity of a physician delivering news, and then immediately begun finding new improvements to make, because Toma could no more leave an engine alone than the river could leave a bank alone.

Junie was on deck, coiling lines with the quick economy she'd learned in a single season—from runaway to able hand faster than anyone Ada had ever shipped. Her arm was still wrapped where the boarding knife had caught her, but she used it without favoring it now, and when she caught him looking she raised her chin in that defiant way of hers, daring him to make something of it. He didn't. Some wounds became stories. Hers was becoming one already; he'd heard her telling it to Miriam last night on the foredeck, embellishing freely, the knife longer and the fight darker with each telling, and Miriam letting her, because Miriam understood that a story retold is a wound redrawn.

Miriam Sato stood at the rail with a leather satchel on her shoulder—the government's satchel, though it held nothing of the government's anymore except her resignation, signed and sealed, delivered to the last way-station with a courier's fee she'd paid from her own nearly-empty purse. Her testimony against Vane was already moving downriver ahead of them, riding the mail packets like a spark on dry grass. By the time they reached the lower landings, every harbor master would know Corvo Vane's name the way they knew the names of storms. His fleet would be seized or scattered. His buyers would find other sellers, or find nothing. It didn't matter anymore. What mattered was that Junie's town would stand, and every town like it, because the papers that would have condemned them lay in the deepest channel of the drowned canyon, wrapped in chain, given to the river beyond all reach of fleet or fortune.

Some inheritances are meant to be let go. Ada had learned that at the bottom of a channel, thirty feet down, in the green silence where his brother waited. He'd learned it holding a sealed case over the rail and feeling how light it was, how light it had always been—all that killing and drowning and burning for a weight of paper that the current took without even a ripple of complaint.

Ruth came up the trail behind him, moving with the deliberate care of a woman whose knees had opinions but whose will had never lost an argument to them. She lowered herself onto the cairn's edge beside him without asking, which was her way, and looked out at the narrows with eyes that had read this water before there was a flood, before there was a gorge gate, before the maps changed and the world along this river changed with them.

"You buried it, then," she said.

"I gave it back."

"Mm." She considered the compass where it lay. "My husband had a watch. Stopped the day he died. I carried it twenty years before I understood I wasn't carrying him. Just the stopping." She tapped the stone once, softly. "The river'll keep it honest. Better than a drawer does."

Ada looked at her. Old pilot, old grief, old knowledge—and still here, still shipping out, still calling lines through rapids no pilot had survived before her. "You ever think about staying ashore?"

"Every winter." She grinned, toothless and fierce. "Then the ice breaks up and I remember I'm a liar."

Below, Toma's voice rose from the engine hatch in a stream of profanity so inventive that Junie laughed out loud across the water. Something had seized, or threatened to seize, or was being prevented from seizing through sheer verbal force. The boat shuddered, caught, steadied. Toma emerged wiping his hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration, and shouted up at the ledge with the unshakable confidence of a man who had run the rapids and intended to keep running everything else:

"She's ready when you are, Captain! Don't let the old woman talk you into waiting for a better tide. There's no better tide. There's only tides!"

"There's only tides," Ruth agreed solemnly, and Ada laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the first one he could remember in longer than he wanted to count.

He stood. He looked at the compass one last time, at the clouded glass and the wandering needle and the engraving that had finally become true, though not in any way his brother could have meant it: *for the man who always knows the way.* Ada knew the way now. Not because the needle pointed. Because the crew did.

"Keep it safe," he told the river. "Both of you."

Then he turned and climbed down toward the boat, and Ruth followed, and neither of them looked back, because the dead don't need watching—they need remembering, and remembering is done facing forward.

* * *

They cast off on the afternoon tide, and the *Kestrel's Wake* went down through the gorge gate like a word being spoken correctly after long practice.

Ruth called the lines from the bow, her voice carrying that particular music it found only in moving water. Ada steered. The wheel felt different under his hands—not lighter, exactly, but honest, the way a tool feels when you've stopped pretending it's something else. The narrows roared around them, white water climbing the hull, spray coming over the rails in cold sheets, and Junie whooped from the foredeck like the wild thing she was, and Toma sang something obscene and rhythmic in the engine room to keep the beat of his pistons company, and Miriam stood at the rail with her face turned into the wind, eyes closed, wearing the expression of a woman who had finally put down something heavy and discovered her arms still worked.

They shot the gate in eleven minutes. Ruth timed it. She announced the time to no one in particular, wrote it in the little grease-pencil notebook she kept in her coat, and said, "Could be faster," which everyone understood to mean she was already planning to prove it.

Beyond the gorge, the river opened wide and easy, running gold-green through country that was beginning, at last, to look inhabited in a good way—smoke from chimneys, nets drying on racks, children running the banks to wave at them. Word traveled fast on a river. Faster than mail, faster than fleets. They knew the *Kestrel's Wake* now at every landing from the confluence down. They knew what her crew had done, or versions of it, each version larger and less accurate than the last. At Miller's Bend, an old man had pressed a sack of apples into Junie's arms and refused payment, saying his sister lived in the mining country and his family ate tonight because of what they'd sunk. Junie had cried about it later, privately, and denied crying, publicly, and Ada had pretended to believe her, which was its own kind of kindness.

Now, running the open water with the evening coming on soft and violet, Ada found himself doing something he hadn't done in ten years: thinking about tomorrow without flinching.

There were charters waiting. Real ones—grain contracts, mail runs, a survey outfit downriver that had heard about Ruth's passage of the gorge and wanted her badly enough to name a number that made Toma whistle. There was refitting to do, a proper haul-out in the fall, new planking for the hull, maybe—Toma's eyes had gone bright describing it—a boiler conversion, if the money held. Money might hold. Money, for the first time since Ada had taken the wheel of this boat, might actually hold.

But that wasn't the inheritance either. He understood that now, standing at the wheel with the sunset laying a road of fire across the water ahead of them. The charter money would spend. The hull would wear. Even the river would change, as it always changed, cutting new channels and abandoning old ones without apology. None of that was what he carried.

What he carried was a crew.

A runaway who'd become the best hand aboard. An engineer who'd bet a boat's heart on his people and been right. An old woman who owned the gorge and every secret in it. A clerk who'd traded a career for a conscience and never once looked back. People who had boarded his boat as strangers and debts and desperate chances, and who had somehow, tide by tide, become the thing he'd stopped believing he deserved: a family, loud and wounded and stubborn, arguing about dinner in the galley right now in voices that carried clear across the water.

Miles escaped—that was how he had measured his life. Miles put between himself and the drowning, himself and the guilt, himself and the wheel of a ferry on a night gone wrong. Every landing a place to flee from, every charter a means of motion, motion itself a kind of anesthesia. He had thought he was running a boat. He had been running.

No more. These were miles chosen. This heading, this crew, this river accepted rather than endured. Tomorrow they'd take the grain contract, and next month the mail run, and in the fall the haul-out, and none of it would erase anything—the ferry was still at the bottom of the channel, the case still in the deep, his brother still gone—but the going would be different now. Chosen going. The only kind worth doing.

"Captain." Junie appeared at his elbow at the wheel, apple in hand, biting it with a crack. She pointed forward with the fruit. "Light on the starboard bank. Landing lamp. Somebody's signaling."

Ada saw it: a lantern swinging in the established pattern—three short, one long. Not distress. Request. He picked up the glass and focused it on the landing, where a small figure stood on the dock beside a stack of crates and, of all things, a piano, of all cargoes, roped down under canvas.

"Charter?" Junie asked hopefully.

"Looks like freight." He handed her the glass. "Piano, by the shape of it."

"A piano." Junie lowered the glass, grinning. "Upriver? Who moves a piano upriver?"

"Somebody with more money than sense and worse pilots than ours." Ada eased the wheel over, and the *Kestrel's Wake* leaned into the turn, answering him sweet as a promise. "Or somebody who heard there's a crew on this river that doesn't say no to hard water."

Behind him, Ruth appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, reading the landing lamp over his shoulder, already calculating the tide. Toma's head popped up from the engine hatch, smelling cargo the way sharks smell blood. Miriam came forward from the rail, satchel over her shoulder, and asked quietly, "Shall I draft terms?"—and Ada realized she'd been drafting terms in her head since the lantern started swinging, because that was who she was now, theirs, one of them, done running too.

The lamp swung its patient signal. The river ran gold toward the darkening hills. Somewhere behind them, at the bottom of a drowned canyon, a brass compass rested on a bed of stone, pointing every direction at once, and Ada Eriksen steered his family toward the light without needing it at all.

"Bring us alongside," he said. "Gently. We're carrying a piano."
