## 1. Debts in Fair Weather

The rain held off for eleven days that spring, and everyone in Fair Weather agreed afterward that it was the worst luck the town had ever had.

Fair weather was a lie the highlands told in order to lull you. Rosalind Thornbury had learned that lesson at nineteen, crouched under a tarpaulin while a cell the size of a cathedral walked over her camp and took her tent, her radio, and two years of field notes into the sky. She had learned it again at twenty-six, when the flood took the low quarter of Karst Hollow and forty-one people with it, and she had stood on the levee in the aftermath counting bodies because there was no one else willing to do it. Now, at thirty-four, she was still learning it — learning it the way a debtor learns interest, patiently, repeatedly, with a kind of grim professional admiration.

Eleven days of blue sky. The river dropped. The mud on the ridge roads baked into cracked plates you could walk on without losing your boots. The market stalls along the canal put out their awnings, and the awnings faded in the sun, and children who had not seen dry ground since autumn ran shrieking through the shallows like the water itself was a novelty. And all the while, in the back room of the Harbormaster's Office, a man named Callum Reeve sat with a ledger open in front of him and waited for her to come to him.

She made him wait nine of the eleven days. It was the only power she had left, and she spent it the way she spent everything now: too slowly, and not nearly enough.

* * *

On the ninth morning she woke before dawn in the loft above the chandlery, which was not hers but which she had occupied long enough that the chandler had stopped charging her rent and started, instead, simply deducting it from a running total he kept in his head and mentioned, cheerfully, whenever she bought lamp oil. The loft smelled of hemp rope and tar and the ghost of a hundred storms stored in canvas. Through the gap in the shutters she could see the mountains.

They were the reason she had never left. That was what she told herself, anyway, though she knew it was only half true. The other half was that leaving required money, and money required work, and work in the highlands meant guiding, and guiding meant the weather, and the weather was the only thing in her life that had never once lied to her. The peaks stood up out of the dark in ranks, white-capped even in fair weather, holding the last of the night's cloud against their shoulders like men shrugging off coats. Somewhere up there, past the tree line, past the passes, was country so violent and so beautiful that it had killed three of her friends and she still dreamed about it weekly.

She dressed in the dark. Wool shirt, canvas trousers, the boots with the good sole and the bad lace. She braided her hair wet from the basin because there was no time to dry it and because wet hair in the wind kept her honest about how cold the morning was. Down in the chandlery, old Maren was already sweeping, and he looked at her the way he always looked at her on the days she went to the Harbormaster's Office — a look that was half pity and half the particular respect one sailor gives another who has volunteered to sail into something.

"You want coffee first?" he said.

"Coffee after," she said. "If I drink it first I'll have something to lose."

Maren grunted. He was missing two fingers on his left hand and had opinions about everything, most of them correct. "Reeve's been asking after you."

"I know."

"He's been asking after you *politely*."

"That," said Rosalind, pulling on her coat, "is what frightens me."

* * *

The walk across town took twelve minutes and cost her more than the walk itself. Fair Weather in fair weather was a town pretending. The canal gleamed. The gulls worked the fish stalls. A woman selling oranges called out prices in the lowland tongue, and two dockhands argued amiably about a crane winch, and everywhere Rosalind went, people saw her coming and found urgent business facing the other direction. Not rudeness. Worse than rudeness. The careful, deliberate blindness of people who knew what the Vane Syndicate did to those who were seen being kind to its debtors.

She couldn't blame them. Three years ago she'd have done the same.

The Syndicate had come to the highlands the way water comes into a hull — quietly, below the waterline, through seams nobody thought to check. They called themselves an insurance concern, then a shipping concern, then a land concern, depending on the year and the audience, but the shape of them never changed. They lent. They waited. They collected. Edmund Vane himself had passed through Fair Weather twice that Rosalind knew of, once in a convoy of black vehicles that turned the mud of the main road to grease, and both times the whole town had developed a sudden fascination with the interiors of their own shops. He was not a large man, by reputation. He did not need to be. Large men were for the places where the Syndicate wanted you frightened. Where they wanted you *compliant*, they sent arithmetic.

The arithmetic lived in Callum Reeve.

* * *

The Harbormaster's Office had been built by a government that no longer existed, back when the highlands had mattered enough to administer. Its stone was stained black at the base by a century of floods, each one recorded in a brass line inside the doorframe, and Rosalind had always liked that about it — a building honest enough to keep its own scars where anyone could read them. The current Harbormaster was a figurehead the Syndicate tolerated the way a landlord tolerates a cat. The real business happened in the back room, behind a door with no nameplate, where the light came green through the canal-facing window and the air tasted of ink and cold tea.

Callum Reeve looked up when she entered, and smiled, and she hated him a little for the smile, because it was genuinely warm. That was the trick of him. He was not cruel. Cruelty was inefficient; cruelty made people run. Callum Reeve was patient the way the sea was patient, and pleasant the way the sea was pleasant, and if you forgot what he fundamentally was, you drowned.

"Rosalind." He closed the ledger gently, as if it were sleeping. "Sit down. You look terrible."

"I've been working."

"On what?"

"Salvage. A barge went down at the second bend in the autumn melt. Cargo recovery."

"And how did that pay?"

"It paid," she said, "some."

He opened the ledger again, to a page she suspected he had marked with a ribbon, and turned it toward her with two fingers, the way a dealer turns a card. Numbers marched down it in his small, immaculate hand. Principal. Interest, compounded quarterly, because the Syndicate believed in the seasons. Penalties, itemized with a precision that was almost tender. At the bottom, the total, and beneath the total, in a different ink, the number that the total became every ninety days whether she breathed or not.

She didn't flinch. She had practiced not flinching in front of this ledger for two years, and practice works, mostly, if you do it often enough.

"The barge job paid some," Reeve repeated. "And the survey work in the winter paid some. And the escort contract paid some. And yet." He tapped the total once, lightly, a pianist finding middle C. "Here we are."

"Here we are."

"You know what I admire about you, Rosalind? Genuinely?" He leaned back. Green light moved on his face. "You have never once asked me to forgive any of it. Most people beg. Some people threaten, early on, before they understand. You just keep walking in here and looking at the numbers like they're a storm front. Assessing. I find that restful."

"What do you want, Callum?"

"What I always want. For you to be solvent. It's in my interest, you know. Dead debtors are bad debtors. Fled debtors are worse." He folded his hands. "Which brings me to why I asked you in. There's work."

Her stomach tightened, and she kept her face still, because the last time Callum Reeve had said *there's work*, the work had been running Syndicate cargo upriver during a curfew, and she had done it, and she had hated herself with a thoroughness that had lasted months.

"Not cargo," Reeve said, reading her correctly, because he always did. "Nothing like that. This is a salvage commission. Legitimate. Contracted, papered, signed. A client of ours has lost property in the high country — north and west, up past the leased lands — and wants it recovered before the monsoon opens. He wants a guide. Someone who knows the high routes, the river fords, the weather. Someone with a crew."

"I don't have a crew."

"You can afford one," Reeve said, "if you take this."

"How much?"

He named the figure.

It was a good figure. It was, in fact, slightly more than the figure she needed, which was how she knew it was bait, because nothing in the highlands was ever worth exactly what it cost. She made herself think it through the way she'd think through a crossing: current, depth, footing on the far bank. Pay off the principal. Clear Maren's running total. New rope, new radio battery, six months of food. And underneath all of it, quiet as silt, the thought she tried never to let surface: *enough to leave.* Enough to get down to the coast, buy passage, start somewhere the mountains couldn't reach her and neither could the ledger.

"Who's the client?" she asked.

"A private party. You'll meet him before you sign."

"*Him*?"

Reeve's smile deepened by a degree. "You'll meet him tomorrow. There's a vehicle coming up from the lowlands tonight. Until then — consider the numbers. Consider the season. The monsoon opens in thirty days, give or take, and after it opens, nothing moves in the high country until it closes, and you know better than anyone what the high country does to the unprepared in the meantime." He slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. Blank, except for a letterhead she didn't recognize: a stylized peak inside a circle. "Terms will be filled in at the meeting. Read everything this time, Rosalind. Last time you didn't, and we both remember how that went."

She took the paper. It was heavy stock, expensive, absurd in a town where the fishmongers wrapped purchases in old chart pages.

"And if I say no?"

Reeve spread his hands, all warmth, all patience. "Then we're having this same conversation in ninety days, and the number at the bottom will be larger, and the season will be worse, and you'll be eleven days older and no drier. But sure. Say no. I'm not the one who decides these things." He picked up his pen, uncapped it, and returned his attention to the ledger as though she had already gone. "Oh — one thing. The client mentioned a name. Asked if you knew it."

She stopped at the door.

"Quill," Reeve said, without looking up. "Ada Quill. Ring any bells?"

The name landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it hit bottom.

"No," said Rosalind Thornbury, who was lying, and whose voice, thank God, had been trained by twenty years of screaming wind to stay level. "Never heard of her."

"Mm," said Callum Reeve, and wrote something down.

* * *

She did not go back to the chandlery. She walked instead, without deciding to, out along the canal and up the levee road, past the brass flood lines, past the last of the houses, to where the ground gave up on being a town and became grass and rock and the long climb toward the ridges. The mountains stood over her, white and indifferent. The sky above them was the hard, rinsed blue of fair weather, and she watched it the way a gambler watches a deck being shuffled.

Ada Quill.

Everyone in the highlands knew the name, the way everyone knew the names of the dead. Ada Quill, prospector, the woman who had mapped half the northern watershed by hand in the years before the surveys, who had found routes the government mappers missed and mineral claims the companies pretended didn't exist, who had gone up into the high country alone one autumn fourteen years ago and simply not come down. No body. No camp. Just her cabin near the ridge road, cold stove, clean floor, rifle gone, and a daughter — a girl of maybe ten — who had been taken in by the mission at Karst Hollow and grown up, as far as Rosalind knew, into someone the whole valley had agreed not to talk about, because grief plus mystery plus a child makes a story, and stories in the highlands attract the wrong kind of attention.

Rosalind had met Ada Quill exactly once, eleven years ago, on the ridge road above the second ford, in weather that would have killed anyone else. Rosalind had been twenty-three and lost and half-frozen, leading a survey team that had trusted her and shouldn't have, and Ada Quill had appeared out of the murk like something the mountain had decided to lend her — a broad, weathered woman in oilskins, silver-streaked hair plastered flat, eyes the color of wet slate — and had walked them down off the ridge in whiteout conditions without a compass, reading the wind off the rock faces, humming to herself the entire way. At the treeline she had stopped, looked at Rosalind, and said: *You listen well. That's rarer than you'd think, in this trade. Come find me someday, when you're ready to learn the north country proper.*

Rosalind had never gone. There had been the survey contracts, and then the debts, and then the years of walking into Callum Reeve's green-lit office, and the north country proper had remained a door she meant to open. And then Ada Quill had walked into the high country and become a story, and the door had closed.

Except now a client of the Vane Syndicate was asking after her by name.

Rosalind stood on the levee until the sun cleared the eastern ridges and laid a hard bright path across the canal, and the town below her woke fully into its eleven days of borrowed blue. Fair weather. The lie the highlands told. Somewhere north and west, past the leased lands, past the tree line, whatever Ada Quill had gone looking for was still up there — or whatever had gone looking for *her* was — and Edmund Vane's money wanted it found before the rains came to bury the trail.

She should say no. Every instinct she had honed in twenty years of storms said no. Take the shame, take the slow bleed, take the ninety-day conversation, but do not sign a blank paper with a circle-mountain on it and follow Syndicate money into the one corner of the world that still belonged to ghosts.

She started back down the levee road toward the chandlery, and that was when she saw the woman.

She came up the ridge road from the north, on foot, alone, and even at half a mile's distance Rosalind knew her stride — the long, economical walk of someone raised on scree, weight always forward, arms loose. Young. Early twenties. Wearing a prospector's pack far too big for her, straps cut down and re-stitched, and a canvas hat with a brim gone soft as cloth from years of rain. She walked like the mountain owed her nothing and she intended to collect anyway.

As she drew closer, the morning light came around the shoulder of the ridge and found her face, and Rosalind stopped walking entirely, because the face was impossible. It was a face she had seen once, eleven years ago, framed in whiteout, above a smile that had no business existing in that wind — and it was also a face she had seen in charcoal, drawn from memory by mission children, pinned for years to the noticeboard at Karst Hollow beside the words *MISSING*.

The woman reached the levee gate, saw Rosalind standing there staring, and halted. Up close she had Ada Quill's slate-colored eyes and none of Ada Quill's ease; she looked like someone who had walked a very long way on very little sleep, and had recently heard news that had rearranged the ground under her feet.

"You're Thornbury," the woman said. Her voice was roughened, lowland-edged, but the accent underneath was pure high country. "The guide. They said in the valley I'd find you in Fair Weather."

"Who's asking?"

The woman set her thumb briefly against her own collarbone, an odd, formal little gesture, like sealing something.

"My name's Sefa Quill," she said. "Ada Quill was my mother. And three days ago, somebody shot her."
