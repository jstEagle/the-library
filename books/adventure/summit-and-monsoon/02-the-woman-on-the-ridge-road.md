## 2. The Woman on the Ridge Road

The storm came off the high country at four in the afternoon, the way storms did in that part of the world — not arriving so much as assembling itself, cloud by bruised cloud, out of nothing but heat and grievance. Rosalind Thornbury watched it build from the shoulder of the ridge road with her hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had lost its waterproofing two seasons ago, and she felt the old pull in her chest, the one that had never once paid a bill.

*There you are,* she thought.

The truck sat behind her on the gravel verge, an '89 Kestrel with a cracked windshield and a radar dome bolted to the bed that had cost more than the vehicle and was worth less every year. Debt collectors had been through it twice looking for anything of value. They'd taken the spare radio and a case of instruments she'd have wept over if she'd had the time. What they'd left was enough to chase weather and nothing else, which was, she supposed, the only honest summary of her life anyone had ever written.

She let the storm come to her. That was the discipline nobody understood about the work — that most of it was standing still while the sky made up its mind. The air ahead of the front went strange and greenish, the light thickening like syrup, and the first fat drops hit the dust around her boots and died there, each one raising a small gray ghost. Then the wind arrived, smelling of wet stone and crushed pine, and the whole ridge exhaled.

Rosalind climbed into the cab and drove north along the ridge road with the storm pacing her on the left, close as a jealous lover. Lightning walked down its flank in long white strides. She knew this road the way other people knew their mothers' voices — every switchback, every culvert, every stretch where the fog came down so hard you drove by faith alone. She'd spent eleven years learning it, and it had given her back exactly nothing except the certainty that she was good at the one thing no one would pay for.

Her phone buzzed against the dash. She didn't look at it. She knew what it was. The Vane Syndicate's collections office sent messages in a tone of exquisite politeness, the way undertakers ask after your health. *Ms. Thornbury, we remain hopeful of resolving your account before the end of the quarter.* Fourteen thousand owed, accruing interest at a rate that felt personal. Her mother's medical bills, half of them; the wrecked season, the other half; and underneath it all, like silt under floodwater, the small ruinous fact that she had signed papers without reading them because the man across the desk had smiled and called her *the best eyes in the highlands.*

The rain hit then, all at once, a wall of it, and the wipers gave up any pretense of order. Rosalind slowed to a crawl. The world outside the glass became a gray-green blur shot through with the strobe of lightning, and she was thinking about turning back when she saw the figure in the road.

It stood at the far edge of her headlights' reach, swaying slightly, arms wrapped around itself. A woman, by the set of the shoulders. No pack, no rain gear — just clothes gone dark with water, plastered to her frame. In eleven years on these roads Rosalind had found broken-down vehicles, stranded hikers, one escaped convict, and a great many cows. She had never found someone walking alone on the ridge road with a storm cell directly overhead, because everyone who lived within a hundred miles knew better.

She stopped anyway. That was the other thing about her, the thing the debt collectors couldn't repossess.

The woman came toward the truck before Rosalind even had the window down, moving with a lurching urgency that told Rosalind everything before she saw the rest — the gray lips, the bloodless face, the way her hand slapped the door twice, asking permission and taking it in the same gesture.

"Please," the woman said, and the word came out shredded. "I need — I'm looking for someone."

"You're looking for a hospital," Rosalind said, already reaching across to shove the passenger door open. "Get in."

The woman got in. She moved like her joints were full of sand, and once she was seated she didn't so much sit as collapse into the shape of sitting, her head tipping back against the rest, rain running off her chin in ropes. Up close she was older than she'd looked from the road — sixty, maybe more, though it was hard to tell with people who'd been weathered the way she'd been weathered. Her hands, folded in her lap, were a map of old injuries: knuckles swollen wrong, a scar across the left palm like a healed lightning strike.

"I'm not sick," she said. "I'm dying. There's a difference, and I haven't got time to explain it."

"Everyone says that in my truck."

"Do they." The woman turned her head, and her eyes — pale, almost colorless, the eyes of someone who'd spent fifty years squinting into distance — took in the cracked windshield, the taped radar display, the rosary of parking tickets hanging from the mirror. Something moved through her face that might have been recognition, or relief, or both. "You're the storm chaser. Thornbury. You're the one who pulled the Hollis boy out of the wash at Miller's Bend."

"That was six years ago."

"People up here don't forget things like that." She coughed, wet and deep, and Rosalind's medic instincts — such as they were, secondhand, borrowed from a disgraced friend — catalogued the sound and disliked it. "I've been walking since dawn. I was going to try the town, but the river's up at the ford, and I thought — if anyone's fool enough to be on this road today—"

"You heard the thunder and gambled on me."

"I heard the thunder and prayed," the woman said. "The gambling came after."

Lightning flashed and the thunder followed almost immediately, a crack like the sky splitting along a seam. Rosalind put the truck in gear. "Hold on. We'll go down to Karst Hollow — there's a clinic—"

"No clinics." The woman's voice sharpened, and for a moment it had iron in it, the voice of someone used to being obeyed in bad terrain. "No hospitals, no police, no officials of any kind. Do you understand me? If you take me anywhere official, everything I'm about to give you dies in a filing cabinet."

Rosalind looked at her. Rain hammered the roof. Somewhere behind them the storm was chewing through the timber on the ridgeline, and ahead the road dropped into a darkness already filling with fog.

"And what is it you're about to give me?" Rosalind asked.

The woman reached inside her coat — slowly, deliberately, showing her hand empty first, an old habit from a hard life — and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with wire. It was smaller than a bread loaf and heavier than it should have been. She held it in both hands on her knees like something consecrated.

"My name was Ada Quill," she said. "Prospector. Thirty years in the high country, most of them legally. And three weeks ago I found something in the passes that has gotten two men killed already, and will get more killed before it's done, unless it gets out." She coughed again, longer this time, and when she lifted her head there was blood at the corner of her mouth, bright against the gray. "I don't have children I can trust. I have a daughter I haven't spoken to in nine years, and the people hunting me know her name. So I'm giving it to a stranger with bad credit and a fast truck, because you're the only person I've met in a week who isn't afraid of the wrong things."

"That's quite a speech," Rosalind said quietly. "You rehearse it walking?"

"Every step." Ada Quill almost smiled. It transformed her face for a moment, showed the young woman who'd once walked into those mountains with a pick and a mule. Then the smile guttered out. "I'm not asking you to believe me yet. I'm asking you to drive."

Rosalind drove. Down off the ridge, into the fog bank, wipers flailing, the world reduced to twenty feet of gravel and the yellow pulse of the hazard lights she'd switched on out of some deep animal prudence. Beside her, Ada Quill talked — haltingly at first, then with gathering force, the way rivers talk when the ice goes out.

She told her about the convoy. A government run, eight months ago, official freight, sealed manifests, going over the pass at Broken Trucks in the last clear window before winter. She'd been trapping fox above the tree line and seen it go through, and later, coming down after the melt, she'd found where it had ended up — not at the bottom of the pass where the official report said, but three miles east, off the mapped route entirely, in a canyon that didn't appear on any survey she'd ever seen. Wreckage everywhere. Bodies. And crates, dozens of them, stamped with markings that meant nothing to her and everything, she suspected, to whoever had ordered the route changed.

"There were documents," Ada said. "Waterproof cases, the kind surveyors use. I took what I could carry. I read what I could bear." Her voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone carrying something fragile across ice. "It wasn't freight, Ms. Thornbury. It was evidence. Of what happened at Corven's Landing, two years back. The village they said was destroyed by the flood."

Rosalind's hands tightened on the wheel. Everyone in the highlands knew Corven's Landing. Forty-one dead, officially an act of God, the kind of tragedy that got a memorial plaque and a line in the almanac. She'd driven past the rebuilt settlement a dozen times.

"The flood didn't kill them," Ada said.

"How do you—" Rosalind stopped. Started again. "Who covered it up?"

Ada Quill was quiet for a long moment. The fog pressed against the glass. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped, as if the truck itself might be bugged, as if the mountains had ears.

"Follow the money that flowed in afterward. Follow the company that bought the reconstruction contracts. Follow the man whose signature is on the convoy's authorization, buried three pages deep in a document he thought would never see daylight." She turned her colorless eyes on Rosalind. "Vane. Edmund Vane. Your creditor, if the gossip in the low towns is worth anything."

The truck's tires hummed on wet gravel. Rosalind felt the number — fourteen thousand, accruing — settle onto her chest like a stone.

"I owe them money," she said.

"I know." Ada's mouth twisted. "God has a sense of humor. I walked thirty miles to find the one person in the highlands who can't afford to help me."

* * *

They stopped at the old fire watchman's hut below the second switchback, because Ada's breathing had gone wrong — a wet, rattling sound, each breath costing visibly more than the last. Rosalind got her inside, onto the cot that still smelled of decades of pipe smoke, and built a fire from the split logs stacked under the eave by some watchman now retired or dead. She had a first-aid kit in the truck, stripped by the collectors down to gauze, tape, and a bottle of aspirin, and she did what she could with it, which was nothing, and they both knew it was nothing.

"Pneumonia?" Rosalind asked, though she already knew the answer was bigger than pneumonia.

"Started as pneumonia," Ada said. "In the canyon. I slept three nights in wet wreckage, Ms. Thornbury. Whatever's killing me, it had a head start." She gestured at the oilcloth bundle, which Rosalind had set on the table between them like a bomb. "Open it."

Inside the oilcloth: a canvas map case, scarred and soft with age; a leather journal swollen with damp; and a stack of documents in clear waterproof sleeves, maybe forty pages, dense with typescript, tables, signatures. Rosalind touched the top page and felt the paper tremble under her fingers and realized the trembling was hers.

"The map shows the canyon," Ada said. "Every approach, every route the Syndicate's people would use to reach it. I marked the wreckage. I marked where I cached the crates I couldn't carry — there are more, do you understand? Enough to bury them. But the cache is useless without someone who can get up there and bring it down, and I am done getting anywhere." She paused, caught her breath, and the effort of the next words seemed physical. "And there's a girl. My daughter. Sera. She doesn't know I'm dying, and she must never learn it from them. If they can't find the evidence, they'll look for leverage, and she's the only leverage I have left. Find her before they do. Tell her—" For the first time, Ada Quill's composure cracked, and what showed beneath was just an old woman, alone, in a hut full of smoke and rain. "Tell her I kept the promise. She'll know which one."

Outside, the storm raked the hut, and somewhere upslope a tree went over with a groan like a living thing.

"Why me?" Rosalind said. "You said it yourself. I'm broke. I'm owned. You could find a journalist, a lawyer—"

"A journalist writes what an editor allows. A lawyer argues what a court permits." Ada's voice was thinning, fraying at the edges, but her eyes stayed fixed on Rosalind's with terrible clarity. "This needs someone who can drive into a monsoon and walk out of a canyon and knows every game trail in the high country, and who owes the devil money, because that means the devil underestimates her. He thinks you're finished. Finished people are invisible." She coughed, long and awful, and pressed the map case into Rosalind's hands with the last of her strength. "Thirty days. After that, the Syndicate's survey teams start working the eastern passes, and when they find the canyon — and they will find it — they'll burn what's left. Thirty days, Thornbury. Buy my death with something."

Rosalind held the case. It weighed nothing and everything. Through the one clean pane of the hut's little window she could see the storm marching away down the valley, dragging its lightning behind it like a bridal train, beautiful and indifferent, and she thought: *this is what it looks like when the sky makes up its mind.*

"What do I do about you?" she asked. "Tonight. The clinic—"

"Bury me in the morning, on the rise above the hut, facing the pass." Ada lay back on the cot, and her breath rattled like wind through a broken window. "And then stop wasting the hours talking to a dead woman."

* * *

She lasted until a little after dawn.

Rosalind sat with her through the worst of it, holding a hand mapped with old injuries, listening to a life recounted in fragments between breaths — a mule named Bess, a claim staked at nineteen, a husband lost to a crevasse, a daughter born in a tent during a storm much like this one. Toward the end, Ada's eyes fixed on the ceiling beams as if reading something written there, and she said, clearly, twice: *"The eastern approach. Don't trust the eastern approach."* And then, near the very last, softer, in a different voice entirely, the voice of nine years ago: *"Sera. The gate's open, love. Come home whenever."*

After, Rosalind dug the grave herself in the iron-gray light, the ground half-frozen, the spade from the woodshed bending on the first rock and surviving on stubbornness. She buried Ada Quill facing the pass, as instructed, and stacked stones over her because the highlands respected stones, and stood over the mound with her hat off in the drizzle feeling the shape of her own life quietly rearranging itself around a new weight.

Then she went back inside, dried the map case by the dead fire's residual warmth, and opened it properly for the first time.

The map was beautiful — hand-drawn, annotated in a cartographer's precise slant, the canyon rendered in cross-section with the cache sites marked in red. And beside the canyon, in fresh ink, Ada had drawn something else: a rough sketch of a road junction, a bridge symbol, and a name. *Karst Hollow.* Below it, a single line:

*If you cannot climb to the truth, make them come down to meet it.*

Rosalind stared at it for a long time. Then she took out her phone, scrolled past fourteen messages from the Vane Syndicate's collections office, and dialed the only number in the highlands she trusted — a disgraced medic who owed her a favor and a teenage runaway who owed her rent, and between the three of them, perhaps, thirty days' worth of miracle.

The phone rang. On the table, the journal fell open to its last written page, and Rosalind, glancing down, saw that Ada had recorded one final entry, dated yesterday, in a hand grown shaky:

*Met a chaser on the ridge road. She stopped. Prayed for that.*
