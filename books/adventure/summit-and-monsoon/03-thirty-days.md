## 3. Thirty Days

The rain came to Fair Weather three days after Rosalind signed her name, as if the sky had been waiting for her to commit before it made its position known.

She stood in the doorway of the harbormaster's office and watched it arrive — not falling so much as *arriving*, a grey curtain drawn across the bay, erasing the fishing fleet, the breakwater, the far hills, until the world contracted to the width of a street and the sound of water hammering tin. The locals called this the First Warning. The old-timers said you could set your calendar by it: thirty days, give or take, between the first hard rain and the monsoon proper, when the highlands closed like a fist.

Thirty days. That was what Vane had given her. That was what the mountains would give her, whether she was ready or not.

Behind her, the harbormaster — a woman named Okonkwo with forearms like dock rope and a voice that carried over engines without effort — slid a ledger across the counter.

"Vessel survey came back," she said. "You want the good news or the bad news?"

"I want the truth."

Okonkwo almost smiled. "Then I'll give you both at once. The *Kestrel's* hull is honest work. Someone loved her once." She tapped the page. "But she's been sitting in salt water on a mooring nobody paid for, and love doesn't keep. You've got rot in the transom, a fuel system full of varnish, and an engine that hasn't turned over since before the last election. If you're going upriver into the Green Wall in thirty days, you'll need every one of them, and a miracle besides."

"How much for the miracle?"

"That depends how much of the work you can do yourself."

Rosalind looked out at the rain. Somewhere under all that grey was a boat she'd bought with money she didn't have, pledged against a debt that grew like kudzu. Her father had taught her one rule about storms: *you don't fight the weather, you fight the clock.* The weather always won eventually. But a well-run clock could put you in harbor first.

"I can do most of it," she said. "I'll need parts. And hands."

"Hands cost money."

"Everything costs money. That's rather the point of my life at present."

* * *

The parts list ran to two pages. Rosalind wrote it by lamplight in the cabin of the *Kestrel* herself, because paying for a room had become a category of expense she could no longer justify. The boat smelled of bilge and old canvas and, faintly, of someone else's cigarettes from some other decade. Rain drummed the deck above her head like fingers drumming a table, impatient, waiting for her to make good.

She worked through the night with a pry bar and a headlamp, pulling soft wood from the transom in long pale strips that came away like flesh from bone. It was ugly, intimate work. By dawn her hands were split and her shoulders burned and she had learned the boat's body the way you learn anything worth knowing — by hurting it and being sorry.

The money problem solved itself the way money problems do: badly, partially, and with interest. Vane's advance covered materials. It did not cover labor, which meant Rosalind spent her days elbow-deep in fiberglass resin and her evenings walking the waterfront bars of Fair Weather looking for people desperate enough to work for shares.

Desperation, she discovered, has a particular look. It isn't the shuffling ruin you'd expect. It's often clean. It sits up straight. It makes eye contact a half-second too long, because it has nothing left to lose and knows exactly what that feels like.

* * *

She found Dr. Imara Sefu on the ninth day, in the charity clinic behind the fish market, stitching a trawlerman's scalp by the light of a window that hadn't been cleaned since the last administration.

Rosalind had heard the name before — everyone in the lowlands had. Sefu had been a trauma surgeon with a reputation that crossed borders, until the incident at Meridian Station. The official record said negligence. The unofficial record, whispered in the places where unofficial records live, said she'd refused an order to triage by wallet, treated the poor man bleeding out instead of the rich man with the bruise, and lost everything for it. Which version was true, Rosalind couldn't say. But she watched the woman's hands move — sure, fast, economical, not one wasted motion — and thought: *that's what competence looks like when it's been ground down but not killed.*

"You're staring," Sefu said, without looking up.

"I'm hiring."

"Then you're wasting your time. I don't do fieldwork anymore."

"Why not?"

Sefu tied off the stitch, cut the thread, and only then turned. She was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of tiredness that lives in the eyes rather than the face. "Because fieldwork is where I made my mistake. Because out there, past the last road, there's no one to check your work except God and the patient, and God doesn't file complaints." She stripped off her gloves. "What kind of expedition?"

"Salvage. Upriver, into the highlands. Monsoon season."

Sefu laughed — a short, dry sound with no humor in it. "Of course it is. And what are you salvaging?"

"Government property. Wrecked convoy, somewhere past the Leased Land."

Something moved behind the doctor's eyes. Not recognition — Rosalind would have sworn to that later, under oath if it came to it. Just a flicker, the way a fish moves under still water. "A convoy," Sefu repeated. "Which convoy?"

"The one nobody talks about."

For a long moment the clinic noise filled the silence — the trawlerman groaning, a child coughing somewhere in the back, rain on the tin roof. Then Sefu said, "Shares?"

"Ten percent. Plus food, plus the satisfaction of outrunning the weather."

"Five percent more and I bring my own kit."

"Done."

They shook on it, and Rosalind walked back out into the rain feeling, for the first time in weeks, something adjacent to hope. It wasn't until much later — too much later — that she would remember the flicker under the water, and wonder what the doctor had recognized in the word *convoy* that Rosalind herself had not yet understood.

* * *

The runaway found her, rather than the other way around.

Pip — no surname offered, no questions tolerated on the subject — appeared on the *Kestrel*'s gangplank on the twelfth day carrying a duffel bag, a wrench set, and the aggressive confidence of someone who had recently made an enormous decision and could not afford to reconsider it.

"I hear you need an engine hand."

"I hear you're fourteen."

"Fifteen." A chin lifted. "And I rebuilt a generator last winter that a grown man couldn't fix. Ask anyone at the cannery."

Rosalind asked. Everyone at the cannery confirmed it, along with the further detail that Pip had slept in the generator shed and eaten from the workers' mess and vanished the morning the cannery owner started asking his own questions about where the child had come from.

The responsible thing — the thing any sane person would do — was to send the kid back down the plank and report them to whatever passed for authorities in Fair Weather. Rosalind stood at the rail and weighed this. She thought about debts, and about the kinds of families that produce runaways, and about the fact that in thirty days she'd be running rapids in rising water with a crew of strangers, and that an engine hand who could actually work an engine was worth more than a clear conscience.

"You get seasick?" she asked.

"No."

"You follow orders?"

"When they're good ones."

"And when they're bad ones?"

Pip grinned, and there was something in that grin — bright, unguarded, entirely unbroken — that hurt to look at, the way sunlight hurts after a long time below decks. "Then we find out which of us is right."

"Welcome aboard," Rosalind said, and hated herself a little, and did it anyway.

* * *

The daughter was harder.

Rosalind had sent three letters to the address in the prospector's papers — *M. Quill, Ridge Road, above the ferry crossing* — and received nothing back but silence and weather. On the sixteenth day, with the transom glassed and the engine turning over rough but turning, she took the ridge road herself.

It climbed through terraces gone wild, past farmhouses with their shutters nailed for the season, up into cloud. The house at the top was small and stone and older than the road, and the woman chopping firewood in the yard stopped mid-swing when Rosalind came through the gate.

She had Ada Quill's jaw. That was the first thing Rosalind noticed — the same blunt, stubborn line from chin to ear that she'd seen on the dying woman in the hospice bed. Everything else was different: younger, broader, hands calloused in a farmer's pattern rather than a prospector's.

"You're the one writing letters," the woman said.

"I'm Rosalind Thornbury."

"I know who you are. Mama wrote me about you, near the end." The axe came down; the log split. "Wrote that you'd come. Wrote that I should listen to you." Another swing. "I'm still deciding whether that was love or delirium."

"It was probably both. Mothers are allowed."

That earned her a pause — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday be built. The woman leaned on the axe handle. "Nadia Quill. And before you ask: no, I don't want anything to do with her maps or her mountains or her dying business. I buried that woman twice. Once when she chose the ridges over us, and once in March. I don't intend to dig her up again."

"She left you something."

"She left me a lifetime of reasons to stay on flat ground."

"She left you a share," Rosalind said. "In whatever the map leads to. Ten percent, hers, willed to you. I've seen the paper."

Nadia stood very still. Down in the valley, the clouds were organizing themselves into something with intent. When she spoke again her voice had changed — quieter, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice. "What does the map lead to?"

"A wrecked convoy. Government plates. Past the Leased Land, high up."

The axe handle creaked under her grip. For a moment Nadia Quill looked not angry but *afraid*, and the fear was old, worn smooth like a river stone, the fear of something she'd been afraid of for years.

"My mother talked about a convoy once," she said slowly. "Drunk, one winter, which was the only time she talked about anything true. Said she'd seen something up in the passes she shouldn't have seen. Said men came asking after her the following spring, and she moved towns twice in a year, and after that she never took a contract above the tree line again. I thought it was the drink talking." She looked at Rosalind. "You're telling me it wasn't."

"I'm telling you I don't know yet. That's why I need to go."

"And if it's what I think it might be? If those men come asking again — this time about *you*?"

Rosalind thought of Vane's office, the rain on the glass, the handshake that had felt like a trap closing. "Then I suppose we'll find out what my ten percent is worth."

Nadia studied her for a long time. The wind came up the valley and flattened the grass and carried the smell of coming water. Somewhere below, faint as memory, the ferry horn sounded.

"Mama wrote one more thing in that letter," she said finally. "She wrote: *the girl reads weather better than I ever did, but she trusts the wrong people, and it will nearly kill her.*" She picked up another log, set it on the block. "I'm coming. Not for the money. Somebody on this expedition has to know which people are wrong."

* * *

By the twenty-fifth day the *Kestrel* floated, which felt like a personal miracle. The transom held. The engine ran — roughly, complaining, but running. Sefu's medical crates were lashed below; Pip lived in the engine room and emerged only for meals, coated in grease, happier than any human being had a right to be; Nadia had moved aboard with two sea chests and a rifle wrapped in oilcloth that she declined to discuss.

On the twenty-eighth day, Edmund Vane's car came down to the docks.

It was black and long and absurdly clean for the mud of Fair Weather, and it stopped at the head of the pier while the rain fell around it like a curtain held respectfully open. Vane stepped out under an umbrella held by a driver who never once got wet himself, and walked the length of the planks in polished shoes that did not slip, and stood at the *Kestrel*'s rail looking at her with the expression of a man inspecting a horse he intended to ride hard.

"Thirty days," he said. "You've used twenty-eight."

"I've used them well."

"So I see." He turned. Behind him, at the pier's foot, a second man had emerged from the car — big, quiet, wrong in a way Rosalind couldn't name, standing in the rain without an umbrella as though weather were a courtesy extended to lesser men. "I wanted to introduce Mr. Callum. He'll be joining the expedition. Company insurance, you understand. These are valuable assets we're recovering."

Rosalind looked at Callum. Callum looked at her, and smiled with nothing in it whatsoever.

"Room's tight," she said.

"We'll manage." Vane produced an envelope — thick, real, the weight of it obscene. "Half now, half on delivery. And Rosalind—" He smiled then, warmly, the warmth of a heating vent in a cold room. "The monsoon opens in two days. Whatever happens up there, whatever you find or fail to find — the Syndicate expects discretion. Total discretion. It's in the contract. Page nine."

He pressed the envelope into her hands, and the money was warm from his pocket, and she hated how badly she needed it.

"Two days," Vane repeated, stepping back toward the dry black mouth of the car. "The Green Wall won't wait, and neither will I."

The car pulled away. Callum stayed on the pier a moment longer, watching the boat, watching *her*, memorizing something. Then he followed.

Rosalind stood in the rain with half a fortune in her hands and the whole storm coming, and heard Nadia say quietly behind her, from the shadow of the companionway:

"There's one of the wrong ones."
