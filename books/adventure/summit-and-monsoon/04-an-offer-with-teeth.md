## 4. An Offer With Teeth

The rain came to Fair Weather the way debt did — slowly at first, then all at once, and never quite leaving.

Rosalind Thornbury watched it from the window of the Kestrel Room, the Syndicate's office above the old customs house, where the harbor fog pressed against glass gone soft with age. Below her, the town went about its business in the wet: fishermen hauling tarped nets, a woman beating a rug from a second-story balcony, children running the gutters with paper boats that would not survive the hour. Everything here was temporary. Everything here was held together with rope and prayer and the stubborn arithmetic of people who owed more than they owned.

She had been waiting forty minutes. She knew because she'd counted, and counting was what you did when you had nothing else to spend.

The room behind her was furnished in the style of men who wanted you to know they could afford better. Mahogany that had never seen weather. A carpet thick enough to swallow footfalls. On the walls, framed charts of coastlines the Syndicate had no honest reason to chart, and one large map of the highlands — the interior, the green wall of mountains where the monsoon spent half the year trying to wash the country back into the sea. Someone had pinned small red flags along a river system Rosalind knew too well. She tried not to look at them. She looked anyway.

The door opened without a knock.

"Miss Thornbury." The secretary was a thin man named Pell whose voice always sounded like it was apologizing for something he intended to do anyway. "Mr. Vane will see you now."

* * *

Edmund Vane received her standing at the window, hands clasped behind his back, as though he'd been posed there by a sculptor who specialized in patience. He was older than his reputation — silver at the temples, a face built for banknotes, the kind of handsome that came from good food and never once being told no. He wore gray. Of course he wore gray. Gray was the color of money that didn't need to shout.

"Rosalind Thornbury." He said her name like he was testing the weight of it. "Storm chaser. Salvage pilot. Formerly of the Meteorological Service, formerly of the Coastal Survey, formerly—" he smiled, thin as a razor's edge, "—formerly a great many things."

"Formerly employed," Rosalind said. "Currently solvent. That's more than most people in this room can say."

Vane laughed. It was a good laugh, warm on the surface, and completely empty underneath, like a drum. "I'm told you're direct. I find that refreshing in a town where everyone lies beautifully." He gestured to the chair across from his desk. She didn't sit. He seemed pleased by that, which annoyed her more than anything else so far.

"You bought something," Vane said. "Three weeks ago. From an estate sale in Marsh Town. A document."

Rosalind kept her face still. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, a sound like static, like the sea rehearsing.

"I buy a lot of things."

"Not this thing." Vane turned from the window at last and sat on the edge of his desk rather than behind it — a man who understood that looming was less effective than seeming casual. "A survey map. Hand-drawn. Provenance traced to a prospector named Ada Quill, deceased this spring, God rest her, in circumstances the coroner found unremarkable."

Something cold moved through Rosalind's chest, slow as the tide. *Unremarkable.* Ada Quill had died alone in a rented room with her boots still on, and the town had called it unremarkable because Ada Quill had been poor, and poor women died unremarkable deaths every day in Fair Weather.

"What's your interest in a dead prospector's map?" Rosalind asked.

"My interest," Vane said, "is in completeness. My company holds salvage rights across a considerable portion of the interior. When a document surfaces that purports to show terrain inside those holdings — terrain my people have surveyed, logged, and filed — I like to know about it. Unrecorded features are liabilities. Rivers that aren't where the charts say they are. Passes. Wrecks." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "Convoy routes."

The word landed between them and neither picked it up.

"The Syndicate will buy it," Vane continued. "Generously. More than generously. You paid, I'm told, eleven crowns and a bottle of gin."

"Twelve crowns," Rosalind said. "And the gin was mine."

"Twelve crowns and a bottle of gin. And I am prepared to offer you four hundred."

Four hundred crowns. Rosalind felt the number the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck in a dark street. Four hundred crowns was her debt to the Syndicate cleared twice over. Was a new hull for the *Kestrel*. Was Callum's medical license fees, was Juniper fed and shod through winter, was the particular silence of waking up owing nothing to anyone. It was everything she had come here wanting, wrapped in gray wool and handed to her with a smile.

It was also four hundred crowns for a piece of paper worth twelve.

"No," Rosalind said.

Vane raised an eyebrow, genuinely curious now. "No?"

"A map isn't worth four hundred crowns unless someone's afraid of what's on it. And nobody pays four hundred crowns to look at a map. They pay four hundred crowns so nobody else looks at it." She met his eyes. "So either you tell me what's in my highlands, Mr. Vane, or you can keep your money and I'll keep my paper, and we'll both go on wondering about each other."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain, and beneath it, faintly, the harbor bell counting out the hour.

Then Edmund Vane smiled, and it was the first honest expression she'd seen on him, and it was worse than all the dishonest ones.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's the woman who flew into the Veyra cell in 'nineteen with instruments failing and came back with readings the Service still uses. I read your file, Miss Thornbury. All of it. Do you know what struck me? Not the storms. Anyone can be brave in a storm; storms are simple, they only want to kill you. What struck me was the debts. Page after page after page. You are, financially speaking, the most interesting wreck in this harbor." He stood, walked around the desk, and opened a drawer. "So let me make you a different offer. One with teeth."

He laid a folder on the desk between them. Inside were photographs — grainy, official-looking, stamped with Service letterhead. Rosalind recognized the first one before her brain finished processing it: herself, younger, in Service blues, standing beside a meteorological balloon rig. The second was a ledger page. Her ledger page. Every crown she owed, itemized, cross-referenced, annotated in a clerk's neat hand.

"You already own my debt," she said quietly.

"I own the paper. Paper is boring." Vane tapped the photographs. "What I want is *you*. Sixty days from now, the monsoon season opens, and I am sending a salvage expedition into the interior. Officially: recovery of government property from a convoy lost last autumn in the Karst Hollow region. There are crates registered to the Ministry of Roads that my company has contractual right to recover. Unofficially—" he closed the folder, "—you guide us up the Quill route, we recover the cargo, and your entire ledger burns. Every crown. Clean slate. Plus two hundred crowns cash on completion, which I mention only because I suspect you'll refuse the first part out of pride and I'd hate for pride to cost you twice."

"And the map?"

"Stays with you. I don't need it. I need the woman who reads it." He leaned forward. "You see, Miss Thornbury, maps can be copied. Instinct can't. Ada Quill spent thirty years learning those mountains, and then she sold everything she knew to a stranger for twelve crowns and a drink, which tells me either she was dying or desperate, and my sources say both. But you — you've chased weather through the Green Wall twice. You know how the monsoon thinks. That's not something I can buy. So I'm buying the next best thing."

Rosalind looked down at the folder. Her own face stared up at her from the photograph, young and certain, wearing a uniform she'd been proud of once, before the Service folded and the pensions evaporated and the sky stopped being a science and became just weather again.

"And if I say no?"

"Then nothing happens today. Or tomorrow." Vane's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, the tone of a man discussing freight rates. "But debts accrue interest, Miss Thornbury, and the Syndicate's collectors are thorough men working in a small town. Your boat sits at anchor off Tern Street. Boats drift. Cargo goes missing. Licenses lapse. I wouldn't do any of it myself — I'm offering you a fortune — but the machine doesn't always distinguish between refusal and delay." He straightened his cuffs. "I'd hate to see the *Kestrel* impounded. I hear she's the last thing you own outright. Well — nearly."

The rain hammered the windows. Somewhere below, in the customs house, a door slammed, and the sound traveled up through the floor like a pulse.

Rosalind thought of Ada Quill dying with her boots on. Thought of the map rolled in oilcloth under her bunk aboard the *Kestrel*, twelve crowns of dying woman's knowledge, and how Ada had gripped her wrist at the end — Rosalind hadn't mentioned that part, hadn't told anyone — how the old woman had pulled her close with strength that shouldn't have been left in her and said, *Don't sell it. Don't sell it to anyone who smiles.*

Edmund Vane was smiling.

"How many people on this expedition?" Rosalind asked.

"Eight. Nine with you. Myself included — I believe a patron should see his investments firsthand." He ticked names off with evident pleasure. "My head of security, Renn Ostrava, whom you'll like; he doesn't talk. A cartographer. Two engineers. A camp doctor — though I understand you have your own medic these days, a Dr. Callum Reyes, late of St. Brigid's? Disgraced, I'm told, but competent. Bring him if you like; I pay well and ask few questions. That's the Syndicate way."

"And the convoy," Rosalind said. "The one that's lost. What was it carrying?"

"Ministry freight. Road-building equipment, mostly. Survey records." He waved a hand, bored already. "Paperwork and machinery. Why?"

"Because paperwork sinks deeper than machinery," Rosalind said. "And machinery doesn't usually need nine people and a storm season to dig it out of a pass."

Vane studied her for a moment, and behind the banknote face something moved — quick, guarded, gone again, like a fish turning under gray water.

"You're sharper than your file suggests," he said. "That's good. I want sharp people on this trip. The interior eats the soft ones." He extended his hand. "Thirty days, Miss Thornbury. We leave when the first cell comes off the wall. Think about it tonight. But think quickly — the season won't wait, and neither, I'm afraid, will your creditors."

Rosalind didn't take his hand. She looked at it instead — clean, manicured, unmarked by rope or salt or any honest work — and thought about teeth. An offer with teeth meant the teeth were somewhere, and they weren't in the money.

"I'll give you my answer tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow, then." Vane returned his hand to his side without offense, as though her refusal of it were simply another term he'd anticipated. "Pell will see you out. Oh — and Miss Thornbury." She paused at the door. "The map. If anything should happen to it before we depart — fire, theft, misadventure — the offer stands regardless. I'm buying your judgment, remember, not your paper. Though naturally I'd expect you to reproduce its contents from memory as best you can."

*Naturally,* Rosalind thought. *Naturally you'd expect me to hand you a dead woman's mountains from memory, free of charge, the moment my copy inconveniently burns.*

"Good night, Mr. Vane."

* * *

The rain followed her down the customs house steps and out into Tern Street, where the lamplighters were losing their argument with the wind. Rosalind walked with her collar up past shuttered chandlers' shops and the dark windows of the Mariners' Bank, past the pawnbroker's where three weeks ago she'd hocked her father's sextant to cover a mooring fee, and hated herself a little for the relief she'd felt, and hated Vane more for knowing exactly what that relief was worth.

The *Kestrel* rode at her mooring off the Tern Street dock, riding low, riding tired, her paint gone the color of old dishwater. Rosalind dropped into the tender and rowed out through the chop, and the harbor took the little boat and shook it like a dog shakes a rat, and she let it, because her arms needed the work and her head needed the cold.

Below decks, the lamp threw its small warm circle. Callum was asleep in the aft berth, one arm hanging, a medical journal open on his chest, rising and falling. Seventeen years a surgeon before St. Brigid's revoked him for a decision he still wouldn't explain to her — a man who could put a chest back together and couldn't look at his own reflection in the galley mirror. Juniper was curled in the bow compartment with the cat, both of them pretending they weren't keeping watch, the way teenagers and cats do.

Rosalind sat at the chart table. She unrolled the map.

Ada Quill's hand covered every inch of it — thirty years of mountains rendered in ink gone brown as old blood. Rivers drawn with the confidence of someone who'd drunk from them. Elevations noted in a cramped, furious script. And there, in the Karst Hollow region, in the pass the Ministry charts showed as blank green, a mark Rosalind had taken for a surveyor's symbol the night she bought it: a small rectangle, crossed through, with a date beside it in Ada's handwriting.

*Last leaf-fall. They came through at night. No lights.*

No lights. Convoy at night, in the highlands, in leaf-fall, running dark. Road-building equipment didn't run dark. Machinery didn't run dark.

Rosalind sat very still in the lamplight while the *Kestrel* breathed around her, timbers ticking, rain finding the one seam above the galley that no amount of pitch would fix. Four hundred crowns to burn her ledger. Two hundred more besides. Thirty days.

*Don't sell it to anyone who smiles.*

She thought: take the job. Go up, watch everything, say nothing, come down rich. She thought: refuse, and lose the boat by spring, and Callum's fees, and Juniper's winter, and whatever was left of her own name. She thought about the crossed-out rectangle, and the date, and *no lights*, and about how Edmund Vane had said *survey records* in the exact voice of a man describing something he'd already burned once.

From the bow compartment, Juniper's voice, sleep-roughened: "Did he offer you enough?"

Rosalind rolled the map slowly, along its old creases, and tied the oilcloth.

"He offered me enough to stop being afraid," she said.

Juniper was quiet a moment. Then: "Is that a yes?"

"It's a maybe." Rosalind pinched out the lamp, and the dark came in off the water, full of rain. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow I have to go find out why a dying woman crossed out a truck."

In the black, almost too soft to hear, Juniper said: "Maybe you should find her daughter instead."

Rosalind lay back on the bunk and listened to the monsoon beginning, far off over the green wall, a sound like the whole world clearing its throat.
