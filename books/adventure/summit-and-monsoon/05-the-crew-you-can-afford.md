## 5. The Crew You Can Afford

The hiring hall on Cordwainer Street smelled of wet wool, cheap kerosene, and the particular despair of men who had been awake too long. Rosalind stood at the long scarred counter with Vane's advance burning a hole in her coat pocket — five hundred in scrip, convertible at any Syndicate bank, which meant convertible nowhere that mattered — and read the board of names for the fourth time.

The rain had followed her down from the ridge road. It came now in a fine grey sift that turned the cobbles to grease and made the gas lamps halo like drowned saints. Inside the hall it was worse: steam rising off forty bodies, breath fogging the high windows, and beneath it all the low murmur of the unemployed doing arithmetic with their lives.

She was looking for three things. A medic. A driver who could handle the switchbacks above Karst Hollow with his eyes shut and his nerve intact. And someone young enough and stupid enough — no, she corrected herself, someone *hungry* enough — to carry loads up scree at altitude without complaint.

What she could afford was none of these things.

"Thornbury." The clerk behind the counter said her name the way a man spits out a fishbone. "You're back."

"I'm back."

"You cleared out of here in March owing the hall two weeks' posting fees."

"I'll square it today." She laid a ten-mark note on the counter and watched him look at it the way a starving man looks at bread he suspects is poisoned. The Syndicate's mark. Everyone in this room knew what it meant to take Vane money, and everyone in this room would take it anyway by Friday.

"There's a woman," the clerk said, grudgingly, wiping the counter where her note had sat as if it might stain. "Came in yesterday asking after expedition work. Highlands work." He lowered his voice. "Medic, she says. But there's talk."

"There's always talk."

"The talk is she lost her license. Ashfall Sanatorium. Something about the fever ward."

Rosalind felt the old tired weight settle onto her shoulders, familiar as a pack frame. "Where do I find her?"

* * *

Dr. Imogen Sallow worked out of a room above a chandler's shop on Tidewater Lane, and she received patients in a space barely wider than a coffin. When Rosalind climbed the stairs — past a hand-lettered sign reading CONSULTATIONS, NO QUESTIONS — she found the doctor elbow-deep in the chest cavity of a gull.

Not a patient. An anatomy lesson. The bird lay splayed and pinned on a oilcloth-covered table while Sallow traced the wing bones with a steel probe, narrating to an audience of one: a boy of perhaps fifteen with a burn-scarred forearm and the watchful stillness of a stray cat.

"—and here, you see, the humerus is hollow. Not weak. Hollow. The whole architecture of the thing is built on the principle that lightness survives." She looked up without surprise. "You're not my next appointment. My next appointment is a boil."

"I need a medic for a highland expedition," Rosalind said. "Thirty days, maybe more. Altitude work. Monsoon season."

"Monsoon season." Sallow wiped her hands on her apron with the unhurried thoroughness of a surgeon closing up. "And your patron?"

"A private party."

"A private party paying what, exactly? Because I can smell Syndicate scrip through a closed door, and I've learned what it costs to be paid in it."

The boy by the table had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Listening-still.

"Four hundred for the run," Rosalind said. "Half up front."

"Two hundred up front buys a lot of morphine and very little loyalty." Sallow untied her apron. "Ashfall. You've heard."

"I heard you lost your license."

"I lost nothing. I kept forty-one people alive in a ward built for twenty when the administration cut the coal ration to save money, and when eleven of them died anyway I wrote a report saying so, with numbers, and the report reached the papers before it reached the board." She smiled, and it was not a warm expression. "They took my license. They could not take my hands. Ask anyone in the marsh parishes whose child I pulled through the red fever last winter. Ask them what my license is worth."

"I'm asking what yours costs."

"Six hundred. And I choose what goes in the medical kit, and nobody opens it but me, and if we find people hurt out there — anyone, whatever side they're on — we stop and treat them. That's the price. Write it into the contract or don't hire me."

It was more than Rosalind had budgeted for the entire medical line. It was also, she realized with a small cold shock, the first honest number anyone had quoted her in months.

"Done," she said.

Sallow studied her for a long moment, the way she'd studied the gull. "You're the one who bought Ada Quill's map."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. The boy's head came up sharply.

"How—"

"Ada Quill drank herself to death in the Anchor and Compass over eleven days, and in the last three she told everyone who'd buy her a drink that she'd finally sold the thing that mattered. Half the dockside knows. The other half will know by Thursday." Sallow began pulling instruments from a drawer, wrapping them in linen with brisk economy. "Which means every sharp operator between here and the pass knows a salvage expedition is forming, and knows roughly where it's going, and knows it's carrying Vane money. You didn't buy a map, Thornbury. You bought a beacon."

The boy stood up so fast his chair went over.

"That's my mother's map," he said.

He was not a boy, Rosalind saw now, up close. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, all elbows and hunger, with Ada Quill's exact grey eyes set in a face that had never once been called pretty and had stopped caring. His voice cracked in the middle of the sentence and he pushed through the crack as though charging a door.

"She sold it to *you*. She died in a gutter and sold the only thing—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I'm coming on this expedition."

"You're not."

"I know those hills. I grew up in them until I was nine. I know what my mother knew about that country because she talked when she drank and I listened when she did. You want someone who can read her markings? Her shorthand? Because she had a shorthand, and I'm the only person alive who can read it."

Rosalind looked at Sallow. Sallow raised one eyebrow, which in her language constituted a full paragraph.

"What's your name?"

"Wren."

"Last name?"

"Just Wren."

Age, history, references — none of it existed, apparently. What existed was the crack in his voice and the fact that he was right about the shorthand; Rosalind had spent two nights with the map and three of its marginal annotations defeated her completely, little hooked glyphs like bird tracks in snow.

"Driver first," she said. "Can you drive?"

"No."

"Then you're a porter and a reader, and you get paid half rates, and if I say walk away from something, you walk away."

Wren's chin came up. For a moment Rosalind saw Ada Quill entire — the same stubborn architecture, the same refusal to be light. Then he nodded once, hard, like a man signing something.

* * *

She found the driver where the clerk had said she would: in the yard behind the Broken Axle, losing money at dice and winning arguments. Callum Reeve was a broad, weathered man with a face like a cliff face that had been arguing with the sea for fifty years and intended to keep arguing. He drove the high passes for the mail service until, the story went, he'd refused to carry a Syndicate consignment during a flood warning and dumped the whole load in the Karst river rather than risk the bridge.

"They never proved it was deliberate," he said cheerfully, when Rosalind mentioned it. "But they never gave me another route either. So. You're the Thornbury woman. Word is you're running Vane's errand into the Green Wall in the teeth of the season."

"Word travels fast."

"Word travels fast when it's being *carried*." He gathered the dice into a leather cup and looked at her with sudden directness. "Ask yourself who wants that word moving, and why. A quiet expedition hires quiet. Yours has been shouted from the rooftops since the prospector died. That's not luck. That's design."

A cold thread walked down Rosalind's spine. She thought of the clerk's easy gossip, of Sallow's warning, of how quickly everyone seemed to already know.

"Are you telling me not to go?"

"I'm telling you I'll drive for you, because I owe the passes a debt and I mean to pay it, and because whoever is shouting your business from the rooftops is going to be very surprised when you come back down with whatever's up there." He grinned, gap-toothed and unrepentant. "Besides. Four hundred, you said? I hear you're paying sixes now. I have ears."

* * *

That night, in her rented room above the chandler's shop — Sallow had insisted, "cheaper than an inn and I keep odd hours" — Rosalind spread the map across the floor by lamplight and stared at the annotations she couldn't read.

Wren sat cross-legged opposite, knees drawn up, watching her with Ada's grey eyes.

"Show me the ones you can't make out."

She pointed. He leaned close, and his finger hovered over the hooked glyphs without touching them.

"She used birds," he said quietly. "Every mark is a bird. This one's a kestrel — means 'high wind here, always.' This is a nightjar — means 'camp, water nearby, but don't trust the water.' And this one—" his finger stopped, and something moved across his face, grief and fury tangled together, "—this is a storm petrel. She only ever drew the petrel twice that I ever saw. Once on a letter she sent me when I was eight, that I didn't understand until tonight. And once here."

"What does the petrel mean?"

Wren looked up at her, and outside the window the rain thickened against the glass, and somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark at nothing.

"It doesn't mark the land," he said. "It marks a warning. It means: *whatever you think you're going to find, you're wrong.*"

* * *

Down in the marsh town, in a room above a different kind of shop, a man finished writing a letter, sealed it, and addressed it to an office in the capital with a Syndicate franking stamp. He wrote quickly, in a neat bureaucratic hand:

*Expedition forming. Crew of four confirmed. Medic: the Sallow woman. Driver: Reeve. Also a youth, identity unconfirmed. Departure within the week.*

He hesitated, pen hovering, then added one more line:

*The Quill girl may also be involved. Advise immediately.*

He blotted it, sealed it, and went down into the rain to post it, whistling.
