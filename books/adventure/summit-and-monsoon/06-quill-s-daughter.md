## 6. Quill's Daughter

The town of Marrow Bend sat in the crook of the river like something the water had tried to swallow and given up on. Rosalind came into it at dusk, when the light went amber and thick as resin, and the corrugated roofs along the main street caught the last of it and threw it back in dull orange sheets. Mud had dried in the wheel ruts of the road in long, curling ridges, the fossil record of the last truck through — which, judging by the depth of them, had been weeks ago.

She left the Land Rover at the edge of town, under the leaning fig tree by the shuttered fuel depot, because a vehicle with a Syndicate transponder in its dash was not a thing you parked where people could read it. The walk in took ten minutes. Her boots found every one of the puddles.

Marrow Bend smelled of river rot and woodsmoke and, underneath it all, the green metallic promise of coming rain. The monsoon was still three weeks out by her reckoning, but the air already carried that weight, that held-breath quality, as though the whole sky were a lung filling slowly. She'd grown up in towns like this one. Towns where the weather was the only rich thing.

The name she had was Ada Quill. The address was a guess: the prospector's letter had mentioned a sister who kept a laundry down by the landing, and if the sister still lived there, then the daughter would be within reach of it. Families in river towns didn't scatter far. They couldn't afford to.

The laundry was easy to find — steam bleeding from its vents into the blue dusk, the slap of wet cloth against stone keeping time like a slow heart. A broad woman with grey-streaked hair worked alone inside, wringing sheets over a trough, her forearms slick to the elbow. Rosalind stood in the doorway until the woman looked up.

"I'm looking for Ada," she said. "Ada Quill."

The wringing stopped. The woman studied her for a long moment, taking in the road dust, the cracked knuckles, the look of a person who slept in her vehicle more often than not. "You're the third this month," she said. "Men, the other two. Wearing good boots they didn't want to get dirty." She nodded toward the hills north of town. "Whatever she's got, people want it. I'll tell you what I told them. Try the ridge road. And don't come crying to me about your boots."

* * *

The ridge road climbed out of Marrow Bend in switchbacks so steep the Land Rover's engine dropped an octave on each turn, and by the fourth bend the town below had shrunk to a scatter of orange lamplight and the river was a black ribbon stitched with moon. Rosalind drove with the windows down. The air changed as she rose — cooler, cleaner, threaded with eucalyptus and wet stone. Somewhere upslope a night bird called three notes and got no answer.

The cabin, when she found it, was less a cabin than a stubbornness. It crouched on a shelf of rock above the road, built from salvaged timber and corrugated iron in no color nature had intended, with a rain barrel at each corner and a wind turbine made from a truck alternator turning lazily on a pole of pipe. Solar panels, two of them, angled toward the east. A garden terraced into the hillside in careful steps. Whoever lived here had decided, at some point, never to need anyone again.

A dog announced her before she reached the gate — a lean, yellow-eyed thing tied near the steps, all voice and no intention. Then the door opened and a lantern swung out, and Rosalind saw her.

Ada Quill was perhaps twenty-three, tall and narrow, with her father's deep-set eyes and none of his warmth. She wore men's work trousers and a canvas jacket too big for her, and she held the lantern high enough to blind and low enough to keep her own face dark. Behind her shoulder, visible in the doorway's glow, hung a rifle on pegs — placed, Rosalind noted, at exactly the height a person could grab it without thinking.

"You're from the Syndicate," Ada said. Not a question. An accusation shaped like one.

"No."

"Vane's men wear good boots too." The lantern didn't waver. "Third one this week. My aunt counts them for sport."

Rosalind spread her hands, empty, palms out — an old gesture, older than guns, meaning *I am only hands*. "My name is Rosalind Thornbury. Your father sold me something before he died. I'm not here to take anything from you. I'm here because I think he wanted you to have a choice about it."

Something moved behind Ada's eyes — quick, quickly buried. She looked at Rosalind for a long time, the way you look at weather coming over a ridge, deciding whether it will pass around you or come straight down.

"He died in the charity ward at St. Brendan's," Ada said finally. "Alone. Did you know that?"

"I know he died holding my hand."

That landed. Rosalind watched it land — saw the girl's jaw tighten, saw the lantern dip an inch. The wind turned the turbine overhead with a soft mechanical whisper, and somewhere down the valley thunder cleared its throat, far off, undecided.

"Come inside," Ada said. "Don't touch anything."

* * *

The interior of the cabin was a mapmaker's confession. Charts covered every wall — topographic sheets, hand-drawn surveys, aerial photographs annotated in three colors of ink. A long table under the window held brass instruments laid out on felt like surgical tools. The room smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the same pipe tobacco Rosalind had caught on the dying man's coat in the ward at St. Brendan's. He had been here. Recently. This room had been his harbor, and his daughter had kept it exactly as he'd left it, which was either love or grief or both wearing each other's clothes.

Ada set the lantern on the table and finally let Rosalind see her face fully. Up close she was prettier than the darkness had allowed, but it was a hard beauty, unspent, like currency hoarded past its usefulness. There were shadows under her eyes that spoke of nights spent awake with charts.

"My father wrote to me four times in eleven years," Ada said. "I returned three of them unopened. The fourth came the week after he died, forwarded from my aunt's, and I read it standing in the yard with the envelope already torn because I thought it was a bill." She pulled a folded paper from the drawer of the table and laid it flat, smoothing it once with both hands, an unnecessarily tender motion. "It says a woman named Thornbury would come. That I should listen to her. That the thing in his strongbox wasn't treasure and wasn't mine to sell, but was mine to decide about." Her eyes came up. "He wrote it in March. Before he ever met you. How did he know your name?"

Rosalind sat down slowly on the offered stool. "Because I'd been writing to him for two years. Letters to every prospecting office in the territory, asking after a route through the northern highlands. He answered the last one. We corresponded for eight months. He told me he had a daughter he'd failed, and that if anything happened to him—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "He asked me to find you. Not the money. You."

"That's sentimental," Ada said flatly. "He wasn't sentimental. He was a coward with a compass."

"Yes," Rosalind agreed. "Both things can be true. The compass doesn't care what else a man is."

For a moment the girl almost smiled — a flicker, gone as fast as it came, like fish rising. She turned to the wall of charts and pulled down the largest one, spreading it across the table and weighting its corners with the brass instruments. It was the northern highlands, drawn in her father's cramped, certain hand: contour lines dense as fingerprints, rivers inked in blue, and in the upper quadrant, circled twice in red, a pass with no name on any government survey.

"The convoy went through here," Ada said, tapping the circle. "I've known that since I was sixteen. He used to talk in his sleep. Coordinates, mostly. Once, a number of trucks. Once, a word I didn't understand until years later — *evidence*. I thought he meant ore samples." She straightened. "So. Tell me why a storm chaser with Syndicate debt on her neck wants to walk into that pass during monsoon season, and tell me true, because I've spent my whole life being lied to by men with maps, and I can smell it now the way you'd smell rain."

Rosalind told her true. All of it — the debt, the dying prospector, the map bought with the last of a stranger's breath, Vane's offer with its teeth in it, the salvage contract that paid too well to be salvage. She watched Ada's face while she talked and saw the girl cataloguing every detail with a surveyor's precision, filing each admission where it belonged.

When she finished, Ada was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind had begun to carry the first real smell of the season — that vast green exhalation off the lowlands, wet vegetation and distance, the monsoon announcing itself the way armies announce themselves, by smell, long before arrival.

"You're going to betray him," Ada said at last. "Vane. You already know you are. You just haven't admitted it yet."

Rosalind held her gaze. "I'm going to finish the job I sold him. What happens after is weather."

"That's a lie you're telling yourself, not me." But Ada said it without heat, almost gently, the way you correct a child's arithmetic. She began rolling the chart, deliberate, unhurried. "Here's what I know that your map doesn't. The pass floods. Not with rain — with runoff. My father marked three crossing points that hold even in peak season, and one of them is a bridge the government doesn't have on its books anymore, because officially it was never built. If you go in with Vane's crew and their heavy vehicles and their ignorance, you'll lose a truck in the water and probably a man, and Vane will call it the mountain's fault."

"And if someone who knew the crossings came along?"

"Then that person would be walking into a massacre site with the man who helped bury it." Ada tied the roll with cord, tight, final. "My father spent twenty years running from whatever's in that pass. It killed him anyway, slower. I swore I'd never follow his maps anywhere." She looked up, and in the lamplight her father's deep-set eyes were bright and furious and afraid. "So ask me properly. Ask me the real question, Thornbury. Not whether I'll guide you. Whether I'll help you burn him down."

The lantern flame leaned in a draft from the door seam. Down in the valley, Marrow Bend's lights trembled behind the gathering cloud, and the thunder spoke again, nearer now, no longer undecided.

Rosalind opened her mouth to answer —

and the dog outside began to bark, not at the storm, but down the ridge road, at headlights climbing slow through the dark.
