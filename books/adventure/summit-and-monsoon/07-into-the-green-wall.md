## 7. Into the Green Wall

The rain began an hour before they reached it, a fine grey sift that turned the road to grease and made the mules lay their ears back in sullen protest. Rosalind watched it come down out of the hills in curtains, whole veils of water sweeping across the valley like the skirts of passing ghosts, and she understood why the locals called this stretch of country the Green Wall. From the lowlands it looked like nothing — a dark green smudge on the horizon, a suggestion of elevation. Up close it was a cliff face of living things: bamboo and wild banana and strangler fig, lianas thick as a man's arm hanging in festoons, ferns erupting from every crevice in a riot of unheeded green. The road entered it through a cutting so narrow that two wagons could not have passed abreast, and once inside, the sky vanished.

"Charming," said Dr. Wren Calloway, from her seat on the lead wagon. She had not spoken much since Fair Weather. She sat with her medical bag on her knees like a woman holding a sleeping child, and she watched the walls of vegetation slide past with the flat expression of someone cataloguing all the ways a place could kill you.

"It gets better," Rosalind said.

"I doubt that."

"It doesn't get worse, either. That's the bargain."

Wren made a sound that was almost a laugh. Almost.

Behind them came the second wagon, driven by Juniper — sixteen years old, all elbows and defiance, who had talked her way onto this expedition by claiming she could drive anything with legs or wheels and then proving it on the first morning by backing a loaded cart through a gate narrower than the cart. She wore a stolen rain poncho three sizes too big and a grin that the rain had not yet managed to wash off. She had never seen mountains before. She kept saying so, as if repetition might make it true.

And behind Juniper, on foot, came Sera Quill.

Rosalind heard her before she saw her — the deliberate crunch of boots on wet stone, unhurried, metronomic. Sera walked at the rear of the column the way a lighthouse stands at the edge of a shelf: fixed, watchful, facing outward. She carried her mother's rifle slung across her back and her mother's map rolled in oilcloth inside her coat, and she had not yet forgiven Rosalind for either fact. The map had been bought from Ada Quill with Rosalind's last coin while Ada lay dying in a marsh-town flophouse; Sera had arrived two days too late for the death and one day too early for the funeral, and she had signed on to the expedition with the air of a woman collecting a debt of her own.

The cutting opened. The trees closed overhead. And the world went green.

* * *

It was not darkness, exactly. Light came down through the canopy in shafts and coins, broken into emeralds and coppers, so that everything moved through a kind of underwater shimmer. The air changed too — thickened, sweetened, freighted with rot and blossom and the mineral smell of wet stone. Sounds arrived from everywhere and nowhere: the ticking of water finding its way down through leaves, the far complaint of some bird that sounded like a hinge in need of oil, the constant small industry of insects. The road beneath them was old, older than the Syndicate, older than the government survey stones they'd passed at noon, and in places the jungle had taken it back entirely, so that the mules waded hub-deep through standing water and the wheels hissed like things being scalded.

"Stop." Sera's voice, from behind. Not loud. It didn't need to be.

Rosalind raised a hand and the column halted. Water dripped. Somewhere ahead, something large moved through the undergrowth with a patience that suggested it had all the time in the world and no interest whatsoever in them.

"What is it?" Juniper whispered, loudly.

Sera came forward along the line of wagons, eyes on the treeline to the east. "We're being followed," she said.

Rosalind felt the words settle over the party like the rain itself. "How long?"

"Since the river crossing yesterday. Two men, maybe three. They keep well back. They're good." A pause. "Not good enough."

"You didn't think to mention this earlier?"

"I mentioned it to you at dawn."

"You muttered something about dust at dawn while I was harnessing a mule that was trying to eat my hat."

"Dust," Sera repeated, without inflection, "on a trail that hasn't been dry in six weeks. Means riders. I'm telling you properly now because we're about to enter leased land, and after that, whoever follows us stops being an accident and starts being a message."

Rosalind looked back the way they'd come. The road unspooled behind them into green murk, empty as far as the eye could reach, which was not far. "Vane's people?"

"Vane's people ride ahead of him, not behind. These are patient. Patient means paid by the day." Sera resettled the rifle on her shoulder. "My mother ran these roads for twenty years. She always said the ones who follow quietly are the ones you should worry about. The loud ones just want your cargo."

"And the quiet ones?"

"Want to know where you're going. So they can arrive first."

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a waystation — a government rest house from the survey era, its roof fallen in, its walls claimed by a fig tree that had grown straight up through the middle of the main room and spread its crown where the ceiling used to be. Rain fell through the branches and pattered off the leaves, so that inside the ruin it rained more gently than outside, a second-hand rain. They got a fire going under the broken chimney, and the smoke rose through the tree and was shredded by the canopy above.

Juniper cooked. She was, Rosalind had discovered, the only member of the expedition who could make rice and dried fish taste like a decision rather than a punishment. She moved around the fire with the easy competence of someone who had fed herself on the streets of Fair Weather since she was twelve, and she talked the whole time, filling the green silence with a running commentary on everything she saw, because silence, Rosalind suspected, was the thing Juniper had run away from.

"—and there's a moth the size of my *hand*, did you see it? It flew past the fire and I thought it was a bat, except bats don't fly like they're drunk—"

"Eat," said Wren.

"—and the frogs, have you heard the frogs? They started at sundown and they haven't stopped, it's like the whole forest is one enormous throat—"

"Eat, Juniper."

Juniper ate, grinning, rice steaming in the lamplight. Then, with her mouth full: "Do you think the people following us are close enough to smell this?"

The question landed in the middle of the camp like a stone in still water. Rosalind looked up. Wren's hands went still around her bowl. Even the mules seemed to pause.

"Sera told you?" Rosalind said.

"She tells me things. She says I've got ears like a fox and a mouth like a landslide, and only one of those is useful." Juniper swallowed. "Is it true? Are we being followed?"

Rosalind weighed it. There was an argument for keeping the girl ignorant — ignorance kept fear small — but she had learned, in thirty years of chasing weather across three continents, that frightened people who don't know why they're frightened do far stupider things than frightened people who do. And Juniper would find out anyway. The girl collected information the way the forest collected water.

"It's true," she said. "Two or three riders, holding back. Sera's watching for them."

"Are they bandits?"

"Bandits take what you have. These haven't tried to take anything."

"So they want something else." Juniper turned her bowl slowly in her hands. In the firelight her face was suddenly older, the street-kid mask slipping to show the calculating thing underneath. "They want to know where we're going."

"That," said Sera, from the darkness at the edge of the firelight, where she had been sitting unseen for some time, "is exactly what they want."

Juniper jumped a foot in the air. Wren sighed. Rosalind found herself, against all sense, smiling.

* * *

She took first watch, as she always did, though Sera always argued about it and always lost, because Rosalind had learned long ago that sleep came easier to her when the storm was hers to hold. She sat under the ruined eaves with her oilskin collar up and watched the rain move through the beams of her lantern, and she thought about money.

She thought about it the way a drowning woman thinks about air. Eleven thousand crowns owed to the Vane Syndicate, accruing interest at a rate that meant the number grew while she slept. The *Kestrel* — her aircraft, her pride, her coffin-on-a-wire — impounded in a Fair Weather hangar, held against the debt. This job was supposed to clear it. One season, one salvage, one rich man's obsession, and then the ledger went clean and Rosalind Thornbury went free. That had been the arithmetic in Fair Weather, warm and dry and plausible.

Out here, under the Green Wall, with unknown riders ghosting their trail and the monsoon building somewhere over the peaks like a debt of its own, the arithmetic felt thinner. Edmund Vane had been charming at the signing. He had shaken her hand with a grip like a manacle and said, *Bring me what my grandfather lost, Captain, and I'll make you whole.* He had not said what his grandfather had lost, or how, or why a man with a fleet of modern aircraft needed a dying prospector's hand-drawn map to find it. Rosalind had told herself these were the ordinary eccentricities of the very rich.

She was beginning to suspect they were not.

A sound: boot-sole on wet stone, soft, deliberate. Her hand went to the lantern shutter. Then Sera materialized at the edge of the light, moving with that unnerving economy, and crouched beside her.

"You should sleep," Rosalind said.

"In a moment." Sera looked out at the black wall of the forest. For a long time she said nothing, and Rosalind let the silence stand, because she had learned that Sera Quill's silences were not empty; they were full, like held breath.

"My mother drew that map when she was nineteen," Sera said at last.

Rosalind turned. In the dimness Sera's face was all planes and shadow, unreadable, but her voice had a hairline crack in it that hadn't been there before.

"She never talked about these hills. Twenty years running cargo and salvage and she'd take any route, any pass, except the high country above the Karst. If a client wanted to go up there, she'd quote them double, and if they wouldn't pay double, she'd turn the job down. I asked her once, when I was young. She said, *Some places are graves, little bird, and graves should be left to keep their dead.*" Sera's jaw tightened. "Then she spends her last year of life drawing a map to the one place she refused to go. Selling it to a stranger. With instructions — you told me she gave you instructions."

"She did. *Follow the old military road past the third survey stone. Don't stop before the pass. Whatever you find, take it down the mountain whole.*"

"*Whole,*" Sera repeated. "Not *sell it*. Not *bring it to me*. Whole. As if whatever's up there isn't cargo at all." She looked at Rosalind directly now, and the firelight caught the anger in her face and made it burn steady, banked, like coal. "I spent two weeks after her funeral going through her things. Every ledger, every letter, every scrap. Do you know what I found about the year she was nineteen? Nothing. A gap. Twenty years of meticulous records and one blank page, dated the same summer that convoy went missing — the convoy Vane's paying us to find."

The rain ticked on the leaves overhead. Down in the dark, a frog fell silent, then another, a spreading quiet like a ripple.

"You think she knew," Rosalind said quietly. "What happened to it."

"I think my mother spent her whole life carrying something she couldn't put down. And I think she sent us up this mountain to put it down for her." Sera stood, fluidly, and pulled her rifle strap higher on her shoulder. "So when we find it, Captain — whatever it is — remember whose map brought us here. Not Vane's. Hers."

She melted back into the dark. Rosalind sat alone with the lantern and the second-hand rain, and thought about a nineteen-year-old girl on a mountain road, and what she might have seen that took forty years to say.

* * *

Dawn came late under the canopy — a slow greying rather than a sunrise, the light filtering down through a hundred feet of leaves until it arrived exhausted. They broke camp in mist. The road climbed. By mid-morning the cuttings were steeper, the stone older, and they passed the first of the survey markers: a moss-eaten pillar bearing the crest of a government that had, by all accounts, stopped maintaining anything this far inland a generation ago.

It was Juniper, riding point on the lead mule, who saw it first.

"Captain!" Her voice cracked upward with excitement. "There's a sign! A real sign!"

They gathered where the road bent around a shoulder of rock. Nailed to a tree — nailed, absurdly, to a living tree that had grown around the board's edges as if swallowing it — hung a weathered placard, its paint flaked to ghosts:

**LEASED CONCESSION No. 9 — VANE SYNDICATE HOLDINGS**
**ENTRY BY PERMIT ONLY**
**ALL PERSONS ENTERING WITHOUT PERMIT ARE TRESPASSERS AND WILL BE DEALT WITH ACCORDINGLY**

Below the official text, in newer paint, someone had added a line in a crude, hurried hand:

**TURN BACK**

Rosalind read it twice. Beside her, Wren exhaled slowly through her nose. Juniper looked from the sign to Rosalind and back again, her grin faltering.

"Your employer's land," Sera said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The concession covers the upper valley," Rosalind said. "Vane holds the timber rights. Or claims to. Half these leases are paper and muscle — the government sold the same valleys to three different companies in the same decade." But even as she said it, she was looking at the fence.

Because there was a fence. New wire, new posts, strung along the treeline where the road crossed onto the flat, and beyond it, half-hidden in the green, the shapes of buildings — a depot, a bunkhouse, a tall mast flying a pennant that even at this distance she could read: the white chevron of the Vane Syndicate. Smoke rose from a cookhouse chimney. Figures moved between the structures. It was not an abandoned claim. It was an operating post, staffed and supplied, sitting squarely across the only road to the pass.

And it explained the followers, she realized. The patient riders hadn't been following them at all. They'd been herding them — gently, day by day, a mile at a time — toward this gate.

From the depot, a whistle blew. Once, twice. A door banged. Four men emerged into the clearing, and one of them carried a rifle with the casual authority of a man who had never once been asked to put it down.

Rosalind looked at her crew: Wren with her bag clutched tight, Juniper wide-eyed on the wagon bench, Sera already easing the rifle sling loose across her back. Behind them, the road they had come by stood empty and dripping. Ahead, the Green Wall parted around a wire fence and a pennant and four men walking toward them through the rain.

"Well," said Rosalind, and reached for the permit papers in her coat, knowing even as she did that paper was about to prove the thinnest thing she owned. "Everyone smile. We're expected."
