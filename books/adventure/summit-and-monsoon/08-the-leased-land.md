## 8. The Leased Land

The rain held off for two days, which in the highlands was less a blessing than a held breath.

Rosalind felt it in her teeth the way she felt pressure drops before a cell built — that low, waiting hum, the sky stacked gray and patient above the ridgeline like a creditor outside a door. The green wall of the interior rose ahead of them, tier on tier of forest climbing into cloud, and somewhere past the third ridge, past the rivers and the switchbacks and the places where the map's ink had gone soft with the old woman's blood, lay the convoy.

But first, the leased land.

They came to the boundary at midmorning, where the government road — such as it was, a ribbon of graded gravel surrendering by degrees to the jungle's patient reclamation — crossed a line that existed on no map Rosalind had ever bought. It existed on paper in a filing office in the capital, four hundred pages of survey and easement bought and paid for by the Vane Syndicate, and it announced itself in the physical world the way Vane announced everything: with money.

A gate. An actual gate, steel-painted and new, spanning the road on posts sunk in poured concrete, with a guard hut beside it and a solar panel glinting on the hut's roof like a beetle's back. Behind it, the forest went on exactly as it had before, indifferent, ancient — but the gate said *mine* in a language everyone in these hills had learned to read.

Rosalind brought the truck to a stop fifty meters out and left it idling.

"That's new," said Callum from the passenger seat. He had the medic's habit of stating the obvious when the obvious was bad, as if naming a wound were the first step to treating it.

"It wasn't on Quill's notes." Rosalind studied the hut. One guard visible, maybe two. A radio mast. A dog, chained, lying flat in the shade with its chin on its paws and its eyes tracking them with the flat patience of a thing that had been fed on schedule all its life. "Nothing's been on Quill's notes since the third ridge. She stopped writing things down around the time she started dying."

In the truck bed, under the canvas, she heard the small sounds of Juno shifting — the runaway had taken to riding back there with the gear, claiming the cab smelled like Callum's antiseptic, which it did, and which was, Rosalind suspected, only half the reason. The girl was seventeen and had spent her life learning that the safest place in any vehicle was the part nobody looked at. Rosalind hadn't corrected her. There was time enough out here for everyone to be seen whether they wanted it or not.

Maren Quill came around the side of the truck on foot, as she had walked the whole morning, refusing the cab, refusing conversation, refusing everything except the pace of her own boots. She stopped at Rosalind's window. She had her father's jaw and her mother's narrow, weather-slanted eyes, and she wore both expressions at once like a woman carrying two buckets up a hill.

"Vane land," she said. Not a question.

"Leased," Rosalind said. "There's a difference, legally. Not that the fence cares."

"My father hated this stretch. He used to say the Syndicate didn't buy the land, they bought the *argument* — bought it so thoroughly that no one could even remember what the argument had been." Maren looked at the gate a long moment. "He'd have gone around. North, through the karst. Three days extra."

"Three days extra is three days of monsoon on us, and we're already eating the calendar." Rosalind drummed once on the wheel. "We have papers. Legitimate ones. Vane signed the expedition authorization himself — that's the joke of it, we're carrying his own signature through his own gate."

"And when they radio ahead and someone asks why a salvage crew needs a route that swings past the old convoy road?"

"Then we find out how much the man in that hut knows." Rosalind put the truck in gear. "Which is information we're going to need eventually anyway."

* * *

The guard was young, maybe twenty, with a rifle slung more for the photograph of it than the use of it, and a name patch that read D. SERRANO in thread already going gray at the edges. He took Rosalind's papers with the solemnity of a boy handling something above his pay grade, carried them into the hut, and read them twice, moving his lips.

Rosalind waited by the gate and let the silence work. She had learned long ago that in the hills, whoever spoke first at a checkpoint paid for the privilege.

Callum leaned against the truck's flank and lit nothing — he'd quit smoking two years ago and still went through the ritual of patting his pocket, finding it empty, and looking vaguely bereaved. "You know what I keep thinking," he said, low. "A gate means someone expects visitors. Gates are for things people want to keep *counted*."

"Or kept out."

"Both. Same fence." He nodded at the forest beyond. "You see any survey stakes? Any cut lines? Any sign anyone's actually *doing* whatever the lease says they're doing out here?"

Rosalind had been seeing it since they'd rolled through the gate. Nothing. No exploration crews, no drill pads, no tailings, no marker tape. The Syndicate held four hundred square kilometers of some of the richest unexamined highland in the territory, and it sat on it like a dragon on a hoard it never counted — which meant the lease wasn't for what was *in* the land. It was for what had happened *on* it.

She didn't say any of this out loud. Serrano came back with the papers and a clipboard and a form, and the form wanted names, and Rosalind gave the names on the papers, which were true, and the purpose on the papers, which was true the way a skeleton is true — salvage of wrecked commercial transport, authorized route, authorized personnel — while omitting everything that made it a body and not a diagram.

Serrano copied it all out in careful pencil. Then, without looking up, in the tone of a man asking about the weather: "You want to watch the river gauge at Karst Hollow. If it's over the third mark, don't try the ford."

"We're not crossing at Karst Hollow," Rosalind said.

"No." He handed the clipboard back. "People say that."

* * *

They drove another hour into the leased land before Juno finally climbed out of the truck bed and into the cab, which was how Rosalind knew the girl had seen something that frightened her. Juno appeared at checkpoints the way water appeared in low ground — inevitably, but never where you were looking.

"There's a village back there," Juno said. "Or what was one. Off the east side of the road, maybe four kilometers. I could see the roofs from the ridge cut."

"Lease says the whole parcel's uninhabited," Callum said. "Resettled, compensated, the whole phrase. It's in the authorization's land-status annex. I read it last night. Twice, because I thought I'd misread it."

"You didn't misread it," Maren said from the back seat. She had finally accepted the cab that morning, and had spent the hour since looking out the window with the fixed, cataloguing attention of someone relearning a country that had rearranged itself while she was gone. "Serrano's village was resettled too. That's why a local boy guards a gate for a company that isn't his — there's no other work left where the work used to be. My father wrote about it. The compensation never arrives, or arrives as scrip for the company store, or arrives and is immediately owed back out in fees. The land's empty because the land was *made* empty, and then the emptiness was rented back to the people who emptied it." She said it flatly, without heat, which somehow made it worse.

"You knew him," Juno said. "Serrano."

"Everyone knows everyone out here. His mother sold me eggs in Karst Hollow when I was twelve." Maren turned back to the window. "He didn't warn us about the river because he's kind. He warned us because someone told him to warn everyone, and he does what he's told, because the last person out here who didn't do what they were told—" She stopped.

"Go on," Rosalind said quietly.

"No," Maren said. "Later. When we're past the river. Some things you don't say inside the fence."

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of ground above a stream, inside the lease, and Rosalind took first watch because she didn't trust anyone else to take it seriously and didn't trust herself to sleep.

The night was enormous and close. No rain yet, but the air had the weight of it, the whole sky hanging like wet canvas waiting for the thumbtack. Insects ran their endless accounting in the dark. Down the slope, the stream talked to itself over stones. And far off — far enough that she might have invented it — there was a sound Rosalind knew from her years of chasing weather across three continents and knew did not belong to weather: engines. More than one. Running steady, not traveling. Generators, maybe. Or a convoy idling through the night somewhere over the ridge, holding position, holding *something*.

She woke Callum at midnight for his watch and told him about the engines and watched his face do the thing it did — the flinch, the swallow, the deliberate settling. Callum Voss had lost his license over a field hospital in the border war, over a decision that had been right and had ruined him anyway, and ever since he had treated bad news the way he treated a hemorrhage: with pressure, applied fast, before it spread.

"Generators," he said.

"Maybe."

"Convoy's been dead eleven years, Roz. Whatever's running out here, it's not ghosts."

"No," she agreed. "Ghosts are cheaper."

He almost smiled at that, and she counted it a victory, and went to her bedroll, and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning brought the rain at last — not the monsoon proper, not yet, but the scouts it sent ahead: a warm, fine, drifting rain that made the forest smoke and turned the road's gravel to grease. They made slow miles. The land climbed. And in the middle of the afternoon, at a bend where the road ran along a cut hillside with the drop falling away green and dripping on their left, they found the truck.

It was a Syndicate truck — white cab, the stylized V of the company on the door, faded to a ghost of itself. It sat off the road's shoulder, nose-down in the ditch, half-swallowed by a flowering vine that had been at it for years. The tires were gone. The glass was gone. The engine block was gone, cut out with torches by someone who had known what they were doing, and the cab's doors hung open like a man shrugging after a long story.

Rosalind stopped and got out, and the others followed, because that was the thing about a crew in the field — curiosity was contagious and caution was not.

"Stripped," Callum said. "Years ago. Locals, probably — engine's the only part worth the walk out here."

"Locals don't torch the paperwork," Rosalind said.

She'd found it when she leaned into the cab: the ash. A drift of gray in the footwell, packed down by rain into a felted mat, and fused to the dashboard above it the browned shadow of paper that had burned where it lay. A glovebox full of documents, burned *in place*, carefully, thoroughly, by someone with time and fuel and a reason.

Maren crouched at the ditch's edge, looking at the ground under the truck, at the wheel ruts baked into the mud by some dry season long past. There had been more than one vehicle here, once. A whole line of them, pulled off the road, and one left behind — or one that couldn't be driven out.

"This is the convoy road," she said. "My father's map has it running up the far side of that ridge. This is where it branches." She stood and looked north, where the forest closed over the hills like water closing over a dropped stone. "Eleven years, and nobody's towed a truck off the side of the road."

"Nobody's *allowed* to touch it," Rosalind said. "That's the point of a lease. You lease a wound so no one can come look at whether it healed."

Juno had drifted to the back of the truck, to the empty bed, and was standing very still in the rain. "There's writing," she said. "Under the paint. Someone wrote on the inside of the bed wall and then someone painted over it, but the paint's peeling."

They crowded in. Under the peeling white, in what had been grease or tar applied by a finger, in a hand that had written fast and pressed hard:

**THEY WERE ALREADY DEAD WHEN THE TRUCKS CAME. ASK KARST HOLLOW. ASK WHO PAID.**

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, a single word in the same hand:

**SORRY.**

Nobody spoke. The rain ticked on the cab roof. Somewhere up the ridge, faint and steady, the engines went on with their patient work.

Maren reached out and touched the word *sorry* with two fingers, the way you'd touch a grave marker, and when she turned around her face had gone from weathered to carved.

"Karst Hollow," she said. "That's where we're crossing tomorrow." She looked at Rosalind, and Rosalind looked back, and between them passed the whole unsaid weight of it — the map, the money, the man with Vane's signature at the bottom of their authorization, the gate and the guard and the leased emptiness, all of it suddenly arranged in a different order, the way a landscape rearranges itself in the last light before a storm cell breaks.

"We cross at the ford," Rosalind said at last, "and we keep our eyes open, and we say nothing to anyone until we're past it." She climbed back into the cab. "And Maren — whatever your father wrote that you're not telling us yet."

Maren stood a moment longer in the rain, her hand still resting on the truck's rusted rail.

"Tomorrow," she said. "After the river. I promise you, Rosalind — after the river, you'll wish I'd told you tonight."

* * *

They made camp early, hemmed in by the climbing walls of the gorge road, and Rosalind lay awake under the canvas listening to the stream swell by degrees in the dark, louder each hour, the rain arriving now in earnest bands off the ridge, and thought about the third mark on a gauge in Karst Hollow, and a guard who warned strangers about a ford he'd been told to warn them about, and a dead man's apology painted under a company's white.

By the time she slept, near dawn, the water below the camp was running brown and fast, and the monsoon had found them.
