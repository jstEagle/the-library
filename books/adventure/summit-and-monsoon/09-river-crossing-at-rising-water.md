## 9. River Crossing at Rising Water

The river announced itself an hour before they saw it — a low, continuous thunder rolling up the valley like a train that never arrived. Rosalind heard it first, of course. She always heard weather before anyone else, some animal inheritance she'd never been able to explain or shake. She stopped on the trail where it bent around a shoulder of black rock, tilted her head, and held up one hand.

"What?" Juno asked, hitching her pack higher. The girl had gone quiet in the last two days, the bravado of Fairweather's back alleys wearing thin against the reality of wet jungle and leeches and the endless green wall pressing in from both sides.

"Listen."

They listened. Callum heard it next — his face changed, that careful clinical neutrality cracking just enough to show something underneath that might have been dread. Ada heard it last, or pretended to, standing apart as she always did, her father's map rolled in oilcloth and strapped across her back like a rifle.

"The Seri," Rosalind said. "And it's up."

"It was down when I crossed it in the dry season," Ada said. "Knee-deep. You could walk it."

"You crossed it in the dry season." Rosalind resumed walking, faster now. "Everything you know about this country is six months out of date and lying to you."

The trail dropped through a stand of bamboo so dense the light came down in green coins, and then the trees opened all at once, the way a curtain opens, and there it was.

The Seri at flood was not a river. It was a brown ocean being poured sideways through a gap in the mountains. It filled the valley floor from wall to wall, half a kilometer of churning water carrying whole trees — not branches, *trees*, sixty-foot trunks with their root balls still clutching red earth — tumbling end over end like toys in a gutter. The roar swallowed everything. Rosalind had stood inside thunderstorms that were quieter than this. She had once put her ear against the fuselage of a plane in a hailstorm and heard less.

On the far bank, barely visible through the spray-haze, the trail climbed away toward the highlands. Between here and there: nothing but water and what the water wanted.

"Well," said Callum. "That's unfortunate."

* * *

They made camp above the flood line on a shelf of granite, and Rosalind spent the afternoon doing what she did best, which was watching the sky and hating it.

The clouds over the western peaks had begun to organize. That was the word for it — they weren't drifting anymore, they were *arranging themselves*, stacking into towers with flat gray anvils, and the air had that taut, charged stillness she'd felt a thousand times on the plains before a tornado dropped. The monsoon wasn't due for another week by any almanac. But almanacs were written for farmers, and she was beginning to understand that this country kept its own calendar.

"Barometer's falling fast," she told Callum, who had come to stand beside her at the ledge's edge. He was cleaning his instruments — he cleaned them constantly, a surgeon's compulsion, though the only surgery he'd performed in six years was stitching Juno's arm after a thornbush. "If this rain reaches the headwaters tonight, we'll have another meter on the river by morning."

"So we cross today."

"So we cross today."

He didn't answer right away. Down at the water's edge, Juno and Ada were wrestling the raft together — two hollowed log canoes lashed side by side with saplings, salvaged from an abandoned logging camp a day back. It looked, Rosalind thought charitably, like something that would float. She thought about it uncharitably too, and decided not to say so aloud.

"Rosalind." Callum kept his voice low, his eyes on his hands. "The girl asked me yesterday how much Vane is paying us. I told her the truth. She did the arithmetic. Four shares, minus what we owe the outfitters in Karst Hollow, minus your debt — which she also knows about, because she can read a ledger better than either of us." He paused. "She asked me if people die on jobs like this. I told her no."

"But?"

"But I'm a bad liar and she knew it." He snapped the case shut on his stethoscope. "I've been thinking about what happens if someone goes into that water. Hypothermia takes maybe ten minutes in current like that. Drowning takes less. And I have exactly nothing in my kit for either, because I left the good equipment — along with my license and my entire former life — in a hospital locker in the capital."

"We'll be careful."

"We're always careful," Callum said. "That's what everyone says afterward, in the report."

Rosalind looked at him. There it was again, the thing under the thing — the reason a disgraced medic with a ruined name had signed onto a suicide expedition for money he claimed not to care about. Not the payment. The penance. She'd seen it in enough broken people to recognize the shape of it.

"Nobody dies today," she said. "I've outrun worse than a river."

Callum almost smiled. "You've outrun storms. Storms don't grab you by the ankles and pull."

"No," Rosalind agreed, turning back to the sky, where the first anvil cloud had gone the color of a bruise. "They just wait."

* * *

They crossed at the narrowest point Ada could find, where a rocky island split the channel and promised a rest halfway — a promise the river had no obligation to keep.

Rosalind went first, alone, because that was the price of leading. She rigged a line across the first channel using a throwing weight and forty meters of rope, anchored to a boulder the size of a house, and ferried herself across hand over hand with the raft trailing behind on a second line. Even in the shallower water near the anchor the current hit her like a living thing, shoving at her hips, trying to peel her fingers off the rope. Halfway across a floating log came through — she saw it coming and couldn't dodge, could only kick hard and pray, and the trunk missed her by the length of her forearm and kept going downstream at the speed of a galloping horse.

She reached the island, collapsed on the stones, and laughed until her ribs hurt. Fear always did that to her, afterward. It was the one joke the world told and only she got it.

Then she rigged the far anchor, and waved the others across.

Juno came second, on the raft, hauling the ferry line while Rosalind and the rope took the strain from the island. The girl was light and strong and terrified and pretending magnificently not to be, and when the raft ground against the island stones she stepped off with her chin up and her knuckles white and said, "Is that all?" in a voice that cracked on the last syllable.

"That's the easy part," Rosalind said gently. "Second channel's wider."

Callum came third. He crossed the way he did everything — methodically, grimly, gripping the rope with both hands while the raft bucked beneath him, his medical bag strapped to his chest like a child. A wave broke over the gunwales midway and he vanished to the waist for three full seconds, and Rosalind's heart stopped, and then his head came up streaming and he kept pulling, and when he reached the island he sat down heavily among the stones and said nothing at all for a long minute.

Which left Ada.

* * *

Later, Rosalind would replay those ninety seconds many times, and each time she would see something different in them. But in the moment, all she saw was this:

Ada Quill came down to the water's edge with the map strapped across her back, and the wind off the river flattened her shirt against her body, and she stood there a moment looking at the crossing the way a person looks at a door they aren't sure they want to open. Then she stepped onto the raft and pushed off, and for twenty meters everything went the way it was supposed to go.

At thirty meters, the river changed its mind.

Rosalind saw it before anyone — the boil of water upstream, the dark shoulder of something big moving under the surface, and then the wave itself, a standing wave born where the flood had found some new obstruction on the riverbed, rolling down the channel directly at the raft. There was no warning she could give that would help. There was nothing anyone could do. The wave took the raft amidships and flipped it like a pancake, and Ada Quill went into the Seri at flood.

Time went strange. Rosalind was already moving — she registered that distantly, her hands on the rope, boots skidding on wet stone, the whole world reduced to the brown chaos of the water and one small dark head bobbing in it. Ada surfaced gasping, grabbed for the ferry line, missed. The current took her. She went downstream faster than a person can run, and the second channel was fifty meters wide, and below the crossing the river narrowed between rock walls and became, briefly, something even the maps marked with a warning symbol.

"Line!" Rosalind screamed, and Juno was already there — the girl had the spare coil in her hands, no hesitation, no thought for herself, and Rosalind looped it twice around her own waist and ran the granite shelf parallel to the bank, paying out rope, judging, the way she judged storm motion, lead and drift and speed.

"Ada!" Her voice was nothing against the roar. "The rope! Get the rope!"

Whether Ada heard it or simply saw it, Rosalind never learned. But on the third pass of the coil — Rosalind whirling it, the world tilting, her boots at the very lip of the wet stone — the girl's hand shot out of the water and closed on the line.

The jerk nearly took Rosalind off her feet. She dug in, leaned back, felt something in her shoulder tear hotly, and held. Callum's arms locked around her from behind. Juno threw her whole slight weight on the rope. For ten seconds that lasted a year, the river pulled and the three of them pulled, and the river — which always wins eventually, which wins every war it fights, given time — let go.

They dragged Ada onto the stones of the far bank, and she lay there coughing brown water, alive, furious, her father's map still strapped to her back, soaked through but dry inside its oilcloth.

Rosalind sat down hard beside her, shoulder screaming, and started laughing again, that awful post-fear laughter, and couldn't stop.

* * *

They made a fire on the far bank, because hypothermia was real and Callum insisted, and because none of them could have moved anyway. The four of them sat around it in a row like survivors of a shipwreck, steaming, watching the Seri eat its own banks.

Juno broke the silence first. She was wringing out her sleeves and not looking at anyone. "I thought she was dead," she said. "When the raft went over. I thought — " She stopped, jaw tight, and wrung harder.

"You did well," Rosalind said. "With the rope. That was quick thinking."

"I wasn't thinking." The girl's voice came out raw. "That's the whole point. I didn't think at all. I just — did it." She looked up, and her eyes were too bright. "Is that what it's like? Being brave? Because it feels terrible."

Nobody answered that, because nobody had a good answer, and the fire crackled, and somewhere upstream the thunder that wasn't thunder rolled on.

It was Ada who finally spoke, when her shaking had stopped. She had unwrapped the map — checking it, drying it, cradling it — and now she sat holding it open on her knees, staring at it with an expression Rosalind hadn't seen on her before. Something troubled. Something almost afraid.

"This doesn't make sense," Ada said.

"What doesn't?"

"The route." Ada turned the map toward the firelight. Her finger traced the inked line — her father's line, drawn in that cramped, certain hand — from the crossing, up through the leased lands, into the high passes. "We're going up the eastern valley. Through the Syndicate concession. That's what the line says." Her finger stopped. "But look at the annotations. Look at the watermarks in the paper. These notes are older than the route — years older. He drew the route *on top of* something else. Something he'd mapped long before." She looked up, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "My father didn't draw us a path to the convoy wreck. He drew us a path to something else, and hid the wreck along the way. Or—" she hesitated, and Rosalind watched the thought land on her like a physical weight, "—or the wreck isn't where Vane thinks it is at all."

The fire popped. Upstream, the sky over the western peaks had gone fully black, and the first fat drops of the early monsoon began to hiss into the flames.

Rosalind looked at the map, and the map looked back, keeping whatever it knew.

"Then tomorrow," she said slowly, "we find out what it is he actually drew."

Above them, unseen in the dark, the storm closed over the ridge line like a lid.
