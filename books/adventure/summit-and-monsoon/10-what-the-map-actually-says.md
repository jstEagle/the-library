## 10. What the Map Actually Says

The rain came at dusk, as it always did in the lowlands this time of year — not a storm yet, only a rehearsal of one, a fine grey hiss against the tarpaper roof of the waystation where they'd stopped to wait out the flooded ford. Rosalind sat with the map spread across a crate that had once held engine parts and now held everything she owned that mattered, and for the hundredth time since Fair Weather she asked it a question it refused to answer.

It was oilcloth, older than she was, soft as a shirt cuff from decades of folding along the same creases. Someone had drawn the highlands on it by hand — the whole spine of them, from the green wall of the foothills up past the tree line to the bare granite crowns — in ink that had gone brown as old tea. Trails were marked in a careful draftsman's hand. Elevations were noted in a margin script so small she needed lamplight and squinting both. And across the middle third of the sheet, where any sane mapmaker would have put the pass, there was nothing. A blank. A deliberate, patient absence.

"You're holding it wrong," said Pip.

The kid was seventeen, all elbows and opinions, currently draped over an upturned bucket near the stove with a tin mug of something that was mostly condensed milk. "You keep looking at the middle because the middle looks like where the treasure should be. But look at the edges." Pip pointed with the mug. "Nobody draws a blank space that clean by accident. That's not missing information. That's information."

Rosalind looked. She had looked before — she would have sworn she'd looked — but exhaustion makes a person see what they expect, and what she'd expected was a salvage route, a line from the lowlands to the wreck, the shortest path between her debt and her freedom. What she saw now, following Pip's finger along the eastern margin, was a second set of markings entirely. Tiny. Faint. A sequence of numbers running down the edge like a column of ledger entries, each one paired with a symbol she'd taken for decoration: a triangle, a circle bisected by a line, three short strokes like claw marks.

"That's not trail notation," she said slowly.

"No," said a voice from the doorway.

Mara Quill stood there with rain still beaded in her hair, her slicker dripping onto the boards, a canvas satchel of firewood under one arm. She'd been out checking the tie-downs on the trucks. She came in the way she did everything — without hurry, without warmth, setting the wood down beside the stove and crouching to feed it before she even took off her gloves. It had been nine days since she'd joined them at the crossroads, nine days of measuring silences, and Rosalind had learned to read those silences the way she read cloud banks: something was building behind them, and it would break when it broke.

"My mother drew that map," Mara said to the fire. "Not found it. Drew it. Everyone assumes a prospector buys maps. Ada made them. Twenty years of survey work for the Interior Ministry before she went independent, and after that she kept surveying anyway, because she couldn't stop. Those symbols on the margin—" she nodded at the map without looking at it— "are hers. Her own shorthand. She used to mark water sources that way. Triangle for spring, circle for cistern, the claw marks for flash-flood channel." A pause. "Except she wouldn't have marked flood channels on a salvage map. There's no reason to. You don't route a convoy through a flood channel unless you want people to think that's the route."

Rosalind felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it did when a storm cell shifted direction underneath her instruments. "So the middle of the map is a lie."

"The middle of the map is bait," Mara said. She straightened, wiped her hands on her thighs, and finally turned around. In the stove light her face was unreadable, but her eyes weren't; they had the flat shine of someone who had spent days deciding to say something and hated every hour of the deciding. "She knew someone would come looking for whatever she'd found. She was dying, and she sold you that map, and she wanted you to find it — I believe that, I've had nine days to decide whether I believe it and I do. But my mother didn't trust anyone she hadn't tested first. And she never, ever gave away the real thing on the first page."

Callum emerged from the back room where he'd been inventorying the medical kit for the third time, a habit Rosalind recognized as guilt wearing the costume of diligence. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a surgeon's hands and a drunkard's steadiness returning to them day by day, and he looked from Mara to the map with the wariness of a man who has seen secrets ruin rooms before. "Am I interrupting something?"

"Yes," said Pip, cheerfully. "Sit down."

* * *

They worked through the evening while the rain thickened outside, from hiss to rattle to the drumming rush that meant the real weather had arrived off the coast. Callum held the lamp. Pip transcribed the marginal numbers onto the back of a ration list. Rosalind flattened the oilcloth with her palms and tried to make herself slow down, which had never come naturally to her; her whole life had been built on reading fast and committing early, and it had made her money twice and ruined her four times, and here she was again, leaning over a document that could hang her or save her, wanting to skip ahead.

"Read me the numbers again," Mara said.

Pip read them. "Fourteen. Nine. Fourteen. Two. Seven. Nineteen. Eleven. Six. Then the claw marks, then twenty-two, five, sixteen."

Mara closed her eyes. Rosalind watched her lips move, counting something silently, and remembered — with the small cold shock of recognition — that Ada Quill had taught her daughter surveying the way other mothers taught prayers. Of course she had. Of course the cipher would be something Mara already carried in her head, unbreakable precisely because it lived nowhere but in a dead woman's child.

"Elevations," Mara said at last. "Trail junctions on the western approach, keyed to elevation in hundreds of meters. Fourteen hundred, nine hundred, fourteen again — no. Wait." Her brow furrowed. "That can't be right. Fourteen-twice means—" She stopped. She opened her eyes and looked at the map, really looked at it, and Rosalind saw the color leave her face in the space of a breath, draining down through her jaw and throat like water through cracked ground.

"Mara?"

"She wasn't marking a route." Mara's voice had gone very quiet. "She was marking a chain of custody. These aren't elevations of junctions. They're dates. Day-month pairs. Fourteenth of the ninth month. Second of the seventh. Nineteenth of the eleventh." She pressed her fingertip to the margin, below the last number, where the ink had smudged into the oilcloth fibers. "These are supply runs. Convoy schedules. My mother mapped the *schedule*."

The rain filled the silence for a moment, vast and indifferent.

"A schedule of what?" Callum asked, though Rosalind could hear in his voice that he had already guessed, the way a medic guesses at a wound before the bandages come off.

"I don't know," Mara said. "But look at the claw marks. Flash-flood channel — except she's drawn it *across* the pass road, right here, at the narrows above Karst Hollow. And then the last group: twenty-two, five, sixteen. The twenty-second of May. This year." She looked up. "Two weeks ago. Something moved through that pass two weeks ago, and my mother wanted whoever held this map to know exactly when, and exactly where, and exactly how the weather would cover it."

Rosalind sat back on her heels. Outside, lightning flickered behind the ridgeline, a silent white pulse, thunder arriving seconds later like an afterthought. She thought about the story Ada Quill had told her in that rented room that smelled of camphor and blood — the dying prospector pressing the oilcloth into her hands, saying *follow it true or don't follow it at all*, and the payment changing hands, and the woman's eyes closing with what Rosalind had chosen to believe was peace. She had thought she was buying a route to a wreck. Everyone bought routes to wrecks. That was the business. That was the whole honest, grubby business.

But nobody paid a storm chaser's rates to reach a schedule.

"Convoy," Rosalind said aloud, testing the word. "Government convoy. That's what the patron's people told us — a lost shipment, high-value cargo, official channels too embarrassed to admit where it went down. That's the job Edmund Vane is paying for." She traced the blank space in the map's center with one fingernail. "And Ada scrubbed the actual location and left us the timetable instead. Because the location isn't the secret. The secret is *what was on the trucks*, and *who sent them*, and *why the record of it has to disappear*."

"You don't know that," Callum said. Not unkindly. The way a man says it when he hopes he's wrong.

"No," Rosalind admitted. "I don't know it. I know the Syndicate doesn't spend money recovering things it wants found. I know Vane signed our contract himself, which a man worth his fortune does not do for a truckload of tinned rations. And I know your mother sold this map to a stranger on her deathbed instead of leaving it to you." She said it carefully, watching Mara's face. "Why? Why not her own daughter?"

For a long moment Mara didn't answer. The stove ticked. Somewhere under the wind, the river they couldn't cross until morning spoke in its gravel voice.

"Because if it was in my name, they'd have come for me," Mara said finally. "Whoever *they* are — and I think we both know the shape of them by now. She'd been hiding from them for two years. Did she tell you that? No, of course she didn't. She told you a story about a lucky strike and a fair price." A bitter almost-smile. "Ada Quill didn't have lucky strikes. She had evidence. She'd been carrying something for two years that she couldn't use and couldn't destroy, and when the sickness got her she had maybe a month to figure out how to move it into hands that might actually do something. So she found a storm chaser with debts." Her eyes came up, level and unforgiving. "She found *you*. Because you're desperate enough to go anywhere and stubborn enough to finish, and because you owe the Vanes money, which means you can get close to them."

The room seemed to shrink. Pip had stopped writing. Even the fire sounded quieter.

"So I'm not the buyer," Rosalind said. "I'm the courier."

"You're the mule," Mara said. "I'm sorry. I've had nine days to say it softer and I couldn't find the words. But there it is. She picked you the way you'd pick a pack animal — strong enough for the terrain, and with no idea what's in the load."

* * *

They argued until midnight, and then they stopped arguing, because arguing requires the belief that someone might change their mind, and by midnight everyone had said their piece twice.

Callum's position was the simplest: walk away. Fold the map, take the advance they'd already spent half of, and vanish into some province where the Vane Syndicate's ledgers didn't reach. He'd run before — they all knew the shape of that story, the hospital inquiry, the license revoked, the long gray years after — and he was the only one among them who knew exactly what it cost and was willing to pay it again. "Dead men testify badly," he said, packing his pipe he no longer smoked. "Whatever's in that pass, it killed a convoy full of armed men. It'll kill six ragged fools with two trucks and a borrowed winch."

"It won't kill us," Pip said, with the magnificent confidence of seventeen. "It's *weather*. Weather's the one thing we're actually good at." And when Rosalind gave the kid a look: "What? You chase storms for money. Somebody finally hired you to chase one that matters. Are you telling me that's worse?"

It was worse. That was the terrible truth Rosalind kept turning over like a stone in her pocket. If the map was what it appeared to be — and Mara's face, Ada's daughter's face, had settled that question better than any argument — then the job Edmund Vane had purchased was not a salvage. It was an erasure. He wasn't paying them to recover the convoy's cargo. He was paying them to reach it first, confirm what it held, and bury it deeper than the monsoon ever could — and the most efficient burial included the diggers. She had signed a contract with a man who intended, in all likelihood, to have her killed, and the only reason she was still breathing was that the killing was cheaper at altitude, far from witnesses, wrapped in weather.

And yet.

She looked at the map. At the brown ink, the patient hand, the tiny marginal figures of a dead woman who had spent her last month of life engineering exactly this: a chain of custody disguised as a treasure hunt, delivered into the hands of the one person alive who had both a reason to enter the pass and a reason to betray the man funding her. Ada Quill had gambled everything on a stranger's desperation and a stranger's conscience, in that order.

"Here's what happens tomorrow," Rosalind said. The rain had softened to a steady whisper; the river was dropping; in eight hours they'd cross and start the climb into the green wall, and beyond it the ascent, and beyond that the pass where broken trucks waited in the fog like the bones of some extinct animal. "We cross at first light. We go up. We find what's in that pass." She held up a hand before Callum could speak. "Not because I've decided anything. Because the decision isn't ours to make until we know what we're deciding about. Evidence you haven't seen is just a story, and I'm not throwing away my life — or anyone else's — over a story. We look. Then we vote. Same as we agreed at the fire."

"And if what we find confirms it?" Mara asked quietly. "If it's what she thought it was?"

Rosalind folded the map along its old creases, feeling the oilcloth resist and yield, resist and yield, like something alive being asked to hold its breath.

"Then Edmund Vane gets his salvage," she said, "and I decide what he actually receives."

Outside, the clouds were tearing open along the ridge, and through the gap the high country showed itself for the first time in days — black peaks against a bruised sky, the passes still white with old snow, and above the farthest saddle a single anvil-headed cloud standing up into the stratosphere, enormous and calm, the way a gun is calm before it goes off.

Mara followed her gaze to the window. "Storm season's opening early this year," she said.

"I know," Rosalind said. "We need to be above Karst Hollow before it does."

And beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilskin where no one had thought to look, the radio receiver Callum had scavenged in Fair Weather clicked softly to life on its own — someone keying a signal from somewhere high and cold — and none of them heard it at all.
