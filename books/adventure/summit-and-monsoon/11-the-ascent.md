## 11. The Ascent

The road ended where the map said it would, which was the first honest thing the journey had given them.

Rosalind stood at the wheel of the lead truck and looked at what remained of it: two ruts swallowed by fern, a rusted gate lying on its back in the weeds like a dead animal, and beyond that, the mountain rising green and total out of the morning mist. The highlands did not welcome you. They simply permitted you to arrive, the way a creditor permits a debtor through his door.

Behind her, the second truck idled, coughing blue smoke from an exhaust patch Callum had soldered at dawn with more optimism than skill. She could hear Pip arguing with the tailgate latch, and beneath that, the low murmur of Maren Quill reading the terrain the way other people read scripture.

"End of the road," Rosalind called back. "From here we walk."

No one cheered. That, too, felt honest.

* * *

They made camp that first night at the treeline's lower hem, where the air still held the valley's warmth but already smelled different — colder, mineral, like wet stone and something faintly sweet rotting under the leaf litter. Rosalind distributed loads with the economy of a woman who had learned never to carry anything she couldn't drop running. Rope, pitons, the medical kit, food for nine days if nobody got greedy, and the map, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, where she had taken to keeping it the way other people kept a knife.

Maren built the fire small and clean, three sticks and a whisper of flame, and cooked rice with dried fish without once looking up to see whether anyone approved. She was twenty-three and moved like someone who had been told her whole life she was in the way and had decided, somewhere in the last month, to stop believing it. Her mother's compass hung at her throat on a leather cord, and Rosalind had noticed she touched it before making any decision, however small — which way to pitch a tarp, whether to boil water twice. A superstition, maybe. Or an inheritance.

Pip, sixteen and all elbows, sat as close to the fire as skin could bear and complained about the cold anyway. The boy had run away from a mill town and a father whose fists had opinions, and had attached himself to the expedition in Fair Weather by hiding in Rosalind's truck bed among the spare tires, and had proven useful enough since that she'd stopped pretending she'd mind. He carried double rations without complaint and talked constantly, which in the mountains was its own kind of insulation.

Callum sat apart, as he always did, cleaning instruments he hadn't used. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a surgeon's hands and a drunkard's patience, struck off four years ago for something he never explained and Rosalind never asked about. He had signed on for half pay and no questions, which suited them both.

And then there was Renn.

Vane's man sat at the edge of the firelight with his rifle across his knees, cleaning it though it was already clean, and said nothing unless spoken to, and when spoken to said exactly enough. He was forty, maybe, gray at the temples, with a face like a closed ledger. He had joined them at the last moment with Vane's compliments and Vane's money and the cheerful implication that he was there to help. Nobody believed the implication. Everybody pretended to.

"Water source tomorrow?" Renn asked, in the tone of a man logging inventory.

"Spring at the saddle, if the map's right," Rosalind said. "After that, we're drinking rain."

"And after that?"

"After that," she said, "we stop asking questions I haven't answered yet."

He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth he'd produced in six days, and it chilled her more than the altitude.

* * *

The ascent proper began on the third day, and the mountain began testing them before breakfast.

The trail — trail was generous; it was a suggestion the land occasionally honored — climbed through cloud forest where the trees wore moss like old fur and every surface dripped. The light came down green and dim, filtered through canopy so thick it might have been underwater. Their boots sank into humus soft as flesh. Leeches found Pip within the hour and he found them an hour after that, shrieking and slapping at his ankles while Maren showed him, with clinical detachment, how salt worked better than panic.

By noon they were climbing hand over hand up root-ladders and slick stone, packs dragging at their shoulders like living things that wanted to lie down. Rosalind set the pace and refused to let herself think about the pace, because thinking about it invited despair, and despair at altitude was a luxury that killed.

Callum struggled. She watched him struggle — the heavy chest, the breath whistling thin at the top of each rise, the pauses he disguised as admiring the view. Once, on a ledge above a drop that went down into green nothing, he stopped altogether, both hands on his knees, and she saw him look at the drop a moment too long.

"Doctor," she said, using the title deliberately, the way you'd hold out a hand.

"I'm fine." He straightened. Sweat had carved channels through the dust on his face. "Just remembering I'm fifty-two."

"Fifty-two and the only one of us who can put a chest tube in," she said. "That makes you the most valuable thing on this mountain. Act accordingly."

Something moved behind his eyes — gratitude, or grief, or the memory of being needed and losing it. He nodded and climbed on.

Maren, meanwhile, climbed like she'd been born on this grade. Rosalind watched her read the rock, choosing holds without hesitation, and wondered how much of Ada Quill lived in those hands. The daughter had spent eleven years not speaking to the woman who'd drawn the map now pressed against Rosalind's ribs, and had joined this expedition — so she claimed — to understand why her mother had died alone in a Fair Weather flophouse rather than come home. Rosalind suspected there was more to it than that. There always was. But the girl hauled her share and asked sharp questions and touched her mother's compass at every fork in the trail, and that was enough for now.

At dusk they reached the shoulder below the saddle and made camp in the lee of a granite spine, and the temperature fell through the floor of the world. They ate cold. Even the fire wanted a bribe tonight.

It was Pip, wrapped in his blanket like a cocoon with opinions, who broke the silence.

"How much farther to the pass?"

"Two days, if weather holds," Rosalind said.

"And then the trucks."

"And then the trucks."

"The government ones." He said it carefully, tasting it. "The ones nobody's supposed to know about."

"Pip."

"I'm just saying it out loud so it stops rattling around in my head." He pulled the blanket tighter. "My da always said anything the government loses in the mountains stays lost. Said men went up looking for wrecks and came back with pockets full of nothing and heads full of worse."

"Your da sounds like a philosopher," Callum said dryly.

"He sounds like a man who owed money to people like Mr. Vane," Pip said, and the fire cracked, and nobody answered, and Rosalind lay awake a long time listening to the wind learn their names.

* * *

On the fourth day they crossed the saddle, and the mountain showed them its other face.

The southern slope fell away into a valley so deep the clouds pooled in it like milk in a bowl. On the far side, ridge upon ridge receded into haze, blue fading to ghost fading to nothing, and the scale of it pressed down on them the way debt presses — not painfully, exactly, but constantly, reminding you how small your portion was.

Maren stood at the edge of the drop with the wind pulling her hair sideways and her mother's compass open in her palm.

"She came up here," she said. Not to anyone. To the valley. "Eleven years ago. Right around here, maybe. And she never once wrote and said, come see this."

"People don't write the things that shame them," Callum said quietly.

"What's shameful about a view?"

"Nothing." He looked out at it. "That's usually the problem."

Rosalind left them to it. Some altitudes were internal, and you couldn't guide a person through those; you could only wait at the top of the climb and pretend you hadn't watched them make it.

She found Renn checking the map over her shoulder — no, not the map; her face, reflected in the moment she unrolled it. He did it smoothly, the way he did everything.

"Saddle spring's real," he reported. "I filled the drums. We should talk schedule."

"We should."

"Vane wants the site reached inside ten days of departure. We're on five. Storm season—" he glanced at the sky, which was doing something complicated on the far ridges, towers building and dissolving, building again taller— "opens early some years."

"You've chased storms, Renn?"

"I've driven through them." A pause with weight in it. "There's a difference."

"There is," Rosalind agreed. "One of them means you chose to be there."

His eyes came around to hers, flat and gray as slate, and for a moment she thought he might actually answer that. Instead he shouldered two water drums and walked toward camp, and she stood alone at the edge of the valley feeling the shape of the trap she was standing in — the one with walls made of money and time and a man's patient arithmetic — and told herself, as she had told herself every night since Fair Weather, that she was still the one holding the map.

It helped less each day.

* * *

The fifth day the weather turned thoughtful.

Not bad — not yet — but changed. The morning came up hot and windless, the air thick enough to chew, and the clouds over the western ridges stood up straight instead of lying along the peaks like they belonged there. Rosalind had spent twelve years reading skies the way bankers read ledgers, and what this sky was saying made her jaw tighten.

"Cumulonimbus, building by midday, anvil by afternoon," she murmured to Maren as they climbed. "If that goes up over the divide tonight, we'll get lightning, maybe hail. Nothing lethal. But it's the opening act."

"Opening act of what?"

"Of the season." Rosalind shaded her eyes. "Monsoon doesn't arrive, Maren. It *commits*. One day it's rumors and heat. The next, the whole sky moves in and doesn't give the mountains back until September. Everything we do after that happens in the rain, in the dark, on ground that's trying to slide away underneath us."

"So we hurry."

"So we hurry," Rosalind said, "and we hurry carefully, because the mountain kills hurrying people faster than lazy ones. There's a difference between speed and haste. Speed has a plan."

Maren considered that, touching her compass. "My mother's notes said the same thing. Word for word, almost."

"Your mother chased weather?"

"My mother chased *paying work*, same as everyone. But she kept weather logs. Forty years of them. Dates, pressure readings, what the sky looked like before it turned." Maren's voice did something complicated. "I thought it was just an old woman's habit. Now I think it was how she stayed alive up here alone."

Rosalind filed that away next to the map, next to the ledger in her head where Vane's interest compounded daily. Everything on this mountain was turning out to be evidence of something.

* * *

They made the high camp at midafternoon, a shelf of ground below the final ridge with a stream threading through it and just enough flat space for four shelters. Rosalind called it early — deliberately. The clouds over the west had organized themselves into something with structure and ambition, and she wanted bodies rested and gear lashed down before whatever was coming arrived to inspect them.

By dusk the storm was visible in full dress rehearsal: a wall of cloud standing on the far ranges, lit from within by silent flickers, blue-white pulses like a heartbeat behind smoked glass. No thunder reached them yet. The silence was somehow worse.

Pip stood watching it with his mouth slightly open. "Is that close?"

"Twenty miles. Maybe fifteen."

"Will it hit us?"

"It'll miss us by a few miles and shake our teeth loose doing it." Rosalind checked the guy-lines a third time. "But it's a message, Pip. That cell is the scouts. Behind it, coming up from the coast over the next few days, is the army."

Callum emerged from the medical kit long enough to glance at the horizon. "And Vane's ten-day window?"

"Shrinking."

"And if we don't reach the wreck before the monsoon commits?"

Rosalind looked at him. At Maren, who had stopped pretending not to listen. At the firelight catching the rifle across Renn's knees, and the man's face above it, unreadable as ever, watching her the way he always watched her — like a man confirming the location of something before deciding whether it needed to exist.

"Then we reach it during," she said. "In the rain. In the dark. On ground trying to slide away. Because there is no version of my life where I go back to Edmund Vane empty-handed, and I suspect—" she tipped her chin at Renn— "there isn't one for any of us."

Nobody argued. That was the trouble with true things; they ended conversations.

* * *

The storm reached them near midnight, and it was magnificent.

It passed to the north, as promised, close enough that the lightning turned the inside of the tents white and the thunder arrived not as sound but as pressure, a fist closing around the whole shelf of mountain. Rain came sideways. The stream below camp doubled its voice. Somewhere upslope, rockfall clattered down in the dark like applause from an unseen gallery.

Rosalind lay awake in her shelter, listening, doing what she had done for twelve years in a dozen cockpits and truck cabs: reading the storm's structure through its noise. This one was clean, fast-moving, already dying as it crossed the divide. Tomorrow would be washed and clear and cold.

But behind it — she could feel it the way animals feel earthquakes — behind it was the real thing, gathering over the lowlands, fattening on the warm sea. Three days. Four. Maybe five if they were lucky, and luck was a currency she'd been overdrawn on for years.

Against her ribs, through the oilcloth, the map pressed its paper silence against her heartbeat. Somewhere over that final ridge, two days' climb through country that ate roads and remembered no mercy, lay the pass of broken trucks, and whatever truth had died there with them.

She was almost asleep when she heard it: boots on wet stone, unhurried, stopping just outside her shelter. A shadow blocked the last of the fire-glow.

"Thornbury." Renn's voice, pitched low, conversational. "You'll want to see this."

She crawled out into the rain's ragged tail end. He was standing at the edge of camp facing south, and she followed his gaze, and her stomach dropped through the mountain.

Down in the black valley, tiny with distance but unmistakable, lights were climbing the switchback trail they'd abandoned five days ago. Lanterns. Six of them, maybe eight, moving in disciplined order, gaining altitude steadily through the night.

"Whoever they are," Renn said quietly, "they're following our route. Our camps. Our pace." He turned his head, firelight sliding across the closed-ledger face, and for the first time since Fair Weather he sounded less like an employee and more like a man delivering a verdict. "So either your dead prospector sold more than one copy of that map—"

"—or somebody hired their own expedition," Rosalind finished.

Above them the last thunder rolled away eastward, and the mountain listened, and waited, and gave up none of its answers.
