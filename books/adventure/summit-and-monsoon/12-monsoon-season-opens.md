## 12. Monsoon Season Opens

The first rain came at dawn, and it did not fall so much as arrive.

Rosalind woke in the lee of the truck with the taste of iron on her teeth and the air gone thick as wool. For a moment she could not name what had changed — only that the silence of the highlands, that vast green hush they had climbed into for six days, was no longer silence. It was a held breath. The forest stood motionless in the grey half-light, every leaf angled upward, every bird gone from the canopy as though the whole valley had been evacuated by some silent decree.

Then the sky opened.

It began not with drops but with a sound — a low, grinding roar far above them, like a millstone turning somewhere beyond the clouds — and then the first sheets came down, straight and silver and dense enough to blur the ridgelines into watercolor. Within a minute the world had shrunk to the radius of the tarp strung between two trees, within five it had shrunk further still, to the circle of lamplight inside the truck's canvas back, where four people sat shoulder to shoulder and listened to the monsoon announce itself like an army crossing a border.

"Well," said Callum, the medic, in the flat voice he used for bad diagnoses. "That's it, then."

"That's it," Rosalind agreed.

* * *

They had made camp the night before on a shelf of ground above a stream that, when Rosalind had last looked at it, had been a polite ankle-deep trickle chuckling over red stones. She went out into the rain now, crouching under the tarp's dripping edge, and looked at what the stream had become. It was a brown torrent the width of a road, hauling branches and whole uprooted ferns along its surface like flotsam after a shipwreck. The stones were gone. The sound it made was not the sound of water anymore; it was the sound of weight, of tonnage, of something with intent.

She stood there longer than she needed to, letting the spray soak her to the skin, because there was a lesson in it and she wanted the lesson written clearly. Six days of climbing through the green wall of the interior, following Ada Quill's map through abandoned leases and overgrown survey cuts, and she had begun to let herself believe the weather would be a background thing — atmosphere, texture, a little drama for the journal. The monsoon had waited until she believed it, and then it had come down like a verdict.

When she climbed back into the truck bed, Wren the runaway had already built a small fire on a sheet of tin, defying all logic, and was feeding it slivers of damp kindling with the patience of someone who had grown up cold. The girl looked up at her, rain-wet hair plastered to her forehead, eyes too old for fifteen.

"It's early," Wren said. "The season. Isn't it? The prospector's notes said—"

"The notes said mid-month." Rosalind wrung out her sleeves. "It's four days early."

"Is that bad?"

Rosalind considered lying, decided against it. Lying to this crew cost more than it bought. "It means the rivers will rise before we're across them instead of after. It means the pass road turns to soup. It means everything we planned is now a suggestion."

Callum snorted from his corner, where he was cataloguing his medical stores with the grim devotion of a man counting bullets. "Everything we planned was a suggestion before. Now it's a rumor."

* * *

Mira Quill did not speak during the first day of rain. This was not unusual — Mira rationed her words the way other people rationed money — but Rosalind noticed that she spent the whole of it at the tailgate, under the awning, with the map open under oilcloth and her mother's journal beside it, cross-referencing in pencil, making small marks that Rosalind could not read from where she sat. Mira had inherited her mother's handwriting and none of her warmth; her annotations were tiny, precise, and merciless.

Near dusk, when the rain eased from deluge to merely relentless, Mira beckoned Rosalind over without looking up.

"The map has a seasonal layer," she said. "I didn't see it before. My mother drew it in different inks — look." She tilted the oilcloth so the dying light caught the paper. Beneath the route lines Rosalind had been following, fainter, in a blue-grey ink that had faded almost to nothing, ran a second set of markings: dates. Little dates, penciled in the margins beside each river crossing, each ford, each stretch of the pass road. *Passable to 14th. Risky after. Dead by 20th.* And beside the Karst Hollow crossing, in Ada Quill's cramped hand: *No.*

"No?" Rosalind said. "No what?"

"No anything. No crossing, no bridge, no argument." Mira traced the line of the route with one finger, up past the leased land, past the river camps, toward the notch in the ridgeline where the convoy had died. "She wrote this eleven years ago, near the end. She'd crossed these hills in every month of the year. If she says Karst Hollow kills you after the twentieth, then Karst Hollow kills you."

"And today is?"

"The ninth."

"Eleven days."

"At the rate the water's rising? Fewer." Mira rolled the map with a snap of finality. "We move fast or we don't move at all."

* * *

They moved fast.

Or rather, they moved as fast as the monsoon permitted, which was a different thing entirely. The second day of rain turned the track ahead of them into a canal; the third dissolved a switchback behind them with a sound like a door slamming, forty meters of hillside sliding away into the valley in a single groaning exhalation that left everyone silent for a mile afterward. They drove when they could drive and walked when driving meant dying, winching the truck along collapsed sections with Callum and Wren hip-deep in floodwater, hands raw on wet cable, while Rosalind stood in the rain reading the slope above them for the tremor of the next failure.

It was on the fourth morning — the eleventh, by Mira's count — that they reached the leased land.

Rosalind had known it was coming; the map showed it plainly, a block of territory shaded in Vane Syndicate green, and Edmund Vane's sealed orders, which she carried folded against her ribs like a splinter, had been explicit about the route. But knowing did not prepare her for the reality of it. The lease was not a fence or a gate. It was a scar. The hills here had been stripped to the mineral bone — terraced, benched, gouged into amphitheaters of raw red earth that the rain was now carving into bleeding gullies. Runoff poured off the workings in braids of rust-colored water, joining, thickening, staining the river below the color of a wound. There was no birdsong. There was no canopy. There was machinery under tarps the size of chapels, and a company camp on stilts with satellite dishes on its roof, and men in orange slickers who watched their truck pass with the incurious hostility of guards watching prisoners.

Wren pressed her face to the window. "What happened to it?"

"Money happened to it," Callum said.

Mira said nothing, but Rosalind saw her looking at the ruined slopes with an expression she could not read — and understood, suddenly, that somewhere in those stripped hills, in some cut or adit or flooded pit, Ada Quill had worked her last seasons. That the daughter was looking for her mother in the wreckage and finding only the reason her mother had died broke and estranged and selling maps from a cot in a lowland town.

They cleared the lease by midafternoon and climbed back into living forest, and even the drowned, roaring dark of the unbroken jungle felt like mercy after it.

* * *

That night the storm found them properly.

It came down the valley an hour after dark, and Rosalind felt it before she heard it — a pressure change, a flattening of the air, the fire guttering sideways though there was no wind yet. Then the wind arrived, all at once, and with it a rain so violent it was less water than assault, driven horizontal, hissing through the camp like gravel. The tarp tore loose at one corner and Wren was out into it in seconds, barefoot, hauling the line down and re-anchoring it while branches cracked overhead like rifle shots. Callum braced the medical chest with his body. Mira held the map case against her stomach with both arms, curled around it like a child.

And Rosalind stood at the edge of the light and watched the lightning walk the ridgeline north — toward the pass, toward the wreck, toward whatever the government convoy had carried and Edmund Vane had paid her to bury — and felt, with a clarity that frightened her more than the storm, that the weather was not an obstacle in this story. It was a character. It had opinions. It had been waiting for them the way the Syndicate had been waiting for them, patiently, certain of the outcome.

The cell passed in forty minutes, moving north, taking the heat of the night with it. In the sudden ringing quiet, water dripped from everything. Wren sat shivering in the doorway, soaked through, grinning like a lunatic.

"Did you feel that?" the girl said. "The pressure. Right before. Like the whole sky leaning on you."

"I felt it," Rosalind said.

"My mum used to say that's how you know it's honest weather. When it leans on you first. Everything else is just showing off."

Callum, wrapping the girl in a dry blanket with hands that were gentler than his voice ever was, muttered that her mother sounded like a woman who'd never filed an insurance claim. Wren laughed. Even Mira's mouth moved at one corner, which from Mira was applause.

Rosalind checked the barometer Callum kept bolted inside the cab. It had fallen further overnight, and was still falling, smooth and steady, like something going under water.

* * *

On the twelfth morning they reached the river.

Ada Quill's map called it a crossing. What lay before them was a quarter-mile of churning chocolate water studded with the pale heads of boulders, rising even as they watched, the banks sloughing off into it in soft wet collapses. On the far side, dim through the rain-veil, the track climbed toward the pass — visible, close, taunting. Between them and it: the flood.

Rosalind stood on the bank a long time, rain running off her chin, doing arithmetic. Winch lines wouldn't reach. The truck would float and roll and kill whoever was in it. Swimming was suicide dressed up as courage. Which left the crossing upstream — the place marked on the map with Ada Quill's blue-grey ink and a date, *passable to 14th*, and today was the twelfth, and the water was already at the tops of the mid-channel stones.

Two days early. Everything, two days early.

"We could wait," Callum said behind her. Not hopefully. He knew the answer.

"If we wait, the crossing closes, and then the only way to the pass is Karst Hollow, and Karst Hollow is *no*." Rosalind turned. "So we cross tomorrow at first light, when the overnight rain drains off and before the next cell builds. One attempt. Everyone roped. Nobody argues with the water."

Mira had the map open despite the rain, holding the oilcloth over it with her body. She was staring at the crossing mark, and her face had gone strange.

"What?" Rosalind said.

"There's a note." Mira's finger hovered over a smudge of blue-grey pencil so faded she must have been reading it by memory and faith as much as sight. "Under the crossing. I thought it was just a date. It's not." She looked up, and for the first time since Rosalind had known her, Mira Quill looked afraid. "It says *the water keeps what it takes*. And underneath that—" Her voice caught, steadied. "Underneath that, in my mother's hand: *I buried him on the far bank. God forgive me, I took the ledger page from his coat.*"

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere in the flood, a tree the size of a church went by, traveling fast, rolling slowly like a drowning animal.

"A ledger page," Rosalind said quietly.

"From the convoy," Mira said. "Eleven years ago. She was here. She saw it happen — or found it after. Whatever my mother was running from, whatever killed her—" She folded the map closed with hands that were not quite steady. "It started at this crossing. And tomorrow we wade into it."

Above them, far up the valley toward the pass, thunder rolled — long, low, and continuous, less like weather than like distant artillery. The barometer in the cab was still falling.

Rosalind looked at the water keeping what it took, and made herself stop counting the days.
