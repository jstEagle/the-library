## 13. Sealed Orders

The rain came the night before they were due to leave, a thin cold drizzle that hissed against the tarpaulins of the supply yard and turned the lantern light into soft yellow smears. Rosalind stood beneath the awning of the quartermaster's hut and watched Edmund Vane's men load the last of the crates onto the mule train, and she thought about how a man's money always smelled of something — gun oil, in this case, and cedar, and underneath it all the faint chemical bite of new banknotes.

Vane had arrived an hour after dark, unannounced, in a canvas-topped truck that ground up the yard's mud ramp with its headlights doused. He stepped down into the drizzle without a coat, as if weather were a courtesy extended to lesser men, and Rosalind had to give him this: he did not flinch from it. The rain beaded on his grey shoulders and he walked through it the way he seemed to walk through everything, upright, unhurried, a man who had decided long ago what the world was going to do for him.

"Miss Thornbury." He stopped under the awning, close enough that she could see the rain caught in the silver at his temples. "You'll want to look at these before we're on the road."

He held out a leather folio, stiff and expensive, and inside it were the orders.

She had expected manifests. Waypoints, perhaps, or a list of coordinates dressed up as survey data. What she got instead was three pages of dense, typed text on paper so heavy it felt like currency, each page stamped along its lower edge with a wax seal the colour of dried blood. She read the first page twice, then a third time, because the words refused to arrange themselves into anything sensible.

*The bearer is instructed to proceed by the eastern route only. Under no circumstances is the convoy to divert west of the Karst line. Final orders are contained herein and are to be opened upon arrival at the marked position, and by no one but the bearer.*

"The marked position," Rosalind said slowly, "being the wreck."

"Being where I tell you it is." Vane took off his glasses and polished them on his cuff, an oddly domestic gesture that made her trust him less, not more. "You have your map. I have my reasons. They are not the same reasons, and that is precisely why the arrangement works."

"And the seals?"

"A formality." He put the glasses back on. His eyes behind them were pale and patient, the eyes of a man watching a ledger balance itself. "Break one before you reach the position and the payment structure changes. Not disappears — I'm not a barbarian — but changes. You'd find the arithmetic considerably less generous."

Rosalind looked past him, out into the yard, where the mules stood hipshot under their loads and the crew moved between them in the lamplight. Merrick was checking the medical panniers with the grim thoroughness of a woman who had once run a field hospital and now counted bandages like a miser counts coins. Pip was supposed to be securing the fuel drums and was instead showing Wren Quill how to work the winch, both of them lit up with the particular laughter of people who did not yet know what they were carrying. Wren had been with them nine days. Nine days of silence about her father, broken only once, when Rosalind had asked what Ada Quill had been like at the end, and Wren had said, *quiet*, and nothing else.

"You understand," Rosalind said, folding the folio closed, "that I don't lead expeditions blindfolded. It's how people die."

"People die on expeditions because they panic, Miss Thornbury. Because they argue. Because they decide, somewhere above the tree line, that they know better than the person paying for the rope under their feet." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "I'm not asking you to go blindfolded. I'm asking you to go *disciplined*. There's a difference, and the difference is thirty thousand crowns, payable on your return, plus clearance of certain outstanding obligations that I'm given to understand have become... urgent."

The word *urgent* landed exactly where he intended it. Rosalind felt it settle into her chest like a swallowed stone. The Syndicate's collectors had come to her door eleven days ago. She had not told the crew. She had not told anyone. But Vane knew — of course he knew; the debt and the job had come through the same hands — and he wore that knowledge the way other men wore signet rings.

"I'll keep your seals intact," she said.

"I never doubted it." He turned up his collar against the drizzle. "One more thing. My man Callum will be travelling with you as far as the high camp. You've met."

"I've met."

"He has his own copy of the route. If anything should happen to you — anything at all — the expedition continues under his hand. For everyone's sake, I hope it doesn't come to that. He's a capable man, but he lacks your..." Vane paused, selecting the word with the care of a jeweller. "*Instincts.*"

He left the way he had come, doused headlights and all, and the truck's taillights drowned in the rain long before the sound of the engine did.

* * *

Merrick found her an hour later in the supply hut, sitting on a crate of condensed milk with the folio open on her knees and a storm lamp burning low beside her.

"You've been staring at those pages since he left," Merrick said. She dropped onto an opposite crate, joints cracking, and helped herself to the flask at Rosalind's elbow without asking. She was fifty-one years old and built like something the army had tried to break and failed, and she drank the way she did everything else: efficiently, without apology. "So. What does our patron want?"

"He wants us to take the eastern route."

"There is no eastern route. There's a goat track and a riverbed."

"There is now."

Merrick lowered the flask. In the lamplight her face was all planes and suspicion. "And the seals?"

"Open at the position. By me alone. Break them early and the money turns to dust."

"Hm." Merrick turned the flask in her blunt fingers, around and around. "You know, in the field we had a rule about sealed orders. Command gave them to officers going into country where the radios didn't reach. And every officer who ever opened one early told me the same thing afterward." She looked up. "They wished they hadn't. Not because of what was written. Because of what it meant that someone hadn't trusted them with it plain."

"You think he doesn't trust me."

"I think a man who trusts you pays you and lets you work. A man who doesn't pays you and watches." Merrick tipped her head toward the yard, toward the shape of Callum moving among the mules, checking girth straps with a patience that looked, to Rosalind's eye, exactly like rehearsal. "That one's not here for his health. And he's not here for yours either."

"Vane says if anything happens to me, Callum takes over."

"Vane says a lot of things." Merrick stood, wincing, and took the flask with her, which was its own kind of commentary. "Get some sleep, Thornbury. Tomorrow you start lying to your crew, and I've found that goes easier on a full night's rest."

Rosalind sat alone after she left, the lamp guttering, the sealed pages heavy in her lap. Three times she set her thumb against the edge of the top seal. Three times she took it away. Whatever was written there would still be written tomorrow, and the day after, all the way up the goat track and the riverbed to whatever the map had promised and Vane had paid for. There was no hurry in it. That was the thing about sealed orders — they were patient, and patience, in her experience, was the property of traps.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, in that blue-grey hour when the mist lay in the valley like milk poured into a bowl and the mountains above were only a rumour of darker blue. The mule train went out through the yard gate in single file, hooves sucking in the wet clay, and Rosalind rode at the head with the map tube strapped across her back and the folio inside her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel it with every breath like a second heartbeat.

Wren fell in beside her at the first mile marker. The girl rode badly, all elbows and grievance, having learned horses young and mules never, but she had refused the offer of a place in the rear and Rosalind had not argued. There was something in the girl that needed the front of a column the way some people needed a window seat.

"Your friend Callum," Wren said, after a mile of silence.

"He's not my friend."

"No?" Wren glanced back down the line, to where Callum rode fourth, equidistant between the supplies and the people, which Rosalind had noticed too. "He asked me last night where my father used to take his surveys. Western slopes, mostly. I told him." Her jaw tightened. "I shouldn't have told him. I don't know why I told him."

"Because he asked kindly," Rosalind said. "That's usually why."

Wren was quiet for a while. The track climbed along a spur above a stream running fast and brown with the first rains, and the sound of it filled the space between them, water worrying at stone, patient as everything else up here.

"My mother says the mountains keep what men bury in them," the girl said finally. "She said it the week before she died, and I thought it was just a thing dying women say. But Ada—" She stopped herself, corrected. "My father wrote it in a letter once. Same words. Like they'd agreed on it." She looked at Rosalind, and in the flat early light her eyes were older than seventeen. "What are we digging up, Ms. Thornbury?"

"Salvage," Rosalind said. "A government convoy lost in the floods two years back. Radios, instruments, things worth recovering."

"That's what you tell the crew."

"It's what's true."

"It's what's *safe*," Wren said. "Those aren't always the same thing out here. My father taught me that too." And she nudged her mule forward, up the line, leaving Rosalind alone with the map tube and the folio and the small cold voice in her chest that had been getting louder since the supply yard, saying: *you know there's more than salvage in those trucks. You've known since the night the old woman pressed the map into your hands and made you swear.*

The track bent westward. According to the folio, it must not.

Rosalind reined in, waited until the whole strung-out line came abreast of her in the mist, and pointed east, up a slope of scree and stunted pine where no track showed at all.

"Eastern route," she called down the line. "Single file. Watch your footing."

Behind her, half-hidden by the mist and the mules, she saw Callum take out a notebook and write something down.

* * *

They made the high shoulder by late afternoon, and camped in the lee of a rock face streaked white with bird lime, and the rain found them again at dusk, heavier now, with thunder walking the ridgelines to the north like something enormous changing its mind. Merrick dosed Pip's blistered hands and Wren's sunburn and pretended not to notice that Callum ate apart from the others, facing downslope, where a man could watch the way they'd come.

Rosalind checked the seals by lamplight, all three of them, intact, and slept with the folio under her head, and dreamed of brown water rising over a road full of trucks, and woke before dawn with the dream's silence still in her ears — the terrible specific silence of a place where every radio has gone dead at once.

On the third morning they reached the Karst line, where the limestone broke through the earth in grey blades and the eastern track, such as it was, ended at a wall of rock no mule could climb. Beyond it, visible even through the rain-haze, the western valleys opened green and vast, and somewhere in that green, according to a dying woman's map, was the pass of broken trucks.

Callum rode up beside her. He was pleasant about it, in his careful way. "Patron's orders say east of the Karst line, Miss Thornbury. This is the line."

"This is the line," Rosalind agreed. She reached inside her coat and drew out the folio, and held it up so he could see the seals, unbroken, blood-red in the grey light. "And these say the final orders open at the position. Which means somebody drew a map that contradicts somebody else's map, Mr. Callum, and one of them is wrong."

She watched him weigh it. She watched him decide — visibly, the way a man decides to step onto ice — that the seals mattered more than the route, because the seals were what he could report, and routes were just weather.

"East, then," Callum said. "There's a way over the shoulder. Harder. Slower."

"Everything worth doing is," Rosalind said, and turned the mule train north along the blade-edge of the karst, east of the line, away from the pass, away from the truth, following her patron's sealed instructions into country that grew stranger and higher with every mile — until, near dusk, half-buried in the scree at the side of the track, Pip shouted and held up something small and rusted that glinted in the last light.

A military buckle. Government issue. Two years in the weather, and a hundred miles from any road that appeared on any map Rosalind had ever seen.

Nobody spoke. The rain ticked on the rocks. And far to the west, beyond the karst wall, thunder rolled up out of the green valleys like something answering.
