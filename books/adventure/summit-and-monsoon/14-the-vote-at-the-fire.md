## 14. The Vote at the Fire

The storm held off that night, as if it too were waiting to hear what they would decide.

They made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder where the trail bent away from the river, and Rosalind built the fire small — a fist of flame in a ring of wet stone, more smoke than light. Smoke was honest, at least. It told the truth about the wind, and the wind had gone strange all evening, sliding down from the pass in slow, cold breaths that smelled of iron and high snow. The kind of wind that came before weather with intentions.

The sealed orders sat on a flat stone between them, where everyone could see them and nobody wanted to touch them.

Vane had given them to her in Fairweather, in a leather envelope waxed shut and heavy as a held breath. *Open only at the leased land*, he'd said, and smiled his banker's smile, and she had been too deep in the arithmetic of her own ruin to ask the right questions. Now the envelope lay open, and the two pages inside had been read four times each, passed hand to hand around the fire like a thing that might bite, and the silence they'd left behind had weight and texture, like wet wool.

Dr. Callum Reeve broke it first, because Callum always broke silences, the way other men broke horses — roughly, and because he couldn't stand the feel of them.

"Well," he said, turning his tin cup in his scarred hands. The burn scars ran from his wrist to his elbow, from the field hospital at Kessarin that no longer officially existed. "I've read it three times. I keep hoping it'll turn into something else. It doesn't."

"It won't," Rosalind said.

"Say it anyway. Out loud. I want to hear it in the open air where it can't hide."

Rosalind looked at the fire instead of at them. The orders were simple, in the way that cruelty is often simple. Vane's instructions, in his own clipped hand: upon reaching the leased land, the expedition was to proceed directly to the wreck site, secure all documentary material found there, and burn the rest. Burn the vehicles. Burn the bodies, if bodies there were. Burn anything that burned. And the final paragraph, the one that had made Nell Quill go white and quiet: *No personnel attached to this expedition are to retain knowledge of the site's contents. Discretion is a condition of final payment.*

*Discretion is a condition of final payment.* Nine words that meant: none of you are meant to come down off this mountain.

"He's not paying us to find it," Nell said. Her voice was flat, which was worse than if she'd shouted. She sat with her knees drawn up, her mother's old survey compass open in her lap, the needle trembling. "He's paying us to erase it. We're not a salvage crew. We're a mop."

"You don't know that there's anything to erase," Pip said.

Everyone looked at the boy. Pip — fifteen years old, all elbows and stubbornness, a runaway out of the Lowlands dockyards with a stolen barometer and an encyclopedic memory for weather — had been the first to volunteer for the expedition and the last to understand what he'd volunteered for. He was wrapped in a blanket two sizes too large, and his face in the firelight was all sharp angles and trying-not-to-be-afraid.

"The convoy," he said. "The wreck. Nobody's actually seen it. Maybe it's nothing. Maybe it's just trucks that slid off the road in a storm two years back. Maybe the papers are tax receipts."

"Maybe," Rosalind said.

"But you don't think so."

"No," Rosalind said. "I don't."

She told them then, because they deserved it told straight, the way she'd pieced it together. The convoy had gone up the pass in the last week of the old monsoon, government plates, government escort, and had never reported in. The official record said *lost to flooding*. But the leased land — Vane's leased land, bought through three shell companies in the same season — sat exactly along the convoy's route, a corridor of private ground twenty miles long, sealed to surveyors, sealed to searchers, sealed to the families who'd petitioned for two years to know where their sons and daughters had died. And now a salvage expedition, funded by the man who owned the seal, carrying orders to burn what it found.

"You think he did it," Callum said. Not a question.

"I think he's spent a fortune making sure nobody finds out what happened," Rosalind said. "Men don't buy that much silence over tax receipts."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, a branch came down under the weight of the wet, a soft groan and a crash, and everyone flinched, and everyone pretended they hadn't.

Nell spoke without looking up from the compass. "My mother sold him that map."

The words came out of her like something she'd been carrying under her ribs for days. Ada Quill — dying of the lung-rot in a rented room in Marsh Town, selling her life's survey work for enough to bury herself with — had sold the route to a man who came asking no questions, or had sold it to Rosalind, who'd carried it to Vane without asking any. Either way, the map had passed through their hands and into his, and now it pointed at a grave he wanted dug deeper.

"She didn't know," Nell said. "I keep telling myself she didn't know. She was mapping the pass for the water tables. She'd been over that ground a hundred times. If she'd known there was a convoy up there — if she'd known what he wanted —" Her thumb moved over the compass bezel, around and around. "She'd have burned the map herself before she let him use it."

"Maybe," Callum said gently. "Or maybe it doesn't matter what she'd have done. It matters what we do."

* * *

Rosalind stood, because sitting still had become impossible, and walked to the edge of the firelight where the granite shoulder dropped away into the dark. Below them the river ran loud, swollen with the week's rain, a sound like a crowd muttering. Above them the pass was invisible, but she could feel it the way she felt all high ground — a pressure at the back of the skull, an old instinct from ten years of chasing weather across the ranges. The monsoon was coming. Every bird, every insect, every drop of pressure in the air said so. They had perhaps four clear days left, maybe five, before the green wall came up the valley and closed the highlands for the season.

Four days to the wreck site. Four days back, if the river let them. It was going to be close even if nothing went wrong, and something always went wrong.

She thought about the ledger in her head, the one she kept the way other people kept prayers. Eleven thousand owed to the Vane Syndicate, compounding monthly. The *Stormcrow* mortgaged twice. Her name on a list of debtors that got shorter only two ways — payment, or the river. The job paid enough to clear it. All of it. Every debt, every lien, every humiliating monthly visit from the Syndicate's soft-voiced collectors. One job. One season. And all she had to do was not see what she saw.

*Discretion is a condition of final payment.*

He'd told her, in his way. He'd told her from the beginning, and she'd heard only the number.

"Ros." Callum's voice behind her. He'd come to the edge of the light, cup in hand, and stood beside her without looking at her, which was his way of being kind. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm counting."

"Money?"

"Days. Money. Both." She watched the river's muttering dark. "If we turn back, he doesn't pay. If he doesn't pay, we're done — all of us. And he knows we've read the orders, which means he can't let us be loose with what we know anyway. Turning back doesn't buy safety. It just buys being a problem he solves later, on his schedule, in his way."

"You've thought about running."

"Every night since the envelope." She almost smiled. "The *Stormcrow* could be in the Marsh Town marshes by the end of the week. New names. It works in ballads. In practice, Vane's collectors found a man last spring who'd been hiding under a different name for six years, in another province, with a new wife and a new trade. They didn't hurt him. They hurt her, and then he paid. That's the arithmetic. There's no down off this mountain that Vane doesn't own."

"Then it's not really a vote, is it. It's a eulogy either way."

"No." She turned to look at him. In the firelight his burn-scarred arm caught the orange light and held it, and his eyes were tired in the way of a man who had seen triage and knew that some choices were only about which wound bled slowest. "There's a third thing. It's just a stupid thing."

"The papers," Callum said.

"The papers. Whatever's in that convoy — if there's anything in that convoy — it doesn't belong to him and it doesn't belong to us. It belongs to the families who've been petitioning for two years. It belongs to whatever court still has a spine. We take it down off this mountain and we put it somewhere he can't buy, can't burn, can't bury." She heard her own voice harden and didn't soften it. "And then we run like hell, and maybe we live, and maybe we don't. But we stop being his mop."

"That's not a job anymore. That's a war."

"I know."

"He'll come for us. Not collectors. Real men. He has them."

"I know that too."

Callum was quiet a while. Down the slope, the river muttered its crowd-murmur, and the wind came down from the pass again, cold and iron-smelling, and Pip's barometer — the boy checked it hourly, obsessively, the way other boys checked their pockets for coins — was falling, falling, falling.

"Kessarin," Callum said at last.

She waited. He never talked about Kessarin. The field hospital that had been ordered abandoned with patients still in it; the fire that had covered the abandonment; the inquiry that had found no one responsible because everyone responsible had been promoted. His name had been on the duty roster. His hands had carried what they'd told him to carry. He'd come down off that mountain with scars and a struck-off license and a silence he'd been keeping for six years.

"I carried the records out," he said. "That was my job, the last week. The real records, not the ones the inquiry saw. I was told to burn them and I told them I had. I kept them in a footlocker for four years and then I got scared and I burned them myself, one night, in a stove, and I've regretted it every day since. Not because it would have saved me. Because it would have saved *them* — the ones in the wards. Somebody, somewhere, would at least have known their names." He looked at her. "I'm not burning anything for Edmund Vane. Whatever the vote is, that's my vote. I just needed to say the rest out loud."

"Thank you," Rosalind said, and meant it more than she could shape into words.

* * *

They gathered the crew at the fire because a vote deserved a fire, and a fire deserved the truth.

Rosalind laid it out plainly, all of it: the orders, the arithmetic, the three roads. Take Vane's coin and do as ordered, and come down rich and complicit. Turn back, and come down broke and hunted. Or take the evidence and make for the lowlands and the light, and come down — if they came down at all — poor, hunted, and clean. She didn't dress it up. She'd been on enough mountains to know that lies weigh more at altitude.

"Nobody's bound," she finished. "Not by me, not by the contract, not by anything but your own conscience. Pip, you're fifteen. If you vote to go home, nobody thinks less of you. I'll see you to the low road myself."

Pip sat very straight, the blanket around his shoulders like a borrowed general's cloak, and looked into the fire for a long time before he spoke.

"I ran away from the docks because the Syndicate owned everything there," he said. "The cranes, the tally-houses, the men who checked the ledgers. My father worked their ledgers for nineteen years and they threw him out with nothing when the numbers stopped liking him. I used to think if I got far enough up into the high country, past the passes, I'd be out from under them." He laughed, short and bitter, a sound too old for his face. "Turns out they own the high country too. So no. I'm not going home. Home is theirs. I vote we take the papers and I vote we make them sorry."

"Nell?"

Nell had not touched her supper. The compass lay closed in her lap now, and her hands were still, which for Nell was the loudest thing about her. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and even, the voice of a woman reading a survey she'd already completed in her head.

"I spent two years not knowing where my mother went in the last season of her life. Two years of petitions, and every one of them came back stamped by an office I now know Vane owns. There are families like mine all down the valley. Mothers. Sons." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were the grey of the coming storm. "If that convoy up there has their names in it — if it has the truth in it — then I don't care if I come down with a single coin. I vote for the papers. And I vote that if anyone tries to stop us, we stop them first."

The words hung there, harder than anything else said that night, and nobody softened them.

"Callum?"

"You heard me at the ridge," he said. "The papers. And I'll add this — I'll swear the medical oath over it if that helps anyone. *First, do no harm.* I did harm once, by obeying. Never again."

Three votes. Rosalind looked around the fire — the boy with the stolen barometer, the daughter with the dead mother's compass, the medic with his scarred hands — and felt something she hadn't felt since the debt first closed over her: not hope, exactly. Hope was a fair-weather thing. This was something that worked in storms. Purpose, maybe. Or just the strange, stubborn warmth of people standing in the same cold wind on purpose.

"And me," she said. "Four. We take the evidence. We take it to the marsh town, to whoever still prints the truth in this province — I know a name, an old contact, we'll find them. And we keep it hidden on the way down, and we trust no one we don't have to, and we assume every stranger on the trail is Vane's. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said three voices.

"Then we move at first light, we make the leased land by tomorrow night, and we —"

Pip's hand closed around the barometer so suddenly that the firelight flashed on the brass.

"Ros." His voice had gone thin. "It's dropped again. Twenty points since sundown. That's not a front, that's not a front at all — that's a cell. A big one. It's coming up the valley faster than the charts say anything should move."

Rosalind turned east, toward the pass, and saw it now — what she'd been feeling at the back of her skull all evening and refusing to name. Far up in the black, where the stars should have been, there was a darkness deeper than the night itself, a wall of nothing with lightning buried inside it like veins, flickering silent and slow, the way storms flicker when they're still hours out and deciding.

The monsoon wasn't coming in four days.

It was coming tomorrow.

"Get the tarps on the packs," Rosalind said. "Double lines on the tents. And somebody wake me the moment that lightning gets loud — because if that cell crests the pass before we do, the leased land is going to be the wettest, meanest ground in the province, and we'll be standing on it."

She looked back once at the fire, at the three faces around it, at the sealed orders lying on the stone like a dead thing.

They had voted. Now the storm would vote too.
