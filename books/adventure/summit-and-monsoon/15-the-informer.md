## 15. The Informer

The rain came in the night like a debt collector, soft at first, then insistent, drumming on the tarps until the whole camp seemed to breathe under it. Rosalind woke with her hand already around the knife beneath her bedroll — an old habit from years she did not like to count — and lay listening to the water find every seam in the world.

They had made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder the porters called the Sleeping Dog, three days up from the river crossing, and the monsoon had followed them the whole way like something that had caught their scent. Not the true storms yet. This was only the overture, the warm-up rain that turned the trails to grease and made the jungle exhale a smell of rot and green fire. The real season would open within the week. Everyone knew it. Everyone pretended otherwise.

She dressed in the dark, pulled back the flap of her tent, and stood in the grey half-light watching the camp.

Six tents, a cook fire drowned to a hissing memory, the mules hunched and miserable under their oilskins. Dev was already awake — Dev was always already awake — crouched by the mess tarp with a tin cup, steam rising off it into the drizzle. Wren's tent was dark; the girl slept like the dead these days, worn down by the climb. Dr. Okafor's lamp burned low behind canvas, the amber glow of a woman reading herself to sleep, if Rosalind had to guess, or not sleeping at all.

And at the edge of the camp, half-shielded by a curtain of wet fern, stood Callum Reeve.

He had his back to her. He was holding something up — not a cup, not a compass. It caught what little light there was and threw it back flat and dull. A signal mirror? No. Too small for that. He turned it in his fingers, looked at it, then at the treeline, then tucked it away inside his jacket with a motion so quick and practiced it might have been nothing at all.

Rosalind stood very still in the rain and felt the cold arrive not from the weather but from somewhere behind her sternum.

* * *

She said nothing at breakfast.

That was the discipline of it — the thing she'd learned in nine years of chasing storms across three continents, the thing the Vane Syndicate had never understood about her even as they bled her dry: you do not flinch at the first sight of rotation. You watch. You let it organize itself. You learn its size before you name it.

So she ate her rice and salted fish and passed the tea, and she watched Callum Reeve eat his breakfast like a man with nothing in his pockets but lint.

He was forty-something, lean as a fence rail, with a face weathered down to its essentials and a laugh he deployed exactly when a laugh was wanted. He'd come to her in Fair Weather with solid references — two salvage runs into the eastern ranges, a season guiding for a mining survey — and he knew the highlands better than any man she could afford, which was to say better than almost no one. She'd hired him for his maps-in-the-head, his feel for country, the way he could look at a ridgeline and tell you where the water would go when the sky opened. He'd been worth every rupee so far. That was the trouble with informers. They were always worth the money.

"Storm's organizing," Dev said, nodding at the western sky where the clouds had begun to stack themselves into architecture. "By tomorrow night we'll want to be off this face."

"Off it or under it?" Wren asked. The girl had appeared sometime during breakfast, hair still damp, eyes sharp as ever despite the exhaustion carved under them. She'd taken to sitting close to Rosalind at meals, and Rosalind had stopped pretending not to notice that she liked it.

"Under it, if we're smart," Callum said easily. "There's a cave system past the saddle. Dry as a sermon." He smiled at Wren, and Wren smiled back, because Wren smiled at everyone, because that was how a runaway survived.

Rosalind drank her tea and thought: *dry as a sermon.* Was that true? She didn't know. That was the poison of it, now. Every fact he offered came wrapped in the question of why he was offering it.

Ada Quill's map rode against Rosalind's ribs in an oilskin sleeve, as it had since the river crossing, since the night she'd finally sat down with it properly and understood what the old woman had drawn there — not just a route, but a confession, a route to the pass where the convoy died and the truth along with it. Edmund Vane wanted that map buried. Edmund Vane wanted everyone who'd seen it buried with it. And someone in this camp was telling him where they slept at night.

Or maybe no one was. Maybe the man at the ferns had been checking a pocket watch, or a locket, or a lucky coin. Maybe the cold in her chest was just the altitude.

But she had been lied to by professionals for nine years, and she knew the weight of a secret in a man's hands. Callum had held something last night, and he had held it the way you hold a thing you don't want seen.

* * *

She tested him at midday.

The trail climbed through bamboo so dense they moved inside it as though inside a green cathedral, light falling in broken shafts, mules blowing and slipping on the wet stone steps cut into the hillside a century ago by hands nobody remembered. Rosalind let the column string out and dropped back until she walked beside Callum, which was normal enough; she walked beside everyone eventually. That was a leader's trick too.

"The Sleeping Dog," she said. "You've come this way before?"

"Twice." He stepped over a root without looking at it. "Once in the dry season, once in a bad October. Different country in the rain."

"The caves past the saddle. You've sheltered in them?"

"One of them. The big one, north side of the stream."

"How far?"

"Two hours, maybe three in this mud."

She watched his eyes when he answered. His eyes went to the trail, to the ridge, to the mule ahead — everywhere competent, everywhere steady, nowhere evasive. Which was either the truth or the performance of a lifetime.

"And the other caves?"

"There aren't others worth the name. Why?"

"No reason. I like to know my options before the sky falls."

"You always do," he said, and grinned at her, easy as anything, and something about the ease of it — the *practicedness* of it — settled the question in her mind the way a plumb line settles in a well. Not proof. But weight.

That afternoon she took Dev aside under the pretense of checking the mules' hooves, and told him, in a voice pitched below the rain, everything she'd seen and suspected.

Dev listened the way he listened to heartbeats: completely, without expression. When she finished he was quiet a long moment, working a hoof pick through packed mud.

"You're sure it wasn't a watch?"

"It wasn't a watch."

"A mirror. Some men check themselves."

"He faced the trees, Dev."

"Mm." The medic set down the pick and straightened, rolling his bad shoulder — the one that had ended his career in the city hospitals, the one that ached before weather. "You know what I did, before? Before they struck me off?"

"You tell the story at every fire."

"I'm going to tell it again, because it applies." He looked out at the grey wall of the valley. "I had a colleague. Good surgeon, better liar. For two years he was stealing from the pharmacy — small amounts, untraceable amounts — and selling them. And every time someone raised a suspicion, the evidence pointed somewhere else, because he was the one arranging where it pointed. You know how we finally caught him?"

"How?"

"We didn't. We fed two different stories to two different people and waited to see which one came back changed." He picked the hoof pick up again. "It came back changed in eleven days."

Rosalind looked at him. Rain ran off the brim of his hat between them like a beaded curtain.

"You want to feed Callum a lie."

"I want to feed *someone* a lie. If it comes back changed, we'll know whose mouth it went through." He shrugged, that economical shrug that carried whole philosophies. "It costs us nothing but a little theater."

"And if it doesn't come back changed?"

"Then you owe Callum an apology and me a bottle."

* * *

The lie they chose was a route.

At the evening fire, with the rain hammering the tarp overhead and the mules stamping in the dark, Rosalind announced the next day's plan loudly enough for the whole camp: they would cross the saddle at first light, descend the northern drainage, and make for the cave Callum had described — the big one, north side of the stream. Two days' rest there while the front passed, then the final ascent toward the pass.

Except she didn't say the big one. What she said, deliberately, folding the falsehood into ordinary conversation like a pill into meat, was: *"the southern cave, under the split rock."*

There was no southern cave. There was no split rock. She had invented it that afternoon with Dev, a landmark that existed nowhere except in the words spoken at that fire — spoken once, clearly, and never repeated, so that if the plan changed in anyone's report, the change would have a single possible origin.

Callum had nodded along with the rest of them. He'd even added a detail — warned that the approach was exposed to rockfall in heavy rain, suggested they rope the mules. Helpful. Precise. The helpfulness of a man who intended for the information to matter.

Wren caught her eye across the fire, and Rosalind saw the girl had understood something was moving beneath the surface of the evening, though not what. Later, in the dark, zipping the two tents close together as they did now — the girl's nightmares had become a nightly weather of their own — Wren whispered:

"What's wrong with Callum?"

Rosalind lay staring at the canvas ceiling, listening to the rain interrogate it.

"Why do you ask that?"

"He watches the trail behind us. Always. Nobody else does that except you, and you're supposed to." A pause. "Also he asked me yesterday how long you'd known Ada Quill. Like it mattered. Like he was counting."

The cold came back, colder than before. Not because of Callum — because of Wren. Because the girl had seen it too, and children who had learned to read adults that carefully had usually paid tuition.

"Go to sleep, Wren."

"Are we in danger?"

The honest answer was *yes, we have been since Fair Weather,* and the kind answer was *no,* and she gave neither. "Everyone sleeps," she said. "I'll take first watch."

* * *

First watch was hers alone under the dripping eaves of the tarp, rifle across her knees, the fire banked to coals. The rain slackened near midnight to a fine drifting mist, and the clouds tore open briefly above the western peaks, showing stars, hard and indifferent, before closing again like a wound.

She thought about Ada Quill dying in that rented room with the map pressed into Rosalind's hands and her daughter's name in her mouth — *Maren, find Maren* — and about how the old woman had known, even then, that the map was a dangerous thing to carry. *They'll come for it,* Ada had rasped. *Vane's people. They always come.* Rosalind had assumed the danger would come from outside, from Syndicate men on the trail, from Renn and his hired muscle shadowing them through the green wall. She had built her defenses facing outward.

She had not considered that the enemy might sleep ten feet away and share her tea.

Near two in the morning, she heard him move.

Not to relieve himself — the sound was wrong for that, too deliberate, boots found and laced rather than stumbled into. She kept her eyes half-closed and her breathing even and watched through her lashes as Callum crossed the camp's edge, unhurried, and stopped at the fern screen. He stood there a moment. Then he took out the object again — whatever it was, flat and small — and angled it toward the sky, toward the gap in the clouds, and held it there.

Light flickered. Three pulses, a pause, two more. Faint, disciplined, gone in four seconds.

Then darkness, and after a darkness so complete it rang, a reply: two pulses from somewhere down-valley, so distant it might have been heat lightning, might have been nothing.

Might have been.

Callum stood a moment longer, listening. Then he put the thing away, returned to his tent with the same unhurried tread, and was still.

Rosalind sat with the rifle across her knees and her heart going like a trip-hammer and did not move for a long time. The rain resumed. The coals ticked. Somewhere in the black forest a bird gave its one wet note, over and over, like a question nobody would answer.

So. It was confirmed, then — or as confirmed as anything got in this country. Callum Reeve was signaling to someone in the valley below, someone close enough to answer within minutes, which meant Renn's people were closer than any of them had guessed. Closer than a day. Perhaps hours. And everything she had told the fire tonight — the southern cave, the split rock, the two days' rest — was already traveling down that dark slope at the speed of a man's conscience.

Which meant, if Dev was right, that tomorrow Renn would be waiting at a place that did not exist.

* * *

She woke Dev before dawn. He emerged from his tent with his coat already buttoned, as if he'd been lying dressed and waiting, which she suspected he had.

"Well?" he said.

"It's him. Signal mirror. He flashed the valley at two o'clock and something answered."

Dev exhaled slowly through his nose. In the pre-light his face was all planes and shadow. "The false route?"

"Gone down the wire. If your theory holds, Renn's men will move on the southern cave tonight or tomorrow."

"Then we should decide what we do about it before the camp wakes." He glanced back at the sleeping shapes under the tarps — Wren curled tight as a fist, the porters breathing in chorus, Okafor's tent dark now, and Callum's tent, silent, ordinary, damning. "Because there are only two plays, Rosalind. We confront him, and we lose the advantage and maybe lose a guide in country that kills the unwary. Or we say nothing, keep the lie alive, and walk him exactly where we want him."

"And if we walk him into a trap, we're using a man as bait. That's a hanging offense in some jurisdictions."

"In most jurisdictions so is what Vane did at that pass." Dev's voice was quiet and level. "You've been choosing between clean hands and survival since Fair Weather. I'd stop pretending the choice is new."

Below them, down the black throat of the valley, the mist was beginning to lift off the river, and in the lifting mist — far off, at the very limit of seeing, where the world dissolved into grey — Rosalind thought she saw movement. A flicker on the far ridge. Smoke, perhaps. Or men.

Or nothing. But she had stopped believing in nothing.

She turned back to the camp, where the fire needed building and the day needed starting and six people trusted her, five of whom deserved it.

"Wake the others," she said. "We cross the saddle at first light — same as announced. Same route." Her eyes went once, briefly, to the silent tent at the camp's edge. "Every word of it."

Dev studied her face. "And the trap?"

"Let's see what walks into it," Rosalind said, "before we decide whether we're the hunters or the bait."

Behind them, unnoticed by either, the flap of Callum's tent had moved — a hand's width of darkness, a pair of eyes adjusting to the grey — and closed again, softly, before the light could find it.
