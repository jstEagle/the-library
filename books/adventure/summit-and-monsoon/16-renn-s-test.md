## 16. Renn's Test

The man came up from the lowlands on a mule that hated him, and everyone in camp pretended not to watch him arrive.

Rosalind saw him first from the cook fire, where she'd been scraping mold off the last of the rice cakes with a knife edge gone dull as a spoon. He came around the shoulder of the ridge in that long, unhurried walk some men learn in places where hurrying gets you killed — weight forward, eyes moving, hands loose and empty and somehow not empty at all. Grey coat, grey eyes, a face like weathered limestone. The mule beneath him sidled and rolled its eye at every shadow, and he let it, because correcting it would have cost him attention he preferred to spend elsewhere.

"Company," said Wren from the tarps, where she'd been mending a torn strap with her teeth and both hands.

"I see him."

"He's got a Syndicate pin. Under the collar. I can see the shine."

"You can see a shine under his collar from forty meters."

"I can see everything," Wren said, with the serene confidence of seventeen, and went back to chewing the strap.

Callum emerged from the medical tent wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white, back when the world was younger and better laundered. He looked at the rider, and then at Rosalind, and something passed over his face like the shadow of a bird — there and gone, too quick to name. "That'll be Renn," he said quietly.

"You know him?"

"Everyone who ever worked for the Syndicate knows Renn. He doesn't inspect sites. He inspects *people*." Callum folded the rag into smaller and smaller squares. "Whatever you're about to say to him, say it like you've never heard of a government convoy in your life."

The man dismounted before he reached the fire, which was either courtesy or a way of showing them how comfortable he was on his feet. He looped the mule's lead over a dead branch without asking, and the mule, sensing an ally in its suffering, stood still for the first time all morning.

"Miss Thornbury." His voice was mild, almost apologetic, the voice of a man about to deliver bad news to a patient's family. "My name is Renn. Mr. Vane sends his regards."

"Mr. Vane has my regards as well," Rosalind said. "Along with my invoice."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile; more the memory of one, kept pressed flat like a flower in a book. "May I sit?"

"It's your expedition's money that bought the ground we're sitting on."

He sat. He sat the way he did everything — economically, completely, no part of him left over for anything else. Up close he smelled of nothing at all, which struck Rosalind as stranger than any smell would have been. Everyone out here smelled of something: woodsmoke, sweat, the sour green of wet canvas, the medicinal bite of Callum's kit. Renn smelled of clean cloth and cold air, as if the world declined to leave a mark on him.

"I've come to look at the route," he said. "Vane wants assurance the salvage window holds. Thirty days of dry season is a promise the mountains don't always keep, and he's invested considerably." He produced a folded paper — the contract, her copy, the one with her signature at the bottom like a small wound. "I also carry amended terms. A completion bonus. Significant."

Wren had stopped pretending to mend the strap. Ada Quill had come out of her tent without seeming to cross the distance between, standing at the edge of the fire ring with her arms crossed and her father's map — their map, the map — invisible but present somewhere on her body the way a heartbeat is present. Nobody spoke. The fire cracked a stick and the sound went through the silence like a shot through a church.

"Terms don't amend themselves," Rosalind said. "What's the catch?"

"No catch. An addition. Mr. Vane asks that I accompany the party to the site."

"To the trucks."

"To the salvage." Renn's grey eyes rested on her with the gentle patience of a man watching water find its level. "You understand his position. The cargo is valuable. Government freight, sealed manifests, equipment worth recovering even damaged. He wants a trusted set of eyes on the recovery itself. Inventory control."

"Inventory control," Rosalind repeated.

"It sounds bureaucratic because it is. Bureaucracy is how money stays money." He unfolded the amendment and held it out. She didn't take it. After a moment he laid it on a flat stone by the fire, weighted it with a pebble, and withdrew his hand as though setting down something fragile. "There's no obligation to read it aloud. But I'd suggest reading it."

Ada moved first. She crossed the fire ring, picked up the paper, read it top to bottom with a speed that made Rosalind certain she'd missed nothing, and handed it to her without comment. Her face gave away less than Renn's did, which Rosalind would not have believed possible an hour ago.

The terms were simple. The bonus doubled if Renn verified the cargo manifest at the site. It tripled if the recovery proceeded under his direct supervision. And there, in clause four, written in the bland language of men who buried knives inside paragraphs: *all recovered materials remain the sole property of the Vane Syndicate, including documents, correspondence, and records of any nature.* Including documents. Including records. Of any nature.

She thought of the dying prospector's breath on her ear. *They'll tell you it's salvage. It isn't salvage.* She thought of Edmund Vane's smile across a desk in Fair Weather, the desk with the ledger on it, the ledger with her name in it eleven times.

"Your Mr. Vane," she said, "is very interested in paperwork for a man hiring people to pull truck parts out of a ravine."

"Paperwork is what makes wreckage worth something," Renn said. "Otherwise it's just scrap and sad stories."

* * *

They argued about it that evening in the medical tent, which had become by unspoken consent the only room in camp where the walls were thin enough to hear trouble coming and thick enough to hide behind.

"We say no," Ada said. "We turn him back. We tell Vane his inspector spooked the mules."

"And lose the bonus, and insult Vane, and have Renn ride back down the mountain knowing exactly what we wouldn't let him see," Rosalind said. "That's worse than letting him come."

"Is it?" Ada's voice stayed flat, but her knuckles were white where she gripped her own elbows. "Because my father died on that road, Miss Thornbury. Whatever's in those trucks, he spent his last years trying to get someone to look at it. If we bring Vane's own hound to the site—"

"We bring him," Callum said.

Both women turned. Callum sat on the supply crate he used for a stool, the rag finally abandoned, his big hands hanging between his knees. He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"You want to explain that?" Ada said.

"I want to explain that I've met Renn before. Two years ago, at a Syndicate clinic outside Harrow Bend. He came to audit the medicine stores." Callum's mouth twisted. "Audit. Three days he stayed. Talked to every nurse, every porter, asked each of us the same questions in different orders. Where were you on the night of the theft. Who else had keys. What did you see. He caught a porter stealing morphine not by searching anything but by noticing the man's boots were new." He rubbed his face. "He's not coming to verify a manifest. He's coming because Vane suspects something. Because somebody talked."

The tent went very quiet. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea deciding whether to come ashore.

"Somebody talked," Rosalind repeated slowly. "Meaning what? Meaning Vane heard rumors about the convoy?"

"Meaning Vane heard *something*. Why else send a man whose entire profession is finding out what people are hiding?" Callum looked up at her, and there was something in his face she'd never seen there before — fear, yes, but underneath it a strange, grim steadiness, like a man who'd been waiting years for a wave he'd finally decided to stand in front of. "Rosalind. When he audits us, he's going to ask questions. And I'm going to be standing there with two years of things I know sitting behind my teeth."

"What things?"

"That's the problem," Callum said. "I don't know yet which ones will hang us."

* * *

Renn proposed the test himself, which was the most unnerving thing about him.

They found him at dawn on the second day already awake, already packed, sitting on a stone at the camp's edge with the mule saddled and the grey light just beginning to show the ridgeline above them — the ridgeline they'd have to climb, the green wall beyond it, the pass hidden somewhere in the cloud. He'd made tea. He offered the pot around, and nobody took any, and this seemed to please him.

"There's a route question," he said. "Two ways up to the pass. The river gorge, fast but exposed — flash flood country, this time of year especially. Or the ridge trail, slower, treeline cover, but it adds three days and the map—" he paused, delicately, "—the map Miss Quill's father left specifies one of them. I'd like to know which."

"The map specifies the ridge," Ada said immediately.

"Does it." Renn turned to Rosalind. Not accusingly. Worse than accusingly — *interestedly*, the way a lens is interested in whatever falls under it. "And you agree with the map?"

"I agree with staying alive."

"Then we take the ridge." He rose, brushed off his coat, and swung up onto the mule, which groaned with the resignation of the long-suffering. At the edge of camp he reined in and looked back down at them, and for a moment the mildness fell away entirely, the way a mask doesn't fall so much as simply stop being worn, and what was underneath was neither cruel nor kind but simply *thorough*, a mind built like a trapline, set and patient and everywhere at once.

"One thing more," he said. "In Harrow Bend, two years ago, there was a medic. Big fellow, good hands, drank too much after a bad night with a burned family. Disappeared afterward. Some said shame. Some said the Syndicate paid his debts and bought his silence." His gaze touched Callum, rested there a moment, and moved on without hurry. "Funny thing, the highlands. Small country. Everyone eventually meets everyone."

He rode out ahead of them up the ridge trail, alone, unhurried, and Rosalind stood watching him go with the amendment contract burning a hole in her pocket and the taste of tea she hadn't drunk coating her tongue like ash.

"He knows," Wren said softly beside her. "About Callum. He's known since he rode in."

"Yes."

"So why hasn't he done anything?"

Rosalind watched the grey coat dwindle among the trees, and felt the answer settle over her like the first cold drops of a storm still hours away but already chosen.

"Because a trap isn't sprung when the animal steps in it," she said. "It's sprung when the animal's carrying something worth catching."

Behind them, far to the south, past the green wall, past the pass they couldn't yet see, thunder spoke once — a single low word in a language the mountains had been fluent in for centuries. Ada looked at the sky, then at the map case strapped against her ribs, then at Rosalind.

"Three days to the pass," she said. "We need to decide what Callum knows before Renn decides it for us."
