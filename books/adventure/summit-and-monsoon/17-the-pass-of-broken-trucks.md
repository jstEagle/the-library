## 17. The Pass of Broken Trucks

The rain had followed them for two days like a debt collector, patient and impersonal, and on the third morning it stopped all at once, the way a held breath is released. Rosalind woke to silence so complete she thought for a moment she had gone deaf. Then the mountain spoke: water ticking off every leaf in the forest, the groan of saturated earth somewhere below them shifting its weight, and far off, high up, a sound she could not at first name—a long metallic complaint, like a ship settling.

She lay in the wet canvas of her bivouac and listened to it until she understood.

Trucks. Many trucks. Metal remembering what had happened to it.

* * *

They climbed out of the river gorge as the mist burned off, and the forest changed around them the way a room changes when someone has died in it. The trees grew older here, taller, their trunks furred with moss to the height of a man, and between them the undergrowth thinned as if the soil itself were tired. Callum walked with his medical bag slung across his chest like a bandolier, and he kept glancing at the slope above them without seeming to know he was doing it.

"You feel that?" he asked finally.

"I hear it," Rosalind said.

"No. Before that. The quiet." He wiped rain from his face with the heel of his hand. "Ten years of field medicine teaches you one thing above all others. Places where bad things happened stay quiet afterward. Animals know. They don't come back for a long time."

Juno, ahead of them with the rope coil over her shoulder, snorted. "That's not science, that's ghost stories."

"It's both," Callum said mildly. "Most true things are."

Maren Quill said nothing. She had said very little since the river crossing, since the water had taken their fourth raft and nearly taken her with it, and since Rosalind had hauled her out by the collar of her jacket while the river tried its best to keep her. She climbed now with a fixed, furious economy, as though the mountain owed her something and she intended to collect it personally. Her mother's map was wrapped in oilcloth inside her shirt, against her skin, and twice that morning Rosalind had seen her touch it through the fabric—not checking it was there, Rosalind thought, but checking it was still hers.

The switchbacks steepened. The air went thin and green-smelling, cold enough now that their breath showed. And then the trees simply ended—not gradually, not at a treeline, but along a straight edge, as if drawn—and beyond that edge the mountainside opened into a long saddle of grey scree and shattered rock, and standing in it, half-swallowed by twenty years of landslide and lichen, was the convoy.

Rosalind had imagined it, in the way you imagine anything you've been paid to find: a shape, an outline, trucks arranged in some orderly ruin. What was actually spread before them was nothing so tidy. It was a battlefield that had been left where it fell.

There had been perhaps thirty vehicles. She counted eleven she could be sure of before she stopped counting. They were not lined up along the road—there was no road anymore, just a scar of graded earth running the length of the pass—but scattered, flung, some of them upside down, some of them folded around trees that had grown up through their engine blocks in two decades of indifference. The nearest truck sat upright still, its cab door open, a curtain of moss hanging from the steering wheel like a green veil over an empty chair. Farther up the slope, two vehicles had come to rest nose to nose, locked together like animals that had fought to mutual death. One of the flatbeds had gone over the drop on the far side and lay below them among the boulders, visible only as a rust-red suggestion, a wound in the stone.

And everywhere, everywhere, the metal sang. Wind moved through broken windows and torn panel seams and made each wreck its own instrument, and together they made the sound she'd heard at dawn—the whole pass humming its one low note, twenty years without stopping.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

"Well," Juno said at last, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted it to. She cleared her throat. "Well. That's a lot of trucks."

"That's a massacre," Callum said quietly.

"We don't know that," Rosalind said.

Callum looked at her. He didn't argue. That was the worst part of him sometimes, Rosalind thought—he never argued when he was right, he just looked at you with those tired clinical eyes and let you sit in your own sentence until it rotted.

Maren had walked forward, out onto the scree. She stood among the first of the wrecks with her head tipped back, reading them the way other people read weather. "Convoy formation," she said. Her voice was flat, professional, and Rosalind remembered suddenly that this woman had spent six years doing salvage assessment in coastal yards, that she knew wreckage the way priests knew scripture. "Lead vehicle there, gun mount on the roof—see the ring bolt? They were expecting trouble. Second and third are troop carriers, benches welded in. Then cargo. Then more escorts at the rear." She pointed without touching. "But look at the spacing. Look at it."

Rosalind looked. The trucks weren't evenly spaced the way a convoy moves. They were clustered, bunched, several of them overlapping tracks, as though they had been driving close together, too close, or as though—

"As though they stopped," Rosalind said slowly. "All at once. In the middle of the road."

"They stopped," Maren agreed. "And then everything came from the sides." She turned a slow circle. "No shell craters. No burn patterns consistent with ordnance. Scorch on these two—" she nodded at the locked pair, "—but localized. Fuel fires, after." She crouched, picked something from the gravel, turned it over. A rifle casing, green with age, so corroded its brass had gone the color of old teeth. Then another. Then a handful of them, scattered through the scree like grain.

"Small arms," Callum said. He had come up beside her without seeming to cross the distance. "A lot of small arms. From multiple positions." He stood, and his face had gone the particular grey he reserved for things he had seen before and hoped never to see again. "This wasn't an ambush that went wrong. This was an ambush that worked perfectly, and then someone spent a long time making sure."

"Making sure of what?" Juno asked.

Nobody answered her. The wind moved through the pass and the broken trucks hummed their note, and somewhere high above a bird crossed the grey sky and did not land.

* * *

They made camp at the edge of the treeline rather than among the wrecks—no one proposed it, but no one proposed otherwise either, and when Rosalind started pitching the tarps under the last of the trees, the others simply gravitated there, the way water finds the low ground. Only Maren worked out in the open, moving from truck to truck with her notebook, cataloguing, and Rosalind watched her a while before following.

The interior of the lead vehicle smelled of rust and wet rot and, beneath it, faintly, impossibly, of old paper. The seats were gone to foam and frame. But the dashboard was intact, and clipped to the dash was a clipboard, and on the clipboard, sealed in plastic that had yellowed but held, was a manifest.

"Maren."

Maren came. She read over Rosalind's shoulder, lips moving slightly. "Convoy K-7," she read. "Ministry of Interior escort detail. Origin redacted. Destination—" She stopped. Read it again. "Destination redacted too. Cargo listed as 'agricultural equipment, twelve units.'" She looked up. "Twelve units. There were four flatbeds."

"Maybe they were big units."

"Rosalind." Maren's voice was very even. "I've done salvage manifests my whole working life. Nobody lists agricultural equipment under Ministry of Interior escort with a gun truck front and rear. And nobody"—she tapped the page, at the bottom, where a signature line sat empty, the ink line waiting twenty years for a name that had never been written—"nobody leaves a manifest unsigned unless the person meant to sign it never got the chance."

Outside, Juno's voice floated in, bright and too loud, the way voices get in places like this. "Hey! Hey, you guys! There's stuff back here! Like—personal stuff!"

They found her at the rearmost troop carrier. She had the back doors open and was standing very still, which for Juno was its own kind of alarm. Inside, stacked with a care that had survived two decades of damp, were kit bags. Dozens of them. Names stenciled on the canvas flaps, faded but legible. Beside them, boots. Pairs of boots, aligned. And on the truck's inner wall, someone had nailed up a photograph—a squad, arms around shoulders, squinting into sun somewhere green and peaceful—and beneath it, in marker gone brown with age, someone had written *back in 40 days.*

"Forty days," Juno whispered. She touched the letters. "It's been—it's been way more than forty days."

"Yes," Callum said, behind her. Gently. "It has."

She turned around and her face was doing something complicated, seventeen years of armor failing all at once. "Where did they go? If their bags are here, where did they—"

"Juno." Rosalind took her shoulder and turned her away from the truck, toward the scree, toward the far slope, and let her look. Let her understand what the absence of forty men looked like when it was written in a mountainside. Juno looked for a long moment. Then she sat down right where she was, in the mud, and put her head between her knees, and Callum knelt beside her with his hand on her back and said nothing, because there was nothing medical to say.

* * *

Rosalind found Vane's foreman, Detch, at the camp's edge, cleaning his rifle with the methodical contentment of a man who liked his tools better than his company. Two of the hired porters sat nearby playing cards; the rest were scattered through the trees, and Rosalind noted, without letting her face change, that three of them had drifted closer to Detch since the morning. Since the wrecks. As if pulled by some gravity she couldn't see.

"Your boss will want a report," she said.

Detch didn't look up from the bolt assembly. "He'll want the cargo."

"The cargo's agricultural equipment. Twelve units. According to the manifest."

Now he looked up. Just briefly. Just long enough. "Then your job's easy, isn't it." He slid the bolt home with a click. "Find twelve units of agricultural equipment."

"Twenty-year-old equipment in a massacre site. You want me to dig up plows, Detch?"

"I want you," he said, standing, shouldering the rifle, smiling with exactly none of his face, "to do the job you were paid for, Ms. Thornbury, and not stand around making speeches about things that don't concern you. Mr. Vane is very interested in efficiency. He gets unhappy with inefficiency. And when Mr. Vane gets unhappy, people remember it for a long time." He walked past her, then paused. "Nice work finding the site, though. Genuinely. Everyone said you couldn't. Old Quill's map and all." His smile widened a fraction. "Shame about the map getting so water-damaged on the river crossing. Be careful with it."

He walked away. Rosalind stood in the dripping green light and felt the shape of the trap settle over her like the weather coming in. He knew about the map. He knew it had survived the river—or he believed it had, which amounted to the same thing. Which meant Vane had never intended for the map to survive at all. Which meant that once the cargo was found—or whatever Vane actually wanted found—the map, and probably its keeper, would become inefficient.

She went to find Maren.

* * *

Maren was at the far end of the pass, at the last wreck before the slope rose toward the notch in the ridge—the direction the map's final markings pointed, up and over, toward whatever lay beyond. This truck was different from the others. It was an office vehicle, a command unit, its side stenciled with a ministry crest that someone had already tried, unsuccessfully, to scratch out. Its rear doors hung open. And inside, unlike the troop carrier, there was nothing. No kit, no papers, no personal effects. The interior had been stripped with a thoroughness that was itself a kind of evidence.

Except.

Maren was crouched at the doors, and she didn't turn when Rosalind's boots crunched on the scree. "Under the floor plate," she said. "Someone pried it up and put something back down. Badly. See the screw heads—stripped, wrong size, two of them missing entirely." She glanced up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "My mother marked this truck on the map. Did you know that? Not the convoy. This truck. One small x, and the notation in the margin—I translated it last night, after everyone slept." She reached into her shirt and drew out the oilcloth packet, unwrapped it with hands that were not quite steady, and showed Rosalind the margin of the old map, where Ada Quill's handwriting marched in tight, urgent lines:

*The proof is in the floor of the word-carrier. Everything else is bodies.*

"'Word-carrier,'" Rosalind repeated.

"Command vehicle. Communications truck." Maren looked back at the open doors, at the stripped interior, at the badly replaced floor plate. "She knew exactly which truck. Twenty years ago, she knew. And she came back for it, or she tried to, or—" Maren stopped. Swallowed. "Or she spent her whole life not being able to get here, and she sent me instead, and she never told me why, and now I'm standing here and she's dead."

"Maren—"

"She wrote *everything else is bodies*, Rosalind." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, visibly, furiously. "My mother knew what this was. She knew, and she carried it alone for twenty years, and she died in a flophouse in the lowlands with strangers going through her pockets, and the only thing she left me was a map and a margin note." She pressed the backs of her hands against her eyes for one second—one second only—and then lowered them, and she was Quill's daughter again, granite under the grief. "Help me with the floor plate."

They worked in silence. The screws fought; the plate came up grudgingly, exhaling twenty years of trapped air, mineral and dark. Beneath it, wedged into the chassis cavity, wrapped in oilcloth older than Juno, was a metal document case, military issue, its latch fused with corrosion.

Maren lifted it out as if it were a sleeping animal. It was heavier than paper had any right to make it. She set it on the tailgate. Her thumb rested on the latch.

"Whatever's in there," Rosalind said quietly, "once we open it, we can't un-open it. And Detch is watching us right now—I can feel it—from the trees. And Vane is two days behind us with a radio. So before you turn that latch, you need to decide what you're going to do with what you find. Because there'll be no deciding after."

Maren looked at the case. At the pass full of humming metal and forty pairs of boots and a photograph promising return in forty days. Somewhere above them, thunder muttered—not the storm yet, only the storm rehearsing.

"My mother sent me here," she said. "Not Vane. Not the Syndicate. Me." And she broke the latch.

Inside, packed in wax-sealed bundles that had kept the damp out for two decades, were photographs. Dozens of them. And documents. And beneath the photographs, at the bottom of the case, a ledger in a clerk's careful hand, its columns ruled and numbered, recording—in entries that grew steadily less careful as the pages turned—exactly what the convoy had been carrying when it stopped in the middle of the road, and who had given the order, and whose signature, unmistakable, ambitious, still wet with power after all these years, appeared at the bottom of the final page.

Edmund Vane.

From the treeline, a branch cracked. Detch was no longer pretending to clean his rifle.
