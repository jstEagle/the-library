## 19. Ada's Last Camp

They found it on the third day of the ascent, in a fold of the mountain where the trail narrowed to a shelf of wet stone and the forest pressed close enough to breathe on them.

Rosalind saw it first — or rather, she felt it, the way a chaser feels a pressure drop before the sky admits anything. A wrongness in the green. The canopy ahead had been broken and regrown wrong, saplings crowding at angles no wind would have chosen, and beneath them, half-swallowed by moss and fern, stood the ribs of a tarpaulin shelter stretched over a frame of cut poles.

"Stop," she said, and the word went down the line like a stone dropped into still water.

Sera Quill pushed past her before anyone could speak again. She moved fast for a woman who had spent nine days climbing on rice and resentment, boots finding the shelf-edge without looking, and then she stopped as abruptly as if she'd hit glass.

"That's her tarp," she said. Her voice came out flat, stripped of everything but fact. "Olive drab, patched at the left corner with orange. She tore it on a fence wire outside Kessel's Yard the winter I was twelve. She never fixed it proper. Said a patch you could see was a patch doing honest work."

Nobody answered. There was nothing to answer with. The rain came down through the canopy in fat, slow drops that struck the leaves like fingers tapping on wood, patient, indifferent, counting.

* * *

They made camp thirty meters below it, because Rosalind would not let anyone sleep under another person's ghost, and because the shelf above was too narrow for four tents anyway. But the truth, which she did not say aloud, was simpler: she wanted to go through Ada Quill's last camp alone first, in the grey light before full dark, and decide what her crew should be allowed to know.

The camp told its story in the order of its abandonment.

The fire ring first — three courses of river stone, blackened, still holding the shape of a cookfire built by someone careful, someone who banked coals instead of wasting them. Beside it, a tin plate gone to rust-lace. A spoon. A tin of tea opened and finished, the lid tucked back on out of habit even though there was nothing left to protect. Rosalind crouched over the ring and touched the stones. Cold, obviously. Nine years cold, maybe ten. But the care in their placement said something about the hands that had set them, and those hands had belonged, she was increasingly certain, to the same woman who had drawn the map now riding oilcloth-wrapped against Rosalind's ribs.

The sleeping hollow next: a depression in the needle duff shaped like a body, long and narrow, edged with a border of flat stones someone had placed one at a time, an act of housekeeping so absurdly domestic this far into the green wall that Rosalind had to look away from it for a moment. Above the hollow, lashed to two trees, a line had once been strung — the bark showed the wear-grooves — for drying socks, or fish, or hope.

And then, wedged in the cleft of the largest tree, wrapped in waxed canvas and tied with wire, a box.

She sat with it on her knees for a long time before she opened it. The rain thickened around her, filling the forest with its white noise, and somewhere downslope Pip was arguing with Wren about stove fuel, their voices rising and falling like birds. The wire was stiff with oxidation. It took her knife and both thumbs and a small, involuntary sound of effort to twist it free.

Inside: a journal. A water-stained army surplus notebook, its cover soft as cloth from handling. A photograph, curled and sun-bleached. And a brass cartridge casing, .303, plugged at the mouth with a scrap of paper rolled tight as a wick.

Rosalind opened the journal to the first page and read by the failing light until her eyes ached, and then she read on anyway.

* * *

The handwriting changed three times across the pages, and each change marked a woman coming apart by degrees.

At the start it was confident, slanted right, the script of someone who wrote supply manifests and letters home without hesitation. Entries about weather. About game trails. About the price of diesel in the lowland towns and the idiocy of buyers who thought garnets grew on trees. Dates marched forward through a wet season and into a dry one. Sera appeared — *the girl writes twice a month whether I answer or not, which is more than I deserve and exactly what I raised her to do* — and Rosalind felt the words land in her chest like a dropped tool.

Then the dates jumped. Weeks at a time. The handwriting tightened.

*Convoy came through the pass two nights back. Not on any schedule I know. Nine trucks, tarped, running dark, no lights on the switchbacks, which is a way to kill yourself on that road unless you don't care about dying. I counted axles from the ridge. Counted again going down. Nine trucks in, and I'll swear on my mother's grave only six came back out the far side.*

*Went up yesterday to where I heard the engines stop. Found things I will not write down in a book that can be taken off my body. Suffice to say the government story in the papers — bandit ambush, all souls lost, trucks burned — is a story written by men who were not there. Some of what burned was burned after. Fuel does not burn in patterns like that. People do not burn in patterns like that either, and yet.*

*I took photographs. Old habit. Document everything, my father said, because memory is the first thing men buy.*

The third hand was barely a hand at all — letters crowded, drifting, the writing of someone working against pain or fever or fear, probably all three. The entries stopped being dated and started being numbered, as if days had become too slippery to trust with names.

*IV. They are looking for me now. Saw a boat on the lake with an engine too quiet for fishing. Men in the town ask questions dressed as friendliness. I have moved the box. If you are reading this, you are either my daughter, in which case I am sorry for everything, every single thing, or you are someone who bought my map, in which case you should know the map is true and the truth is worth more than the garnet seam I let you believe you were buying.*

*VII. Coughing blood today. High altitude, bad water, or the other thing. Does not matter which. What matters is getting this to the tree before my hands stop obeying. The cartridge holds the names. Not my names — theirs. The men who signed. I copied them from documents I should not describe. Three signatures. One of them belongs to a family whose name opens doors in every city in this country, and if I am right about which Vane signed it, then the massacre at Karst Pass was not a mistake that got covered up. It was a service that got rendered.*

Rosalind closed the journal.

She sat in the ruined camp of a dead woman, in the rain, in the middle of a mountain that did not care, and she understood with total clarity that she had not bought a treasure map. She had been handed a live grenade by a dying stranger, and Edmund Vane had paid her passage knowing exactly what it was.

* * *

She told them everything by the fire. All of it — the journal, the photograph of a younger Ada standing squint-eyed beside a truck she had no business standing beside, the cartridge casing she passed around the circle so each of them could hold the weight of three signatures they hadn't read yet.

Pip held it longest, turning it in thin fingers, sixteen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who had seen massacres before. "So the salvage job," Pip said finally. "The convoy wreck we're climbing toward. That's not cargo."

"It's evidence," Rosalind said. "Or it was. Nine years of monsoon may have eaten most of it. Paper doesn't survive up here. Metal does, sometimes. Bodies—" She stopped. Wren was looking at her with an expression Rosalind recognized: the medic's face, the one worn by people who had seen what remains when paperwork fails. "Bodies do, worse luck."

"And Vane sent us to fetch it," Wren said quietly, "so he could burn it properly this time. Along with, I'm guessing, the four of us."

"He needs the site located and confirmed first. Then he comes up with his own people and his own fire." Rosalind fed a branch to the flames and watched the rain hiss off it. "We're the survey crew for our own graves. That's the shape of it. I'd have seen it sooner if I weren't so busy drowning."

Sera had not spoken since the camp. She sat apart, on a root, the journal open on her knees though she wasn't reading it — she had read it twice already, Rosalind had watched her lips move. Now she looked up, and her face in the firelight was the face from the ridge road, the one that had joined this expedition for reasons she'd stated as debt and duty and something she refused to name.

"She knew," Sera said. "At the end. She knew what she was sending whoever found it into."

"Yes."

"Good." Sera closed the journal with a snap. "Then nobody gets to say she didn't warn them. Nobody gets to make her a fool posthumously. She was the sharpest person I ever met and everyone in every town called her mad, and she walked into these mountains alone with a camera and a grudge and she *documented*, and I will not sit at this fire and pretend that was madness." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, glaring around the circle as if daring anyone to offer comfort. "We go on. We find the pass. We take what's there. And then we decide — *we*, not him — what happens to it."

* * *

They slept badly, all four, in the way of people who have learned too much in one evening. The rain settled in for the night, steady and businesslike, drumming the tarps, filling the dark with sound.

Rosalind took second watch because she knew she wouldn't sleep regardless. She sat under the lean of a boulder with the journal in her lap and read it again by candle stub, slower now, mining it. Coordinates, half-legible, in the margins. Sketches of ridgelines. A note about a spring that ran bitter — *do not drink, selenium, learned the hard way* — which explained, she realized with a grim flicker, why Wren had been complaining of headaches since the river crossing.

Near midnight, movement below the camp.

Not an animal. Animals announced themselves; the forest taught you their grammar, the rustle-and-hold of deer, the blunt crash of boar. This was silence moving with intent. Rosalind killed the candle and went still, and let her ears do what twenty years of storm work had trained them to do: separate signal from noise.

There. Footsteps on wet stone, downslope, unhurried. One person. Pausing at intervals — pausing the way a man pauses when he's checking a bearing, or checking that he isn't followed.

A shape resolved against the faint grey of the rain-shine: broad-shouldered, tall, moving with a soldier's economy even under a civilian pack. It stopped at the edge of the firelight's reach, at the exact boundary where a cautious man chooses to be seen.

"Thornbury." The voice was low, roughened by the climb, and familiar in a way that made the hair rise along Rosalind's arms before her memory caught up to it. "Don't reach for anything. I've come alone, and I've come to talk, and whether that ends well depends entirely on what you do with your hands in the next five seconds."

The shape stepped forward into the light.

Callum Renn — Vane's expedition security chief, the quiet man with the military bearing who had checked their rifles at the trailhead and smiled exactly never — stood dripping at the edge of Ada Quill's last camp, hands visible and empty, rain streaming off his jaw.

"I know what's in the box," he said. "I've known for nine years. I was there when the orders were signed."
