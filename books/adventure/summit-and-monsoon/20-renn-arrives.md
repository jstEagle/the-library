## 20. Renn Arrives

The storm gave them an hour of mercy, and none of them trusted it.

Rosalind stood at the mouth of the wrecked convoy's shadow, watching the clouds to the south where they had begun to organize themselves into something deliberate, something with intent. The light under that cloud deck had gone the color of old pewter, and the air had a pressure to it she felt in her back teeth, in the scar tissue along her ribs, in every place the weather had ever marked her. Thirty years of chasing storms had taught her to read the sky the way Callum read a wound — not by what it showed but by what it refused to show.

Behind her, the pass of broken trucks lay open to the rain-softened morning. Six vehicles, maybe seven, their bodies bloomed with rust and green mold, their tires long since rotted to lace. The lead truck had gone off the road and down the slope, and it was down there, half-swallowed by the hillside, that they had found what Quill's map had promised: the sealed cases, the documents wrapped in oilcloth, the photographs that made Wren — the runaway, seventeen and sharp as broken glass — go quiet for the first time since Rosalind had known her.

The massacre at Karst Hollow. The names of the dead. The signatures of the men who had signed it away.

And among those names, in a clerk's careful hand: *E. Vane.*

"We should move," Callum said. He came up beside her, favoring his left leg the way he always did now, the old injury from his disgrace at St. Brenner's flaring in the damp. His medical bag hung off his shoulder like a habit he couldn't break. "If Vane knows what this convoy carried, he'll have sent someone. He won't trust the weather to do his work."

"He sent the weather first," Rosalind said.

"Then he's sending something worse second."

She didn't argue. That was the thing about Callum she'd come to rely on in the three weeks since she'd scraped him out of a lowland gambling den with a debt of his own and a license revoked for a mercy killing no court would call a crime: he didn't waste breath on fear. He just converted it into motion.

* * *

They made camp in the shell of the third truck, the one whose cargo bed still had its canvas frame standing, torn but serviceable. Ada built the fire small — she was good with fires, good with everything practical, and Rosalind had stopped being surprised by it somewhere around the river crossing. She was Quill's daughter in more than blood. She had the same way of touching a map, the same economy of movement, the same habit of looking at a horizon like it owed her money.

But she hadn't spoken much since Ada's last camp — since they'd found, tucked beneath the driver's seat of the lead truck, a second journal in her mother's handwriting, twenty years old, describing in flat, unflinching detail what the government survey team had seen at Karst Hollow before the official record was rewritten. Ada had read it twice by firelight and then folded it into her coat against her chest, and she had not taken it out since, and she had not said one word about it, and everyone in the crew understood that this silence was load-bearing. You did not lean on it. You built around it.

Wren sat sharpening her knife on a whetstone, the sound a small dry whisper against the wet hush of the mountains. She'd been sharpening it for an hour. It did not need sharpening.

"You think they're coming tonight?" she asked, not looking up.

"I think Edmund Vane doesn't spend money on salvage expeditions he intends to survive," Rosalind said. "I think when the payment stops feeling like payment and starts feeling like leverage, he sends Renn."

At the name, even the fire seemed to draw in on itself.

They all knew Renn by reputation if not by face. The Syndicate's collector, its closer, its quiet man. Rosalind had met him once, four years ago, across a desk in Fair Weather while Vane's clerks itemized her father's debts into a number she couldn't read without her hands shaking. Renn had sat in the corner the whole meeting and never spoken, and afterward she could not have described a single feature of him except his hands, which were very still, and his eyes, which were patient in the way water is patient with stone.

"He came to our district once," Wren said. The whetstone paused. "After the tannery fire. A man had been skimming from the Syndicate's protection payments. They found him in the canal. Renn was seen having tea with the harbormaster the same afternoon, calm as Sunday." She resumed sharpening. "He drinks tea while it happens. That's the story anyway."

"Stories do work," Callum said. "That's what makes them useful to men like him."

"And if the stories are true?"

Callum didn't answer that. Nobody did. The fire popped, and somewhere up-slope water dripped from a hundred leaves in the long slow rhythm of a forest wringing itself out.

* * *

It was Ada who heard them first.

She raised one hand — not a dramatic gesture, just fingers lifting slightly off her knee — and the camp went silent the way a held breath is silent. Rosalind tilted her head. Rain-drip, wind in the cedars, the groan of the wrecked trucks settling as their metal cooled. And underneath it, faint but getting less faint:

Engines. Two of them. Low gears, climbing.

"They're on the ridge road," Ada breathed. "Still a mile out, maybe more. The switchbacks will slow them."

"How many men can't you hear?" Rosalind asked. "Bikes are loud. If he wanted quiet, he'd have come on foot, and we'd have heard nothing until it was too late."

"So he wants us to know," Wren said.

"He wants us to *decide*," Rosalind said. "There's a difference. Renn doesn't ambush. Ambush means paperwork afterward, questions, bodies to account for. He arrives openly and gives you a chance to be reasonable, because reasonable is cheaper than dead." She was already moving, kicking dirt over the fire, her mind running the pass the way she ran a storm cell — every feature catalogued, every exit weighed. "And if we're not reasonable, then the mountain does his work for him and he goes home dry."

The evidence — the oilcloth packets, the photographs, the ledger pages with Vane's signature — went into the bottom of Callum's medical bag, beneath the bandages and the splints, wrapped in a spare shirt. It looked like nothing. It weighed like a confession.

"The copies," Ada said.

"Wren has them?"

Wren patted the inner pocket of her jacket, where three photographs and two ledger pages rode flat against her ribs. "Nobody frisks a kid," she said. "Not even him."

"Especially not him," Callum murmured. "He'll want to seem decent."

* * *

They came around the final switchback as the light failed, and Rosalind watched them from the shadow of the second truck with her hands loose at her sides and her heart beating slow and cold.

Two motorcycles first, mud-flecked, ridden hard. Then a grey all-terrain truck with its lights off, moving on momentum alone, which told her the driver knew this road or didn't care whether the mountain kept him. Six men dismounted. Seven. Eight. They spread out with a drilled ease that made her stomach tighten — not thugs, then, or not only thugs. At least two of them moved like soldiers who'd been paid to forget they'd been soldiers.

And then Renn himself stepped out of the truck's rear seat, and Rosalind finally put a face to the patience.

He was smaller than she'd expected. That was the first obscenity of him. Grey-haired, neat, wearing a waxed coat that had never once been rained on hard enough to matter, carrying nothing in his hands. He looked like a schoolmaster at the end of a long career, like a man who judged flower shows. Only his eyes moved the way she remembered — slowly, thoroughly, taking in the trucks, the slopes, the cold ashes of their fire, the four figures emerging from cover because concealment had already stopped meaning anything.

"Miss Thornbury." His voice was mild, almost warm, pitched to carry without effort. "You've cost me a very uncomfortable drive."

"You could have waited for the road to dry out," Rosalind said.

"In my experience, Miss Thornbury, roads in these highlands are never dry. One simply chooses one's discomforts." He clasped his hands behind his back and surveyed the convoy wreckage with the air of a man inspecting property. "Edmund asked me to convey his regards. He's been following your progress with great interest. The river crossing especially. He said you handled it with real skill."

"Tell him I charge extra for skill."

Something flickered at the corner of Renn's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a smile had been considered and found unnecessary. "He also asked me to tell you that the expedition's terms have changed. The original contract provided for payment upon recovery of salvage of commercial value. What you've recovered instead—" he gestured gently at the medical bag, at the coat over Ada's chest, at everything, "—is not salvage. It's sediment. Old, ugly sediment that sensible people let stay buried. Edmund is prepared to be generous about the misunderstanding. Full payment. Double, in fact. Plus transport home for you and your people, debts cleared, ledgers clean. In exchange for the contents of that bag, and your sworn word that this valley keeps its mouth shut."

"And if we're not sensible?"

"Then I've been authorized to recover the material myself," Renn said, "and to ensure that no witnesses complicate the historical record." He said it the way a man reads a shipping manifest. Then he took a step forward, and though his hands stayed behind his back, the eight men behind him shifted, and the shift had a grammar to it that Rosalind's body understood before her mind finished translating: positions closing, angles cutting, the truck behind them suddenly a wall instead of a shelter.

"But I'd rather not," Renn continued, in exactly the same tone. "Killing is expensive. Not in money — in *afterward*. Afterwards pile up. So consider, please. All of you. The girl walks away rich. The doctor gets his license problem made to disappear — Edmund owns people at the college, it would take a letter. Miss Quill—" and here his gaze settled on Ada, soft and horrible, "—gets to stop running from her mother's ghost. Everyone gets what they actually want. All it costs is the truth, which nobody has ever once been able to eat."

The rain started again, fine and needling, beading on Renn's immaculate shoulders.

"You have until first light," he said. "Sleep on it. People decide better after sleep. And Miss Thornbury—" he turned to go, then paused, and for one moment the schoolmaster fell away and she saw the thing underneath, the still water over the deep drop, "—your father owed us eleven thousand crowns when he died. Edmund forgave it as a courtesy. He can unforgive it just as easily, and there are nieces in Fair Weather who bear your family's name. First light."

He walked back to the truck. The engines woke, grumbled, idled. The eight men withdrew to the far side of the pass, making their own cold camp within sight of theirs, close enough to watch, far enough to talk.

* * *

They didn't talk, the four of them. Not at first.

They sat in the black gut of the third truck with the canvas flapping and the rain coming harder, and Rosalind felt the shape of the trap settle over them with the weight of the whole sky. Run north, they'd be followed down a mountain in the dark. Run south, deeper into the highlands, they'd starve above the tree line. Give up the evidence, and Karst Hollow stayed a closed grave with Vane's boot print on the lid — and they'd still never be safe, because men like Renn don't leave loose threads out of gratitude.

It was Wren who broke the silence, her voice thin in the dark.

"My brother used to say there's always a third door." She was staring at her knees. "He said rich men build two doors so you fight over which one to use, and while you're fighting, you never look for the third."

"There's no third door here," Callum said.

"There's always a third door."

And Ada spoke for the first time since the ridge road. Her voice was rough from disuse, steady from something older than disuse.

"The lead truck," she said. "The one that went over the edge. My mother wrote about it. There's a drainage tunnel under the pass — miners cut it sixty years ago, before the road. It comes out on the south face, below the scree, a full day's climb from anywhere Renn's trucks can follow." She pulled the journal from her coat at last, opened it to a page she had clearly memorized, and turned it toward the coals' faint glow. "She drew it herself. She drew everything herself. She just never got the chance to use any of it."

Rosalind looked at the faded ink lines, the confident hand, the little annotation in the margin — *in case they ever come for the truth, go down the mountain the way the water does* — and felt something in her chest turn over, grief and fury and a fierce, unreasonable hope all tangled together.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled along the ridge like a door being locked.

First light was five hours away. The third door was open, and it went straight down through the dark of the mountain — and Renn's men, Rosalind noticed as she rose, had already begun to move.
