## 21. The Long Way Down

The storm that had brought Renn Vane's men up the mountain did not follow them down.

It hung above the pass like a held breath, a bruise-colored ceiling pressing on the broken trucks, and Rosalind understood — with the animal certainty of someone who had spent her life reading sky — that it was not finished with them. It was waiting. Storms were patient in a way men were not; they could afford to be. They always got where they were going.

They went the other way.

Not back through the pass — that road was watched now, and would be watched until the Syndicate was satisfied that nothing and no one had come out of it alive. Instead they took the eastern face, the one Ada Quill's map marked with a hand-inked warning: *the long way down. take water. take time. take nothing for granted.* The old prospector had scrawled it in pencil gone soft with damp, and beneath it, almost as an afterthought, a small sketch of a switchback trail stitched down the mountainside like a seam on torn cloth.

"It's not a road," Maren said, studying the map by the grey light of first morning. "It's a suggestion."

"It's a chance," Rosalind said. "We haven't had many of those lately."

They broke camp in the dark before dawn, moving by feel and memory, because fires drew eyes and eyes drew bullets, and because none of them wanted to look too long at what the pass had left behind. The evidence — the ledgers, the photographs, the sealed orders with Vane's own signature — rode wrapped in oilcloth against Wren's chest, strapped there with the same care she'd once used for the things she stole. She hadn't let anyone else touch it since the pass. She slept sitting up with her arms crossed over it like a woman guarding a wound.

Dr. Iyer came last out of the trees, favoring his bad knee, and paused at the edge of the drop to look back up at the pass one final time. The broken trucks were black shapes against the blacker sky. Somewhere up there, under a tarp of stones and branches, lay what remained of Callum Reyes.

"He should have come with us," Iyer said quietly. It was not the first time he'd said it. Rosalind suspected it would not be the last.

"Callum made his choice," she said. "Same as we're making ours."

"And which is ours? The payment or the justice?"

Rosalind looked at him. In the pre-dawn grey his face was all hollows and stubble, ten years older than it had been thirty days ago, and she thought about the ledger she kept in her head — the debts, the interest accruing, the clean arithmetic of survival — and how little any of it seemed to mean anymore.

"Ask me when we're at the bottom," she said. "If we get to the bottom."

* * *

The long way down began as kindness and ended as cruelty, the way most mountains do.

For the first hour the trail was generous — a broad shelf of stone angling gently through pine forest still dripping from yesterday's rain, the air cold and resinous and so clean it hurt after the cordite stink of the pass. Birdsong. Actual birdsong, the first any of them had heard in days, some small unseen thing fluting from the mist below them as though the world above the treeline had never known violence at all.

Wren walked point. She'd appointed herself without discussion, and no one argued; the girl moved through country like water finding its level, reading the ground the way Rosalind read sky. Twice in that first hour she stopped them with a raised fist and pointed silently to fresh sign — a snapped branch, a boot print in soft earth — but both times the tracks were old, days old, going the wrong direction. Hunters, maybe. Or ghosts.

Ada Quill's daughter — Sefa, who had joined them at the river crossing and still spoke mostly in silences — walked beside Rosalind and said nothing for a long time. When she finally did speak, her voice was low, pitched only for Rosalind.

"She knew this trail."

"Your mother knew every trail on this mountain."

"No." Sefa shook her head. "This one. Specifically. She drew it from memory, not survey. Look at the spacing of the switchbacks — they're even, but they're *her* evenness. She walked this down herself, more than once." A pause. "She never told me why she was up here."

Rosalind didn't answer right away. Below them the forest opened onto the first real view of the descent: a green immensity falling away in folds and ridges toward a river valley lost in haze, the whole of it breathing mist like something alive. Somewhere down there was the lowlands. Somewhere beyond that, a journalist, a ferry, a chance. And somewhere behind and above, patient as weather, Renn Vane.

"Maybe," Rosalind said at last, "she was looking for the same thing we found."

Sefa's jaw tightened. "Then she died for nothing."

"No," Rosalind said. "She died first. That's different. What we do next decides whether it was for nothing."

* * *

By midmorning the kindness ran out.

The shelf narrowed, then gave way entirely to a staircase of broken rock where the trail became less a path than an argument between gravity and whoever had first dared to disagree with it. They descended in single file, hands on stone, boots testing each hold before trusting it. Loose shale sang under their feet — a thin, ringing sound that carried, and every time it started Rosalind's shoulders climbed toward her ears.

Iyer's knee was failing. He didn't complain, which was worse than complaining; he simply slowed, and slowed again, and began to lean on the mountain more than walk beside it. On the fourth switchback Rosalind dropped back beside him.

"How bad?"

"I've delivered babies in monsoon ditches with worse footing," he said. "I'll manage."

"That's not what I asked."

He was quiet for three steps. Four. "It's the old fracture," he admitted finally. "The one from the hospital. Cold and wet and climbing — it's the trifecta." He smiled, and it was the tired smile of a man who had spent years being brave in front of people who needed him to be. "Don't tell Wren. She'll try to carry me, and she will actually try, and we'll both die of embarrassment before we die of anything else."

"I won't tell her if you don't tell her I'm scared," Rosalind said.

"You're scared?"

"Terrified. Constantly. Since the day I signed Vane's paper." She looked down the mountain, at the haze swallowing the valley. "I just got good at walking anyway. That's all courage is, I think. Walking anyway."

Iyer studied her for a moment. "You know," he said, "when you hired me, I thought you were exactly what everyone said. Mercenary. Ledger-hearted. A woman who'd sell her grandmother's teeth if the rate was right."

"And now?"

"Now I think the ledger was armor." He tested his next foothold, winced, committed. "And somebody's been shooting holes in it."

* * *

They ate at noon in the lee of a granite shoulder, cold rice and dried fish and rainwater caught in a leaf-cup, and while they ate the weather turned.

Rosalind felt it before she saw it — a pressure change, a thickening, the light going strange and yellow-green the way it did when the atmosphere began to load itself like a weapon. She stood, walked to the edge of the ledge, and looked south.

There. Far off across the valley, but not far enough: a tower of cloud building over the highlands, white and gold at its crown and dark as iron at its root, growing as she watched. Not the storm that sat on the pass. A new one. A younger, hungrier thing, feeding on the same wet heat that fed everything in this country during monsoon season.

"How far?" Sefa asked, coming to stand beside her. The girl had learned to read Rosalind's silences the way Rosalind read sky.

"Twenty miles. Maybe twenty-five."

"Moving?"

"Toward us. Everything moves toward us this time of year." Rosalind chewed the inside of her cheek, doing the arithmetic — distance, speed, terrain, Iyer's knee, Wren's stubbornness, Sefa's silence, her own exhaustion worn down to the quick. "Six hours. Eight if we're lucky and it dawdles. Mountains make storms dawdle sometimes. They also make them mean."

"So we move."

"So we move."

Wren had already packed the rice. Of course she had. The girl appeared at Rosalind's elbow with the oilcloth bundle cinched tight and her chin set at the angle that meant argument was already lost. "I heard 'six hours.' I can hear fine."

"I know you can."

"Then stop talking about me like I'm not here." But there was no venom in it — just the raw edge of a kid who had learned that being underestimated was the most dangerous thing in any storm. She adjusted the straps across her chest. "Nobody's taking this off me. Just so we're clear. Not for rest, not for river crossings, not if I'm dying. Especially not if I'm dying."

"We're clear," Rosalind said.

"Good." Wren shouldered past her to the trailhead, then stopped, and for a moment the hardness flickered and something younger showed underneath. "The man in the pass. Callum. He carried it first, didn't he? Before us."

"Yes."

"Did it kill him?"

Rosalind thought about the tarp of stones. About the choice Callum had made at the fire, and the other choice he'd made at the end, the one that had bought them their head start with his life.

"No," she said. "What he chose killed him. What he carried is what keeps it from being nothing."

Wren nodded slowly, absorbing this the way she absorbed everything — completely, permanently, into whatever fierce architecture she was building inside herself. Then she turned and started down the mountain, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

The afternoon was the mountain testing them.

Rain came first — not the storm, just the mountain's ordinary malice, a thin cold drizzle that slicked the shale and turned the switchbacks into greased stairs. Then fog, rising from the valley in slow white tongues until the world shrank to twenty feet of trail and the sound of their own breathing. Then the trail itself betrayed them: a washout, a full arm's length of the path simply gone, slumped away downslope into a chute of loose stone that fell away into whiteness with no visible bottom.

They gathered at the edge like mourners.

"Around it?" Iyer asked.

"There's no around," Sefa said. She crouched, testing the broken edge, pulling at a root. "Above there's cliff. Below—" she gestured at the fog, eloquently.

"One at a time," Rosalind decided. "Wren first. She's lightest. Find the line and hold the rope for the others."

Wren was already uncoiling rope. She crossed the gap the way she did everything — fast, committed, terrifyingly casual — stepping along a spine of bedrock that the washout had exposed, arms out, weight low, and on the far side she drove a piton into a crack, rigged the anchor, and threw back a thumbs-up that managed to be both reassuring and insufferable.

Iyer went second, on the rope, jaw clenched, and made it with a sound halfway between a laugh and a prayer. Sefa went third, fluid and silent. Rosalind went last, because that was the rule she'd set and rules were the last furniture standing in the burned house of her authority.

Halfway across, the rock moved.

Just a little. A settling, a sigh, the mountain shifting its weight — but under her boot the spine of bedrock tilted a degree, two degrees, and gravel went whispering away into the fog below, and for one long suspended second Rosalind hung between the rope and the void with her heart hammering somewhere up around her teeth.

"Hold!" Wren's voice, sharp as a whistle. The rope snapped taut. "Weight back! ROSALIND. Weight *back!*"

She leaned back. The bedrock settled. The gravel stopped whispering. And then she was moving again, faster now, hands burning on the rope, and then the far side was under her feet and she was kneeling on solid stone, breathing like a landed fish, while four people pretended very hard not to have seen what they'd all seen.

"Well," Iyer said at last. "That's the closest call today. Statistically speaking, we're due several hours of peace."

"Never say statistics on a mountain," Sefa said. "Mountains are superstitious."

* * *

They came down out of the cloud at dusk, and the world reassembled itself around them piece by piece — first the sound of water, then the smell of it, then the valley floor itself: terraced slopes gone wild, bamboo crowding the trail, and below it all the river, swollen brown and muscular with the season's rain, running fast through a gorge whose walls rose sheer on either side.

At the bottom, where the trail met the valley, stood the ruins of a waystation — an old government relay post, roof fallen in, walls furred with moss, a flagpole leaning at a drunkard's angle with no flag. Ada Quill's map marked it with a single word: *shelter.*

"Camp here?" Wren asked.

Rosalind looked at the sky. The southern horizon had gone the color of a bruise, and the cloud tower she'd seen at noon was no longer a tower but a wall, wide as the world, and beneath it the air had that charged, waiting quality she knew better than her own heartbeat.

"We camp," she said. "Light, dry, quiet. We eat everything perishable. We sleep in shifts." She looked at each of them in turn — Iyer easing himself down against the wall with a grunt, Sefa already checking the sightlines, Wren standing guard over the bundle on her chest like a small fierce dragon on its hoard. "Because tomorrow we hit the lowlands, and the lowlands are where the Syndicate's net is thinnest and where it's also impossible to hide. Open ground. Checkpoints. Ferry landings with one road in and one road out. Every eye in the country will be looking for five people carrying a dead man's proof."

"And the storm?" Sefa asked.

Rosalind turned south again. The first distant thunder reached them then, a long low rumble rolling up the valley like something enormous clearing its throat, and the hair on her arms lifted — half from the static in the air, half from something older than reason.

"The storm," she said, "is going to arrive tonight or tomorrow, and it's going to be directly overhead when it does. I've chased cells like that my whole life. I know what they do. They don't go around. They don't negotiate." She looked back at the ruins, at her crew, at the fragile, impossible cargo they carried. "Everything we do between now and then, we do knowing the sky is about to fall. So rest. All of you. While the sky's still holding."

Thunder rolled again, closer.

And in the deepening dark, far down the valley where the river bent toward the lowlands, a light blinked twice — deliberate, rhythmic, unmistakable — and went dark.

Sefa saw it too. She said nothing. Neither did Rosalind.

But neither of them slept.
