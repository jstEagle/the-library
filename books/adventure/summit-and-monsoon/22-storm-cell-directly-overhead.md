## 22. Storm Cell Directly Overhead

The sky had been lying to them all morning.

Rosalind had learned long ago that a storm never announced itself honestly. It sent ahead of it a false calm, a greenish light, a heat that pressed down on the shoulders like a hand. The highlands above the pass had gone quiet in that particular way she distrusted most — no birds, no insects, not even wind, just the mountains holding their breath while something enormous assembled itself behind the western ridgeline.

She stood at the mouth of the wrecked convoy's cargo truck, the waterproof document case strapped tight across her chest, and watched the clouds come.

They did not drift. They *arrived* — a wall of them, boiling up over the saddle between two peaks, black at the base and bruise-purple above, lit from within by flickers of silent lightning like a lantern wrapped in smoke. The anvil top spread outward until it blotted half the sky, and beneath it the air began to move, a cold outflow rushing downslope through the pines, bending the grass flat, carrying the smell of ozone and wet stone.

"Mara!" she shouted over her shoulder. "Get away from the edge!"

The girl was standing on the shattered bed of the lead truck, hair whipping, staring up at the cloud face with an expression Rosalind recognized from mirrors — that storm-chaser's hunger, that terrible pull toward the beautiful and lethal thing. She'd seen it in herself at nineteen, standing on a levee in the delta country while a twister walked the far bank like a curious animal. It was the look of someone who believed the sky was speaking to them personally.

"Mara. *Now.*"

The girl came, reluctantly, sliding down off the rusted chassis. Behind her, Callum emerged from the second truck, his medic's bag over one shoulder, his face gray. He had spent the last hour photographing everything — the crates, the stenciled lot numbers, the ledgers they'd pried from the sealed strongbox — and his hands were shaking now, though not from fear. From what he'd read.

"You should see this," he said quietly, holding up a page. "Before the rain takes it."

"I've seen enough."

"No." His voice cracked. "You haven't. There are names, Roz. Villages. Dates. It's not just supplies that went missing. It's people who were supposed to receive them. Someone signed off on rerouting famine aid to—" He stopped, swallowed. "Vane's signature is on three of these pages."

Above them, the first thunder arrived — not a crack but a long rolling roar, like a mountain deciding to speak.

"We'll deal with Vane when we're not standing inside his weather," Rosalind said. "Move. Down to the tree line. Now."

* * *

They made the trees as the first fat drops came down, huge and slow and warm, striking the dust like small explosions. Then the temperature dropped ten degrees in a minute, the warm rain turned cold, and the sky simply opened.

It was not rain as Rosalind understood rain. It was a vertical ocean. The forest vanished. The trucks vanished. Sound became a single continuous roar, so total it flattened thought, and the world contracted to the few feet of visibility around each of them — Callum's pale coat ahead of her, Mara's hand locked in hers, the ground turning instantly to a river of red mud.

They found shelter where the mountain offered it: an overhang of granite beneath a leaning shelf of rock, deep enough for four bodies if four bodies didn't mind touching. Sparrow got there first, the runaway boy soaked to the bone, teeth chattering, and pulled Mara in after her. Callum came last, dragging the document case Rosalind thrust at him, and then they huddled together under the stone and listened to the world end.

Lightning hit somewhere close — close enough that the flash and the bang were the same instant, a white annihilation followed by a concussion Rosalind felt in her chest cavity. The smell of scorched pine drifted through the rain.

"It's directly overhead," she said. No one needed telling. "We don't move until it passes. Not one step. If anyone asks why, tell them about the lightning."

"Nobody's asking anything," Sparrow said. His voice was thin. "I counted eleven strikes since we sat down. In like two minutes."

"That's a cell in its mature phase. It'll burn itself out in twenty minutes, maybe thirty. Then we get the back edge, and after that—" She stopped.

"After that what?" Mara asked.

After that, Rosalind thought, we're on foot, forty miles from anywhere, carrying proof that could hang Edmund Vane, with a Syndicate tracker somewhere behind us and Vane himself somewhere ahead. But she said only: "After that we decide what kind of trouble we're in."

Callum laughed — a short, unhinged sound. "What kind do you think we're in?"

"The interesting kind."

* * *

The twenty minutes passed in fragments.

Sparrow talked, because Sparrow always talked when he was frightened, a low stream of words about the ferry towns and the card games and the year he'd lived rough before Rosalind caught him trying to pickpocket her kit in a Fair Weather market. Nobody told him to stop. The talking was a rope they were all holding.

Mara held the map — Ada's map, her mother's map, the thing that had started all of it — wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. She hadn't spoken much since the pass. Rosalind had watched her, over the past days, transform from a stranger with a grudge into something harder and stranger: a woman becoming her mother's daughter in real time, learning the terrain the way Ada must have learned it, alone, decades ago, with nothing but stubbornness and a pencil.

"She knew," Mara said suddenly, during a lull in the thunder. "My mother knew about this convoy. That's why she drew the route. That's why she kept the map hidden her whole life."

"You don't know that," Callum said gently.

"I know she never sold it. Everyone wanted it — the Syndicate offered her money twice, I heard my aunt say so. And she wouldn't. She said some maps aren't for sale, they're for *returning*. I used to think it was just her being strange." Mara's jaw tightened. "She was keeping it safe until someone could finish what the convoy couldn't."

Outside, the rain eased fractionally — from catastrophic to merely torrential — and through the gray curtain they saw the slope below the pass had become a series of waterfalls, the gullies running brown and violent, whole trees riding the flood.

"And now we're the ones carrying it," Sparrow said. "Which means we're the ones everyone wants dead."

"Cheerful," said Callum.

"He's not wrong," Rosalind said.

* * *

When the cell finally dragged itself eastward — the rain slackening to a steady drumming, the light lifting from that drowned green-gray to something merely gloomy — Rosalind climbed to the lip of the overhang and read the mountain.

It was worse than she'd feared. The track down from the pass was gone, erased, replaced by a braided scar of mud and rockfall. One of the wrecked trucks had shifted, groaning, its nose now hanging over the collapsed section of road. The gully they'd planned to descend was a whitewater chute. Somewhere below, she knew, the rivers would be rising fast — the crossing at Karst Hollow would be suicide by tonight.

But the storm had given as well as taken. Looking back up the trail toward the north, she saw their own tracks obliterated, their backtrail erased as thoroughly as if they'd never existed. Whatever Renn was following, he was following it blind.

"We can't go down the way we came," she announced, dropping back under the rock. "And we can't stay. This slope sheds water; another cell builds behind that one and we'll be under a debris flow by dark. So we take the ridge."

"The ridge?" Callum peered out at the sawtooth line of rock to the east, dripping in the mist. "That's not a route, that's a spine."

"It's a route if you've got good boots and bad judgment. It stays above the flood lines, it's too steep for horses, which means too steep for Renn's men, and it comes down at the Hollow from the north side, above the bridge." She looked at each of them in turn. "It'll take two days instead of one. We'll be wet, hungry, and miserable. Questions?"

"One," Mara said. "Why does Vane want us dead instead of just taking the case?"

Rosalind had been asking herself the same question since the pass, and the answer had been sitting in her stomach like a stone. "Because we've read it," she said. "A case can be stolen back. People who've memorized what's in it have to be buried. That's how the Syndicate has always worked — ask me how I know." She touched the strap across her chest. "So we don't memorize. We copy. Tonight, at camp, every page gets copied twice, in two different hands, and the copies travel separately from the original. Three fires to put out before they can smother one."

Callum nodded slowly. Sparrow said, "I can write small."

"You can write *tiny*, it's frankly alarming," Rosalind said, and for a moment, absurdly, they all almost smiled.

Then, from down-valley, faint but unmistakable beneath the retreating thunder, came a sound that took the smile from every face.

An engine. More than one. Grinding up the flooded track from the lowlands, gears laboring, coming toward the pass.

* * *

They moved.

Up, not down — onto the ridge spine while the light still held, four figures climbing through streaming fog with the drowned valley falling away on either side. Rosalind set the pace, testing each hold, and behind her came Mara with the surefootedness of someone raised on scree, then Sparrow, then Callum, who swore continuously and fluently and never once stopped moving.

Halfway up the first pitch, Rosalind looked back.

Far below, at the edge of visibility, headlights crawled along the ruined track toward the convoy site. Two vehicles. Maybe three. She watched them reach the wrecked trucks and stop, tiny points of light winking in the murk, and she imagined men in oilskins spreading out through the mud, finding the opened strongbox, finding the empty crates, understanding.

"They're here," she said.

"Can they see us?" Sparrow's voice came up from below, tight.

"Not in this fog. But they'll find our camp sign, and they'll know which way we went, and Renn doesn't need tracks when he's got a map of his own." She resumed climbing. "Faster, everyone. We want the far side of the first saddle before full dark."

The fog thickened as they gained height until it was less weather than substance, a white blindness they climbed through by touch. Rosalind navigated by feel and memory and the compass she kept in her breast pocket, calling holds back down the line: "Crack on your left. Loose flake, don't trust it. Big block, step high."

Once, crossing a stretch of bare slab slick with runoff, Mara slipped. Rosalind caught her wrist without thinking — a reflex older than thought — and for a moment the two of them hung there, the girl's boots skating on wet stone, the drop beside them vanishing downward into nothing at all. Then Mara found her footing, and they stood pressed together against the rock, breathing hard, the fog swirling around them like something alive.

"My mother trusted you," Mara said, close to her ear. "With a map worth killing for. Why?"

Rosalind considered the honest answers — that Ada had been dying, that dying people choose badly, that Rosalind had owed the Syndicate more than her life was worth — and discarded all of them.

"Because I was the only person she ever met who loved storms more than she feared debt," she said. "And she figured that meant I'd understand what the map was really for."

"What's it really for?"

"Finishing things." She released the girl's wrist. "Climb."

* * *

They made the saddle as the light failed, a notch of broken stone between two towers of granite, and there — mercifully, impossibly — they found the old shepherd's bothy Ada had marked on her map with a tiny inked star. A drystone hut, roof partially fallen, walls solid, floor dry. It might have been built yesterday or five hundred years ago; the mountains kept their own accounts.

Inside, they got a fire going from damp scrub and Callum's emergency tablets, small and smokeless, and while the others dried what could be dried, Rosalind laid out the documents on a flat stone and they began the copying.

It was grim work. By firelight, by the light of a single candle stub, Callum and Mara transcribed page after page — lot numbers, requisition orders, the routing manifests, and worst of all the marginalia in a staff officer's neat hand: dates and village names, quantities crossed out and revised downward, a bureaucratic arithmetic of deprivation that amounted, line by line, to a sentence of death written in tidy columns. Sparrow copied duplicates in his alarmingly tiny script. Rosalind read each page once aloud, committing what she could to the vault of memory, then passed it on.

Near midnight, Mara stopped writing.

"This one," she said. Her voice was very quiet. "This one has my mother's name in it."

Rosalind leaned over. The page was a supply ledger from nine years back. In the column of villages whose aid allotments had been slashed to nothing — the villages later listed, in other documents, as sites of "unrest" and "necessary security action" — was a name Rosalind knew from the map's margin notes. Ada's home valley. The place Ada Quill had walked out of as a young woman and never returned to.

Below it, in the officer's hand, a single annotation: *Quill, A. — emigrated. Monitor.*

"They knew she had the map," Mara whispered. "All those years. They were watching her. And she knew they were watching, and she still kept it, and she still—" The girl pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "She died in a ditch in Fair Weather selling trinkets, and these monsters wrote *monitor* next to her name like she was weather."

Outside, the wind rose. Through the gap in the fallen roof they could see the clouds to the west, lit from within again, another tower building on the horizon — the monsoon feeding itself night after night, an engine with no off switch.

"Second cell's forming," Rosalind said. "It'll be on us by dawn. Which means Renn's men are pinned at the pass tonight, same as we'd have been. We've bought ourselves six hours." She rolled the originals back into the case, strapped it on, and looked around the fire at the three faces lit orange in the dark — the disgraced medic, the runaway, the prospector's daughter — and felt something she hadn't felt in years, something she'd assumed had been repo'd along with everything else.

Not hope exactly. Purpose, maybe. Its poorer cousin, but it kept a body moving.

"Sleep in shifts," she said. "Two hours on watch. Wake me second."

She took the first watch herself, sitting in the broken doorway with the case against her chest and the compass open on her knee, watching lightning walk the western ridges like something searching for them specifically.

It was near the end of her shift, the eastern sky still hours from gray, when she heard it — carried up the wind from the valley below, faint and rhythmic and utterly impossible at this hour, at this altitude, in this weather.

Hoofbeats. Many of them. Coming up the ridge line from the south.

The direction of Karst Hollow. The direction of the bridge. The direction Vane would come from, if Vane had stopped waiting for Renn's report and decided to climb up and bury the truth personally.

Rosalind rose, case in hand, and woke the others with a hand on each shoulder.

"Get your boots on," she said. "He's here."
