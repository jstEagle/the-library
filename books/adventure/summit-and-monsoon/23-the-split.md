## 23. The Split

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, and what remained was worse than the rain — a silence so complete that Rosalind could hear her own pulse ticking in her ears, and beneath it, far below the ridge, the mutter of water finding its way through country that no longer wanted to hold it.

She stood at the mouth of the overhang with the evidence case strapped across her chest like a second heart. Inside it: the ledgers, the photographs, the radio transcripts, the manifest stamped with a Syndicate seal that Edmund Vane himself had pressed his thumb to eleven years ago. It weighed less than four kilos. It weighed more than the mountain.

Behind her, under the dripping lip of stone, the camp was not sleeping. Nobody had slept since the vote at the fire two nights ago, and nobody would sleep properly again until this was finished one way or another. She could hear Halloran repacking the medical kit with the deliberate, over-loud precision of a man who wanted to be heard thinking. She could hear Juno turning the pages of her paperback without reading them. And she could hear Maren Quill doing nothing at all, which was its own kind of noise.

"You're going to stand there until you fall off the edge," Maren said behind her.

"I'm deciding which way we go down."

"We decided."

"No," Rosalind said. "We voted. That isn't the same thing, and you know it."

The vote had been five to two. Take the low road, the fast road, the road to Karst Hollow and the ferry and the journalist who might or might not still be waiting in the marsh town. Get the evidence out. Damn the payment, damn the Syndicate, damn the consequences. Rosalind had voted for it herself. She had looked every one of them in the eye while she did it, and she had meant it.

But votes were made around fires, in the warm, with food in your belly and rain on the tarp singing you toward courage. Mornings were different. Mornings were where courage went to be audited.

* * *

They broke camp in grey half-light, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridgeline they had reached the place the map called the Yoke — a saddle between two shoulders of the mountain where the trail split around a granite spine as old as anything in the highlands. To the left, the path dropped steeply through bamboo and wet shale toward the river valley and Karst Hollow, a day and a half of hard walking if the weather held. To the right, the old survey track climbed along the flank of the ridge before descending the long switchbacks toward the pass where the convoy had died — where Ada's last camp still stood, where Renn's men would be watching, where the Syndicate's reach was longest.

Nobody said which way they were going. Everybody knew.

It was Callum who finally stopped walking.

He had been quiet since the vote — quieter than usual, and Callum's usual was already a kind of silence. He stood now at the fork with his pack straps twisted in his fists, a big man made small by the landscape, and when he spoke his voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a man reading terms from a contract.

"I'm taking the right-hand trail."

Rosalind turned. "Say that again."

"You heard me." He wouldn't look at her. He looked at the granite spine instead, at the water beading on it, at anything. "I've got a sister in Lowmarsh with two kids and a husband who drinks. I've got eleven years of wages owed to me by men who don't forgive, and I've seen what happens to people they do forgive. You all voted your conscience. Good for you. Genuinely." His jaw worked. "But my conscience has dependents."

"So you're going back to Vane." Halloran's voice cracked across the saddle like a whip. The medic had come up the trail fast, face flushed, hands still smelling of the antiseptic he'd used on Juno's blisters an hour ago. "You're going to walk back to the man who put a massacre in a hole and tell him where we are."

"I'm going to walk back down a mountain, is what I'm doing. What I tell Vane when I get there is my business."

"It'll be a map of our route. That's what it'll be."

Callum's head came around at last, and there was something raw underneath the flatness. "You think I don't know how this looks? You think I haven't been doing the arithmetic every night since the trucks?" He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I carried one of those crates, Rosalind. I helped dig. I'm as guilty as any man on this mountain, and guilt doesn't feed my sister's kids."

"No," Rosalind said quietly. "But neither does betrayal."

"That's not what this is."

"Then what is it?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And in the pause, Juno spoke.

The girl had been hanging back at the rear of the column the whole morning, hood up, seventeen years old and trying to look like she wasn't. Now she pushed the hood back and her voice came out steadier than Rosalind had ever heard it.

"My brother sold me to a labor broker for the price of a motorbike." Her eyes were dry. They had been dry since the night at the fire, since whatever had happened inside her during that vote had finished happening. "Everyone told me he loved me. Everyone told me family was the thing you could count on when nothing else held. And then the thing that was supposed to hold was the thing that sold me." She stepped forward, boots sliding a little on the wet shale, and pointed at the case against Rosalind's chest. "That's the only thing on this mountain that's ever told me the truth about anybody. I'm not letting it walk back down with him."

"Girl—" Callum started.

"Don't." One word, and it landed hard enough that the big man actually flinched. "You watched me set the flares at the river crossing. You taught me how to read water. For three weeks you were—" She stopped herself, swallowed. "You don't get to be both people, Callum. Not today."

* * *

The argument that followed lasted forty minutes and settled nothing, because arguments like that never settle anything; they only reveal the shape of what was already broken. Halloran threatened. Callum pleaded. Maren stood apart with her arms folded, saying almost nothing, watching the trail behind them with the restless attention of a woman who had grown up in this country and knew what silence on a mountainside cost.

It was Maren who ended it.

"Enough." She hadn't raised her voice. She never needed to. "We're standing in open ground having a shouting match about whether one of us is going to sell the rest of us, and every minute we stand here is a minute closer to Renn's scouts hearing us do it." She looked at Callum for a long moment, and something passed across her face that wasn't quite anger — something older and wearier, the look of a woman recognizing a familiar species of cowardice because she'd spent years feeding it in herself. "My father took Syndicate money twice. First time he told himself it was just a loan. Second time he told himself it was just information. By the third time he'd stopped telling himself anything at all, because the telling was the part that hurt, and men will give up anything except the hurting." She unslung her pack and set it down between them, deliberate as a border stone. "So here's how this goes. You take the right-hand trail, Callum. We won't stop you. But you go with nothing of ours — no rations past tomorrow, no flare gun, none of the medicine Halloran packed for you when you weren't looking. And you go knowing that everything you say to Vane, we've already assumed you'd say. Rosalind planned the descent expecting it. Ask her."

Every head turned to Rosalind.

It was true, and she let it be true. Two nights ago, after the vote, while the others slept, she had sat with the map by candle-stub and redrawn their route twice — once assuming loyalty, once assuming the leak. The second version was uglier. It traded speed for cover, hugged the treeline, gave up the ferry schedule and the direct approach to the marsh town in exchange for a chance — only a chance — of arriving ahead of whatever Callum's tongue set in motion.

"The right-hand trail reaches the pass by midafternoon," she said. "If Renn's already moved men up from the lowlands, that's where they'll be. If he hasn't, Callum's arrival will summon them within the hour." She met his eyes and found, to her surprise, that she felt no hatred — only a vast, tired pity, the kind you feel for a man drowning in water shallow enough to stand up in. "Walk fast, Callum. And when you get there — when you're standing in front of Renn with your story ready — ask yourself one question first. Ask yourself whether Edmund Vane has ever once paid a witness what he promised him. Ask yourself what happened to the last man who knew too much about the convoy."

"He was a stranger to Vane," Callum said hoarsely. "I'm crew."

"So was the convoy crew," said Maren.

* * *

He went.

They watched him go, all four of them, standing at the fork in the trail like mourners at a graveside that had no body in it. He climbed the right-hand path with his head down and his stride long, and he did not look back, and somehow that was the worst of it — that he couldn't afford even the decency of a glance, because a glance would have meant admitting what the glance would see.

Juno cried after all, then. Silently, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand as though the tears were a malfunction she intended to fix by force. Halloran put a hand on her shoulder and left it there, and said nothing, because there was nothing in his kit for that.

Rosalind turned to the left-hand trail.

It fell away beneath her feet in a staircase of roots and slick stone, plunging into bamboo so dense and green it seemed lit from within, and the air came up out of it cool and mineral and thick with the smell of crushed leaves and coming rain. Somewhere below, the river they'd crossed at rising water was still talking to itself, lower now, but not done. The monsoon had opened. This was only the space between its sentences.

They walked. Four of them now, strung out single file along the ledge, moving the way she'd drawn it on the map — hugging cover, trading hours for safety, trusting arithmetic over hope. The case rode against her chest and she could feel it there with every heartbeat, four kilos of paper that had already cost them Callum, that might yet cost them everything.

Near midday they halted in a hollow where a stream cut through the bamboo, and while Halloran doled out water and hard bread, Maren came and crouched beside Rosalind at the edge of the green.

"You knew," Maren said. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost gentle. "Before the vote, even. You knew he'd break."

"I hoped he wouldn't. There's a difference."

"Not much of one, out here." Maren turned her canteen in her hands. "There's something else you should know, since we're being honest with each other today. The right-hand trail doesn't just reach the pass. It passes Ada's last camp. My mother's things are still there — the tin box, the letters. Callum knows what's in it because I told him, back when I trusted him, because I thought—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again. "If Vane's men find that box before we do, they'll burn it. And there's a page in it, Rosalind. A page with names on it. Names of people in the lowland villages who tried to testify, eleven years ago, and what happened to each of them. It's corroboration. It's the difference between our word and proof."

Rosalind closed her eyes. The arithmetic redrew itself, ugly and instant. Every hour they spent taking the safe road was an hour the box burned hotter in its little tin grave.

"How far is it from the pass?"

"Half a day's climb above it. Off the trail. Hard ground."

"And Renn's men will be at the pass by afternoon."

"Yes."

Rosalind opened her eyes and looked down the left-hand trail, down toward the ferry and the journalist and the clean, sensible plan. Then she looked back the way they'd come, up toward the smoke-colored heights where the wrecked trucks lay rusting in the grass and a dead woman's letters waited in a tin box for someone brave enough or stupid enough to come back for them.

Below them, faint but unmistakable, the river changed its note — deeper, faster, the sound of water that had begun to rise again. High up on the western ridges, clouds were assembling themselves out of clear air with the terrible patience of an army.

"Halloran," Rosalind called. "Juno. Pack it up."

Maren looked at her. "Which way?"

Rosalind shouldered the case higher against her chest and felt it answer with the small, stubborn weight of the truth.

"We're going back up," she said. "Both roads. Before the storm closes them forever."
