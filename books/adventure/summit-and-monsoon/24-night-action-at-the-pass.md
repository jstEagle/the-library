## 24. Night Action at the Pass

The storm came down the pass like something with intent.

Rosalind had watched a hundred storms arrive, had learned to read their moods the way sailors read water — the green-black bruising of cloud, the sudden hush before the first fat drops struck dust. But this was different. This was the monsoon's opening argument, delivered in full voice, and it fell upon the Pass of Broken Trucks as though the sky itself meant to erase what lay there.

She stood beneath the cantilevered wreck of the lead transport, rain hammering the overturned hull above her head in a rhythm like artillery. Inside, by the guttering light of a shielded lantern, Ada Quill's journal lay open across her knees, its pages soft as cloth from decades of damp. Beside her, Callum crouched over the sealed document case they'd pried from the convoy's strongbox that afternoon, his hands steady even now, even here.

"They'll come tonight," he said. Not a question. He'd been saying it since dusk, and each hour had only made him more certain.

"You don't know that."

"I know Vane." Callum's face was half shadow, half lantern-gold, the scar through his eyebrow pulling white when he frowned. "I signed his pay chits for six years, Roz. Men like him don't send letters when they can send weather. And he has weather."

Outside, lightning split the dark and showed them the pass entire: the strangled road wedged between cliff and gorge, the scattered skeletons of the convoy rusting where they'd died, and beyond, far below, the river gone brown and furious with the season's first flood. Somewhere up-slope, past the switchbacks, Renn's party was camped. Or moving. Or already descending.

That was the arithmetic Rosalind couldn't stop doing. Nine of them, counting herself. Four rifles among the lot, two of them older than the runaway. Against whatever Vane had hired, which would not be fewer than eight and would be better armed and better fed and untroubled by conscience.

"We could still run," she said, though she didn't believe it either. The descent was a day's climb in dry weather. In this, it was a coffin route.

"Run where?" Callum asked gently. "He owns the lowlands. He owns the ferry. He owns the marsh town's only journalist worth bribing." He closed the case. "No. We hold the pass. It's the one ground where numbers don't matter much — narrow frontage, bad footing, and every man who comes down that road has to do it in single file under our guns."

"And after?"

"After," Callum said, "we get the evidence off this mountain if we have to carry it in our teeth."

* * *

They made their preparations the way poor people always did: with ingenuity standing in for equipment.

Wren — the runaway, seventeen and all elbows and nerve — turned out to have a gift for improvisation that bordered on the unsettling. She rigged warning lines across the upper approach, fishing wire strung ankle-high between rusted axle-shafts, each hung with tin cups scavenged from the mess truck so that any man crossing them would announce himself in percussion. She called it her orchestra. She grinned while she said it, but her hands shook, and Rosalind pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice was sometimes the only kindness you could afford a frightened person.

Sefa, the disgraced medic, distributed what little remained of her kit: a vial of adrenaline, sutures, a bottle of laudanum measured out in careful doses. "Anyone shot," she said flatly, "you bring them to me fast. Cold slows the blood. Fear speeds it. So don't be cold and don't be afraid, and I'll pretend those are reasonable requests."

Mira Quill — Ada's daughter, estranged these fifteen years, found at last on the ridge road with her mother's map folded inside her coat — took the left flank alone. She'd insisted. She knew this country from childhood summers trailing her mother through it, and she moved through the wet dark without a lantern, without sound, a ghost returned to haunt her own inheritance.

Rosalind kept the center, at the mouth of the lead transport, where the road narrowed to barely four meters between wreck and gorge-edge. If anyone came, they came to her.

By midnight the rain had settled into a steady roar, and the world contracted to lantern-radius and heartbeat. Rosalind sat with her back against cold steel, rifle across her thighs, and let herself think about money for exactly one minute — the debt, the Syndicate's interest compounding like rot, the clean ledger she'd chased her whole adult life and never once caught. Then she put it away. There were things worth more than ledgers. She was only beginning to learn their names.

The orchestra sang at 2:14 in the morning.

* * *

It began as a single bright note — one tin cup ringing somewhere up the switchbacks — then another, and another, a cascading carillon of small betrayals echoing down the pass. Rosalind was on her feet before conscious thought caught up, killing the lantern, pressing into the transport's shadow.

"Count," Callum breathed beside her.

She counted. Seven distinct strikes, spaced along perhaps thirty meters of trail. Seven men, or fewer moving carelessly, or more moving carefully. In this rain, with this dark, there was no way to know which.

A voice floated down through the deluge, amplified by the rock walls, unhurried and almost courteous: "Miss Thornbury. Edmund Vane extends his compliments and a final offer. Walk out with the documents and your people keep their health. Stay, and we take them regardless. You have until the lightning counts twenty."

Lightning flashed. Rosalind counted. At nineteen, she raised her rifle and fired at the voice.

Her shot sparked off rock high and wide — she hadn't expected otherwise; the voice had been pitched to mislead — but it accomplished what she wanted. The night tore open. Muzzle flashes bloomed up-slope like a field of terrible flowers, a dozen or more, stitching the wreck with ricochets, and in that strobing light she saw them: figures advancing down the road in loose file, using the dead trucks for cover, professional and patient.

"Left flank!" she shouted, though Mira needed no telling. From the darkness above the road came three shots in quick succession, and two of the muzzle-flashes went out permanently. Men screamed. The file dissolved into cover.

What followed was not a battle so much as a long, vicious negotiation conducted in gunfire. The Syndicate men were good — disciplined, coordinated, calling positions to each other in clipped phrases — but the pass punished coordination. Every advance funneled them into Wren's orchestra, and every time the tin cups rang, Callum or Rosalind poured fire into the sound. Sefa dragged the wounded — theirs and the enemy's alike, because she was Sefa — behind the second truck, working by lightning-flash, her hands red to the wrist.

Twice the line nearly broke. Once, three attackers reached the transport itself, and Rosalind fought the last of them hand to hand against the hull, a wiry man with a knife who whispered apologies as he tried to open her throat — "Nothing personal, miss, it's wages, it's only wages" — until she drove her knee into him and sent him over the edge into the gorge, and heard the river take him, and did not sleep well afterward for a long time.

And once — the worst once — a grenade arced over the wreck and burst ten meters short, and the concussion lifted Rosalind and slammed her down and filled her ears with a single endless tone, and when the world reassembled itself, Wren was dragging her by the collar, shouting words Rosalind couldn't hear, her young face streaked with rain and blood that wasn't hers.

"How many?" Rosalind rasped, when sound crawled back.

"Four down that we know. Maybe six. They've pulled back up the switchbacks." Wren swallowed. "But Roz — there's more coming. Lights. Vehicle lights, way up top. A whole column."

* * *

Dawn did not so much arrive as leak, gray and grudging through the rain. The pass looked like a battlefield because it was one: spent brass glinting in the mud, dark stains diluting to pink, the wounded moaning under tarps. They had held. Barely. Two of theirs hurt — a broken collarbone, a graze — none dead. Sefa called it a miracle; Callum called it a reprieve.

Rosalind stood at the pass mouth, watching the distant lights descend through the rain, and understood with perfect clarity that holding had only bought them a morning.

Callum joined her, following her gaze. "Column's got maybe forty men and a mortar carrier," he said quietly. "We can't hold that. Nobody holds that."

"No," Rosalind agreed. Her eyes went to the document case, to Ada's journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and then down — down the gorge wall, where the flooded river thundered white and brown, and where, if Ada's old notes were true, an abandoned survey track clung to the cliffs for six desperate kilometers before meeting the lowland forest. "We don't hold. We go down the Long Way. All of us. Tonight, ahead of the column."

Callum stared at her. "That track hasn't been walked since the survey. In monsoon. With wounded."

"That's why they won't expect it." She turned back toward the others, toward the ragged crew who had followed her this far and deserved better than a grave in the pass. "Get everyone moving. We're leaving the trucks to the dead."

Behind them, the first shell of the morning whistled down and struck the lead transport where they'd slept, and the Pass of Broken Trucks began, at last, to come apart.
