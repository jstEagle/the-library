## 25. The Descent

They went down into the cloud the way drowned men go down into water — not by choice, and not all at once, but by degrees, each one colder than the last.

Rosalind took point with the map folded into her breast pocket and Callum's field dressing strapped tight over the gash on her forearm. Behind her came Mara Quill with the evidence case lashed to her back like a coffin lid, and behind Mara came Juno, sixteen years old and gray-lipped, one hand fisted in the rope between them because her legs had stopped entirely answering questions. Callum took the rear, because Callum always took the rear, because a medic's place in a column was wherever the bleeding was most likely to start.

The pass was behind them. What was left of the convoy was behind them. What Renn and his men had left of the night was behind them too, though none of them said his name aloud, as if the syllables might carry uphill on the wind and bring him down upon them like a hawk on a hare.

"Rope lengths," Rosalind said, not turning. Her voice had to be flat, or it would crack, and if it cracked they would all hear what was underneath it. "Check your rope lengths."

"Checked twice," Callum said.

"Check again."

"Rosalind."

"Check *again*, Callum."

She heard him sigh, heard the wet rasp of his hands on the line, heard Juno mutter something that was either a prayer or a complaint — with Juno it had never been possible to tell the difference — and then they were moving, four small animals descending a mountainside that had decided, sometime in the last hundred thousand years, that it did not particularly care for company.

The trail was not a trail. It was a suggestion. Ada Quill had drawn it on the map in faded pencil — *goat path, then scree, then the old survey cut, then the river* — and Ada Quill had been dead for eleven days, and Rosalind was beginning to suspect that the old woman had drawn the last stretch from memory of a memory, or from spite, or from some prospector's private joke played on whoever came after her. The goat path existed. The scree existed with a vengeance. The survey cut, when they finally found it, was a slot in the mountainside barely wider than a man's shoulders, choked with fern and the bones of a landslide, and it went down and down into a green dark that smelled of wet stone and crushed leaves and something older, something like the inside of the earth holding its breath.

The monsoon had not followed them over the pass. Not yet. That was the mercy, if you wanted to call it one, and Rosalind had stopped wanting to call things mercies somewhere around the third day. But the cloud had. It sat on the mountain's shoulders like a wet gray animal, and inside it the world shrank to thirty feet, then twenty, then ten, until each of them was alone with the rope and the rock and the sound of their own breathing, and the others existed only as tugs on a line and voices flattened by mist.

"Rosalind." Juno's voice, thin, from behind. "Rosalind, I can't — the rock's all — it moves when I—"

"Weight on your feet, not your hands. Hands are for balance, not for hanging."

"You said that already."

"I'll keep saying it until you do it."

A pause, and then the small scuffing sounds of a girl obeying, which was its own kind of victory. Rosalind had learned, over four days and one night of the worst country she had ever crossed, that you did not comfort Juno. You gave her instructions. Instructions were a handhold. Comfort was just another way of falling.

* * *

They stopped at midday — or what Rosalind judged to be midday, because the cloud made noon and dusk the same color — on a shelf of rock where a spring came weeping out of the cliff face and ran down the stone in a beard of moss. Water. They drank like animals, all four of them on their knees at the seep, and it was the best water any of them had ever tasted and none of them said so, because saying so would have meant admitting how bad the last two days had been.

Callum checked the arm. He did it without asking, the way he did everything now, his hands moving with the economy of a man who had lost the right to waste motion. He peeled the dressing back, looked, clicked his tongue.

"It's clean," he said. "You're lucky. Renn's man swung wide."

"He wasn't swinging wide. He was missing." Rosalind flexed her fingers. The cut ran from elbow to wrist, a long shallow kiss of the machete, and beneath the antiseptic sting it throbbed with her pulse like a second heart. "He was aiming for the case."

"Then you were lucky twice."

"Luck's not a plan, Callum."

"No," he said quietly, tying off the fresh dressing. "It's a currency. And you're spending it faster than anyone I've ever met."

Mara sat apart, as she always sat apart, with the case across her knees. She had not let it out of her reach since the pass. Not to sleep — she did not sleep — not to eat, not when Juno had slipped on the scree and gone down twenty feet on the rope and screamed the whole way and been hauled back up with her palms flayed red. Mara had watched all of it with the case between her boots. Rosalind understood. It was her father in there, in a way. Ada Quill's map had led them to the convoy, and the convoy had led them to the truth, and the truth was that eleven years ago a government column had rolled into a valley called Seremban Bend and come out the other side empty, and the people who had paid for the column and buried the report and shot the witnesses had names, and one of those names was Vane.

"Your father knew," Rosalind said to her, not unkindly. It was the first time either of them had said it aloud since the pass.

Mara did not look up. Her thumb moved over the case's buckle, back and forth, a small metronome. "He drove for them. One run, he said. One run, and the debt was paid, and we'd have the farm back." Her voice was very even, the way water is even right before it goes over the edge. "He didn't know what was in the trucks. Or he knew and didn't ask. I've spent eleven years deciding which, and I still don't know, and he's dead, so I never will."

"Does it change what we do with it?"

"No." She said it fast, and then slower, like she was testing the weight of it. "No. It doesn't. Whatever he was, the people in those trucks were people. And Vane paid to make them into nothing." She looked up then, and her eyes in the gray light were the exact color of the cloud, and Rosalind thought: *there it is. There's the thing that's going to get us all killed, or save us. Same thing. It's always the same thing.*

"Then we keep it dry," Rosalind said, "and we keep it moving, and we get it off this mountain before the weather or Renn decides otherwise."

Juno, wringing out her socks with the grim competence of someone who had been homeless long enough to have opinions about socks, snorted. "Renn's already decided. You saw him at the pass. He wasn't even angry. That's the worst part. He was just — working."

"He's a professional," Callum said.

"He's a *butcher* with a payroll," Juno said, and then, smaller, pulling the sock up over her ruined palms: "He looked at me. At the pass. When the flare went up. He looked right at me and I don't think he even saw a person. I think he saw a line item."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing true to say against it.

* * *

The survey cut ended in the afternoon in a wall of collapsed rock, and after that there was only the scree, and the scree was the mountain's true face: a mile of loose gray stone angled like a held breath, running from the cloud above them down into the river-valley fog below, and every step of it a negotiation with gravity that gravity intended to win.

They roped at fifty feet. They went down in the way you go down scree, which is to say half walking and half controlled falling, each stride releasing a hiss and slide of stone that echoed in the fog and made every one of them flinch, because sound carried, and somewhere above them or behind them or inside the sound itself there might be men with rifles listening.

Halfway down, the mountain moved.

It began as a sound — a deep, dry cough somewhere upslope — and then the fog above them turned to falling rock, and Rosalind had one heartbeat to make the oldest decision in the mountains, the one every guide makes once and never forgets: *rope or no rope.* The slide was coming down the line of the slope like a gray river, and it would carry them, and the rope tied them together, and tied together they would all go into it like beads on a string.

"Off rope!" she screamed. "Off rope — *now* —"

Callum's knife was out before she finished the word. She heard it — the single bright snick — and then the tension went out of the line and the world was rock and roar. She threw herself flat against the slope, arms over her head, and the slide went over her and around her and past her in a river of grinding stone, a hundred tons of the mountain changing its mind about where it wanted to be, and she felt a boulder the size of a coffin pass close enough to stir her hair, and then it was gone, downslope, diminishing, and the fog was full of dust and the ringing quiet that comes after.

"Sound off," she coughed. "Sound off. *Sound off*—"

"Mara."

"Juno." A sob in it. "Juno, I'm here, I'm — I let go, I'm sorry, I let go—"

"You did *right*," Rosalind said. "Callum."

A pause. Long enough to stop her heart.

"Here." His voice, downslope and to the left, ragged with dust. "Here. I'm — I'm in it up to the knee. I need a hand."

They dug him out together, all three of them working in the gray murk, and when they had him free he sat down on the scree and laughed, a short terrible bark of a laugh, and held up his boot, which was split from toe to ankle, and beneath it his foot, which was not.

"Not broken," he said. "Bruised to the bone. I can walk. I can't run."

"Nobody's running," Rosalind said. "We're done running. From here it's the river, the river's the road, and the road goes down."

"And Renn?" Mara said.

Rosalind looked back up the slope, at the scar the slide had cut through the fog, at the mountain that had just, for no reason it cared to explain, spared them.

"Renn's on the other side of that," she said. "For now. So we don't stop. Not for anything."

* * *

They reached the river at last light, if light was the word. The valley fog lay over the water like a second river, and the river itself ran high and brown and muscular with the monsoon's leavings, and somewhere down that brown throat, two days' travel, was the lowlands, and the marsh towns, and — if the map's last annotations were true, and Ada Quill's annotations had been true so far in the way that a knife is true — a ferry, and a road, and the world.

Callum found a fisherman's hut, half-collapsed, its owner gone to the lowlands or dead or never real. They got a fire going small and screened, because cold was a killer too and Juno had stopped shivering an hour ago, which was worse than shivering. They ate the last of the rice. They dried the case by the fire, turning it like a roasting bird, and Mara never took her eyes off it.

Rosalind sat at the doorway with her arm throbbing and her whole body a map of bruises, and looked down the valley into the fog, and let herself, for exactly one minute, think about the money. The payment. Thirty days' work, the job that would clear the ledger, that would take her name off the Vane Syndicate's books and put her back on the river with a clean hull and no one to run from. That money was ash now. She had known it since the pass, known it the way you know a bone is broken before the pain arrives. There was no version of this that ended with a payment. There was only the version that ended with the truth in the light, or the truth in the river, and her with it.

"Rosalind." Juno, beside her, knees up, chin on them. "What happens when we get down?"

"Boat. Then the marsh towns. Then a journalist."

"You know one?"

"I know *of* one. In Karst Hollow. A woman who prints things the Syndicate has paid twice to stop being printed." Rosalind turned the map over in her hands, and in the firelight the old prospector's pencil lines seemed to move, like something alive under the paper. "It's not much of a plan."

"It's more of a plan than I've ever had," Juno said. "Mostly my plans are just *away*."

Behind them, Callum was already asleep, sitting up, the way soldiers and the disgraced sleep. Mara was whispering to the case, or to her father through the case, or to no one.

And far up the valley, where the fog met the dark and the dark met the mountain, Rosalind heard it — so faint she could have made it herself, so wrong she knew she hadn't.

A whistle. Three notes, low, evenly spaced.

A surveyor's signal. The kind of thing a man uses to find his men in fog.

She put her hand over Juno's mouth before the girl could speak, and in the firelight she mouthed the only words that mattered:

*He's coming down.*
