## 26. The Bridge at Karst Hollow

The rain found them an hour before the bridge did.

It came down the valley in a grey curtain, soft at first, almost apologetic, and then all at once with the full weight of the monsoon behind it, as though the sky had been holding its breath since the pass and had finally decided to exhale. Rosalind stood at the head of the column with the map case strapped across her chest like a bandolier and watched the water come, and thought: *we are too late by exactly as much as we needed to be early.*

Behind her, the others picked their way down the switchbacks of the old logging road. Mara Quill went first among them, sure-footed as a goat, the canvas satchel of documents wrapped in oilcloth and lashed to her back. Then Silas Crane, moving with the careful economy of a man whose hands had once mattered more than his legs and who was still learning to trust the reverse. Juno came last, sixteen years old and furious about it, carrying the radio like it was a sleeping animal she'd been told not to wake.

They had left the pass four hours ago. Four hours, and the convoy wreck behind them still smoking in memory — the trucks split open like seed pods, the crates stamped with government seals, the photographs that Silas had held up to the grey light with hands that would not stay steady. Evidence of Karstvale. Evidence of what the Syndicate had helped bury. Enough paper, Rosalind hoped, to hang Edmund Vane twice over.

If it reached the lowlands. If it reached anyone.

The road dropped through stands of bamboo gone mad with rain, bending and thrashing, their leaves shedding water in sheets that hit the ground like small applause. Below them, somewhere under the green roar, Karst Hollow waited — a cleft in the highlands where the river ran white over drowned limestone, and where, if the map told the truth and the map had told the truth so far, a suspension bridge spanned the gorge on cables older than any of them.

"The map says the bridge was rated for two tonnes," Mara called forward, when the road bent enough that she could see Rosalind's back. "In 1961."

"The map also said there was a tea house at the pass," Rosalind said.

"There was a tea house at the pass."

"There was a *foundation* of a tea house at the pass."

Mara didn't answer, but Rosalind heard her laugh — a short, sharp sound, half relief, that she'd been hearing more of since the pass. Grief did that sometimes. It loosened people after it finished crushing them. Mara had buried whatever ceremony she could manage for her mother at the last camp above the tree line, in ground too hard for digging, and had come down the mountain quieter and somehow lighter, as if she'd set down something she'd carried so long she'd forgotten its weight.

The road steepened. The bamboo gave way to fern and slick red clay, and then the trees opened all at once and there it was.

Karst Hollow.

The gorge cut through the plateau like a wound, two hundred feet of grey-green air between walls of weathered limestone riddled with caves and arches, the stone dissolved into flutes and pinnacles by ten thousand years of water. The river at the bottom was unrecognizable from anything the map might have shown — no longer a river but a brown ocean moving sideways, whole trees riding it like dead men, the water climbing the rock walls in slow, muscular surges. The sound filled the hollow completely. It wasn't a noise you heard so much as a place you stood inside.

And across it, absurdly fragile, hung the bridge.

Two steel cables sagged from concrete anchorages on either rim, draped like a clothesline some giant had strung and forgotten. Wooden planks ran between wire cross-ties, most of them present, none of them trustworthy-looking. The whole structure swayed — not dramatically, not yet, but with a slow metronome sway that matched nothing, that belonged to itself, that said: *I have been alone out here a long time, and I do what I like.*

Juno came up beside Rosalind and stared at it with the flat expression of a teenager being shown a museum exhibit. "No," she said.

"It's the only crossing for forty miles."

"No," Juno said again, with feeling.

Silas set down his pack and looked at the bridge the way he looked at wounds — professionally, without flinching, cataloguing everything wrong. "Anchorages look intact," he said at last. "Cables are rusted but they're cable, not rope. It's the deck that'll kill us. And the wind." He glanced up at the sky, which had gone the color of wet slate, lower than it had been all day. "This storm isn't passing over us, Roz. It's settling in. That's a sitting cell. We cross now or we don't cross tonight."

"And if we don't cross tonight?"

"Then we spend the night on this side of the river with Vane's money in our pockets and Vane's men somewhere behind us, and we hope the river doesn't rise another meter, because if it does, this side becomes an island."

Rosalind looked at the water. It had already taken the old ferry landing on the far bank; she could see the roof of the boathouse just breaking the surface, current combing through its eaves. The river was rising even as she watched. She could measure it against a pale stripe of stone on the near wall — a foot of new water in the time it had taken them to walk down from the ridge.

"We cross now," she said.

* * *

They crossed the way you cross thin ice, one at a time, spread out so that no two weights ever shared the same span of deck.

Mara went first, because she insisted and because Rosalind couldn't find a reason that wasn't simply fear wearing her voice. The bridge took her weight with a groan that traveled the whole length of the cables, a deep bass note that rose through the rock and into their boots. She walked with her hands loose at her sides, eyes fixed on the far anchorage, never looking down, and the planks clattered and shifted under her feet like a floor of loose teeth. Halfway across, a gust came up the gorge — the storm arriving properly now, shouldering through the gap — and the whole structure leaned, lazily, horribly, ten degrees to the east, and Mara stopped walking and stood very still and let it lean beneath her, and then it swung back, and she walked on.

She reached the far side. She turned and raised one hand, and even at that distance Rosalind could see her face was bloodless.

Silas went second. He crossed badly — he admitted afterward that he'd crossed badly — freezing at the first lean, gripping the wire handrails until the rust printed his palms orange, making the last twenty feet on his knees. But he made it, and he stood on the far bank breathing like a man surfacing, and that was two of them safe, and the arithmetic of it made Rosalind's chest hurt in a way she refused to examine.

Juno went third, with the radio, and crossed better than any of them — light, quick, almost careless, the way young bodies are careless about things that terrify them, running the last few planks with a whoop that the gorge swallowed whole.

Which left Rosalind, the map, and the middle of the bridge.

She stepped onto the first plank and the world changed. There was no ground anymore. There was only the cables' long catenary curve, the wind coming up the hollow in gusts you could lean into like a wall, the river below — and she did look down, once, because she was who she was — the river below a churning brown chaos thirty fathoms of air beneath her boots, carrying a dead cow past the cave mouths with the unhurried confidence of something that knew it would carry everything eventually.

Halfway. The wind gusted. The bridge leaned.

And in the lean, in the terrible slow swing of it, Rosalind saw what the others hadn't seen from the bank, because the angle had been wrong and the rain had been wrong and the light had been wrong.

On the far side of the gorge, up on the ridge road where it climbed away from the anchorage toward the lowlands — dust. Not rain-dust. Engine dust. A vehicle, moving fast, coming down the switchbacks toward the bridge from the north.

She got off the bridge. Her legs were shaking so badly that Juno had to take her arm, and she let her, and together they pulled the map case and the satchel up off the anchorage platform and back from the edge, into the cover of a limestone overhang where the rain couldn't reach.

"How many planks missing?" Silas asked, already working — he'd been counting while he crawled, because of course he had. "If we have to cut the deck—"

"Silas." Rosalind pointed up the north road.

He looked. His face did something complicated and settled on grim.

"That's a Syndicate truck," Juno said. She'd produced binoculars from somewhere — the girl hoarded equipment like a magpie — and had them pressed to her eyes. "Canvas back. Two men visible. Maybe three." A pause. "It's Renn's truck. I know the dent in the fender. I've been sleeping next to that dent for three weeks."

The bottom dropped out of Rosalind's stomach, quietly, the way it does — no drama, just a sudden cold absence where certainty used to be.

Because Renn had been behind them. Renn had been behind them since the pass, since the night vote at the fire, since someone on their own crew had passed word along a radio frequency they'd never found. Behind them meant the south road, the way they'd come. Ahead meant—

"He didn't follow," Rosalind said slowly. "He *anticipated*. He knew the bridge was the only crossing. He's been driving around the gorge since before dawn." She looked at the sky, at the sitting storm, at the rising river. "He didn't need to chase us. He just needed to be standing at the only door."

* * *

The truck reached the far end of the ridge road and stopped, two hundred yards from the anchorage, engine idling, wipers going. Through the rain it was a dark shape with a suggestion of menace, the way shapes are. Nobody got out. That was worse than if they'd gotten out. It meant they were comfortable. It meant they were waiting for the people on the wrong side of the gorge to understand their situation fully.

"They can't cross either," Silas said. "Not with that many missing planks and not with us on this end. If we pull the anchor bolts on the north side—" He stopped. Looked at the anchorage. Looked at the river. "We'd strand ourselves too. River's up six inches since we crossed. In an hour it'll be up two feet. If we drop the bridge, we're on an island with a sitting storm and no way out, and so are they, and then it comes down to who's better armed."

"They're better armed," Juno said.

"Then we don't drop the bridge."

Mara had been silent through all of it, standing at the lip of the overhang with the rain streaming off the stone an inch from her face, watching the truck. When she spoke, her voice was quiet and level and it stopped all of them.

"My mother drew this map."

They turned.

"She walked this gorge forty years ago, when the bridge was new and the government still pretended the highlands were worth governing. She told me once — I was nine, I didn't listen, I never listened — she told me there were two ways across Karst Hollow." Mara turned from the rain. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "The bridge for carts and cattle. And the karst itself. The caves. The limestone under our feet is full of them — you can see the mouths from here. She mapped the dry ones. The ones that thread under the river and come up on the north side past the old quarry." She tapped the oilcloth satchel at her back. "It's in here. Page eleven. I dismissed it because I thought she was being romantic. My mother was never romantic. She was *thorough*."

Rosalind felt hope arrive in her chest like a bird landing — sudden, fragile, liable to startle. "A cave route. Under the river."

"If the levels hold. If the maps are right. If forty years haven't collapsed them." Mara's mouth twisted. "If, if, if. But it's a door they don't know exists, and the man in that truck knows every door we're supposed to use, because my mother's map is the reason he's here at all." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes, grief and anger braided tight. "He read it. Before he burned the copy. I watched him. He read every page except the ones he thought were a dead woman's sentiment."

Up the road, the truck's door opened. One figure descended — big, deliberate, unhurried, a man built like the anchorages themselves. Even through the rain and the distance, Rosalind knew the silhouette. Everyone who'd spent three weeks in Renn's orbit knew it.

He walked to the head of the bridge, alone, and stood there with his hands at his sides, and shouted across the gorge. The river ate most of it, but the shape of the words carried, and Juno, translating through cupped hands and guesswork, went pale.

"What's he saying?" Silas asked.

"He says—" Juno swallowed. "He says Vane wants the documents and the map, and only those. He says the rest of us can walk away south and no one will look for us." Another gust; the bridge swayed; Renn stood unmoved on the far anchorage like a figurehead. "He says he gives us until the river takes the bridge. After that, the offer's closed."

Silence, except for the water, which never stopped, which would never stop.

"He's lying," Juno said. "About letting us walk. Isn't he."

Nobody answered, which was an answer.

Rosalind looked at her crew — the disgraced medic wringing rain from his sleeves, the runaway clutching binoculars like a talisman, the prospector's daughter holding her mother's ghost in oilcloth — and felt the old arithmetic start up in her head, the ledger-brain, the debt-brain, the voice that had gotten her into Vane's office in the first place. *Give him the papers. Save your skin. Live to owe another day.*

She looked at the river instead. It had risen past the boathouse roof entirely now. Somewhere under that brown flood lay the lowlands, the marsh towns, the journalist whose name Silas had whispered like a password — and the only roads to it were the bridge, the flood, and page eleven of a dead woman's map.

"Mara," Rosalind said. "Show me page eleven."

Mara knelt and opened the satchel, shielding the pages with her body, and the rain hammered the limestone over their heads like applause, like warning, like a countdown — and up the north road, behind Renn's shoulder, headlights bloomed in the grey.

A second truck. And behind it, unmistakable even at that distance, the long black gleam of a car that cost more than all of their lives stacked together.

Edmund Vane had come to watch the door close himself.
