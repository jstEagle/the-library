## 27. What Callum Knew

The bridge at Karst Hollow was behind them, and none of them could stop looking at Callum.

They had made camp in the lee of a collapsed lime kiln, a half-moon of old stone that caught the wind and broke it into eddies. Rain came down in ropes. Rosalind had rigged the tarp with the last of their cord, and beneath it the four of them sat in a wet half-circle around a fire that fought for its life, while Callum sat apart with his wrists bound in front of him by his own medical tape — a detail Rosalind noted and did not comment on, because it said something about a man, the neatness of his own captivity.

He had not denied it. That was the thing she kept returning to. When she had put it to him on the bridge — *You told them where we'd cross* — he had not denied it, had not blustered or wept or reached for a lie. He had simply looked at the water going by under the broken span and said, "Yes."

Now the fire popped. Sefa-Joy sat closest to him, arms wrapped around her knees, watching him the way you watch a snake you've decided not to kill yet. Wren had positioned herself near the tarp's edge with a hand on her pack strap, ready to run, though there was nowhere left to run to. Ada Quill's daughter — Maren — stood at the perimeter with her rifle slung and her back to all of them, facing the rain, as if she could not bear to look at any of it.

Rosalind fed the fire a stick of kiln-wood and let the silence do its work. She had learned long ago, chasing storms across three continents, that the most dangerous weather was always the kind that hadn't broken yet. You waited for it. You gave it room.

"Talk," she said finally.

Callum lifted his head. He looked terrible — they all did — but there was a particular quality to his exhaustion, a settledness, like a man who has set down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its shape. "Where would you like me to start?"

"The beginning. The hospital in Corvane. The license you lost. All of it — because I want to know when Vane got his hooks in, and I want to know how deep."

He exhaled through his nose, almost a laugh. "You've been saving that question since the pass. I could see it sitting behind your teeth every time I stitched someone up. Every time I handed you the penicillin." He shifted, wincing — his shoulder was still wrong from the descent, and he'd refused to let anyone reduce it but himself, which was its own kind of confession. "All right. The beginning."

* * *

"I didn't lose my license for drinking," he said. "That's the story everyone tells, and I let them tell it, because the truth was worse and more boring. I lost it for reporting what I saw."

The fire cracked. Out past the kiln wall, the monsoon worked the ridgeline, a long gray animal worrying at the bones of the mountain.

"There was a mine collapse, eight years ago. Halvern Deep. Forty-one men underground when the shaft flooded. The company — and you can guess which company — reported eleven dead. Eleven. Because forty-one would have triggered the inquiry, and the inquiry would have found the drainage reports they'd falsified, and the falsified reports would have led back up the chain to men who owned newspapers and judges and, eventually, a salvage syndicate that laundered its money through mining concerns." His bound hands turned over in his lap, an odd helpless gesture. "I was the camp medic. I signed eleven death certificates. And then I kept a twelfth ledger, in my own hand, of every name the survivors gave me of men who hadn't come up. Thirty names. I sent it to the provincial registrar."

"And?" Rosalind said quietly.

"And the registrar's office burned six days later. With my ledger inside it. And two weeks after that, a man from the Medical Council arrived with a file on me thick as a Bible — debts I didn't owe, prescriptions I'd never written, a patient death in my first year of practice that had been ruled accidental twice already. They took the license. They didn't take the debt. The debt is how they keep people like me. You know how that works, Rosalind. You're living inside the same machine."

Sefa-Joy's voice came out low and flat. "So Vane owns your paper. Your paper bought your silence. And we're the cargo."

"You were never cargo," Callum said. "You were the reason the expedition existed. Think about it. Vane needed this route walked. He needed the convoy found — or rather, he needed to be certain of what was found, and certain of who found it, and certain that no one who found it ever came back down. A guide alone couldn't guarantee that. A crew with a man inside it could."

Wren's voice, thin and hard: "The note. At the leased land. The one under the stone."

"Yes." He didn't flinch. "I told them the crossing point at Karst Hollow. I told them your pace, your rations, that Maren had joined at Quill's cabin. I told them about the map — that it existed, that Rosalind kept it on her body, never in a pack."

"That's why they went for her at the river," Maren said, without turning around. Her voice carried strangely through the rain, stripped of everything. "Not to take the map. To mark her. To make her think she'd lost it, so she'd check it, so they'd know where it lived."

"They're thorough," Callum said. "Vane is thorough. It's his defining virtue. Men mistake him for greedy, but greed is impatient. Edmund Vane has never once been impatient."

* * *

Rosalind got up and walked to the edge of the tarp. Not away from him — she wanted him to see her face — but to where the light of the fire fell differently, harder.

"The massacre," she said. "The convoy. Tell me what you know. Not what you guessed. What you were told."

And here, at last, something moved behind his eyes. Something that had been waiting.

"I wasn't told anything," he said. "That's the point. I was told nothing, very carefully, by a man whose whole craft is telling people nothing. But I was there, Rosalind. Eight years ago. Before the license, before the ledger, before any of it — I did a rotation with the provincial medical service, out of Fort Merren. And in the spring of that year, a government convoy went up into these same highlands carrying — officially — survey equipment and a land adjudication team. Three trucks, an officer named Brandt commanding, and a civilian consultant attached from outside the ministry. A consultant whose expenses, I later learned, were paid by a private concern."

"Vane."

"Vane. There was a dispute over the highland leases. The valley communities below the pass had filed claims — ancestral tenure, water rights, the whole slow legal machinery that poor people use because it's the only weapon they're given. The adjudication team was supposed to rule on those claims. Instead—" He stopped. Started again. "Instead the convoy went up, and the claims were settled another way, and the official record says the convoy was lost in a storm slide with all hands. Eleven dead. Again eleven. They like eleven; it stays under the threshold."

Maren had turned now. She was looking at him across the fire with an expression Rosalind couldn't read — grief and fury braided too tight to separate. "My mother knew," she said. "Ada knew. That's why she had the map. That's why she hid."

"Your mother was the convoy's guide," Callum said gently. "Yes. I pieced that together years later. She was the only one who came down. Whatever happened up there, whatever she saw — she carried it out alone, and she spent the rest of her life making sure the route was written down somewhere no one could burn it. Not a ledger in a registrar's office. Stone and skin and memory. She was smarter than I was."

The rain intensified, hammering the kiln stones, and for a moment nobody spoke. Wren had gone pale. Sefa-Joy's jaw was set like something poured and cooled.

"So the evidence," Rosalind said. "In the wreck. What exactly are we carrying down this mountain?"

"Brandt kept a field journal — officers always do, even when ordered not to. And the consultant kept records of payments. Disbursements. If either survived the slide in any form, it's in the convoy's strongbox or the officer's effects. Your mother's map doesn't just mark the wreck, Rosalind. It marks which truck. She numbered them."

* * *

Later, when the others had taken what sleep the night allowed, Rosalind sat across the coals from Callum and asked the question she'd been holding since the bridge.

"Why tell us now? You could have stayed quiet. We'd have walked you down off this mountain, split the payment, and you'd have vanished into the lowlands with your debt paid and your skin intact. One more day of silence. Why break it?"

Callum looked into the fire for a long time. When he answered, his voice had changed — smaller, older, worn down to the grain.

"Because on the bridge, when they started shooting, Sefa-Joy put her body between Wren and the open water without thinking about it. And because Maren stood over her mother's grave this morning and didn't cry until she thought no one was watching. And because you checked my shoulder splint twice tonight even though you hate me right now, because that's who you are." He flexed his taped hands. "I have spent eight years being the man who keeps quiet. I watched thirty names go into a fire and I told myself survival was the price of someday doing better. Someday is a debt too, Rosalind. It compounds. And out there on the water today, watching them try to kill a nineteen-year-old girl, I understood that mine had come due."

Rosalind said nothing. The fire settled. Somewhere above them, thunder moved along the escarpment like furniture being dragged across a ceiling.

"One more thing," Callum said, as she rose to take her watch. "You should know. The men on the bridge weren't the end of it. Vane doesn't send one hand when he means to close a fist. There will be more between here and the lowlands — and they won't be trying to mark anyone this time."

She paused at the tarp's edge, rain streaming past her boots.

"How many?"

"I don't know. But I know where they'll be waiting." He met her eyes across the dying fire. "There's only one road down. The ferry at Karst Hollow isn't the crossing they'll guard — it's the marsh town beyond it. Every road, every river, every net out of these highlands passes through one place. And I know, because I'm the one who told them: they'll be holding it before dawn."

Rosalind stood in the rain a moment longer, letting the cold work its way through her, thinking of the map against her ribs, the journal in Maren's pack, the eleven names and the thirty names and the forty-one, all of it pressing downward toward the lowlands like the floodwater itself.

Then she turned and woke the others. They had a ferry to beat, and for the first time since Fair Weather, Rosalind Thornbury knew exactly what she was carrying — and exactly who was waiting to take it from her.
