## 28. The Lowlands and the Net

The highlands let them go grudgingly, the way a drowning man releases his grip on another—not all at once, but finger by finger, each ridge surrendering them lower and lower into air that thickened like broth. By the third morning after the pass, Rosalind could feel the change in her ears before she saw it in the land: a softening of pressure, a wet warmth that pressed against the eardrums like a palm laid flat over each side of her head. The monsoon did not end at the tree line. It simply changed its manner. Up in the broken country it had come at them sideways, screaming, armed with gravel and lightning. Down here it fell straight and patient, a gray curtain drawn from heaven to earth, drumming on leaves so broad that each drop landed with its own small note, millions of them, an orchestra tuning in the dark.

They came down out of the last foothills at dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath that ceiling of cloud—a bruised pearl light, sourceless, that made everything look as though it were remembered rather than seen. The trail widened. The mud changed character, from the gritty scree-paste of altitude to a deep red loam that sucked at their boots with a sound like kissing. And there were cattle tracks now, and cart ruts, and once—Rosalind stopped the whole column with a raised fist—the unmistakable print of a boot sole with a manufacturer's tread, fresh enough that water had not yet pooled in it.

Sela crouched over it for a long moment. She had hardly spoken since the pass. Since the trucks. Since what they'd found in the trucks, and what Callum had done afterward with the tarp and the shallow grave he'd insisted on digging in ground too wet to hold anything. Her hands, when she took them from her coat, were steady, but Rosalind had learned to read steadiness the way other people read faces. There was a kind that meant calm and a kind that meant a wire pulled too tight, and Sela's was the second kind.

"Somebody military," Sela said. "Or somebody who wants to look military."

"Same thing out here," said June.

The girl had come a long way from the sullen stowaway who'd hidden in Rosalind's truck bed outside Fair Weather with three days of stolen bread and a switchblade she didn't know how to hold. She moved differently now. She watched the treeline without being told to. She had stopped complaining about the rain somewhere around the river crossing, and Rosalind suspected she'd never fully start again. There were things the monsoon gave you if it didn't kill you, and one of them was a permanent recalibration of what counted as discomfort.

Callum brought up the rear, as he always did, and Rosalind hung back until he drew level with her. He looked terrible in the way that only healthy men can look terrible—hollow-eyed, unshaven, carrying weight that had nothing to do with his pack. He hadn't slept properly since the pass. None of them had, really, but Callum had been the one to open the crates, and Callum had been the one to understand fastest what they were looking at, because Callum had spent four years in government service learning exactly how documents like those were supposed to be filed, stamped, signed, and then made to disappear.

"You felt that?" Rosalind asked him quietly, nodding at the boot print.

"I've been feeling it since the ridge." He wiped rain off his face with the heel of his hand. It didn't help. Nothing helped; the rain simply reassembled. "We're being funneled, Roz. You see it yet?"

She had. That was the thing she'd been chewing on since before first light. The terrain itself had turned cooperative—too cooperative. For six days the descent had been a fight, every route a choice between cliff and washout, and now suddenly the land had opened like a hand offering them a path: a broad, gentle declivity running southeast, well-drained, mercifully free of deadfall, trending exactly the way they needed to go. Toward the lowlands. Toward Karst Hollow, and the bridge, and beyond it the marsh towns where a man might find a radio transmitter, a ferry, a journalist willing to listen.

"It's a net," she said. "Somebody's rolled us downhill into a funnel."

"Vane?"

"Vane doesn't know this country. His men don't either." She turned the thought over, testing it. "But Renn does."

At the name, Callum's jaw tightened. Renn—the informer, the man who'd walked among them for two weeks with a smile and a shared flask and then put a radio signal into the night sky that had nearly cost them everything at the pass. Renn, who was still out there somewhere behind them, or ahead of them, or both. Renn, who knew these highlands the way Rosalind knew storm cells: intimately, superstitiously, cell by cell.

"We should cut east," Callum said. "Cross the divide while we've got the legs for it."

"And walk into the flash-flood country with the rivers already up?" Rosalind shook her head. "No. East is where the water goes when it kills people. We stay on the spine, we move fast, and we get down to the lowlands before whatever's waiting gets organized. Speed is the only weapon we've got left."

Callum looked at her for a moment longer than the observation deserved. Then he nodded once and moved forward to tell the others, and Rosalind stood alone in the drumming rain for a few seconds, letting herself admit what she hadn't said aloud: that speed was also the only weapon *they* had, whoever they were, and that every hour since the pass had been an hour borrowed against a debt she couldn't name.

* * *

They made eleven miles before noon, which was obscene by the standards of the terrain, and paid for it by early afternoon when June went down hard on a stretch of slick clay and slid eight feet toward a drop-off before Sela caught her wrist. After that Rosalind called a halt under an overhang of black rock where a spring seeped out of the hillside, cold and faintly sulfuric, and they drank and ate cold rice and the last of the dried fish, and nobody spoke much.

It was June who broke the silence, in the way June broke silences—sideways, staring at nothing.

"Do you think anybody's even looking for us?" she said. "Like—back home. Do you think anyone noticed we're gone?"

Rosalind chewed her fish slowly. She had learned, over years of leading people through bad country, that questions like this were never about the answer. They were about whether someone would bother to give you one.

"The Syndicate's looking," she said. "That counts."

"That's not what I mean."

"No," Rosalind said. "It isn't." She passed the girl her own canteen, which was fuller. "But here's what I know. Six weeks ago none of us had anybody looking for us either. Not really. Now we've got four somebodies standing in the rain making sure the others eat. That's not nothing, June. In my experience it's more than most people ever get."

June looked at her with an expression that was trying very hard to be scornful and failing, because underneath it something had landed. Then Sela, unexpectedly, spoke from the shadows at the back of the overhang.

"My mother used to say the lowlands would be the easy part."

Her voice was flat, and everyone went still, because Sela had said almost nothing about her mother since Ada Quill's last camp, since the grave they'd found there, since the journal with its final entries in a hand gone shaky and hurried. Since the map, which had started all of it, and which Rosalind now carried sewn into the lining of her jacket in oilcloth, along with the copies of the convoy documents—or what remained of them after the fire at the pass. One set. One chance.

"She said the mountains try to kill you honest," Sela went on, "but the lowlands lie to you. Everything looks gentle. Everything looks finished. That's where people stop paying attention." She looked up, and her eyes in the gray light were her mother's eyes exactly. "She was talking about prospectors getting robbed in their sleep. But I don't think it stops there."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer it with. Rosalind got up, brushed the clay from her knees, and called the march back on.

* * *

They hit the first road at dusk.

Not a real road—the word barely applied—but a graded track, wide enough for a truck, running along the shoulder of the valley floor with drainage ditches on either side full of rushing brown water. Graded meant maintained. Maintained meant someone with money and authority considered this valley worth keeping open, and in the current state of the territory Rosalind could think of exactly one organization with money and authority to spare.

They followed it anyway, but off the surface, moving through the trees parallel to the track, visible to each other only as pale smudges in the failing light. The rain had settled into its evening register, steady and businesslike. Somewhere ahead, far off, a dog barked—the first sound of human habitation in nine days—and the sound went through the group like a current. Even Rosalind felt it, a pull in the chest, the animal longing for lit windows and roofs and other voices.

An hour past full dark, they smelled woodsmoke, and crept close enough to see it: a hamlet strung along the track, a dozen houses on stilts, a single generator-powered bulb burning yellow above what was probably a storehouse or a cantina. And parked beside it, mud to the windowsills, engine ticking as it cooled—

A vehicle. White. Unmarked except for the number stenciled on the door.

Rosalind lay in the wet ferns at the edge of the clearing and counted. Two men under the awning, smoking, sheltered from the rain. One more visible through a window, seated, head bent over something on a table. A fourth, probably, sleeping. The white vehicles were Syndicate—she'd seen the fleet at Vane's compound, at the beginning, back when she'd still believed this was a salvage job. The numbers meant logistics. Logistics meant coordination. Coordination meant the net wasn't a metaphor anymore.

She wormed backward until the trees closed around her, then found the others huddled at the base of a great fig tree whose roots rose from the ground like the buttresses of a cathedral.

"One vehicle, four men, holding position at the junction," she breathed. "They're not patrolling. They're sitting on the crossroads. Every path out of these hills comes down to the valley floor, and every path on the valley floor passes one of those junctions."

"They're not hunting us," Callum whispered. "They're sieving us."

"Yes." She hated how calm her voice sounded. Inside, something was pacing a cage. "Which means they know roughly where we'll emerge, and they can afford to wait. We're tired, we're low on food, and every day we delay, they add another vehicle."

"So we go around," June said. "There has to be another way."

"There is. Karst Hollow." Sela said it before Rosalind could, and there was something in her voice—an old bitterness, a family grievance—that made Rosalind turn to look at her. "The bridge. My mother talked about it. The only crossing over the Meridian gorge south of the falls. The main road uses it. Which means—" She stopped.

"Which means it's the one place they'll guard heaviest," Rosalind finished. "And the one place we can't avoid, unless we want to swim the Meridian in flood season."

The Meridian in flood season. Rosalind had seen it once, years ago, from the far bank—a mile of brown violence carrying whole trees like matchsticks, the roar of it audible before you ever saw the water. People did not swim the Meridian in flood season. People were occasionally found downstream of having tried.

Silence, except the rain, which filled silence the way it filled everything.

"There's another option," Callum said at last, very quietly. "Marsh towns are past the bridge, yes. But there's one before it. Ferrow's Landing—it's not on most maps, it's a fuel depot and a fishing camp, half-abandoned. If there's any transmitter in this whole valley outside the Syndicate's own network, it'll be there. Somebody's shortwave, a weather station, anything." He paused. "And there was a journalist working the marsh towns, before the crackdown. Name of Okafor. My old service contacts mentioned her. She was asking questions about the interior convoys months ago. Nobody would talk to her." His mouth twisted. "Because everybody was afraid. But we wouldn't have to talk. We'd just have to hand her the truth and let her do what journalists do."

"Assuming she's alive," Sela said.

"Assuming she's alive," Callum agreed. "And assuming Ferrow's Landing isn't already on their list. It's a gamble either way. Bridge or landing."

"It's not a gamble either way," Rosalind said. "It's a gamble both ways, and we only get to lose once." She looked around at their faces—June's young and sharp, Callum's exhausted and resolute, Sela's unreadable as deep water. "We rest two hours. Then we move past this junction wide, on the west side, and we make for Ferrow's Landing. If it's clean, we transmit. If it's not—" She shrugged. "Then we'll know the net's tighter than I think, and we'll deal with the bridge when we get there."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about the last ten days: argument had burned out of them like oxygen from a sealed room, and what remained was a strange, quiet unanimity that Rosalind found more frightening than mutiny. Tired people agreed with everything. Tired people followed anyone who seemed to know the way.

She took first watch anyway, sitting with her back against the fig roots, watching the distant yellow bulb burn above the Syndicate vehicle, and thinking about debts. She owed the Vane Syndicate more money than she could earn in ten lifetimes of storm chasing, and as of the pass, she owed them something else entirely: she was, in their ledger, no longer a debtor. She was evidence. And evidence got buried.

* * *

They skirted the junction in the smallest hours, moving in single file through a plantation of rubber trees so old and untended that the canopy had closed overhead into a dripping tunnel, the trunks scarred with spiral cuts long healed over, ghost-taps from some abandoned enterprise. The dark under the trees was total. They moved by touch and by the occasional lightning flicker far to the north, silent thunder rolling around the horizon like furniture dragged across a floor above them.

It was during one of those flickers that Rosalind saw the thing that stopped her heart.

Light, off to the east. Not lightning—wrong color, wrong rhythm. A white beam sweeping laterally across the valley floor, perhaps two miles distant, regular as a lighthouse. Vehicle-mounted. And then, as her eyes adjusted and another flicker came, she picked out the shape below it: not one vehicle but a line of them, parked nose to tail across the eastern approaches, headlights fanned out to paint the fields. A checkpoint. A proper one, with canvas shelters and the tiny orange points of cigarettes moving like fireflies.

The eastern side of the valley was closed.

Which meant the sieve had already tightened while they slept, and the western side—the side they were on—was the open mesh. For now. She stood in the dripping dark doing arithmetic she didn't like: distances, hours, the pace of exhausted bodies, the rate at which men in dry vehicles could redeploy. The margin was thin. The margin was the kind of thin that thinned further while you watched it.

She touched Sela's shoulder and pointed, and Sela looked, and Rosalind watched her understand it in real time—the slight collapse of her shoulders, the way her hand rose unconsciously to the jacket pocket where she carried her mother's journal.

"Ferrow's Landing is west," Sela breathed.

"For now."

They pushed harder after that. Nobody complained, which was its own kind of alarm. By the time the sky went from black to the color of wet slate, they had covered ground that should have taken twice the time, and the land was changing again around them—flatter, wetter, the red loam giving way to black muck threaded with channels of standing water that reflected the gray sky like spilled mercury. Palm stands replaced the hardwoods. Egrets stood in the shallows on absurd stilts, motionless as the dead. The air grew warmer and thicker and carried new smells: rot, fish, diesel, woodsmoke, the green vegetable stink of flooded ground.

Civilization, of a kind. The lowlands.

They found the first sign of Ferrow's Landing mid-morning: a rusted sign nailed to a palm, FERROW'S LANDING — FUEL & PROVISIONS, the letters shot through with corrosion, one corner hanging. Then a channel marker. Then, through the trees, the gleam of open water—a lagoon, wide and flat, dotted with fish traps—and along its near shore a scatter of buildings on pilings, a fuel dock, a crane lying on its side like a fallen animal.

Rosalind dropped flat at the treeline and glassed the settlement through the rain with binoculars gone soft with fungus. Boats at the dock, small ones. Smoke from two chimneys. Figures moving—one, two, three, four, five, loading crates onto a boat with the unhurried economy of people doing ordinary work.

Ordinary work. That was the problem. She couldn't tell. Fishermen loaded crates. So did Syndicate logistics crews. So did men staging a checkpoint, or men emptying a town before a sweep, or men doing any of a hundred things that had nothing to do with her and everything to do with her.

"What do you see?" Callum whispered.

"People," she said. "Too few to be a garrison. Too many to be abandoned." She lowered the glasses. "There's a building on the rise behind the dock—see it, with the antenna mast? Could be a comms shed. Could be a decoy."

"Could be a trap," Sela said.

"Yes," Rosalind said. "Could be."

They lay in the ferns and the black mud and the patient rain, four people at the ragged end of their strength, looking down at the last town between them and the truth. Behind them, somewhere in the hills, the net was drawing closed strand by strand. Ahead of them, in the smoke and the drizzle and the ordinary movements of ordinary-seeming people, lay either salvation or the mouth of it.

Rosalind was still weighing it—still turning over approach routes, fallback routes, the exact words she'd use walking into that settlement with her hands visible and her voice level—when June, beside her, went rigid and gripped her arm hard enough to bruise.

"Roz," the girl whispered. "The antenna. Look at the antenna."

Rosalind raised the glasses again. The mast on the rise was turning. Slowly, deliberately, in a full circle—sweeping the valley, the way a man sweeps a room with a lamp when he knows someone is hiding in it.

Someone at Ferrow's Landing was already looking for them.

And then, as she watched, the beam stopped. Held. Pointed—she checked it twice, heart slamming, telling herself she was wrong, checking a third time—directly at the treeline where they lay.
