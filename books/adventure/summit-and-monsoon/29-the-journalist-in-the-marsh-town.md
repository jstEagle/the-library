## 29. The Journalist in the Marsh Town

The lowlands announced themselves first by smell — a thick green reek of rot and standing water that rose through the truck's floorboards like something breathing beneath the road. Then by sound: frogs in their thousands, a wall of them, singing the marsh awake as the light failed. Rosalind had grown up two days' ride south of country like this, and her body remembered it before her mind did, the way the humidity settled on the skin like a damp hand, the way the air itself seemed to have weight.

They came down out of the hills in the last grey hour, the road unwinding from the broken country above into a landscape gone soft and drowned. Rice paddies stretched to either side, mirror-flat, holding the dying sky in sheets of tarnished silver. Water buffalo stood hip-deep in the flood, patient as stones. Somewhere behind them, above the tree line, the storm they had outrun still worked itself against the peaks, a dull flicker in the clouds like a lamp turned low behind a curtain.

"Fuel's low," Callum said from the driver's seat. His voice had been stripped down to its barest workings since the pass, since the bridge, since whatever it was he had carried in his head all these years and finally set down among them. He drove the way a man walks who has been forgiven something and does not trust the forgiveness — carefully, with his eyes always moving. "And I don't like the look of the road ahead."

Rosalind looked. The causeway ran out ahead of them across a drowned plain, and where it met the water there was a town — if you could call it that. A scatter of lights on stilts, low and yellow, reflected doubled in the black flood. Boats tied up along a spine of raised road. A church or a warehouse with a corrugated roof, impossible to tell. Smoke rising thin and blue from cook fires.

"Sallow's Reach," she said. "Quill's map had it marked. Last settlement before the ferry crossing."

"And the journalist?"

"Marsh towns are where people go to disappear," Rosalind said. "And where other people go to find them."

In the truck bed behind them, under a tarp that had seen better decades, sat the case. Ada's case. The one they had pried from the wrecked convoy at the cost of a man's life and Callum's last illusions — the sealed orders, the photographs, the ledger of names that proved the massacre at Karst Hollow had not been an accident of war but an arrangement, signed and countersigned, and that Edmund Vane's signature sat third from the top. Rosalind had not opened it since the pass. She found she could not look at it for long. It was not the horror of what was inside. It was the ordinariness. The neat handwriting. The way atrocity, committed on paper, looked like bookkeeping.

Wren slept against the tailgate, her cheek on her pack, one hand still curled around the pry bar she refused to put down. Sefa sat beside her, awake, watching the lights of the town with an expression Rosalind could not read and had stopped trying to. The medic had made her peace with something on that mountain, or declared war on it; either way she had gone quiet in a way that was worse than her old bitterness.

"How do we find one man in a place like that?" Sefa asked.

"We don't find him," Rosalind said. "We let him find us. His name is Ovid Reyes. He used to write for the *Coastal Standard* before the Syndicate's lawyers ate the paper alive. Quill said he'd gone to ground in the marsh towns, drinking, writing things nobody printed. Quill also said he owed her a debt she never called in." Rosalind looked out at the water. "I'm calling it in on her behalf. She'd have hated that. She'd also have done the same."

* * *

Sallow's Reach was built on the conviction that the water would take it one day and that there was no point hurrying the process. The houses stood on pilings black with age, leaning into one another like drunkards supporting each other home. The raised road — a causeway of packed earth and broken concrete — served as the town's single street, and along it the flood had deposited the usual inventory of marsh-town life: a fuel depot with a hand pump, a general store selling tinned meat and rope and mosquito coils, a temple with a tin roof gone to rust-orange, and, at the far end where the road gave out into boat landing, a tavern whose sign had been repainted so many times the words had become geology rather than language.

The rain caught them halfway in — not the mountain storm, nothing so violent, but the lowland rain that came down warm and steady and endless, the kind that did not fall so much as arrive, as though the air had simply decided to become water. They left Callum with the truck at the fuel depot, the tarp cinched down, the case beneath it, and a shotgun across his knees that he held the way a man holds a thing he has recently learned the true weight of.

"You're sure about this?" he asked Rosalind as she stepped down.

"No," she said. "But sure isn't on offer anymore. Watch the road. If anything comes down it that isn't us, you drive."

"Where?"

"Anywhere. Through the water if you have to. The case doesn't stop moving until it's in someone's hands who isn't paid to lose it."

The tavern was called, according to its sign, the *Halfway*, and it was exactly that — halfway between the water and the land, halfway between a business and a ruin. Inside, it smelled of kerosene, dried fish, and the sweet-sour breath of rice spirit. A dozen men sat along the walls under a ceiling fan that turned with the enthusiasm of a wounded bird. A radio played something mournful and far away. Every head turned when Rosalind came in with Sefa and Wren behind her, and every head turned back with the studied indifference of people who had learned that noticing things was a habit with a cost.

She found him at the end of the bar, or what remained of him.

Ovid Reyes had been a large man once — she could see the architecture of it in the shoulders, the big knotted hands wrapped around a glass of something pale. But the largeness had gone to seed, collapsed inward, so that he seemed to be a big man's ghost wearing a big man's shirt, buttoned wrong. He was maybe fifty. His hair had gone entirely grey, not gradually but all at once, the way hair does after a bad year. On the bar in front of him, squared carefully against the wood's edge, lay a notebook — an actual paper notebook, fat with clipped clippings and loose pages — and a pencil worn down to a stub.

He did not look up when she sat beside him. "We're closed," he said.

"The sign outside says otherwise."

"The sign outside is a liar. It's been lying since before you were born." He drank. Up close she could see the tremor in his hand, fine as a tuning fork, and the way he steadied the glass with his other palm — an old drunk's technique, practiced into invisibility. "You're not from the Reach. Nobody from the Reach has boots like that. Mountain boots. You've come down the Karst road."

Rosalind kept her voice level. "You knew Ada Quill."

The pencil stopped turning between his fingers. For a long moment the only sounds were the fan, the radio, the rain on the tin roof like static that never resolved. Then he laughed, once, without any humor in it at all, a sound like a branch breaking.

"Knew her. Yes. That's one word for what Ada Quill and I did to each other." He turned on his stool at last, and his eyes were bloodshot and clear, which was the worst combination — a man drunk enough to be honest and sober enough to mean it. "She's dead, isn't it. I've been waiting three years for someone to walk in here and tell me she's dead. I could hear it coming the way you hear a storm over the hills."

"She died in the spring," Rosalind said. "In a room over a chandlery, with a map in her hand and a debt on her name. She sold me that map. She told me where to find you."

"Of course she did." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Ada never let a debt die. Not because she was greedy. Because a debt was a thread, and as long as the thread ran between you and another person, you weren't alone in the world." His voice caught on the last of it, and he covered the catch with the glass. "What did the map show you?"

Rosalind weighed it. There was no version of this conversation that began with a lie and ended well; she had learned that much on the mountain, at the fire, in the vote they had taken with the storm coming down. "It showed a convoy," she said. "A government convoy that went into the Pass of Broken Trucks eleven years ago and never came out. It showed what the convoy was carrying. And it showed that the ambush wasn't an ambush. It was an erasure. Signed off at the top." She let that sit. "Vane's name is on the page, Reyes. Third from the top. Countersigned."

The glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

For a moment she thought he might simply come apart there on the bar stool, and she braced for it — the weeping, or the rage, or the terrible laughter of a man whose whole ruined life is suddenly, horribly vindicated. But Reyes did none of those things. He set the glass down with exaggerated care, the way a man handles explosives, and picked up his pencil, and turned to a fresh page in the fat notebook, and his hand — the tremor gone, or mastered, or forgotten — began to write.

"Names," he said. "Give me the names. Everyone who's seen the case. Everyone who knows what's in it. Dates, places, the route you took down. Start with the pass and work forward. Don't skip anything. Especially don't skip the things that seem unimportant."

"Why?"

"Because in eleven days the Syndicate's lawyers will stand up in a courtroom in the capital and say that the Karst Hollow file is a fabrication assembled by a disgraced storm chaser, a struck-off medic, and a dead prospector's ghost, and every one of those three will have a reason to lie, and I will have exactly one chance to prove they don't." He wrote as he talked, fast, the pencil hissing. "Proof isn't the document, Ms. — what is your name?"

"Thornbury. Rosalind Thornbury."

"Proof isn't the document. The document can be stolen, burned, discredited, or — and this is the one that keeps me up at night — *authenticated so carefully it becomes a forgery*. You'd be amazed how that works. Proof is the pattern. It's the truck part numbers that match the depot requisitions. It's the fuel logs. It's the fact that a certain bridge at Karst Hollow was rebuilt six weeks after the massacre by a contractor who was paid out of an account with Vane's fingerprints on it. I have spent three years and most of my liver collecting the pattern around the hole where the document should be. You've walked in carrying the hole." He looked up at her, and there was something in his face now that had not been there before — not hope, nothing so generous, but a kind of terrible readiness, the look of a man who has been holding his breath for eleven years and has just been told he may exhale. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? You haven't brought me a case. You've brought me a *story*. And I have been starving for one."

Wren spoke from the shadows by the door, where she and Sefa had drifted. "He's like Quill," she said. "She talked like that. Like everything was a thread."

"Everything *is* a thread," Reyes said, not unkindly. "Pull the right one and the whole shirt comes apart."

* * *

They worked through the night in the back room of the Halfway, at a table scarred by decades of elbows, under a lamp that drew moths in slow orbiting spirals. Reyes laid out his archive the way a surgeon lays out instruments — clippings, copies of shipping manifests, a photograph of a funeral where the wrong number of coffins had been carried, a letter from a widow in the lowlands who had never received a death notice and had never stopped asking why. Against each piece Rosalind set what the case held, and Reyes's pencil flew, and the pattern grew on the table between them like frost spreading across glass.

It was Sefa who found the flaw.

She had been quiet all evening, sorting photographs with a nurse's efficiency, and when she looked up her face had the grey set of someone about to deliver bad news. "The ledger," she said. "Page nine. The payment to the bridge contractor — it's dated the twelfth of the month. But the convoy went in on the ninth, and the pass was sealed for a week after. Landslide, official story." She tapped the page. "Nobody pays a contractor to rebuild a bridge before the landslide that closed it. Not unless the landslide was scheduled."

Reyes went very still. Then he began, very quietly, to laugh — not the broken laugh from the bar, but something else, something almost joyful, and it was worse to hear than the other. "Eleven years," he said. "Eleven years I've been drinking in this swamp, and the answer was a *date*. One date. A date I couldn't have because I didn't have the ledger." He pressed both palms flat on the table, over the papers, as if to keep them from blowing away in a wind only he could feel. "This goes to print the moment I can reach a wire office. There's a ferry at the head of the delta. Two days down the waterway, then the telegraph station at Corrigan's Landing. From there, this is in a dozen papers inside a week, and no lawyer on earth can unprint it."

"And Vane will know it's coming before the ink dries," Rosalind said. "He's known we were in the hills since the pass. He let us come down. Which means he wants something, and I've been trying all night to work out what."

"Maybe he wants the case back," Wren said.

"No." Rosalind shook her head slowly. The rain had thickened against the roof, and the lamp guttered, and in the stuttering light she saw it — saw it the way you see the shape of a storm cell on a radar screen, the whole rotation of it at once. "If he wanted the case, he'd have taken it on the mountain. He had men there. He had *Callum* there." She glanced at the door, where Callum's silhouette stood watch against the lamplit street, and lowered her voice. "He doesn't need the case. The case is paper. He needs us to *stop*. And there's only one way to stop us now, and it isn't bullets, because bullets make martyrs and martyrs make headlines. It's the ferry. It's making sure that when we get to Corrigan's Landing, the wire office is closed, the editor is bought, and the story arrives as a lie. He doesn't have to kill us. He just has to make sure nobody believes us." She looked at Reyes. "How do you reach the ferry?"

"The morning boat. Leaves from the landing at first light, down the delta channel. Two days if the water's kind."

"The water won't be kind," Rosalind said. "It's monsoon. And we've got a truck, a debt, and every reason to believe there's a man on that ferry already who's being paid to make sure we never reach Corrigan's Landing." She stood, and her knees ached, and the case on the table seemed suddenly very small for everything it held. "So we go tonight. We take the boat before the boat expects us, and we don't tell a soul in this town we're going."

Reyes was already gathering his notebook, his clippings, his pencil stub, moving with the sudden terrible efficiency of a man who has been waiting eleven years for a departure and refuses to be late for it. "The ferry at the head of the delta," he said, shrugging into a coat too big for the man he'd become. "You should know one thing before we step out that door, Ms. Thornbury. The ferryman who runs that crossing — Ferro, they call him — he used to run cargo for the Syndicate. Retired. Supposedly."

"Supposedly?"

"Supposedly." Reyes paused at the door, hand on the latch, and the lamplight caught the grey of his hair and the old, stubborn fire behind his eyes. "Which means either he's the safest man on the water, or he's the first one they bought. We'll know which when we're halfway across and it's too late to swim."

Outside, the rain came down in the dark like a verdict, and down at the landing, unseen in the black flood, the morning boat rocked at its mooring — and tied two berths down, a lantern burned that no fisherman would admit to lighting.
