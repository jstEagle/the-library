## 30. The Trap at the Ferry

The river at Marrow's Crossing ran the color of steeped tea, swollen with a week of highland rain, and the ferry that crossed it was less a boat than an argument between rot and rope. Rosalind saw it first from the top of the levee road, a flat wooden barge tethered to a cable strung bank to bank, and her stomach tightened around something cold before she could name why.

Too quiet.

Marsh towns were never quiet. They hummed — with generators, with argument, with the wet business of living beside water that wanted your land back. But Marrow's Crossing sat under its tin roofs like a held breath. A dog watched them from beneath a cart and did not bark. Smoke rose from exactly one chimney, thin and straight in the windless gray morning, as if someone had lit a fire for show and then stepped away from it.

"Rosalind." Callum's voice was low. He had stopped walking, his medical bag shifted to his front where he could drop it fast. He'd been doing that since Karst Hollow, since the bridge, since they learned what Callum knew and what it had cost him to say it aloud. "Look at the ferry."

She looked. The barge rode low at its moorings, gunwales nearly kissing the brown water, and there were men on it. Four of them, maybe five, arranged without any arrangement at all — leaning on the rail, crouched by the winch, one apparently asleep against the cable drum. Arranged the way soldiers arrange themselves when they want to look like ferrymen.

"They're waiting for us," Wren said. The girl had gone very still, her hand inside her jacket where she kept the knife Rosalind had given her and pretended not to know about. "This is the only crossing for forty miles. We told the journalist we'd take the ferry."

"We told him because it's true," Rosalind said. "There isn't another way across."

"There's swimming," Wren said.

"In that?" Callum nodded at the river. It moved with the slow muscular patience of something that had already drowned better swimmers than any of them. Debris turned in the current — a fence post, half a roof, a tree still wearing its leaves like a drowned man still wearing his coat. "You'd be carried into the delta before you made the far bank."

Sera Quill said nothing. She stood a little apart, as she always did now, her father's map case slung across her body on a strap she'd cut down and re-stitched herself. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth inside waxed canvas inside a second oilcloth, was the copy — the evidence, the photographs, the manifests, the testimony of what the convoy at the Pass of Broken Trucks had carried and what Edmund Vane's men had done to the people who found it first. The original had gone into the river at Karst Hollow along with Renn's second-in-command and a good deal of Rosalind's blood. The copy was the thing now. The copy was everything.

"The journalist," Sera said quietly. "Arun. He said he'd meet us at the crossing. He said he had a boat on the far side."

"He said a lot of things," Rosalind said. "He also looked at me like a man who'd already written half the story and needed us to finish it. That can go two ways."

"Which way is this?"

Rosalind didn't answer. She crouched instead at the lip of the levee, in the tall marsh grass that whispered and hissed around them, and studied the town the way she'd once studied storm cells — reading structure, reading rotation, looking for the place where the thing would turn.

Marrow's Crossing was three streets deep. A trading post with its shutters open. A chapel with its shutters closed. A row of houses facing the water, their stilts black with old flood lines. The ferry landing was a shelf of rotted planking jutting from the near bank, and above it, on poles, hung a bell — the kind you rang to summon the ferryman from the far side. Except the ferry wasn't on the far side. The ferry was here, moored, manned, patient.

And the bell rope had been cut.

"You don't cut the bell rope," Rosalind said, mostly to herself. "Not unless you don't want anyone summoning anything. Not unless the ferry is exactly where someone wants it."

* * *

They withdrew a mile back along the levee to a ruined pump house, its brick throat choked with ferns, and held a council that consisted mostly of Rosalind talking and the others letting her, which was how her councils always went and which she suspected they permitted out of pity as much as trust.

"Options," she said. She counted them off against her fingers, and her fingers were unsteady, and she hated that they noticed. "One: we walk upriver and try to find a crossing. That's two days through flooded ground with Vane's people behind us and probably ahead of us, carrying a wounded man—" she tipped her chin at Callum, whose arm was bound and splinted and who had refused, with a medic's stubbornness, to be described as wounded, "—and we arrive wherever we arrive exhausted, with no boat, no contact, and no story left but the truth, which nobody buys anymore."

"Two?" Wren said.

"Two: we take the ferry. We ring whatever's left of the bell, or we swim the cable, or we bluff our way aboard, and we cross under the guns of men who are being paid to make sure we don't reach the far bank."

"And three?"

"Three," Rosalind said, "we find out who those men are before we decide anything."

It was Sera who solved it, an hour later, by doing the thing she did best, which was disappearing. She went into the town alone, in a borrowed sarong and with mud worked into her hair, looking like a hundred other marsh girls hauling water and minding nothing, and she came back at dusk moving fast through the ferns with her eyes too bright.

"It's not Vane's men," she said. "Not directly. It's the Syndicate's local factor — a man called Ossler. He runs debt on this whole stretch of river. Everyone in town owes him. He put the word out yesterday: strangers coming down from the highlands, four of them, one woman leading. Anyone who helps them loses their land. Anyone who stops them gets their debt torn up and a bonus besides."

"Charming," Callum said. "Debt as a weapon. He learned from the best."

"There's more." Sera sat down on a fallen beam, and her voice dropped. "The ferryman. Old Tam. He's Ossler's cousin, but his son — Tam's son — died in the flood two years back, and everyone says it was because Ossler wouldn't release the levy money to raise the levee. Kept it. Lent it back out at interest. So Tam has been made to do this, and Tam hates it, and Tam has been drinking since dawn."

Rosalind felt something shift in her chest, the old familiar tilt, the compass needle swinging toward trouble. "Drinking men make mistakes."

"Or confessions," Sera said. "He told the girl who brought him his bottle — she's a friend of a friend — that he doesn't sleep anymore. That he sees his son every time the river rises." She paused. "He also said there are only four men on the ferry, and one of them is useless, and Ossler himself comes tomorrow at first light to watch them taken. He wants to see it done. He wants to tell Vane he did it personally."

Silence in the pump house. Outside, the marsh sounds resumed — frogs, wind, the river's endless swallowing — and Rosalind became aware that all three of them were looking at her, waiting, and that whatever she decided would become the shape of all their lives for the next day and possibly forever.

She thought about the ledger. She thought about the number that had started all of this, the debt she owed the Syndicate, the clean arithmetic that had seemed so simple in fair weather: do the job, get paid, survive. She thought about how many times since the Pass she had reached for that old arithmetic and found it wouldn't balance anymore, because some things, once entered, changed the value of every other line.

"Tomorrow at first light," she said. "Ossler comes to watch. So we give him a show."

* * *

They went down to the landing in the last dark before dawn, and the plan was a fragile thing, built of timing and nerve and the particular desperation of people with nothing left to lose but each other.

Callum went first, alone, limping visibly, hands raised, calling out in the flat reasonable voice of a man surrendering. "We're coming in! No weapons! We just want passage!" He let them see him — the bloodied bandage, the ruined arm, the shuffling gait — and four shapes detached themselves from the ferry's shadows to meet him, and Rosalind, watching from the reeds with Wren, counted them the way Callum had taught her to count pulses. One at the gangplank. Two flanking. One hanging back by the winch, the smart one, the dangerous one.

Then the lights went out.

That was Wren's work — the trading post generator, sabotaged in the night with a pocketknife and a handful of Sera's borrowed patience, and the whole town dropped into darkness except for the single lantern the ferrymen carried, which suddenly made them blind while the reeds stayed full of eyes.

What followed took ninety seconds and felt, to Rosalind, like a season. She came out of the water — she'd waded the shallows downstream and come up the pilings, soaked to the ribs, freezing, alive — and took the man at the gangplank from behind with the boat hook she'd pried loose from the landing, and he went down into the river with a sound like a dropped sack, and the current took his rifle before it took anything else. Wren dropped from the cable drum onto the shoulders of the second, all elbows and fury and fourteen years of running away from things finally running toward something. The third man froze, then ran, and the fourth — the smart one, the dangerous one — got his pistol up and leveled it at Callum.

And Tam, the ferryman, drunk and grieving and standing at his own winch with his dead son's name worn smooth on the locket around his neck, swung the cable brake lever with both hands and broke it across the man's skull.

The pistol went off into the sky. The birds went up off the marsh in a black sheet. And then it was over, and there were four Syndicate men disarmed and bound with their own tow-rope, and one broken lever, and one old man standing over them breathing like a bellows, and Rosalind Thornbury waist-deep in the reeds holding a boat hook and shaking so hard she could barely climb the pilings.

* * *

Ossler came at first light, as promised, in a longboat flying the Syndicate's gray pennant, with six armed retainers and a parasol, because even in the marsh country the Syndicate's factors traveled like minor royalty.

He came around the bend of the river at the exact moment Rosalind had calculated he would, and he found — this was the part she'd argued for at the fire, the part Wren had called mad and Sera had called necessary — the ferry sitting mid-river, loaded, moving, with Tam at the helm and the four bound guards visible on deck and Rosalind Thornbury standing at the rail in plain sight, unarmed, waiting.

Ossler's longboat slowed. Through the gray morning light Rosalind watched him read the scene: the ferry crossing, the woman at the rail, the guards bound, the pennant of his own authority fluttering over a situation entirely outside it. She watched him do the arithmetic that all the Syndicate's people did, the same arithmetic that had ruled her life for three years — cost against return, risk against reward — and she watched him arrive at the answer she'd bet all their lives on.

Ossler was a factor. Factors did not die for factors' errands.

His longboat turned aside. It did not pursue. It made for the far bank, and Ossler stood in it with his parasol furled, watching them cross, and Rosalind lifted one hand — not a wave, not quite; more like a mark entered in a ledger — and held his eyes until the current took them apart.

Tam brought the ferry in against the far bank with the tenderness of a man handling something holy. When the plank went down, the old ferryman caught Rosalind's sleeve.

"My boy," he said. His breath was still liquor and grief. "The levee money. You'll tell them that too? Whoever reads what you're carrying. Tell them it wasn't the river that killed my boy. Rivers don't count money."

Rosalind looked at the old man, at his cracked hands and his son's locket, and she understood that the case Sera carried had stopped being about Vane and the convoy somewhere back in the highlands. It had become a ledger of a different kind — every debt the Syndicate had ever written in other people's blood, and this crossing, this ferry, this broken old man were simply the newest entries.

"I'll tell them," she said. "Every word."

On the far bank, in the scrub beyond the landing, a figure detached itself from a stand of bamboo and came toward them at a run — a lean man with a satchel and a camera and the frantic energy of someone who had spent two days watching a river and wondering if his sources were already dead.

Arun, the journalist. Alive. On the right side of the water.

But behind them, across the river, Ossler's longboat had stopped at the near bank, and Ossler had unfurled his parasol again, and he was speaking urgently into a field telephone whose line, Rosalind realized with a cold settling in her gut, ran to a relay tower — and relay towers ran to the Syndicate's central exchange, and the exchange ran to Edmund Vane.

The message was already moving. Somewhere north, in a house with clean windows, a man who had buried a massacre once was learning that it had just climbed onto a ferry.

Vane would come himself now. Rosalind had known it since Karst Hollow, had felt it approaching like pressure before a storm cell. And ahead of them, past the marsh road, past the delta towns, lay the lowlands — and the net.

"Everyone aboard?" Arun gasped, reaching them, camera banging against his chest.

Rosalind looked back once at the river, brown and patient and full of everything it carried.

"No," she said. "But we're crossing anyway."
