## 31. Ransom and Reckoning

The rain had found a rhythm by morning — not the wild percussion of the storm cell but a steady, patient drumming on the tarpaulin stretched over the ferry house, as though the sky itself were counting out the hours until something was decided.

Rosalind sat with her wrists bound in front of her with electrical tape, on an upturned crate beneath the ferry house's broken skylight, watching grey water slide past the pilings. Her left eye had swollen to half its size where one of Renn's men had backhanded her at the dock, and every time she blinked she tasted copper. The tape bit when she flexed her fingers. She flexed them anyway. Circulation was a thing you fought for, hour by hour, the same as anything else worth having.

They had taken everything off her at the dock: the map tube, the satchel, Callum's journal, the waterproof case with the photographs sealed inside it. All of it now sat in a dry-bag at the feet of the man who called himself Mr. Aldous, who wore a Syndicate-grey rain slicker that had never once seen honest weather, and who smiled at her the way accountants smile at figures that won't add up.

"Here's the shape of it," Aldous said for the third time. He crouched so their eyes were level, which he probably thought was courtesy. "Mr. Vane doesn't want you dead, Ms. Thornbury. Dead guides tell no one where they've hidden copies. What he wants is simple: you send word to your people — the medic, the girl, the daughter — telling them to bring in whatever they're holding. The negatives, the documents, the second map if there is one. They come in, hand it over, walk away rich. You walk away breathing. Everyone's ledger balances."

"And if they don't come?"

Aldous glanced out at the river, brown and swollen, carrying whole trees downstream like matchsticks. "Then the river does what rivers do this time of year. It's monsoon country, Ms. Thornbury. People drown. It's practically seasonal."

She looked at him for a long moment. Rain came through the broken skylight in a thin bright thread and struck the boards between them, darkening the wood grain by degrees.

"You've never been upcountry," she said. "Have you."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The way you say *monsoon* — like it's a season on a calendar. Like it's weather you can schedule around." She leaned forward as far as the tape allowed, which wasn't far, and her voice dropped low enough that only he could hear it over the water. "I have spent eleven years driving into storms that killed better-prepared people than you will ever be. And I am going to tell you something for free, Mr. Aldous, because it's the last free thing you'll get from me. Your boss thinks he bought a hostage. He didn't. He bought a woman whose crew has already voted once on whether to save her life, and they voted wrong, bless them, and they'll do it again."

Aldous held her gaze for a moment longer than was comfortable for either of them, then stood, smoothed his slicker, and walked to the far end of the ferry house where two of Renn's hired men played cards on a cable spool. Rosalind settled back against the piling and closed her good eye and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn.

The message had gone out at first light — dictated while a man wrote it down, her voice steady, her hands taped. *Come to the Karst Hollow ferry. Come alone. Bring everything. Do it and we all live.* She had said it plainly, without code, without subterfuge, because any code would be caught and any subterfuge punished, and because — this was the part she turned over and over, like a stone in the pocket — because she genuinely did not know what she wanted them to do.

That was the reckoning, and it had nothing to do with ransom.

If Sefa and Wren and Ada came, they died or worse, and the evidence died with them, and Edmund Vane went on buying senators and sinking convoys until the end of his comfortable life. If they didn't come, she drowned in this brown water or took a bullet in some quiet cove, and maybe the evidence survived, and maybe it didn't, and either way she'd never know. There was no version of this where everyone walked out clean. There hadn't been since the pass. Since before the pass, if she was honest — since the day she'd signed Vane's paper with a pen that cost more than her truck.

Her father used to say that debt was just a promise wearing chains. She'd thought it was wisdom when she was nineteen. Now she understood it was a warning.

* * *

Wren found the message where the runner was supposed to leave it — folded inside the hollow gatepost at the marsh town's north road, exactly as the note had instructed, because whoever wrote these instructions had done this kind of work before and knew how couriers thought.

She read it twice under the awning of the chandlery while the rain sheeted off the tin roof, and then she carried it back through the flooded lanes to the room above the fish smokehouse where Sefa was re-dressing Ada's arm.

Ada sat very still while Sefa worked, which was unlike her. The wound along her forearm had gone from angry red to a bruised purple at the edges, and the skin around the stitches was hot to the touch, and both of them knew what that meant even if neither of them had said it aloud. Infection, in a marsh town, in monsoon season, with no proper hospital within a hundred miles and no money to reach one if there were.

Sefa read the note over Wren's shoulder. His hands, usually so steady — hands that had stitched a man's scalp by lantern light in a moving truck — went still on the paper's edge.

"They have her," he said quietly.

"They say they have her."

"You believe otherwise?"

Wren chewed the inside of her cheek. She was seventeen and had been running since she was fifteen, and she had learned in those two years to read people the way other people read weather — quickly, instinctively, with consequences attached. "I think Rosalind Thornbury doesn't write notes like this. Look at it. *Bring everything. We all live.* That's not her voice. That's someone dictating to her and writing down what they wanted to hear."

"It's her handwriting."

"It's her handwriting because they made her write it. That's different." Wren set the note on the table between them, smoothing it flat with one finger, an unconscious gesture, as if the paper might confess something under pressure. "So the question isn't whether it's a trap. It's a trap. The question is what kind."

Ada spoke from the chair, her voice roughened by fever. "The kind where they kill us all and burn the evidence."

"Yes," Wren said. "But also—" She stopped. Started again. "Also the kind where she's actually tied to a post somewhere waiting for us, and every hour we spend being clever is an hour they spend deciding she's not worth the trouble anymore."

Silence, except the rain and, below them, the smell of smoking fish rising through the floorboards like a second weather.

Sefa finished the bandage, tied it off, and sat down heavily on the cot across from Ada. He was forty-three years old and had lost his license over a decision made in four seconds in a burning clinic, and he had learned since then that most moral questions were easy and the hard ones were only hard because both answers cost something real.

"If we go," he said, "we go knowing it's a trap. If we don't go, we keep the evidence safe and we let her die, and I will carry that the rest of my life, and so will you, and so will she, right up until the minute it stops mattering to her."

"We could split it," Wren said. "Send a copy ahead. Hide the originals."

"With whom? The journalist?" Sefa shook his head slowly. "Marsh is crawling with Syndicate eyes. Every boatman, every barkeep. We trusted Delacroix because we had no choice, and it worked, but trust is a currency and we are nearly broke."

Ada was looking at the window, at the rain, at nothing. When she spoke, her voice had the flat clarity that fever sometimes grants, stripping a person down to load-bearing walls. "My mother sold that map to Rosalind with her last breath because she believed someone would finally look at what's in that convoy. Not bury it. Not trade it. *Look at it.*" She turned her head, and her eyes were too bright. "I'm not going to be the reason it ends up in Vane's safe. But I'm also not going to sit in this room and listen to her drown. So whatever we decide, decide it fast, because my arm is making the argument that I won't be useful much longer either way."

Wren looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at the note.

"We vote," he said. "Like at the fire. All three. No proxies."

They voted. It was not unanimous, and that mattered — Wren said later that it was the only reason she trusted the decision, because a unanimous vote in a panic is just fear wearing a costume, but a two-to-one vote taken slowly, with everyone's reasons heard, is a crew doing its work.

They would go. But not as written.

* * *

Rosalind counted the hours by the thread of rain through the skylight and the changing of the guards, and by late afternoon she had learned three things.

First: there were six of them, not the four she'd first guessed. Two were Renn's old river men, hard and silent; the other four were Syndicate contract muscle out of the lowlands, soft-handed and nervous, the kind who checked their weapons too often.

Second: Aldous reported upward by radio every two hours, on a schedule, from the ferry office at the landward end, and the antenna wire ran along the ceiling joists directly above her crate.

Third: nobody had thought to search the ferry house itself, because nobody who worked for Edmund Vane had ever needed to improvise.

The ferry house was a relic of the old service, abandoned when the bridge downstream washed out and the crossing moved. Its machinery remained: the winch drums, the chain drive, the great iron wheel that had once hauled the ferry cradle across the current. And bolted to the wall beside the wheel, forgotten for twenty years, hung a maintenance kit — and inside the kit, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a hacksaw with half a blade left in it.

She got her bound hands onto it sometime in the fifth hour, working the tape against the blade's teeth in slow strokes disguised as fidgeting, an inch at a time, pausing whenever footsteps crossed the boards. The tape parted strand by strand. By the time the light through the skylight went from pewter to bronze, her wrists were free, and she kept them loosely bound whenever anyone looked, a trick of posture she'd learned from a smuggler in another life.

She did not yet know what she would do with freedom. That was the trouble with plans made in captivity: they were all escape and no destination. Six men, one causeway, open marsh on three sides and a flooded river on the fourth. Even loose, she was one woman with a swollen eye and no weapon, and the nearest help was Wren, Sefa, and a fevered girl with a bad arm — if they came at all, and if they came, they were walking into exactly the cage she was sitting in.

Unless the cage changed shape.

She watched Aldous take his evening radio call, watched him nod at whatever the voice on the far end said, watched him glance at her afterward with something new in his face — not cruelty, but the particular discomfort of a man who has just been told the price has gone up. When he crossed to her afterward, he crouched again, level, courteous, and this time the courtesy had edges.

"Change of terms, Ms. Thornbury. My principal is no longer satisfied with the evidence alone. He wants the girl as well. The prospector's daughter. Alive."

The rain filled the pause.

"Why?" Rosalind asked, and heard in her own voice how carefully flat she was keeping it.

"That's above my station." A thin smile. "Though I'd guess it rhymes with *witness*. A daughter who can testify to what her mother found, in her own name, in front of cameras — that's worth more than paper. Paper can be called forged. Blood can't."

"She'll never come."

"Then you'll persuade her. Tomorrow, when they arrive, you'll stand where they can see you, and you'll tell them the terms have improved, and you'll sound convincing." He stood, brushed off his knees. "Or we stop pretending there's a negotiation, and my associates start demonstrating what they're for. I'd rather not. I find messes unprofessional."

He walked away. Rosalind sat in the failing light with her heart going like a trip-hammer and her mind going cold and clear, the way it always did at the exact moment a storm stopped being weather and became a problem.

Because Aldous had just made a mistake. He'd told her the truth — or enough of it. Vane didn't just want the evidence buried. He wanted Ada silenced personally, permanently, in a way that couldn't be undone by a copy surfacing later. Which meant Vane was afraid. Genuinely afraid. Men who were merely greedy sent accountants; men who were afraid sent butchers.

And afraid men made mistakes. They doubled the guard on the wrong door. They scheduled things. They assumed the trapped animal would behave like a trapped animal.

Rosalind looked up at the antenna wire overhead, then at the ferry wheel rusting in its housing, then out through the open river doors at the causeway where, in perhaps fourteen hours, three people she loved more than she'd ever planned to would come walking into the rain.

*All right,* she thought. *Then we change the shape of the cage.*

* * *

They came at dusk the next day, as arranged — but not as arranged.

Aldous stood on the ferry dock with four of his men arrayed behind him, the dry-bag of evidence at his feet, and watched the single boat work its way up the flooded channel through the last grey light. One boat. Three figures. As instructed. He permitted himself the small satisfaction of a plan arriving on schedule.

What he could not see, in the rain and the dusk, was that the boat rode high and light, and that the three figures in it were bailing continuously, and that none of them were Wren, Sefa, or Ada.

It was the marsh town's two oldest fishermen, brothers who had run contraband through these channels since before the Syndicate existed, paid with the last of Sefa's money and a promise, and between them, lashed under a tarp in the bilge, a scavenged engine from a pirogue and forty feet of det cord borrowed from a quarry foreman who owed Rosalind Thornbury a favor from a flood six years ago that she had never mentioned to anyone.

And what Aldous could not hear, over the rain and the engines, was the sound beginning upstream — a low, gathering roar, the sound of the sluice gates at the old quarry reservoir giving way in sequence, because Wren had spent the previous night climbing in the dark with a wrench and Rosalind's handwritten instructions, and because the quarry's neglected spillway, as every child in the marsh towns knew, emptied directly into the channel above the ferry crossing.

The river began to rise. Fast.

On the dock, one of the card players straightened, frowning at the water. "Level's coming up," he said. "That's not right. That's not rain-level."

In the ferry house, Rosalind slipped her cut bonds fully free, took the crowbar from the maintenance kit, and put her shoulder to the main chain-drive housing with all the desperation of a woman who had exactly one chance and knew it. The rusted bolts shrieked. Somewhere outside, a voice shouted — and then the shouting multiplied, and the first of the floodwater came through the pilings like a brown fist, and the lights on the dock went out.

Through the river doors, in the strobing dark, Rosalind saw the dry-bag sweep off the dock into the current, saw Aldous screaming orders that the water ate, saw the ferry cradle — freed now, the chain drive turning under her crowbar one last groaning revolution — swing out across the channel on its old cable, directly into the path of the incoming boat.

Three figures stood up in it. One raised a lamp.

Even at that distance, even through rain and ruin, Rosalind knew the silhouette: broad shoulders, a medic's deliberate calm, and beside him a small fierce figure waving both arms over her head like a signalman, and behind them both, pale and burning-eyed, a girl with her mother's jaw.

"*Rosalind!*" Wren's voice tore across the water. "*Grab the line!*"

A rope arced out of the dark, trailing orange rope-lighter flame, and hit the boards two feet from her hand.

Behind her, upstream, the roar grew to a thunder, and the first wall of the released reservoir came around the bend like a moving hill.

Rosalind grabbed the line.
