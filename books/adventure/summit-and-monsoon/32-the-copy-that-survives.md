## 32. The Copy That Survives

The rain had thinned to a grey whisper by dawn, and the marsh town of Lowmere woke smelling of woodsmoke and drowned things. Rosalind sat in the back room of the Ferryman's Rest with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had no intention of drinking, watching the water rise against the pilings beneath the floorboards. Somewhere below, the river was doing what rivers did after three days of monsoon — taking back everything that had ever been built too close to its temper.

Her left arm was splinted from elbow to wrist. The medic who had set it — not Callum, Callum was dead, Callum had been dead for eleven days now, and still her mind kept reaching for him like a tongue probing a missing tooth — had done competent work. A stranger's hands. That was what survival felt like lately: strangers' hands on you, stitching and binding, while you tried to remember which parts of yourself were still yours.

Maren Quill slept on the cot in the corner, curled around the canvas satchel like a child around a dog. She hadn't let it out of her reach since the ferry. Even asleep, one hand rested on the buckle.

Wren sat across the table from Rosalind, seventeen years old and older than that, turning a playing card over and over — the jack of spades, face worn blank. The girl had stopped talking much, after the ferry. Not the frightened silence of before the pass; something harder. She watched the door the way soldiers watch doors.

"They'll come back," Wren said, without looking up.

"I know."

"Not Vane himself. He doesn't get his boots wet. But men like the ones at the ferry. Men whose whole job is making problems stop being problems." The card flipped. Blank face. Jack. Blank face. "We should move."

"We can't move yet." Rosalind nodded toward the cot. "She hasn't slept in two days. And I need to know if the journalist is even real before we walk into another room with only one exit."

"She's real." Wren said it flatly, the way you'd report the weather. "I checked. Sela Marchand. Writes for *The Meridian Dispatch* under a man's name because nobody prints a woman's byline out here. She's been filing stories about the highlands for two years. Disappearances. Leased land nobody's allowed onto. She's been circling this story the way we circled the storm."

Rosalind looked at her. "When did you check?"

"Last night. While you were being stitched." The card stopped moving. Wren laid it down between them, jack up. "Somebody has to do the thinking while you're busy bleeding."

It stung, and Rosalind let it sting, because the girl was right and because pride was a luxury item now, priced beyond her means. Outside, a cart rattled through standing water, and someone shouted at an ox in a language of pure frustration. Ordinary sounds. The world insisting on itself.

On the cot, Maren stirred but didn't wake. In the grey light her face looked carved rather than fleshed — her mother's jaw, her mother's stubbornness, all of it weathered down to something geological. She had carried the satchel through the river, through the reeds, through the mile of mudflat where they'd crawled while the Syndicate men searched the ferry with lanterns. She had carried it when Rosalind couldn't, when Rosalind's arm hung wrong and the world kept tilting. Whatever else happened, Rosalind thought, that debt would never be entered in any ledger anyone could collect on.

"The copy," Rosalind said quietly. "Show me again."

Wren hesitated. Then she reached into her coat — the oversized coat she'd stolen off a fence post in Karst Hollow, months ago, that still smelled faintly of sheep — and drew out a oilskin packet no bigger than a prayer book. She unwrapped it on the table with the care of someone handling a reliquary.

Photographs. Eleven of them, small, grainy, silver-grey. The convoy in the pass, taken from above — from the ridge where Ada Quill had crouched with a camera she'd traded half a season's wages for. Trucks split open like seed pods. Bodies laid in rows by men wearing government grey, photographed before they were covered. And the documents: manifests, requisition orders, a letter bearing the Vane Syndicate's seal instructing that "the matter of the eastern convoy be concluded without further correspondence."

And beneath the photographs, folded twice, the letter itself — the original, water-stained, Ada Quill's blood still ghosting one corner brown.

"You understand what this is," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question. She needed to say it aloud anyway, needed the words to exist in the air between them, because words spoken became real in a way that plans and hopes did not. "This isn't salvage anymore. This isn't payment. This is a verdict waiting for a courtroom."

"I know what it is," Wren said. "I've known since the fire at the pass, when we voted. I just didn't know if you'd still be alive long enough to use it."

* * *

Sela Marchand came to them at midday, through the rain, alone, as arranged.

Rosalind had expected someone harder-edged. The woman who ducked under the low lintel of the Ferryman's Rest and shook out her umbrella was small, round-shouldered, forty-something, with ink stains on her fingers and spectacles fogged immediately by the warmth of the room. She looked like a schoolteacher. She looked, Rosalind thought, exactly like the kind of person the Syndicate would never see coming, which was perhaps the most dangerous thing about her.

"You're Thornbury." Her voice was quiet, precise, the voice of someone used to being listened to in silence. "You look terrible."

"I've been told the story improves my appearance considerably."

"It usually does." Marchand settled onto the bench across from them, accepted tea she also didn't drink, and glanced once at Maren — awake now, sitting up, the satchel strap looped around her wrist. "Before anything else: I need to tell you what happens to people who bring me stories like the one I think you're carrying. Because you deserve to hear it from me and not discover it later."

"Go on."

"Two years ago a drover named Pell came to me with testimony about the leased land. What was happening up there, why the permits were sealed. Three weeks later he drowned in a stream he'd crossed ten thousand times. Last spring, a clerk in the Survey Office sent me copies of freight records. She was reassigned to a coastal posting within the month and hasn't answered a letter since." Marchand removed her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve with unhurried thoroughness, replaced them. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you. I'm telling you so you understand that I will treat what you have accordingly. Nothing goes in print until it can't be unpublished. Nothing stays in one place. Nothing trusts a lock."

"And if we walk away?" Maren asked. Her first words since the ferry, rough as gravel. "If we decide it's not worth the drowning?"

Marchand looked at her steadily. "Then I'll be sorry, and I'll keep asking, and eventually someone braver or more desperate than you will bring it to me, and your mothers and fathers will have died for a story someone else gets to finish. That's the truth of it. Evidence doesn't rot, Miss Quill. It just waits. The question is whether it waits in a drawer or waits in the world."

The rain ticked against the shutters. Down at the ferry landing, a bell buoy clanged its idiot rhythm.

"There's a condition," Rosalind said. "One."

"Name it."

"The names. Ours. Mine, hers—" she nodded at Maren, at Wren— "the medic who died getting us this far. They stay out of it. The story is about the convoy and the cover-up and Edmund Vane. It is not about us. We are not heroes in this version. We're not in it at all."

Marchand considered her for a long moment, and behind the fogged spectacles something moved — recognition, maybe, or respect. "You understand that makes the story weaker. Readers need faces."

"Readers need truth. Faces they can buy later, in the penny editions, when it's safe." Rosalind leaned forward, and her splinted arm knocked the teacup, and she ignored it. "Edmund Vane has spent twenty years buying every face that ever threatened him. If ours go in the paper, we become the story, and the story becomes about catching us instead of convicting him. No names. That's the price."

"No names," Marchand agreed. "For now." And there was enough iron in the *for now* that Rosalind knew the fight wasn't over — that someday, when the Syndicate fell, the journalist would come back wanting the human ledger, the scars and the debts, the whole wet mess of them. Fine. Someday. If there was a someday.

Marchand spread a waterproof map case on the table. "Now. Show me what survived the river."

* * *

They gave her the photographs but not the original letter. That stayed in the oilskin, against Maren's ribs, where it had lived since the pass. Marchand examined each photograph twice, made notes in a pocket book in a script so small it might have been code, and asked questions in the flat relentless manner of a woman who had learned that the second answer was always truer than the first.

How many trucks. How many bodies. Who wore the grey. How long between the massacre and the arrival of the covering party. Whether the covering party wore Syndicate colours or merely drove Syndicate mules. Whether Ada Quill had said anything, at the end, about where else she'd hidden things.

That last one stopped the room.

"Why?" Maren asked.

"Because a woman who photographs a massacre once usually photographs it twice," Marchand said. "Negatives, Miss Quill. Prints burn. Negatives hide. If your mother was half as careful as I think she was, there's a second set somewhere, and the Syndicate knows it, and that's why they haven't stopped hunting you. They aren't chasing a letter. Letters can be denied. They're chasing proof that can't be."

Maren went very still. Rosalind watched her — watched the girl's hand close slowly around the satchel strap until the knuckles whitened, watched her stare at the middle distance where memory keeps its warehouse.

"She had a cache," Maren said finally. "Near the last camp. She called it her *post office*. I thought it was supplies. I was fourteen and I thought it was supplies." Her voice cracked on the number, on the years between fourteen and now, on all the letters never sent. "There was a hollow tree. Or a rockfall. She'd disappear for an hour sometimes and come back with nothing and say she'd been posting letters to the dead."

Marchand wrote one line in her book and underlined it.

"Where," she said, "was the last camp?"

And Rosalind felt the shape of the next days assemble themselves, unwelcome and inevitable, like a storm cell building past the horizon. Back up. Back into the green wall. Back along the route they'd bled down, past the bridge at Karst Hollow where Callum fell, past the pass of broken trucks, up to the cold camp where an old woman had waited for a daughter who took too long coming home.

"We don't know yet if we're going," Rosalind said — to Marchand, but really to herself, really to the room.

"Yes, you do," said Wren, folding her jack of spades away. "Because if we don't find those negatives, the Syndicate will. And then everyone in this room drowned in a river for nothing."

* * *

Evening came down on Lowmere like a lid. The rain returned, softer now, a patient rain, the kind that meant the monsoon had settled in for weeks and the lowlands would drown by inches. Rosalind stood at the window of the back room and watched lamplight smear across the flooded street, and behind her she could hear the sounds of the others preparing without having decided to prepare: Maren repacking the satchel, weighing it, redistributing weight with a salvager's instinct; Wren cleaning the pistol with the grim economy of someone who had stopped pretending to be squeamish months ago; Marchand's voice, low, giving instructions to a boatman she trusted, arranging a departure that would look like flight and function as bait.

Rosalind unbuttoned her shirt cuff one-handed and looked at the splint. Beneath it, the arm ached with a deep tidal pain, and beneath the ache, older, lay the whole geography of the season: the pass, the river, Callum's hand going slack in hers, the vote at the fire when six exhausted people chose justice over money and knew even then what it would cost.

Ada Quill had bought a map with her dying breath and given it to a stranger, because a stranger was the only person desperate enough to follow it. And now the map was gone — lost at the ferry, sunk with the pirogue, feeding fish — and it didn't matter anymore, because the map had only ever been a key, and they had already opened the door.

A knock at the street door. Three knocks, spaced wrong — too deliberate, too confident for the weather.

Wren was up with the pistol before the third knock finished. Maren slid the satchel under the cot. Marchand simply closed her notebook and became, instantly, a tired traveller waiting out the rain.

Rosalind went to the door and opened it a hand's width.

A boy stood on the step, twelve years old maybe, soaked through, holding out an envelope sealed with wax. Behind him the street ran with water and the lamplight bent in the current, and somewhere far off upstream thunder spoke once, softly, like a man clearing his throat before a speech.

"For the lady with the bad arm," the boy said. "Paid a crown to bring it tonight. Fella said you'd know what it means."

She broke the seal. Inside, a single line in a hand she recognized — the elegant, unhurried script from the ransom note, from the offer with teeth, from every document the Syndicate had ever put its name to:

*You have the copy. We have the negatives' location. Come to the trading post at Two Rivers and we will discuss what each of us is willing to lose.*

Rosalind read it twice. Then she turned to the room, to the three faces watching her, and held the paper up to the lamp so they could see the words, and outside the thunder cleared its throat again, closer now, and began.
