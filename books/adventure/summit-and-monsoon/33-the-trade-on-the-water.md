## 33. The Trade on the Water

The marsh town had no name that appeared on maps, only on the lips of the people who lived there — a scatter of stilt houses and fish-drying racks at the edge of a brown lagoon, where the river forgot its hurry and spread itself thin among the reeds. The rain came down in a fine, patient sift, the kind that did not fall so much as settle, beading on the oilskin of the boats and running in cold threads down the back of Rosalind Thornbury's neck.

She stood at the rail of a borrowed punt and watched the water breathe. It was the color of steeped tea, and it moved with a slow, muscular patience, as if it had all the time in the world and knew something she didn't. Somewhere out in the reed beds, a heron lifted off with a sound like a sail filling, and then the marsh went quiet again except for the rain and the creak of mooring ropes.

Behind her, in the covered stern, the others waited. Callum sat with his back against the gunwale, his medic's bag between his feet like a dog he didn't trust anymore. He had shaved, badly, for the occasion — a gesture toward respectability that fooled no one, least of all himself. Pip crouched at the edge of the canopy, sixteen years old and coiled like a snare, watching the reeds with the flat attention of someone who had learned young that the world was mostly things waiting to go wrong. And Mara Quill — Ada's daughter, her father's jaw and her mother's silence — sat with the copy wrapped in oilcloth across her knees, her hands flat on top of it, as though it might bolt.

It was a copy. That was the whole architecture of the morning. The original — the photographs, the convoy manifests, the field reports with their careful, monstrous euphemisms — had gone east two nights before, sewn into the lining of a journalist's coat, on a boat that was by now somewhere beyond the reach of anyone's money. What remained in Mara's hands was a faithful twin, made in a marsh-town back room by lamplight, and it existed for one purpose only: to be traded away like it was the only one in the world.

"Wind's shifting," Callum said quietly. "Coming around to the northeast. That'll bring the squalls off the highlands by evening."

"Then we're done before evening," Rosalind said.

"And if we're not?"

She didn't answer that. There was no version of *if we're not* that she wanted to say out loud with Pip listening.

The plan was simple, which was to say it was the only kind of plan that survived contact with the Vane Syndicate. The Syndicate wanted the evidence. The Syndicate had money, boats, and men, and after the shambles at the ferry — after Renn's ambush had come apart on the mudflats and left two of their own face-down in the shallows — they wanted it badly enough to negotiate rather than simply take. That was the one advantage Rosalind had ever held over men like Edmund Vane: not leverage, exactly, but the fact that violence, done twice and failed twice, starts to look expensive.

So there would be a trade. On the water, in the open, in the middle of the lagoon where no one could rush a boat from either shore. The copy for the paper — the debt paper, the ledger entries, the little signatures that had turned her life into property. And, because Rosalind had insisted and the Syndicate's broker had, after a long silence, agreed, the others' papers too. Callum's. Pip's. Mara's. Clean ledgers all around, in exchange for a lie wrapped in oilcloth.

She turned from the rail. "Remember how this goes," she said. "Nobody speaks unless I speak. Nobody reaches for anything. We hand over the package when their boat is alongside, not before, and the moment the papers are in our hands, Mara drops the package over the side — weighted, sunk, gone. They can dive for it if they like. The lagoon's nine feet deep and full of leeches."

"And if they don't honor it?" Pip asked. "If they take the package and just — go?"

"Then we're in a boat in the middle of a lagoon with no papers and no money, and they've got a copy of documents that are already on a train east." Rosalind allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Which means they'd have traded their only leverage for nothing. Vane's people are ruthless, but they're not stupid. Stupid is the one thing the Syndicate can't afford."

"Vane," Callum said, and the name sat in the boat like a stone.

"He won't come himself," Rosalind said. "He sends people. That's the whole point of being Edmund Vane."

She believed it when she said it. She said it the way you say a rope holds, testing it with your weight, and the rope held, and she went on believing it right up until the moment it didn't.

* * *

They rowed out at midmorning, through the channels where the reeds stood higher than a man's head and closed over the boat like green water parting and healing. The rain had thickened by degrees into a proper monsoon drizzle, soft and endless, and the air smelled of mud and fish and crushed vegetation, the sweet rotting breath of the marsh. Pip rowed; Rosalind steered. Callum sat with his hands folded, and Mara held the package, and for a long time the only sound was the dip and gurgle of the oars and the hiss of rain on the lagoon.

The meeting place was a drowned tree — some great hardwood that had died standing when the river changed its mind years ago, its bare branches rising from the water like the ribs of something that had drowned reaching for the sky. It was visible from a long way off, and it was visible from nowhere else, which made it honest. Rosalind had chosen it herself.

The Syndicate's boat was already there.

It was a long, low river launch, black-hulled, with an engine that idled so quietly it was less a sound than a presence, a vibration felt in the soles of the feet. A man stood at its stern under an umbrella held by another man, and the man under the umbrella was not Renn.

Renn, Rosalind knew, was in a marsh-town clinic with a broken arm and a fractured orbit, courtesy of the ferry mudflats. This man was older, thinner, gray at the temples, dressed in a linen suit that the rain had begun to ruin, and he wore the expression of a man doing arithmetic he found distasteful. She had seen him once before, years ago, across a desk in a Syndicate countinghouse in the lowlands, when her debt had been a number on a page instead of a weight on her chest. He had been the one who explained the terms. He had been very polite.

"Harrow," she said, as the boats came together, fenders kissing, ropes handed across.

"Miss Thornbury." His voice was dry as ledger paper. "You look well, considering."

"I've been swimming in better water than this, but not recently."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been surgically removed. "Shall we conclude our business?"

They concluded it slowly, the way such things are always concluded, each side watching the other's hands. The Syndicate's launch carried four men besides Harrow and the umbrella-bearer, two of them visibly armed and two of them visibly pretending not to be. Rosalind's boat carried a disgraced medic, a runaway, and a prospector's daughter, and the only weapon aboard was a boat hook, which Rosalind had chosen deliberately, because a boat hook looks like a tool right up until it doesn't.

Harrow produced a leather folio. Inside it, in ordered stacks, were papers. Rosalind took them one at a time and read them in the rain, tilting each page so the water ran off, checking names and amounts and the little embossed seals, and Harrow stood and watched her do it with the patience of a man watching weather.

Callum's discharge and debt release. Pip's — the runaway's real name written out and struck through, the Syndicate's claim on a child formally dissolved, which told Rosalind something ugly about what the Syndicate had been holding over a sixteen-year-old that she filed away to think about later, or never. Mara's. Her own. Four lives, four signatures, four small bureaucratic resurrections.

"The interest accruals," she said, at her own last page. "The entries from the third quarter."

"Struck," Harrow said. "You'll find the arithmetic clean, Miss Thornbury. The Syndicate honors its settlements. It is the settlements that are difficult."

"The settlements are difficult," she repeated, "because you people make them difficult."

"The Syndicate," Harrow said, "makes nothing difficult. People make things difficult by refusing to understand what things cost." He extended one pale hand, palm up. "The package, if you please."

Rosalind nodded to Mara. Mara rose, steady, her face as blank as her father's had ever been on a bad day on the mountain, and held out the oilcloth bundle. One of the armed men took it, weighed it in his hand, and passed it to Harrow, who did not open it. He simply looked at it, and then at Rosalind, and something passed across his face that she would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It was not triumph. It was not suspicion.

It was, she realized much later, apology. Or as close to apology as a man like Harrow could manufacture.

"The papers are yours," he said. "The Syndicate considers all accounts closed." And then, as the folio changed hands, as Callum took it and clutched it to his chest like a rescued child, Harrow said, quietly, in a voice pitched for her alone across the gap between the boats:

"You should know that Mr. Vane was not informed of this arrangement."

The rain filled the silence.

"He learned of it this morning," Harrow went on. "He is not a man who enjoys learning things late. I would not linger in the marsh, Miss Thornbury. I would not linger anywhere."

Rosalind's mouth was dry. "You're telling me this is a trap."

"I'm telling you," Harrow said, "that I have been paid to conclude a transaction, and I have concluded it. What Mr. Vane does with his own money and his own boats is, I regret to say, no longer within my department." He straightened his ruined cuffs. "Good fortune, Miss Thornbury. You will need it, and you will not receive it from us."

The engines of the black launch woke with a low thrum, and the boat drew away into the rain, and the reeds closed behind it, and the lagoon healed over as though nothing had ever happened there at all.

* * *

For a full minute, nobody in the punt spoke.

Then Pip said, "We got them. We actually — the papers. We're —" The word wouldn't come, so Pip said another one instead: "Free."

"Free," Callum echoed, and laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound, and pressed the folio against his face, and Rosalind saw that his shoulders were shaking and understood that it was not entirely laughter. A man does not carry disgrace for eleven years without the setting down of it hurting worse than the carrying.

Mara said nothing. She was watching the reeds where the launch had gone, her father's jaw set, and when she finally spoke, it was to say the thing Rosalind was already thinking.

"He warned us."

"He warned us," Rosalind agreed.

"Syndicate men don't do that."

"No," Rosalind said. "They don't."

She looked down at the folio in her hands, at her own name on her own release, at the clean arithmetic of a debt she had carried so long she had stopped feeling it as a weight and started feeling it as a skeleton — something structural, something she was built around. It was done. It was finished. She was, on paper, a woman who belonged to no one.

And Edmund Vane had learned of the trade that morning.

"Get us moving," she said. "Back channels, the long way, no engine noise. We're off the water within the hour and on the east road before dark."

"Because of Vane," Pip said. It wasn't a question.

"Because of Vane." Rosalind looked east, where the rain was thickening toward the highlands, where the sky had begun to organize itself into something with intent. "Harrow sold us the papers and sold us the warning in the same breath, which means either the Syndicate's countinghouse has developed a conscience, or Edmund Vane has stopped paying the countinghouse. And if it's the second —"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. They had all seen what Vane's money bought when it stopped buying paperwork and started buying something else: the burned camp at Ada's last ground, the men at the ferry, the long patient machinery of a man who had buried a massacre once already and was very good at burying things.

They rowed. The reeds parted and closed. The rain came down.

* * *

They were twenty minutes from the east landing, deep in the channels where the water ran narrow and the reeds scraped the gunwales on both sides, when they heard the engine.

It was not the quiet, apologetic thrum of Harrow's launch. It was a heavy, deep-water engine, the kind that moved boats with steel plates and men with rifles, and it was behind them, back toward the drowned tree, and it was getting louder.

"Kill the oars," Rosalind breathed. "Into the reeds. Now."

Pip shipped the oars without a splash and hauled on the reeds, and the punt slid into the green wall and stopped, rocking, hidden, while the channel behind them filled with sound. The rain came harder. Somewhere far off, over the highlands, thunder cleared its throat.

Then the boat came around the bend.

It was a patrol launch, gunmetal gray, riding low and mean, with a canvas canopy and a bow gun under a tarp that fooled nobody. It slowed as it passed their hiding place — slowed, Rosalind thought, just a heartbeat longer than the channel required — and in the stern, under an umbrella that two men held between them against the wind, stood a figure in a long dark coat.

Edmund Vane was not a large man. He was not a small man. He was a man who had been described, in the society pages, as *unremarkable*, and the description had been the most frightening thing about him. He stood at the rail of his launch and he looked out at the reeds — at their reeds, at the exact stretch of green wall where a punt with four people in it was holding its breath — and he did not look away, and he did not smile, and the rain ran off the brim of his hat in a thin, steady line.

The launch went by. The engine faded. The reeds creaked.

"He saw us," Pip whispered. "He looked right at us. He *saw* us."

"No," Callum said. "No, he —"

"He saw us," Rosalind said quietly. "And he let us go."

She looked down at the folio in her hands, at the clean papers, at the closed accounts, and understood, with the cold clarity that had come to her twice before in her life — once on a ridge road, once in a burning camp — that the trade was not finished. That it had never been about the copy at all. That Edmund Vane had let them keep their papers for the same reason a man leaves a lamp burning in a window: so that the people inside would stay exactly where they were, feeling safe, until he was ready to come for them.

And far to the northeast, over the highlands where the convoy had died and the truth had lain buried in the rain, the storm was coming down off the mountains like something that had been waiting all season for permission.
