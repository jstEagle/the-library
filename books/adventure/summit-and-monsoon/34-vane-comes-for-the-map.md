## 34. Vane Comes for the Map

The marsh town woke slowly, the way it did every morning — a stretching of light across the water, the fishing boats grumbling out through the reeds, the smell of mud and diesel and woodsmoke braiding itself into something the locals called simply *home* and Rosalind had learned to call *survivable*. She stood on the sagging porch of the ferry house with a tin cup of tea going cold in her hands and watched the fog lift off the channel, and she tried to remember the last time she had stood somewhere and not been afraid.

It was a long time ago. She couldn't find the date of it. It was like looking for a coin dropped in deep water.

Behind her, inside, Maren was sleeping in the chair by the cold stove, her boots still on, the oilskin packet with the map's true contents tucked inside her jacket the way she had slept with it for three nights now, one hand curled over it like a child over a doll. Callum was awake, of course. Callum was always awake, it seemed, a man who had burned through his capacity for sleep somewhere in the highlands and never gotten it back. He sat on the steps below her, whittling a stick into nothing, and Tally was down at the water teaching herself to gut fish from an old woman who charged her in stories.

The evidence had reached the journalist. That was done. The copies were made, the pages photographed, the testimony recorded in a voice that shook and then steadied. Whatever happened now, the words existed outside of them, in a room in the marsh town, in a tin box under a floorboard, in a head that would carry them to the coast by week's end. Rosalind had told herself this every morning like a prayer. *It's out of your hands now. It's loose in the world. Loose things are hard to kill.*

But she had kept the map. The original. Ada Quill's dying gift, ink and grease-pencil and blood, the thing that had led them up the mountain to the pass of broken trucks and everything that came after. She had told herself she kept it because it might still be useful, because there were names on its margins that the photographs hadn't caught, because sentiment was a kind of ballast. She had told herself a great many things.

The fog was nearly gone when the boat came up the channel.

She saw it before she understood it — a long, low shape sliding through the reeds with the particular silence of money. Not a fishing boat. Not a patrol skiff. It was a river cruiser, dark-hulled, twin engines idling down to a purr, and it wore its wealth the way the fishing boats wore their rust: completely, without apology. It came alongside the rotting pier at the end of the ferry house's walkway and tied off with lines that looked like they cost more than the pier.

Rosalind set down her tea. "Callum."

"I see it." He was already on his feet, the whittling knife closed away, his face gone to that flat gray place he went when the world asked too much of it. "Tally. *Tally.*" He raised his voice without shouting, the old medic's trick, the voice that carried across a triage line. The girl looked up from the fish table, saw their faces, and came at a run.

"Is it—" Tally started.

"Go get Maren," Rosalind said. "Don't wake her fast. Wake her like there's a fire."

There were four men on the pier. Three of them stood at the rail of the cruiser with the stillness of hired muscle, and the fourth came down the gangway alone, and Rosalind's stomach dropped through the boards of the world, because it was not a lieutenant, not a lawyer, not a messenger.

It was Edmund Vane himself.

She had seen him twice before in her life. Once at a charity auction in the lowlands, across a ballroom, a silver-haired man laughing at something a judge had said. Once at a distance, on the day she'd signed her first contract with the Syndicate, a figure at a window in an office tower, turned away, looking at the weather. He had been a shape both times, a fact rather than a man. Up close he was disappointingly ordinary — medium height, broad through the shoulders, a face like a closed ledger, dressed in field clothes too clean to have ever touched dirt. He wore a rain jacket with the tags still on the zipper pull.

"Rosalind Thornbury." He said her name like he was checking it against an invoice. "You look terrible."

"The monsoon does that." Her voice came out steadier than her hands felt. She folded her arms so the hands were hidden. "You're a long way from your office, Mr. Vane."

"I go where the paperwork goes." He stopped at the foot of the gangway and looked past her at the ferry house, at the sagging porch, at the channel with its morning traffic of reed boats, and something in his face did the arithmetic of the place and found it wanting. "This is where you've been hiding. I expected more effort."

"We weren't hiding. We were finishing a job."

"You were finishing a *catastrophe*," Vane said, "and then you were hiding. Let's not romanticize it. May I?" He gestured at the porch steps without waiting for permission and climbed them, and Rosalind stepped aside because the alternative was to touch him, and she would not touch him. Callum shifted to put himself between Vane and the open door, and Vane looked at him with mild interest, the way a man looks at a dog that has decided to be brave.

"Doctor Okafor," Vane said. "Or is it *former* Doctor Okafor? I read the tribunal summary. Fascinating reading. You made three decisions in that field hospital that no reasonable board would have second-guessed, and you've spent six years letting them eat you alive. People are strange."

"Get to it," Callum said.

"I intend to." Vane settled himself against the porch rail, unhurried, a man who had never once in his life been where he did not choose to be. Behind him, the three men on the pier had not moved, but Rosalind noted that one of them had drifted to the end of the walkway, closing the road inland. Not subtle. Vane didn't think he needed subtlety. That was going to matter.

"Here is where we find ourselves," Vane said. "Somewhere in the last ten days, a journalist in this charming town received a package of documents relating to a convoy that went into the highlands last season and did not come out. The documents are authentic — I've had people confirm that from a distance, and the confirmation was not what I paid for, which tells you something about the people I pay. The journalist is planning to take them to the coast. And the woman who found them is standing on a porch holding a cup of tea, pretending she doesn't know what I'm talking about."

Rosalind said nothing. The silence was the only card she had that he couldn't take from her.

Vane smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind that had opened a thousand doors. "The map," he said. "Ada Quill's map. I want it."

"The map's destroyed."

"The map is in that house, or on your person, or in the mud under this porch, because people like you don't destroy things, Rosalind. That's the whole tragedy of people like you. You *keep* things. You keep receipts and letters and your mother's rings and maps bought from dying women, because you believe objects mean something. I, on the other hand, believe objects are only worth what they cost to replace." He spread his hands. "And I am prepared to pay. Enormously."

The door opened behind them and Maren came out, Tally's hand on her arm, and Rosalind watched the girl take in the scene — the cruiser, the men, the silver-haired man on the rail — and watched her face do what it always did, close down to flint.

"You," Maren said.

"Ada's girl." Vane turned, and for a moment — a moment Rosalind would think about later, in the bad hours — something moved behind his eyes that was not entirely cold. Recognition, maybe. Or its counterfeit. "You have her jaw. She never told me she had a daughter. Of course, she never told me a great many things. That was rather the arrangement."

"You knew my mother." Maren's voice was very flat.

"I knew a prospector who could read country like a priest reads scripture, and who could find water in a rock, and who came to me eleven years ago with a story about a convoy route through the highlands and a fee for her silence, and who took the fee and disappeared, and who I assumed was dead until your crew started asking questions in the wrong towns." Vane's smile thinned. "Grief makes people careless. So does hope. You've been carrying both."

"Say what you came to say," Rosalind said.

"Fine." He straightened off the rail. "Here is the trade. The map, today, and everything in your possession that came off that convoy — papers, photographs, personal effects, the lot. In exchange: your debts to the Syndicate are void. All of them. Yours, Doctor Okafor's, the girl's family's — yes, I know about the girl's brother, don't look so surprised, I know about everything, it's the one skill I've maintained — and Miss Quill's inheritance of her mother's obligations, which she may not know she has, but she does. Four clean ledgers. Four people who can wake up tomorrow and own their own lives. That is what I am offering for a piece of oiled paper."

"And if we say no?" Tally asked. She was sixteen and furious and her voice cracked in the middle, and Rosalind loved her for it.

Vane looked at her with something almost like kindness, which was worse than anything else he could have done. "Then the journalist reaches the coast and publishes, and the Syndicate fractures — that's already begun, by the way; there are men in my own organization who've spent this week discovering their spines — and the inquiry comes, and the inquiry finds the convoy, and the convoy's manifest, and the convoy's *orders*, and the orders trace back to the office of Edmund Vane, and I go to trial, and I lose, because I will lose, the evidence is that good. And in the course of the inquiry, the names of the people who found the evidence will be entered into the record. And the Syndicate, whatever survives of it, will have a great deal of time, and a great deal of money, and a very long memory." He paused. "I'm told the marshes flood badly in the next season. I'm told the roads inland are washed out. I'm told a great many things about this region's vulnerability to misfortune."

"You're threatening a child," Callum said.

"I'm describing weather," Vane said. "Weather happens to everyone. That's the beauty of it."

The channel lapped at the pilings. Somewhere across the water a woman was singing to her nets, an old song with no words in it that Rosalind knew, and the ordinary morning went on around them as if the world had not just been offered for sale on a rotting porch. Rosalind became aware that her heart was very slow and very loud, the way it got before a storm cell dropped, the stillness under the green light.

"And the journalist?" she said. "The copies already made? The testimony already given? Killing the map doesn't kill any of that. You know it. That's why you're here yourself instead of sending men — because the map was never the whole of it, and you need to know what else we kept, and who else we told, and whether the copy that survives is enough." She took a step toward him, and she saw one of the men on the pier shift his weight, and she kept her voice low and even. "You're not buying paper, Edmund. You're buying peace of mind, and you can't afford it, because we already sold the truth to a man with a press, and the press doesn't take returns."

For the first time, the smile left Vane's face entirely, and what was underneath it was tired. Genuinely, humanly tired — a man who had been awake for many nights doing arithmetic that kept refusing to balance.

"No," he agreed quietly. "It isn't enough. Nothing is enough now. That is what I came to learn with my own eyes, and I've learned it." He looked out at the channel, at the fishing boats, at the ordinary day. "My father built the Syndicate on the principle that everything is purchasable. He was right his whole life. I have spent eleven years since that convoy learning the exceptions to the rule, one at a time, and each one has cost me more than the last." He turned back. "You have until the tide turns. The map, and everything else, and the four of you walk away rich and free and alive. Or the tide turns, and I stop being a buyer and become something else, and you'll find out what the Syndicate keeps in its hold for occasions like this. It's on the boat behind me. It has been since I left the coast."

"The tide turns in four hours," Callum said.

"I know precisely when the tide turns," Vane said. "I always know."

He went down the gangway without looking back, and the three men fell in around him, and the cruiser's engines came up from their purr to a low growl as it backed off the pier and held station in the channel — not leaving, Rosalind noted. Holding. A dark shape on the gray water with the morning fog burning off around it, patient as a debt.

They stood on the porch and watched it, four people who had climbed a mountain together and come down again and thought the worst was arithmetic and weather.

Maren spoke first. "He knew my mother. He *knew* her, and he talked about her like a line item."

"He talked about everything like a line item," Rosalind said. "That's how he survives. It's also how he's going to lose." She turned and looked at them — the medic with his whittled stick still in his fist, the runaway with fish blood on her sleeves, the prospector's daughter with her hand pressed flat over the oilskin packet inside her jacket, guarding a map that had already done its work and was now only a relic and a target.

"The tide turns in four hours," Rosalind said. "So we decide in three. And this time there's no vote to hold, because I already know what you'll all say, and I already know what I'll say, and I think even he knows it, which is why he brought whatever's in that hold." She looked back at the cruiser, at the dark water sliding past its hull, and the first thin clouds were coming up from the south, high and white and organized, the leading edge of a system the size of a country.

The storm season wasn't over. It had only been waiting.

"Get the boat ready," she said. "We're not going to be here when the tide turns."

Behind them, out in the channel, a hatch opened on the cruiser's deck, and men began moving cargo — long, careful, deliberate — into the shadow of the hold.
