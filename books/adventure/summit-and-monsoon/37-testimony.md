## 37. Testimony

The hearing room had once been a customs house, and it still smelled of salt and old paper, of decades of cargo manifests and bribes filed neatly in triplicate. Rain came against the tall windows in long silver sheets, and beyond the glass the harbor lights of Karst Hollow smeared and swam like fish beneath the surface of the world.

Rosalind sat at a table too small for what was being asked of her. They had given her water in a chipped glass and a chair with one short leg, so that every time she shifted her weight the whole arrangement rocked, a small absurd metronome counting out the hours. Across from her, three commissioners sat behind a scarred bench: an old woman with a magistrate's bearing named Oyelaran, a nervous younger man whose notes never kept up with his questions, and a third who said almost nothing at all but watched Rosalind the way a man watches weather coming over a ridge — not with fear exactly, but with respect, and a kind of dread.

The room was not empty besides them. Along the back wall sat observers: two lawyers from the provisional authority, a stenographer whose fingers hovered above her machine like birds waiting for a signal, and — Rosalind felt her before she saw her — Ada Quill's daughter, Wren, in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, her hands folded in her lap as though she were holding something there that would escape if she loosened her grip.

Rosalind had slept four hours in three days. Her ribs were strapped where the river had slammed her against the bridge piling; her left hand still did not fully close. But her voice, when they swore her in, came out level and clear, and that surprised her. It sounded like someone else's voice. It sounded, she thought, like her mother's.

"State your name and occupation for the record."

"Rosalind Thornbury. Storm guide. Salvage navigator." A pause. "Debtor."

The last word landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. The young commissioner looked up. Oyelaran did not blink.

"You are aware," Oyelaran said, "that testimony given here may expose you to prosecution under the salvage licensing statutes. That your own expedition was, by any strict reading of the law, unlicensed."

"I'm aware."

"And you give it anyway."

"I buried enough already," Rosalind said. "One more thing under the ground seemed like poor company."

* * *

They began, as she had known they would, with the map.

The dying prospector's map — bought with morphine promises and forty seconds of a stranger's last lucid breath — lay now in an evidence sleeve between two panes of plastic, and even sealed away it seemed to Rosalind to have lost none of its weight. It had been drawn on oilcloth, in a hand that shook and steadied and shook again, and it showed the highlands the way only someone who had loved them could draw them: not as terrain but as memory. The contour lines curved like handwriting. In the corner, in faded ink, a single initial: *A.Q.*

"Describe how you came into possession of this document."

Rosalind told them. She told it plainly, without ornament, because ornament would have cheapened it — the hospice ward that smelled of lilies and disinfectant, the old woman's fingers closing around hers with a strength that had no business surviving the body it lived in, the words that came out between breaths like stones from a landslide: *the convoy, the pass, what they carried, who sent them back.* And then the price, scrawled on a scrap: not money. *Find my daughter. Tell her I didn't sell her.*

Wren made a small sound against the wall. Nobody turned to look at her, which was its own kindness.

"The map led you to the Pass of Broken Trucks," Oyelaran said. "And the convoy."

"It led us through everything else first. The leased land. The river at rising water. The monsoon opened while we were on the ascent." Rosalind's strapped ribs ached at the memory of it, the green wall of the storm coming down the valley like something alive. "Vane wanted the wreck found fast and quiet. Storms don't do either."

"Edmund Vane commissioned the expedition."

"He financed it. He guided it, in his way. Every leg of the route he chose kept us inside Syndicate territory. Every supply cache we drew from was Syndicate stock. I understood that later. At the time I thought he was simply thorough." She turned the chipped glass a quarter-turn on the table. "I was drowning, Commissioner. Drowning people aren't good judges of the rope they're thrown."

* * *

The afternoon session was worse, because the afternoon was about the crew.

They asked her about Callum Reyes, the disgraced medic, and she told them about the night at the fire when the vote was taken — whether to open the sealed orders Vane had given her, or burn them unread. She described how Callum had argued for opening them, how the runaway girl, Pip, had voted with her gut and her anger, how Wren had said nothing for a long time and then said the thing that decided it: *My mother died carrying this. We don't get to pretend we didn't hear her.*

She described Renn — Vane's man, the informer stitched into their crew like a bad seam — and the night action at the pass, when the truth of him came out under lightning and muzzle flash. She did not soften it. She told them that Renn had held a gun on teenagers and a medic, that he had done it believing to his bones that he was the loyal one, and that when the storm cell came down directly overhead and the hillside let go, it was Callum who went back for him. Renn had survived. He was, she had heard, giving testimony in another room, in another city, telling a version of events in which his loyalty was a virtue and his silence was obedience.

"Which is it?" the young commissioner asked. "Loyalty or obedience?"

"There's a difference," Rosalind said, "and it's the width of a human life. He crossed it. Most people never find out which side of it they live on until the rain starts."

Oyelaran leaned forward. "Tell the commission about the evidence itself."

And here Rosalind stopped turning the glass.

Because the evidence was not paper, mostly. Paper burned, paper drowned, paper could be copied and copies denied. What the convoy had carried — what Edmund Vane's Syndicate had helped bury in the Pass of Broken Trucks along with eleven government surveyors and a junior minister who had seen too much — was film. Photographic plates, sealed in waxed canisters, documenting a village cleared by fire and a road built over it, and the signatures of the men who ordered it, and the dates. Physical proof, heavy as ballast, undeniable as a corpse.

"We recovered nine canisters intact," Rosalind said. "Of fourteen."

"What happened to the other five?"

"They're in the river," she said. "Along with most of my map, half my gear, and nearly me. The bridge at Karst Hollow went out under us during the descent. Vane's second team cut the timbers." She looked at the window, at the rain. "He didn't want witnesses. He got weather instead. Weather doesn't take orders."

* * *

They broke at dusk. The stenographer's machine ticked as it cooled. A clerk brought bread and hard cheese and left it untouched on the side table, and Rosalind sat alone in the hearing room watching the harbor lights fight the rain, and Wren came and sat across from her without asking.

For a while neither spoke. Then Wren slid something across the table: a small square of oilcloth, cut from the ruined map, the corner with the initials. *A.Q.* Water-stained. Still legible.

"Pip fished it out of the river," Wren said. "Off the rocks below the bridge. She's been drying it by the stove for two days." Her voice was steady in the way of someone who has decided to be steady and will pay for it later, alone. "I keep thinking about what she said. At the end. *I didn't sell her.*" Wren's jaw worked. "But she did leave me. Twenty years. And then she bought her way back with a map and a dying breath, and I'm supposed to call that even?"

"No," Rosalind said. "You're not supposed to call it anything. That's the trick of it. Everyone out here wants a ledger that balances. Debts paid, sins weighed, accounts closed." She touched the oilcloth square, careful, the way you touch a wound that might still be alive. "There isn't one. There's just what you carry forward and what you leave behind, and you don't get to choose all of it."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. "Callum says you're going to testify about the debt tomorrow. About the Syndicate's ledger. Your actual ledger."

"I am."

"You know what that costs. The debt doesn't vanish because the Syndicate does. It transfers. Someone will own you by Friday."

"Yes," Rosalind said.

"Why?"

Rosalind considered the question honestly, which took longer than she expected. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere out in the dark the harbor bell began its slow count.

"Because I watched a man choose the payment over the justice," she said finally, "and I watched what was left of him afterward. Edmund Vane is going to spend the rest of his life rich and safe and hollowed out, and I'd rather be poor and full than learn his trick." She pushed the oilcloth square back across the table. "Keep that. It's yours. Not the debt. Just the name."

* * *

The second day, they brought out the canisters.

Nine of them, arranged on a trestle table before the bench, wax seals cracked, plates exposed to the light for the first time in years. When the first plate was developed and pinned to the display board, the room went very quiet — the particular quiet of people seeing something they had all, in some compartment of themselves, always known.

The young commissioner's pen stopped moving. The silent third commissioner stood, walked to the board, and studied the image for a long time. Then he turned around and, for the first time, spoke.

"Read the endorsements," he said.

Oyelaran read them aloud. Names. Dates. Countersignatures. Three of the names sat on the provisional authority's own oversight committee. One of them had signed the warrant authorizing this very hearing.

The clerk near the door quietly left the room. No one stopped him.

"Ms. Thornbury." Oyelaran's voice had changed — flatter now, more careful, a voice walking on river ice. "You understand what you've put in front of this commission."

"I understand that five canisters are still in the river," Rosalind said, "and that rivers give things back when they're ready. I understand that Edmund Vane has lawyers, ships, and friends in this building. And I understand that yesterday there were three names on that endorsement list, and today one of them signed your warrant, which means somebody upstairs already knows what's on that table." She rose, slowly, ribs protesting, and stood as straight as the strapping allowed. "So ask your questions fast, Commissioner. Because the storm's come inland, and this time it isn't mine to guide."

Through the tall windows, out past the harbor lights, the sky over the lowlands had gone the color of wet slate, and the first wind of the new front came down the estuary and set the rain against the glass sideways, like something thrown.

Oyelaran looked at the board, and at the window, and made her decision.

"Commission is in recess," she said. "Resumes in one hour — in closed session." Then, quieter, almost to herself: "And someone find out where Edmund Vane's ship is right now."

The answer, when it came twenty minutes later, arrived with the harbor pilot's launch and a boy who ran the length of the customs house stairs two steps at a time: the *Meridian Star*, Vane's private vessel, had weighed anchor at dawn — headed upriver, toward the marsh towns, toward the ferry crossing, toward the journalist who had been asking questions in the wrong taverns.

Toward, Rosalind realized with a cold drop of certainty, the last copy of everything.

She was moving before the boy finished speaking, Wren falling into step beside her, Callum's voice calling from the corridor — and outside, the monsoon, early and furious, came rolling up the valley to meet them.
