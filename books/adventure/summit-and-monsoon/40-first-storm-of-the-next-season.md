## 40. First Storm of the Next Season

The first storm of the new season came in off the sea on a Tuesday, the way the good ones always did, and Rosalind Thornbury felt it in her hands before she heard a word about it on any radio.

She was under the truck at the time — the same truck, older now, its frame patched with welds that Callum swore would outlast them all — chasing a brake line that had developed opinions of its own. The air had gone thick and sweet around midday, that green-metallic taste she'd known since childhood, and her scarred left hand had begun to ache along the old fracture lines where the bone had knit crooked in a field hospital two seasons back. She lay on the creeper with grease to her elbows and felt the pressure drop like a held breath, and she thought: *there you are.*

"You feel that?" she called out.

From somewhere near the cab, Wren's voice floated down, fourteen going on fifteen now and taller than any runaway had a right to be. "Feel what?"

"The storm."

A pause. Then, grudgingly, from the direction of the tool bench: "I feel nothing. I feel a completely normal afternoon." But even as she said it, Wren came around the fender with her face tipped up toward the western sky, where the light had begun to go strange — that bruised, pearlescent strangeness that meant the monsoon had found its way inland again, right on schedule, indifferent to everything that had happened beneath it last year.

Rosalind slid out from under the truck and stood, working the stiffness out of her back. The yard looked different in this light. The corrugated shed they'd raised over the winter caught the early glare and threw it back in dull silver. The rain barrels were full. The vegetable beds Ada's daughter had bullied into existence along the south fence were heavy and green, absurdly productive for soil that had spent decades growing nothing but rust and gravel. And at the edge of the property, where the ridge road dropped away toward the lowlands, the world was already softening into gray.

"Get the shutters," Rosalind said. "Before it eats the windows again."

Wren went, and Rosalind stayed a moment longer, watching the wall of weather build itself out of nothing — the towering anvil cloud, the curtain of rain already visible beneath it like a gray veil hung between heaven and the hills. A year ago she would have been driving *toward* that. Chasing it. Selling herself to it one contract at a time, one debt at a time, until the ledger owned her whole.

Now she just watched it come, the way you watch an old dog cross a familiar room.

* * *

They gathered in the kitchen when the first fat drops began to hit the tin roof — all four of them, plus the radio murmuring its warnings from the shelf. It still surprised her sometimes, how ordinary the arithmetic of their lives had become. Four plates. Four chairs. A table Callum had built from salvaged planks that still smelled faintly of creosote no matter how many times she sanded it.

Mira arrived first, as she always did, carrying an armload of firewood against a cold that wouldn't arrive for hours. She was steadier these days. Her hands, which had shaken for months after the pass — after what they'd found in the trucks, after what Vane's men had done at the ferry — were steady now except in deep sleep, and even then only sometimes. She'd gone back to work in the spring, small clinic work, the kind of medicine that didn't require anyone to choose between a patient and a paycheck. She still kept the old kit packed by the door. Some habits were load-bearing.

"Barometer's falling fast," she said, setting the wood down. "Faster than last year's opener."

"Last year's opener didn't have to live up to anything," Rosalind said.

Ada came in from the garden with her collar up and her hair plastered flat, unbothered, a basket of beans against her hip. She was not, strictly speaking, Quill's daughter anymore in any way that mattered — the name on her papers was her own now, chosen, claimed — but in this house she answered to Ada, and Ada she remained. She'd spent the winter transcribing her mother's journals into something legible, page by page, and in the spring a university press had taken them. *Field Notes of a Prospector*, they were calling it. Ada had insisted the royalties be split three ways, and had won the argument the way she won most arguments, which was by simply continuing to exist in a state of immovable correctness until everyone else surrendered.

"Radio says it'll make landfall by dark," Ada reported, dripping. "Coastal towns are sandbagging. Ferry service is suspended."

"Ferry service," Callum repeated, from his chair by the stove, "is always suspended."

He said it mildly, but the room heard the echo underneath it anyway. The ferry. The trap at the ferry. The night the water ran high and Vane's men had come for the map and the copies and them, and Rosalind had learned exactly how much of herself she was willing to spend to keep a dead woman's truth alive. Callum had been there. Callum had carried her out of the water. Callum knew things about that night he had never fully told, and none of them had ever pressed him, because some ledgers you keep closed on purpose.

He poured tea now, four cups, unhurried, a big man made comfortable in his own quiet. The disgrace that had followed him out of the medical corps had long since stopped mattering; out here, past the reach of licensing boards and gossip, he was simply the man who stitched you up and told you the truth about your odds. That was enough. That had always been enough.

"Storm cell's tracking northeast," Mira said, tapping the radio's dial. "Could clip the highlands. Could miss."

"It won't miss," Rosalind said.

They all looked at her.

"It never misses," she said. "Not this range. Not in the first week. The mountains pull it in. Ask my hand."

Ada smiled despite herself. "Your hand is not a meteorological instrument."

"My hand has a better record than the government bureau."

Outside, the rain arrived in earnest — not the gentle soaking kind but the first hard volleys, fat drops detonating on the tin roof like distant gunfire, and beneath them the wind beginning its low conversation with the eaves. Wren came pounding down from the loft, shutters secured, breathless with the particular joy of a teenager who has been told a storm is coming and intends to enjoy every minute of it.

"Can we watch it from the ridge?" she asked. "Just the front part. Before it gets bad."

"No," said three voices at once.

"One hour," said the fourth, which was Rosalind's, and which drew looks from the other three. "We watch from the tree line, not the open ridge. We're back before the squall line. And if the lightning starts counting under ten seconds, we run, no arguments, no heroics." She pointed at Wren. "You especially."

"I never do heroics," Wren said, with the serene confidence of someone who absolutely did.

* * *

They watched the storm come in from beneath the big cedar at the tree line, the four of them shoulder to shoulder, and it was — Rosalind admitted to herself, watching Wren's face lit green-white by the first far lightning — worth the risk, whatever the others thought.

The front moved across the valley like a living thing, dragging its skirt of rain behind it, and where it passed the world changed color: the gold fields went silver, the river went white, the far hills vanished and reappeared like thoughts you couldn't hold onto. Thunder rolled up the valley floor and broke against the highlands above them, and the highlands took it and gave it back doubled.

Rosalind had chased storms across three provinces and half a lifetime. She had stood on ridges with instruments screaming and money bleeding out of her accounts, had driven into walls of water on roads that dissolved behind her, had once — in another life, under another name on a contract — ridden out a cell directly overhead with hail the size of fists hammering the roof of a truck while Edmund Vane's voice crackled through the radio asking whether she'd reached the pass yet, and whether she understood what was at stake, and whether she remembered what happened to people who disappointed the Syndicate.

She remembered. She remembered all of it. The convoy. The broken trucks in the pass, and what they'd carried, and the mass grave the government reports had called a "logistical discrepancy." Ada's mother's map, inked in a dying woman's careful hand. The vote at the fire, when they'd chosen justice over payment knowing full well what it would cost. The descent. The bridge at Karst Hollow. The trade on the water, and Vane himself coming for the map with that patient, reasonable cruelty of his, and Rosalind saying no — saying it plainly, with a gun in the mud beside her and the storm coming down like judgment, and meaning it.

Vane was in prison now. Not for the massacre — the courts were slower than storms, and that reckoning was still grinding through tribunals and testimony, witness by witness, document by authenticated document. He was in prison for the ferry, for the men he'd sent, for the trap that had killed two of his own and nearly killed Callum. The Syndicate had fractured the way rotten things fracture: suddenly, loudly, from the inside. Half its names were in the newspapers now. The other half were running.

And the evidence — the photographs, the manifests, the journal pages sealed in oilcloth — had reached the light. A journalist in the marsh town had seen to that, a tired woman with ink-stained fingers who had listened to four soaked, half-dead strangers in her kitchen and believed them, which Rosalind still counted among the great mercies of her life. The story had run in the spring. It was still running. Tribunals did not move quickly, but they moved, and every day they moved was a day the dead in the pass grew slightly less abandoned.

"Look," Wren said, pointing.

Below them, out on the flats, a waterspout had touched down over the flooded fields — a pale rope connecting earth to cloud, wandering, drunken, magnificent. It stood there for perhaps forty seconds, swaying, and then it collapsed back into the rain from which it had been born, and the valley went on being drowned and ordinary.

"That's the biggest one I've ever seen," Wren breathed.

"It wasn't that big," Ada said.

"It was enormous."

"It was medium."

"You have no sense of wonder."

"I have an excellent sense of wonder. I also have a sense of scale. They coexist."

Rosalind let them argue and watched the rain instead, and felt the old ache in her hand, and thought about the woman who had drawn the map. *You get one life,* Quill had written once, in a margin, in pencil, in handwriting that slanted downhill like everything else about her. *Spend it on something.* Rosalind had spent hers badly for a long time — on contracts, on debts, on distance. She was spending it differently now. Not well, exactly. Nobody out here lived well. But deliberately. On purpose. On people.

"Time," Mira said quietly, nodding at the sky, where the lightning had begun to count itself down. "Under ten seconds."

They ran.

* * *

That night the storm hit the house full force, and they rode it out the way they rode out everything now: together, with the lamps lit and the kettle on and the radio murmuring, playing cards by lamplight while the roof roared and the windows streamed and somewhere outside the world came apart and put itself back together.

Callum lost spectacularly at cards, as he always did, and accused the deck of conspiracy, as he always did. Ada won, because Ada always won, and distributed her winnings — matchsticks, in this economy — with the magnanimity of an empress. Wren narrated the storm through the window like a sporting event. Mira sat close to the lamp with her mending and only occasionally glanced at the door when the wind hit it hard, and each time Rosalind caught her eye and each time Mira nodded, *fine, fine,* and went back to her needle.

Later, when the others had gone up to the loft and the lean-to, Rosalind stayed at the table alone with the lamp turned low and the storm hammering the dark.

There was a drawer in the table. In the drawer there was a tin box. In the tin box there was almost nothing: a burnt corner of oilcloth. A single spent matchstick from a night of cards that had mattered more than any night since. And a scrap of paper — the only fragment of the original map that had survived the water and the fire and the trade, a piece no bigger than her palm, showing a bend in a river and, in Quill's precise hand, a notation: *here the ground remembers.*

She kept it not because she needed it. The route was in her head, and in the tribunal records, and in the published journals, and in a hundred thousand printed copies of a newspaper story that refused to die. She kept it because some debts should stay visible. Because she had owed a dying stranger a promise, and promises out here were the only currency that never devalued.

The rain eased sometime past midnight, the way the big ones did, settling from fury into a long steady drumming that could go on for days. Rosalind banked the stove, checked the shutters out of habit, and stood a moment at the window looking north, toward the pass, invisible now behind rain and dark but as present to her as her own hands.

*Here the ground remembers.* It did. It always would. But the ground no longer remembered alone.

She was turning from the window when she saw the light.

Far off, down the ridge road, down where the track dropped toward the lowlands and the flooded crossings — a single point of white, bobbing, swinging, coming up through the storm. A lantern. Or a torch. Moving slowly, the way people moved when they were hurt, or lost, or carrying something too heavy to hurry with.

Rosalind stood very still and watched it climb.

Behind her, upstairs, the house slept. Outside, the rain fell on the graves and the gardens and the rusted bones of the pass alike, impartial as ever. And up the dark ridge road, through the first storm of the season, someone was walking toward her door — and in this country, in this weather, at this hour, nobody walked anywhere unless they had nowhere else in the world to go.

She took the lantern down from its hook, and opened the door, and stepped out into the rain to meet them.
