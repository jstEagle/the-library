## 1. Miles, Not Years

The trestle at Mile 41 had lost another plank to the winter, and I made a note of it the way other men make notes of birthdays.

*Plank four, north end, gone. Plank seven, half-gone, leaning east like a drunk leaving church.*

I wrote it in my ledger — a battered thing with a cracked spine and a water stain shaped vaguely like a fish, which I had decided was either an omen or a joke, depending on the day. Then I measured the gap to the next pier with my tape, checked the figure twice, and walked on.

Three years of this. Forty-one miles since dawn would have been a lie; forty-one hundredths, more like. But the ledger didn't care about scale. The ledger only cared that the numbers were true.

That was the arrangement I'd made with the world after the war: I would stop counting days, and in exchange the days would stop counting me.

* * *

You could still smell Karelvo on this line if you knew what you were smelling for. Coal smoke and creosote, mostly, but underneath it something older — the ghost of a country that had been on the maps when I was apprenticing and wasn't anymore. The border had moved. The river had moved more. And the mountain lines, the ones that used to thread through the passes like stitches holding two nations together, had been closed by decree and left to rot in the damp.

Which suited me fine. Rotting things don't ask questions.

I came up over the shoulder of the ridge as the sun did, both of us slow about it, and stopped at the old milepost — cast iron, painted over so many times the numbers had become geology rather than lettering. I scraped frost off the top plate with my thumbnail out of habit. Habit is most of what I have. The rest is a tape measure and opinions about drainage.

From up here you could see the whole valley of the Vire laid out below, brown and swollen from last night's storm, carrying whatever it had taken from the high country down toward Greyharbor and the sea. The river looked pleased with itself. Rivers always do after a flood. They've been somewhere, they've taken things, and they have no intention of giving any of it back.

I'd learned that lesson myself, once. I just hadn't learned it yet, that morning. That came later.

* * *

Tomas hated the mountains.

I want to be clear about that, because everything that came afterward has a way of rewriting the past into something nobler than it was. My brother Tomas Voss, three years my junior and half a head taller, thought the mountain lines were a punishment invented by sadists. He liked flat country and big stations and the sound of a good locomotive working hard on level track. He said the mountains made him feel like the world was holding its breath, and he didn't trust anything that held its breath.

"You'll die in a tunnel," he told me once, on the platform at Serel Junction, with snow coming down sideways and both of us young enough to find that funny. "Some day. Mark it. Freya Voss, dead in a tunnel, and the last thing he ever did was check a measurement."

"Somebody has to," I said. "Otherwise the tunnels fill up with people who don't."

He laughed. He had a laugh that carried — you could pick it out of a crowded yard, a crowded war, a crowded anything. For years after he disappeared I kept hearing it. In the rattle of loose couplings. In wind through a broken window. Once, horribly, in the voice of a man selling chestnuts at Greyharbor market, and I had to walk away mid-transaction because my hands had started shaking and I didn't want the chestnut man to see.

They never found him. That's the official version, and by official I mean there was no version at all. The Meridian Star went into the mountains in the last month of the war and did not come out, and neither did its crew, and when the armistice came and the borders were redrawn, nobody with the authority to look was interested in looking. There were too many trains that hadn't come out. Too many crews. The war had eaten whole regiments; one passenger run more or less was a rounding error.

But Tomas wasn't a rounding error. Tomas was the person who taught me to whistle through my teeth, who covered for me when I froze up during my first shunting accident, who wrote letters from every posting that were three-quarters jokes and one-quarter worry about whether I was eating properly. You don't get to round a man like that down to nothing.

So I did what engineers do with problems nobody else will solve. I started measuring.

* * *

By midmorning I'd reached the Serel branch junction, where the main closed line split toward the tunnel mouths, and I sat on the rusted buffer stop to eat bread and hard cheese and watch the river do its work below.

The storm had rearranged things overnight. It always does. A flood is just the river going through your belongings without asking, and the Vire had been through mine plenty of times — silt over the low track bed, debris hung in the fence wire six feet up, a birch tree deposited across the old loading ramp like a gift nobody wanted. I logged all of it. Debris, birch, approximate weight, position. Because that's the trick I found, the one that keeps a man walking when walking is all he's got: if you write the world down small enough, it stops being enormous.

A dead nation. Write down its mileposts. A missing brother. Write down the trestles between here and wherever he isn't. Grief with no address. Give it a chainage, a station number, a maintenance schedule. It becomes a route then. Routes can be walked.

I know how that sounds. I've had three years of solitude to hear exactly how that sounds. But I'd challenge anyone to sit where I sat, on that buffer stop, with the cheese going stale in my hand, and come up with a better system. The men who drank their way through it weren't wrong, exactly. They were just measuring in the wrong unit.

I was halfway through repacking my bag when I saw it.

Down on the near bank, maybe sixty yards below the junction, where the river had chewed a fresh bite out of the earth overnight, something pale showed through the mud. Not driftwood — driftwood doesn't sit square. Not stone. Square, pale, deliberate. Man-made.

I told myself it was a foundation block. Old culvert headwall, exposed by scour. I told myself that all the way down the slope, sliding on wet grass, telling myself with great conviction, because part of me already knew better and that part was beating fast.

It was a chest.

Iron-strapped wood, river-scoured but whole, wedged in the fresh cut of the bank like it had been set there on purpose — though nothing about rivers is ever on purpose. Mud had half-buried it. One corner stood clear, and on that corner, under the grime, there was a stamp pressed into the metal binding.

I knelt in the muck and worked at it with my knife, and the river talked behind me the whole time, patient and enormous, the way it talks when it knows something you don't.

The stamp came clean. An eagle with spread wings, broken by a diagonal bar. Beneath it, worn but legible:

*REPUBLIC OF KARELVO — STATE PROPERTY.*

Karelvo. Dead three years, same as everything else. Same as the line I'd been walking. Same, as far as the record cared, as my brother.

I should have left it. Any sensible man leaves state property from a drowned country where it lies. Sensible men don't dig chests out of riverbanks alone in the mountains; sensible men log the location and report it and go back to their trestles.

I dug.

* * *

The lock was ruined — the river had done the work of a crowbar over however many seasons it had spent worrying at the seams — and the lid came up with a groan of protest and a belch of cold, sour water.

Inside: sodden paper. That was the first thing, and my heart went down before my hopes did, because paper and rivers are natural enemies and the river nearly always wins. But whoever had packed this chest had packed it like an engineer, and I say that with professional respect — oilcloth layers, wax-sealed bundles, the works. The outer documents were pulp. The inner bundle, when I peeled back the oilcloth with fingers I was pretending were steady, was damp but alive.

A manifest. Official freight and passenger documentation, Karelvo State Railways, final run.

And printed across the header, in the plain bureaucratic type that governments use for things they expect to outlive them:

*MERIDIAN STAR.*

I sat down in the mud. I'm not ashamed of that. Some news arrives standing up and some arrives sitting down, and this one came in like a landslide.

The Meridian Star. Last run, final week of the war, vanished in the Serel passes with passengers, crew, and — according to three years of rumor I'd tried very hard not to listen to — a fortune in state gold evacuated ahead of the collapse. The train that didn't come out. The one they rounded down to nothing.

My hands were shaking now and I let them, because there was no one to see, and I turned to the crew list with the particular dread of a man who has spent three years not looking for a name and is about to look.

The list ran to two pages. Fireman, brakeman, conductor, mail clerk, guards. Names, ranks, home depots. The ink had bled a little at the edges but the center of each line held, and I read down the column the way you read down a casualty list — fast, wanting it over, dreading the end.

Third page. Attachments and riding staff.

*T. VOSS — Asst. Mail & Security Officer.*

The river kept talking. The birch tree lay across the loading ramp. Somewhere above me a trestle waited to be measured, plank four, north end, gone.

I read the line eleven times. I counted, afterward, because counting is what I do. Eleven times, and each time the name stayed exactly where it was, spelled correctly, in his handwriting — because it was in his handwriting, the signature beside the printed name, that quick slanted script I'd known since we were boys signing each other's schoolbooks. *T. Voss.* Alive long enough to sign a manifest. On board the train that never came out. Attached to the mail car, of all places — attached to security, to cargo, to whatever the Republic had trusted to a drowned train in its dying week.

Three years I'd measured trestles so I wouldn't have to think about this moment, and now the moment was here, and do you know what I felt? Underneath the shaking and the cold and the roaring in my ears?

Relief. Ugly, shameful relief. Because a name on a manifest meant he'd been *there*. It meant the not-knowing was over, or at least it had a shape now, a chainage, a location. It meant that somewhere under this valley, in the dark under Mount Serel, there was an answer with my brother's name on it, and answers — even terrible ones — are things an engineer can work toward. We're built for it. Give us a fixed point and we will find our way to it if it kills us.

Often it does. But that's a different chapter.

I wrapped the manifest back in its oilcloth, put the chest's remaining contents — more paper, a sealed tin I didn't open, a brass key stamped with the same broken eagle — into my bag, and climbed back up to the junction with the mud drying on my knees like armor.

The light was going gold on the water. Downstream, far off, maybe twenty miles as the crow flies and thirty as the river bends, Greyharbor sat in its smoke, and beyond it the sea, and beyond that the world, which had spent three years agreeing to leave me alone.

I opened the ledger to a fresh page. My hand had steadied. Funny what a man can carry steady once he's finally picked up the actual weight.

At the top of the page, instead of a mileage figure, I wrote the only number that mattered anymore:

*Mile 0 — Meridian Star.*

Then I closed the book, shouldered my bag, and started downriver, toward Greyharbor, walking faster than I had walked in three years.

I didn't know yet that I was racing anyone. I didn't know that the river gives up its secrets to whoever asks loudest, and that somewhere downstream a man named Aldous had already asked. All I knew was that my brother had signed a manifest on a drowned train, and that for the first time since the war ended, the miles ahead of me had a destination.

It occurs to me, writing this, that I should have enjoyed that last innocent hour. The river would have agreed. It always knows before we do.
