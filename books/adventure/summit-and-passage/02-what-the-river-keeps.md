## 2. What the River Keeps

The storm came up the valley in the night like a debt collector, and by morning the Vell had swallowed half of Mile 114.

I stood on what was left of the embankment with my ledger under my coat and my boots sinking into mud that hadn't been mud yesterday, and I did what I always do when the world rearranges itself: I measured it. The river had taken roughly forty feet of ballast, exposed the old drainage culvert that some Karelvoan engineer in a smarter century had laid with more care than anyone deserved, and deposited, against all probability, a tree across Mile 115 with its roots pointing at the sky like a man surrendering to authorities he doesn't respect.

I wrote all of it down. Forty feet. One culvert, exposed. One tree, insolent.

Three years of this. You'd think a man would run out of river.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't take your memory first. It takes your clock. The morning after the armistice, I woke up and couldn't have told you what day it was if you'd put a gun to my head — which, in certain districts, was not an unreasonable thing to fear. But I could tell you the distance from my billet to the ruined station house. Four hundred and twelve meters. I'd walked it every day to look at the wrecked rolling stock, because looking at wrecked rolling stock was the only honest work left to me.

So I traded years for miles. It seemed like a fair exchange at the time. Years are just miles for people who aren't going anywhere.

* * *

The chest found me before noon.

I'd worked my way down to the waterline to check the culvert — old habit, checking things that belong to dead men's timetables — and there it was, wedged between two slabs of riprap where the flood had thrown it and the ebb had forgotten it. Oak. Iron-banded. About the size of a coffin built for someone the carpenter didn't like very much.

Rivers give up strange things after storms. I've pulled out fence posts, a piano, once an entire chapel bell that rang for a full minute as it rolled down the bank, tolling for nobody, scaring crows off three hillsides. A chest wasn't even in the top ten.

But the seal on it stopped me with my crowbar already fitted under the lid.

Karelvo.

You don't see that seal anymore. Nobody does. Karelvo stopped being a place the way a candle stops being a flame — not destroyed, exactly, just no longer burning. Three years ago it had been a nation on my maps, and Tomas had been somewhere inside its borders, driving trains through mountains for a railway company whose name I never learned because the letters stopped coming and then the country itself stopped coming, dissolved into two occupation zones and a border commission that met quarterly to argue about bridges.

The wax had survived better than the wood. A double-headed river serpent, coiled around a mountain peak. I'd seen it on customs documents during the war, stamped by clerks who are almost certainly dead now, sealing papers that are almost certainly ash.

I should have walked away. Let me be clear about that, since hindsight is going to spend the rest of this story insisting I'm an idiot. A sealed chest bearing the arms of a drowned nation, washed out of a flooded borderland I had no business loving as much as I did — any sensible man leaves it for the salvage boats. There were laws about this now. There were men with guns who enforced the laws about this, and most of them worked for whoever paid best.

But the chest had come forty miles downriver, minimum. And everything that comes forty miles down the Vell comes out of the Serel country. Out of the closed lines. Out of the mountains where the maps went blank the same season the letters stopped.

I pried it open right there in the mud, because patience is a virtue I've never been able to afford.

* * *

Waterlogged paper is a strange material. Dry it wrong and it becomes a brick; dry it right and it becomes a ghost. I knew this because I'd spent one miserable winter after the war working recovery duty, flattening soaked archives on clotheslines strung through a requisitioned granary, learning that documents drown slower than people and grieve longer.

So I didn't open the oilskin packet fully. I carried it back to my camp — a converted section house at Mile 112, one room, one stove, one cot, one wall of maps that would have gotten me shot in certain company — and I built a low fire and I dried the pages flat on a board, one at a time, the way you feed a fire you're afraid of.

There were four documents that survived well enough to read.

The first was a bill of lading. Freight, mixed consignment, consignor redacted — literally blacked out, in ink so heavy the page had torn under it. Somebody had wanted that line gone badly enough to fight the paper for it.

The second was a customs declaration, Karelvoan, dated eleven days before the armistice. Route: Serel Tunnel Line, northbound. That made my hands go still on the board. The Serel Tunnel Line. I'd spent two summers mapping its approaches from the outside, walking the closed right-of-way, measuring trestles over water that shouldn't have been there. The tunnels beneath Mount Serel had been flooded since the last year of the war — everyone said demolition charges, everyone said sabotage, and everyone said it in the tone of voice people use when they've agreed not to ask follow-up questions.

The third document was a manifest. Passenger and crew, vessel — no. Not vessel. Train. I remember the exact moment my eye caught on the word, because I actually laughed, alone, in a cold room, at a piece of paper. A train with a crew list, like a ship. But that was Karelvo all over. They ran their mountain expresses like naval vessels. Captains, not conductors. Watch rotations. A purser.

The Meridian Star.

I'd heard the name before, the way you hear a word in a language you don't speak — it passes through you without catching. Salvage talk in Greyharbor taverns, mostly. A rich train lost in the floods, they said. Gold in the mail car, they said. Every drowned thing has gold in it, according to taverns. Taverns are optimists.

Then I read the crew list.

And here I have to be honest with you, because this account is worthless otherwise: I did not want to read the name. Some part of me — the part that had spent three years converting time into distance precisely so that nothing could ambush me — understood what kind of document dries flat on a board in front of a dying fire at dusk, and understood the odds, and wanted to fold the page closed and put it in the stove and keep my forty feet of lost embankment and my insolent tree and my ledger full of miles.

But I am an engineer. Engineers read the whole specification.

**V. Tomaš — Second Engineer, Motive Power.**

Tomas Voss. They'd written his name backwards, or I read it backwards, or the grief simply reached up out of the page and turned the world ninety degrees; I genuinely could not tell you which. What I can tell you is that I sat on the floor of that section house for a long time — how long, I couldn't say, which is the joke, isn't it, the man who traded away his clock getting robbed of it again — staring at eleven letters in faded iron-gall ink.

He was alive when that manifest was written. That's what a crew list means. Somebody saw him. Somebody counted him. Eleven days before the armistice, a clerk with decent handwriting looked at my brother's face and wrote down his name and his posting, and Tomas stood there being alive, probably complaining about the coffee, because Tomas complained about coffee the way other men complain about governments.

Three years I'd told myself he was dead. Not hoped it — told it. There's a difference, and the difference is that hope is a load-bearing wall and telling is a door you nail shut. Dead was cleaner. Dead meant the letters stopped for a reason that had an ending. Missing meant the letters might start again tomorrow, and tomorrow after tomorrow of that will hollow a man out like rot in a trestle pile — everything standing, nothing sound.

Dead, I could measure. Missing, I could only walk.

And now the river had handed me a third category, worse than both: *was aboard.* Was aboard the Meridian Star, northbound through the Serel Tunnel Line, eleven days before the end. Was aboard a train that every drunk in Greyharbor swore lay at the bottom of a flooded mountain with a fortune in her belly.

Which meant my brother wasn't scattered somewhere in an unmarked grave along a broken front. My brother had an address.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I want to report that I behaved sensibly — ate something, banked the fire, followed my own advice about rest and judgment — but the truth is I sat up all night with the manifest and the customs declaration and the bill of lading with its angry black bar of censorship, arranging and rearranging them on my map table like a man dealing cards to himself.

Because here's what kept snagging me, the splinter under the skin: the freight.

A passenger express doesn't run north through a war zone in the last days of a losing war carrying *mixed consignment.* Mixed consignment is a phrase for peacetime, for cloth and machine parts and somebody's grandmother's furniture. In the final eleven days, the only things moving north through Karelvo were people fleeing and things people fleeing couldn't carry. Gold went south, toward the ports, toward the ships. Evidence — well. Evidence goes wherever someone thinks it will be safe, or wherever someone thinks it will be useful later, and those are rarely the same direction.

And the consignor was blacked out. On a domestic waybill. You black out a consignor when the name would tell the reader something dangerous. Governments black out names. So do the people who rob governments.

I got up four times in the night to look at the map. The Serel country spread across the top third of it, my own pencil lines crawling over it like frost — mileages, bearings, the ghost outlines of trestles I'd paced out with a surveyor's chain and a stubbornness my doctor called a symptom. The flooded tunnel complex sat in the middle of it all, a blank oval where six kilometers of rail line used to be. I knew the approaches. I knew the ventilation shafts, the ones still open, the ones the maps forgot. I knew the gradient of the old grade into the north portal better than I knew the back of my own hand, which is saying something, because I've looked at the back of my own hand a great deal these past three years, counting the scars.

Somewhere under that blank oval, if the taverns were even ten percent right, lay the Meridian Star.

Somewhere under that blank oval, if the manifest was right, lay the answer to a question I had nailed shut three years ago and had just, with my own hands, pried open again.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and indifferent. I boiled water I didn't drink. I shaved, which surprised me — some animal instinct about presenting yourself properly before a change of state, though whether the change was mine or the world's I couldn't have said.

Then I did the engineer's thing. The thing that has kept me alive through a war and its aftermath, the thing Tomas used to tease me about — *you'd schedule your own funeral, Frey, and complain about the turnout* — I made a list.

One: establish provenance. Where exactly had the chest entered the river? Debris lines after a flood tell you their history if you read them right, and I intended to walk the banks downstream of Serel gorge until I found the matching scar on the landscape.

Two: confirm the wreck. Tavern talk is worth exactly what you pay for it. But there were older men on the river — ferrymen, fish-trap tenders, a heron of a lockkeeper at Dole who remembered the tunnel line running — and old men on rivers remember everything, especially the things they were paid to forget.

Three: Greyharbor. Because anything valuable at the bottom of the Vell watershed would eventually concern Greyharbor, and whatever concerned Greyharbor would eventually concern the salvage interests, and the salvage interests in this new dispensation of ours had a single name attached to them, spoken in dockside conversations the way you'd speak a weather system. Aldous. Corvane Aldous. I'd seen the name on placards for his dredging fleet, on the new customs houses going up along the waterfront, on the lips of officials who'd recently developed better coats.

I didn't know yet why that name mattered. I want that on the record too. On the morning after the storm, Corvane Aldous was to me a silhouette on a poster, a magnate, a man who owned machines that ate rivers. I knew nothing about Karelvo's final government, nothing about who had signed which evacuation order, nothing about the price of a route through mountains. I was a surveyor with a dead brother's name on a wet piece of paper and a plan that fit on an index card.

Walk the banks. Ask the old men. Go to Greyharbor.

It's a good plan, in the way that a trestle is a good bridge — right up until the flood comes down the valley.

I packed the documents in oilcloth inside my spare shirt, wrapped the bundle in my survey tarpaulin, and put it at the bottom of my rucksack under the ledger. Then, on second thought, I took it out and put it inside my coat instead, against my chest, where I could feel it. Old habits from the war. You carry the irreplaceable things against your body, where losing them means losing you first.

At the door I stopped and looked back at the room — the maps, the cot, the stove, the ledger on its table with three years of distances recorded in a hand that never wavered — and I had the distinct sensation of a man leaving a house he already knows he won't live in again.

Outside, the river had dropped a foot overnight and was running brown and businesslike, hauling the storm's leavings toward the sea. Somewhere upstream, under a hundred feet of green water and six kilometers of drowned tunnel, a train sat tilted in the dark with my brother's name in her books.

"Eleven days," I said to the river, because talking to rivers is free and talking to myself had started costing more. "You kept him eleven extra days, and you never said a word."

The river, being a river, declined to comment.

I locked the section house — force of habit; there was nothing inside worth stealing except the maps, and anyone clever enough to steal them was clever enough to pick the lock — and turned south, toward Greyharbor, walking the old towpath with the manifest riding warm against my ribs.

Mile 112 to Greyharbor: nineteen and a quarter, by my count.

For the first time in three years, I counted the miles without wanting them to be the whole of the journey.
