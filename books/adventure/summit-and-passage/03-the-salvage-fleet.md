## 3. The Salvage Fleet

Greyharbor smelled the way all drowned towns smell: tar, brine, and money changing hands too fast to count. I came down off the mountain road with a chest strap-burned into my shoulders and a manifest folded against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I told myself I was only there for rope and lamp oil. That was a lie. I was there because Tomas's name had been sitting in my pocket for two days, and a man can only carry a name that heavy so far before he needs to set it down somewhere loud.

The town had grown fat since the war. Karelvo's collapse had turned Greyharbor from a fishing port into a salvage capital — half the docks were cranes now, and the other half were lawyers. Every third building flew the flag of some recovery concern or other, and every flag was newer than the last. The river gave up its dead on one side of town, and the other side sold them back at markup.

I tied the chest under a tarp in a stable loft near the customs house — paid the stableman double not to look at it, which is the going rate for blindness anywhere — and walked down to the water with my ledger under my arm, looking like exactly what I was: an engineer with nothing better to do than measure things.

* * *

The proclamation was nailed to the customs house door, and there was already a crowd around it, which told me everything before I read a word. Crowds don't gather around good news.

BY DECREE OF THE PROVISIONAL ADMINISTRATION OF THE BORDERLANDS, it began, and then spent four hundred words saying what could have been said in ten: the wreck of the steam vessel Meridian Star, located in the waters of the Serel gorge, was hereby declared salvage of record, and all rights of recovery vested exclusively in the Aldous Recovery Consortium. Any party attempting salvage without consortium writ would be subject to forfeiture, fine, and imprisonment.

At the bottom, in smaller type, as if embarrassed by its own arithmetic: recovery operations to commence within six tides.

Six tides. Three days. Somebody had found my wreck while I'd been hauling it piece by piece up a riverbank, and somebody with a bigger boat and a longer reach had already signed for it.

"Read it twice," said a voice beside me. "It gets worse the second time."

The speaker was a woman leaning against the customs house wall with the studied idleness of someone who was working. Dockworker's coat, sleeves rolled, forearms scarred with rope burns in the pattern you get from years of catching lines that didn't want to be caught. Dark hair cut short and practical. Eyes that did the thing dockworkers' eyes do — cataloguing everyone who passed, pricing them, filing them.

"I only read it once," I said.

"Lucky you." She pushed off the wall. "Second time you notice the date. Decree was sealed nine days ago."

Nine days. Before the storm. Before the chest. Which meant the wreck had been located weeks ago, maybe months, and the paper trail had been moving through offices while the river kept its own counsel.

"That's fast work," I said.

"That's Aldous work." She said the name the way sailors say the name of a reef they've lost friends to. "Corvane Aldous doesn't find wrecks, friend. He finds out where wrecks are going to be found, and he's standing there when they are." She looked me over once, top to bottom, unhurried. "You've got surveyor's hands and a soldier's walk and you just read that notice like it owed you money. So either you're a rival salvager, which would be stupid, or you're something more interesting."

"And if I'm neither?"

"Then you're a very good liar, and I'd still buy you a drink, because liars know things." She stuck out a hand rough as wharf planking. "Sela. And before you ask — no, I don't work for him. I load his barges, which is different. Employees get paid. I get paid and I keep my eyes open."

* * *

The tavern she chose was called the Drowned Bell, which struck me as either very honest or very stupid branding for a port full of salvage men. It was dim, low-ceilinged, and smelled of fried fish and cheaper decisions. Sela got us a corner table by the simple method of glaring at the people already in it.

"Aldous," she said, once we had drinks neither of us touched. "You want to know who you're up against, so listen. Corvane Aldous started the war as a shipping clerk and ended it owning half the contracts to move other people's armies around. When Karelvo went under, every government office burned or flooded or both — except the ones Aldous had already bought. He came out of the peace with warehouses full of documents nobody else had, and documents are worth more than gold, friend, because gold just sits there being gold."

"You sound like you admire him."

"I sound like I've watched him work." She turned her cup a slow quarter-turn. "Three years ago there was a passenger ferry called the Vessa, sank in the shallows off Cape Maren. Forty-one dead. Salvage rights should have gone to the families — there was a law about it, old Karelvo law. Aldous got the decree instead. Took him eleven days. Nobody knows how, nobody asks, because asking questions about Aldous is how you end up loading barges at night shift until your arms fall off."

"The Meridian Star," I said. "What do people say she was carrying?"

Sela looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide something. It was like watching a lock turn.

"People say gold," she said. "People always say gold. But I helped load his dredgers last week — the big one, the Corvane's Pride, and two dive tenders, and a decompression barge, and enough chain and cable to moor a cathedral. You don't rig that much gear for a strongbox." She leaned in. "And here's the part I'm telling you because I liked your face when you read the decree, and I'll deny it tomorrow. He brought a man aboard the Pride two nights ago. Civilian clothes, government face. The kind of face that used to sit behind desks in Karelvo's ministries deciding which trains ran and which didn't. Aldous met him below decks, private, and the whole dock crew got sent home early. You don't clear the dock for gold."

"What do you clear the dock for?"

"Guilt," Sela said. "Or evidence of it. Depends which side of the hanging you're standing on."

* * *

I walked the waterfront after she left me, because walking is what I do when thinking gets too loud, and Greyharbor's waterfront had plenty of distance in it. Half a mile of pilings and cranes and barges, and I measured it without meaning to — forty-one paces between the fish market and the chandlery, sixty-two along the north quay — because that's the shape my mind makes of the world. Miles, not years. Distances instead of griefs.

The fleet sat at anchor in the outer roads where I could see it whole, and seeing it whole was a mistake. Six vessels, and not one of them small. The flagship was a converted gunboat with her guns stripped and her holds gutted for diving gear, black-hulled, name picked out in gold leaf: CORVANE'S PRIDE. Around her clustered the tenders and the dredger and a pair of patrol skiffs with the lean hungry lines of things built to chase. Steam stood up from her stacks in a straight column, patient, certain. Everything about the formation said: we have already won, we are merely waiting for the tide.

Six tides. I counted them the way I counted everything now. One tide to provision. One to negotiate — because a man like Aldous never launches without making sure the local officials have been made comfortable first. Two for the run upriver against the current, if the weather held. That left two. Two tides to do whatever I was going to do, and I didn't yet know what that was, only that the manifest in my breast pocket listed a fireman named T. Voss among the crew of the Meridian Star, and that Aldous had cleared his own dock before meeting a man with a government face, and that those two facts were pointing at each other across three years of dark water like lamps.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said to Sela, the thing I'd carried up from the riverbank and hadn't set down even in the stable loft: the chest I'd pulled from the mud wasn't cargo. Cargo washes loose. Cargo scatters. That chest had been wedged into the bank with intent, lashed and weighted and hidden by someone who knew the river and knew the wreck and wanted whatever was inside to survive. Someone who'd had time, underwater, in the dark, dying maybe, to think about who might come looking someday.

Tomas had gone missing three years ago, in the borderlands, in the last weeks of the war, on a courier run he'd never explained. The Meridian Star had gone down — I'd learn later exactly when, but the manifest's dates put her final voyage inside that same window. I am an engineer. I do not believe in coincidence. I believe in loads, and stresses, and the point where a structure fails, and every structure I knew of had failed at the same joint: my brother, this train, this man Aldous, this drowned borderland.

* * *

I went back to the Drowned Bell at dusk, and Sela was there, which I suspected was not luck. She'd saved my corner table by the efficient method of putting her boots on the bench.

"Well?" she said. "You've walked the waterfront twice and stared at the Pride long enough to get noticed. So either you're planning something idiotic, or you're planning something suicidal, and I've got coin on idiotic."

"Neither. Yet." I sat down across from her. "I need to know how deep the Serel gorge runs above the falls. Old charts, pre-war. Survey notes. Anything."

Something moved behind her eyes — recalculation, re-pricing. "Why?"

"Because Aldous's divers will go down through the gorge mouth, the way any sane salvage operation would. Deep water, cold current, months of work if the wreck's where I think it is." I kept my voice level, the voice I used for reading bridge inspections aloud. "But the Meridian Star didn't sink in open water. She went into the flooded tunnel system under Mount Serel. The old rail line. There's a station down there, and maintenance galleries, and if you know the gradients — and I know the gradients, I've spent three years walking those closed lines — there's a way in that doesn't need six ships and a cathedral's worth of chain."

Sela was quiet for a moment. In the lamplight I saw her make the same calculation I'd made on the road: the value of a secret against the value of a share.

"You're the madman who walks the dead lines," she said slowly. "There's talk of you. The engineer who measures trestles nobody will ever use again. I thought it was a story dockhands tell."

"It's a habit."

"It's a map," she said. "A living one, in a head, and Aldous doesn't have it, and that's worth more than you know." She pulled her boots off the bench and leaned forward, and the idleness dropped away entirely, and underneath it was someone quick and hard and, I thought, fundamentally decent in the way of people who've decided decency is a tactical advantage. "Listen to me, Voss. Whatever you're thinking — going in alone, ahead of the fleet, some lone-wolf hero business — don't. Aldous has divers who worked the harbor blockade during the war, pulling bodies off scuttled ships. He has patrol skiffs, and the river authority in his pocket, and a decree that makes you a criminal the moment you touch that water. Alone, you're a corpse with a head start."

"So what would you advise? Giving up?"

"I'd advise you stop counting tides and start counting friends." She stood, dropped a coin on the table, and looked down at me with something that was almost a smile. "Come back here tomorrow, an hour after dawn. There are people in this town who owe Aldous nothing and the river everything, and some of them can hold their breath a long time. If you're serious — truly serious, not grief wearing a plan as a coat — I'll introduce you. If you're not, stay away, because I've seen what happens to people who half-commit against that man, and it isn't drowning. Drowning is quick."

She left. I sat a while in the lamplight with my untouched drink and the manifest against my ribs, doing sums.

Six tides. One friend, possibly. A stolen wreck, a criminal decree, a mountain full of drowned tunnels, and a brother's name written in a dead man's ledger.

Outside, the tide was turning. I could hear it — the long low exhale of the river giving back what it had taken, the water dropping inch by inch off the pilings, marking time the way I used to mark it, in miles.

Two tides' lead, if I was lucky. Less, if Aldous was luckier.

I finished the drink after all. It tasted like rope and decisions, and I went to find a bed I wouldn't sleep in, because at dawn I had charts to steal and a crew to beg for, and somewhere under Mount Serel, in the dark where the water kept what it kept, my brother was waiting to find out whether anyone had ever bothered to come looking.

* * *

One more thing, and I set it down here because the ledger is where I keep true things: as I crossed the quay that night, past the dark bulk of the Corvane's Pride, a light burned in her stern cabin. Through the glass I saw a man at a desk — broad-shouldered, silver at the temples, writing with the unhurried strokes of someone signing things that become inevitable once signed. He didn't look up. He didn't need to. Men like that never look up; the world arranges itself beneath their pen.

But as I passed, I swear the light shifted, and for half a heartbeat his shadow lay long across the deck plates, reaching toward the rail, toward the water, toward the drowned dark of the gorge —

— and it reached, I thought, exactly as far as a hand reaching for a train manifest. For a name on a crew list.

He was measuring something too. And I had six tides to make sure I measured faster.
