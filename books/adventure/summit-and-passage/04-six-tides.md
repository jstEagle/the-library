## 4. Six Tides

I did not sleep that night. I want to say it was grief keeping me awake, or hope, or some noble fire lit in my chest by the sight of my brother's name in a dead man's handwriting. The truth is less poetic: the chest was sitting on my table, dripping onto floorboards I'd planed myself, and every time I closed my eyes I saw *TOMAS VOSS — FIREMAN* in faded ink and woke up convinced I'd imagined it.

By the third time, I gave up on sleep and lit the lamp.

The manifest lay flat where I'd left it, curling at the edges as it dried, the paper doing that thing drowned paper does — trying to become what it was before the river took it. I understood the impulse. Three years of pretending to be a surveyor instead of a man with a hole in him, and one sheet of pulp had undone all of it.

Tomas Voss. Fireman. Meridian Star.

Fireman. Not passenger. He'd been *working* the run. My little brother, who couldn't keep a bicycle upright on a straight road, had been firing a locomotive through the Karelvo borderlands in the last month of the war, when the whole country was coming down like a bad roof.

I made tea I didn't drink and pulled out the maps.

* * *

People think mapmaking is drawing. It isn't. It's arguing with the land until the land admits what it's done. Every line I've ever inked represents an hour of standing in mud with a chain and a level, asking a hillside politely to be the shape it actually is. The maps of the Serel district were the best work I ever did, which is a grim joke now, because they were also the last commissioned before the border commission folded and the whole valley got written off as a drowned country nobody wanted to remember.

I spread them across the table, weighted at the corners with the tea tin, a spanner, a stone from the riverbank, and the manifest itself. Four corners held down by four objects, like the page was a body and I was performing some amateur funeral rite.

Here's what I knew about the Meridian Star, or thought I knew, which in my experience is the same currency, just minted at a lower purity. In the final weeks of the war, when Karelvo's government was evacuating everything that could be carried — ministries, archives, gold reserves, people who could pay — the rail lines were running day and night. The Meridian Star was one of the last trains north out of Vellmark, the old capital. It never arrived. Official story: bridge washout near Mount Serel, locomotive and consist lost with all hands. Unofficial story, the one the river people told: it went into the mountain.

Into the mountain. Trains don't go into mountains. Except these ones did, because the Serel tunnels were the engineering marvel nobody survived to brag about. Nine kilometers of bored granite carrying the main line under the massif, twin bores, ventilation shafts you could drop a house down. When the war ended and the border moved, somebody — I never learned who, and I've spent three years not finding out, which takes real discipline — opened the diversion gates on the upper Serel river and let the water do the demolition. The tunnels flooded. The portals were sealed with masonry and official forgetting. And the Meridian Star, if the river people were right, had been in there when the taps were turned.

A train sunk inside a mountain behind a wall of government concrete. That's not a wreck. That's a tomb with a cargo manifest.

I found the tunnel profiles first. Sheet fourteen, the Serel bores, drawn in my own hand three years and one lifetime ago. Grade, curvature, clearance, the lot. Then I pulled the hydrology sheets — sheet nineteen, twenty-two — and laid them over.

And there it was. The thing I should have seen years ago and hadn't, because grief makes a man efficient at exactly one thing: not looking.

The flooded section of the lower bore didn't end at the portal seal. It ran back — deep back — under the shoulder of the mountain itself, and the old alignment passed directly beneath the saddle where the upper river made its elbow. Which meant the water in those tunnels wasn't just tunnel water. It was connected, through seepage channels and the old drainage adits we'd cut for construction, to the river above. To *my* river. The river that had coughed up a salvage chest stamped with the seal of a nation that no longer existed, forty miles downstream of a mountain full of drowned railway.

Chests float. Chests travel. A chest wedged into a drainage adit for three years, working loose in a storm surge, riding the seepage channel down and out through the old construction adit below the portal — I traced it with my finger, line by line, and the route was so clean it felt rehearsed. The river hadn't given me a gift. The river had given me directions.

The wreck was under Mount Serel. And the only way in was the way I knew better than anyone alive: the closed lines.

* * *

Let me explain something about "closed" as the railway administration uses the word, because it will matter later. Closed does not mean gone. Closed means the budget line was struck, the staff dismissed, the gates chained, and the whole apparatus handed over to weather and rust and whatever grows in the dark. The rails are still there. The tunnels are still there. The gradients are still there, patient as arithmetic. A closed line is just an open line holding its breath.

And I had spent three years walking them. Measuring trestles. Recording clearances. Filling ledger after ledger with distances because miles were easier to count than days. I had told myself it was a job, then a habit, then a way of being dead without the paperwork. Turns out it was training. Turns out the universe is a patient employer and pays wages late.

Six tides. That's what Sela had said Aldous had — six tides before his fleet launched from Greyharbor. I stood at my window and looked at the river, brown and swollen after the storm, running fast enough to carry a man's opinion away with him.

Greyharbor to the Serel approaches by water: four days with a loaded dredger, five if the bars were ugly, and Aldous would have pilots who knew the bars. Call it five days, plus fitting out, plus however long it took a magnate to stop giving speeches to himself in the mirror. Six tides, Sela said. Tides aren't days; tides are twice-daily promises. But roughly — roughly — I had five days.

What did I need? A boat small enough to move fast and shallow. Air, if it came to going under, which it would, because the portal seals weren't going to open themselves and the adits were neck-deep at best. Lights. Rope. Someone who could dive, because I can hold my breath about as long as I can hold a tune. And someone who could lie convincingly to harbor officials, because the moment Aldous's decree reached the local magistrate, the Serel district would be legally his, and trespassing on a magnate's wreck carries penalties I'd rather read about than experience.

I owned a boat. I owned lights and rope. I owned three years of maps and a head full of gradients. What I didn't own was lungs, lies, or luck.

That's when I started making the list. You know the kind of list. The kind where every name on it is a person you owe an apology to in advance.

* * *

Dawn came up grey over the valley, the way it always does here, like the sky reporting for duty without enthusiasm. I packed the manifest in oilcloth and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where I could feel it when I breathed. Ridiculous. It was a piece of wet paper. It was also the first evidence in three years that Tomas had existed anywhere other than inside my skull, and I treated it accordingly.

Before I left, I did something I hadn't done since the war. I took down the photograph.

It lives in the bottom drawer of the survey table, under the spare nibs, face-down, because face-up it looks at you. Two young men on a locomotive footplate, squinting into sun. The engineer is me, thirty pounds heavier and certain the world ran on timetables. The fireman is Tomas, grinning like the firebox owed him money, one hand on the firedoor, coal dust to the elbows. Our mother took it the day I qualified him on the Vellmark branch. She said, *Look after each other.* I said, *Always.* The war said nothing at all. The war just took its half of the bargain and left me holding mine.

"I'm coming," I told the photograph. It didn't answer. Photographs never do. But I put it in my breast pocket anyway, opposite the manifest, so that my brother rode over my heart and his name rode under it, and if that's sentiment, then let the record show the engineer was sentimental and paid for it later.

Then I sat down with the maps one more time and did the arithmetic properly, because wishing doesn't move tonnage and neither does grief.

Route in: the old service spur off the closed Vellmark line, up the Serel gorge to the lower portal. The spur's last surveyed condition — my survey, last autumn — was passable to a handcar for six kilometers, then washout, then walkable. Two days' travel with gear if the weather held, three if it didn't. Weather wouldn't hold. Storm systems were stacking up over the divide like creditors outside a debtor's door.

Route under: the lower bore, flooded from the portal seal inward. The water level in the tunnels sat, per my gauges, eleven meters below the crown of the bore at the saddle — which meant air space, a long black throat of it, navigable by boat for perhaps two kilometers past the seal before the bore dipped through the sag curve and went fully under. Past the sag, divers' territory. Cold, dark, zero visibility once you stirred the silt, and a ceiling that hadn't been inspected by anyone but bats and ghosts since the war.

And somewhere in that drowned dark, tilted across the ballast like a dropped toy, the Meridian Star. With a fireman's name on her crew list and — if the river people's stories had any ore in them — a fortune in her mail car.

Five days. One washed-out spur. Two kilometers of black water. Eleven meters of diving I didn't know how to do. Against a magnate with a fleet, a decree, and presumably divers who'd actually dived before.

Any reasonable man would take the manifest to the authorities and let institutions handle it. I considered this for nearly four seconds. Then I remembered that institutions were the ones who'd flooded the mountain in the first place, and that Corvane Aldous owned the kind of men authorities have lunch with.

No. This was a job for strays and thieves and anyone desperate enough to say yes.

I tore a page from the back of the ledger — the mileage ledger, the one that had measured my whole hollow era — and wrote a list. Not distances this time. Names.

Sela. Dockside smuggler, Greyharbor. Owes me for a favor involving a locked customs shed and a very confused night watchman. Can lie, pilot, and steal, often simultaneously.

Osk Brann. Deserter, last heard of diving for salvage out of Marnsk. Can hold his breath longer than I can hold a grudge, which is saying something.

Pip Wren. Apprentice, sixteen, knows engines the way priests know scripture and has the survival instincts of a moth at a lantern festival. Will follow me anywhere, which is precisely the problem.

Three names. Three people who'd each lost a war in their own private way. I looked at the list and felt the strangest sensation, one I hadn't felt since the footplate photograph: not hope exactly. Hope is a passenger. This was more like steam building in a boiler you'd written off as cold — pressure where there'd been nothing, wanting somewhere to go.

For three years I had measured my life in miles because miles don't ask questions. Miles don't look at you across a breakfast table and ask why you're still wearing mourning for a man with no grave. Miles just accumulate, honest and dumb, and you tell yourself forward motion is the same thing as living.

But a mile is just a unit. And units exist to measure something else.

I folded the list into my pocket with the photograph and the manifest, banked the lamp, and stepped out into the grey morning. The river was high and loud and going somewhere, and for the first time since the war ended, so was I.

Six tides. Twelve chances for the sea to change its mind. I meant to beat it there by three.

What I didn't know yet — what no amount of map-reading could have told me — was that Aldous had already bought the informant who'd sell us all out, and that the race I was planning had been handicapped before I took my first step.

But that's getting ahead of the story. First I had to go to Greyharbor and convince three broken people to follow a madman into a mountain.

The tide, as they say, waits for no man.

Fortunately, neither do I.
