## 5. Crew of Strays

### Freya

I'd always assumed recruiting would be the hardest part of any mad plan. It turns out mad plans recruit themselves, provided you're willing to stand in the right doorway long enough.

The doorway in question belonged to the Saltgull, a tavern in Greyharbor that smelled of tar, herring, and the particular despair of a town that had watched its shipping trade die twice — once in the war, once in the peace that followed. I'd been standing there for the better part of an hour, working through my third cup of something the barman called coffee the way a man might call a landslide a renovation, when Sela found me.

She found me the way a current finds a hull: inevitably, and from an angle I hadn't planned for.

"You're the one asking about divers," she said, sliding onto the bench across from me without invitation. She was small, quick-eyed, with a scar running through one eyebrow like a signature she'd stopped bothering to explain. "And rail lines. And the Serel tunnels. And you asked all of this in a town where Corvane Aldous owns the harbormaster, the constable, and at least two of the priests."

"I asked quietly."

"You asked like a man who's never had to ask quietly before. Engineers." She said the word the way other people said *children* or *dogs* — with fond exasperation. "I'm Sela. I run cargo. Some of it is on manifests."

"I need a pilot who knows the upper Serel reach. The channels have shifted since the war."

"Everything's shifted since the war. That's rather the point of me." She turned her cup over, examined the bottom, and set it right again. "Here's my situation, Voss. I've got two debts — one to a lender in Greyharbor who expresses his displeasure through furniture, and one to a man in Karelvo who expresses his through fire. The second one's the reason I can't take honest work, and the first one's the reason I can't afford dishonest work that pays slowly. What are you offering?"

"A share of whatever's in the Meridian Star."

I'll give her this: she didn't laugh. She looked at me for a long moment with those quick eyes doing arithmetic I couldn't follow, and then she said, "You actually believe it's down there."

"I've got a manifest with the seal of Karelvo's treasury on it and a wreck location that matches the old survey charts. I believe it enough to be dangerous."

"Good. Believing it enough to be safe would just get us both hanged for trespassing on Aldous's decree." She stuck out her hand across the table. Small, calloused, grip like a mooring line coming taut. "One condition. I pick the diver. I'm not trusting my life underwater to anyone I haven't at least seen drunk."

"Fair."

"And the apprentice — you'll need one. No man can run a winch, hold a lamp, and keep his own head above water all at once, no matter what the ballads say."

"I hadn't planned on ballads."

"You'd planned on nothing. That's the trouble with engineers. You think the world's a blueprint." She drained my coffee, made a face that suggested the landslide had gotten worse, and stood. "Osk Brann. Barracks row, third door with the paint peeling. Tell him the tide's turning and he'll know. And for the apprentice — there's a girl who sleeps in the stationmaster's office at the dead line depot. Pip Wren. She's been keeping the clocks wound on a line that hasn't run in three years."

"Why?"

Sela shrugged, already moving toward the door. "Same reason you walk the closed lines, I'd expect. Ask her yourself."

I did ask her, two days later, and she gave me an answer I didn't expect and have never forgotten. But I'm getting ahead of the ledger.

### Osk

The man came to my door and said *the tide's turning*, and for a moment I thought the war had finally come to collect.

That was the countersign, you understand. Last time I heard it, it was spoken by a lieutenant with a map of the Karelvo coast and a promise that the navy would cover our withdrawal. The navy did not cover our withdrawal. I spent nine hours in the water that night, and I came out of it the only man of my section who came out of it at all, and the army's response was to note in my file that I had been *absent without leave during an unauthorized tactical withdrawal* — which is the army's way of saying they were sorry, and it was my fault.

So when Freya Voss stood in my doorway with rain running off his coat and said the old words, I did what any sensible deserter would do.

I tried to close the door.

He put his boot in it. Engineers. Built like their bridges — stubborn, over-engineered, and liable to fail catastrophically under unexpected load.

"I need a diver," he said. "Deep water. Cold water. A wreck under Mount Serel."

"Then you need a corpse with certificates. Try the fleet."

"The fleet works for Aldous."

"The fleet works for whoever pays. That's what a fleet is." I got the door as far as his boot would allow, which was about a hand's width, and we regarded each other through the gap like two men arguing over a fence neither owned. "I know who you are, Voss. The line-walker. You've been measuring trestles on the Serel branch for three years like a man counting rosary beads. Everyone on the river knows you. Everyone on the river also knows what happens to people who get between Aldous and something shiny."

"And yet you're still talking to me through this door."

I had no answer for that, which he probably knew, which is the most annoying kind of silence.

The truth — and I've had three years to get comfortable with the truth, it being the only thing I own outright — is that I miss the water. That's the obscene joke of it. Nine hours drowning and I miss it. There's a quiet down there that the surface never manages, even in the dead line depots and the empty barracks and the rooms where deserters drink alone. Down there, nothing is pretending. The pressure is honest. The dark is honest. Everything up here is a man in a uniform telling you the withdrawal is authorized.

"I'll want half up front," I said, opening the door.

"I haven't got half of anything."

"Then I'll want a reason. A real one. Not treasure — treasure's a reason for Sela, she's built for it. I mean a reason you'd dive for if the chests came up empty."

He was quiet a while. Rain ticked off the eaves. Somewhere down the row a woman was singing to a child, a Karelvi lullaby, the kind that ends in a minor key because all our lullabies do.

"My brother was crew on the Star," he said finally. "Tomas Voss. Fireman. He's been missing three years, and the river just handed me his name on a manifest, and I intend to find out which column he belongs in — the saved or the lost. I'd dive for that if the wreck were full of ballast stones."

Well. There it was. I'd have preferred a lie, honestly. Lies you can work with.

"Third door was the right one," I said, and went to pack my kit.

### Pip

The man from the closed lines came to the depot on a Tuesday, because of course he did. Everything important in my life has happened on a Tuesday. I've checked. It's not superstition, it's record-keeping.

I knew who he was before he said his name. Freya Voss walks the Serel branch every few weeks, and I've watched him from the signal box more times than I'd admit under oath — a tall, gray man with a ledger under his arm, checking trestles that haven't carried a train since before I was old enough to be trusted with one. The other kids in Greyharbor used to say he was a ghost. I used to say ghosts don't write things down. Ghosts don't need to.

"You're Pip Wren," he said, in the doorway of the stationmaster's office, which is my office, in the sense that I sleep in it and have moved the desk to suit myself.

"Yes."

"You keep the clocks wound."

"Someone has to. A line's not dead just because the trains stopped." I'd rehearsed better arguments than that, but he was looking at the clocks — three of them, all ticking, all set to the old timetable — and I lost my nerve a little. "It's maintenance. If they ever reopen the line, the schedules have to mean something. You can't just start a railway from nothing. The time has to have been kept."

He looked at me for a long moment. People usually look at me for a short moment and then pat me on the head, which I permit because I'm small and it's easier than fighting, but this was different. This was the look you give a colleague.

"Where'd you learn engines?"

"Apprenticed. Before the closure. Driver Hask, may he rest, said I had good hands and a bad attitude, and that the combination makes a fine engineer provided the hands stay humble." I held them out, palms up, so he could inspect the calluses. "I can fire a locomotive, read a gauge, sweat a joint, and I know the Serel branch better than you do, because I've memorized the gradient tables and you just walk it."

"I doubt that."

"Gradient's four percent from the tunnel mouth to the watershed, with a ruling curve at the trestle over the Kess that you'd take at no more than twelve miles an hour even if the trestle were new, which it isn't, because the third span has a crack in the lower chord that you've been writing down in that ledger for three years instead of telling anyone, because there's no one to tell."

Now he looked at me the way people look at a gauge that's reading something impossible.

"You read my ledger?"

"You leave it in the signal box when it rains. I dust the signal box." I folded my arms. "I want in on whatever it is. And before you ask why — it's because everyone I've ever worked for is gone, and every engine I've ever fired is rusting, and I am sixteen years old and the most useful thing I do is wind clocks. I'd rather die on a live line than rust on a dead one. Also I want a share, but the line thing first, because it's the true reason and you seem like a man who sorts the true reasons from the paid ones."

He sat down slowly on the stationmaster's chair, which I'd have charged him for if he'd asked permission.

"How old did you say you were?"

"Old enough to have memorized the gradient tables. Ask me the ruling curve at the Kess again."

### Sela

Here's what nobody tells you about assembling a crew: it's like loading a boat. Everything's fine until you put the fourth thing aboard, and then the whole arrangement develops opinions.

We took my boat, the *Kindly Tern*, upriver on the evening tide — me at the tiller, Voss navigating with his nose in a chart, the deserter sitting in the bow like a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy insufficient, and the girl in the engine well asking Osk questions about his dive kit with the cheerful relentlessness of a cat investigating a locked drawer.

"So you can hold your breath for how long?"

"Long enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I give for free."

"See, that's exactly the sort of thing a man says when the real answer is embarrassing. Voss, is his real answer embarrassing?"

"Leave the man alone, Pip."

"I'm establishing baseline competence. On a crew, you want to know who's competent and who's just confident. I'm competent. He might be confident."

Osk looked at me over the girl's head with an expression of profound appeal, and I grinned at him and gave the tiller a nudge to bring us round the marker at the mouth of the Kess. It was the first time in two years I'd been on the water with a full crew and a cargo worth the name, and I'll confess the feeling surprised me. It felt like before. It felt like the war hadn't happened and the lender didn't exist and the man in Karelvo was just a story smugglers tell each other to keep the young ones honest.

I didn't trust the feeling. Feelings on the water are like weather — useful to note, fatal to believe.

We made camp that night in the lee of the old Serel branch embankment, because Voss wanted to start surveying at first light and because the embankment kept the wind off. Osk built the fire with the economy of a man who'd built ten thousand of them in worse places. Pip produced, from some bottomless interior pocket, tea, bread, a jar of pickled something, and a small wrench, which she set out on a cloth with the ceremony of a field surgeon laying out instruments.

"Where'd you get all that?" Osk asked.

"I packed."

"You packed before you were hired?"

"I was hired the moment I recited the ruling curve at the Kess. Everything before that was negotiation theater." She poured the tea. "You two were both soldiers, yes? I can tell. You sit facing the dark and you put your boots where you can find them."

Osk and I looked at each other across the fire. It's a strange thing, being identified as a type by a child. We were both quiet a moment, and I think we were both thinking about the same night, the same coast, the same water — though he was in it and I was offshore in a hold full of contraband, listening to the guns and doing the arithmetic of whether to run.

"I was a runner," I said, because someone had to break it. "Cargo, mostly. Sometimes people. Toward the end, mostly people, and the cargo was whatever they could carry. I got good at reading water at night with the lights out." I turned my cup in my hands. "The last night, the evacuation routes were wrong. Everyone said so afterward — the guides, the gunners, everyone. The boats went out the channel and the channel was covered. I lost people I'd promised things to." I looked up. "So when a man tells me he's chasing a wreck that went down with Karelvo's treasury aboard, my first question isn't *how much*. It's *why was she on that route at all*."

The fire popped. Down the embankment, the river moved in the dark, patient as a creditor.

"The Star was on the Serel line because it was supposed to be safe," Voss said quietly. "That's what I intend to find out. Whether it was safe, and who knew it wasn't."

"And if it turns out somebody sold the route?"

"Then I'll want names."

"Good," said Osk, into the fire. "I'll want faces."

"I'll want the ledger," said Pip, and we all looked at her, and she shrugged. "Evidence keeps better written down. Also I just think it's the right tool. Everything's the right tool if you're methodical."

I lay back against my pack and looked up at the sky, where the clouds were breaking apart over Serel's black shoulder, and I thought: *four of us. A line-walker with a dead brother, a deserter who misses drowning, a child who keeps time for trains that don't run, and a smuggler with two debts and a talent for reading water at night.* If I'd been assembling a crew for the job, I'd have picked different people. Better people. People with fewer holes in them.

But then, holes are where the river gets in. And we were going where the river had been.

"First light," Voss said. "We survey the tunnel mouth."

"First light," we said — Osk like a man reporting for duty one more time, Pip like a girl winding a clock, and me, God help me, like a woman who believed in something again.

Above us, on the shoulder of the mountain, something glinted — there and gone, quick as a signal lamp. Osk saw it too. I watched his hand drift, casual as anything, to the knife at his belt, and I marked the spot on the ridge, and I said nothing, and neither did he.

Not yet. Some things you check the tide on first.
