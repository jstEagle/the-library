## 6. The Closed Line

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head because paper burns and heads mostly don't — that when Freya Voss said "it's a simple job," I knew we were all going to die.

He said it at dawn, standing at the mouth of the old Serel branch tunnel with his hands on his hips like a man admiring a garden instead of a hole in a mountain that hadn't seen daylight in eleven years. Behind him, Sela was loading her pistol for no reason anyone could identify. Osk Brann stood apart, arms crossed, radiating the particular silence of a man who had already decided how he'd die and found it acceptable.

"Simple," Freya said again. "The portal's sealed with a steel bulkhead. The line beyond is intact as far as the second ventilation shaft. We open the door, walk in, and come out above the flooded galleries under Mount Serel."

"And what's between here and there?" I asked.

"Eleven years of neglect."

"Neglect," Osk repeated, in the tone other men reserve for words like *plague* or *ex-wife*.

Freya smiled. It was the smile of a man who had spent three years alone in the mountains talking to trestles, and I recognized it, because I'd worn one like it myself after the conscription rolls took my father's mill and gave back nothing. It's the smile of someone who has made an arrangement with grief: *you stay quiet, and I'll keep working.*

So we went into the mountain.

* * *

Let me explain who I am, since apparently I'm telling this story. Pip Wren. Apprentice engineer, formerly of the Greyharbor works, currently of nowhere, which is where everyone in this crew is from. I'm nineteen, small enough to fit in places grown men can't, which Freya called an asset and I call the entire reason they hired me. My war debt is ordinary: a dead father, a burned town, a skill set nobody pays for anymore because the railroads are closed and the rivers are full of salvage men.

That's the thing about this crew. Nobody joined for adventure. Sela runs cargo that doesn't exist on manifests. Osk deserted from a navy that no longer has a country to be loyal to. Freya measures his life in miles because miles don't ask him questions about the years. And me — I just wanted to be near working machinery again, even if the machinery was dead and buried.

We spent the morning at the bulkhead. It was a steel plate, riveted shut, with a Karelvo customs seal still legible through the rust — a double-headed river bird, wings spread over crossed keys. Freya ran his thumb across it the way you'd touch a gravestone.

"They sealed these lines the week after the armistice," he said. "Officially to prevent smuggling. Unofficially so nobody would go looking at what the last trains carried."

"You think Tomas came through here?" Sela asked. She'd learned by then that mentioning the brother was permitted, but only sideways, only while working.

"I think every train to Serel came through here." Freya set down his pack and started pulling tools. "Which means if he's anywhere, he's past this door."

Osk and I drilled out the rivets while Freya directed from behind us, calling corrections in the flat voice he used for anything emotional. Left a quarter-turn. Mind the shear. The drill bit wandered once and caught my knuckle, and I swore with a vocabulary my mother would have charged me rent for, and Osk — without looking up — passed me a rag.

It was the first time he'd handed me anything that wasn't an order or a warning.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Osk Brann before that day: that he'd been a fleet diver, that he'd walked away from Corvane Aldous's employ with nothing but his gear and a face like a locked door, and that Sela kept a knife within reach whenever he sat downwind of her. He spoke maybe forty words a day, and half of them were depth measurements.

Here is what I learned about him inside the mountain: none of that mattered, compared to what his hands knew.

The bulkhead came free at midday with a groan like the mountain clearing its throat, and the air that breathed out of the tunnel was cold and mineral and old. Freya went first with the lamp, because of course he did; the man had a genius for being exactly where the danger was most interested in being.

Inside, the line was beautiful. That sounds mad, but I'm an engineer's apprentice, and I'll tell you what beauty is: a tunnel eleven years abandoned whose drainage channels still run true, whose brickwork still carries the arch load the way the drawings promised, whose rails gleam faintly under the lamp where condensation has polished them. Somebody built this right. Somebody built it for trains that would run for a hundred years, and it got nine.

Freya touched everything. Wall, rail, signal bracket, the ghost of a chalk mark on the tunnel wall — a route number, 7-K, faded to a whisper. He moved through the dark like a man walking through his own house with the furniture rearranged.

"Water table's higher than the surveys said," Osk observed somewhere behind us. His voice carried strangely in the tunnel, flattened by stone. "Listen."

We listened. Under our footsteps, beneath the silence, there was a sound like slow breathing — water moving somewhere below the ballast, patient as interest accruing.

"The galleries under Serel are already drowned," Freya said. "This section should be dry. Should be."

"*Should be*," Sela muttered, "is going on my headstone."

* * *

The trouble started at the third support gallery, four hundred meters in.

You have to understand the construction. The old engineers didn't line the whole tunnel in brick — too expensive — so at intervals they cut wider chambers and shored them with timber frames, oak posts and cross-beams, meant to hold until the rock settled and then be forgotten. Eleven years of forgetting later, those frames were holding up a mountain on courtesy alone.

Freya stopped at the mouth of the third gallery and held up the lamp, and I watched his face do something complicated.

"That left post is split," he said quietly.

"It's been split for years," I said, because I could see the crack running up the grain like a lightning scar. "It's holding."

"Holding isn't standing." He crouched, pressed his palm against the timber, listened to it the way Osk listened to water. "The load's transferred to the frame behind it. If we go through quiet, single file, hugging the right wall—"

"If we go through at all, we're idiots," Sela said. "There's another way around?"

"No."

"Then say idiot louder, engineer, because I want it in writing."

But she went anyway. That was the thing about Sela I was starting to understand: she complained the way other people prayed. It wasn't meant to change anything. It was meant to keep her soul in practice.

Freya went first. Then Sela. Then me, because I was smallest and he wanted me where he could hear me. Osk took the rear with the spare lamp and most of the gear, and I remember thinking, as I edged along the right wall with my shoulder brushing cold rock, that the arrangement put the two people least likely to save each other at opposite ends of the line.

The gallery was maybe thirty meters long. I was halfway across when I heard it.

Not a crack. A sigh. Timber doesn't break loud, not at first; it exhales, fibers letting go of each other one by one, and if you've ever stood next to a dying beam you know that sound in your spine before your ears catch it. I froze. Ahead of me, Freya's lamp swung around.

"Pip. Move. Now."

I moved. That much I'll give myself. I moved directly toward him, which was the wrong direction, because the left post — the split one, the one we'd discussed, the one the mountain had been politely ignoring — chose that moment to stop being polite.

The frame let go with a report like a cannon, and the ceiling came down in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Dust hit first, a solid wall of it, and then rock, and then the world turned into noise and weight. I felt a slab clip my shoulder and spin me, felt the floor come up, felt the lamp go out of someone's hand and burst somewhere close, and then I was on my stomach in the dark with thirty tons of Mount Serel settling onto the place where I had very recently been breathing.

The dust was total. You couldn't see a hand in front of your face. I couldn't tell if I was screaming until I heard myself doing it.

Then a hand closed on my ankle.

Not gently. Osk Brann did not do gentle; gentle was for men who had time. He hauled me backward through the rubble by the leg, blind, both of us coughing, and when the dust thinned enough to show shapes I saw what he'd seen and I hadn't: the collapse hadn't sealed the tunnel ahead of us. It had sealed it *behind* us — and the gap Freya and Sela had cleared through to the far side was now a chimney of falling stone, and I had been lying in the middle of it.

There was a second exhalation from overhead. Smaller. A crack propagating through the surviving frame, deciding whether the rest of the gallery would follow its brother down.

Osk looked up at it. Then he picked me up — all of me, like luggage, like I weighed nothing, which I don't but which no one had ever treated as irrelevant before — and ran.

We came out the far end of the gallery as the rest of the frame finished its argument with gravity. The boom of it followed us like applause.

* * *

Afterward we sat in the dry stretch beyond, five lamps' worth of light pooled together, everyone coughing dust into their sleeves. Freya checked my shoulder, probed the bruise with clinical fingers, pronounced it "not broken, badly bruised, keep it moving." Sela pressed a flask into my hands and told me to drink, and I did, and it was terrible, and I loved her for it.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The mountain said plenty. Somewhere behind the fresh collapse, water was already finding new paths, running faster than it had that morning, filling the spaces we'd made.

"He saved you," Freya said finally. Not to me. To Osk.

Osk shrugged, which on him was a speech.

"You didn't have to go back in."

"Yes, I did." He said it without drama, the way he reported depths. "She was in the fall zone. I was nearest."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

Sela snorted. "Navy man pulls apprentice out of a rockfall and calls it arithmetic."

"What would you call it?" Osk asked her, and it was the first question I'd ever heard him ask anyone.

She looked at him for a long moment, weighing something. "Luck," she said at last. "Yours and hers. Don't spend it all in one tunnel."

But something had shifted, and everyone felt it. Trust isn't a decision, I learned that day. It's a debt you incur without signing anything. Osk had paid something for me in that gallery — not blood, but the next thing down from it, the willingness — and whatever ledger Sela kept on him had a new column now, and the column wasn't empty.

As for me: I'd gone into the mountain afraid of it, the way you're afraid of weather, impersonally. I came out afraid of it personally, intimately, having felt its weight try to make me part of itself. But underneath the fear was something else, something I didn't have a name for yet. When Osk's hand closed on my ankle in the dark, my first feeling hadn't been terror. It had been certainty. Absolute, unearned, stupid certainty that the hand belonged to someone who would not let go.

You can build a family out of less than that. I've seen it done with nothing at all.

* * *

We camped that night in a maintenance alcove off the main bore, a brick-lined pocket built for track crews, big enough for four bedrolls and a stove the size of a hatbox. Freya wouldn't allow a fire at first — bad air, bad idea — until Osk tested the draft with a candle and declared the ventilation shaft alive, drawing, breathing for us. So we had heat, and heat meant we had talk, and talk meant the crew stopped being four strangers sharing a disaster and became something else, though none of us would have said the word.

Sela told a story about running contraband insulin upriver during the embargo, lying to a patrol boat with such conviction that the patrol captain ended up apologizing to *her*. Osk, coaxed by darkness and exhaustion, described the bottom of Greyharbor harbor — the drowned ferry district, streets of it, streetlamps still standing, fish moving through the windows of a schoolhouse. "Pretty," he said, "if you can forget what it used to be."

And Freya — Freya talked about the line. Not the wreck, not Tomas, not Aldous. The line. How the 7-K branch was engineered to fall two feet per mile, precisely, so the drains would never need pumping. How the man who designed it signed the drawings *E. Halloran, Chief, Karelvo State Railways*, and how Halloran had probably died in the evacuation without knowing his drains were still working, still faithful, eleven years after the country that employed him had stopped existing.

"Countries end," Freya said, staring into the stove. "Good work doesn't have to."

I fell asleep to the sound of water moving through Halloran's drains, steady as a pulse, and dreamed of nothing at all, which after the day I'd had was luxury.

I woke once, deep in the night, to voices. Freya and Osk, keeping watch, speaking low.

"Why her?" Osk asked. "The apprentice. You could have hired muscle."

"She fits places," Freya said. "And she reads drawings better than men twice her age. And—" a pause, long enough that I nearly gave away that I was awake, "—she looks at broken things like they're worth fixing. I need somebody on this crew who believes that's possible."

Silence. Then Osk, quietly: "Do you believe it?"

Another pause. Longer.

"I believe in the work," Freya said. "Go sleep, Brann. Your watch is done."

I lay there in the dark with my bruised shoulder and my hammering heart, and I thought about the gallery, and the sighing timber, and the hand on my ankle, and I understood that I had just heard the truest thing anyone had said all day: *she looks at broken things like they're worth fixing.*

Because so did he. That was the whole expedition, wasn't it? One broken man going into a mountain to dig out another broken man, three years drowned, and calling it engineering.

In the morning we broke camp and walked on toward the flooded galleries, and the tunnel grew colder, and the sound of water grew louder, and by the time the rails ahead of us vanished into black standing water — dead calm, mirror-flat, holding our lamplight like a held breath — I had stopped asking whether we'd survive this.

I'd started wondering what the water was keeping down there, and why, when Freya raised his lamp against the reflection, his face went white as the chalk marks on the wall.

"There," he whispered. "On the waterline. Do you see it?"

I did. We all did. Scratched into the tunnel wall, just above the flood line, in a hand that shook with cold or fear or both:

*IT WAS NEVER GOLD. T.V.*
