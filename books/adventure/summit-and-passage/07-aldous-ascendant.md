## 7. Aldous Ascendant

There is a particular expression a bureaucrat makes when he realizes the money on the table is real. It is not greed — greed arrives later, once the man has had time to rehearse his appetite in private. What you see first is something closer to terror. The face of a man who has spent thirty years being paid in favors and titles, suddenly confronted with a number that could buy the office he sits in, the building around it, and the street it stands on.

Minister-Adjunct Havel Ferro was wearing that face now, across the walnut desk in my stateroom aboard the *Corvane's Pride*, and I confess I was enjoying it the way one enjoys a well-made clock: for its precision.

"Mr. Aldous," he said, "this figure — it exceeds the entire annual budget of the Interior Ministry."

"Yes," I said. "That was rather the point."

I let him sit with it. On the desk between us lay a leather folio containing a salvage decree signed by three officials who had not yet been bribed, which is to say it was worthless paper awaiting my signature to make it valuable. Ferro was the third. He had come out to the dredger that morning under the pretext of inspecting safety compliance, which told me he'd already been shopping me to his colleagues and wanted to see what he was selling before he sold it. A sensible man. Sensible men are the easiest to buy, because they have already done the arithmetic themselves; you merely confirm their answer.

"You understand," Ferro said, and here came the ritual, the little dance of conscience every official performs so he can tell himself afterward that he resisted, "that the Meridian Star sank in Karelvan waters. That nation no longer exists, but the treaty framework—"

"The treaty framework," I said, "was drafted by men who assumed Karelvo would still exist to honor it. It didn't. The river doesn't care whose flag the wreck flew. Neither, I suspect, do you."

He flinched. Good. I find that a small cruelty early in a negotiation saves an hour of posturing later.

"I served Karelvo," Ferro said quietly. "I signed the evacuation orders myself."

I set down my pen. This was interesting. Not the confession — half the officials on this coast served Karelvo, and most of them wear the fact like a scar they show at dinner parties — but the way he said it. Not as a credential. As a wound.

"And how did that go for you?" I asked.

"They gave me a commendation." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "And a posting far from the border, where I wouldn't have to look at the river."

"Ferro." I leaned back. Outside the stateroom's salt-fogged glass, the Greyharbor fleet lay at anchor — six hulls, cranes folded like sleeping herons, my name painted on each bow in letters visible from a mile off. Vanity? Perhaps. But vanity is only advertising with better posture. "You want to know why I'm spending more than your ministry's annual budget pulling a dead train out of a drowned mountain. Everyone wants to know. They assume gold. They assume I know something about the cargo manifest that the archives lost. And because they assume treasure, they assume greed, and because they assume greed, they assume I can be bought or frightened like any other greedy man."

"Are those assumptions incorrect?"

"Entirely." I smiled at him, and watched him decide, in real time, whether the smile made him safer or less safe. That ambiguity is the whole art of power, if you're wondering. Certainty comforts people; uncertainty opens wallets. "What I want, Minister-Adjunct, is for the past to be mine. All of it. Every document, every record, every sealed case that went down with that train. Because there are men in this world who believe history belongs to whoever writes it down, and I have never been content to let other men hold the pen."

He studied me for a long moment. Then he said, "You were in the government too. The last one."

Ah. So he *had* done his research. I'd wondered when it would surface; everyone always digs eventually, and I prefer they do it early, while I'm present to curate what they find.

"I was Undersecretary for Transport," I said. "The youngest in Karelvo's history. I signed the order that put the Meridian Star on that final run."

Ferro went very still. "You knew her route."

"I designed her route."

* * *

Let me tell you about the last government of Karelvo, since everyone who serves me will learn it eventually and I would rather they hear it from me than from an enemy.

Karelvo in its final year was a country being eaten alive by its own maps. The front had collapsed twice; the capital was burning coal it didn't have; and the ministries ran on a currency of rumor, because rumor was the only thing still circulating reliably. I was thirty-one years old and running the transport portfolio, which in wartime meant I decided which trains carried soldiers and which carried flour, and both decisions killed people, and I slept fine. I mention that not as bravado but as biography. Some men are broken by such work. I was calibrated by it. There is a difference, and the difference is me.

The Meridian Star was the pride of the northern line — a luxury express we kept running through the war partly for morale and partly because the foreign press loved photographing it, gilded and absurd, steaming through a starving country like a diamond on a corpse. In the last winter, when the enemy guns reached the mountains, the government decided to evacuate. Not the people — the people were already walking out on frozen roads, and God rest them, most of them didn't make it. We evacuated *paper*. Treaties, ledgers, correspondence, the diplomatic cases from four embassies, the evidence files of a dozen investigations into our own corruption. The Meridian Star's last run was scheduled to carry all of it over Mount Serel and into exile, where it would become someone else's history.

I signed the routing order myself. I chose the tunnel line beneath Serel because it was the only one the artillery hadn't reached.

Three days later, the enemy shelled the pass anyway. The tunnel flooded. The Star went down inside the mountain with everything aboard, and the government lasted another eleven weeks before the flag came down for good, and the river closed its account, and the world moved on to newer atrocities.

Now. Here is the question I have spent fifteen years refusing to answer honestly, and I will pose it to you instead, since you've been patient: did I sell that route?

Because that is the story going around certain circles — that Corvane Aldous, young genius of the transport ministry, took enemy money and fed them the Serel tunnel coordinates, and the shelling that drowned the Meridian Star was not fortune but invoice. There are men who believe it. There is a journalist in Greyharbor who has written two columns about it, both carefully deniable, both accurate enough to sting. And there is a version of events — my version, the one I tell at dinners when I've had enough wine to be charming and not enough to be careless — in which the shelling was coincidence, tragedy, war, and I am merely the man who has spent a fortune trying to recover his country's drowned soul.

Which version is true?

I'll tell you this much: I know exactly what was in those diplomatic cases. I inventoried them personally. And among them were documents that would hang a great many men who are currently respected — including, if the archive clerks did their jobs, documents concerning a certain undersecretary's private correspondence with parties across the lines. Whether that correspondence was treason or insurance depends entirely on who is holding the pen. I intend to hold the pen. That is not greed. Greed wants things. I want *authorship*. There is no price for that, which is why no one can buy me, and no threat against it, which is why no one can frighten me. Ferro understood none of this, but he understood the number on the table, and the number was sufficient.

He signed. Of course he signed. They always sign.

* * *

My chief of operations, Dain Merek, was waiting for me on the aft deck when I came out, and I knew before he spoke that the news was bad, because Dain has two postures: relaxed, meaning nothing is wrong, and extremely relaxed, meaning something is badly wrong and he is deciding how to survive telling me.

"Problem?" I asked.

"Vosk," he said.

Ah. Vosk. My survey chief, twenty years in my employ, a competent man with a widow's pension and a gambling habit I had tolerated because competence is rare and habits are cheap. Two nights ago, Vosk had gotten drunk in Greyharbor and told a woman — a dockworker, by the reports, though I suspected she was more than that — that the fleet wasn't after gold at all, that Aldous wanted the mail car specifically, that there were papers down there worth killing for.

"He questioned the mission," Dain said. "Publicly. To strangers."

"He speculated about the mission. Questioning implies thought." I looked out at the water. Gulls were working the wake of a supply skiff, screaming their idiot hearts out. "Who is the woman?"

"No name yet. Dark hair, river accent, works the east docks. Vosk says she bought him the brandy."

Someone buying information with four coppers of liquor and getting a fortune in return. I admired the economy. I made a note to have her found — not harmed, merely *found*, because a person who trades that efficiently is either an asset or a liability and I don't leave the classification to chance.

"And Vosk?"

Dain hesitated. That was the real report. Twenty years, and now hesitation.

"He's asking to be reassigned. Says the dive schedule is reckless. Says the tunnels under Serel are unstable and we'll lose divers. Says—" Dain stopped.

"Says what, Dain."

"Says he helped load the Star's mail car, back when. Says some cargo should stay drowned."

Well. There it was. I felt something move behind my ribs — not guilt; I had that surgically removed decades ago, along with several other expensive organs — but something adjacent. Recognition, maybe. Vosk had been there. Vosk had loaded the cases. Which meant Vosk knew the inventory, or part of it, which meant Vosk was now the second-most dangerous man on this coast, after me, and dangerous men who develop consciences are like unsecured cargo in heavy weather.

"Reassign him," I said.

Dain relaxed visibly. "To which vessel?"

"To the mainland. With severance. Generous severance — a year's pay, a letter of recommendation, my personal regards." I turned to look at him, and let him see my face do the thing it does. "And put a man on the ferry with him. If he talks to anyone else — a journalist, an official, a drunk in a tavern — the regards become regrets. Am I understood?"

"Understood, sir."

"Dain." I softened my voice, because the next part required warmth, and warmth delivered coldly is more effective than warmth delivered warmly. "Twenty years, you've served me. Have I ever once punished a man for honesty?"

"No, sir."

"For incompetence, yes. For disloyalty, catastrophically. But honesty — never. Vosk was honest. He'll be paid for it. But he was honest *loosely*, in public, to strangers, about matters that could cost me everything, and that is not honesty, that is leakage. Do you see the distinction?"

"I see it, sir."

"Good. See that Vosk sees it too. Gently. He's earned gentle."

Dain went to arrange it, and I stood alone at the rail watching Greyharbor smoke in the evening light, and I will admit to you — since you've come this far with me, and since no one aboard can hear — that I thought about Tomas Voss.

Not by choice. Memory is like that; it ambushes the powerful as readily as the poor, and with worse manners. Tomas Voss had been a junior clerk in my ministry, bright-eyed, hopeless at cards, brother to some railway engineer upcountry. It was Voss who had first brought me the rumor that the Meridian Star's diplomatic cases contained material implicating senior figures in the evacuation disaster — the routes, the timings, the thousands who died on the frozen roads because someone sold the schedule. He brought it to me because he trusted me. Because I was the reformer, the young undersecretary, the man who gave speeches about accountability.

I told him I would investigate. I told him to keep the originals safe — aboard the Star, ideally, where no committee could suppress them. And then the Star drowned, and Tomas Voss drowned with it, or so the record says, and the truth went down locked in a mail car beneath a mountain, and I have spent fifteen years and a considerable fortune making sure it stayed there.

Until now. Until the river shifted, and a fisherman found wreckage, and my survey ships confirmed the hull sitting intact in the flooded galleries under Serel like a coffin I'd ordered built and then forgotten to check.

Somewhere out there, apparently, there is a brother. An engineer who measures trestles and keeps ledgers. Dain's people had flagged the name weeks ago — Freya Voss, poking at the closed lines, asking questions in river towns. I had filed him under nuisance. I was beginning to suspect he belonged under threat, and the sooner I reclassified him, the cheaper the correction.

Below me, the tide was turning. Six tides, my divers said, until the Serel windows opened and the fleet could work the wreck. Six tides until the past surfaced, and I learned at last which of my stories was true — or, more precisely, until I ensured that whichever was true became irrelevant, because I would be the one telling it.

I took out the decree Ferro had signed and read it once more by the deck lamps. Salvage rights, exclusive and perpetual, granted to Corvane Aldous and heirs. The law, purchased. The river, chartered. The dead, subpoenaed.

"Six tides," I said to the water, and the water, being water, declined to negotiate.

Behind me, Dain returned with the evening reports and a peculiar expression — the look of a man carrying news he hasn't yet decided how to price.

"Sir. The east dock woman. We found her."

"Name?"

"Sela. She crews on a smuggler's boat." He paused. "And sir — she wasn't asking about the wreck for herself. Witnesses say she was asking on behalf of someone. An engineer. Keeps a ledger. Measures everything."

I smiled, and meant it, which is rarer than you'd think.

"Well," I said. "Family reunions. How the river does arrange them."

I gave the order to weigh anchor at dawn — two tides ahead of schedule — and somewhere below the darkening water, I liked to imagine, the Meridian Star heard me coming.
