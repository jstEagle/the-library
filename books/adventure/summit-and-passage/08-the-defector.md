## 8. The Defector

There are three ways to leave a ship like the *Corvane's Mercy*, and I had spent eleven days cataloguing all of them from the navigation deck like a woman pricing her own funeral.

The first was over the side — fast, wet, and fatal if the watch rotation caught you, which it would, because Aldous paid his lookouts in fear and fear keeps better hours than any wage. The second was with permission, which meant a shore assignment, which meant Aldous deciding you were more useful elsewhere, and Corvane Aldous did not decide things for other people's convenience. The third way was the one nobody talked about, because it required a reason worth dying for.

I found mine folded inside a chart tube in the master's cabin, and it had a name on it that made my hands go cold: *Voss, T.*

* * *

Let me tell you what it is to be navigator for Corvane Aldous, since history will ask, and I intend to answer before it does.

You stand at a table covered in paper worth more than the town it floats above, and you draw lines. The lines are always honest — soundings, currents, tide tables, the drowned geography of a river that has swallowed an entire borderland and keeps its secrets the way a miser keeps coins. The lines don't lie. It's what the lines are *for* that rots you.

Aldous came to me on the eleventh night out of Greyharbor, when we were anchored off the Serel flats waiting for the spring tide to build. He stood at my chart table without being invited, which he never did, and he set down a single sheet of paper — a crew manifest, water-stained, salvaged from God knew where — and he tapped one name with one finger.

"Find out who else carries that name," he said. "There's a railroad man working the closed lines. Voss. If he comes looking for this wreck, I want to know before he knows we're here."

"And if he doesn't come?"

Aldous smiled the way frost forms — gradually, and everywhere at once. "Then you've wasted an evening, Miss Reyne, and I've wasted nothing, because now I know how you handle uncertainty." He left the manifest on my table like a tip for a servant.

I should have burned it. That's what I tell myself at three in the morning, lying in my narrow bunk listening to the dredge engines breathe. I should have fed it to the stove and drawn my honest lines and kept drawing them until the money bought me a house so far from any river that I'd never hear water again.

Instead I read it. All of it. Every name on the Meridian Star's passenger list, every name on her crew, and there — third from the bottom of the engineering roster, ink faded but legible — *T. Voss, fireman.*

And beneath the manifest, tucked where only someone who handled charts for a living would think to look, a second document. A salvage order, countersigned by an official of the provisional government whose signature I recognized because I'd forged it twice already that season. It authorized recovery of "sealed diplomatic materials, priority absolute," and it said, in language no salvage order has any business using: *recovery personnel are not to survive disclosure.*

I sat with that sentence for a long time. Outside, the river moved against the hull with the patience of something that had already eaten a nation and wasn't finished.

Here is the arithmetic I did, and I did it honestly, because I'm a navigator and honesty in calculation is the one virtue they couldn't take from me: Corvane Aldous served in Karelvo's last government. I'd known that since Greyharbor; half the fleet knew it, and the half that knew admired him for it, the way men admire a wolf for getting fat in a hard winter. Karelvo's last government sold its own evacuation routes. Everyone in the borderlands knows the story — the trains sent down the valley roads while the gunners waited, thousands of souls marched into the killing ground by timetables printed in their own capital. I lost cousins to those timetables. So did everyone.

I had told myself Aldous was merely a carrion bird picking at the bones. Rich, ruthless, but merely hungry. The salvage order said otherwise. The salvage order said he wasn't scavenging the wreck — he was *burying* it, along with anyone who might testify to what it held.

So I did the arithmetic. Stay, and be complicit in murder number two, committed against whoever this railroad man Voss turned out to be, and against my own crewmates when the order reached its final clause. Or go, carrying the schedule, and put myself in the hands of strangers who had every reason to shoot a defector from the fleet on principle.

I've been called calculating my whole life, usually by men who meant it as an insult and usually while losing an argument to me. Fine. I calculated. And the sum that came out of it was this: I could not buy back what I'd helped Aldous find. But I could decide what it got used for.

That's not redemption. Don't let anyone sell you redemption; it's a currency that only circulates among people who've never needed it. What I wanted was simpler and harder — I wanted to stop being useful to him.

* * *

Getting off the *Mercy* took four nights of preparation and thirty seconds of execution, which is the correct ratio for most terrible ideas.

The fleet ran six vessels: two dredgers, the *Mercy* and the fat old *Bountiful Tide*; three patrol skiffs; and Aldous's barge, all brass and black lacquer, where he held court like a man auditioning for a throne nobody was offering. We lay anchored in the narrows below Mount Serel, and the skiffs ran a watch pattern I had drawn myself, which was either the great irony of my life or its one small mercy — because I knew exactly where the gaps were, having built them in.

I went over the side at slack water before dawn, in a rubber suit stolen from the dive stores, with a waterproof satchel strapped to my chest containing the schedule, the salvage order, and the manifest. I swam the gap between the second and third skiff during the change of watch, when the lanterns swing wide and the helmsmen yawn, and I came ashore on the shingle below the old Serel tramway like something the river coughed up.

Which, in fairness, is what I was.

The railroad man's camp was two miles upstream, in the lee of a collapsed signal box, and I walked toward it through the grey light feeling every year of my thirty-four and several I hadn't earned yet. I'd expected a fire, sentries, some sign of competence. What I found was a boy — sixteen at most, all elbows and engine grease — sitting up suddenly beside a dead fire with a wrench raised like a sword.

"Don't," I said, hands open. "I'm heavier than I look and you'd strain something."

"Who—"

"Ilsa Reyne. Navigator, Corvane Aldous's fleet, formerly of the Karelvo hydrographic service, currently unemployed as of about forty minutes ago." I set the satchel on the ground between us and stepped back from it, which is the oldest trick in the book and it worked, because the boy looked at the bag instead of me. "Wake your people. I brought something they'll want to see."

By the time the others came — and they came fast, moving quiet, which told me these were not tourists — I'd counted four of them in my head and assigned each a probability of shooting me. The smuggler woman, Sela, dark-haired, knife already loose in her hand: sixty percent, rising. The big diver, Osk Brann, whom I knew by reputation from the Greyharbor docks, a deserter with a navy tattoo half burned off his forearm: twenty percent, but only because he seemed tired rather than merciful. The engineer, Freya Voss himself, gaunt and grey-eyed with a ledger under his arm like a breviary: unknown. He had his brother's face around the eyes, and seeing it did something to my chest I hadn't budgeted for.

"You're the navigator," Voss said. Not a question. "The one who plotted the fleet's approach lanes."

"I am."

"Then you know every channel, every anchorage, every watch rotation they run."

"Yes."

Sela spat, delicately, to the side. "Then you also know how to sell all of it back to him the moment he doubles your price."

"Sela." Voss didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to; whatever he was, these people orbited him like iron filings around a lodestone, and I noted that with professional envy, because I'd spent years trying to make men follow me and had only ever managed to make them obey.

"She's right, though," I said. "It's the obvious objection. So let me save us all the hour it would take to say it politely." I crouched and opened the satchel, laying the documents on a flat stone in the dawn light. "This is Aldous's dive schedule. Spring tide, six days from now, first descent at slack before dawn. Four divers, hard-hat rigs, working the mail car of the Meridian Star specifically — not the strongroom, not the bullion vault. The mail car. Ask yourselves why a man spends a fortune mounting a salvage fleet to rob a train's *letters*."

Osk Brann picked up the salvage order and read it twice. His jaw did something complicated. "Recovery personnel are not to survive disclosure," he read aloud. "That includes his own divers."

"It includes everyone," I said. "It's why I'm standing here instead of asleep in my bunk. I drew the man's charts for eleven days thinking he was a vulture. He's not a vulture. Vultures wait for things to die. He kills them first and then files the paperwork."

Silence, except the river. The boy — Pip, Sela called her, in a tone that managed to be both exasperated and fiercely protective — stared at the manifest with her lips moving, reading names.

Freya Voss picked up the crew list last. I watched him find the name. I watched a man's whole architecture change behind his eyes — not collapse, nothing so kind as collapse, but a load-bearing wall shifting, everything above it settling into a new and worse shape. Three years he'd carried a question, and I'd just handed him the answer's address.

"He was fireman," Voss said quietly. "On the Star."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry yet." He looked up, and his eyes were the flat grey of the river before weather. "Be sorry or be useful, and prove which one by staying alive long enough for me to tell the difference."

"That's fair," I said. "I'd have said something colder."

Something moved across his face that on another man I'd have called a smile. "Sela," he said, "she sleeps where you can see her."

"Oh, she sleeps where I can *reach* her," Sela said. "Seeing's optional."

* * *

They didn't trust me, which was correct of them, and I want that entered into the record too: they were right not to.

I spent my first day in their camp proving usefulness in small denominations. I corrected their mental map of the fleet's positions — the *Mercy* lay a mile closer to the mountain than Voss had estimated, and the third skiff patrolled a loop he didn't know existed. I told them Aldous had bought the harbormaster at Greyharbor and two officials besides, and named the price of each, because corruption is just navigation by another method and I keep good logs. Osk listened to all of it with his arms crossed and his face shut, but at the end of the day he handed me a bowl of stew without comment, and in the economy of men like Osk Brann, a bowl of stew is a signed treaty.

Sela never took the treaty. She sat across the fire from me that night sharpening a knife that was already sharp, watching me with the patient hostility of a cat watching a door it intends to be behind.

"Ask," I said finally. "Whatever it is. Asking's cheaper than sharpening."

"What did you do for him? Before. In Karelvo."

Ah. There it was. I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch, because some questions deserve the courtesy of a pause.

"I drew the evacuation timetables," I said. "Not the ones that were sold — the originals. The honest ones. Routes that would have gotten people out. My office produced fourteen thousand copies, and then the ministry recalled them and issued different routes, and I was young enough to believe it was logistics. Rounding errors. Wartime confusion." I turned the stick. "It was neither. And I've spent nine years navigating for men like Aldous because it was easier than navigating away from what I'd been part of. Until eleven days ago, when I read a salvage order that used the same handwriting as the recall notice."

Sela considered this, knife resting. "That's either the truest thing I've heard all year or the best-rehearsed."

"It's both rehearsed and true. I've been composing it for eleven days. That's what guilt does — it makes you draft."

To her credit, she almost laughed. Almost. "Voss trusts you."

"Voss is deciding whether to. There's a difference, and I'd thank you to respect it, because I'm deciding too."

"Deciding what?"

"Whether I'm doing this because it's right," I said, "or because it feels right, which is a different animal entirely and bites harder." I met her eyes across the fire. "You'll know before I do. People like you always do."

She went back to her knife. But she moved the stew pot an inch closer to my side of the fire, and in Sela's currency, that was a letter of marque — conditional, revocable, but issued.

* * *

On the second night, Freya Voss spread his maps on the signal-box floor and asked me the question I'd been dreading, because I'd already answered it for myself and hated the answer.

"If we move tonight," he said, tapping the flooded line beneath Mount Serel, "how long before Aldous knows?"

I knelt beside the map and let myself see it the way I saw all charts — as a living thing, tides breathing through it, men moving across it like weather. "Two tides," I said. "Maybe less. His divers are ready early because he's not paying them to be careful. And there's a skiff running the upper narrows on a loop I designed — it passes within hailing distance of this stretch of bank every ninety minutes after dark." I touched the map where the loop crossed the old tramway road. "If we move our boats, we cross its eye."

"So we wait for the gap."

"The gap closes in—" I stopped. Recalculated. Felt the cold walk up my spine the way it had in the master's cabin, reading that sentence about survival and disclosure. "No. It doesn't close. I changed the rotation yesterday, before I left. Told the skiff officers Aldous wanted tighter coverage of the narrows." I looked up at him. "I tightened my own escape route out of existence. Whatever window we use, we use it against a net I made stronger."

The fire cracked. Pip Wren, curled against Osk's side, had gone very still, the way young things go still around grief they don't understand yet.

"Then we go anyway," Voss said. "Tonight. Through your net."

"With what?" Sela said. "We've got one boat with a hole in her bilge and supplies for four days."

"Then we'd better steal the rest on the way," Voss said, and there it was again, that almost-smile, that thing that wasn't quite humor and wasn't quite madness but lived in the house between them. He rolled up the map. "Miss Reyne. You know their signals?"

"Every flag, every lamp code, and the challenge word changes at midnight — it's 'serel' until then, 'passage' after."

"Then you're point," he said. "Congratulations. You've traded one fleet for another."

I thought about correcting him — telling him I hadn't joined anything, that I was cargo riding with them until the arithmetic resolved — and then I looked around the fire at the smuggler and the deserter and the apprentice and the engineer with his brother's ghost standing behind his shoulder, and I decided the correction could wait.

"All ahead slow, then," I said. "And somebody had better show me where you keep your flags, because if we're crossing my own net, I intend to do it in style."

We broke camp within the hour. And as we pushed off into the dark current, with the floodlights of the *Mercy* blooming far downstream like a second, colder moon, I took the helm of a stranger's boat and steered it straight at the trap I'd built, and I confess — I confess to this page if to no one else — that some traitorous corner of me was *glad*. Glad to be moving. Glad to be aimed at something instead of away from it.

The river took us north. Somewhere behind us, a skiff's lantern swung wide on its loop, obedient to my orders, hunting for exactly the kind of boat I was now driving.

Ninety minutes, I'd told them. I checked my watch, and began, quietly, to count.
