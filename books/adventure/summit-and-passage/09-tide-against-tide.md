## 9. Tide Against Tide

I've been chased before. Twice by customs cutters, once by a husband in Vellmark who owned a faster boat than his wife gave him credit for, and once — and I'll deny this to my grave — by a flock of geese with a genuine grievance. So when the patrol skiff came around the bend of the Sorel with its running lamps lit and its engine snarling like a tax collector, my first feeling wasn't fear.

It was professional offense.

"Is that us?" Pip Wren asked from the bow, where she'd been sitting with her boots dangling over the water like a girl on a fence rail instead of a fugitive on a stolen barge.

"That's us," I said.

"Are they shooting?"

"Not yet." I eased the throttle forward and felt the old girl answer — a shudder through the deck plates, a change in the water's voice under the hull. "They're showing us their lights first. That's how it works. Lights, then a hail, then the guns. It's a whole ceremony. Very civilized."

"How long does the ceremony last?"

"Until somebody breaks etiquette."

Behind me, Osk Brann was already moving without being told, which is the thing I liked about him and the thing that scared me most. Deserters learn fast which sounds mean paperwork and which sounds mean drowning. He started lashing down the dive gear with the calm of a man folding laundry while the house burns.

Freya Voss stood at the wheel beside me, ledger clamped under one arm like a breastplate. He hadn't put it down since Greyharbor. I half expected him to sleep with it, and knowing him, he did.

"How far to the lock?" he asked.

"Kessel Lock. Four miles if the river cooperates."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then three and a half and an apology to whatever we hit." I glanced at the skiff again. It had closed maybe two hundred yards in the time we'd been talking. "Problem is, they know about Kessel too. Fleet charts are better than mine these days. Aldous bought the survey office lock, stock, and barrel."

"Can we beat them there?"

I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the first sight of those lamps, and didn't love the total. "No. But we don't have to beat them. We have to arrive at the same time as them, in the right order, going the right speed, and then we have to make the lock do something locks aren't supposed to do."

Freya looked at me the way engineers look at things — like the problem was a machine and every machine has a seam somewhere. "What aren't locks supposed to do?"

"Close while someone's inside them."

* * *

Here's what you need to know about river people, and why Aldous's fleet, for all its dredgers and floodlights and money, was always going to be playing our game on our ground: the river doesn't belong to anyone, but it keeps accounts. Every eddy, every sunken piling, every stretch where the current runs lazy or mean — somebody alive knows it, and that somebody isn't on the fleet's payroll.

I grew up on the Sorel. My mother ran cargo that didn't ask questions and my father ran a ferry that sank twice, both times on purpose, both times insured. By ten I could read the water better than the almanacs. By fifteen I could take a loaded barge through the Narrows at night with the lamps doused. The war took a lot from a lot of people, but it never got around to taking the river from me, mostly because the river kept proving more dangerous than the war.

So when the skiff's searchlight swept across our stern and found us — a white slap of light, there and gone, like being noticed by God's bored clerk — I wasn't thinking about escape routes. I was thinking about Kessel Lock, and specifically about the maintenance gallery under the eastern gate chamber, and specifically about the counterweight chain that some lazy warden had let rust for eleven years because the budget said replace it next fiscal year and then the nation of Karelvo stopped having fiscal years.

"You're smiling," Freya said.

"I'm planning."

"They look identical on you."

"That's the trick of it."

Pip scrambled back from the bow, all elbows and alarm. "They're hailing us. There's a man with one of those horn things."

"Megaphone," Osk said.

"I knew that. I was testing you."

The voice came across the water anyway, tinny and official: *"Unidentified vessel, this is an Aldous Salvage Company patrol. Heave to and prepare for boarding. This is your only warning."*

"Heave to," Pip repeated. "That means stop, right?"

"It means stop," I said.

"We're not going to stop."

"We are absolutely not going to stop." I brought us hard to starboard, into the shadow of the old quarry bluff, where the current ran contrary and the fleet's charts — drawn from a launch in high summer, I'd bet my mother's ring on it — showed a comfortable channel that was actually a gravel bar wearing two feet of water as a disguise.

The hull kissed the bar. Just kissed it — a long grinding shudder, like the boat clearing her throat — and then we were through, and behind us I heard the skiff's driver realize, a half-second too late, that the water ahead of him was lying.

The sound a planing hull makes when it meets gravel at speed is not a sound you forget. It's a sound like a giant tearing a very expensive piece of paper. Their searchlight swung wildly, painted the bluff, the trees, the sky, and then their engine note dropped from fury to embarrassment as they backed off the bar and went looking for deep water the polite way.

"Did we—" Pip started.

"Don't celebrate," Osk said. "They'll be around the bar in four minutes. They know this stretch now."

"He's right," I said. "That bought us distance, not safety. Distance is just safety on credit."

Freya had the ledger open against the wheel housing, pencil moving. Of course it was. "Two and a quarter miles to Kessel," he said. "Their skiff does what, twelve knots in the straight? We do seven?"

"Seven and a lie," I said. "But yes. They'll catch us in the last mile no matter what I do. So the last mile is where we stop running and start fighting."

"Define fighting," Osk said.

"Define rigging," I answered, and looked at Freya, and watched the engineer come up behind his eyes.

* * *

We sent Pip over the side at the willows above the lock, because she was the smallest, the lightest, and — though I'd never say it where she could hear — the most expendable, in the strictly mathematical sense that losing any of the rest of us would cost more grief per pound. She swears now that she volunteered. History is written by whoever talks loudest afterward.

Her job was simple and horrible: drop into the maintenance gallery through the drain grate, climb down into the gate chamber, and knock the retaining pin off the counterweight drum so the eastern gate would fall free when the mechanism called for it to rise. A gate that falls instead of rises. A lock that closes instead of opens. Eleven years of rust meant the pin wouldn't be easy, but rust also meant nobody at the lock had tested anything since the war, which meant nobody would notice until it mattered.

"I want it on record," Pip said, dripping, at the edge of the grate, "that I passed my swimming certification with a merit badge."

"On record," Freya said gravely. "Noted in the ledger."

"You're not writing it down."

"I'm writing it down."

She went. And then there were three of us on the barge, doing the hardest thing in any chase, which is nothing. We ran slow. We ran honest. Seven knots, lamps lit, a fat slow target crawling toward Kessel Lock like we had nothing in the world to hide, because the only way the trick works is if the skiff believes we're stupid.

"They're coming," Osk said. He'd taken the stern watch with a pair of borrowed glasses. "Around the bar. Two minutes."

"Any damage from the gravel?"

"Their port sponson looks crumpled. They're listing a hair to the left."

"So they'll come at us angry," I said. "Good. Angry drivers aim at what they hate instead of what they should fear."

Freya stood beside me at the wheel, and I'll say this for the man: three years of walking dead rail lines had taught him stillness the way monks learn it, except his meditation was distances. "Sela," he said quietly, "if the pin doesn't come out—"

"Then we get boarded, searched, and probably arrested, and Aldous gets the wreck and the evidence and Tomas's name stays a line in a manifest forever." I kept my eyes on the lock gates ahead, pale in the dusk, standing open and patient as a held breath. "So let's both decide the pin comes out."

The megaphone voice again, closer now, furious now: *"Unidentified vessel, you are ordered to heave to. Boarding party standing by. Final warning."*

"Final warning," Pip's voice crackled in my memory, from a hundred card games. *Call their bluff, Sela. Call it.*

"Answer them," I told Freya.

He blinked. "What do I say?"

"Anything official-sounding. You're a railroad man. Pretend you're talking to a supplier who owes you money."

Freya raised the signal lamp, thought for a moment, and flashed back in crisp merchant code — slow, deliberate, insulting in its patience. The skiff hesitated. I could feel them decoding it, could feel the moment the pilot read *freight consignment, Kessel transit authorized, verify with harbormaster* and made the fatal calculation that bureaucracy might be involved. For eleven seconds — I counted — the fleet's pursuit slowed to match ours, because no one in history has ever wanted to be the man who boarded the wrong barge and had to write the report.

Eleven seconds is a long time on a river.

Then someone aboard got suspicious — I saw the searchlight snap back onto us, saw the skiff surge — and it didn't matter anymore, because we were in the throat of the lock, and Pip Wren was either a merit-badge swimmer or she wasn't.

* * *

A lock, for those who've never trusted their life to one, is a stone bathtub with doors at both ends. Boats float in, the doors close, the water level changes, the far doors open. The whole system runs on the agreement that the doors obey the machinery and the machinery obeys the warden and the warden obeys the schedule.

We were asking the eastern door to break that agreement.

We slid into the chamber at seven knots with the skiff eating our wake at twelve, and the western gate began to close behind us on its ancient schedule, and the winch bells rang, and somewhere below us a girl with a wrench and a merit badge was fighting a pin that hadn't moved since before she was born.

Fifty feet of chamber left. The skiff's bow crossed the sill.

Forty feet. Their searchlight found us full-on, blinding, and the megaphone voice abandoned ceremony entirely: *"Stop! STOP OR WE FIRE!"*

Thirty feet. A rifle cracked, and something whined off the stonework above Osk's head, and Osk didn't even flinch, just shifted his shoulders slightly, the way a man does when rain starts.

Twenty feet. The western gate boomed shut behind us with a sound like a bank vault having opinions.

Ten feet. The skiff was fully committed, inside the chamber, engines reversing hard, too late, always too late —

And the eastern gate fell.

Not rose. Not lowered with hydraulic grace. *Fell*, eleven years of rust shearing loose all at once, thirty tons of iron dropping into the water with a concussion I felt in my teeth and my ribs and probably my future children's teeth. The wall of water it threw hit the skiff broadside and picked it up like a toy in a flooded gutter, slammed it against the chamber wall with a crunch of splintering timber, and set it down again sideways, shipping water, crew spilling along the stones like dropped groceries.

Our barge rode the surge up and over — I had the throttle wide, nose into the wave, the way you take any ugly water — and then we were through the eastern sill and out into the dark upper reach, and behind us the lock was sealed shut with a fleet patrol boat trapped inside it like a fly in amber.

For about four seconds, it was the finest moment of my life.

Then I smelled smoke.

* * *

The rifle shot, it turned out, had done more than whine. The second round — fired during the chaos, by whom I never learned — had gone into our deck cargo, and our deck cargo was kerosene tins, because smugglers travel the way smugglers travel, and the tins had done what kerosene tins do.

We fought it together, the four of us, buckets and blankets and Osk's bare hands, and we saved the barge and lost nearly everything on it. The fuel for the launch stove. Half the diving compressed air. The food — most of it, gone in a greasy black tongue of flame that Pip beat at with her own coat, her face lit orange and furious. The spare parts. The chart case survived; Freya held it above the waterline like a priest saving relics, and I suppose to him that's exactly what it was.

By midnight we were anchored in a willow stand, four people on a scorched deck, eating the last intact tin of beans cold, passing one spoon around.

"Well," Pip said finally. "That was incredible."

"That was expensive," I said.

"That was *incredible* and *expensive*. Both things can be true. I contain multitudes."

Osk laughed — actually laughed, a short rusty sound like a gate opening after years shut, and I caught Freya glancing at him with something that might have been the beginning of trust, or at least the end of suspicion.

I sat at the stern with my share of beans and did the sums I always do after a job, because the river keeps accounts and so do I. We'd escaped. We'd crippled a patrol skiff and embarrassed the great Corvane Aldous, and there was a warm bright coal of that in my chest that I intended to enjoy. But the ledger's other column was real too: no fuel surplus, no air for more than two divers' worth of bottom time, food for five days if we ate like refugees. Survival had sent us a bill tonight, and it was itemized, and the interest compounded daily.

And there was one more entry, the one I kept turning over. The skiff had found us too fast. Out of all that river, all those bends, they'd come around the quarry bluff already on our line, already hunting, like something had told them where to look.

I didn't believe in coincidence on the water. The river punishes it.

Which is why, when Ilsa Reyne stepped out of the willow shadows onto our deck three days later — alone, hands visible, carrying papers she claimed were Aldous's dive schedule — I listened to everything she said with my hand resting very casually on the boathook.

Because I'd already learned the lesson of Kessel Lock: on this river, the gates that open easiest are the ones somebody else has rigged.
