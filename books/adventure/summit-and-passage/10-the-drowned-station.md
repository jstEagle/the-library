## 10. The Drowned Station

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a mountain that has swallowed a railway, and I had been listening to it for three years before I finally understood it wasn't silence at all. It was holding its breath.

We came down out of the service gallery at dusk — or what I called dusk, because down there dusk was a decision you made based on your knees and your fuel, not the sky. The tunnel sloped away beneath our boots at a grade I knew from the survey sheets: one in forty, running east toward Serel's root. Water came with us, ankle-deep and patient, the way water always is. It doesn't chase. It just arrives, eventually, wherever you were foolish enough to go.

"Tell me again why we're walking into a flooded hole in a mountain," Sela said, behind me. She had a lantern in one hand and a knife she wasn't hiding in the other, which was her way of being friendly.

"Because the fleet is coming up the river with dredges and dive barges and enough floodlights to be visible from the moon," I said, "and we are coming through the back door. That's the whole strategy. It's not clever. It's just the only door we have."

"And the back door is underwater."

"The back door is *mostly* underwater. There's a difference. Ask any engineer."

"I'm asking you, and you're not answering."

Pip laughed — a short, startled sound, like she'd surprised herself by having it. Three days on the river with us and she was still rationing her laughter like it was hardtack. Osk Brann walked at the rear with the air tanks lashed to his back and said nothing, which was Osk's way of saying everything was fine, and if it wasn't fine, he'd mention it in the same tone he used for mentioning the weather.

Ilsa Reyne walked in the middle, where nobody had to decide whether to trust her. She carried Aldous's dive schedule sewn into her coat lining, and she carried something else too — a look she got when the water rose past her knees, a kind of flinch she thought nobody saw. I saw it. I'd spent three years perfecting that exact flinch. It's the look of a person who has already drowned once and is only waiting for the river to notice its mistake.

The station announced itself before we saw it, the way stations do. First the grade leveled out — platforms are built flat, and flatness in a mountain tunnel is a signature, like handwriting. Then the walls widened, and my lantern found tile. Green tile, cracked, furred with white mineral bloom, running in a band at waist height around walls that opened out into a vaulted dark.

"Kestrel Halt," I said. My voice came back off the wet stone, smaller than I'd sent it.

"You know it?" Pip asked.

"I surveyed it. Never stood in it. It closed the year before the war — a branch stop on the Serel spur, forty passengers a week on a good summer. There's a photograph of it in the regional archive. Benches. A clock. A woman selling apples off a cart." I swept the lantern across the dark. "The apples are gone."

"Everything's gone," Sela said. "That's rather the theme."

The water stood chest-deep across the platform, which meant the station's lower level — waiting rooms, offices, the stationmaster's quarters — was fully drowned. But the Serel spur had been built with a maintenance gallery above the platform, a walkway for track workers, and I'd read its dimensions off a blueprint so many times I could have drawn it from memory. We climbed. Rust rungs, a hatch swollen shut that Osk opened with one shoulder and a grunt that Pip echoed, and then we were up on the gallery walk, dry-footed for the first time in six hours, in a corridor barely wide enough for two.

We made camp in what had been the signal room. There was even a bench, iron-framed, bolted to the wall, built by people who assumed the mountain was permanent. We lit the stove Sela had carried — she carried a stove, this woman, up a cliff, because "cold is a choice" — and hung our wet things, and ate the kind of meal that keeps you alive without pretending to be food.

It was Pip who found the wall.

I'd sent her to scout the gallery eastward, on the logic that the youngest legs should do the walking and the oldest pride should do the admitting. She came back at a run, which in that corridor meant a fast, echoing slap of boots.

"Freya. There's writing. On the walls. All down the gallery — names, and dates, and —" She stopped, caught her breath. "There's a lot of it. You should see."

I should tell you what I felt, and I will, but I want to be honest about the order of it. First I felt the surveyor's reflex: *graffiti in a closed structure means recent occupation, means access, means a route I don't have on my maps.* The professional brain comes first. It always comes first. It's the armor I built after the war, and it has saved my life, and it has also, more times than I can count, arrived at the scene of a feeling a full minute before the feeling does, and stood there blocking the door.

Then the feeling got through.

Because I knew who had been in that gallery. The Meridian Star's last crew had run the Serel spur — Ilsa had confirmed the route, the timing, the final run before the line was flooded and the train went into the water with everything else. If there was writing on those walls, it was theirs. It was three years old and it was thirty years old and it was written by men and women who had, within days, been dead.

I took the lantern and I walked.

* * *

The gallery ran forty meters before the first collapse, and in those forty meters, the last crew of the Meridian Star had left the only record of themselves that the world was ever going to get.

They'd used lamp black, chalk, paint stolen from the maintenance lockers — anything that would hold to damp tile. Some of it had run, black tears down the green and white. Some had held. I went slow, reading, and the others came behind me with their lights, and for a while nobody spoke, which for Sela was a kind of miracle, and for Osk was simply Tuesday.

*DAY 9 OF THE RUN. KESSLER ATE THE LAST OF THE GOOD COFFEE AND HAS BEEN SENT TO THE BACK OF THE TRAIN IN PERPETUITY.*

*IF YOU ARE READING THIS, THE LINE IS CLOSED AND I AM EITHER RICH OR DEAD. — M. OKONKWO, FIREMAN.*

*SEREL STATION APPLES: 4/10. WOULD NOT RECOMMEND. — ANON.*

Small things. Jokes. The scratch of ordinary people being ordinary in the last week of their lives, because none of them knew — or if they knew, if they'd been told the line was ending and the water was coming, they had decided that the correct response was to rate the apples.

"Freya." Pip's voice, very quiet. "There's more. Down here. It's — I think it's a list."

It was a list. A crew roster, written in a careful hand, each name followed by a home town. *V. Amara, Dussek. H. Kessler, Vran. M. Okonkwo, Greyharbor. T. Voss, Karelvo City.*

The lantern did not shake. I want that on the record. The lantern did not shake. My hand did, and the lantern went with it, because that's how lanterns work, and the light swung across the tile and came back and found the name again and again, and I stood there reading two words — *T. Voss* — the way a man reads a telegram he's spent three years dreading and three years hoping for, both at once, in the same breath, which is a thing grief and hope can do when they share a body.

"Freya?" Sela. Close. Not touching me. She'd learned that much about me by then, God help her.

"He signed it," I said. My voice sounded like it had come up from the flooded lower level, through forty feet of dark water. "He was here. Three years I've been measuring miles, and he was *here*, on this wall, in this —" I stopped. Started again. "I surveyed this gallery in the second year. I stood where I'm standing. I was maybe four meters from this wall and I didn't check the tile, because checking tile isn't in the survey protocol, and my brother was *four meters away* —"

"Freya." Sela again, and this time her hand did land, on my shoulder, brief and hard as a stamp on a document. "Breathe. Then read the rest. There's more under the roster. It's addressed to someone."

I breathed. I read.

Below the roster, in the same careful hand, the writing got smaller, tighter, the letters of a man writing fast and trying not to look like it:

*TO WHOEVER FINDS THIS — THE LINE IS CLOSING AND THEY TELL US IT'S ROUTINE. IT IS NOT ROUTINE. WHAT'S IN THE MAIL CAR IS NOT MAIL. I HAVE PUT IT SOMEWHERE SAFE AND I HAVE WRITTEN DOWN WHERE, AND THE WRITING IS NOT HERE. IF YOU ARE THE PEOPLE I THINK YOU MIGHT BE — THE GOOD ONES, THE ONES WHO COME LOOKING — CHECK THE OLD NUMBERING. DAD'S TRICK. YOU'LL KNOW. — T.V.*

The gallery was very quiet. Somewhere below us, water moved against the platform with a sound like slow applause.

"Old numbering," Pip said. "What's old numbering?"

I couldn't answer. Not because I didn't know. Because I knew *exactly*, and knowing it meant that my brother, in the last days of his life, in a tunnel filling with river, had thought of me — had thought of the two of us, boys on our father's work crew, learning to mark culverts and mileposts with the pre-war numbering scheme that the railway had abandoned in the reorganization, the scheme Dad had drilled into us as a game, *the real map under the map, boys, because maps get revised but the mountain doesn't* — and had trusted that I, of all people, would come walking down this gallery someday and read it.

He hadn't left a message for the world. He'd left it for me. Addressed, stamped, and delivered three years late.

I am not going to tell you what I did, standing in that corridor. It's nobody's business, and anyway the others had the grace to study their boots. I'll say only that it took some time, and that when I turned around my face was wet and nobody remarked on it, and Sela handed me a flask, and the flask's contents were terrible, and it was the best thing I'd ever drunk.

* * *

We sat in the signal room afterward, all five of us, and I told them about the numbering.

"Every structure on the old Serel line had two numbers," I said. "The official one, and the construction number from before the reorganization. Dad worked the original build. He taught us both — Tomas and me — that if you ever got lost in the tunnels, you could navigate by the construction numbers, because they ran in a logical sequence and the official numbers ran in whatever order the clerks felt like that decade." I turned the flask in my hands. "The mail car. If Tomas hid something aboard the Meridian Star, he'd have marked its location with a construction number somewhere in the car — scratched, painted, something durable. And the car is in the flooded cavern, tilted, half-buried in silt, at depth."

"Which we can't reach," Osk said, "until we've got the gear set up and the route cleared and at least four tides of preparation."

"Which Aldous will reach first," Ilsa said quietly, "if his schedule holds and ours doesn't."

"Then ours holds," Sela said. "Everyone in this room has done something impossible this week. We're on a streak."

But I barely heard them. I was still in the gallery, in the lamplight, looking at *T.V.* The numbness I'd carried for three years — the numbness I'd built mile by mile, trestle by trestle, the whole architecture of a man who had replaced time with distance so he'd never have to sit still and feel anything — had a crack in it now, and through the crack came not just grief but its opposite, its unbearable twin. Because dead men don't leave you puzzles. Dead men don't trust you with secrets. Dead men don't sign their work like they expect you to come find it.

Tomas had believed, writing on that wall with the water rising, that I would come. Not hoped. *Believed* — the way you believe in load tolerances, in the strength of a beam, in things you've tested.

"Freya." Pip, beside me, her voice low. "He waited for you. That's what the message is. He couldn't have known you'd come — nobody could — but he wrote it like he knew. That's not a message for a stranger. That's a message for a brother."

"He was always like that," I said. "Drove our father mad. Tomas would leave tools in exactly the wrong places and swear he'd remember where, and he always did. He had a memory like a survey chain." I looked at the lantern flame. "I used to think it was carelessness. It wasn't. He just trusted the world to hold still long enough for him to come back to it."

"And it didn't," Osk said. Not unkindly. Osk didn't do unkindly or kindly; he did accurate, and you learned to be grateful.

"No," I said. "It didn't. But he left the map under the map, and I'm the one man on this river who can read it." I stood up. My legs had that post-shock looseness, and I ignored them the way you ignore weather. "So here's where we are. The evidence is in the mail car. The mail car is in the cavern. Aldous has dredges, divers, and a two-tide lead, and we have a stove, a flask of Sela's crimes against distilling, and a dead man's numbering system. I'd say we're winning."

"You would," Sela said, "you're an engineer. You people think a two-to-one deficit is a rounding error."

"It's not a rounding error," I said. "It's a design challenge."

Pip laughed again, easier this time. Even Osk's mouth did something at the corner that in better light might have been a smile. And Ilsa — Ilsa was looking at the doorway, toward the gallery, toward the roster on the wall, and her face had the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like, and I noted it, and filed it, and said nothing, because the file on Ilsa Reyne was already thick and tonight was not the night to open it.

* * *

I went back to the gallery before sleep took me. Alone, with the lantern, because some doors you have to walk through by yourself.

I stood in front of the roster a long time. Then I took out my ledger — the battered one, the one with three years of miles in it, trestle spans and tunnel lengths, the accounting of a man who had measured everything except what mattered — and I found a blank space, and I copied the roster into it. All of it. Every name, every town. V. Amara. H. Kessler. M. Okonkwo. T. Voss. If the wall was going to drown someday, the names wouldn't.

Below them, I wrote the construction number of Kestrel Halt itself — the old number, Dad's number, the one under the map — and then, because I am my father's son and my brother's brother, I added a line of my own, in lamp black I scraped from the gallery wall:

*RECEIVED. — F.V.*

It's not much of an epitaph. But it's a receipt, and in my family, a receipt meant the goods were coming, and the debt would be honored, and no power on this river — not Aldous, not the fleet, not the mountain itself — was going to close the account before it was paid.

Above me, far up the shaft, something shifted in the rock. A settling, a groan, the mountain clearing its throat. Osk's head came around from the signal room, sharp, listening. So did mine.

Because I knew that sound. Every engineer who ever worked Serel knew that sound. And it wasn't the mountain settling.

It was the mountain, far below us, beginning to let the water in somewhere new.
