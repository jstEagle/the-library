## 11. Pressure

The army taught me to dive in a flooded quarry outside Vessik, in water so cold it felt personal. They gave us two weeks, a rubber suit that smelled of tar and regret, and an instructor who opened the first lesson by saying: "The water does not hate you. It simply does not know you exist." I have thought about that sentence more than any prayer I ever learned. It is the truest thing anyone ever said to me about drowning.

Three years later, on the gravel shelf beneath the drowned station at Serel, I stood in front of four people who needed to learn everything I knew in three days instead of fourteen, and I opened with the same line, because it was still true and because I have never been an original man.

"The water doesn't hate you," I said. "It doesn't know you're there. Your job is to convince it otherwise."

Pip Wren looked at me like I'd suggested we negotiate with the river. Sela leaned against a fallen beam with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had already decided the lesson was a waste of time and was staying only because leaving would mean admitting she couldn't swim well enough to argue. Freya stood at the edge of the black water with his ledger under his arm — he carried that thing everywhere, like a psalter — and Ilsa Reyne sat apart from all of them on a dry crate, which was exactly where they wanted her and exactly where she refused to stay.

"Right," I said. "Hoods off. Let's talk about pressure."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about diving, or what everybody tells you and nobody believes until their ears are screaming: you are not built for this. You are a creature of one atmosphere, walking around inside a body that is mostly water anyway, and every fathom down adds another atmosphere pressing on you like a thumb on a grape. At ten meters your lungs compress to half. At thirty, your air goes thick as syrup and your judgment goes with it. We call it rapture of the deep, and it is not a metaphor. Men have drowned taking off their masks to feed fish that weren't there.

"So when it starts feeling wonderful down there," I told them, "when the light goes soft and you think, *I could live here*, that is the water lying to you. That is the moment you check your gauge."

Freya wrote that down. Actually wrote it down, in the ledger, between trestle measurements and tide tables. I found it oddly moving. Here was a man who trusted paper more than memory, who had apparently decided that if his brother could be found anywhere, it would be in the margins of a record. I didn't ask. Deserters learn early which questions cost too much to ask.

We started shallow. The station platform ran out into flooded track bed, and for the first day I kept everyone in water up to their chests, learning to breathe off the old fleet regulators I'd salvaged from my kit — government issue, Karelvoan stamps still on the housings, which made Sela snort every time she looked at one.

"These kept your masters breathing," she said, "and now they keep us alive. There's a sermon in that."

"There's a leak in that one," I said, taking it from her. "There's a sermon in that too."

Pip took to it the way young people take to anything terrifying — badly, loudly, and then suddenly completely. She panicked twice on the first morning, once when her hood filled and once for no reason she could name, just the animal certainty that she was going to die in the dark. Both times I put my hand on the back of her neck, under the water, where she could feel it, and held her there until her breathing evened out. Not holding her down. Holding her *present*. It's the first thing they teach you about panicking divers: don't grab the mask, don't grab the regulator. Give them something solid that isn't trying to kill them.

"You're doing fine," I lied, the second time.

"I'm doing terribly," she said, spitting water.

"Yes," I agreed. "And you'll do it again tomorrow, and it'll be less terrible, and in a week you'll be insufferable about it. That's how learning works. Nobody tells you that either."

Sela was the surprise. I'd expected her to fight the gear, fight me, fight the whole premise. Instead she went quiet in the water in a way I never saw her quiet anywhere else. She floated on her back in the flooded concourse while the rest of us drilled mask clears, staring up at the crack of grey daylight through the collapsed roof, and when I told her to get back to work she said, "In a minute," and for once I let her have the minute. Everyone carries a war debt. Hers, I suspected, had a face on it. So did mine. That's usually why people go quiet looking up through water.

Freya was the problem. Not because he was bad — he was methodical, careful, did everything twice — but because he treated the drills like engineering problems, which meant he treated his own body like equipment, which meant he didn't report the pain in his ears until I caught him wincing on the third descent.

"How long has that been hurting?"

"Since yesterday."

"And you didn't say anything because—"

"It didn't affect the work."

I made him surface and sit out the rest of the day, and he glared at me with the particular fury of a man who has lost an argument to someone he outranks in every way except the one that matters right now. "You equalize early and often," I told him, "or the water takes your eardrums as a toll, and then you're no use to anyone, least of all yourself. The work includes the body, engineer. That's the whole curriculum."

He didn't thank me. But the next morning he equalized early and often, and I counted that as thanks. With men like Freya Voss, you learn to read the ledger, not the face.

* * *

It was on the second evening, around the fire we allowed ourselves because the smoke rose through the broken roof and dispersed, that Ilsa Reyne finally stopped sitting apart.

She'd spent two days being useful in small ways — checking our position against Aldous's published salvage charts, correcting Sela's dead reckoning by a half-mile that mattered, mending Pip's torn glove without being asked. Useful the way a knife is useful. And the crew had spent two days performing tolerance, which is different from trust, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, until Ilsa set down her cup and said:

"You should know what Aldous is actually dredging for."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, water dripped from the vaulted ceiling into the flooded nave of the station, each drop landing with a sound like a slow clock.

"We know," Freya said. "Gold. A nation's treasury, sunk with the Star."

"No." Ilsa's voice was flat, professional, the voice of a navigator reading off coordinates. "I've seen the manifests. I served on his flagship for eleven months; I inventoried his dive manifests myself. The cargo hold of the Meridian Star carried sealed diplomatic cases. Lead-lined. Forty-one of them. No gold worth the word 'fortune.'"

Sela sat forward slowly. "Then why does a man spend a fortune raising a fortune that isn't there?"

"Because the cases aren't money," Ilsa said. "They're evidence. Wartime correspondence. Evacuation route schedules. The kind of paper that hangs men."

I watched Freya's face do something I will remember until I die. It didn't collapse. It *reorganized*. Three years of grief rearranging itself around new information, the way a riverbed reorganizes around a flood — nothing destroyed, everything displaced. His hand went to the ledger. Didn't open it. Just rested there.

"Tomas," he said, so quietly I almost missed it.

"That's the name on the crew list?" Ilsa asked. "Your brother?"

"Don't," Sela said.

"He deserves to know what he's diving toward—"

"He deserves to tell you himself what his brother means to him, and you deserve nothing from him until he decides you've earned it." Sela was on her feet now, and I noted, professionally, that she'd come up standing without seeming to move, which is a smuggler's trick and a good one. "You sailed with that man for eleven months. You navigated for him. What were you doing while he was buying evacuation routes and selling them to gunners? Drawing his charts?"

"Drawing his charts," Ilsa said, without flinching. "Yes."

"At least the deserters run," Pip said, very quietly, from the edge of the firelight. "You stayed."

That landed. I saw it land. Ilsa Reyne had answers prepared for suspicion, accusation, even hatred — you could see the armor plates — but Pip's small cold voice went straight through a joint in it, and for a moment she was just a woman sitting by a fire with nowhere left to sit apart.

"I stayed because staying was survivable," she said. "I'm here because survivable stopped being enough. You can believe that or not. But believe this: Aldous reaches this mountain in six tides, maybe fewer, and when he comes he brings forty divers and lights enough to turn night into noon, and the only advantage we have is that we know what's in those cases and he thinks we don't. Throw me out if you want. Keep the information."

Nobody threw her out. Nobody spoke for a long moment either. The fire cracked. The water dripped its slow clock.

It was Osk Brann who broke it — me, that is, and I've wondered since why it was me. Maybe because I know what it costs to be the man nobody trusts in the room. Maybe because I'd spent two days watching her mend gloves and correct charts and sit where she was put, and I know discipline when I see it, having used it myself as a disguise for years.

"She dives with us," I said. "Roped to me. If she signals the fleet, I'll feel it before anyone else, and I'll deal with it at depth, where there's no audience and no appeal." I looked at Ilsa. "That's not trust. That's engineering. Trust gets built after."

"Accepted," she said.

"Osk—" Sela started.

"Sela." Freya's voice, quiet, final. "He's right. Loyalty we assume is worth nothing. Loyalty we choose—" He stopped, and I understood he was thinking of Tomas, of whatever choice his brother had made aboard that train, and he finished differently: "Loyalty we choose is the only kind that holds under pressure. Everything else is just proximity."

Sela sat back down. She didn't forgive anyone, and she made sure we all knew it, and honestly I respected her more for it. Forgiveness issued cheap is just another way of lying.

* * *

On the third day I took them down to twenty meters, into the flooded tunnel mouth where the old branch line ran under the mountain, and that's where the training stopped being theory.

The dark down there is total. Our lamps made circles maybe two meters wide, and beyond the circles the water pressed in like a living thing deciding whether to let us pass. Pip breathed too fast and burned through her air in half the planned time, and I brought her up myself, hand on her harness, and at the surface she cried — not from fear, she insisted, from anger, which I believed, because anger is just fear that's found somewhere to stand.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Don't apologize for wanting to live," I said. "Apologize if you stop."

Freya went last, alone with me, and I watched him in the lamplight as the tunnel swallowed the daylight behind us. He moved through that black water like a man walking a street he grew up on. At one point he stopped, laid his palm flat against the tunnel wall, and held it there, and I saw his lips move — counting, probably, or saying a name. When we surfaced he didn't speak for an hour, and I didn't make him.

That evening Ilsa unrolled her copy of the fleet's dive schedule across three crates and weighted the corners with stones, and we gathered over it like conspirators, which is what we were.

"Aldous runs his main assault through the north ventilation shaft," she said, tapping. "Forty divers, staged in relays. But the mail car sits here—" her finger moved south, "—at the bottom of the cavern, past the junction chamber. His plan is to strip the gold route first and reach the cases second. If we go through the service tunnel behind the junction, we can be at the mail car before his first relay finishes rigging."

"Through the service tunnel," Freya repeated. He was staring at the chart, but I could see he wasn't seeing the chart. He was seeing blueprints from another life. "Those maintenance tunnels were condemned before the war. Half of them won't exist anymore."

"And half of them will," Ilsa said.

It was Sela who noticed it first — she had an eye for wrongness on paper, the way some people have an ear for a false note. "This schedule's dated," she said. "Six days old. You defected four days ago. What's happened since?"

Ilsa went still. It was a small stillness, controlled, but I've spent my life underwater reading bodies in bad light, and I know what a held breath looks like even on dry land.

"The informant," she said finally. "Aldous took a prisoner in Greyharbor. A harbor pilot who'd sold charts to smugglers. By the time I left, Aldous had... methods applied." Her finger came down on the chart, on the mark for Mount Serel itself. "He may not need six tides. He may need two."

The fire had burned low. Above us the mountain held its water and its dead, and somewhere out on the river, I understood, the lights of a dredger were already moving toward us through the dark, faster than any of us had planned for, faster than any of us were ready for.

Freya closed the ledger.

"Then we dive tomorrow," he said. "At first light. All of us."

And I looked at the four faces around that dying fire — the smuggler, the apprentice, the traitor, the engineer — and I thought: the water doesn't hate us. It doesn't need to. We're bringing everything it needs ourselves.
