## 12. The Fleet Arrives Early

I had a theory about Corvane Aldous, and it was this: he was never late because he never believed anyone else's clock. Time, for men like him, was not something you lived inside. It was something you owned, like a dredger or a magistrate, and other people simply rented it without knowing.

I'd worked for him four years. I knew his schedules the way a wife knows a husband's snoring — intimately, resentfully, and with no illusions that it would ever change. So when I stood on the ridge above Mount Serel's drowned valley and saw the first light bloom green beneath the water, two full tides before his dive plan said he could possibly be there, my first feeling wasn't surprise.

It was the sick, cold certainty of a woman who has just remembered she left a door unlocked.

* * *

Let me back up, because the crew deserves to have their ignorance recorded fairly. They hadn't done anything wrong. They'd trusted me, which in my experience is the same category of mistake but takes longer to punish.

We'd made camp three days earlier in the shell of an old signal house on the eastern shoulder of the valley — a stone hut with a collapsed roof and a view straight down into the flooded basin where, somewhere under forty feet of cold green water, the Meridian Star lay on her side among the bones of the station yard. Freya had chosen it for sightlines. Sela had chosen it for the chimney, which drew. Pip had chosen it because there was a shelf at exactly the right height for her charts, which told you everything about Pip.

Osk had chosen nothing. Osk approved of things by grunting near them.

The plan was simple, and I should have known that was the problem. Aldous's schedule — the one I'd carried out of the fleet in my coat lining, copied out in my own hand so the original could go back where no one would look — gave him six tides of positioning before the first dive. Dredgers are slow. His salvage barges needed to anchor in sequence, his dive lines needed surveying, his compressors needed to be hauled up the old haul road. Six tides. I'd built our whole approach around those six tides the way you build a house around a hearth.

"He'll position the *Corvane's Pride* here," I'd said, tapping the chart, "and the barges there and there. He won't dive until the third tide at the earliest. He's careful underwater. It's the only place he's careful."

"And above water?" Freya had asked. He has a way of asking questions that sound idle and aren't, like a man testing ice with his whole weight.

"Above water he's a hurricane wearing a good coat," I said. "But we don't have to be above water."

Everyone had nodded. Everyone had believed me. I want that on the record, because when it went wrong, Sela looked at me with an expression I'm going to be seeing behind my eyelids for the rest of my life, and I want it noted that she had every right to it and I had earned every degree of it.

* * *

It was Pip who spotted the lights first, just after dusk on what should have been the second night of our margin.

She came out of the signal house holding a tin cup of tea she'd forgotten to drink, which for Pip was equivalent to a man leaving his own wedding. She stood beside me at the ridge edge and didn't say anything for a long moment, and then she said, very quietly:

"Ilsa. What's that?"

Below us, deep in the basin, the water had begun to glow.

Not firelight — firelight flickers, breathes, argues with itself. This was steady. Engineered. A bloom of pale green spreading outward from some point beneath the surface like a stain, then a second bloom farther north, then a third, until the whole drowned valley looked like something luminous rotting under ice.

Floodlamps. Lowered on cables from anchored hulls. Three points of light meant three vessels in position, and three vessels in position meant the anchoring was already done, and the anchoring was supposed to take six tides.

"Those can't be his," Pip said. "Can they?"

I didn't answer, because answering would have made it true, and because my mouth had gone dry enough to store grain in.

Freya came out of the hut fast — he must have seen the light on our faces — and Sela behind him, and Osk last, moving the way Osk moved everywhere, like a man who had decided long ago that hurrying was for people who hadn't yet learned what panic costs.

Freya reached the ridge edge and looked down, and I watched three years fall off his face and get replaced by something older and harder. He didn't ask what it was. He was a railroad engineer; he knew floodlamps the way a priest knows candles.

"How early?" he asked me.

"Two tides," I said. "Maybe more. That's not possible. That's not — his barges draw too much for the haul road, he'd need to—"

"Someone told him where to look." Freya said it flatly, without accusation, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted. "He didn't find this valley faster. He skipped finding it. Someone gave him the location."

And then everyone looked at me.

* * *

Here is what I did while they looked: I thought about Rennick.

Rennick was a river pilot who ran supplies for the fleet, a soft-bellied man with a gambler's debts and a laugh like a hinge that needed oiling. He'd been taken aboard the *Pride* nine days ago, picked up off a skiff near Greyharbor on suspicion of running messages between the salvage crews and the dockside unions. I'd seen him brought aboard. I'd seen Aldous receive the news of his capture with that terrible mildness of his, the tilt of the head, the small courteous smile, the voice like warm oil poured over something that used to be alive.

Aldous had asked him, in front of the officers' mess, entirely conversationally, whether he knew the waters around Mount Serel.

And Rennick had laughed. And I had turned back to my charts and told myself that whatever happened next in the aft hold was the cost of doing business on Aldous's water, and that I was a navigator, not a jailer, and that a person could only carry so much before she put some of it down.

I had put it down. Rennick had known these waters. Rennick had piloted the survey runs over the drowned valley two seasons ago, before Aldous sealed the charts. If you wanted to skip six tides of searching, you didn't torture a map out of a man — you tortured a memory, and memories break faster than maps.

"He took a pilot," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Nine days ago. Rennick. He ran surveys over this basin. Aldous will have — he'll have gotten the location from him."

"You knew this man," Freya said. Not a question either.

"I knew him." I kept my eyes on the green bloom below. It seemed easier to confess to the water. "He owed money to everyone from Greyharbor to the Karelvo line. He'd have lasted—" I did the arithmetic and hated myself for doing it, hated how smoothly the numbers came. "He'd have lasted a day. Maybe less. Aldous doesn't use force the way you're imagining. He uses time. He just doesn't let you leave the room, and he keeps talking to you, politely, forever, until you'd tell him your mother's grave location to make it stop."

Sela spat over the ridge edge, a long deliberate arc, though whether at Aldous, at Rennick, or at me, she let remain ambiguous. "So the schedule's ash."

"The schedule's ash," I agreed. "The dive plan, the positioning sequence, all of it. What's left is what he does once he's here, and that part I still know. He'll flood the basin with lights first — show himself, scare off any competition. Then he'll set a perimeter. Patrol skiffs on the water, pickets on the haul road. By morning this valley will be a locked room."

"Then we're in it," Pip said.

There it was. The sentence none of us had said yet, hanging in the dark like the lamps below. I watched them each arrive at it separately — Pip first, because she was young and honest; Osk second, because he'd probably known since the first light bloomed and was waiting for someone else to say it; Sela third, with a slow curl of her lip that might have been fury or might have been relish; Freya last, or rather not at all, because Freya had been living inside that sentence for three years and had merely invited the rest of us in.

"Yes," I said. "We're in it."

* * *

We spent the night counting his fleet, because counting is what you do when you can't fight and can't flee and refuse to do either.

From the ridge, in the hours after midnight, the basin resolved itself into a geometry of menace. The *Corvane's Pride* sat anchored at the northern lip of the drowned valley, her running lights strung stem to stern like a carnival that had come to town to repossess it. Two salvage barges flanked the dive site, their cranes folded against the sky like sleeping insects. A patrol skiff ran a lazy circuit of the water, its searchlight sweeping the shoreline in long indifferent strokes, the way a landlord inspects rooms he already owns.

"There," Osk rumbled, pointing at a cluster of lanterns on the western shore. Tents. A picket line. Men moving between fires with the unhurried confidence of people who had not yet been shot at.

"Thirty, forty men ashore," Sela murmured. "Plus skiff crews. Plus divers." She glanced at me. "Your master travels well."

"He's not my master," I said, and heard how thin it sounded, and pressed on anyway. "The divers matter most. He'll run dive teams in shifts, four hours down, eight up. First teams will be surveying the wreck structure, mapping entry points. He won't go for the cargo hold immediately — he's methodical. He'll want the whole ship understood before he touches it."

"That buys us time," Freya said.

"It buys you a day," I said. "Maybe two. After that he finds the mail car, and after that nothing we do matters, because he'll have what he came for and the rest of us become a tidying problem."

Nobody asked what kind of tidying problem. Nobody needed to. I had watched Aldous tidy before.

What I didn't say — what I kept behind my teeth where I keep most things — was the other thing the lights told me. Three blooms, three hulls in position, anchors set in the dark of a single night. That wasn't haste. That was preparation that had begun before Rennick ever broke, before the *Pride* ever left Greyharbor. Which meant Aldous had suspected the location for days. Which meant my defection, my stolen schedule, my careful little theft in the night — he had known about it, or guessed it, and had simply declined to care, because a schedule is only useful to the people who believe in schedules, and Corvane Aldous believes in nothing but arriving.

He hadn't beaten my information. He'd made it worthless, the way you make a counterfeit bill worthless by buying the mint.

* * *

Near dawn, the patrol skiff's searchlight found the signal house.

I felt it before I saw it — a pale wash sliding across the broken roof, through the gap where glass used to be, crawling up the far wall like something drowning climbing out of the basin. We had banked the fire hours ago; the hut was cold and dark and we were flat against the floor with the discipline of people who had all, in various wars, learned what a searchlight means. Pip's breathing went shallow and quick against my ear. Osk put one enormous hand flat on her back, not comforting, just weighing, the way you'd hold down a hatch cover in weather.

The light held on us for ten seconds. Twenty. Long enough for me to count the rivets in the wall and compose, in order, three separate prayers to gods I'd stopped believing in during the fall of Karelvo, and one apology addressed specifically to the four people lying in the dark around me.

Then it moved on, unhurried, sweeping down toward the water, and somewhere below a whistle sounded twice — routine, dismissive, a man marking *nothing here* on his circuit sheet.

We lay still for another hundred heartbeats anyway. Some lessons you don't rush.

"They know the valley's occupied," Freya whispered finally. "They'll have seen our smoke yesterday. They're choosing not to come up."

"Because coming up costs men," Sela breathed back, "and he doesn't spend men when starving works cheaper. We've got food for five days. Water's fine, there's a spring. But the moment we move toward the water, they'll see us, and the moment they see us—"

"—we're a tidying problem," Pip finished, and gave a small hysterical laugh, and clapped her own hand over her mouth.

Freya rolled onto his back in the dark. I couldn't see his face, only the shape of him against the faint grey of the broken roof, and the stillness of him, which was not calm. I've stood next to calm men. Calm is loose. This was a drawn wire.

"There's another way down to the water," he said at last, so quietly I had to lean in to catch it. "Service tunnel. Maintenance access off the old summit line — it comes out behind the station yard, under the cliff. On paper it's sealed. On paper half the mountain is sealed." A pause. "Half-remembered blueprints and a bad lamp. That's what we've got."

"That's what we've always had," Sela said. "It's practically a motto."

Osk grunted near the plan, which from Osk was a benediction.

I stayed on my back and listened to the fleet below us — the clang of barge bells, the cough of compressors starting up, the first dive team of the morning being called to the rail in Aldous's clipped, courteous voice carrying impossibly across the water. Somewhere down there, in the green-lit cold, his divers were swimming through the dead train's corridors, touching the walls my brother's colleagues had touched, opening doors that had been closed since the war. And somewhere in that drowned iron, if Tomas Reyne's last report was worth anything, the truth about Corvane Aldous was sitting in a sealed diplomatic case, patient as a landmine, waiting for whoever got there first.

My brother. Yes. I haven't mentioned that yet, have I. There's a reason defectors don't lead with their whole inventory.

The searchlight swept past the ridge again, and below us the mountain's drowned heart glowed like a thing being interrogated, and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the door is already locked, that the race was over.

This was a siege now. And we were the ones inside the walls.

Freya began rolling up the blueprints.
