## 13. Through the Service Tunnel

The blueprints were in my head, which is the worst possible place to keep blueprints.

I want to be clear about that up front, because later — when Pip was coughing river water into my collar and Ilsa was asking me, with the flat calm of a woman doing arithmetic on other people's lives, how sure I was about the third junction — I want it on record that I answered honestly. I said: *sure enough.* Which is a word engineers use when they mean seventy percent and are hoping nobody asks for the decimal.

The problem with half-remembered drawings is that memory is a draftsman who works for free and takes liberties. It rounds corners. It fills gaps with optimism. Somewhere in the last three years of walking closed lines and measuring trestles, I had memorized the Serel maintenance schematics the way a man memorizes a prayer — not because he expects an answer, but because the repetition keeps something worse at bay. And prayers, whatever else they do, do not hold back water.

We went in at slack tide, because Osk said we would, and because the alternative was going in when the mountain was actively inhaling.

* * *

Let me describe the service tunnel before I describe what happened inside it, because the tunnel deserves its own paragraph, the way a bad road deserves a warning sign.

It ran behind and beneath the main gallery complex — a spine of poured concrete no wider than a coffin laid lengthwise, built in the last decade of Karelvo's existence by men who assumed the mountain would outlive the nation. They were right, in the way that graves are right. The walls sweated. The floor was a chain of puddles connected by ambition rather than water. Every forty meters there was a relief niche where a maintenance crew could press themselves flat while a handcar went by, and every hundred meters or so the ceiling dropped by a hand's width, as if the mountain had been slowly closing its fist around the idea of a railway for thirty years and had nearly finished.

Ilsa went first with the lamp, because she knew the fleet's positions and because Sela still didn't trust her enough to let her walk behind anyone's back. That's not a figure of speech. Sela had told her so, directly, at the fire two nights previous: *You walk in front where I can see your hands.* Ilsa had accepted this arrangement with the grace of someone who understood she'd earned it.

Osk came second, then Pip, then me, then Sela with the rifle wrapped in oilcloth against the wet. We moved in the crouched shuffle of people who have all, in different wars, learned that standing upright in a tunnel is a decision you make once.

"Talk to me, Voss," Sela said from behind. Her voice came low off the concrete. "Where does this thing come out?"

"Behind the fleet's staging gallery. There's a junction chamber maybe six hundred meters in — old ventilation crosscut. From there we drop into the north service gallery, come up under their floodlight line." I wiped sweat and tunnel-grime off my chin. "They'll have posted divers at the main approaches. Nobody watches a wall."

"Nobody watches a wall," Sela repeated, "because walls don't usually open."

"This one opens if you know where the hinges are."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then it's a very long wall."

She made a sound that in a better-lit place might have been a laugh. Behind me, Pip's breathing was quick and shallow, and I found myself counting it the way I counted trestles — one, two, three — as though rhythm were something I could lend her.

Here is the thing I hadn't said out loud yet, to her or to anyone: three years ago I would not have been counting. Three years ago I walked lines alone and liked them that way, because a crew is just a list of names you'll eventually lose, and I had already done my losing for one lifetime. Tomas taught me that lesson early and thoroughly. The war finished the syllabus.

But somewhere between Greyharbor and here — somewhere in Osk teaching Pip to clear her mask without panicking, in Sela splitting rations four ways without being asked, in Ilsa sitting awake through the night watch because sleep was a country she'd been exiled from — the arithmetic had changed. I hadn't noticed it happening. That's the trick of it. You don't notice a family assembling. You notice it the way you notice weather: suddenly, and only when it's already on top of you.

So I counted Pip's breaths, and I hated myself a little for how much it mattered, and we went deeper.

* * *

The first junction came where I said it would. Small mercies; I banked them.

The second junction was where memory started lying to me. In the blueprint — the real one, ink on linen, the one some dead Karelvan draftsman had drawn with a straightedge and a steady hand — there was a sump chamber at meter four-twenty, a low room where groundwater collected and got pumped out through a riser shaft. My memory had filed it as *damp.*

What we found was a lake.

Not a dramatic lake. No waves, no roar. Just black water filling the chamber from wall to wall, ceiling lost somewhere above it, the lamp light dying a meter past the surface like a held breath. A length of cable ran along the left wall at head height — the old pump conduit, still anchored, still carrying its insulators. A handline, if you trusted a cable that hadn't been inspected since before the armistice.

Osk put his lamp close to the water and read it the way Sela read rivers. "Two meters deep, maybe less. Cold enough to stop your hands in ten minutes." He looked back at us. "We go along the cable. One at a time. Face in the air pocket at the far side — there'll be one, the chamber roof rises past the sump line." He glanced at me. "There *is* a rise?"

"There's a rise," I said, seventy percenting it as hard as I could.

He nodded like seventy percent was a number men worked with, and maybe in his war it had been.

Sela went first — of course she did, she wasn't going to send Ilsa anywhere she wouldn't follow — and then Ilsa, whose strokes were fleet-trained and economical and made the rest of us look like what we were. Then Osk took Pip's pack and his own and swam them across one-handed, holding the cable with the other, and came back for his rifle, which he carried overhead like a man wading a sacred text across a river.

Pip went fifth. I went last, because I'm the engineer and the engineer goes last, that's not sentiment, that's doctrine — somebody has to be able to see the whole line fail.

Which is why I saw it happen.

* * *

She was three meters from the far wall when the mountain shifted.

I want to be precise, because precision is the only inheritance my trade ever gave me and I intend to spend it. It wasn't an explosion. It wasn't even loud, not really — a groan from somewhere below and behind us, deep in the structure, the sound concrete makes when a load path reroutes itself through members that were never asked to carry it. The fleet's blasting, probably. Aldous's dredgers chewing at the main gallery a few hundred meters away, indifferent to what their vibrations did to the skeleton of the place. Or maybe it was nothing but the mountain settling after thirty years of neglect, the way an old man settles into a chair he'll never get out of.

Either way, the cable moved.

Just a hand's width — the anchor bracket at the near wall, corroded through at its root, letting go with a crack like a knuckle popping. The cable sagged, whipped, and threw Pip sideways off her line. She went under with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, which is the sound of a person trying to shout underwater: a flat *whump*, nothing, bubbles.

"Pip—" Osk was already turning, but he was on the wrong wall, ten meters of black water between him and her, and the cold takes hands in ten minutes and panic in one.

I didn't decide anything. I want to say that too, because everyone always wants the hero to have decided. There was no decision. There was a gap in the world where Pip was supposed to be breathing, and my body crossed it before my mind was consulted — boots, coat, ledger, everything, straight off the ledge into water cold enough to make my skull ache like a struck bell.

The dark down there was total. Not lamplit dark — *mineral* dark, the dark of a place that has never once in its history been lit. I swam blind along the memory of the cable's line, lungs already burning, and my left hand hit fabric — pack strap, her pack, dragging her down. I cut it. Where the knife came from I couldn't tell you; the hands knew. Then her wrist, thin as a rail spike under my fingers, and I hauled.

She came up fighting. That's the part people don't understand about drowning — the drowning fight whoever saves them, because the body has decided the world is water and everything in it is the water's. She clawed my face, got a fist in my collar, drove me under once. I let her. I'd read about this in Osk's salvaged manuals, back when reading them was theory: *give the panicking swimmer the surface, take the grip from behind.* So I gave her the surface, dove, came up behind her, locked an arm across her chest, and kicked for the far ledge with one arm and both legs while she thrashed and the cold wrote its name in my bones.

Ten minutes, Osk had said, before the hands stop. I remember thinking, with the strange bookkeeping clarity that arrives in moments like this: *we will not need ten.*

My shoulder hit stone. Hands — Osk's, huge and certain — took her from me, and then Sela's voice was saying words I couldn't parse, and then I was on my back on wet concrete with the lamp swinging somewhere above me and Pip on her side, coughing, coughing, coughing, each convulsion a small victory.

"Up," Osk was saying to her, one hand flat on her sternum. "Good. Again. Get it out. Good girl."

"I'm f-fine," Pip managed, which is what everyone says, and then she was sick and shaking and saying it again, *I'm fine, I'm fine,* and Sela wrapped her in the spare coat and held her like the coat was load-bearing.

I lay there getting my breath back and discovered my own hands were shaking worse than hers. Three years of walking alone, measuring miles, keeping the world at ledger's distance — and one girl going under had undone all of it in eleven seconds. I could feel the change in me the way you feel a bridge take a new load: not painfully, exactly. Just aware, now, of everything I was carrying.

"You went in after her," Sela said. She'd come over to me. Her face was unreadable, which with Sela meant the opposite of indifference.

"Somebody had to. Osk was on the wrong wall."

"Voss." She crouched. "You can't swim."

"I grew up on a river town."

"That's not the same thing and you know it." But something in her face had shifted, some assessment concluded and filed. "You went in anyway."

"It seemed like the thing to do at the time."

She snorted, stood, and went back to Pip, and I understood that I had passed some examination I hadn't known I was sitting.

* * *

We lost twenty minutes to the crossing and the shaking that followed it, and I spent them the way I spend everything: doing sums. The fleet's dive rotation, per Ilsa's stolen schedule, gave us a window measured in hours, and the window was shrinking with every meter of tunnel we paid for in time. The cold was in all of us now. Pip's lips had gone from blue toward merely gray, which Osk pronounced acceptable, and I chose to believe him because the alternative was turning back, and turning back meant surfacing into Aldous's floodlights with nothing.

"The rise," I said, when we moved again. "Past the sump. Air pocket. Thirty meters, then the junction chamber."

"You said that before the lake," Sela noted.

"I say a lot of things. Most of them hold."

"One in three isn't odds I'd ship cargo on."

"Good thing you're shipping people instead. They float."

That earned me a look over her shoulder that contained, buried somewhere in it, actual warmth. Progress.

The rise was there. I'd like to report that I felt vindicated, but mostly I felt the specific exhaustion of a man whose luck keeps working and knows, statistically, that it can't. We climbed out of the water into a pocket of stale air under a ceiling of dripping rock, and Osk got a fire-pan going — no flame, just chemical heat, the kind deserters learn to love — and we sat in a row like laundry drying, and Pip leaned against my shoulder because there was nowhere else warm, and I sat extremely still so as not to disturb the arrangement.

"You know," she said quietly, when her teeth had stopped chattering enough to permit conversation, "back at the fire, when you asked why I came — I said the wages."

"I remember."

"It wasn't the wages." She was looking at the far wall, at nothing. "My whole town flooded when they closed the Serel branch. Everyone scattered. I've been apprentice to four different crews since and none of them ever—" She stopped, restarted. "You carry people out of water. That's the job, basically. That's the whole job."

"Sometimes the job is rails."

"Rails are just how you get to the water," she said, with the terrible logic of the young, and fell asleep against my shoulder mid-sentence, which I took as a verdict.

Ilsa watched us from across the pocket, lamp low. "Aldous would have left her," she said. Not accusingly. Worse than that — informatively, the way she delivered most things, as data points from inside the machine. "Fleet protocol. An incapacitated diver is ballast. He'd have logged the loss and adjusted the schedule."

"I know," I said.

"He offered me a commission once. Told me power was just the distance between your decisions and their consequences." Her mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "I used to think that was wisdom."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's just a long way to fall." She turned her lamp toward the continuation of the tunnel. "Junction chamber's ahead. Your wall, Voss. Let's see if it opens."

* * *

It opened.

I won't pretend the hinge mechanism cooperated — thirty years of corrosion had welded the counterweight track, and we spent forty minutes and most of our remaining pry-bar leverage persuading it — but when the panel finally swung inward on screaming hinges, it swung into darkness that smelled of diesel and rope and human occupation. The fleet's staging gallery. Their backs, in other words, turned to us.

Below and beyond, through a grate rusted into lace, I could see them: floodlights blooming green-white in the flooded cavern beyond, diver lights moving among them like slow fish, the silhouette of Aldous's dredging gantry straddling the water. And there, tilted in the glow, half-swallowed by the dark —

The Meridian Star.

Three years of miles ended at that grate. I gripped the rusted iron and looked at my brother's train, and behind me I heard Sela check the oilcloth on her rifle, and Osk flex his hands, and Pip — awake again, gray but upright — whisper, "That's it. That's really it."

"Yes," I said.

And then Ilsa, beside me, went rigid. "That's wrong," she breathed. "Their dive rotation — those aren't salvage lights. Look at the spacing. That's a search pattern."

"A search for what?" Sela asked.

Ilsa's answer came flat and fast, the way she said things that scared her:

"For us. Someone told them we were coming."
