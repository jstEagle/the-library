## 14. The Meridian Star

The cavern opened around us like the inside of a bell that had been rung once, violently, and never allowed to stop ringing.

Ilsa's lamp swept across a ceiling of wet stone hung with mineral curtains, and below us — far below, down a slope of collapsed ballast and drowned track bed — a shape that my mind refused for three full seconds to name.

It was a train.

Of course it was a train. I had known it was a train since the night I pulled a manifest out of a rotting chest on the riverbank. But knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals entirely, and the animal in front of me was enormous: the *Meridian Star*, lying on her side at maybe forty degrees, half-buried in silt, her boiler forward section crushed where she'd come off the old Serel incline and slid into what was then, presumably, open tunnel before the mountain drank it whole. Three years underwater. Three years of cold black water moving through her windows like slow ghosts.

"There she is," Osk said, and his voice through the rebreather line sounded strange — flattened, reverent, the way men talk in churches they don't believe in.

"Sixty years of engineering," Pip said, "and she looks like a dropped toy."

"She's not a toy," I said. "She's a grave. Mind your manners."

I didn't mean it as a joke, but Sela laughed anyway, a short bark of static in my ear. That's Sela. She finds the laugh in everything because the alternative is finding the bottom of a bottle, and she's already been down there and didn't like the rent.

We descended along the old service catwalk that clung to the cavern wall — Ilsa had it on Aldous's charts, which meant Aldous had it, which meant we were walking a route our enemy could walk behind us at any hour. I kept telling myself the fleet was still two tides out. I kept not quite believing it. Hope and paranoia make poor bunkmates; neither one sleeps.

The water grew colder as we dropped. The lamps made small trembling circles, and beyond them the dark had texture — it wasn't empty, it was *full*, full of stone and silt and the slow chemistry of a mountain digesting a railway. Somewhere above us, sealed and forgotten, were platforms where commuters had once complained about the coffee. Now the only passengers were eels and the occasional bewildered pike that had swum up from the reservoir.

The mail car came into view first, because the mail car had been at the rear, and the rear had broken free when she went down. It lay apart from the main hulk, upright-ish, tilted against a fallen rib of tunnel arch like a drunk leaning on a fence post. Karelvo Royal Mail, the faded lettering said, gold leaf gone green. The door hung open. Water had been going in and out of that door for three years, patient as a tax collector.

"Tomas rode in that car," I heard myself say.

Nobody answered. There was nothing to answer. We went in.

* * *

Inside, the mail car was a cathedral of paper.

That sounds like poetry. It was actually a horror. Three years of submerged correspondence — sacks of it, thousands of them, swollen into a single fused mass that filled the car from floor to ceiling like some pale fungal growth. Letters. All those letters. People writing to their mothers, their sweethearts, their banks, their lawyers, their government ministries, all of it stopped mid-journey, mid-sentence, delivered nowhere by a train that never arrived anywhere again.

Pip ran her lamp along the nearest sack and read an address aloud, her voice cracking on the second word. A town in the eastern valleys. A town I remembered from the war maps, the kind of town you learned about by learning it no longer existed.

"Leave it," I said, gentler than I felt. "We're not here for the mail."

"We're here for diplomatic cases," Ilsa said. "Sealed cases, mail car, per the manifest." She'd said it before, twice, and each time I caught the flicker in her eyes — the question of whether she was leading us to treasure or leading us into Aldous's hand. I'd decided, provisionally, to trust her, mostly because Sela hadn't thrown her off the skiff yet, and Sela's instincts were better than any chart I owned.

We dug.

It was ugly work. The mass of sodden paper came away in slabs, and beneath it the car's fixtures emerged one by one — sorting racks, a dead lantern, a clerk's stool still bolted to the floor as if its owner might be back after lunch. My hands found things I tried not to look at. A child's drawing, ink blurred to a blue ghost. A wedding invitation. An envelope marked URGENT in a handwriting that had been urgent for three years and would stay urgent forever.

Forty minutes in, Osk's crowbar rang against metal.

"Case," he said. "Here. Under the sacks — wedged in the rack."

It was exactly what the manifest promised: a steel diplomatic case, Karelvo's double-headed eagle stamped into the lid, the seal intact, the lock intact, the whole thing preserved by cold water and the particular stubbornness of wartime bureaucracy. Osk hauled it free and set it on the sorting table, and we stood around it in a ring of lamplight like mourners at a very strange funeral.

"Is that it?" Pip asked. "The fortune?"

"That's it," Ilsa said. "Whatever's in it."

"Could be gold," Sela said hopefully.

"Could be receipts," Ilsa said.

"It's not either," I said.

They looked at me. I was looking at the case, but not at the case — at the space beside it in the rack, where the silt pattern was wrong. Where something rectangular had lain for years and then been taken out recently, recently enough that the sediment hadn't fully resettled. Recently, if I was any judge of water, which I am, within the last few days.

Or weeks.

My heart did something complicated.

"Someone's been here," I said.

"Aldous?" Osk's hand went to his knife, which is Osk's answer to most questions.

"No. Look at the silt. This was disturbed before the fleet launched — before anyone knew this cavern was reachable. And whoever took it didn't take it out the way we came in." I turned my lamp toward the rear wall of the car, where the emergency panel sat behind its rusted hatch. The hatch was ajar. Behind it, the maintenance crawlway — the one that ran parallel to the old incline, the one I'd half-remembered from blueprints that had gotten four people alive through a collapsing tunnel two days ago.

Someone had crawled out of a sunken train through a flooded service tunnel. Alone. In the dark.

And then I saw the book.

It was tucked into the frame of the emergency panel, wrapped in oilcloth tied with wire, and it was dry. Not damp — *dry*, sealed well enough that three years of the mountain's patience hadn't touched it. My hands were shaking so badly I couldn't work the wire, and Pip reached over without a word and undid it for me, because Pip notices things, including the things I'd rather she didn't.

A journal. Cheap bound paper, the kind railwaymen carry. On the flyleaf, in a hand I would have known blindfolded, upside down, across a crowded room, across three years and a war:

*T.V.*

Tomas Voss.

* * *

I don't remember sitting down. I remember the case under me — I was sitting on the diplomatic case, which later struck me as funny in a way that nothing else has managed to strike me lately — and I remember the lamp held steady in Pip's hand because mine wouldn't hold steady, and I remember opening the cover.

His handwriting. God, his handwriting. He wrote the way he drove a locomotive — fast, confident, slightly too much pressure on the curves. I'd spent three years measuring my life in miles specifically so I wouldn't have to think about that handwriting, and now here it was, filling page after page, and every mile I'd ever walked collapsed to zero.

The early entries were ordinary. That nearly broke me worse than anything that followed. Fuel figures. Timetable complaints. A joke about the dining car's soup that was exactly the kind of joke Tomas told, the kind you laugh at and then spend a decade failing to forget. Dated six weeks before the sinking. He'd been running the Meridian Star's mail route for a season. He mentioned me once — *"my brother writes that he's mapping the closed lines. Someone should tell him the lines aren't closed, they're just tired."*

Someone should tell him his brother isn't grieving, he's just tired. Same disease. Different symptom.

Then the entries changed.

*"Something wrong with the manifests. Third time this month a case comes aboard unlisted. Heavy ones. Guarded ones. Crew told to ask nothing. Asking nothing has never been my best subject."*

I read faster. The lamp circle seemed to shrink.

*"Followed the guard's schedule tonight. Cases board at Greyharbor, always Greyharbor, always signed out by an agent who doesn't give a name. I copied the seal impression. It matches nothing in the postal registry. It matches something else — I've seen it before, on requisition papers during the retreat, papers nobody was supposed to see. I know whose office used that stamp."*

I knew too. I'd known since Greyharbor, since Sela leaned close on the dock and said the words *Corvane Aldous wants more than cargo.* But knowing a thing secondhand is a coat you can take off. Knowing it in your dead brother's handwriting is a coat stitched to your skin.

*"He's selling routes." The entry was short, pressed hard enough to tear the page. "Not cargo. Routes. Evacuation corridors out of Karelvo — the safe passages, the ones civilians were given. He's been passing them to enemy gunners through the shipping brokers. That's why the columns died. That's why the roads died. They weren't lost. They were SOLD. Thousands of people walked exactly where he told the guns to look."*

The water in the mail car was cold. I know it was cold, objectively, four degrees maybe, cold enough that Osk had been timing our exposure in careful increments. I felt none of it. I felt like I was burning.

Sela put a hand on my shoulder. She didn't say anything. There was nothing to say and she's smart enough to know it.

The last entries were dated days before the sinking. Tomas had hidden the original documents — the route schedules, the broker correspondence, the proof — in a diplomatic case and booked it aboard the Star unlisted, planning to move it somewhere safe, somewhere it could hang the man who'd sold a nation's families to the artillery. And someone had found out. The final entry, written in a hurry, the pen tearing through in places:

*"They know. Boarding tonight regardless. If anything happens, the case is in the mail car, third rack, under the grey sacks. If I don't come back — tell Freya it wasn't the river that took me. Tell him I finally found a line worth mapping."*

Tell him I finally found a line worth mapping.

I am not going to describe what happened to me next, except to say that Pip held the lamp, and Osk stood watch at the door, and Sela stayed with her hand on my shoulder for as long as it took, which was longer than any of them signed on for, and none of them left. That's the whole report. That's all anyone needs to know.

When I could speak again, I said: "Third rack. Under the grey sacks."

Osk and I turned to the racks together, and there it was — the disturbance in the silt I'd noticed, the rectangle gone from its resting place. Except it wasn't gone. Wedged deeper in the rack, behind a false panel Tomas himself must have rigged — my brother, the locomotive engineer, hiding documents the way I hid grief, in the structure of things — sat a second case. Smaller. Sealed. Dry.

He'd moved it. Before the end, he'd moved it deeper, and he'd left the journal where a searching hand would find the first case's absence and go looking — and find him instead. Find his voice. Find the truth wearing his handwriting like a signature.

"He wanted whoever came looking to understand first," Ilsa said quietly. "Before they took the evidence. He wanted them to know what it cost."

"He wanted *me*," I said. "Somehow he figured I'd be the one."

"Did he leave anything else?" Sela asked.

I turned the last pages. Tucked behind the back cover, a single loose sheet, folded twice. A hand-drawn sketch — a route, in railwayman's notation, out of the cavern system through the deep galleries, marked with times and tide levels. An escape plan. Drawn by a man who planned to survive, right up until he didn't.

And beneath the sketch, one more line, added later, the letters slower, deliberate:

*"If you're reading this, little brother, the water's rising and I've got a case and a promise to keep. One of us gets to be angry about all this. Make it count."*

I folded the sheet and put it inside my jacket, against my chest, where it sat like a coal.

Then the lamp at the door went dark — Osk's hand clapped over it — and his voice came low through the water, flat as a depth gauge:

"Lights. Above us. Moving down the catwalk."

Not our lights. Too many. Too white. Too orderly.

Aldous hadn't been two tides out after all. He'd been directly overhead the entire time — and now he was coming down.
