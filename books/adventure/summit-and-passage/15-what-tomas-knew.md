## 15. What Tomas Knew

*Freya*

The lamp we'd salvaged from the drowned station burned with a stutter, the kind of light that made shadows move like they had opinions. I sat with Tomas's journal on my knees and didn't open it for a long time. There's a particular cowardice in a man who can face a flooded tunnel but not his brother's handwriting.

The others gave me room. Osk checked his dive gear twice, which was his way of praying. Pip curled against Sela's side like a cat pretending not to watch. Ilsa stood at the edge of the lamplight, half in it, half out — which, I was learning, was where she lived.

"Read it," Sela said finally. Not unkind. Just done waiting. "Dead men don't write books to be admired. They write them to be heard."

So I opened it.

The first pages were ordinary, and that nearly broke me worse than anything that came after. Tomas wrote about the weather on the mountain line. About a broken coupling he'd fixed with wire and stubbornness. About the coffee in the Meridian Star's dining car, which he described as "an insult to beans everywhere." His hand slanted right, quick and impatient, the same hand that used to beat me at cards by cheating badly enough that you couldn't prove it.

I laughed. It came out wrong, somewhere between a laugh and something else, and nobody looked away, which I appreciated more than I can say.

Then the entries changed.

*They've moved me to the mail car detail. Odd cargo this run. Sealed diplomatic cases, chained to the frame. Aldous's own seal on them. A man doesn't chain documents unless someone else wants to read them.*

I read that line three times. Around me, the cavern dripped its slow count, water finding its way down through a hundred years of stone.

"He was crew," I said. "Not a passenger. He took a job on the Star."

"To get aboard," Ilsa said quietly. "Or to stay aboard. There's a difference."

"There always is," Sela said.

I kept reading. The lamp guttered; Pip got up without being asked and fed it oil from our dwindling stock, an extravagance none of us commented on.

*Asked the purser what's in the cases. He said "paper." Paper doesn't need armed escort. Paper doesn't need a man from Aldous's own office riding along, sweating through his coat in October.*

*Followed the escort man tonight. He met someone at the Greyharbor layover. Civilian clothes, government bearing. I know that walk. I've seen it on men who sign things that end other people.*

My hands had gone cold. Not the cavern's cold — another kind, rising up from under the ribs.

"Tomas wasn't running from the war," I said. "He was investigating it."

Nobody answered. The journal did the answering.

*Found a copy of the routing orders in the mail car waste bin — careless of them, or arrogant, or both. Cross-checked against the schedules I keep for the line. The evacuation corridors out of eastern Karelvo were published to the enemy's gunnery offices six weeks before the fall. Every bridge, every tunnel window, every timetable. They weren't shelled at random. They were shelled on schedule.*

Sela made a sound low in her throat, like something tearing loose underwater.

I kept reading because stopping would have meant drowning in it, and I've always been better moving than still.

*I have the original orders now. Copied the cipher key. If I'm wrong about who signed them, then I'm a dead fool. If I'm right, then Corvane Aldous sold forty thousand people their deaths and billed the railway for the transport.*

Forty thousand. The number sat in the lamplight like a stone none of us could lift.

"That's the evacuation," Ilsa said. Her voice had gone flat, controlled, the voice of a woman holding a door shut from the inside. "The eastern corridor. I was — I worked those timetables. We told families the routes were safe. We printed pamphlets."

She stopped. Whatever came next, she swallowed it, and I decided I'd never ask her to take it back out.

"The gunners knew when the trains ran," Osk said slowly. He'd gone very still, the way he went still before a dive. "That's why the bridges went down clean. That's why the tunnels — I pulled bodies out of the Serel cut for two months after. We thought it was luck. Their luck."

"It was arithmetic," I said. "Tomas found the ledger."

* * *

*Sela*

I've watched men read death warrants before. Usually their own. Freya read his brother's, page by page, and the terrible thing was how calm he stayed — the calm of a boiler building pressure past every safe mark, needle climbing, no gauge left to read it.

I know that calm. I wore it for two years after the war took my boat and everyone who ever slept aboard her. It's not peace. It's the sound a thing makes just before it decides what it's going to become.

"Keep going," I said, because someone had to, and because I wanted to know too. Don't let anyone tell you curiosity is a gentle thing. Curiosity is what drags you to the wreck site. It's what makes you open chests stamped with the seals of drowned countries.

Freya turned pages. The entries got shorter as October became November. Shorter and harder, like a man writing while walking, or running.

*Escort man caught me copying. I talked my way clear — told him I was auditing freight weights. He believed me or pretended to. Either way they know someone's asking questions. Moving the evidence tonight. Chaining it into the mail car frame where no one will look, behind the sorting racks. If the Star reaches the capital, it goes to the press. If it doesn't reach the capital—*

There the entry stopped mid-sentence. The next one was dated four days later.

*They've changed the route. New orders, top priority, sealed. The Star runs the Serel line instead of the coast. Nobody reroutes a diplomatic train through a war zone unless they want it in one.*

I felt the whole cavern tilt. Or maybe that was me.

"He saw it coming," Pip whispered. She'd crept close without any of us noticing, drawn like everything young is drawn to fire and grief. "He knew the train was going to sink."

"He knew more than that," Freya said. His voice came out sanded flat. "Look at the date. The reroute order is dated three days before the Star went down. And look who countersigned it."

Ilsa leaned in. I watched her read the name, and I watched the last of whatever she'd been carrying for Aldous die in her face. Good, I thought, with a cruelty I didn't bother to apologize for. Let it die. Dead weight sinks ships.

"Aldous," she said. "Countersigned the reroute himself. He put the train in the guns' path."

"No," Freya said, and here his voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture across all that pressure. "He put it exactly where he wanted it. Think. The evidence was aboard. Chained to the mail car frame. You don't let forty thousand people's death warrant sail to the capital and into print. You sink it. You sink it with the crew who found it, and you call it enemy action, and you build a salvage company on the wreck twenty years later so no one else ever gets there first."

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if it were listening.

"And Tomas," I said, because somebody had to say it, and I'd rather it be me than watch Freya do it to himself. "Was he on the train when it went down?"

Freya turned to the last written page. I'd been watching him ration them, the way you ration water in a lifeboat. This one he read silently first, lips moving, and I saw him age ten years between the first word and the last.

Then he read it aloud.

*If you're reading this, I didn't make it off, or I made it off without the case, and either way the truth is still down there with the Star. Freya — if it's you, little brother, if it's somehow you — don't grieve me yet. Get the case. Hang him with it. And then go home and measure something in years instead of miles, because I know you, and I know what you'll do with the grief, and I'm telling you now: don't. The case is in the floor safe under the sorting rack, third rivet from the door, painted over. Trust the river people. Trust the deserter. Trust no one who smells of money.*

I looked around the circle. Osk, the deserter, had his jaw tight. I was the river people. And Freya —

Freya closed the journal with both hands, gently, the way you close a coffin, and sat there breathing.

"He knew us," Pip said, bewildered. "He wrote that before he ever met us. How did he know?"

"Because he knew his brother," Osk said. "And he guessed the rest. Men like Voss travel in types."

"Third rivet from the door," Freya said. "Painted over." He looked up, and his eyes in the lamplight were the eyes of a man standing at the top of a long drop, deciding whether falling might not be flying. "It's still down there. Twenty years at the bottom of a flooded mountain, and it's still down there, chained in a floor safe, waiting."

"Along with everything Aldous sent divers to find," Ilsa said. "Now you understand why he claimed the wreck by decree. Why he launches in six tides. It was never gold."

"It was never gold," Freya agreed. "It was a hanging."

I want to tell you I felt triumph then. What I actually felt was colder and older: recognition. I'd spent years hauling salvage out of the river, and the river teaches you one law above all others — nothing stays down unless the living agree to leave it there. Aldous had built an empire on that agreement. And now four strays and a defector in a flooded tunnel under a dying mountain intended to break it.

"Then we'd best learn to dive," I said, and stood, and started checking Osk's gear myself, because grief is a fine fuel but it burns fast, and work is what keeps the lamp lit.

Freya didn't move for a long moment. Then he tucked the journal inside his coat, over his heart, where a man carries the things that aim him.

"One more thing," he said quietly. "The last entry. Look at the ink."

I looked. Beneath the final lines, faint, pressed hard enough to score the paper but never inked — a pen lifted mid-word — there was a single unfinished stroke. The beginning of a name. T. V., and then nothing.

"He was interrupted," Osk said. "Writing it."

"Or he chose to stop," Freya said. "Because he'd already said everything that mattered."

Above us, far up through the flooded galleries, there came a sound none of us wanted to hear: the deep, rhythmic thrum of pumps. Not ours.

Aldous hadn't waited for six tides after all.
