## 16. Betrayal at Depth

Osk Brann had a theory about drowning. He'd developed it over eleven years of commercial diving, two wars, and one marriage that ended the way most of his things ended — underwater. The theory was this: drowning wasn't the water's fault. The water just did what water did. Drowning was a paperwork problem. Somebody, somewhere, had signed off on a bad number, and you were the number.

He was thinking about this because he was currently forty feet down in a flooded rail tunnel beneath Mount Serel, holding a lantern in one hand and a wrench in the other, checking the seals on a bulkhead door that hadn't been maintained since before the war, and the number he'd signed off on — *two hours of air, easy* — was starting to look like bad paperwork.

The cavern opened ahead of him like a cathedral that God had abandoned mid-renovation. The Meridian Star lay on its side in the gloom, a hundred and forty tons of dead locomotive and passenger cars, its brass fittings furred white with mineral bloom. Above it, the drowned station. Around it, the dark, pressing in with the patience of something that had already won.

He checked his gauge again. Still bad paperwork.

"Osk." Freya's voice crackled through the line — they'd rigged a telephone wire between the surface camp and the work site, copper thread singing through forty feet of black water, and it was the finest piece of engineering Osk had ever been party to, mostly because it meant he could hear another human being while he worked. "How's the door?"

"Door's a liar," Osk said. "Seals are shot. I can pack them with canvas and tallow, buy us maybe a day if the fleet comes knocking. But if they come knocking *hard*—"

"How hard is hard?"

"You remember the Kestrel Lock disaster? Nineteen-eleven?"

"I remember the song."

"The song is generous."

He worked the canvas into the seal grooves by feel, the lantern light going amber and soft, and for a few minutes there was just the work, and the work was good. Osk had learned in the war that the only reliable mercy was a task with edges. You could hold a task like a rail in a storm. Everything else — country, cause, the face of the girl you'd married in a fever of leave-time — everything else washed out from under you.

He'd been a combat diver for Karelvo in the last year of the war. Sapper, technically. His job had been to swim into harbors and make other people's ships stop being ships. He'd been good at it, which was the problem. Good at it meant they kept sending him, and kept not telling him what was on the ships, and one gray morning in the harbor at Vell his charge had gone off under a ferry that the manifest said was carrying munitions and that the screaming said was carrying families. He'd surfaced, taken off his rig, walked inland, and kept walking for six years.

So he understood desertion from the inside. It wasn't cowardice. Cowardice would've been easier. It was arithmetic. You added up what your obedience had purchased and the sum came back *families*, and after that you were done with causes. Causes were just arithmetic with the ugly columns hidden.

Which was why, when he surfaced that evening and found the crew around the fire — Freya reading Tomas's journal again by lamplight, Sela cleaning a pistol that didn't need cleaning, Pip asleep sitting up with her head against a crate, Ilsa Reyne sitting slightly apart the way she always did — he felt the arithmetic start again, and hated it.

Because Ilsa had been wrong about something. Small. The kind of wrong you only catch if you've spent eleven years memorizing numbers.

She'd said the fleet's dive rotation was six hours on, six off. He'd asked her to confirm the surface-supply hose lengths, because hose length told you working depth, and working depth told you how long Aldous's divers could stay on the wreck. She'd said eighty meters. Eighty meters of hose meant a compressor barge, and a compressor barge meant the *Iron Corvane* herself was anchored closer than her charts showed.

He hadn't said anything. He'd told himself he'd misheard, or she'd misspoken, or it didn't matter.

But Osk had learned the hard way what it meant when a number didn't sit right in your gut. It meant somebody had signed off on it.

* * *

The signal came at slack tide, three nights later.

Osk was on watch, which was a generous word for a man sitting on a rock with a rifle he'd sworn off using, watching the black water of the flooded tunnel mouth breathe in and out with the river's rhythm. The camp was a ledge of collapsed masonry above the waterline, deep enough inside Mount Serel that the sky was a coin of gray through a shattered ventilation shaft. You could forget the world existed down here. Some part of Osk had been trying to for six years.

He heard it before he saw it — a click, metal on metal, soft as a beetle turning over.

Across the ledge, in the dark, Ilsa Reyne's hand was on the signal lamp. The shutter was half-raised. Beyond the tunnel mouth, far out on the flooded gallery, a light answered. One flash. Two. Gone.

Osk didn't move. That was the strange part, the part he'd think about later. Eleven years of instinct said *shoot*, and his hands stayed in his lap like two dead fish. Maybe it was because shooting Ilsa meant admitting what the flash meant, and admitting it meant the fleet was coming, and the fleet coming meant the door, and the door meant—

The arithmetic. Always the arithmetic.

He watched her close the shutter. Watched her sit back down against the wall, fold her hands, and become, in the space of four seconds, a sleeping woman. It was masterful. He'd have applauded if his stomach weren't trying to climb out through his ribs.

He looked at the rifle. He looked at the tunnel mouth, where the answering light had died.

Then he did the bravest cowardly thing of his life, which was nothing. He sat on his rock until his watch ended, and he woke Freya, and he didn't say a word, because a word would make it real, and there was still — God help him, there was still a part of him hoping he'd imagined the beetle-click, the shutter, the answering flash. Hoping the numbers were wrong.

The numbers were never wrong. That was the whole tragedy of Osk Brann. He'd have made a magnificent optimist if he'd ever been allowed one wrong number.

* * *

The fleet came at dawn, and they came through the water, which was the one direction nobody had fortified, because nobody fortifies against the sea coming through a mountain.

Osk heard it as a change in pressure first — his old ears knew the feel of displaced water the way farmers know rain. Then the telephone line from the lower gallery crackled and died, cut clean. Then the floodlights bloomed in the depths of the tunnel mouth, six of them in a ragged crescent, and the black water turned the color of strong tea, and shapes moved in it. Divers. A dozen at least, hard-hat rigs, surface-supplied, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had been told the door would be open.

*The door would be open.*

Osk was already moving. He didn't remember deciding to move. He was at the bulkhead door with the wrench in his hand and the wheel spinning under his grip, and behind him Freya was shouting his name and Sela was shouting something else, and ahead of him, in the tea-colored dark, the first diver's lamp swung toward the sound of the wheel.

"Osk, get *back*—"

"Wheel's jammed!" Osk shouted back, and it wasn't, quite, but it was going to be, because he'd pulled the cotter pin from the spindle and in ninety seconds that door would be a wall and nothing on this earth would open it from the water side, and from this side it would take a charge, and the only charge was in his satchel, the old one, the one he'd carried for six years like a relic of the man he refused to be.

"Osk." Freya's voice came low and terrible through the wire. "Osk, don't."

"Somebody holds the door," Osk said. "That's the job. That was always the job. You just didn't put it on the list."

"You're not a door."

"No," Osk said, and laughed, because it was either laugh or do the arithmetic out loud, and the arithmetic went like this: one deserter, six years used up, against a girl of nineteen with her whole ledger blank, a smuggler with a brother to get back to, and an engineer who'd finally found something to measure that wasn't his own grief. "But I've been a wall before. I'm told I'm very good at it."

The first diver reached the light's edge. Osk saw the brass of the helmet, the glass of the faceplate, and behind the glass a young man's face, scared, ordinary, someone's arithmetic too. Osk felt no hate for him. That was the mercy of the work — you never hated the water.

"Ilsa," he said into the wire, and heard her breath catch on the other end. He'd known for three days. He wanted her to know that he'd known, and that it changed nothing about what he did next, because what he did next wasn't for the living to grade. "The hose lengths. You gave them the compressor barge position. I hope he's paying you in something better than forgiveness."

"Osk—" Ilsa's voice, cracking.

"Tell Pip the seals were fine," he said. "I did good work. I want that on the record. Six years of nothing and I want *one thing* on the record."

He pulled the spindle free. The wheel spun loose and dead in his hands, and the door became a wall, and the divers' lamps pressed against it like moths, and he wedged himself into the frame with the satchel against his chest and the detonator in his fist, and for one clear moment — the clearest in six years — Osk Brann was not drowning, was not deserting, was not a bad number in somebody's ledger.

He was a door, and he was shut.

"Freya," he said. "Get them out. All of them. That's the manifest. That's the whole cargo."

The last thing he heard through the wire was Freya Voss saying his name — not shouting it, saying it, the way you say a word you intend to keep — and then Osk Brann closed his fist, and the mountain closed its hand, and the tunnel door became a tomb with a very good seal, packed with canvas and tallow by a professional.

* * *

The blast came up through the rock like the mountain clearing its throat.

Freya felt it in his teeth, in the soles of his boots, in the ledger against his ribs where he'd stowed Tomas's journal wrapped in oilcloth. The ledge jumped. Dust fell from the ventilation shaft in a gray curtain. Pip screamed — not a word, just a sound, the sound of a person whose understanding of the world has just been amputated — and Sela caught her, hauled her back from the edge, held her down with both arms while the roar rolled through the galleries and came back changed, again and again, a tide of noise with nowhere to go.

Then silence. The specific, mineral silence of deep stone.

Freya stood at the edge of the ledge and looked down into the tunnel mouth, where the water was still shivering, and the floodlights of the fleet were dead or dying behind a wall that no longer had a door in it, and somewhere on the far side of that wall was a man who had held a jammed wheel and told a joke about walls and asked for one thing on the record.

"The seals were fine," Freya said aloud. His voice didn't sound like his. "You did good work."

He'd known Osk Brann for eleven days. Eleven days, and the man had walked out of the river with a diver's scars and a deserter's silence and had rebuilt Freya's whole understanding of what courage looked like — not the charging kind, not the flag kind. The kind that was just a man doing the task in front of him with edges on it, holding the rail in the storm, and never once asking the storm to be anything but a storm.

And he'd known — Freya made himself look at it, turned the knowledge over in the light like a bad coin — he'd known about Ilsa. He'd seen it in Osk's face these last three days, the way the old diver watched her, not with suspicion but with a kind of grieving attention, the way you watch a leak you've decided not to report because reporting it would sink everyone. Osk had carried it alone so the crew could keep working. Had carried it alone and then died for it, and died *with it*, and the last thing he'd said into that wire had been for her.

Freya turned.

Ilsa Reyne was standing against the far wall with her hands at her sides, and she wasn't running. He understood that later, when he had time to understand anything — that she'd heard every word through the wire, that she'd stood there and let him turn, because some part of her had already been tried and sentenced by her own court and only wanted the sentence read aloud by someone else.

"You gave them the barge position," Freya said. Flat. He had rage in him, a whole ocean of it, and he set it down between them like a crate neither of them would touch. "Aldous tortured a location out of an informant, Ilsa. We watched the floodlights come two tides early and we thought it was the informant. It was you. It was you the whole time."

"He has my brother," Ilsa said. The words came out of her like splinters, one at a time. "He's had him since Vell. He sends me proof of life every month. A photograph. A date written on his hand. I've been navigating that man's fleet for two years because every month there's a photograph, and every month I tell myself the next job is the last job, and—" Her voice failed and restarted, lower. "The job was never the last job. I knew that. I knew it the way you know a number's wrong and sign off anyway."

Freya looked at her for a long time. Around them the camp was a shambles of trampled canvas and scattered tools; Pip was folded into Sela's shoulder, shaking; the water below the ledge was going dark again as the fleet's lights died behind Osk's wall. Somewhere beyond that wall, Aldous's divers were regrouping, and they had explosives of their own, and the wall would buy hours, not days.

"He'll breach it," Sela said quietly, over Pip's head. "Aldous doesn't stop. You know that."

"I know that."

"So we've got hours. What do we want to do with them?"

Freya looked at the water. He thought about the mail car, and the journal, and the sealed diplomatic cases in the cargo hold with their Karelvo seals intact, and he thought about Osk's last request — *get them out, all of them, that's the manifest, that's the whole cargo* — and he understood, with the terrible clarity that grief sometimes issues like a writ, what the manifest was.

The evidence was the cargo. The crew was the manifest. And Osk had just paid the freight.

"We go down one more time," Freya said. "All of us. We take the cases, and we take the journal, and we go out through the service tunnel before Aldous puts a charge on that door. And Ilsa—" he made himself say it, made it a rope thrown across a gap rather than a blade, because Osk's last word to her had not been a blade, "—you carry the cases. You carry them to the surface with your own hands. That's not trust. That's the job. Do the job and we'll see what's left when it's done."

Ilsa Reyne looked at him, and something in her face broke and re-formed into something harder and cleaner, the way ore breaks and re-forms in a furnace.

"And after the job?" she asked.

"After the job," Freya said, turning toward the water, toward the drowned cathedral and the tilted hulk of the Meridian Star and whatever Aldous's next charge would cost them, "we're going to need a navigator who knows exactly where that man keeps his brother."

He clipped on Osk's rig. It smelled of canvas and tallow and six years of a man who'd finally gotten one number right, and Freya Voss went down the ladder into the dark with his crew at his back and the fleet's next explosion already counting itself down somewhere beyond the wall.

The water closed over his head. Somewhere below, in the tea-colored dark, the Meridian Star waited with its dead and its secrets, and above it, faint through forty feet of mountain and water, he could hear — or imagined he could hear — the fleet's drills beginning to bite into Osk Brann's door.

They had hours. Maybe less.

Freya swam.
