## 17. Corvane's Offer

They took my boots first.

I want to say I understood the symbolism immediately — the stripping of a man's footing, the small humiliations that precede the large ones — but honestly my first thought was that my feet were cold, and my second was that whoever had designed the dredger *Corvane's Pride* had never once considered that a prisoner might be dragged through her corridors barefoot, because the deck plating was riveted steel and the river outside was warmer than the air within.

They took the ledger next. That one hurt more.

A young officer with a face like a closed ledger himself — no offense to ledgers — held it at arm's length as though it might bite him, which, given what I'd written in its margins over three years, wasn't entirely unfair. He turned it over twice, found nothing but distances and dead trestles and a man's whole life measured in miles, and handed it to a subordinate with the expression of someone who has been promised treasure and delivered arithmetic.

"He writes everything down," the officer said, mostly to himself. "Like an accountant."

"Engineers do," I said. "We like to know where we've been. It helps us figure out where we're going."

He looked at me the way you look at furniture that has spoken. Then he left, taking my boots, my ledger, my knife, and — this is the detail that stayed with me, the one I turned over for hours afterward in a cell that smelled of bilge and lamp oil — my coat pocket lint and all, as if thoroughness itself were a virtue, or perhaps as if somewhere in that coat was the thing Aldous actually wanted, and he couldn't afford to guess wrong about which pocket held it.

The cell was not a cell. That was the first thing Corvane Aldous did right, and I resented him for how well he did it. It was a stateroom. A real one, with a bunk wide enough for two and a washbasin and a porthole showing nothing but green water and the distant, wavering suggestion of Mount Serel's drowned shoulder. They locked the door, but they locked it gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child. There was dry clothing folded on the bunk — my size, which told me either they had excellent quartermasters or someone had been reading my file for longer than I'd known I had one.

I sat on the bunk and did what I always did when the world went sideways: I counted.

Trestles between Greyharbor and the Serel junction: forty-one. Hours since Osk Brann held that tunnel door with his shoulders and his stubbornness and finally, finally nothing at all: nine, give or take the ones I'd lost to unconsciousness. Crew members still breathing: three, if you counted me, and Ilsa Reyne, who I was no longer sure should be counted in anyone's column. Crew members who weren't: one, and his name was Osk, and he had laughed exactly once in my presence, and I kept replaying it because I could not remember what joke had earned it, and that felt like losing him twice.

The evidence case was gone from my chest. Of course it was. They'd taken it while I floated half-drowned in their netting, chained or unchained, it hardly mattered; a man can't guard a secret when his lungs are full of river.

So I sat in a comfortable cell aboard the ship of the man who had likely killed my brother, wearing borrowed dry socks, and I waited for the devil to make his appointment.

He came at dusk. Or what passes for dusk inside a ship — the lamps dimmed on some schedule, a courtesy of atmosphere. There was a knock. Two knocks, polite, spaced. The door opened without waiting for my answer, because men like Aldous knock the way storms announce themselves: as formality, not request.

Corvane Aldous was smaller than I expected.

I don't know why that surprised me. Perhaps because Sela had described him the way sailors describe weather — vast, inevitable, arriving on the horizon of every conversation whether invited or not. Perhaps because the fleet he commanded was so enormous that I'd assumed the man at its center must be too. But the person who stepped into my stateroom was of middling height, silver at the temples, with the neat, economical build of a fencer or a clerk, and eyes the color of the river in November: gray, patient, and very hard to see the bottom of.

"Freya Voss," he said, and pronounced it correctly, which almost nobody did. "Railroad engineer. Formerly of the Karelvo Northern Line. Brother of Tomas Voss." He let each fact sit in the air like a card laid face-up. "You've cost me two divers, a patrol skiff, and roughly four hundred crowns in bribed officials who are now asking uncomfortable questions. Do you know what I do to men who cost me money?"

"I imagine there's a room for it."

"There is," he agreed pleasantly. "It's called the payroll office. Sit down, Mr. Voss. Please. You'll want to be seated for this."

I sat. Not because he asked — I told myself it was because standing barefoot on cold steel was undignified, and dignity was the last property I owned outright.

He poured two glasses of something amber from a decanter I suspected cost more than a locomotive axle. He set one in front of me. He did not insist I drink it. That was the second thing he did right, and I hated him a little more for it.

"Do you know what the Meridian Star was carrying?" he asked.

"Evidence," I said. "Diplomatic cases. Sealed. Enough to hang you, if half of what Ilsa brought us is true."

"Mm. Ilsa." Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Accounting. A line item being marked. "Yes. She would have told you that. She was always better at loyalty in retrospect than in practice." He sat across from me, unhurried, crossing one ankle over his knee. "But you're wrong about the cargo, Mr. Voss. Or rather, you're right, and you have no idea what you're right about. May I tell you a story?"

"You'll tell it either way."

"I will," he said, with genuine warmth, as if I'd paid him a compliment. "It's 1911. Karelvo is dying — you remember. Everyone remembers, the ones who were there. The capital's falling, the army's dissolving into the hills, and the government — what's left of it, eleven men in a cellar with a telegraph key — is trying to move everything that matters out of the country before the guns arrive. Gold, yes. Archives. And certain documents." He swirled his glass. "Evacuation routes, Mr. Voss. The routes by which forty thousand civilians were meant to leave Karelvo alive. Trains, ferries, mountain passes. Timetables. The whole great choreography of escape."

The glass stopped moving.

"And those documents," Aldous continued, in the same reasonable voice, "were worth more than gold to the enemy. Because a timetable is a promise, and a promise, kept, tells your artillery exactly where forty thousand people will be, and when, and in what order. Sell the routes, and you don't need to hunt them. They deliver themselves to your guns."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, the dredger's pumps throbbed like a heart that didn't care what happened above decks.

"You sold them," I said.

"I was the Minister of Transport's undersecretary," Aldous said. "I sold them. Yes. Say it plainly; euphemism is for priests." He leaned forward. "And here is what your brother discovered, and here is what your brother did, and I want you to listen carefully, because everyone else who knows this story is dead, and I am tired of being the only historian of my own crime."

* * *

He told it well. That's the thing I keep coming back to, the thing that has kept me up nights since. He told it *well*.

"Tomas Voss was a fireman on the Meridian Star's final run. Not a passenger — crew. He'd been running documents for the rump government for months; small things, codes, courier work. On the last run, he was given the diplomatic cases and told to get them to the coast. He opened them somewhere past the Serel junction — against orders, which speaks well of him — and he read what I have just told you, and he understood that the man who signed the sale order was the same man whose signature appeared on his own evacuation orders." Aldous smiled, thin and terrible. "Your brother was not a fool, Mr. Voss. He knew he couldn't outrun me with paper. So he made the wreck deliberate. Scuttled the Star in the flooded tunnels beneath Serel, where no salvage operation could reach her without a railroad engineer who knew the closed lines — and then he made sure the only such engineer in the world was a man with no reason to ever come looking."

The glass in my hand had not moved. I noticed distantly that my knuckles were white around it, and that I did not remember picking it up.

"He died in the water," Aldous said, softer now. "Holding the cases shut. The mail car flooded fast — she went down nose-first, and he was in it. I've had divers down there, Mr. Voss. Over the years. Quiet ones. They found him in the third car, wedged against the case rack, arms still around it." He paused, and — God help me, and this is the seduction, this is the whole architecture of the man — his voice cracked, just slightly, just enough. "He drowned protecting the truth. The same truth now chained to your chest, or it was, nine hours ago. You and he, Mr. Voss, are the only two men who ever carried that cargo. One of you died for it. I'm offering the other a chance to live off it."

"Off it," I repeated.

"Partnership." He said the word the way other men say *home*. "The evidence is worthless to you. Think it through — you're an engineer, thinking is supposed to be your trade. Publish it, and what happens? A journalist prints it. Governments shrug. Karelvo is a dead nation; its dead don't vote. My interests are diversified across four borders and none of them will extradite a man for crimes against a country that no longer exists. You burn yourself, you burn my name in a newspaper nobody trusts, and Osk Brann is still dead, and the river keeps everything else." He spread his hands. "Or. You sign on. Salvage rights, split sixty-forty in your favor — I'm not a monster, and frankly you've earned consideration; you found my train before my own fleet did. Your people walk away rich and untouched. Pip Wren gets a real apprenticeship, papers, a future. Sela's debts — and she has *such* interesting debts, Mr. Voss, I've read the ledgers — evaporate. And you get answers. Every answer. I knew your brother. I can tell you what he wrote home. I can tell you the last thing he said to the purser at the Serel platform, because the purser survived, and he talks when he's paid."

He let that hang. The lamp hissed. The pumps throbbed.

"And the evidence?" I asked. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "In your version of the partnership. What happens to it?"

"It goes into my archive," Aldous said simply. "Where it stays. Where it has always been going to stay. Mr. Voss — the difference between us is not that I want the truth buried and you want it free. The difference is that I can afford the burial, and you cannot afford the digging. That's all power is. Arithmetic."

I looked at the whiskey. I thought about drinking it, just to have something to do with my mouth.

Here is what I have not told anyone, and may as well confess now, to this page, if to no one else: for about ten seconds — I counted them, because counting is what I do — I wanted to say yes.

Not for the money. Money is just distance in another unit, and I'd had enough distance. I wanted to say yes because he had given me back my brother. Three years of a hole in the shape of a man, and this terrible, elegant monster had walked into my cell and filled it — with details, with a purser, with the image of Tomas's arms around a case rack in the dark, doing the one thing in the world that was most himself, which was holding onto something important while everything else went under. Grief is a hunger, and Aldous had set a table. All it cost was silence. Silence, and letting Osk's death mean nothing, and letting forty thousand ghosts stay unavenged, and becoming, in the gentlest possible way, the sort of man who pours whiskey for the people he ruins.

Ten seconds. Then I thought of Osk laughing that one time, and I thought: he held a door. The least I can do is hold a line.

"Aldous," I said. "Do you know what I did for three years?"

"Walked rails. Measured things."

"Went nowhere," I said. "Deliberately. Because going somewhere meant arriving, and arriving meant the war was over and Tomas wasn't coming back from it. I made my whole life a corridor." I set the glass down, untouched, and stood up. My bare feet on the cold steel felt, for the first time, like mine. "You've just offered me a corridor with gold wallpaper. And I appreciate the craftsmanship, truly. But I'm done with corridors."

Something flickered across his face — disappointment? Respect? The accounting look again, a column closing?

"That's a shame," he said, and stood as well, and for a moment the pleasantness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was exactly what Sela had promised: appetite, patient and total. "Because I should mention — and I mention it only as a matter of completeness — that your friends are currently in a skiff on my river, attempting an approach to my stern ballast compartments, and my watch officer spotted them twenty minutes ago."

The floor seemed to tilt, though the dredger was steady as a courthouse.

"So you see," Aldous went on, retrieving his decanter with the unhurried grace of a man closing a shop for the night, "the offer doesn't expire at dawn after all. It expires in about fifteen minutes. Partnership, Mr. Voss. Or I let the river do what rivers do, and you can spend the rest of your life measuring the distance between this stateroom and the place where your family drowned — twice."

He knocked twice on the door — polite, spaced — and the guards opened it, and he paused on the threshold with my ledger under his arm, which I hadn't seen him take.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your brother's last entry was a mileage count too. Trestles to the coast. He was wrong by four hundred meters. Engineers usually are." He smiled. "Fifteen minutes, Voss. I'll be on the bridge, listening to the water."

The door closed. The lock did not click — he'd left it unlocked, a courtesy, an invitation, a trap with soft walls.

I stood in the lamplight in my borrowed socks, and I did the only thing left that an engineer can do when the schedule collapses and the flood is coming.

I started looking for the ballast valves.
