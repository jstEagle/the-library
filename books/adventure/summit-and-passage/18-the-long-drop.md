## 18. The Long Drop

I've stolen a lot of things in my life. Boats, cargo, a man's wife once — though to be fair, she did most of the stealing herself, I just held the ladder. But I'd never stolen a prisoner off the flagship of the most powerful man on the river, and I want it noted for the record that when Pip Wren suggested it, I laughed.

Not a polite laugh. A laugh with phlegm in it. The kind of laugh that says *you're a child and I'm a professional and this is how we both learn something tonight.*

She just looked at me across the skiff's thwart, rain running off her hood, and said, "You've got a better idea?"

And that was the trouble. I didn't. Freya Voss was somewhere inside the *Corvane's Pride* — Aldous's dredger, all eleven hundred tons of her — chained to a case of diplomatic evidence worth more than every hull in Greyharbor combined, and by morning Aldous would either buy him or bury him. Osk was already dead holding a tunnel door shut so the rest of us could run, and I will tell you plainly: I do not intend to lose two men to that mountain in one week. One is a tragedy. Two is a habit. Three is a pattern, and patterns are how you end up like me — alone on a boat at forty-one, talking to a teenager about ballast compartments like that's a normal Tuesday.

"Walk me through it again," I said, because if I made her say it twice, maybe I'd hear the flaw before the river did.

Pip pulled out the plans. She'd copied them from Ilsa's fleet documents, and Ilsa had drawn them from memory, and memory is a liar with good handwriting, but it was what we had. The *Pride* was a converted ore dredger: three decks above waterline, four holds below, and along her flanks a row of ballast tanks — great iron lungs that filled and emptied to keep her level while the dredging arms chewed the riverbed. Aldous had moored her bow-on against the old quarry wall below Mount Serel, floodlights blazing down into the water, because he thought he owned the night too.

"The aft ballast compartment," Pip said, tapping the plan. "Number four. It vents through these scuppers, here and here. If we open them below the waterline—"

"She takes on water," I said. "Fast."

"Faster than they can pump. The pumps are forward. Osk told me once — he served on a sister ship — the aft pumps were stripped for parts after the war. Nobody fixed them because nobody ever planned to take damage back there."

Osk Brann, dead one day, still solving our problems. That was him all over. The man couldn't leave a broken thing alone, including us.

"So she lists," I said. "Aldous abandons the stern sections. And Freya's being held—"

"Aft. Lower deck. Ilsa confirmed it. They put him next to the evidence lockers because Aldous wants him looking at the case while he talks. Wants him hungry for it." Pip's jaw did something hard. She'd learned that from Sela, whoever that was. "When they evacuate the stern, someone has to walk him out. Someone who knows the ship."

"And that someone is a seventeen-year-old apprentice engineer."

"That someone," Pip said, "is the only person on this boat who can read a ballast diagram without moving their lips."

I looked at the rain. I looked at the mountain, black against the blacker sky, lit from below by the drowned glow of Aldous's floodlights — the whole cavern under Serel burning blue-green through the water like a lamp under a bedsheet. Somewhere down there was a train full of ghosts and a man named Corvane Aldous who had sold an entire nation's escape routes to enemy gunners and called it logistics.

"All right," I said. "But we're doing it my way, which means ugly, fast, and with a lot of swearing."

Pip grinned. It was Osk's grin, borrowed. "Wouldn't respect you otherwise."

* * *

We came in on the ebb, engine muffled under oilcloth, riding the current the way I'd ridden it since I was smaller than Pip — low, quiet, and lying about who I was. The *Pride*'s stern hung over open water on a lattice of mooring chains, and her flank rose up beside us like the wall of a drowned cathedral, riveted iron streaked with rust-weep, portholes glowing yellow. Music came from somewhere above. Aldous was entertaining his officers. Of course he was. Men like that celebrate before the victory; it's the only part they actually enjoy.

The scupper plugs were the easy part. Pip went over the side with a spanner and a knife between her teeth like some storybook pirate, and I held the skiff against the swell and listened to her work — three taps, pause, three taps, the signal we'd agreed on. Each plug came free with a sound I felt more than heard, a soft iron kiss, and then the river began its argument with the ship.

It starts slow, water coming in where it shouldn't. That's the trick of it. A ship doesn't die screaming; it dies politely, a gallon at a time, until some mate notices his boots are wet and by then the arithmetic has already turned against you. We gave it twenty minutes. Long enough for the aft compartment to take on thirty tons of river. Long enough for the deck under our fingers to develop a lean you could feel in your spine.

Then the shouting started, and the music stopped, and the *Corvane's Pride* began to die in earnest.

Boots hammered overhead. Voices — orders, counter-orders, the particular panic of men who have never been wet in their lives discovering the river does not negotiate. Aldous's voice cut through it all, calm as a ledger, telling them to hold stations, that it was nothing, that the pumps would manage. He believed it. That was the beautiful thing about Corvane Aldous: he believed his own money could bail any ocean.

Pip came up over the gunwale dripping, eyes bright as new coins. "Now?"

"Now," I said, and threw her the grapnel.

We went up the mooring chain hand over hand, into the belly of the beast, and I'll be honest with you — there's a version of me that loves this. Always has been. The part that used to run contraband past customs cutters at sixteen, laughing into the wind. The sensible part of me hates her, but she keeps the other parts alive, so we tolerate each other.

Lower deck. The corridor stank of bilge and lamp oil, and it sloped — not much, five degrees maybe, but your knees know. Doors stood open where crew had abandoned their posts. At the far end, two guards stood outside a bulkhead door, and here is where I confess my plan for them was: improvise.

Improvise turned out to mean the deck did it for me. The ship rolled as another ton of river found its home, and one guard grabbed the rail, and I put my shoulder into the other one's chest and we both went down together onto iron plating, and I got an elbow across his throat and kept it there while Pip — bless her ruthless little heart — swung a spanner into the first man's knee and then, when he folded, helpfully stood on his wrist.

"Sorry," she said, not sorry.

"Key," I gasped at the one under me. He shook his head. I pressed harder. He changed his philosophy.

The bulkhead opened on lamplight and the smell of ink and cigar smoke, and there was Freya Voss sitting at a table with a brandy he hadn't touched and a document laid out in front of him like a confession, and the evidence case chained to his own chest — Aldous's little joke, or his little leash, making the prisoner carry the prize.

Freya looked up at us with the expression of a man who has just been informed the sun rises in the west.

"You're joking," he said.

"Everyone keeps saying that," Pip said. "Nobody's laughing. Come on."

* * *

Getting out of a sinking ship is a skill, and most people learn it exactly once. The corridor behind us was already ankle-deep and rising, and the list had grown to the point where walking felt like wading uphill. Freya moved well for a man who'd spent a day in a chair — engineers know ships the way I know rivers, instinctively, weight and balance written into the hands — but the chain and case slowed him, caught on hatches, dragged at his shoulders.

"We can break it," Pip panted, hammering at the padlock with the spanner.

"Don't," Freya said. "If we drop it in the water, it's gone. And if Aldous gets it back—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We'd all read Tomas's journal by now, or enough of it. We knew what the paper in that case was worth and what it had cost.

So we carried it. All three of us, taking turns, which is the whole secret of family that blood never teaches you: nobody carries it alone, and everybody gets a turn.

The stern sections were emptying ahead of the water. Crew streamed past us up ladders, and I did the thing I do best — I became somebody else. Grey coat, officer's bark, "Aft section's lost, get topside, move!" and men moved, because authority is mostly costume and volume. Twice an officer looked at Freya's chains and started to ask questions, and twice the ship answered for me, rolling hard enough to put everyone's hands on the rails and everyone's mind on their own skin.

At the aft cargo hatch, though, the river finally sent its bill collector.

The hatch was dogged from outside — evacuation protocol, seal the stern — and the water behind us was knee-deep and cold as a debt, and Pip got the dogs loose except the last one, seized with rust, and the list was twelve degrees now and getting personal.

"Pip. Out," Freya said.

"Not yet."

"Pip Wren. Out. *Now.*"

"I've almost got it—"

The last dog broke free all at once, and the hatch swung, and the river came through it like everything it had ever wanted to say to us at once. It took Pip off her feet. It took Freya's legs out from under him. It took me against the bulkhead hard enough to rearrange my opinion of ribs.

And Freya Voss — a man I'd known for two weeks, a man who measured his life in miles because years hurt too much — went into that black water after her without a second's thought. No calculation. No ledger entry. Just went.

I found them ten feet down the flooded corridor, or rather I found Freya's boot and hauled, and we came up together in the rising dark, Pip coughing between us, the evidence case still chained to Freya's chest, still dry under his coat, because some things a man holds onto even when the river asks nicely.

"Up," I said. "Ladder. Go."

They went. I went last, because that's the job, and because the sensible part of me wanted one look back down that drowning corridor — at the water climbing the walls, at the lamplight dying green — and told itself it was checking for pursuit, which was a lie, but a useful one. You survive on useful lies. You live on the truth. I'm still working out the ratio.

* * *

Topside was chaos wearing a uniform. Lifeboats swinging out, officers shouting, and above it all, at the bridge rail, one figure standing perfectly still while his ship died under him — Corvane Aldous, watching the water come for his gold, and even from the deck I could see the moment the arithmetic turned against him too. The moment he understood the pumps wouldn't manage. The moment the appetite met the ocean.

He saw us. I'll give him that — the man misses nothing. Across fifty feet of chaos our eyes met, and I watched him understand exactly what had happened, exactly who had done it, and exactly what was walking off his ship chained to a dead nation's testimony. His face didn't change. That was the frightening part. Men scream when they lose money. Aldous just filed it.

Then the *Pride* rolled again — hard, final, the death-roll — and the deck went vertical under us, and there wasn't time for anything but the rail and the black water and the long drop.

I hit the river like a slap from God. Came up coughing, found the skiff, found Pip hauling on the painter, found Freya treading water beside me with one arm and holding the case above the surface with the other, because of course he was, because that man would drown before he'd let paper sink.

"In," I rasped. "Both of you. Move."

We pulled away as the *Corvane's Pride* settled by the stern, her floodlights still burning underwater, lighting up her own drowning — a great iron chandelier going down into the dark, illuminating nothing but silt and the shapes of men swimming for shore. Behind us, on the quarry wall, Aldous's remaining boats were launching, lanterns swinging, voices carrying flat across the water.

And beneath all of it, under the mountain, under the floodlights, the Meridian Star waited with her ghosts, and Mount Serel — I felt it before I heard it, a shudder through the hull of the skiff, deep and patient — Mount Serel began, very quietly, to wake.

Pip looked at me. Freya looked at me. Water ran out of all three of us and pooled in the bilge like a small honest sea.

"Sela," Freya said, teeth chattering, hands locked around the case. "The tunnels. If Aldous's blasting cracked the cavern supports—"

"Then the mountain doesn't care whose evidence it buries," I said, and put the tiller over, and drove us into the dark toward the sound of a world coming down.
