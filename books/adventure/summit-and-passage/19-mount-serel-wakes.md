## 19. Mount Serel Wakes

The mountain spoke first in a language I knew too well — the low groan of stone under strain, the sound every engineer learns to hear the way a sailor hears weather. I'd spent three years listening to dead lines creak and settle, cataloguing their complaints like an old man's joints. But this was different. This was not complaint. This was warning.

We were forty yards from the service tunnel mouth when the first charge went off behind us.

Not ours. Ours had gone off twenty minutes ago, down in the ballast decks of the *Vigilant*, where Sela's improvised arithmetic had turned Aldous's own pumping system into a drowning machine. I'd felt that one through my boots, a deep satisfied thump, the mountain swallowing a piece of the magnate's empire. This second blast was sharper, closer, wrong-angled. Someone on Aldous's crew was setting demolition charges against the cavern wall — trying to open a shortcut to the gold before the water took it.

"Tell me they're not doing what I think they're doing," Sela said. She had Pip by the collar, hauling her along the flooded gallery at a pace that left no breath for conversation. The evidence case rode chained to my chest, and every step knocked it against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

"They're doing exactly what you think they're doing," I said.

"Then we run faster."

Ilsa Reyne was ahead of us with the lamp, her fleet-issue boots slapping the wet catwalk. She'd been aboard the dredger long enough to know its habits, and she'd said something back in the dark of the escape that I hadn't let myself think about yet: *Aldous keeps demolition stores on board. He always plans to take more than he came for.* Now those stores were being used to carve the mountain open from the inside, and the mountain — old, patient, indifferent Mount Serel, which had swallowed a railway whole and never once apologized — was beginning to answer.

* * *

You want to know what a collapsing cavern sounds like? Not thunder. Thunder is honest; it comes and goes. A cavern coming down is quieter than that, at least at first. It's a hiss of displaced air through cracks. It's grit raining onto your helmet, fine as flour, then coarse as gravel. It's the water changing its mind — currents that have run one direction for years suddenly reversing, confused, as new passages open and old ones seal. And underneath all of it, so low you feel it in your teeth rather than hear it, a grinding like millstones the size of cathedrals.

Osk Brann would have known the proper term for it. Osk, who had held the tunnel door with his body so the rest of us could live, who was somewhere behind us now in the black water or pressed into the stone of it, and whom I could not stop carrying even while running for my life. Grief is a heavy passenger. It doesn't care about your schedule.

"Freya." Pip's voice, thin through the rebreather's mask. "The junction. The junction's gone."

She was right. We rounded the last bend toward the maintenance gallery and found the way we'd come in sealed — not collapsed entirely, but slumped, a sagging curtain of rock and twisted rail where there had been a tunnel. The old line I'd mapped three summers ago, measured trestle by trestle in a ledger I no longer owned, was folding in on itself like a fist closing.

Sela swore with the fluency of a woman who'd grown up in harbor bars. Ilsa just looked at me. Waiting. They were all looking at me, and I understood suddenly that this was the job now — not maps, not miles, not ledgers. Me. Whatever I was worth after everything the river had taken, it had to be worth four lives.

"The north bore," I said. "There's a second service tunnel. Parallel. It runs under the old signal house."

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure it existed on paper in 1911. Whether it exists tonight is another question."

"It's better than the question we've got," Sela said, and pushed past me.

* * *

The north bore was real. Half-drowned, ribbed with rotten timber, choked with silt to the knee in places — but real, and heading up-slope, which was the only direction that mattered anymore. Behind us the cavern boomed again, another charge, and this time the concussion came through the water itself, a punch against the chest that staggered Pip into the wall.

And then the water started moving.

Not rising — moving. A current where no current should be, dragging at our legs, pulling back down toward the main cavern as the mountain's plumbing rearranged itself. Somewhere below, the flooded galleries were draining into some deeper void the charges had opened, and everything loose in the water was going with them: silt, debris, a section of ladder torn from its bolts, and — I made myself look, made myself see — lights. Fleet dive-lights, tumbling end over end into the dark, with men attached to them.

I didn't count how many. I want to say that's because there wasn't time. That would be a lie. There was time. I chose not to count.

"Keep moving," I heard myself say, in the flat voice I use when the ledger has no column for what's happening. "Don't look back. Looking back costs you ten seconds, and ten seconds is thirty feet of tunnel."

"That's grim arithmetic, Voss," Sela panted.

"I'm an engineer. Grim is what I do."

* * *

We caught up with Aldous's people at the fork where the north bore met the old ventilation adit — caught up because they weren't moving. Six of them, maybe seven, clustered around a man in a brass-buttoned diving suit too fine for the work, arguing in the panicked shorthand of people whose authority structure has just been eaten by a mountain. Their lamps swung wild. One of them saw us and shouted, and for a moment I thought we'd have a fight on our hands, here, in the throat of a dying tunnel.

Then the far side of the adit came down.

No warning hiss this time, no grit-rain preamble. Just a crack like the world's largest knuckle joint, and forty tons of Serel letting go all at once, sealing the adit and taking two of the fleet divers with it. The shockwave of air knocked us all flat. When I got my feet under me, the argument was over. The fleet men stood staring at the fresh rock face where their colleagues had been, and one of them turned to me — turned to *me*, an enemy, a saboteur, a ghost out of the closed lines — and said, with terrible simplicity:

"Which way out?"

"There's only one way," I said. "It's mine. You can follow it or you can stay."

They followed it.

I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger survives me: Corvane Aldous's men, bought and brutal and complicit, chose the stray crew's path without a shot fired. Fear of the mountain outranks fear of the man. It always has. It's the one lesson empires keep failing to learn.

* * *

We came up out of the bore into the upper gallery — the level above the drowned station, dry-ish, still intact, still holding. And there, lit by our lamps and the flicker of his own, stood Corvane Aldous.

He was not fleeing. That was the first thing I understood, standing there dripping in the bad light. Around him his remaining crew loaded sealed diplomatic cases onto a handcart — the evidence, the treason, Tomas's life's work and death's meaning — stacked alongside strongboxes that could only be the gold he'd promised his investors. He stood over it all like a landlord at an eviction, coat immaculate, face gray with rock dust, and when he saw me his expression did something I'll remember until I die: it brightened.

"Voss." Almost warm. "You're alive. Good. I'd begun to think the river liked you better than it likes me."

"The river hasn't decided yet," I said. "Leave the cases. Take your people and go."

"And walk away from—" He gestured at the cart, and for a moment the charm slipped and I saw the machinery underneath, the appetite with nothing behind its eyes but more appetite. "Do you know what this is worth? Do you know what I gave up for this? Karelvo burned so that men like me could build something out of its ashes, and I will not—"

The mountain interrupted him. A crack ran the length of the ceiling overhead, fast as a whip, and dust poured down like smoke. His own divers broke for the tunnel mouth without waiting for orders. Mine held — Sela with her arm around Pip, Ilsa watching Aldous with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Aldous," I said. "Listen to it. It doesn't care about your fortune. It doesn't care about your name. It was here before Karelvo drew its first border and it'll be here after yours is forgotten. You can't buy it. You can't decree it. All you can do is leave while it's still offering."

For a moment — one long, swaying moment, with the millstone-grind rising beneath our feet — I thought he'd listen. I saw him weigh it, the way he must once have weighed evacuation routes and gun batteries, human cargo against strategic gain. I watched him do the arithmetic that had killed thousands, the same cold ledger, the same grim columns.

Then he put his hand on the gold.

"No," he said. "Bring the cart."

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I set it down plainly because there will be those who call it murder and those who call it justice and both will be wrong and both will be right.

His men tried to move the cart. The cart's wheels, swollen with floodwater, jammed in the rail flanges of the old gallery floor. The mountain groaned again, louder, closer, and the fleet crew — the ones who'd asked me which way out — looked at the cart, looked at the tunnel, and made the choice their master wouldn't. Three of them ran. Two stayed, because staying near Corvane Aldous had always paid better than sense, and the mountain took the gallery's eastern wall in a single exhalation and buried cart, gold, cases, and the two loyalists together under a hundred tons of Serel.

Aldous lived because greed had kept him standing on the wrong side of the cart. He crawled out of the dust cloud bleeding from the scalp, clawed at the rubble with his bare hands until Sela physically hauled him back, screaming a word — *mine, mine* — that I last heard from a drowning man in the war, clutching a crate that turned out to be full of someone else's letters home.

"The tunnel," I said. "Now. All of us, now, or the next one takes the floor."

* * *

We ran. All of us together, magnate and strays, enemies by circumstance and survivors by necessity, up the tilting bore with the water rising at our heels and the stone falling ahead of us. I carried Pip over a gap where the floor had split. Sela half-dragged Aldous, who had finally stopped fighting her and started simply obeying, the way powerful men do when power runs out. Ilsa counted heads aloud like a prayer — seven, seven, seven — keeping us honest.

Behind us, in the dark we didn't look back at, Mount Serel folded the Meridian Star's cavern shut forever. I felt it rather than heard it: a vast settling, a door closing on three years of my life and on a brother's grave I'd never get to stand beside. The train, the journal, the mail car where I'd read Tomas's handwriting by lamplight — all of it going under stone, unrecoverable, unvisitable. A cathedral with no entrance.

Good, some part of me said. Let it keep him. Let it keep the gold. Let it keep everything except the case chained to my chest and the truth inside it, because truth is the only cargo that gets heavier the farther you carry it, and I intended to carry it all the way to Greyharbor if it broke my back.

But Aldous was still alive. Bleeding, stripped of his fleet, his fortune, and his future, but alive, and stumbling up the tunnel beside me in the strobe of failing lamps — and I remembered the look on his face when he'd weighed the mountain against the gold, and I understood that the arithmetic wasn't finished. Men like him don't drown with their treasure. They climb out of the water and start counting again.

Ahead, faint and gray, the bore's mouth opened onto night air and rain. Behind us, the grinding rose to a roar.

And between us — between me and the man who had sold my brother's country to the guns — there remained exactly one tunnel, one exit, and one unfinished account.

I slowed my steps until we were walking side by side.
