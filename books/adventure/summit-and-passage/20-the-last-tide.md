## 20. The Last Tide

The water was rising faster than any of us had calculated, and I had done all the calculating, so that was nobody's fault but mine.

"Freya." Sela's voice came through the speaking tube cracked with static and panic in equal measure. "The lower gallery's gone. The whole western face of the cavern just — let go. It's not a leak anymore, it's a throat."

"I hear it."

"Then say something useful."

"Get them to the shaft," I said. "All of them. Pip first. Ilsa second. You last, because you'll argue, and I haven't got the breath to argue back."

A pause on the line. In three weeks of running rivers and breaking seals and burying a friend, Sela had never once taken an order without commentary. The silence told me she'd already worked out what I was planning, and that she was deciding, right now, whether to fight me on it.

"Don't," she said finally.

"Get them to the shaft."

"Freya—"

"That's not a request, Sela. It's an engineering instruction."

I cut the tube before she could find the words to beat me with, because Sela could beat anyone with words, and I only had so many minutes left to spend on losing arguments. The lamp at my feet threw a shaking circle of light across the service platform, the old maintenance dock where trains had once stopped to take on water and men had once stopped to take on nothing at all, because the mountain did not permit it. Behind me, the flooded gallery boomed like a held breath. Ahead, the last dry stretch of tunnel climbed toward the ventilation shaft, and somewhere along it, I knew, Corvane Aldous was climbing too.

I had seen him in the floodlights. That was the thing I kept returning to, the detail my mind kept handing back to me like a debt collector: not the dredger, not the divers, not the gold he'd refused to leave — him. Wading through thigh-deep water in a diving suit with the helmet off, gray at the temples, calm as a bank manager, directing men to haul treasure cases toward the shaft while the mountain came down around them. A man who had sold ten thousand lives down a river and still stood in the water like it owed him an apology.

Tomas had died keeping a ledger on that man. It seemed fitting that I should finish the accounting in person.

I took the journal from my coat — Tomas's journal, water-spotted, its spine broken from three years in a mail car — and I wedged it into the iron document case, and I chained the case to my chest the way I'd chained it since the dredger, and then I walked into the tunnel to meet the last tide.

* * *

He was there where I knew he'd be, because I'd read the blueprints and he'd read the men who'd drawn them. The junction chamber, where the ventilation shaft met the old service adit — the only dry route up and out, the one corridor the flood hadn't swallowed yet. He stood with his back to me, hands clasped behind him like a man admiring a view, and the water was already through his bootlaces.

"You're the brother," Aldous said, without turning. "The engineer. Voss."

"You've been reading my file."

"I've been reading everyone's file. It's a habit from the government. We were very thorough in Karelvo, right up until the end." He turned then, and up close he was smaller than the floodlights had made him, a compact man with a scholar's stoop and eyes that assessed rather than regarded. "Your brother was thorough too. Did you know that? I never met him. I only ever met his work."

The water reached my knees. I didn't look down. Looking down at rising water is how men drown in their own imaginations before the real water gets them.

"You have something I want," Aldous said. "And I have something you want, which is the continued respiration of four people currently standing in a shaft that I control the pumps for. You did notice my men at the shaft head. Of course you did. You're the brother."

"They're gone," I said. "Sela took them out through the adit twenty minutes ago. Your divers found an empty ladder."

Something moved behind his eyes — the first honest thing on his face. He'd lost men to the collapse, I realized. He'd lost the dredger's stern section, the gold, the schedule, the certainty, and he was standing in a flooding tunnel negotiating anyway, because a man like Corvane Aldous had never learned that some ledgers don't balance.

"Then you've nothing to trade," he said.

"I have this." I put my hand flat on the document case chained to my chest. "Diplomatic seals intact. Karelvo's evacuation schedules. The gunnery correspondence. Your signature on three of the pages — I've read them by lamplight, Corvane, and your hand hasn't changed. A journalist in Greyharbor is already paid to receive it. The only question left in this tunnel is whether it reaches her, or whether it stays down here with both of us."

"You'd die for paper."

"I'd drown for my brother's handwriting," I said. "Paper is just what it's printed on."

He smiled at that, and it was the worst thing I saw all day, because it was a real smile, warm at the edges, the smile of a man who had talked his way past a hundred better men than me in a hundred better rooms. "Let me make you an offer, engineer. The one I made on the dredger stands. Partnership. The salvage rights alone — the lines you've spent three years mapping, reopened under my charter, with your name as chief engineer. You'd have a fleet. You'd have a purpose. Grief is a poor career, Voss. I can offer you a better one."

"You can offer me nothing," I said. "You sold the evacuation routes. You sold the ferries' timing to the enemy gunners, and ten thousand people walked out of Karelvo on a schedule you'd already sold, and my brother found the proof and put it in a mail car because there was no one left to give it to. And then you spent three years buying the river to keep it buried." I felt my voice go somewhere I didn't recognize, low and even, my father's voice, the voice he'd used reading casualty lists. "There is no room in that ledger for partnership."

"Ledgers," Aldous said softly, "can be rewritten."

"So can tunnels."

I want to be honest, since honesty is the only inheritance I have left to spend: I did not plan it as cleverly as it will sound in the retelling. I had planned a dozen versions on the climb up, and every one of them required Aldous to be stupid, and Aldous was not stupid. What I actually had was simpler and older than any plan. I had the blueprints in my head, and he had them secondhand, and there was one thing about this junction chamber that no secondhand man ever knows.

The service adit — the dry corridor at his back, the one he'd have to retreat through — was supported by a penstock gate. A hundred-year-old cast-iron flood gate, built to seal the chamber from the gallery in an emergency, and I had spent the last twenty minutes with a wrench and a cold sweat doing the only maintenance of my career I was ashamed of: I had unseated it. Not opened it. Just unseated — broken the seal, backed off the dogs, left it holding by habit and paint. It would hold against a knock. It would hold against a shove.

It would not hold against the pressure building behind it, which had been building since the western face let go, which was building still, which was going to come through that gate like the fist of every person who had ever walked out of Karelvo on a sold schedule.

"You're stalling," Aldous said. "You're a stalling man. I can hear it in your—"

"The gate behind you," I said, "is open."

He didn't turn. That was his mistake, or maybe his honesty — a man who had never once in his life turned his back on an advantage refused, at the end, to turn his back on a threat. He looked at me instead, and for one moment the bank manager fell away and I saw the clerk he must have been, the small ambitious man in the final government of a dying country, doing arithmetic with other people's lives and finding that the numbers always came out in his favor.

"You wouldn't," he said. "You'd die too. You've got the case chained to your chest — the flood takes the evidence with you. You'd be destroying the only thing that matters."

"No," I said. "I'd be destroying the only thing that matters *to you*."

And I pulled the release lever.

* * *

There is a sound a mountain makes when it takes back what it lent. I had heard it once before, at the trestle collapse in the war, and I had spent three years walking closed lines trying not to hear it again. It is not loud, exactly. It is *total*. It is the sound of every smaller sound being informed that it is no longer required.

The gate went. The gallery came through it — not water, at first, but pressure, a black wall of it, and the chamber's lamplight bent and died, and the last thing I saw clearly was Corvane Aldous finally turning, finally, too late, toward the corridor he would never reach, and I swear to you he was still holding a ledger of some kind, some case, some weight of gold he would not put down, and the water took him and it together the way the river takes a matchstick and the matchbook both.

I went down too. I want that on the record. I did not stand heroically on high ground; I had miscalculated the backwash, or the mountain had overruled my figures, which it had been doing all week. The flood took me off my feet and rolled me down the adit like a coin down a drain, and the case on its chain hammered my ribs with every revolution, and I remember thinking, with the strange bookkeeping calm that comes at the end of a long audit: *the chain holds, the seals hold, Tomas, your work is waterproof, you always were the careful one.*

Then the water found the ventilation shaft, and the shaft did what shafts do — it breathed. The pressure column hit the updraft and the whole bore exhaled, and I was lifted, tumbled, spat upward through a hundred feet of black iron pipe like a cork from a bottle, and I came out of the shaft head into the rain and the gray dawn light and the smell of pine, on my hands and knees in the mud of Mount Serel's shoulder, coughing up half the river, the case still chained to my chest like a heart that had finally started beating for someone else's sake.

They were all there. Sela had them wrapped in tarps at the tree line, and I will carry the look on her face to whatever grave I get, because it was not relief. It was fury wearing relief as a coat. Pip was crying and pretending not to. Ilsa was standing very straight, the way people stand when they've counted heads and gotten the right number and don't trust it.

"You absolute—" Sela began.

"I know."

"—mule-headed, ledger-brained—"

"I know."

"You said it was an *engineering instruction*."

"It was," I said, and held up the case, and the chain, and the water running out of my cuffs, and the dawn coming up behind me over a mountain that was, at that moment, settling itself closed like a book. "And it worked."

Below us, the ground shivered. Dust breathed out of the shaft mouth in a long gray sigh, and then the shaft breathed in, and did not breathe out again. Somewhere down in the dark, the Meridian Star took on its final cargo: a magnate, a fortune, and a hundred tons of mountain, sealed together for as long as the mountain cared to keep them.

I sat down in the mud. I put my hand on the case, and under the case, on my own chest, over my own heart, and I felt them both going.

"Tomas," I said. Out loud. To the rain. "It's done. The books are closed."

The mountain said nothing. Mountains are like that. But the rain came down softer, the way it does when a storm has spent itself on something that deserved it, and Sela sat down next to me without a word, and Pip leaned into my shoulder, and Ilsa stood her watch, and the four of us stayed there in the mud while the last tide went out of Mount Serel for good.

We had lost Osk. We had lost the gold, the dredger, the wreck, and the man I had been three years and three weeks ago. What we had left was a case full of paper, a long walk down to Greyharbor, and a journalist whose name Sela had scratched on the inside of her cuff in case none of us lived to say it out loud.

"Can you walk?" Sela asked.

I took inventory. Ribs: complaining. Legs: present. Heart: still chained to something worth carrying.

"I can walk," I said. "Ask me again at the river."

And we started down the mountain, four of us, with the truth in an iron box and the whole drowned borderland finally, at long last, behind us.
