## 21. Surface

I have thought a great deal about what the surface looks like when you've forgotten it exists.

Here is the answer: it looks like nothing. It looks like gray water and gray sky and a riverbank made of mud and old rail ties, and it is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen in my life, and I am including the aurora over the Karelvo border that one summer before the war, which everyone always said was beautiful and which I slept through.

We came up out of the water like things being born badly. Sela first, because Sela would be first out of a burning building and first into heaven just to make sure the drinks were honest. Then me, coughing up half of Mount Serel. Then Freya, hauling Ilsa by the collar like a cat with a kitten, except the kitten was a grown woman who had betrayed one fleet and helped sink another inside a week, and who was crying so hard she couldn't swim straight.

The mountain sealed behind us. I want to be precise about that, because for years afterward people asked me what it sounded like, and they always wanted something dramatic — a roar, a boom, the fist of God. The truth is it sounded like a door closing in another room. A long settling groan, deep under the water, and then bubbles coming up in sheets, silver and enormous, and then nothing at all. The Meridian Star. Tomas Voss's journal. Osk Brann's tunnel door, which he held until the water took him. Corvane Aldous and his gold and his dredger's bones and every secret Mount Serel had ever been asked to keep — all of it folded shut under a hundred fathoms of rock and river, like a ledger someone finally closed.

Freya stood waist-deep in the shallows and looked back at it for a long time. Long enough that I started to worry about him, standing there in the cold with the current pulling at his legs like the river had opinions about letting him go.

Then he turned around and walked up the bank, and sat down in the mud next to me, and said, "Pip. Count us."

That's an engineer for you. Grief in one hand, inventory in the other.

"Four," I said. My voice came out scraped raw from the floodwater and the screaming, most of which had been mine. "Out of six."

He nodded slowly, like four was a number he'd expected and was still disappointed to meet. "Four," he said. "And the case?"

I pointed. The evidence case was still chained to his chest — I could see the chain links printed into his skin through the torn shirt, a pattern of bruises shaped like a man's whole purpose. He'd gone into the flood with it locked on and come out the same way. Somewhere in the dark between the last tide and the surface, he'd found the key, or decided not to look for it yet.

Sela flopped backward onto the bank, arms spread, and addressed the sky directly. "If anyone up there is taking requests," she said, "I would like warm bread and a bed that doesn't move, in that order, and I would like them immediately."

"You nearly drowned twice," I told her.

"I did *not* drown twice. I drowned once, briefly, as a tactical maneuver, and then un-drowned, which is the hardest thing I have ever done and I once smuggled a piano across the border in a coal barge." She turned her head and looked at me, and under all the bravado her eyes were red-rimmed and wrecked. "You carried me, Wren. Don't think I didn't notice."

"Somebody had to. You were busy being dead."

"Tactically."

* * *

We did not speak much for the rest of that day. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't have required more strength than any of us had left. We built a fire from wet driftwood and old ties, which smoked like a chimney with a grudge, and we ate the emergency rations Sela had sewn into her coat lining — the woman plans for her own drowning the way other people plan for retirement — and we watched the river run past, brown and indifferent, carrying its silt and its secrets south toward Greyharbor.

Ilsa sat apart. Nobody told her to; she just did it, the way you'd keep your distance from a dog that had bitten someone, even if the bite had maybe been justified. Twice I caught Freya looking at her, and both times his face did something complicated and settled on nothing.

It was Ilsa who broke the silence, near dark. "Aldous knew the tunnels would go," she said. "When the charges went off. His own engineers warned him the cavern was unstable. He ordered them to keep cutting anyway."

"He wanted the gold," Sela said flatly.

"He wanted *everything*. That was always the trouble with Corvane. Other men steal because they lack. Corvane stole because stopping felt like dying." She pulled her knees up to her chest. "I navigated for that man for six years. I charted his salvage claims and falsified his tonnage reports and told myself it was just work, it was just numbers, numbers don't drown anybody." Her voice cracked. "Osk Brann held that door for eleven minutes. I timed it. I timed everything on that fleet. Eleven minutes, and I know exactly how long that is, and I will know for the rest of my life."

Nobody answered her. But when we bedded down around the fire, nobody moved away from her either, and I noticed that Freya laid his coat over her where she'd fallen asleep sitting up, and I noticed that she woke just enough to let him, and pretended afterward that she hadn't.

Some forgiveness starts that way. Not with words. With coats.

* * *

Greyharbor looked different coming back to it.

Three weeks earlier I'd walked those docks as an apprentice with a stolen tool roll and no plan beyond following a mad engineer into a mountain. Now I came up the harbor road with salt in my hair, a limp I'd probably keep, and the certain knowledge that I had personally witnessed the death of the richest man on the coast — and that saying so out loud would get me killed faster than the mountain almost had.

Because here's the arithmetic nobody puts on posters: Aldous owned half of Greyharbor. His money sat in the banks, his ships filled the basin, his name was carved over the customs house in letters big enough to read from a moving train. Four soaked survivors claiming he'd died in a flooded tunnel, claiming he'd sold Karelvo's evacuation routes to enemy gunners, claiming the war's worst disaster had a author and not just a date — that wasn't a story. That was a suicide note with footnotes.

Freya understood it too. I watched him understand it, standing at the top of the harbor road with the case chained to his chest under a borrowed coat, looking down at the smoke and the masts and the gulls wheeling over the fish market like the world hadn't ended twice since he'd last seen it.

"There'll be men watching the docks," Ilsa said quietly. "Fleet officers, Aldous's factors, whoever inherits his interests. They'll be waiting for exactly this. Survivors."

"Then we don't go to the docks," Freya said.

"Where, then? You can't hand that case to a constable. Half the constabulary ate from Aldous's table."

"I know." He was quiet a moment. "There's a woman who prints a paper called *The Tidewater Ledger*. Marta Quill. Tomas used to write to her — letters, before the war. She published three of them about the border crossings. Aldous's lawyers buried the paper so deep it only comes out monthly now, and half the print run gets 'lost' in transit." Something moved at the corner of his mouth, not quite a smile. "She never stopped printing anyway. Tomas trusted her. That's good enough for me."

"And when she prints it?" Sela asked. She'd been quiet most of the walk, which for Sela was like a clock going silent — you notice the absence before you understand it. "When the truth's out and can't be buried again. What happens to *us*, Voss? To you? Because I've read enough history to know the man who hands over the evidence usually burns right along with the criminal. Aldous bought his wartime contracts through shell companies. Whose name do you suppose is on some of those shells?"

Freya didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was level and tired and absolutely certain, and I understood in that moment that he'd made this decision somewhere back in the dark, underwater, maybe while the mountain was coming down around him, maybe years ago.

"Mine," he said. "Some of them are mine. I signed papers after the war to get work. Salvage contracts, transport licenses. I didn't read the fine print because I didn't care whether I lived, and men who don't care whether they live will sign anything." He looked at each of us in turn. "So yes. When Quill prints this, my name burns too. I need you all to know that going in. You can walk away now, and I'll hand the case over alone, and none of this touches you."

There was a silence.

It was broken, naturally, by Sela, who snorted loud enough to startle gulls off a piling forty feet below. "Walk away. From the single greatest story of my disreputable life. Voss, I have been shot at, drowned, blown up, and betrayed in your company, and you think I'm going to hop off the train at the interesting part?" She stuck out her hand. "I'm in. But I want it noted in the record that I'm in *annoyed*."

I put my hand on top of hers. "In. Also annoyed."

Ilsa hesitated longest. She looked down at the harbor, at the flagship berth where the dredgers used to ride, empty now, and something in her face went very still. "Six years," she said softly. "I gave him six years, and the only clean thing I ever did was the last week. Print it all, Voss. My name too. Let it burn. Ash keeps better than guilt."

Freya looked at me last.

"Oh, don't do the thing," I said. "Don't do the solemn looking-at-the-apprentice thing. I'm in. Obviously I'm in. I followed you into a collapsing mountain, Mr. Voss, do you genuinely believe I have judgment?" I stuck out my hand, and he took it, and his grip was careful and warm and his eyes were wet, and neither of us mentioned it, because that's how we'd learned to love each other — silently, and mostly through logistics.

* * *

Marta Quill's print shop was above a chandlery on Rope Walk, behind a door with no sign, which is the traditional location for the truth in any port town.

She was fifty-ish and sharp-faced, ink-stained to the elbows, and she opened the door on our third knock holding a composing stick like a club. She looked at the four of us — mud-caked, salt-burned, one limping, one clearly ex-fleet, one radiating smuggler — and her eyes narrowed.

"If you're from Aldous's office," she said, "you're late. I told his man Tuesday that I'd see him in hell, and hell keeps shorter hours than I do."

"We're not from Aldous's office," Freya said. "Aldous is dead."

Now, I have seen a lot of faces do a lot of things in the past months. I watched a man hold a tunnel door against a flood. I watched another man realize his brother had been a hero instead of a ghost. But I don't think I'll ever forget Marta Quill's face in that doorway, because it went through shock and disbelief and grief and something ferociously like joy in the space of two seconds, and then she stepped aside and said, "Get in here before someone sees you," and that was that.

Inside smelled of ink and hot metal and cold coffee. She listened to us for two hours. She didn't interrupt once — a discipline I suspect she'd learned the hard way, printing truth in a town that preferred lies with better financing. Freya told it all: the chest, the manifest, the tunnels, the journal, the evacuation routes sold to enemy gunners, the sealed diplomatic cases, the betrayal, Osk at the door, Aldous refusing to leave the gold, the flood. When he got to his own name on the shell contracts, he said it plainly, without flinching, and I loved him for it and hated the world for requiring it.

Quill took notes in a tiny furious hand. At the end she set down her pencil and looked at Freya over her spectacles.

"You understand what happens tomorrow," she said. "Not the story — the *aftermath*. Aldous's partners will call it fabrication. The courts will call it libel. Men will come to this shop, and to your door, and to the doors of everyone whose name appears in these pages. Some of them will carry warrants. Some won't bother."

"I know."

"And knowing that, you still brought it to me."

"The river kept it long enough," Freya said. "It's time somebody kept it in daylight."

Quill was quiet a moment. Then she reached under the press and brought out a bottle and four chipped cups, and poured, and raised hers.

"To Tomas Voss," she said. "Who wrote me the truest letters I ever set in type, and who I now learn died protecting the proof of them." She drank. "To Osk Brann, whom I never met, and who sounds like the kind of man the obituary pages exist for. And to the four of you, who are about to become either famous or dead, and in my experience the difference is mostly a matter of printing speed."

She was already turning to the press as she said it. Already reaching for type. And I swear the woman began to smile — a hard, bright, dangerous smile, the smile of somebody who has waited years for a night shift worth working.

"How fast can you print?" Freya asked.

"Marta Quill," she said, rolling her sleeves higher, "once set eight hundred words on the Karelvo border crossings while bailiffs were literally climbing the stairs. How fast do you need it?"

"As fast as the truth can travel."

"Then stand clear," said Marta Quill, "and mind the machinery."

* * *

I stayed to watch the first pages come off the press, because somebody had to witness it and because my legs had decided walking was a rumor. The press thundered like a train. Ink blackened Quill's forearms to the elbow. Sheet after sheet came out damp and warm, and on each one, in type that would not be buried, lost, or drowned:

**THE MERIDIAN STAR: THE TRUTH OF KARELVO.**

By dawn the stack reached my knee. By full light, Sela had hired three fishermen who owed her favors, and the pages were going out on every boat leaving the harbor, tucked into cargo manifests and bait wrappings and hymnals, because Sela understands distribution the way sharks understand swimming.

Freya stood in the shop doorway watching the boats go out, the case finally unlocked and empty at his feet, the chain coiled beside it like a shed skin. The morning sun came up over Greyharbor and caught the water, and for the first time since I'd known him, he wasn't measuring anything. Not miles. Not tides. Not losses.

"Pip," he said, without turning around. "What's today?"

I told him the date. He nodded slowly, committing it to memory — not to a ledger, I realized. Just to himself. A man learning, at forty-odd, to keep time the ordinary way.

Down at the docks, a crowd was gathering around a fishmonger who was reading aloud from a damp sheet of newsprint, his voice rising with every line. And at the edge of that crowd, I saw a harbor official tear off his cap and start running — toward the customs house, toward the telegraph office, toward whatever powers still had Aldous's money in their pockets and were about to learn the war wasn't done with them.

Freya watched him go, and rolled his shoulders like a man picking up a load he intended to carry the whole distance.

"Well," he said. "They'll come for us now."

"Yes," I said.

"Good," said Freya Voss, and stepped out into the morning to meet them.
