## 22. Open Lines

The ledger I buried with the old life had a twin. It sat in the bottom of my kit bag for three years, bought the same week as the first one, back when I still believed in redundancy — two books for one man, because a man who measures his life in miles ought to have a spare in case the river takes the first. The river never took it. The river took everything else.

I opened it on a Tuesday morning in spring, on the tailgate of Sela's barge, with Pip's elbow in my ribs and Osk's ghost sitting politely in the empty space where he used to complain about my coffee.

"About time," Pip said.

"About time what?"

"You've been staring at that blank page for ten minutes like it owes you money."

"It might," I said. "Everything else does."

She snorted and went back to greasing the handcar's chain, and I looked down at the first clean page of a book with no miles in it yet, and I wrote, in my careful engineer's hand, the date. Just the date. Then, underneath it, because old habits are load-bearing structures and you don't tear them out without shoring something up first: *Branch line, Vellmark cut to Lower Serel valley. Fourteen miles of dead rail. Condition: dead.*

Underneath that: *Crew: four.*

It was the first entry in three years that wasn't a measurement of distance from something. It was, if you squinted at it right, a measurement of distance *to* something instead. I'd take it. A man has to rebuild his instruments before he can trust his readings.

* * *

The line we'd chosen — or that had chosen us, depending on how much agency you want to grant a drunk conversation on a dock — was the Vellmark branch. It ran fourteen miles up the valley from the river junction to a string of towns the war had amputated from the world: Vellmark itself, then Hossen, then the little farming hamlets past it whose names only appeared on survey maps and gravestones. When the border closed, the branch died with it. Rails rusted, ties rotted, a culvert collapsed at mile nine and took half the embankment with it into a creek that had opinions about its course. Fourteen miles of nothing connecting somewhere to nowhere.

Except it wasn't nowhere. That was the thing nobody with capital had ever bothered to learn. There were eleven hundred people up that valley growing food nobody could buy and needing medicine nobody could ship, because the road washed out every winter and the government — the new one, the honest-ish one, the one Aldous's evidence had scraped a layer of rot off of — had classified the branch as "economically nonviable," which is bureaucratic for *we counted the money and not the people.*

Sela put it differently, the night we decided. She'd come off the barge with a bottle of plum brandy she claimed was medicinal and stood looking up the valley at the dark seam of the old grade cutting through the trees.

"Eleven hundred people," she said. "That's a small war."

"That's not a war at all," I said.

"No," she agreed. "That's the point. First one I've worked that isn't." She passed me the bottle. "So. Are we doing this?"

We were doing this. I don't think any of us said it out loud, but by morning the handcar was loaded and Pip had already drawn up a materials list twice as long as our budget, which is the traditional way engineers begin projects.

* * *

I'll say this for rebuilding a dead line: it is honest work, and honesty was a currency I was short on. For three years I'd walked closed lines measuring distances away from my brother's name, and there is no honest work in that. There's only ritual. But driving spikes into a tie that will carry a schoolteacher's cart next month — that's work that stays done. That's work that looks back at you.

The crew had settled into shapes by then, the way crews do, the way families do when they stop pretending they're anything else.

Pip ran the track geometry. She was nineteen and had been an apprentice to a man who sold her section of the country to both armies in the same week, and she carried that the way she carried her tools — close, organized, checked twice daily. She could sight a curve by eye to within a quarter degree and would tell you so whether you asked or not. In the months since the mountain, since Osk, she'd stopped flinching at water sounds. Mostly. We didn't talk about it. We didn't need to; we'd all been in the tunnels. Some things you carry together by carrying them separately.

Sela ran logistics, which meant she ran everything, which meant she stole appropriately. Her barge ferried rail, ties, and ballast up the river while she cultivated a relationship with the junction freight agent that I have chosen to understand as professional courtesy. She still didn't fully sleep indoors. She still kept a knife under her pillow and a second knife somewhere I never found. But she'd started leaving the barge lamp burning when Pip worked late over the maps, and if you know Sela, you know that's a love letter written in kerosene.

And me. I ran the line itself — grades, drainage, the long slow argument with the creek at mile nine. And I wrote the ledger. Dates now. Weather, progress, names of the men from Hossen who came down with mules to help pull rail. Small true things.

Some mornings I still woke reaching for the old habit — *how many miles today, how far from* — and some mornings the grief arrived anyway, punctual as a mail train, and sat with me while the coffee boiled. Tomas lived in those mornings. Not the missing boy-shaped hole he'd been for three years, but the actual man, the one from the journal, who'd found the worst truth in Karelvo and chained himself to it so it couldn't drown quietly. I'd spent three years mourning a disappearance. Now I mourned a person, which is harder and better, the way a broken bone set properly hurts more than one that healed crooked but lets you use the hand.

He'd have hated this project, incidentally. He'd have called it sentimental and then shown up with a mule and stayed six weeks. Brothers.

* * *

Mile nine nearly killed us in April, which felt fitting. The culvert had failed the way old stone fails — slowly, invisibly, all at once. Half the embankment was gone and the creek had established a new nation in the gap, complete with borders and customs duties.

"We reroute," Pip said, pacing the bank. "Quarter-mile detour on fresh grade. Six weeks."

"We bridge," Sela said. "Salvage girder off the old Hossen spur. Three weeks, if I can float it."

"If you can float it," I said.

"I can float anything," Sela said. "I've floated you."

In the end we floated it — a forty-foot deck girder hauled out of a siding where it had been rusting since before the war, barged up the creek at flood stage with Sela swearing at the water in three languages and the water swearing back. Pip designed the abutments. I set the last wedge myself, standing in the creek to my thighs in snowmelt, hammering home iron while the whole crew watched from the bank, and when the girder took load and didn't so much as sing, Pip let out a yell that scattered crows two valleys over.

That night we camped on the new grade and Sela produced the last bottle of the medicinal brandy and we drank to Osk, properly, the way divers do — poured a measure on the ground first, for the man who held the door. Nobody made speeches. Osk would have charged us by the word.

"The fleet's finished, you know," Sela said, after a while. "What's left of it. Sold for scrap in Greyharbor. I saw the notice."

"Aldous's flagship?"

"Especially that. They're cutting her up for razor blades." She smiled, thin and satisfied. "Somewhere in this country there's a barber shaving a magistrate with Corvane Aldous. There's a justice in that even the newspapers missed."

The newspapers hadn't missed much else. The evidence had gone to a journalist named Corrick in Greyharbor eight months ago, and it had gone off like a depth charge in a fish market. Trials. Resignations. Two ministers gone before the first frost. My own name in the columns too — *engineer Freya Voss, who located the wreck* — printed next to words like *treason* and *evacuation routes*, and every time I read them I thought: good. Let it burn my name. Names grow back. Eleven hundred people in a valley don't, unless somebody lays them a line.

* * *

The first train ran in June.

It wasn't much of a train. A converted inspection car and two flatbeds borrowed against promises Sela had no intention of keeping, hauled by a little tank engine we'd resurrected from a shed in Hossen where it had been sleeping under tarps since the border closed. Pip drove it. She'd taught herself the cab in a fortnight out of a manual older than she was, and she drove the way she did everything — precisely, loudly, and with commentary.

The whole valley turned out. That's not sentiment; that's reportage. At Vellmark junction there must have been three hundred people, and at every hamlet along the run, kids ran the banks alongside us and old men took their hats off, actually took their hats off, to a rust-streaked tank engine doing fifteen miles an hour with a flatbed of seed potatoes. In Hossen they'd strung lanterns across the street though it was broad daylight, because nobody up there had seen a train in nine years and they were going to celebrate it properly regardless of the sun's schedule.

I stood on the running board and watched the valley open up around us — fields going gold, the creek at mile nine running quiet under our new girder, smoke standing straight up in the still air — and I did the thing I'd sworn off for three years.

I counted time. Not miles. Time. Nine years since these people had heard a whistle. Eight months since the trials started. Three years since a storm gave me a chest full of my brother's name. One year since four strangers stood around a fire not trusting each other, and one of them was already gone, and the other three were here, on this train, laughing.

Time, it turns out, is just distance measured honestly.

* * *

That evening, back at the junction, I opened the ledger at the tailgate while the others argued about whether the Hossen engine needed new packing or merely wanted it. Pip won, as usual. The engine would get new packing.

I turned to a fresh page and wrote the date. Under it: *Vellmark branch, complete. First service run, 14 June. Passengers: everyone who showed up.* Under that, because the page had room and because some entries want writing even when nobody asked:

*Tomas — your line runs. I know you'd call it sentimental. Bring the mule anyway.*

Then I sat with the pen hovering, and realized I wasn't done, and that the not-done part was the whole point.

Because there was a map spread across the tailgate — Pip's map, marked up in her merciless pencil — and it showed more than the Vellmark branch. It showed the whole drowned borderland's skeleton: dead spurs, closed branches, orphaned stations, thirty miles of steel waiting for somebody with a handcar, a barge, a stolen girder or two, and no sense whatsoever. Up past Hossen the line climbed toward the high passes. Toward, if you followed the old surveys far enough, a tunnel mouth below Mount Serel that had been sealed since the collapse, behind which lay a cavern, a wreck, and a grave we'd never been able to go back to.

Osk was down there. So was the Meridian Star, and the gold Aldous died clutching, and the dark water where the best of us held a door.

Sela leaned over my shoulder, following my eyes up the map to that penciled circle at the top. She was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful lightness she used for things that mattered.

"The pass line," she said. "That's a hard run."

"That's a very hard run."

"Season's short. Rockfall through the narrows. And the approach goes within two miles of Serel." She tapped the mountain's inked shoulder. "Water's still deep there."

"I know how deep," I said.

Pip had stopped arguing with the engine and come around the tailgate, wiping her hands, reading the map upside down the way she read everything. She looked at the circle. She looked at me. Whatever she saw in my face, she didn't flinch from it — she'd learned that in the tunnels, along with everything else.

"There's a schoolteacher in Hossen," she said, "whose sister's family is up past the pass. No road. No line. Nothing but a mule track that kills someone every other winter." She picked up the pencil and, without asking anyone's permission, drew a firm line from Hossen up toward the sealed heights. "So. Are we doing this?"

Sela grinned the grin she saved for flood stages and impossible cargoes. Somewhere in the dusk a locomotive sighed on the siding, alive again, wanting work.

I looked down at the ledger — dates, weather, names, small true things — and at the blank space under today's entry, which was not blank at all, really. It was a question. It had always been a question. Miles had been my way of not answering it.

I picked up the pen and wrote:

*Next: the pass line. Crew: four. Season: short. Distance: not the point.*

Then I closed the book, and for the first time in three years, the sound of it shutting sounded less like a door than like a departure bell — and above the valley, faint and clear, the Hossen engine whistled once, as if it had read the page over my shoulder and approved.
