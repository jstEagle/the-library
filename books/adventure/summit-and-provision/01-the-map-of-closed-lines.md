## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The map on Yara Dunmore's wall was not a map of places. It was a map of departures.

She'd drawn it herself, years ago, in the flat light of a boarding-house lamp in Corrieholm: every rail line in the western ranges, inked in careful strokes, and over it a second layer of marks—red for lines still running, black for lines closed, and a small, patient circle wherever she had signed a contract and then left before anyone could ask her to stay.

There were a great many circles.

She stood at the window of her room above the freight office in Tallow Bend and looked out at the spur line below, which had been dead for eleven years and which she had been hired, three weeks past, to certify as dead beyond all hope of resurrection. The report was finished. It sat on the desk behind her in a canvas folder, tied with string, forty pages of elevations and drainage notes that amounted, in the end, to one sentence: *This line is done.*

Somewhere south of here, a man named Aldous Petrie was waiting to read that sentence and pay her for it, and Yara was already thinking about the door.

That was the trouble with this work—the work she was good at, the only work she'd ever been good at. Surveying a dead line meant walking something from end to end and pronouncing it finished, and there was a part of Yara, some stubborn cellar-room of her heart, that understood the assignment personally. She could certify herself. She'd have the references.

Below, a cart rattled across the old grade crossing, its driver navigating the sunken rails without looking down, the way you stop seeing the scar on your own hand. Tallow Bend had been built by the railroad and then un-built by it, gently, the way a tide goes out—not all at once but in small withdrawals, the hotel closing first, then the second storey of the dry goods store, then the school. What remained was a town shaped like an afterthought: a freight office with nothing much to freight, a saloon that had learned to serve tea, and a population that watched strangers with the particular hunger of people who remembered when strangers were an industry.

Yara had been in Tallow Bend nineteen days. That was eleven days longer than her average, and she knew it, because she kept count the way other people kept accounts—compulsively, and with a small dread of the total.

*Stopping costs more than moving,* her mother used to say, though what she'd meant was the opposite of what Yara had made of it. Her mother had meant that roots were expensive; that you paid for them in grief when the flood came or the mine closed or the man left, and it was cheaper never to plant. Yara had taken the arithmetic and run it the wrong direction down the same hill: it was staying that cost. Staying meant names. Staying meant that Mrs. Calloway at the tea-saloon would learn how you took your eggs, and that the boy who swept the platform would start saving you the interesting rocks he found along the ballast, and that one morning you'd wake up and realize the town had gotten into you like fine silt into a watch mechanism—harmless in quantity, fatal in accumulation—and leaving would hurt in ways that couldn't be itemized on an invoice.

Moving cost nothing but shoe leather. Yara owned excellent boots.

A knock at the door. Two raps, apologetic, the knock of a man who had spent his life asking permission.

"Miss Dunmore? Freight office said you'd be up here. It's Petrie's man—Jubal. I've a wire."

She opened the door on a young fellow with a jaw full of worry and a telegram held like a live fish. She took it, tipped him a coin he tried to refuse, and read it standing in the doorway so that reading it would remain a thing she was doing rather than a thing that was happening to her.

The wire was from a firm she didn't know—Harrowgate & Voss, Provisioners and Concession Agents, Karst Range District. It said:

*SURVEY CONTRACT OFFERED. ROUTE RECONNAISSANCE KARST RANGE NORTH APPROACH. TERM EXPIRES FIRST SNOWFALL OR DEC 1 WHICHEVER PRECEDES. PERMITS LAPSE AT THAT DATE WITHOUT EXCEPTION. RATE GENEROUS. REPLY BY WIRE.*

She read it twice. The generous rate was generous indeed—generous enough that she read it a third time looking for the trap, because in her experience generosity in a contract was like calm water over a weir: it told you exactly where the drop was.

The terms were the strange part. Not a certification of a dead line this time, but a reconnaissance—a route survey through the Karst Range itself, the high limestone country that every map she owned showed as a blank with a few hopeful dotted lines crossing it, the cartographic equivalent of whistling in the dark. Nobody ran track through the Karst. The rock drank water and swallowed tunnels; the grades were murderous; and the passes filled with snow by the first week of December as regular as rent coming due.

And the permits lapsed at the first snowfall. Without exception.

Which meant somebody wanted this survey badly enough to pay for speed, and wanted it *quietly* enough that the deadline did the enforcing instead of men with shotguns, which was cheaper, and in Yara's experience usually meant the same people.

She should wire back a refusal. She had a completed report to deliver to Petrie, a payment to collect, and a perfectly good plan—already half-formed, the way her plans always were—to drift north along the closed spurs toward the coast towns for the winter, where the work was thin but the rooms were cheap and nobody asked questions. Nineteen days in Tallow Bend was already too many. The circles on her map were getting crowded together. If she stayed much longer, Mrs. Calloway would learn about the eggs.

Instead she heard herself say, "Jubal. Is there a telegraph clerk sober enough to send a wire tonight?"

He blinked. "Old Tam's sober till eight, miss. After that it's a coin toss."

"Then we've twenty minutes." And she went inside and wrote, in her neat engineer's hand: *ACCEPT. DUNMORE.*

It was only later—much later, on a night when the wind off the Karst glaciers made the windows sing—that she would try to explain to someone why she'd accepted, and find that the honest answer sounded ridiculous even in her own head: because the deadline was the kindest thing anyone had offered her in years. A hard date. An expiration. A contract that *ended*, formally, in writing, whether she wanted it to or not—so that when she left, and she would leave, no one could say she'd abandoned anything. The snow would do the leaving for her. The mountain itself would sign the release papers.

Yara Dunmore did not fear the high country. She feared the low country: the valleys where people accumulated.

She packed that night, because packing was a kind of prayer she believed in. Her kit was famous among the few who'd seen it—famous to herself, anyway—for its efficiency. One trunk, two instrument cases (the transit and the level, both her father's, both older than she was and better maintained), a bedroll, oilskin, and a flat tin box of tools that had once belonged to a man who taught her that a chain measurement was only as honest as the person holding the far end of the chain. She'd thought about that a lot lately, for no reason she could name. Honesty at the far end of the chain.

Before dawn she walked down to the platform to wait for the mixed train—the one passenger-and-freight run a week that still deigned to stop at Tallow Bend—and found Mrs. Calloway already there, sweeping the boards, which she did every morning regardless of weather or sense.

"You're going, then," Mrs. Calloway said. Not a question. Towns like this always knew before you did.

"Contract came through."

"Karst way?" The old woman's broom paused. Something moved behind her face—quick, like a fish under ice. "You'll want to talk to Bram Halloway, then. He's up the coast now, but he worked those lines when they were lines. Only one living."

"I don't need a guide."

"No," Mrs. Calloway agreed mildly, and resumed sweeping. "I expect you don't. You'll want him all the same, someday, and you won't know it till you do." She looked at Yara with the frank appraisal of a woman who had watched forty years of travelers mistake motion for purpose. "Nineteen days. That's a long stay, for you. I'd begun to think you liked us."

"The eggs were adequate."

Mrs. Calloway laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and Yara felt the familiar cold pinch under her ribs that meant a place had gotten another fingerhold, and it was time, *past* time, to go.

The mixed train came in at seven, hissing and groaning like an old man lowering himself into a bath. Yara boarded with her kit, took a seat facing backward, which she always did—it let her watch places recede, which was easier than watching them arrive—and as the town slid away she performed the ritual: she took out her map, found the little circle around Tallow Bend, and drew the next one, far to the north, in blank country near a name she'd have to look up.

The circles marched north like footprints. Someday, she supposed, someone would find the map and think it a record of a life. It was closer to a record of evasions. Each circle was a place she'd almost let matter, catalogued and closed, certified dead beyond all hope of resurrection, filed and forgotten.

Except.

Except that somewhere past the third bend of the river, watching the black line of the Karst Range rise against the morning like a wall built by something with more patience than kindness, Yara caught herself doing a thing she hadn't done in years: wondering. Not planning—wondering. Why a company would pay her rate to survey a route no sane engineer would build. Why the permits expired with the snow instead of the season. Why the wire had come to *her*, specifically, of all the drifting engineers on the western lines, a woman whose reputation was not brilliance, exactly, but reliability plus absence—she delivered, and then she was gone, and gone was a quality certain kinds of employers prized above skill.

Somebody wanted a route through the Karst Range, and wanted it fast, and wanted it done by someone who wouldn't be around afterward to answer questions.

Well. She could be that someone. She'd been that someone before. There was no shame in it—or rather, there was shame in it, a low steady hum of it she'd learned to sleep beside the way you sleep beside a railway line, until the trains become silence and the silence becomes home.

The conductor came through and punched her ticket. "Karst Junction?" he asked, reading the destination off the stub, and gave her the look conductors give—the one that says *nobody rides to the end of this line on purpose.*

"Survey work," she said.

"Ah." He nodded, satisfied. "So you'll be wanting to get off at Harrowgate landing, then. Tide town. That's where they stage the range parties." He touched his cap. "Mind the tides there, miss. They run strange. Big lows twice a month pull the sea back half a mile, and folk say—" he smiled, embarrassed by his own folklore— "well, folk say things wash up. Or wash *out*. Old stories. You'll hear them soon enough."

"What kind of things?"

"Places," the conductor said, and moved on down the aisle, and Yara sat with that word the rest of the morning, turning it over, while the mountains grew ahead of her and the circles on her map waited patiently behind, each one a door she'd closed quietly enough that no one had ever heard the latch.

At Harrowgate landing she would take the contract, hire what help the town could offer, and begin. That was the plan, and it was a good plan, and plans were the one family Yara had never failed.

But that evening, as the train climbed the last coastal grade and the sea appeared below—vast, gray, and patient, waiting with the air of something that intended to give back everything it had ever taken, given time—Yara Dunmore opened her tin box to check her instruments and found, tucked between the level and the plumb bob, a folded scrap of paper she had no memory of putting there.

In her own handwriting, though she couldn't recall writing it—a note from some exhausted midnight, probably, some station between stations:

*Ask at the Tollhouse.*

She stared at it for a long mile. Then, because curiosity had always been the one lockpick her caution never managed to confiscate, she folded it away, looked out at the darkening water, and began—for the first time in nine years, and against every rule she lived by—to wonder what it would cost to stop.

* * *

The wire she'd sent from Tallow Bend got its reply at Harrowgate landing, two days later, forwarded through three offices and arriving damp. It read:

*CONTRACT CONFIRMED. REPORT TO DISTRICT OFFICE HARBOR ST. BRING OWN CREW IF YOU CAN GET ONE. — H&V*

*Bring your own crew if you can get one.* Which was company language for *we are not vouching for anyone you find,* which was company language for *we are not going to be connected to whatever happens up there,* which was, Yara reflected, standing on the harbor street with the gulls screaming overhead and the smell of brine and coal smoke in her nose, the most honest sentence any employer had ever sent her.

She looked down the street toward the district office, and then out—at the mudflats beyond the harbor mole, vast and glistening, laid bare by one of those strange big lows the conductor had promised. Half a mile of exposed seabed, steaming faintly in the cold sun, littered with the sea's abandoned furniture: kelp, wreck timber, stones like sleeping animals. And at the far edge of it, where the water stood off in a thin silver line, a dark opening in the cliff face that had no business being there—a cave mouth, tall as a church door, breathing out the cold.

An old man gathering driftwood nearby saw her looking.

"Tollhouse," he said, following her gaze. "That's the one. Tide's out far enough today, if you're minded. Most aren't." He shifted his bundle. "They say it charges admission."

"Charges what?"

"Admission," he said again, patiently, as if to a child. "Everything worth entering does." And he walked on, leaving Yara standing at the edge of the exposed flats with her contract unsigned in her pocket, her instruments on her shoulder, and the strangest sensation that the map of closed lines she carried everywhere—in her trunk, in her habits, in her chest—had just shown her one more mark.

Not a departure, this time.

An entrance.
