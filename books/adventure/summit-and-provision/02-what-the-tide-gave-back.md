## 2. What the Tide Gave Back

The staging town was called Grebe's Landing, which Yara found optimistic, since the only grebe she saw in two days was dead and stuffed and mounted above the bar of the Saltcellar, wearing a tiny felt hat nobody could explain.

"It came in on a crate of nails in '61," the barman said, when she asked. "Nobody remembers who put the hat on. Nobody's going to."

That, Yara thought, was as good a philosophy for Grebe's Landing as any. The town sat where the mountains shrugged down to meet the sea, all salt-bleached timber and rusted cranes, built to feed the mining concessions uprange and now mostly feeding itself on the memory of doing it. Two piers, one of them honest. A rail spur that ran up the valley and stopped where the money had. And a tide that went out twice a day like a landlord clearing out a tenant's things.

She'd arrived on the last through freight, riding the caboose with a conductor who talked the entire six hours about his gallbladder, and she'd taken a room at the Anchorage Hotel because it was the only one whose sign hadn't lost letters in a way that changed the meaning. The bed sagged in the middle like a hammock with ambitions. The window looked out on the harbor, and the harbor looked like it had been left out in the rain for forty years, which, in fairness, it had.

The contract was in her bag. She'd read it four times on the train, which was three more times than she usually bothered. Survey a route through the Karst Range for the Meridian Consolidated Mining and Smelting Company, before the snows, before the concession permits expired at the end of the month. Good money. Hard deadline. The kind of job she took precisely because it had an end built into it, a date after which she could fold her instruments, collect her pay, and be gone before anyone learned how she took her coffee or what she looked like first thing in the morning or any of the other small betrayals of intimacy.

Stopping cost more than moving. She'd learned that young, and the lesson had never needed revising.

She spent the first day walking the spur line out of town, pacing grades with her clinometer and making notes in a hand so cramped her own mother couldn't have read it, which was the point of it. The second day the weather turned clear and cold, the kind of hard blue morning that made the whole coast look freshly minted, and the barman at the Saltcellar — his name was Dree, and he told her this twice — said the words that would ruin her week.

"Spring tide tomorrow," he said, polishing a glass that had given up on being clean sometime the previous decade. "Lowest of the year. You'll want to see the Tollhouse."

"The what?"

"Tollhouse." He nodded out the window, toward the point below the cliffs where the water went flat and gray-green. "Sea cave. Comes out of the water at extreme low tide, only a few days a year, only for an hour or two. Old stories say it charges admission."

Yara turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "Charges what?"

"Admission." He said it the way a man says a thing he's said four hundred times to four hundred strangers and has never once gotten tired of. "That's the story. You go in, you take something out, you owe the Tollhouse. Nobody remembers what the toll is. That's the fun of it." He leaned in, conspiratorial, breath like old beer and older coffee. "Kids dare each other to go in and come out with nothing in their pockets. Some of them do it. Some of them come out with something anyway and won't say what."

"And what happens to those ones?"

Dree straightened up, satisfied. "They come back the next low tide and put it back."

* * *

Yara told herself she was only going to look.

She told herself this at breakfast, over eggs the color of an overcast sky. She told herself this as she walked out along the shingle below the cliffs at half past seven, boots crunching on stones worn round and gray as old teeth. She told herself it again when she reached the point and found the sea drawn back farther than she'd ever seen a sea drawn back, exposing a floor of dark rock ribbed with weed and pocked with pools that held their own private skies.

The cave mouth was exactly where the stories put it, which annoyed her a little. Stories were usually wrong about geography. But there it was: a low black arch in the cliff base, maybe eight feet at its crown, the stone around it scoured smooth and pale as bone. Water still dripped from its lip. The air coming out of it was cold and mineral, and it smelled of old salt and older dark.

She checked her watch. Low water at 8:12. The tide would turn and come back hungry; the locals had told her she'd have ninety minutes, maybe less, and that the cave flooded to its roof on the turn, and that a man named Corliss had drowned in there in 1948 because he'd stayed to finish a cigarette. She didn't smoke. She noted the rest.

She went in.

The floor rose gently, dry stone giving way to dry stone, the walls narrowing and then opening out into a chamber that her lamp found all at once, the way a hand finds a face in the dark. It was bigger than the mouth promised — a vault of black rock maybe forty feet across, its ceiling hung with dry stalactites that had stopped growing when the sea took over and now just dangled there like the punchlines of jokes nobody told anymore. Barnacles crusted the lower walls in a tideline. Above the barnacles, the stone was furred with something pale and soft-looking that she decided not to touch.

"Charges admission," she said aloud, to hear a voice in the dark. Her voice came back to her off the rock, flattened and strange. "What does it charge? Pebbles? Firstborns? I'm a surveyor. I've got a clinometer and half a tin of biscuits."

The cave did not answer, which she'd expected, but she was mildly disappointed anyway.

She worked the walls the way she worked everything: methodically, left to right, lamp high. There were old marks in the stone — initials, dates, a crude ship, a heart containing the letters E plus T that somebody had gone to trouble over. Names went back a long way. 1889. 1903. A cluster of carvings from what she guessed was the war years, when the coast had been nervous and bored. And in one corner, low, half-hidden behind a curtain of flowstone, a mark that stopped her cold: a surveyor's benchmark, cut with a chisel, precise as a signature. A cross in a circle, and beneath it a code: *MC&S Co. B-7.*

Meridian Consolidated and Smelting.

She crouched. Ran her thumb along the cut. It was old — the edges softened by salt, the paint some surveyor had once filled it with long gone — but it was professional work, done by someone who knew stone and cared about it. A company benchmark, inside a sea cave, a mile from anything the company owned.

"Well," she told the cave. "Now you're interesting."

The chamber had a back to it. The floor climbed again past the benchmark, through a squeeze between two slabs, and opened into a second room, smaller, drier, smelling of nothing at all — no salt, no rot, just cold stone and stillness, the air of a place the sea had given up on. Her lamp swept across it and caught a gleam.

Metal. A corner of metal, back in a crevice where the wall met the floor, half-buried under a fall of small rock.

Yara stood very still for a moment and had the conversation with herself that she always had in moments like this, the one between the part of her that was a professional with a deadline and no interest in complications, and the part of her that had become a surveyor in the first place because she could not walk past a locked door or an unexplained number without her whole chest itching.

The professional said: *You have a route to run. The permits expire in three weeks. Whatever that is, it is not yours, and the smart thing is to mark this cave on your map, walk out, and never think of it again.*

The other part of her said nothing at all. It was already kneeling at the crevice.

The box came free with a grinding of pebbles. It was an iron strongbox, the kind banks and railroads had used fifty years ago, maybe two feet long, its paint gone to rust, its handle seized. It was heavy in the way of things full of flat metal. There was no lock on it — or rather, there was a hasp, and the hasp had been broken, long ago, the metal peeled back like a tin of sardines opened by someone in a hurry or with no key.

She set it on a flat stone and worked the lid. It groaned. Inside, packed in rows like loaves in a baker's tray, were glass plates.

Photographic plates. Survey negatives, she realized, drawing one out into the lamplight — heavy glass, eight by ten, each one in a paper sleeve gone soft and brown with age. The sleeves were stamped with a seal: an eagle standing on a mountain, and around it, in letters she could read even through the corrosion of the ink, *MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED MINING & SMELTING — SURVEY DEPARTMENT.*

There were dozens of them. She counted by touch, careful, her gloves on now though she couldn't have said when she'd put them on. Sixty-one. Sixty-one glass negatives, each one a piece of some survey done decades ago, sealed in an iron box, hidden in a sea cave that only existed a few days a year.

She held one up to the lamp, trying to see through the glass. It was too dark to read the image, but she could see the shapes of lines on it — ruled borders, a title block, the dense handwriting of a surveyor's field sheet. She tilted it. Caught a word or two: *...watershed... section 14... diversion...*

The word *diversion* sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Admission," she murmured. "So this is what you charge. Questions."

And then, because she was who she was, she did the thing the smart part of her had been dreading: she looked at the floor of the chamber properly, the way she'd look at any site, reading it.

The pebbles told the story. There was a scatter of them against the far wall that hadn't fallen — they'd been moved, pushed aside in a fan, recently. The weed-damp on the lower stones was scuffed in a line leading from the squeeze to the crevice and back. And there, in the dust of the floor, a boot print. Just one, clear, near the box: a hobnail heel, a man's size, the tread pattern crisp. It had been made since the last rain-wash, and there was no rain-wash in here, which meant it was recent — days, not months. Weeks at the outside.

Someone had been here. Recently. Someone had found the box, opened it — the hasp was old work, but the lid had been lifted since, she saw now, a fresh scrape of rust along the lip — and then had left it. Left it and gone.

Or hadn't gone far.

Yara sat back on her heels and listened to the cave breathe. Water dripped somewhere behind her, patient as a clock. The dark past her lamp was total and had opinions.

"Hello?" she said. It came out steadier than she felt. "If you're in here, I should tell you I've got a lamp, a clinometer, and a very firm belief in leaving people alone."

Nothing. The drip. The sea, far off, beginning to think about coming home.

She looked down at the box, and at the plate in her hand, and at the word she couldn't stop reading even though she'd only seen it for a second: *diversion.* She thought about the company seal on her own contract, sitting in her bag up at the Anchorage. She thought about the deadline. Three weeks. Snows. Permits.

She thought about Dree's story, and the kids who came back at the next low tide to put things back, and the ones who didn't, and what the Tollhouse charged, and whether the toll had already been paid by whoever's boot print this was, and whether they'd meant to come back and hadn't.

The tide would turn in forty minutes. She had a decision to make, and the professional in her had stopped arguing, which was its way of washing its hands.

"Right," she said to the cave, and began, carefully, to repack the plates.

She took the box. She told herself she'd only carry it as far as town — that she'd ask questions, that she'd find out whose boot print it was, that if nobody claimed it she'd turn it over to somebody official, a harbormaster, a county clerk, anyone whose job it was to hold things. She told herself all of it, and she heard exactly how it sounded, and she picked up the box anyway, because it was heavy, and because the word *diversion* had gotten into her blood, and because she had spent eleven years being a woman who walked past things, and her legs were tired of it.

The light at the cave mouth had changed — gone from gray to silver, the sea outside lying flat as hammered tin, already reaching back in long fingers toward the rocks it had let go. She squeezed out through the entrance with the box hugged to her chest, cold iron against her coat, and came out into the enormous low-tide morning with her boots already wet and the clock in her chest running.

Behind her, the Tollhouse swallowed its doorway as the water rose, unhurried, a landlord locking up.

She was halfway across the exposed shingle, counting the pools between her and dry land, when she saw the figure. Up on the cliff path, small with distance, standing where the trail bent — a man, she thought, in a long coat, not moving, not walking, just standing. Watching her. Watching the box.

By the time she'd blinked the sea-glare out of her eyes, the bend of the path was empty.

Yara stood a moment with the cold iron in her arms and the tide coming in around her boots, and thought: *Well. Now the whole week's ruined.*

She walked faster.
