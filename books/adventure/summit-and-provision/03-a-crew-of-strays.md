## 3. A Crew of Strays

The hiring hall in Saltmere was a room the size of a coffin and twice as cold, and it smelled of lamp oil and the particular despair of men who had been waiting for work since the last line closed. Yara Dunmore stood at the counter with her contract in one hand and a list of names in the other, and she listened to the clerk explain, for the third time, why she was making a mistake.

"You want the Karst crossing before the snows," the clerk said. He had a mustache like a dead moth and the tone of a man delivering a eulogy. "In November. With a crew."

"That's the general shape of it."

"You'll get nobody good. Everybody good is gone. The company took them east, and the ones left are—" He waved a hand at the window, beyond which the town's single street sat in the gray light like something washed ashore. "Strays."

"I've been a stray," Yara said. "We're not so bad."

The clerk did not laugh. She hadn't expected him to. She folded the list into her pocket and went out into the cold to find her crew, because a crew was what the contract required and what the mountain would demand, and because she had learned long ago that the difference between a bad idea and a survivable one was usually the people you dragged into it with you.

The names had come to her the way names always came in towns like this—overheard in a boarding house kitchen, passed along by a ferryman who wanted his fare in gossip instead of coin. Fen and Odo Quill, track-walkers, brothers, last of the walking crews on the shuttered Saltmere spur. Maren Cole, who had once been the finest surveyor on the coast and was now the finest surveyor nobody would hire. And Bram Halloway, of whom the ferryman had said only: *if he'll go, you'll get there. If he won't, don't.*

Yara found the brothers first, because the brothers were impossible to miss. They were walking the dead spur north of town with surveyor's chains over their shoulders, though there was no line left to walk and no one to pay them for walking it. She watched them from the embankment for a while before she called down. The elder one—Odo, she'd learn, though at first she thought of him as the tall one, and then as the one with the ruined knuckles—was measuring something. The younger one, Fen, was writing it down, and arguing about it, and laughing about the argument, all at once.

"You're walking a line that doesn't exist," Yara said, when she'd climbed down.

Odo looked at her the way a man looks at weather. Fen looked at her the way a man looks at a door opening.

"We walk it anyway," Fen said. "Somebody's got to remember where it was."

"Eleven years," Odo said. He said it like a sentence, like a weight, like a fact he carried in the same shoulder as the chain. "Line closed eleven years ago. We still walk it every second week. Rails go to rust if nobody watches them. Ties go to rot. Culverts wash out and nobody knows till the whole embankment's gone." He shrugged, an enormous slow shrug that seemed to involve his entire skeleton. "Nobody's coming back to this line. We know that. We walk it anyway."

"Why?"

Odo didn't answer. Fen did, and he answered with the particular gentleness of a younger brother who has had a long time to learn when to speak for the old man. "Because if we stop walking it, then it's really gone. And he—" a nod at his brother, "—spent nine years keeping it alive. You don't just quit on a thing like that. You let it down slow."

Yara thought about the list in her pocket, and about all the lines she herself had let down fast, and said: "I'm surveying a route through the Karst. Before the snows. It pays, and it pays for both of you, and it will be the first line in eleven years that somebody's actually trying to build instead of bury."

Odo's face did something complicated. Fen's did something simple, which was to light up like a lamp.

"When do we start?" Fen said.

"Ask your brother."

Odo looked down the dead spur, at the rails running off into the scrub like a sentence that trailed away unfinished. Then he looked at Yara, and whatever he saw in her face—the same restlessness, the same refusal to quit on a thing—must have satisfied him, because he said, "We'll need to tell Mrs. Aldous at the boarding house we won't be back for supper," in a voice that meant yes.

* * *

Maren Cole was harder to find and easier to convince, which was not the order Yara had expected.

She found her in the back room of the Saltmere library, of all places, bent over a table of old county atlases with a magnifying glass and a pot of cold tea. She was a compact woman in her fifties with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who had spent thirty years leaning over tables and refused, on principle, to apologize for it.

"You're Maren Cole," Yara said.

"I'm nobody," Maren said, without looking up. "Ask anyone."

"I'm asking you to survey the Karst crossing. I need someone who can read ground. I'm told you can read ground better than anyone on the coast."

That got her to look up. Her eyes were sharp and tired and suspicious in about equal parts. "Who told you that?"

"The ferryman. The clerk. Three people in the market who pretended not to know you and then told me anyway, the moment they thought you couldn't hear."

Maren almost smiled. It was a small thing, the almost, but Yara had spent enough years alone to recognize the shape of a door cracking open. "And do you know why nobody will hire me?"

"I know there was an inquiry. Eight years ago. A bridge survey on the Vell Road crossing."

"The Marrow Bridge," Maren said, and her voice went flat and careful, the way voices go when they've said the words too many times. "The span failed. Four men hurt. My numbers were the ones on the file, and the inquiry found the numbers were wrong, and I was struck off, and that's the whole story as the county knows it."

"And as you know it?"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over something in the harbor. "As I know it," she said finally, "the numbers in my field book and the numbers on the file are not the same numbers. But field books get lost, and files get kept, and I was the one with the reputation and no friends." She closed the atlas with a small, deliberate click. "Why are you asking? You need a surveyor, not a cause."

"I need a surveyor who checks everything twice," Yara said. "Because I'm carrying a contract with a deadline that will ruin me if I miss it, and because I've learned that the people the county strikes off are sometimes the only ones who were reading the ground honestly in the first place."

Maren studied her the way she probably studied contour lines—looking for the place where the drawing and the truth diverged. Then she stood, and put on her coat, and said, "I'll want my own instruments. And I'll want it understood that if I say a grade is impossible, you don't argue. You've seen what happens when people argue."

"I have," Yara said, and did not say the rest, which was that she had been on the other side of that particular argument once herself, on a bridge, in the rain, and that she still dreamed about it. That could wait. Damage kept. It was the one thing she'd learned that always proved true.

* * *

Bram Halloway took three days to find and thirty seconds to refuse.

He lived in a beached boat hull above the tideline, two miles south of town, a place that smelled of pitch and woodsmoke and old fish. He was mending a net when Yara came up the shingle, and he did not stop mending it, and he did not look up, and when she said the words *Karst Range* and *guide* and *fair wages*, he said, "No," with the ease of a man declining a second helping of something he's never liked.

"I haven't told you the pay."

"Don't need to. I don't go into the Karst."

"You worked those lines. Everyone says so."

"Everyone says a lot of things." He pulled a knot tight. His hands were enormous, scarred, unhurried. "Find someone else."

Yara stood in the cold wind and considered. She had learned, in her years of drifting, that there were two kinds of no. There was the no that meant no, and there was the no that meant *ask me differently*, and the trick was telling them apart, and the trick to telling them apart was usually just to stay standing there longer than was comfortable.

She stayed standing there longer than was comfortable.

Bram mended his net. The wind came off the water. A curlew cried somewhere down the beach, that thin rising note like a question nobody wanted to answer.

Finally, without looking up, he said: "You're the one who took the papers out of the Tollhouse."

It was not a question, and it landed in Yara's chest like a dropped tool. She had told no one—not the clerk, not the harbormaster, not a soul—that she'd been out to the cave at the last big ebb, or what she'd carried out of it. She'd wrapped the plates in oilcloth and put them at the bottom of her kit, and she'd told herself the tightness in her shoulders was just the weight.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, which was a lie, and by the small pause in Bram's mending, he knew it was a lie, and knew that she knew he knew.

"Mm," he said.

"You know the cave," Yara said. "You went quiet when the ferryman said its name. Everyone goes quiet. It's a story to them—the Tollhouse, the admission, the toll. But you didn't laugh. So either you think the story's foolish, or you think it's true."

Bram set down the net. He looked at her for the first time, and his eyes were the gray of the sea in November, and very old. "I think," he said, "that some doors you open once and spend the rest of your life deciding whether you're glad." He picked the net back up. "No."

Yara walked back along the shingle, and the curlew cried again, and she did not look back, because looking back was a habit she'd trained out of herself years ago and she was not going to let an old man in a boat bring it back.

* * *

She provisioned for five and planned for four. She hired a mule. She sat with Fen and Odo and Maren in the boarding house kitchen over Mrs. Aldous's mutton stew—which was less a stew than a soup that had given up—and drew the route on the tablecloth in pencil, and Maren corrected two of her grades before she'd finished drawing them, and Odo said nothing for an hour and then said, "Culvert at mile nine is gone. Washed out three winters back. You'll want to route around or rebuild," and Yara realized he had been reading the tablecloth the whole time, the way he read the dead spur, out of a loyalty so deep it had become instinct.

It was on the second night, over the stew again—Mrs. Aldous had one recipe and defended it like a fortification—that Fen said, "Did you ask Halloway, then?"

"I asked."

"And?"

"He said no."

Fen and Odo exchanged a look. Maren didn't look up from her field book, but her pencil stopped moving, which from Maren was the same as an exclamation.

"He said no," Yara repeated, daring them to make something of it.

"Everyone says no to Bram," Fen said. "That's not the news. The news is he let you finish asking. Odo asked him two years ago about the high meadows and he said no before Odo got the second word out."

"He's got his reasons," Odo said, into his bowl.

"Everybody's got their reasons," Fen said. "That's what makes them strays."

Yara looked around the table—at the brothers with their eleven dead years, at Maren with her stolen numbers, at herself with her kit full of other people's buried secrets—and thought that the clerk had been right after all. Strays. A crew of animals nobody had come to claim.

"Well," she said, "we leave at first light. With or without our guide."

They left at first light, without him, and made four miles through the foothills before the weather came down out of the north like a lid closing, and they camped in the lee of a cut stone wall that some forgotten crew had built and some forgotten line had needed, and Yara lay in her bedroll listening to the wind and thinking about doors.

She was nearly asleep when she heard the mule snort, and boots on gravel, and a voice she knew said, out of the dark, "Your fire's smoky and your camp's in the wrong place and that mule will be gone by morning."

Yara sat up. Bram Halloway stood at the edge of the firelight with a bedroll under one arm and a coil of rope over his shoulder, looking at the four of them the way a man looks at a mess he's decided, against his better judgment, to help clean up.

"You said no," Yara said.

"I did." He dropped his bedroll by the fire. "Then I sat in that boat all night listening to the tide go out, and the tide goes out twice a day, and both times it sounded like it was asking me something." He looked at her, and then—this was the thing she'd remember, the thing that made her trust him before she understood why—he looked at her kit, at the oilcloth bundle's shape beneath the canvas, and said nothing about it at all.

"Which way did you route around the culvert?" he asked.

"West of the wash," Odo said. "Half a mile."

"East," Bram said. "West is a bog with ideas above its station. And in the morning, we talk about where you're really going and what you're really carrying, because I've been to that cave, Miss Dunmore, and I know what the Tollhouse keeps, and if you're hauling it across the Karst in November, then you'll want a guide who knows every tunnel, every shaft, and every door the company sealed and hoped everyone would forget." He settled down by the fire with the finality of a man closing a ledger. "Now let me sleep. I've been awake fifty years waiting to make this mistake."

Yara lay back down. Around the fire, Fen was grinning at the dark, Odo had already gone back to sleep with the ease of a man who trusted the night watch, and Maren's pencil was moving again, scratching in the firelight like a small determined insect.

Strays, she thought. The clerk had meant it as a warning. But she had been a stray her whole life, and she knew something the clerk didn't: strays were only animals nobody had come to claim. Nobody had come yet.

She pulled her coat tighter, listened to the wind worrying the cut stone wall, and slept better than she had in years—right up until dawn, when the first far-off rumble came down from the high country, and Bram opened one eye and said, "That'll be the mountain moving early this year. We should talk about what's in your kit."
