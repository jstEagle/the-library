## 4. The Fixer Arrives

Maren Cole had spent eleven years learning to recognize a certain kind of man, and Silas Vane walked into the Saltgrass Room at half past seven in the morning looking exactly like the kind she'd hoped never to see again.

He didn't enter so much as arrive, the way weather arrives—gradually, then all at once. One moment the tavern's morning crowd of fishermen and dockhands was grumbling over weak coffee, and the next there was a stillness around a man in a gray wool coat that cost more than the building's mortgage. He was forty, perhaps, with the kind of neat, forgettable face that Maren knew was anything but forgettable. It was the face of a man who had practiced being unremarkable the way other men practiced marksmanship. His eyes, though—those did the work his face declined to do. They moved around the room in slow, patient arcs, cataloging. Counting exits. Counting people. Deciding, with what appeared to be infinite calm, which of them mattered.

Maren kept her head down over her coffee and her toast and her surveyor's field notebook, which she was pretending to study and which actually contained nothing but a half-finished sketch of a gull she'd drawn the evening before out of boredom and a lingering habit of drawing things to scale.

She knew that coat. Not the coat itself, but its genus. Eleven years ago, when the Meridian Bridge report had come apart in her hands like wet paper—when the numbers she'd certified had turned out to be numbers someone else had written, and her name had been on the certification, and the certification had been the whole ballgame—there had been men in coats like that. Men who sat in the back of the inquiry hall and took no notes. Men who spoke to her former colleagues in corridors and afterward those colleagues remembered nothing they'd said and changed their testimony anyway. Men who never raised their voices, never threatened anything on the record, never existed at all as far as any paper could prove.

The inquiry had called the phenomenon "irregularities." Her own attorney, a kind and defeated man, had called it "the weather." You couldn't subpoena the weather. You could only stand in it and get wet.

Silas Vane ordered coffee. Maren watched him do it in the reflection of the window glass behind the counter, a trick she'd learned in the years after the scandal, when she'd developed the professional habit of never quite looking directly at anything that mattered. He thanked the girl who brought it. He tipped. He sat at the corner table with his back to the wall and his face to the room, and he began, unhurried, to drink his coffee like a man who had all the time in the world and wanted everyone to know it.

The trouble with men like Vane was that they never hurried, because hurrying was a confession. The trouble with Maren was that she recognized the whole performance—the patience, the courtesy, the terrible gentleness of it—and her hands wanted to shake, and she would not permit them to.

She ate her toast. She drank her coffee. She sketched a second gull.

* * *

He began asking questions within the hour, and he did it beautifully.

That was the thing Maren wanted to scream at the room about, and of course couldn't: how *beautiful* it was. He didn't go table to table like an investigator. He let the room come to him. By nine he had befriended the harbormaster's clerk with a shared complaint about the weather. By ten he had bought a round for a crew of oyster dredgers and listened to forty minutes of their grievances as though each one were a rare and precious thing. By eleven he had learned, apparently without ever asking a direct question, the entire recent history of the staging town's waterfront—and somewhere in that history, the Tollhouse.

Maren heard it in fragments, because fragments were how the town gave it up. The dredgers told him about the low tide, how it had been the lowest in nine years, how the old sea cave below the bluffs had stood open and dry as a cellar for nearly two hours, first time anyone could walk into it in living memory with their boots dry. The harbormaster's clerk told him about the legend—*the Tollhouse, it charges admission, that's the story, my gran swore her gran paid it*. And then a boy who worked the fish market, a sharp-eyed boy Maren had once caught trying to sell her a "genuine pirate coin" that was unmistakably a flattened button, told him the part that mattered.

"Some surveyor woman went out there," the boy said, and Maren's coffee went cold in her stomach. "Morning after the big low tide. Walked out with a box. Iron box, heavy as sin. Carried it up the bluff road like it was nothing."

The gray coat didn't move. The gray coat didn't so much as lean forward. "Did she now," Vane said, in a voice of mild, pleasant interest, the voice of a man discussing the price of oysters. "An engineer, I understand. Dunmore. Yara Dunmore."

"Well, I don't know about names—"

"She'd have come in on the coast packet. Tall, dark-haired, carries her tools like she was born to them. A theodolite case with a brass corner repaired—here." And he made a small gesture with his hand, indicating the corner, precise as a surgeon. "She'd have hired for the Karst crossing. Guides, track-walkers, that sort of thing. Anyone odd. Anyone who doesn't quite belong."

The boy, delighted by the attention and by the coin Vane now spun onto the counter, gave him everything. The packet's arrival. The boarding house where she'd stayed. The fact that she'd been seen at the Saltgrass Room two evenings running, eating pie, in company with the Halloway man—old Bram, the guide nobody hired anymore—and the track-walker brothers, Fen and Odo, who between them possessed one working coat. And the Cole woman. The disgraced one.

Maren felt the boy's words land on her like a hand on her shoulder.

She did not look up. She had gotten very good, these eleven years, at not looking up. She finished her coffee, which had gone bitter, and she left coins on the table, and she walked out of the Saltgrass Room at the pace of a woman with nothing on her mind but errands, and only once she was around the corner and out of every line of sight did she allow herself to stand against the cold brick of the chandlery wall and breathe.

*He'd have hired for the Karst crossing.* He'd known before he came. He hadn't come to find out whether Yara Dunmore had taken the papers. He'd come to find out *where she was going with them*, and he had narrowed the whole coast down to one road, one pass, and one crew, in under four hours, without asking a single question that would hold up in any court in the world.

That was the method. That was the weather. It never threatened; it only arrived, and by the time you noticed it, you were already soaked.

* * *

She found Yara at the chandler's, haggling over oilcloth.

This was, Maren had learned in the four days since they'd signed papers, a thing Yara Dunmore did with the same total absorption she gave to a grade calculation. The chandler wanted an absurd price. Yara was dismantling the price with the patience of water working on stone, and the chandler was losing, and knew he was losing, and was enjoying it anyway. Maren stood in the doorway and watched her for a moment—the competent hands, the level gray eyes, the utter absence of any sense that she was a woman carrying something that men in gray coats crossed counties to retrieve—and felt the dread in her chest condense into something heavier.

"Yara," she said. "A word."

"One moment. He's about to concede on the grommets." The chandler made a noise of wounded protest. "He is. Watch."

He did. Maren waited through it, and it was a genuine pleasure, and she hated that she noticed it was a pleasure, because it made what she had to say next harder.

They walked toward the bluffs, because Yara liked to check her sightlines and Maren had learned not to ask why, and when they were out of earshot of the waterfront Maren said, quietly, "There's a man at the Saltgrass Room. Gray coat. Asking after a surveyor woman who took an iron box from the Tollhouse."

Yara's stride didn't break. Maren had expected that. What she hadn't expected was the flicker in her eyes—not fear. Calculation. "Description?"

"Forty-ish. Neat. Forgettable on purpose. Back to the wall, face to the room. Befriends the whole town in a morning without asking a thing." Maren heard her own voice go dry and flat, the voice she'd used at the inquiry, and hated it. "He knows your theodolite case has a repaired brass corner, Yara. He knows about the pie. He knows about us."

Yara stopped at last, at the bluff's edge, where the wind came up off the water and made conversation easier and eavesdropping impossible. She looked out at the gray sea for a long moment. "You know him," she said. It wasn't a question. "Not him. His *kind*. You went white as the chalk down there, and you've been white since."

Maren wrapped her coat tighter against the wind. Eleven years she'd kept the story in a locked drawer, and now the drawer was rattling. "There was a bridge," she said. "The Meridian crossing. Eleven years ago. I certified the survey. The numbers were wrong—not my numbers. Numbers that had been substituted, quietly, somewhere between my field book and the final report. The span failed under load test. Nobody died, which was the only mercy in it, but my name was on the certification, and there was a company behind the project with very good lawyers and—" she gestured vaguely back toward the town, toward the Saltgrass Room, toward the weather— "and *men*. Men who sat in the inquiry and took no notes. Men my witnesses wouldn't look at afterward. I was struck off. The actual hand that altered the figures was never found, because it was never looked for, because looking for things is not what those men are paid for."

"And this one?"

"This one is worse, or better trained. He didn't come asking. He came *arriving*. He'll be pleasant. He'll be generous. He'll buy the whole town a drink and remember everyone's mother's name, and by the time anyone realizes what he's doing, he'll already have done it." Maren turned to look at Yara squarely. "What's in that box?"

Yara was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence. When she spoke, she looked at the sea, not at Maren. "Survey plates. Originals, or near enough. Company seal on the casework. I haven't read all of them yet." A pause. "Some of them don't match the filed record. That's all I'll say until I'm sure, because being sure is the job."

"It's not all," Maren said. "You know it's not all. A man doesn't cross a county in a coat like that over mismatched paperwork. Yara—listen to me. I have seen what happens to people who carry things like that and are caught. It doesn't happen loudly. It happens *quietly*. Your witnesses stop remembering. Your permits develop problems. Your bridge—" she stopped herself, but too late, and saw the flinch she'd expected. "I'm sorry. I read the file. Everyone in the profession read the file. It was required reading, in the worst way."

"My bridge," Yara said evenly, "is not your business."

"No," Maren agreed. "But this box is mine now. I signed on to run survey lines across the Karst Range, and instead I've signed on to escort whatever's in that box through country where a man like that will be waiting, and I would like to know what I'm escorting, because the last time I certified something I didn't fully understand, it cost me my name."

The wind gusted. Below them, the tide was going out, and the dark shape of the Tollhouse cave's mouth was beginning to show at the base of the bluffs, black and patient as a held breath.

"Three days," Yara said at last. "We climb in three days. Ask me again on the mountain, when you've seen what I've seen. That's the best I can do, and it's more than I've given anyone in years."

Maren studied her. The restless woman, the woman who drifted, the woman who ate pie with strays and haggled over grommets and slept with her boots by the door. Maren had taken the contract because it was work and because the Karst survey was, God help her, the first legitimate certification anyone had offered her in eleven years. She had not signed on for gray coats.

And yet.

"Then three days," she said. "But Yara—don't go back to the Saltgrass Room. Don't be pleasant to him. Don't let him buy you anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"He'll find you anyway. Men like that always find you. That's the whole of their profession." Maren looked back down at the cave mouth, emerging inch by inch from the retreating sea, and felt the old dread settle over her like the coat she couldn't afford. "The only question is whether you're found holding something he wants, or holding something he *fears*. And I'd very much like to know, before we're on the mountain with no road back, which one we're carrying."

Yara said nothing. But her hand went, once, briefly, to the strap of her pack—where the iron plates rode wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, close as a secret, heavy as a debt—and Maren, who had spent eleven years learning to read what people did with their hands, understood that the answer was already decided.

They were carrying both.

* * *

That evening, Maren did the one thing she'd sworn off for a decade: she went looking through old records.

The staging town kept a shipping office with a public ledger of arrivals, because everything on this coast was public if you knew where to stand, and Maren had built a whole ruined career on knowing where to stand. The *Corvid* had come in on the morning packet run, out of the south. One passenger listed: S. Vane. Occupation, as given: *Company representative, Karst Consolidated Mining & Concession.*

Karst Consolidated. The same name stamped into the lead casework of the plates Yara wouldn't yet discuss. The same name that had held half the mountain's water rights in trust since before Maren was born. She copied the entry in her neat surveyor's hand, then stood a while in the lamplight of the shipping office, feeling the shape of the thing assemble itself the way a traverse assembles—point by point, each fix confirming the last, until the line closes and there is no honest way to call it anything but what it is.

A company man, sent the week the permits came due. A company man, asking after an iron box with the company's own seal. Which meant the company knew what was in the box. Which meant the company had known it was lost. Which meant, somewhere in the eleven years since Meridian, and the fifty years before that, this had happened before—and every time, the weather had come, and soaked the witnesses, and dried, and left no trace.

She was still standing there when Fen found her. The younger track-walker brothers had a way of appearing exactly when a person least wanted company, which Maren suspected was a skill rather than an accident. He looked at her face, and at the ledger, and his usual grin faded into something more careful.

"Bram says the tide's wrong," Fen said. "Says the low water's coming back around tomorrow night, and the cave'll open again. And Odo says there's a gray-coat fella down the tavern buying rounds and asking after us by name, which Odo took as a compliment until Bram went quiet, and Bram going quiet is never good news." He shifted. "So. Which of us is going to tell Dunmore that the mountain's come down to the coast to meet her?"

Maren closed the ledger. Outside, the lamps along the bluff road were being lit one by one, and below them the sea was already beginning its long pull away from the Tollhouse door.

"Nobody tells her anything she doesn't already know," Maren said. "That's what makes her dangerous to them. And that's what's going to make her dangerous to *us*, if we're not careful." She tucked the copied entry into her coat, next to her heart, where she'd once kept a certification she no longer possessed. "Get the brothers ready. Check the gear twice. We climb a day early."

Fen's eyes went wide. "Dunmore said three—"

"Dunmore said three days because she doesn't know what I know." Maren looked out at the darkening water, at the black seam of the cave mouth, at the town where a man with a forgettable face was at this very moment buying his second round of the evening. "I do. And I'm telling you the weather's changing, Fen. We move tomorrow."

She only hoped, walking back through the cold streets with the ledger entry scratching against her ribs like a second pulse, that the mountain would be kinder than the men.
