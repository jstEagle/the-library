## 5. First Ascent

The Karst Range did not so much rise as assemble itself out of the morning fog, ridge by ridge, like a argument being made slowly by someone who enjoyed being disagreed with.

Yara Dunmore stood at the foot of the old haul road with her theodolite on her shoulder and her breath steaming, and she took an honest accounting of what she'd hired. Fen was already twenty yards up the trail, walking it the way he walked everything—like the ground owed him money. Odo was behind him, carrying twice his share of chain because he hadn't wanted to say no when Fen handed it over. Maren Cole had her notebook out before the sun cleared the ridge, sketching the grade of the roadbed from memory, which told Yara more about Maren than any confession would have. And Bram Halloway stood at the treeline with a canvas pack older than she was, looking up at the mountain the way other men looked at old letters.

"Right," Yara said, mostly to herself. "Let's go be wrong in public."

Surveying was, in her experience, ninety percent being wrong and ten percent writing down exactly how wrong you'd been, so that the next fool could be less wrong. She liked that about it. There was no way to bluff a grade. The mountain didn't care how confident you sounded.

The first mile was switchbacks through pine, the old company road still holding its shape under fifty years of needle-fall. The fog came with them, thickening as they climbed until the world shrank to thirty feet of trail and the sound of their own boots. It should have been miserable. Instead Yara found herself listening to the crew talk, and discovering that strays, given a few hours and a shared load, began to orbit each other like anything else that had been knocked loose.

"—all I'm saying," Fen was arguing, "is that a handcar is just a seesaw that got ambitions."

"A handcar is a seesaw that got *employed*," Odo said. "There's a difference."

"You've never been employed a day in your life."

"I'm employed right now. I'm carrying this chain for you, which makes me your employer, technically."

Yara let them argue. It kept Odo's breathing even on the steep parts, and it kept Fen from noticing how often he glanced back to check on his brother. She'd seen brothers like this on work camps all across the closed lines—two halves of one animal, pretending otherwise.

Maren fell into step beside her near the second switchback. "You're staking from the bottom up?"

"Bottom up, centerline first. Why?"

"Because if we're going to run levels through this soup, someone should tell you your instrument man is going to be useless above four thousand feet." Maren nodded back at herself, dry as chalk. "My eyes are fine. My reputation isn't. But altitude and I have an arrangement—I don't trust my readings past a certain point, and I check everything twice."

"That's not useless. That's just careful."

Maren gave her a look that suggested she hadn't heard the word *careful* used as a compliment in some years. "Huh," she said, and dropped back to walk with Bram.

By midmorning they broke out of the trees onto the first scree field, and the fog tore open long enough to show them what they'd taken on: a gray shoulder of mountain stitched with the bones of the old tramway—rotted trestle legs, a rusted ore chute hanging off the slope like a broken rib. Above it, the ridge ran up into cloud and didn't come back down.

"There's our line," Yara said, and felt the old pull in her chest, the one that had kept her moving for eleven years. A route nobody had surveyed properly since the closure. A blank space with her name on it.

She set the theodolite on the highest solid rock she could find and called Odo over.

"You. Chain man. Come learn something."

Odo ambled up, all elbows and apology. "I don't know much about—"

"Good. Less to unlearn." She leveled the instrument, then pointed down the slope to where Fen stood holding the range pole, a red-and-white striped stick that looked, in the scree, like a candy cane planted by a drunk giant. "See Fen? Tell me what he's doing."

"Standing there."

"He's standing at a *known point*. Everything we do today starts from him. Now—" she swung the telescope, found Fen's pole in the crosshairs, "—I read the angle to him. Then I read the angle to where we want the line to go. The difference between those two angles tells us the grade. You follow?"

"Sort of."

"Here's the part that matters." She stepped back and let Odo put his eye to the scope. "A railroad can't climb straight at a mountain. It has to cheat. Every percent of grade costs the engine something—a one percent grade means the locomotive drags itself uphill like a man wading through honey. Two percent, and it's begging. Three percent, and the company starts writing letters." She tapped the scope gently. "So we don't ask the mountain how high it is. We ask it how *gentle* it's willing to be, and we wind the track back and forth until the mountain agrees. That's all surveying a line is. Negotiating with stone."

Odo was quiet a moment, turning the scope, finding Fen, losing him, finding him again. "And the fog?"

"The fog is the mountain talking in its sleep. We wait, or we work fast between the words." She watched him settle the crosshairs on the pole, tongue between his teeth. "Read me the rod."

He squinted. "Four... two? Four-two-something. The little marks are confusing."

"The little marks are tenths. Read the big number, then count up the small ones. Go slow. Slow is fast out here—one bad reading poisons every reading after it, and you won't know which one killed you."

"Cheerful," Odo said, but he was smiling as he said it, and Yara realized she was too.

They worked the rest of the morning that way: Yara at the instrument calling angles, Odo on the rod learning to hold it plumb against the wind, Fen leapfrogging ahead to set points, Maren computing in her notebook with a pencil worn to a stub, checking Yara's figures and finding—she announced with visible disappointment—that they were correct. Twice Yara caught Odo glancing at her after he got a reading right, wanting confirmation, and twice she gave it without making a ceremony of it. That was the whole trick of teaching, she'd learned. You didn't praise. You just kept showing up at the moment the person needed to know whether they'd done it right, until eventually they stopped needing to check.

By noon they'd staked six hundred feet of centerline along the shelf below the old tramway, and the fog had settled in for what looked like a long stay, pressing close and silver, beading on everyone's eyebrows.

They ate cold beans and hard bread in a half-circle behind a windbreak of scree. It was Odo who asked the question.

"Bram. How'd you sleep last night? You weren't in camp when I got up."

"Walking," Bram said.

"Walking where?"

Bram chewed. He was a long time answering, and Yara had learned enough about him in three days to know the silence wasn't stubbornness—it was him deciding how much door to open.

"There's a tunnel comes out under the east shoulder," he said finally. "Old ventilation adit, from when they drove the first bore. Wanted to see if it still held air."

Fen snorted. "You went in a hole in the mountain. In the dark. For fun."

"For information."

"And?"

"And it holds air." Bram folded his bread paper into a neat square, then a smaller one. "It holds more than air. But that's tomorrow's problem."

Yara sat forward. "Which tunnel?"

"Number three. The one that isn't on your map."

"It's not on anyone's map. I checked the company archives in Copperfield—the tunnel plans for the upper bores were never filed. Or they were filed and then withdrawn. There's a gap in the index where they should be."

Something moved behind Bram's eyes—not surprise. Recognition. "That gap's been there a long time," he said.

"You've seen the index?"

"I helped dig the tunnels, girl. I knew where the maps went before you were born." He said it without drama, the way a man reports the weather. But the whole circle had gone quiet. Even Fen had stopped chewing.

"You worked the Karst bores?" Maren said. "The original drive? That was—"

"Fifty-one years ago. I was seventeen. Strong back, weak mind, and the company paid cash." Bram looked up at the fog where the ridge should have been. "Six of us from my village went up that spring. It was good money and we were young and the mountain seemed like a thing you could get the better of, if you were patient." He unfolded the bread paper and folded it again. "I'm the only one who came down."

Nobody spoke. The fog dripped off the rocks, patient as everything else up here.

"The map gap," Yara said carefully. "Do you remember why?"

"I remember a surveyor named Aldous Vane coming up the line in the third summer with a case of plates and a very good coat. I remember him leaving lighter than he came." Bram stood, brushing off his knees. "That's a story for lower altitude. Right now we've got a line to run, and the fog won't hold forever."

He picked up his pack and started up the scree, sure-footed as water running downhill, and Yara watched him go with the feeling of a lock turning somewhere far inside a large dark house.

* * *

The afternoon taught them the mountain's first lesson.

It came as fog does in the high country—not arriving but *condensing*, the world simply deciding to stop existing beyond arm's reach. Yara called a halt on the shelf above the old ore chute, and they stood in a whiteness so complete that Fen, twenty feet away, was only a voice and a rumor of red flannel.

"This is useless," Fen called. "We can't even see the next stake."

"We don't need to see it. We need to hear it." Yara raised her voice. "Odo! Take the rod to stake nine. Count your paces—forty-two from the cairn, bearing as marked. When you're there, shout."

"How'll you know I'm at the right spot if you can't see me?"

"Because I'm going to shoot you with the transit, and if you're standing where you're supposed to stand, the numbers will agree. If they don't, you'll move until they do. That's the whole religion of surveying, Odo. The instrument is God, the numbers are scripture, and you are a sinner with a stick."

From the white came Odo's delighted laugh, and then his boots crunching away, and then, forty-two paces later, his voice: "STAKE NINE!"

Yara bent to the scope. The fog ate the light, but the rod's stripes bled through faintly, ghost-red. She read the angle, checked it, checked it again because Maren's caution was contagious, and called the correction. Odo shuffled left. Shuffled more. Stopped.

"Better?"

"Two inches more."

"How can you possibly know it's two inches?"

"Because mathematics is a cruel mistress and I am her favorite."

The numbers agreed. And something in the crew changed after that—Yara could feel it even through the fog. They'd done a hard thing together in bad conditions and it had *worked*, and now there was a looseness in the way they called to each other across the white, a shorthand forming. Fen started marking stakes with little carved faces so Odo could find them by feel. Maren began calling out computed grades before Yara asked. Bram ranged ahead and came back with reports of footing, of old cut-stone culverts still sound, of a spring running clear at the four-thousand-foot level—"good water," he said, and looked at it a long moment before moving on, though Yara couldn't have said why.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a collapsed powder house, its walls thick enough to stop a blast and almost thick enough to stop the wind. Fen got a fire going in the old hearth with the smugness of a man who believed fire-starting was a competitive sport. Odo laid out the beans. Maren checked figures by lantern. Bram sat apart, cleaning a compass older than the company, and Yara went and sat beside him, because there was a question she'd been carrying all day like a stone in her boot.

"Number three tunnel," she said quietly. "How far does it go?"

"Under the pass. Better part of two miles, if the collapse at the halfway adit hasn't gotten worse." He didn't look up from the compass. "Why?"

"Because if the weather turns early—and it's going to; the birds were flying low today and the barometer's been sliding since noon—the high pass will close. And if it closes while we're on the wrong side of it..." She let it hang.

Bram wound the compass into its cloth. "Then you'd want a way through the mountain that isn't on any map," he said. "Lucky for you, I spent three summers learning every foot of one."

"You could have led with that."

"I led with it the day you hired me. You asked if I knew the range. I said yes." Almost—almost—a smile. "You didn't ask how."

Yara laughed despite herself, and the sound of it surprised her. She couldn't remember the last time she'd laughed on a job. Couldn't remember the last job that had felt like anything but a countdown to departure.

Across the fire, Odo was trying to teach Fen a card game and cheating badly at it, and Maren was pretending not to notice while absolutely keeping score, and the wind came down off the ridge carrying the smell of snow that was still days away but thinking about it.

Yara banked the fire and lay back on her bedroll and listened to the mountain breathe around them, and thought: *eleven years of moving, and it turns out the trick was never motion at all.*

She was nearly asleep when Bram spoke from the darkness, so quietly she might have dreamed it.

"When we come down the far side," he said, "there's a valley you should see before you run your line through it. The maps will tell you it's empty." A pause. "It isn't."

Yara lay awake a long while after that, watching the fog press against the firelight, wondering what kind of emptiness an old man would bother to warn her about—and why, when he said the word *empty*, he'd sounded like a man describing a grave.
