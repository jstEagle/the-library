## 6. The Village That Isn't There

Bram Halloway had a rule about talking: never say in the morning what you could carry until nightfall. Most things, if you held them long enough, got lighter or else stopped mattering. Some things only got heavier, but those you learned to walk around, the way water walks around a stone.

He had been walking around Lowden Hollow for fifty-one years.

The valley announced itself before they entered it—not with any feature a map would bother to record, but with a silence of a particular kind. Bram felt it in his boots first, through the packed gravel of the old service road: ground that had been graded once, by men and mules, and then left alone so long it had forgotten why. The scree slopes above gave way to gentler shoulders. The wind dropped. Even the pikas went quiet.

Yara Dunmore noticed it too. He could tell by the way she slowed at the head of the column, her surveyor's eye reading the land the way other people read letters—quickly, hungrily, missing nothing. She'd been doing that all morning, ever since the fog lifted off the second bench and showed them the long dry throat of the valley running east toward the pass.

"There's no creek," she said, half to herself. "A valley this size, this aspect—you'd expect water."

"You'd expect more than water," Bram said.

It came out of him before he had decided to let it. Four days he had traveled with these people, four days of chewing his words down to gristle and swallowing them, and now one sentence slipped the fence like a yearling colt. Fen looked back over his shoulder. Odo nearly walked into him. Maren Cole, bringing up the rear with her instrument case strapped across her back like a rifle, stopped dead.

Bram kept walking. If he stopped, they'd look at him, and if they looked at him, he'd have to finish, and if he finished—

"More than water how?" Yara asked, falling into step beside him. She didn't crowd. She had the good habit of matching pace rather than demanding it.

"The road we're standing on," Bram said. "Company-built. Standard gauge bed, though they never laid rail here. Graded for carts first, rails later that never came." He toed the gravel. "And the grade's wrong for a haul road. Too gentle. Haul roads go where the ore is. This one goes where people are."

"People," Fen said. "There's nobody out here."

"No," Bram agreed. "There isn't."

* * *

They made camp early that afternoon in the lee of a cutbank, because Bram said his knees wanted it and nobody argued with a man's knees. In truth the light was going amber and long across the valley floor, and he wanted them to see it while it lasted—the way the low sun picked out lines in the earth that noon hid. Old field boundaries. The ghost-rectangles of garden plots, still faintly greener than the surrounding scrub because sixty years of composted kitchen scraps don't surrender easily.

Odo saw it first. He was the youngest, and the young still believed their eyes when the eyes reported something impossible.

"Bram," he said slowly, "why does the grass grow in squares?"

Bram sat down on his pack with the care of a man lowering himself onto thin ice. He looked at the squares. He had expected them to be gone by now, plowed under or washed away, but the mountain keeps what men abandon, holds it like a debt.

"That's a kitchen garden there," he said, pointing. "Beans on the north edge, peas where they'd get morning sun. Over past it, a root cellar dug into the bank—see that dip? Roof caved in '09 or so, but the hole remembers being a hole." He moved his hand. "Tannery stood there. Smithy there. And there—" his finger settled on a flat place where nothing grew at all, not even weed, "—that was the meeting house."

Nobody spoke. The fire crackled and spat. Somewhere up-slope a rock came loose and clicked down through the scree, the mountain clearing its throat.

Yara crouched by the fire, turning her tin cup slowly in her hands. "The maps show nothing here," she said. Not accusing. Asking.

"I know what the maps show."

"And what do they show, Bram?"

He was quiet long enough that Maren started to rise, thinking to spare him, and he raised one hand—not sharp, just final—and she sat back down.

"They show empty range," he said. "Mineral claims, section lines, a proposed spur alignment marked in pencil. No settlement. No well. No spring house. No names." He looked up at the darkening shoulder of the mountain where the company's workings were, invisible from here but present in his mind the way a scar is present under a shirt. "Because there weren't any. Officially. Lowden Hollow was never on a map the company printed after 1898, and every map since copied the ones before, the way lies do."

Fen had stopped eating. Odo's mouth was open. Maren had gone very still in the way of a woman whose profession had just been insulted at one remove—maps were her scripture, and someone had written heresy in them.

"There was a village," Bram said. "Forty-one households at its height. A good spring—it came up cold out of the limestone about a mile north of here, sweet water, never failed, not in the driest August. The Hollow ran on it. Gardens, stock, a flume to turn the wheel at the grist mill. And when the Consolidated Karst Mining Company came into the upper valley, that spring was worth more than everything the village stood on. Water is the one thing a hard-rock mine can't dig for itself. They needed it for the stamps, the boilers, the camps. There wasn't another source within eight miles that would carry the load."

"So they bought it," Yara said.

"They tried. The Hollow wouldn't sell. Not stubbornness—sense. Without the spring there was no Hollow; selling it would've meant selling themselves out of house and home for a price set by the buyer." He fed a stick to the fire. "So the company stopped buying and started arranging. There was a survey done in the winter of '98—a proper one, county-commissioned, establishing the spring as appurtenant to the homesteads, which under the old water law made it theirs, prior right, senior claim. That paper was the village's whole armor. One document."

"And?" Odo asked, though his voice said he already knew. Young people always know; they just need an adult to say the ugly thing out loud so they're allowed to believe it.

"And it never reached the recorder's office," Bram said. "The surveyor who carried it—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "The plates and the field notes went into a strongbox for transport, and the strongbox went onto a coastal packet out of Tollhouse Landing, and somewhere between the landing and the county seat it stopped existing. Company men swore it had never been loaded. The surveyor swore otherwise, but he was a young man with no standing and no money, and the company had both. Two years later he was dead—fever, they said—and the Hollow's claim died with him. Without the recorded survey, the water law ran the other way. The company filed first, filed loudest, filed with lawyers. By 1903 the spring was theirs by permit. By 1905 the Hollow was gone. Not burned, not massacred—nothing so honest. Just bought out parcel by parcel at panic prices once the water was cut, families drifting off through three bad winters, houses sold for lumber and hauled away. Forty-one households. I could name every one of them."

"How?" Maren asked softly. It wasn't a challenge. It was the question of a woman doing arithmetic she dreaded.

Bram looked into the fire. Fifty-one years of walking around the stone, and here he was, standing square in front of it with witnesses.

"Because I carried the chain for that survey," he said. "I was nineteen. Held the far end of the tape in the snow while Mr. Aldous Reeve shot his angles and wrote his numbers, and I watched him seal those plates in wax paper and pack that box with his own hands, and I helped load it on the packet at the Landing, and I stood on the dock and waved goodbye to the truth of forty-one families like it was a cousin taking a pleasant sea voyage." His voice did something he didn't authorize—cracked, just slightly—and he pressed on before anyone could be kind to him. "I was nineteen and I thought papers always arrived where they were sent. That's what being nineteen is."

The fire popped. Down the valley, the last light slid off the ghost-gardens, and the squares went dark.

* * *

Later, when Fen and Odo had turned in and Maren sat sharpening her pencils with the fierce concentration of a woman avoiding conversation, Yara came and sat beside Bram on the cutbank where he'd taken his pipe. He didn't smoke it anymore—doctor's orders, ignored—but holding it gave his hands employment during difficult weather.

"You knew," she said. "In the staging town. When I told you where I'd found the plates. You went quiet."

"I did."

"The Tollhouse. The strongbox with the company seal. That was Reeve's box."

"It was." He turned the unlit pipe over. "I'd have known it anywhere. Reeve had a way of packing a box—corner folds, wax seals stamped with his thumb ring. I helped him pack it. I've seen that box in my sleep more nights than I'd admit to a priest."

"Why didn't you say?"

"What would I have said?" He gestured with the pipe at the dark valley, the buried village, the whole patient landscape of erasure. "'Excuse me, miss, I recognize that box, it contains the corpse of a town I helped kill'? A man doesn't lead with that. A man waits to see whether the woman carrying the box is going to sell it to a collector or use it for kindling or—" he glanced at her sideways, "—or do the thing nobody's done in fifty years with it."

"Which is?"

"Carry it where it was always going." He tapped the pipe stem against his boot heel, twice, like a man knocking on a door he'd been afraid to knock on for half a century. "To the recorder. To the record. A survey doesn't exist until it's filed—that's the whole religion of my trade, miss, your trade too. Ink on the county's page or it never happened. Reeve knew it. That's why he fought so hard to get it there. The company understood it too. That's why they made sure it never arrived. Everything since—the Hollow dying, the water diverted, forty-one families scattered to the winds—all of it rests on one unfiled document sitting in a hole in a cliff where the tide goes in and out like it's breathing on it."

Yara was quiet for a while. Below them, embers shifted in the fire ring.

"When I found it," she said finally, "I thought it was treasure. Honestly—I stood there in that cave with the water coming back in and I thought, this is the kind of thing people wreck their lives looking for." She laughed, without much humor in it. "Then I opened it and it was just... work. Field notes. Angles. Numbers. Somebody's careful, honest, freezing-cold winter's work. I couldn't even read most of it. But I could tell it was true. You can always tell true work. It has a weight false work doesn't."

"Aye," Bram said. "That's Reeve. Honest to the bone. It's why they had to bury the box instead of the man—they couldn't buy him, so they buried his work and waited for him to die of being nobody." He paused. "That last part was me. Being nobody. I let it happen. Went on working company lines for eleven more years, took their wages, walked their grades, and every time I crossed this valley I looked straight ahead and thought about something else very hard."

"You were nineteen."

"I was nineteen for about ten years," Bram said. "Then one morning I woke up fifty and still calling myself nineteen, and I quit the company and took to guiding, and I told myself I'd made my peace." He shook his head slowly. "You don't make peace with a thing like this. You just stop visiting it. There's a difference, and the difference is that it's still there whenever you come back."

Maren had drifted over without either of them noticing—she moved quietly for a big woman—and stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms folded, listening the way surveyors listen, entirely.

"The plates Yara found," she said. "Are they complete? All of Reeve's original run?"

"Every plate, if it's the box I think it is. He was thorough. Notes, plats, the affidavit sworn before the justice at the Landing—signed, sealed, witnessed." Bram's jaw tightened. "Witnessed by me. Nineteen years old, signing my name as witness to the water rights of Lowden Hollow, and then spending fifty years pretending the signature was somebody else's problem."

"An affidavit signed and sealed," Maren murmured, and Bram heard the surveyor waking up behind her eyes, the machinery starting to turn. "If the plates are legible and the affidavit's intact, it's not evidence of a crime, exactly. It's the crime's opposite. It's the thing that should have happened, preserved. Filed late is still filed. Senior rights don't expire because a clerk's desk stayed empty."

"That's the theory," Bram said.

"That's the law," Maren said. "Theory is what lawyers have. Law is what's written down."

From the bedrolls, Odo's voice floated up, thick with sleep and younger-brother bluntness: "So we're carrying a whole town in that case? That's what's been clanking around in there keeping me up?"

Fen's elbow must have found him, because there was a grunt and then giggling, and then quiet.

But it was a fair question, Bram thought, and he answered it to the fire, to the valley, to the forty-one households he could still name.

"Aye, lad. That's what it is."

* * *

He slept badly, as he'd known he would, and rose before the others in the blue hour, and walked out alone onto the valley floor while the frost still held. His knees complained. He ignored them. Some appointments you keep regardless of the body's opinion.

He walked to the flat place where nothing grew, where the meeting house had stood, and he took off his hat.

"We're going through," he said aloud, to the frost, to the foundations, to whoever might be listening from wherever the Hollow's people had ended up—coast towns, inland valleys, graves. "Me and a railroad engineer and a disgraced surveyor and two brothers who argue about everything except each other. We're carrying Mr. Reeve's box up over the pass and down to the county seat, and we mean to put it on the recorder's desk before the snow closes the high country." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Fifty-one years late. But papers don't spoil. That's the one mercy in the whole business."

The frost began to give as the sun cleared the ridge, and the ghost-gardens came back one by one, green rectangles surfacing out of the grey like drowned things rising. Bram stood among them with his hat in his hands until his eyes stung, and then he put the hat back on, and turned uphill toward camp, because there was work to do and, for the first time in fifty-one years, he wanted to do it.

He was halfway back when he saw Yara standing at the edge of camp with the document case open in her hands, and Maren beside her, and the look on both their faces was the look of people reading something that changes the route.

"What is it?" Bram called.

Yara looked up. In the early light her face was pale and set.

"The affidavit's signed," she said. "But there's a second signature on the transport manifest. Whoever received the box at the Landing." She turned the brittle page toward him. "Bram—do you know this name?"

He took the page. He read the name. And the bottom dropped out of fifty-one years of careful walking-around, because the handwriting was elegant, confident, and familiar—the same hand he had seen just last week, in fresh ink, on a letter of introduction carried by a smooth-voiced man in a good coat who called himself Silas Vane, agent of the Consolidated Karst Mining Company.

The box had been received at the Landing by the man who made it disappear. And that man's family had been burying the truth ever since.
