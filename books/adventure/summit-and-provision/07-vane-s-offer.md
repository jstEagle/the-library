## 7. Vane's Offer

The waystation at Cold Saddle was the kind of place that existed only because the railroad had once needed it to, and now persisted out of sheer stubbornness—a plank platform, a stove that smoked more than it heated, and a stationmaster named Pell who had been "just about to retire" for eleven years. He kept a jar of pickled eggs on the counter and a sign that read NO CREDIT, NO COMPLAINTS, NO EXCEPTIONS, and he charged them double for everything because, he explained, they looked like trouble, and trouble cost extra to serve.

Yara paid it. Trouble, lately, had been their whole trade.

They'd been four days on the mountain's lower shoulder, and the crew had developed the particular rhythm of people who were learning to trust each other in increments—Fen and Odo bickering over who got the flat spot by the fire, Maren re-inking her field notes with the ferocity of a woman correcting the historical record, Bram saying nothing at all in a way that somehow organized the whole camp. Yara had caught herself laughing twice on the climb. Twice. She'd made a mental note to be more careful.

The waystation was a supply point, a night out of the weather, a chance to buy oats and lamp oil and, if Pell was feeling generous, actual bread. That was the plan. That was all it was supposed to be.

The man on the platform was not part of the plan.

He stood with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting for a train that had been cancelled years ago, and he was dressed too well for Cold Saddle by about three social classes—a grey wool coat, a hat with a crease in it sharp enough to cut paper, boots that had never once been mistaken for work boots. He was perhaps fifty, lean, with a face that had been handsome once and had since been repurposed into something more efficient. His eyes found Yara the moment she stepped off the trail, the way a magnet finds north.

"Miss Dunmore," he said, and his voice was warm the way a stove in a bank lobby is warm. "I hoped we'd meet under better weather. Silas Vane. I'm with the Consolidated Karst Mining and Railway Company."

Behind her, she heard Bram stop walking. She didn't hear him do anything else—no word, no shift of gear—but she felt the old man's silence change shape, the way you feel a door close in another room.

"You're a long way from anywhere that wants you, Mr. Vane," Yara said.

"I'm exactly where I intend to be. That's rather the definition of my work." He gestured toward the station door with a small courtesy, as if the whole mountain were his parlor. "Might we sit? I've bought the stove an hour of good behavior from Mr. Pell, and there's coffee that is, I'm told, only mostly bark."

Fen leaned close to Yara's ear. "We could keep walking," he murmured.

"We could," Yara said, and didn't.

That was the thing about temptation she'd learned the hard way: it never looked like a trapdoor. It looked like a chair.

* * *

Inside, the stove did its best. Vane poured the coffee himself, which she noted was a technique—men who poured your coffee wanted you to feel in their debt before they'd said a word. She took the cup anyway. She wasn't a fool, but she was cold, and pride made a poor blanket.

Her crew arranged themselves around the room with a tactician's casualness. Maren took the chair nearest the door. Fen and Odo leaned against the wall by the window, arms crossed in identical poses, which they would deny were identical. Bram stood outside on the platform. Yara saw his silhouette through the frost-furred glass, motionless, facing the mountain.

"So," she said. "The company knows I have the plates."

"The company knows a great many things." Vane sat with his ankles crossed, unhurried. "What the company knows less clearly is what you intend to do with them. That ambiguity is expensive, Miss Dunmore. For everyone. I've been authorized to remove it."

"Authorized how much?"

He smiled, appreciating the directness. From his coat he drew a document, folded in thirds, and laid it on the table between them with the reverence of a man laying down a winning card. "A buyout. Not of the plates—that would be crude, and you'd rightly refuse. Of your *contract*. The survey you were hired to complete. The company will pay you the full fee, plus a completion bonus, plus a hardship allowance, in exchange for your professional certification that the route is unviable. Rockfall. Unstable grades. The Karst Range being, as everyone knows, a graveyard of rail ambitions. You sign, you take your crew home warm, and everyone stops pretending there's a reason to cross the mountain before the snows."

Yara looked at the number.

She made herself look at it properly, because refusing a thing you hadn't actually examined wasn't integrity, it was theater. The number was more money than she'd earned in the last six years combined. It was enough to buy out of every debt she carried, enough to put a winter between herself and the next contract, enough to—her mind supplied the image unbidden—enough to stop. Just stop. A house with a door she chose. A stove that didn't smoke. No more drifting from closed line to closed line like a ghost haunting her own profession.

The number sat there being reasonable. That was the horror of it. It wasn't an insult. It was a fair price for a fair day's work, if you pretended the work was a survey and not a resurrection.

"You've thought about it already," Vane observed. "I can tell. You have the look of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

"I have the look of a woman drinking bad coffee," Yara said. "What's the veiled threat? There's always a veiled threat. You've got the posture for it."

Vane's smile thinned by a degree—not offended, recalibrating. He turned his cup a quarter-rotation on the saucer. "No threat. A concern. You've had a difficult career, Miss Dunmore. The Meridian Gorge crossing. Fourteen years ago."

The stove ticked. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Yara said, and her voice came out level, which cost her more than the number on the table had.

"Of course not." He said it gently, which was worse. "The inquiry cleared the engineering. Fault was assigned to a load miscalculation in the freight manifest, a clerical matter. But clerical matters cling, don't they? People remember the bridge. They remember the name of the engineer who signed the span. I only raise it because—" he spread his hands, all reasonableness—"a woman in your position, carrying contested documents across contested ground, might find her professional history becoming a subject of renewed public interest. Inquiries reopened. Old testimony re-read. It would be a shame if the conversation about these survey plates became a conversation about *you*."

There it was. Not a threat—a weather report. *Storms expected. Bring your shame.*

Yara set her cup down. Her hand was steady. She'd had fourteen years of practice making her hand steady.

"The bridge failed because the abutment plans were altered," she said. "I signed the span that was built, not the span that was specified. I said so at the inquiry."

"And the inquiry believed the paperwork." Vane rose, buttoned his coat, and left the document on the table, face up, the number gleaming in the stove light like something that had washed in on a tide. "Paperwork has a way of being believed, Miss Dunmore. You of all people are hoping so, I think. Three days. I'll find you on the mountain, or you can find me at the Cold Saddle telegraph office—Mr. Pell holds messages for a modest fee. If I've heard nothing by then, I'll assume you've chosen the hard way, and I'll act accordingly. I do hope you'll choose the soft one. Hard ways are so expensive, and not only in money."

At the door he paused and glanced back at the window, at Bram's silhouette on the platform, and something moved through his face—quick, unreadable, gone. "Give my regards to Mr. Halloway," he said. "He'll remember me. We've met before, though he may wish he hadn't."

Then he was out into the grey afternoon, and his footsteps on the planks were quiet, and then they were nothing at all.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while.

Fen was the one who finally broke it, because Fen was constitutionally incapable of leaving silence standing. "So that's the fixer. I expected someone uglier."

"He's handsome the way a landslide is scenic," Maren said. She hadn't touched her coffee. She was staring at the document on the table as if it were a specimen she intended to dissect. "Yara. That number. That's not a buyout number. That's a *precedent* number. They priced it so you'd know they take you seriously."

"They priced it so I'd know they're scared," Yara said.

"Both things can be true," Maren said. "That's how the best offers work."

Odo had drifted over and was reading the document upside down, lips moving. "Hardship allowance," he said. "We've had hardship. Do we get the allowance for the hardship we already had, or only future hardship?"

"Odo," Fen said.

"I'm just asking. It's a generous number. If we're being robbed, I'd like the robbery itemized."

Yara almost laughed, and the almost was a lifeline, because the other thing in her chest—the thing with the number on it—was not laughing at all. She picked up the document. It was good paper. Company paper, heavy stock, the seal pressed deep. The same seal, she thought, as the one stamped on the rusted plates in her pack. The company that had buried a village's water under a false line was offering her a winter's warmth to help keep it buried, and the terrible, ordinary truth was that she was cold, and tired, and the number was fair, and no one on this mountain would ever know if she signed.

No one except her. And the five people in this room. And a village that wasn't on any map anymore.

She put the document in her coat, inside pocket, over the heart, where it sat like a coal.

"Three days," she said. "We've got a ridge line to run and weather coming. We'll do the survey work. I'll decide at the deadline, and not before, and nobody's to make it easy for me either way. Understood?"

"Understood," Maren said, and there was something in her voice Yara couldn't read—a tightness, a thought half-formed, a woman looking at a pattern she'd seen before and hadn't finished counting.

* * *

Outside, the light was going the color of old tin. Bram was still on the platform, exactly where he'd been, hands in his coat, facing the mountain as if it owed him an answer.

Yara came and stood beside him. For a while neither of them said anything. The wind came down the valley, and somewhere below, water moved under ice, patient as a debt.

"You knew him," she said. "Vane."

"Knew his father." Bram's voice was gravel poured slow. "Silas is the son. The signature on the false survey—the one that buried Lowden Hollow's water—that was Aloysius Vane's hand. I watched him write it. I was young and I said nothing, and the valley went dry, and I've been walking mountains ever since like a man looking for a door he should have held open." He turned his head, and his eyes in the failing light were the color of the rock they'd been climbing all week. "Now his boy's come to bury it again. That's not a job to him, girl. That's an inheritance."

Yara looked at the mountain. Somewhere up there, under the first skin of snow, a dead village waited with its water rights folded in her pack like a letter forty years late. Somewhere behind her, a fair price sat over her heart, burning a hole she could feel with every breath.

Three days. She'd spent fourteen years moving fast enough that her past couldn't catch up, and now it had sent a man in a good coat to meet her halfway, holding out money and her own worst memory like two ends of the same rope.

"Get the others," she said quietly. "We make the ridge by dark. I want miles between us and that telegraph office before I have to be a better person than I feel."

Bram pushed off the rail and shouldered his pack. "For what it's worth," he said, "you already turned down the coffee he poured. Most folk don't even get that far."

Behind them, through the station window, Maren had taken the buyout document from the table and was holding it up to the last of the light—not reading the number, Yara noticed. Reading the paper. The stock, the watermark, the seal. Her eyes had the look of a surveyor who has just spotted, way off on the far contour of a map, a line that shouldn't be there.

She said nothing. But she folded the document very carefully, and she kept it.
