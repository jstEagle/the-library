## 8. The Bridge She Didn't Burn

Maren Cole had spent eleven years learning to sleep in the presence of her own disgrace, the way other people learned to sleep next to a ticking clock or a snoring husband. You got used to the sound. You even, in strange moments, found it companionable.

But she could not sleep tonight.

The waystation at Kettle Gap was a low stone building with a stove that smoked unless you fed it exactly the right amount of coal, and the crew had claimed their corners of it with the efficiency of people who had all, at one time or another, been thrown out of better places. Fen and Odo were stacked in the bunkroom like cordwood, Fen on top, arguing drowsily about whether a marmot could beat a stoat in a fair fight. Bram sat by the window with his pipe unlit, which Maren had learned meant he was thinking, and which she had also learned not to interrupt. And Yara—

Yara was at the table with Vane's papers spread out in front of her, and she had been sitting there for three hours without moving, in the particular stillness of a woman reading her own obituary.

Maren had seen that stillness before. In a mirror, mostly.

She made tea she didn't want, poured two cups, and carried them over.

"You're staring at that bridge report like it owes you money," she said.

"It does," Yara said. "In a way. It took six years of my life and gave back a name nobody says without flinching." She didn't look up. "You should sleep, Maren."

"So should you. Neither of us is going to." Maren set the cup down on top of the report, deliberately, and pulled out the opposite chair. "So we may as well be useless together. I find it goes faster that way."

Yara almost smiled. It was a small thing, the almost, but Maren had spent eleven years cataloguing small things, because small things were what got overlooked in a survey, in a report, in a life. Small things were where the bodies were buried. Sometimes literally, if the mining company had anything to say about it.

"Read it again," Maren said. "Out loud. The load calculations."

Yara looked at her. "Why out loud?"

"Because your eyes have read it four times and found nothing, which means your eyes are looking for what you already believe—that you failed. Eyes are loyal like that. They find what the heart orders them to find." Maren wrapped her hands around her own cup. "Ears are more cynical. Read it out loud and let's see if your ears catch what your eyes keep forgiving."

Yara exhaled through her nose—the sound of a woman deciding whether to be offended—and read.

"'Load analysis, Harrow's Bend trestle, spring inspection. Estimated live load at time of failure: forty tons. Calculated safe capacity of structure as maintained: sixty-two tons. Conclusion: failure consistent with overloading beyond rated capacity. Contributing factor: engineer's certification of repairs—'"

"Stop." Maren held up a hand. "Say that last sentence again. Slower."

"'Contributing factor: engineer's certification of repairs undertaken the previous autumn.'"

"There." Maren set her cup down with a click. "Do you hear it? That's not an engineering sentence. That's a lawyer's sentence wearing an engineer's coat. An engineer writes: *the repairs did not restore rated capacity.* A lawyer writes: *contributing factor, engineer's certification.* One sentence assigns a cause. The other assigns a person. Somebody wrote that report to end at your name, and then worked backward to get there."

The stove ticked. Out in the dark, wind worried the eaves. Yara sat very still, and Maren watched six years rearrange themselves behind her eyes.

"The repairs were sound," Yara said quietly. It wasn't a question anymore. It was a woman checking the floor before she let herself fall. "I certified them because they were sound. I checked every joint. I *crawled* every span. I was twenty-six years old and I checked that bridge like it was made of glass, because I knew what was riding over it every day."

"Then somebody needed you to be the cause," Maren said, "and needed the report to prove it. Which means—" and here her own voice went careful, because she had learned to be careful around this particular door, "—which means the report is worth reading the way I read reports. Not for what it says. For what it was built to hide."

Yara looked up. "You've done this before."

"I did it once," Maren said. "Badly. It ended my career, so I've had plenty of time to get good at it in theory." She turned her cup a slow circle on the table. "Show me the rest of the file. Everything Vane's people gave you, everything you kept. The inspection logs, the maintenance requisitions, the correspondence. The whole corpse. I want to do a post-mortem."

* * *

They worked until the stove went cold and Fen came out once, half-asleep, to ask if anyone wanted the last of the sausage, was told no by two women who did not look up, and retreated with the wounded dignity of the young.

It was the requisitions that cracked it open. Maren found it the way she'd once found a falsified benchmark in the Aldermoor survey—by noticing that a number was *convenient*. Numbers in honest documents are awkward. They have odd decimals, they don't round, they sit slightly wrong against each other like teeth. Numbers in doctored documents are tidy. Somebody has made them behave.

"Here." She spread three pages side by side on the table, weighted with teacups. "Autumn before the collapse. The company requisitioned timber for the Harrow's Bend repairs. Look at the quantities."

Yara leaned in. "Fourteen hundred board feet. That's... about right for the stringers we replaced."

"Now look at the delivery receipt from the mill."

"Twenty-two hundred board feet."

"Eight hundred feet of timber, paid for, delivered, and never nailed into anything." Maren tapped the page. "And look at the destination code on the mill's copy. It's not Harrow's Bend. It's routed inland. Karst drainage, district seven."

Yara's brow creased. "There's nothing in district seven. There's never been anything in district seven. I surveyed half that drainage this month—it's dry as a sermon."

"Dry," Maren said, and let the word sit there between them like a stone on a table. "Yes. I've been thinking about that word myself."

She reached into her satchel and brought out the thing she had been turning over in her mind for two days, ever since Bram had said *Lowden Hollow* in that flat voice that meant the word was load-bearing. She had copied it by lantern light, in her careful old surveyor's hand, from one of the plates in Yara's strongbox: the header block of the buried water survey.

"Read me the surveyor's certification," she said.

Yara read it. Maren watched her face as she got to the signature line, watched the crease deepen.

"*R. Vane,*" Yara said. "Principal surveyor of record."

"R. Vane," Maren agreed. "And now look at the requisition authorization. The timber that never reached your bridge."

Yara looked. Maren saw her go very still, the stillness again, but different this time—less like grief, more like a trap coming shut.

"Same hand," Yara said. "Same signature. R. Vane authorized the phantom timber, and R. Vane certified the survey that erased Lowden Hollow's water." She looked up, and there was something new in her face, something Maren recognized with a jolt because she had once seen it in her own mirror and then spent a decade mourning it: hope, arriving late and angry. "And the bridge report?"

"Unsigned in the body, but the certification page—" Maren slid it across. "Read the reviewing officer."

Yara read it. Her breath came out slow. "*R. Vane.* He reviewed the report that blamed me. He signed off on the findings. The man who buried a village's water also buried—" She stopped. Started again. "He built the report that ended me. Out of the same office. The same pen. The same *handwriting*—"

"Look at the sevens," Maren said, gently, because this was the part she loved, the part that had always felt like catching a pickpocket in the act. "In his signature and in the requisition figures. He crosses them with a little backward slash, like a European. Nobody in this district writes a seven that way. I've surveyed with forty men in this country and exactly one wrote sevens like that."

"Vane," Yara said. "The fixer. Silas Vane."

"His father, I'd guess, from the dates. R. Vane. But the son learned the trade at the same desk, I'd wager, and I'd wager further he writes his sevens the same way." Maren sat back. "Here's what I think happened, and you tell me where it's wrong. The autumn before your bridge failed, eight hundred board feet of company timber was diverted inland—to district seven, to the Karst drainage—to do something down in that dry valley. Something that needed timber. A flume, maybe. A diversion works. Something to finish the job the buried survey started, which was taking Lowden Hollow's water and giving it to the mine. The bridge repairs were shorted to pay for it. Your stringers were sound; the timber that should have gone into *other* spans never arrived. The bridge failed under a load it would have carried, if the company hadn't been robbing it to rob a village."

Yara's hands were flat on the table. Maren noticed they weren't shaking. She thought that was the bravest thing she'd seen in years.

"The report blamed me," Yara said, "because the report couldn't blame the missing timber. Because the missing timber leads to district seven, and district seven leads to Lowden Hollow, and Lowden Hollow leads to a survey that was supposed to be ash twenty years ago."

"And instead it's in a strongbox in your kit," Maren said, "waiting for a tide."

The stove ticked as it cooled. From the bunkroom, Fen's voice drifted out, muffled and indignant: "—a stoat, Odo, obviously a stoat, they're *built* for it—" and then silence.

"You know what the worst part is?" Yara said. She was laughing a little, a dry, disbelieving sound with no humor in it. "For six years I thought I was running from that bridge. Every job I took, every line I walked, I picked places so far from Harrow's Bend that no one could say my name and mean that day. I told myself I was a drifter because I liked the work. I told myself—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I built my whole life on top of a lie somebody else wrote, and I *maintained* it. I kept it in repair. I'm an engineer. I kept my own disgrace in good working order for six years."

Maren reached across the table and, after a moment's deliberation, put her hand over Yara's wrist. It had been a long time since she'd touched anyone on purpose.

"I did the same thing," she said. "Only I did it with drink and bitterness, which are heavier to carry than miles. You chose the lighter load. Don't be ashamed of the engineering." She waited until Yara lowered her hands. "But hear me, Yara Dunmore, because I've thought about this every day for eleven years and I'll only say it once. A doctored report is a structure. It has load paths. It has members that carry the weight of the lie, and members that are decorative. And every structure has one thing that, if you remove it, the whole thing comes down. Vane's father built his out of your name. He made *you* the load-bearing element. You've spent six years thinking that meant you were the failure. It doesn't. It means you're the keystone. Take yourself out of the wall—" she smiled, and it was not a kind smile, "—and the whole report collapses."

Yara looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked down at the papers, at the tidy criminal sevens, at the phantom timber routed to a valley with no village on any map.

"I nearly took his money," Yara said. "At the waystation. Three days, he said. I stood there doing the arithmetic—what the buyout would clear, where I'd go, how far the snows were—and I caught myself planning the route *away*. Same as always." She shook her head slowly. "I was going to run from this one too. From all of them. From Lowden Hollow, from the bridge, from the whole rotten ledger."

"And now?"

Yara gathered the pages and squared their edges with a crisp, practiced motion—the motion of a woman squaring a set of working drawings, a set she intended to build from.

"Now I'm done running from my past," she said. "I'm going to *use* it. Vane's father made me the keystone. Fine. I've spent six years learning exactly how much weight I can carry." She looked up, and Maren saw that the stillness had gone out of her entirely, replaced by something that moved. "Maren—how fast can you draft? Because when we come down off this range, I don't want to hand the recorder a strongbox and a story. I want to hand him a *survey*. Certified. Cross-referenced. With the bridge report as exhibit B and the timber requisitions as exhibit C and the sevens as exhibit D. I want it airtight. I want it so clean that even a bought judge can't look at it sideways."

Maren felt something in her chest that she hadn't felt since Aldermoor, since before, since a younger woman with clean hands had believed her work could mean something. It was an old muscle. It ached when she used it. She used it anyway.

"I can draft," she said, "fast enough to outrun the snow, if you keep the stove fed and the sausage coming." She began stacking the pages into order, her hands steady, her mind already laying out title blocks. "And Yara—when we file this, we file it against the permit clock. Which means Vane's three days don't matter anymore. Which means when he comes to collect his answer—"

"He'll find out we've stopped deciding," Yara said, "and started building."

From the window, where he had not moved all night and had heard, Maren was quite sure, every word, Bram Halloway took the unlit pipe from his mouth and spoke for the first time in hours.

"District seven," he said. "There's a tunnel comes out above the Hollow. Company bored it. I helped." He looked at them both, and his eyes in the lamplight were old and level. "You'll want to know the way in. Because Vane's men are two days behind us, and the snow is one."
