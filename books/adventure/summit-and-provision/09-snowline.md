## 9. Snowline

The mountain lied about the weather. Fen had known that since he was six years old and his father had told him so over a supper of beans and boiled nothing, but knowing a thing and believing it were two different animals, and Fen had spent the whole morning not believing the sky.

"It's too early," Odo said, for the fourth time.

"Tell the mountain," Fen said. "I'm sure it'll apologize."

They were three days above the tree line, camped on a shelf of granite that Maren's instruments said was the last flat ground before the pass proper, and the light had gone strange — that yellowish, bruised color that meant snow was thinking about it. Yara stood at the edge of the shelf with her collar up, watching the west, where the clouds had stopped being clouds and started being a wall.

"We move now," she said. "We make the saddle before it closes."

Bram looked up from tightening the lashings on the supply sled and said nothing, which from Bram was a full paragraph of disagreement. Fen had learned to read him the way you learn to read weather: by what he didn't say. What he wasn't saying right now was *the sled is too heavy and the grade is wrong and we should have cached half this at the last camp*, and Fen felt his stomach do a small cold turn, because Fen did the arithmetic on food the way other people did it on money, and the numbers were already tight.

They moved. Of course they moved. That was the whole arrangement with Yara Dunmore: she decided, and the world either agreed or got argued with until it did.

The first hour was fine. The second hour the wind arrived, coming down off the shoulder of the peak like something with an errand, and by the third hour Fen couldn't see Odo ten feet ahead of him except as a dark shape that swayed when he walked, which was how Fen knew his brother was tired, because Odo walked straight as a fence post when he was fresh.

Then the mountain cleared its throat.

Fen heard it before he understood it — a deep grinding groan somewhere above and to the left, the sound of a very large thing deciding to become several smaller things. He had time to shout, which he did, uselessly, because everyone had already heard it, and then the slope above the trail let go.

It wasn't a big slide. Later, Maren would call it "a minor release," in the calm voice she used for things that had nearly killed them, and Fen would want to throw a boot at her. But it came down across the trail exactly where the sled was, and it took the sled the way a river takes a leaf — one lurch, one white burst of dust and stone, and then the rope in Fen's hands went slack and the sled was simply gone, over the edge and down into the gray, along with most of their flour, all of their salt pork beyond what they carried, the spare rope, the tent fly, and Odo's spare boots.

The silence afterward was enormous.

"Well," said Odo.

"Don't," said Fen.

"I was just going to say—"

"You were going to say something about the boots."

Odo looked down at his feet, at the one pair of boots he owned now, already damp at the seams. "They're fine," he said, in the voice of a man whose boots were not fine.

* * *

They held council in the lee of a boulder, if you could call it a council, which was four people saying sensible things and Bram saying nothing while looking at the sky like it owed him money.

Yara laid it out flat, the way she did, no drama in it: what remained was eleven days of food for five people if they ate like birds, or seven days if they ate like people, and the pass was four days away in good weather and nobody wanted to guess what it was in bad weather. Rationing started tonight. Half rations, then less if it came to it.

"I've done hungry before," Maren said. "It's survivable. It's just unpleasant."

"That's the spirit," Fen said.

Maren gave him a look. Maren's looks were a language unto themselves; this one translated roughly to *you are twenty-two years old and I am forty-one and I have been hungry in more interesting places than you*. Fen grinned back, because grinning was free and calories weren't.

It was Bram who finally spoke, and what he said made the afternoon colder than the wind had. "Storm's not passing through," he said. "It's settling in. We'll get maybe two hours of walking light tomorrow morning, then it shuts. After that, we walk blind or we don't walk."

"Blind," Yara repeated.

"Whiteout. Snow and cloud and rock all the same color, no horizon, no shadow. Men walk in circles in it. Men walk off edges in it." Bram resettled his pack straps with those slow careful hands. "I've crossed the pass in one. Once. I don't recommend collecting the experience."

Nobody asked what year that had been. Nobody ever asked Bram what year anything had been; the answers were older than the maps and he handed them out like a man paying out rope, a foot at a time, and you didn't tug.

That night they ate half rations around a stove the size of a hatbox, and Fen lay in his bedroll doing the thing he always did when the numbers got bad, which was to count them again as though they might have improved out of spite. They hadn't. Nine days of thin food, four days of pass, and a margin of nothing.

Beside him, Odo was asleep already, breathing in that slow deliberate way he had, as if even unconscious he refused to be a burden. Fen listened to it for a while. Then he closed his eyes and made himself a promise he'd keep to nobody but himself: whatever it took, Odo came off this mountain fed.

* * *

The storm settled in overnight exactly as Bram had said, and in the morning they walked into the white.

Fen had been in fog before. Fog was a curtain; this was a wall painted the same color on both sides. The world shrank to a circle ten feet across with five people in it, and beyond the circle there was simply nothing — not darkness, which would at least have been something, but a pale blank nothing that swallowed sound and distance and, after a while, time. You could look at your own boots and lose track of whether you'd taken ten steps or a hundred.

By noon Odo was stumbling.

He hid it well, the way he hid everything, but Fen had been reading his brother since before either of them could write, and the signs were all there: the shortened stride, the way his head tipped down to watch his feet, the little hitch every dozen steps where his boot found scree instead of trail. And underneath it, worse than tiredness, was the other thing — the heaviness. Odo carried weight differently than other people. Other people set burdens down. Odo absorbed them, quietly, into that big patient frame of his, and Fen knew that somewhere inside his brother a ledger was open, tallying the lost sled, the lost boots, the lost margin, and calling it all his fault, because Odo had been holding the lead rope.

So Fen did the only thing he was any good at. He invented a game.

"Right," he announced, pitching his voice over the wind. "New rules. Counting game."

Odo glanced sideways at him. "Counting what?"

"Steps. We alternate. I count fifty loud, you count fifty loud, and whoever loses count buys the first round at the county seat." He raised his voice. "Maren! You're judge. Disputes go to you."

"I charge a fee," Maren called back from behind them, muffled by wind.

"Fifty percent of nothing," Fen said. "Standard rate."

And that was how they got through the afternoon. Fifty steps each, shouted into the whiteout, Fen making his counts ridiculous — *forty-seven, forty-eight, forty-nine-and-a-half, FIFTY* — until Odo's mouth twitched despite itself, until the twitch became a huff, until somewhere around the two-hundredth exchange Odo actually laughed, a short surprised bark of a laugh that the wind took and scattered, and Fen felt it land in his chest like a coal.

Because here was the thing about Odo laughing: it happened perhaps twice a year, and Fen had learned to farm those moments the way a farmer tends a stubborn orchard. You couldn't force them. You could only keep working the ground.

The games evolved as the day went on, because Fen's games always did. There was the Animal Game, in which each person named an animal they'd seen from a train window and the others had to guess the line and the year — Maren turned out to be disturbingly good at it, identifying a pronghorn sighting to within a season, and claimed this was a surveyor's skill, memory for terrain, and Fen privately suspected it was just that Maren Cole noticed everything, always, including things people wished she wouldn't. There was the Stove Game, which consisted entirely of describing, in loving detail, the meal each of them would cook first upon reaching civilization. Yara, to everyone's astonishment, delivered a fifteen-minute disquisition on fried onions that made even Bram's stomach audibly complain.

But the whiteout didn't care about games. That was the lesson of the day, the one the mountain taught slowly and without malice, the way a millstone teaches: it ground whether you laughed or not. Twice the trail vanished under drifted snow and Bram stood motionless for long minutes, head cocked, listening to the mountain the way you'd listen to a room for footsteps, before choosing a line and walking it. Once Yara's compass spun uselessly near some iron-bearing rock and they lost an hour finding the trail again, an hour that cost food none of them had.

Toward dusk — if it was dusk; the light never changed enough to swear to it — Odo went down.

Not badly. A knee buckle, a slide on scree, caught himself on one hand. But Fen saw it, and saw what came after it: Odo getting up slower than he needed to, standing a beat too long before moving, and behind his eyes that ledger opening again, adding the fall to the column marked *my fault*.

Fen fell too. Deliberately. A theatrical sprawl, arms windmilling, landing on his backside in the snow with a yelp that carried.

"You all right?" Odo was beside him instantly, hauling him up, hands checking him over the way he did, quick and thorough.

"Slipped," Fen said. "Ice under the snow. Whole trail's probably like it." He brushed himself off, wincing theatrically. "You know what helps on ice? Talking. Keeps the blood up. Fifty steps each, starting now, and this time the loser carries the stove tomorrow."

"You hate carrying the stove."

"Exactly. So count carefully."

Odo counted carefully. And Fen walked backward in front of him through the blind white world, shouting numbers, watching his brother's stride lengthen and steady, and understood with perfect clarity that this — this stupid, freezing, calorie-burning nonsense — was the most useful work he had ever done in his life. He'd laid track for six years. He'd never once kept a man on his feet before.

* * *

They camped that night in the hollow of a rock rib, packed shoulder to shoulder, the stove hissing its small defiant heat between them. Half rations again. Maren divided with a jeweler's precision and everyone pretended not to notice that she gave herself the smallest share, and everyone also pretended not to notice that Bram did the same thing, sliding the larger piece onto Odo's tin with a sleight of hand so practiced it had to be fifty years old.

Fen chewed his biscuit slowly, making it last, and watched the snow come down past the gap in the rocks. It fell without wind here, straight down, endless, patient. Somewhere under all of it was their sled, their flour, their salt pork, their margin. The mountain had taken it without effort and without interest, the way you'd brush off a crumb.

"Does it bother anyone else," Maren said into the quiet, "that it isn't angry? The storm. It's not punishing us. It doesn't know we're here."

"No," said Yara.

Everyone looked at her.

"It bothers me that it doesn't need to be angry," she said, turning her tin in her hands. "Anger you can argue with. This is just—" She gestured at the snowfall, the dark, the whole indifferent mass of the range around them. "Arithmetic. Same as the rationing. You put five people and nine days of food against four days of pass, and the mountain doesn't care how the sum comes out."

"That's cheerful," Fen said.

"It's honest. I prefer honest mountains to dishonest ones." She looked at him then, and there was something in her face Fen hadn't seen before — not softness exactly, but the absence of armor, a brief unguarded moment, like a door left open on a lit room. "Your brother walked twelve miles today on a quarter of what he'd normally eat, on a broken trail, in a whiteout. Because you made it a game."

"He'd have walked anyway."

"Yes," Yara said. "But he'd have walked heavy. You made him walk light." She went back to her tin, door closing, conversation over, but the words stayed with Fen, warming him more than the stove did.

Later, wrapped in his bedroll with Odo's slow breathing beside him, Fen ran the sums one more time. Food: thinner than yesterday. Trail: unknown. Weather: shut. By every measure he'd ever been taught, they were losing.

And yet. He thought of Maren giving away her share with a cheat's smooth hands. Of Bram's fifty-year-old sleight of hand. Of Yara's open door. Of Odo's laugh, scattered on the wind, still glowing somewhere in Fen's chest like a banked fire.

They were losing by every measure except one, and the one exception was the only sum Fen had ever really cared about.

He was almost asleep when Bram spoke from the dark, low, so the wind nearly took it.

"Tomorrow we reach the old ventilation shaft. Company tunnel. Sealed sixty years." A pause, long enough that Fen thought he'd dreamed it. "There's a way in. And there's something inside the mountain you all ought to see."

Fen opened his eyes. In the blackness he could just make out Bram's shape, sitting up, facing the sealed heart of the Karst Range like a man facing an old debt.

"What kind of something?" Fen whispered.

Bram was quiet for so long the snow filled the silence.

"The kind they buried," he said.
