## 10. The Tunnel Under the Pass

Bram Halloway had spent fifty years telling himself he would never go back.

He told himself that again now, standing at the base of a rock face that no map in the county admitted existed, while the wind tried its best to peel his hood off and Fen asked, for the fourth time, "So where exactly is 'exactly here,' Mr. Halloway?"

"Here," Bram said.

Fen looked at the cliff. The cliff looked back at him, gray and indifferent, wearing a skirt of scree and a cap of old snow. "It's a wall," Fen said.

"It's a door," Bram said, "if you know how to knock."

Behind them, the others were catching up—Yara first, moving like she'd been poured down the mountain rather than climbed it, then Maren with her instrument case hugged to her chest like a child she didn't trust anyone else to hold, then Odo on the good leg, favoring the bad one with the stubborn dignity of a man who had decided complaining was beneath him but only just.

Yara came up beside Bram and studied the rock face the way she studied everything: quietly, thoroughly, as if it might be lying to her. "You said there was nothing up here," she said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way you'd note weather coming in.

"I said there was nothing on the maps," Bram said. "That's not the same thing."

"No," Yara agreed. "It never is."

Bram set down his pack and began walking the base of the cliff, left hand trailing along the stone, counting under his breath. He hadn't done this count in half a century, and he'd done it last with hands that didn't ache and lungs that forgave him for smoking a pipe during the lunch break, which the foreman had pretended not to see because Bram was nineteen and could drill faster than any two men on the crew.

Forty-one paces. Forty-two. There.

The talus had shifted over the decades, as talus does, but the shape of it was wrong in a way only an old tunnel man would notice—a slope too regular, stones settled into a pattern rather than fallen into one. He knelt and began pulling rocks away with both hands, and after a moment Odo crouched beside him without being asked, and then Fen, and between the three of them they uncovered what the mountain had been politely keeping secret: a timber lintel, black with age, and behind it a wall of fitted stone with a seam down the middle.

"A door," Fen said again, wonder creeping into his voice despite himself. "An actual door."

"The company sealed it in 'sixty-one," Bram said. "When they abandoned the bore." He worked his fingers into the seam. The stones weren't mortared—they never bothered, for doors meant to be opened again someday by men who knew the trick—and they came away one at a time, heavy as sins, each one passed hand to hand down the line and stacked with a care that surprised him. Odo stacked them like he was setting a table.

The last stone came free and the dark breathed out at them.

That was the only word Bram ever had for it, though he'd never said it aloud to anyone: tunnels breathed. Cold air moved through them on their own schedules, in and out with the pressure of the weather outside, so that a sealed bore exhaled when a storm built up beyond the pass. Fifty years ago he had stood in this exact spot and felt that breath on his face and thought it was the most alive thing he'd ever touched.

Now it smelled of cold stone and old iron and, faintly, impossibly, of pipe smoke—which was memory, of course, nothing but memory—but he stood there a moment longer than he needed to before he lit the lantern.

"Well," said Maren, peering past his shoulder into the black. "It's very dark."

"That's generally the feature of tunnels," Bram said.

"I just want it noted for the record."

"Noted," Yara said, and swung her own pack up. "Everyone inside before the light goes. Bram—you're sure about this?"

No, he was not sure. He was fifty years from sure. But he said, "I helped cut this bore. I know every yard of it," and found that his voice held steadier than his knees, and that was enough.

* * *

Inside, the world changed.

It always did. A tunnel wasn't a place; it was a different arrangement of everything. Sound went strange and close. The cold turned from the wind's cold, which pushed at you, to the stone's cold, which simply was, patient and total, the temperature of the deep earth leaking out through fifty years of stillness. And the dark—lantern or no lantern, the dark sat behind you the whole time like a large quiet animal deciding whether you were worth the trouble.

Bram walked point with the lantern held high, and the others fell into line behind him, and he felt the old order reassert itself in his chest: point man sets the pace, point man owns the silence, point man counts the yards. He counted. Two hundred yards in, the bore narrowed and the timbering began, and there it all was, exactly as his hands remembered if not his eyes—the cribbing stacks, four-by-eight, set on the company's cheap side of spec because the engineer had argued for closer spacing and been overruled by a man in a city who would never stand under it.

"You can tell," Yara said softly, running her palm along a timber. "The spacing."

"You can tell," Bram agreed.

"They cut corners."

"They cut everything they could get away with." He heard his own voice come out flatter than he intended. "This whole mountain is a ledger, Miss Dunmore. Every stick of wood in it is an entry somebody fudged."

They walked on. Fen had gone quiet, which for Fen was a phenomenon like snow in July, and Odo had gone quiet too, and even Maren's running commentary had dried up, and the only sounds were boots on old ballast and the drip of water somewhere ahead, keeping slow time like a clock nobody wound anymore.

At six hundred yards the bore opened.

Not much—a widening, a chamber where the rock had fought them and the crews had blasted a room to work in, big enough for a mule cart to turn around. Bram's lantern caught the far wall and threw shadows, and in the shadows were shapes, and the shapes resolved slowly, the way things do in dreams, into iron shelving bolted to the living rock.

And on the shelving: boxes. Dozens of them. Tin, stenciled, rusted at the seams but sound, each one stamped with the seal of the Karst Consolidated Mining & Water Company.

Nobody spoke. Bram heard Yara set her pack down very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands are shaking and you don't want anyone to know.

"What is this place?" Maren whispered, and her whisper had a crack in it.

"This is where they put the truth," Bram said.

* * *

He had known about the chamber for fifty years and had told no one, and the weight of that sat on him now as he watched Yara lift the first tin box down and work its lid loose with a rail spike for a pry bar.

He'd been nineteen. The foreman had liked him because he was quick and asked no questions, and near the end of the bore, when the contract money was running thin and the company lawyers had come up the line in their city coats, the foreman had pulled Bram aside and given him a day's work that wasn't drilling: haul these boxes in, stack them on the shelves, bolt the shelving, don't look inside, don't ask. He'd been paid three dollars extra and a bottle of rye, and he'd taken both, and he had not looked inside, because he was nineteen and three dollars was three dollars.

He had thought about it every year since. Some years more than others. The years after Lowden Hollow went dry, mostly. The years when he'd ride the spur line past the turnoff to the Hollow and see the dust blowing across fields that used to be green, and think: three dollars and a bottle of rye.

"There are bore records in here," Yara was saying, her voice carefully level in the way he was learning meant it wasn't level at all. "Original field books. Daily reports. Water tables—" She stopped. She had a page flat on top of a crate, and her lantern was close over it, and Bram watched her read the same line three times.

"What?" Maren asked.

"The water table readings." Yara turned the page toward the light. "These aren't survey records. These are hydrology. Daily measurements, month after month, from the aquifer under Lowden Hollow." Her finger moved down the columns. "And here—" another page, another book, "—the diversion channel surveys. Cut dates. Flow rates. They measured everything. They documented the whole thing as they did it."

"They kept records?" Fen sounded genuinely baffled. "Of doing the crime? Why would you write it down?"

"Because it wasn't a crime yet," Maren said quietly. She had come forward and was reading over Yara's shoulder, and Bram saw her professional self rise up through the fear like a fish rising to feed. "Look at the dates. These are internal engineering documents. At the time they were made, they were just—work. Routine. It was only a crime afterward, once the concession was filed and the water rights were registered off the falsified plat." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Which means these records prove intent. You can't fake daily readings going back three years. This isn't evidence that they took the water. This is evidence that they planned to."

The chamber was silent except for the drip.

Odo lowered himself carefully onto a crate, bad leg out straight, and said, "So we've got it, then. This is it. This is what we carried the papers out of the Tollhouse to find more of."

"This is better than the Tollhouse," Yara said. "The plates show the false survey. These show the hand behind it." She was already sorting, fast and precise, pulling the boxes that mattered and building a stack. "We take the field books, the diversion surveys, and anything signed. Signatures above all."

Bram stood apart, holding the lantern, watching her work, and the guilt he had carried for fifty years shifted in his chest—not lighter, exactly, but changed, the way ore changes when it's finally smelted. All those years he could have come back. Any year. One day's walk from the spur line, a door he alone knew how to open. He had told himself the mountain was dangerous, the season wrong, the door surely collapsed. He had told himself a great many things.

"You knew," Yara said.

She hadn't turned around. She was still sorting. But she said it plainly, into the quiet, and everyone else went very still.

"Yes," Bram said.

"How long?"

"Fifty years."

She did turn then. The lantern light caught the lines of her face, and he braced for anger—for the cold, clean anger he'd seen her use on broken couplings and broken promises. What he saw instead was worse. She looked tired, and kind, and like a woman recognizing a familiar weight on someone else's shoulders.

"Why now?" she asked.

Bram looked at the shelves. At the boxes. At the dark behind the dark.

"Because a woman pulled some plates out of a hole in the tide and wouldn't put them back," he said. "And because I'm too old to keep telling myself next year." He cleared his throat. "Also because my knees finally hurt less than the not-going."

That got a sound out of Fen—half laugh, half something else—and Yara almost smiled, and the chamber let them have the small mercy of it.

"We should move," Maren said. "If we're taking all of this—"

"We're taking what we can carry," Yara said. "Fast."

* * *

They packed by lantern light, dividing the load: field books wrapped in oilcloth and distributed among packs so no single fall, no single theft, no single frozen river crossing could take them all. Bram showed Odo how to wrap the tin boxes so they wouldn't rattle. Fen insisted on carrying the largest one and then immediately regretted it and then refused to give it up, on principle, sweating and swaying like a mule with opinions.

It was Odo, at the rear, who heard it first.

He raised one hand—not the dramatic way, just a small stop gesture, palm out—and the chamber went silent, five people frozen mid-motion, and Bram felt the floor of the tunnel through his boots before he heard anything with his ears.

Boots. On ballast. Outside. More than two. Moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew where they were going.

"The door," Yara breathed.

Bram was already moving, snuffing the lantern, and the dark came down on them like a lid. He found the wall by touch and pressed himself into the angle where timber met stone, and he heard the others do the same, breathing gone shallow, and out of the black mouth of the tunnel, far off but getting nearer, came a yellow flicker—another lantern, maybe two, and voices, low and male and carrying that particular flatness of men being paid to be thorough.

"How far to the chamber?" a voice said, echoing. Bram knew the cadence. Vane's hired men had been dogging them since the switchback country, always a valley behind, always a day short. Now they were a hundred yards away and closing.

"Half a mile," another voice answered. "Map says the shelving's intact. Vane wants the boxes burned in there. No carry-out, no trace."

Burned. In there. With the timbering fifty years dry and the air movement dead-still beyond the chamber—Bram's stomach turned over. Those fools could bring the whole bore down on themselves and seal the proof forever under a hundred feet of mountain, and call it a job well done.

In the dark, a hand found his sleeve. Yara. Her mouth came close to his ear, and her whisper was barely breath:

"Is there another way out?"

Fifty years ago, there had been. An air shaft, bored up to the ridge to vent the blasting smoke, sealed with a grate he'd helped carry on his own shoulder, a boy of nineteen with a grin and three dollars in his pocket.

"Maybe," Bram whispered back.

"Maybe is my favorite word," Yara whispered. "Show me."

Behind them, down the bore, the first lantern light crept around the curve of the tunnel, and Bram took her wrist and began, silently, to count paces into the dark.
