## 11. Ambush at the Switchback

Odo had learned three things about mountains in his twenty-two years, and two of them he'd learned this week.

First: they were bigger than they looked. Second: they were colder than anyone admitted. And third — the new one, the one still settling into his bones like a stone in a boot — was that a switchback was a liar's piece of engineering. It looked like kindness, all those gentle hairpins stitched up the cliff face so a body wouldn't have to climb straight up. But every one of those turns was a place where the mountain pinched the trail narrow as a hallway, with nothing on the outside edge but air and a long opinion about gravity.

He said as much to Fen while they climbed.

"You complain more than the mule," Fen said. The mule in question — Bram's mule, named Turnip, because Bram explained that everything on a mountain eventually wished it were a turnip, low to the ground and hard to kill — flicked an ear at Odo in what he chose to take as agreement.

"The mule doesn't carry the document case."

"The mule carries the tent."

"The tent is *heavy*, Fen."

"Then it's a fair division of labor." Fen walked ahead of him, easy as Sunday, his long legs eating the grade like it owed him money. That was the thing about his brother. Fen had been walking track since he was fourteen, and he moved through the world the way water moves downhill — no wasted argument with anything. Odo had always felt like he was arguing with the world constantly, quietly, just by existing in it.

Behind them came Maren, bent over her transit like it was a wounded bird she was nursing back to health, calling out grades in a voice that got sharper the higher they went. Yara was ahead with Bram, and every so often Yara would stop and look at something — a cut in the rock, an old culvert, a rusted spike head — and Bram would say three words and she'd write down nine, and Odo had decided weeks ago that whatever was between those two was older and stranger than any of them knew.

And in the document case, slung across Odo's chest where it had been since the tunnel, rode the reason Vane's men were somewhere behind them on this same trail.

Bram had put it there. "You're lightest," he'd said, which was a lie, because Fen was lighter, and everyone knew it, and nobody argued, because what Bram actually meant was *you're the one who'll guard it like a mother hen*, and that was true. Odo worried things. He worried the case now, one hand flat against the leather, feeling the plates inside shift faintly with each step, like the bones of some small sleeping animal.

"They're not going to catch us," Fen said, without turning around. He did that. Read Odo's silences like timetables.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're breathing worried."

"There's a *worried* kind of breathing?"

"There's your breathing, so yes."

Odo laughed despite himself, and the laugh bounced off the rock wall on their left and fell off the edge on their right and was gone, and for a few hundred steps the climb was just a climb, and the morning was just cold sunlight on scree, and the worst thing in the world was the state of his boots.

Then the birds stopped.

* * *

He didn't know how he knew. There was no sound of men, no glint of steel, none of the things stories promised. Just that the small brown birds that had been working the switchback above them — hopping from stone to stone, being busy and important about it — were suddenly not there. The whole slope went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

Odo stopped. "Fen."

Fen had already stopped. His hand came up and back, flat, the way Bram did it. Ahead, Yara turned, and Odo watched her read the same silence, and watched her face do something complicated and quick before it settled into calm.

"Off the trail," she said, low. "Now."

They scattered — Maren pulling Turnip behind a shoulder of rock, Bram moving with a speed that made Odo's stomach drop, because no man seventy years old should move like that unless he'd spent his whole life expecting this exact moment. Odo pressed himself against the inner wall beside Fen, the document case crushed between his chest and cold stone, his heart going like a handcar running away downhill.

For ten heartbeats, nothing.

Then gravel sang above them — that soft, wrong music of boots trying to be quiet on loose stone — and three men came around the upper bend of the switchback.

They weren't soldiers. Odo saw that right away and it somehow made it worse. Soldiers would have been almost respectable. These were the kind of men Silas Vane hired the way other people hired plumbers: competent, unmemorable, paid to make problems disappear. The lead one had a pick handle. The second had a coil of rope and the flat eyes of a man doing arithmetic about how much noise a body makes falling off a switchback. The third —

The third Odo recognized. A big quiet man who'd been at the staging town, who'd shared a table with them one night, who'd told a story about a mule that had made even Bram smile. He'd been *there*. He'd been there the whole time.

"*Halloway's crew,*" the rope man called down, conversational, like hailing across a valley. "Mr. Vane wants a word. Just a word. Nobody needs to get hurt standing on this ledge."

"That's a funny sentence," Yara called back, "coming from a man holding a pick handle."

"It's for the rocks." The man smiled. "Loose ones. You'd be surprised how many rocks come loose on a switchback when a fellow asks them to."

Below them, the trail dropped away in a long white ribbon of scree, four hundred feet of it, down to a boulder field that looked from here like spilled sugar. Odo's legs informed him, in detail, exactly how far that was.

Fen's hand found his sleeve. Not pulling. Just holding. *I'm here*, the hand said, the same way it had said it their whole lives, in every dark bunkhouse and every hungry winter, and Odo felt the fear in his chest rearrange itself into something he could carry.

"Here's my counteroffer," Yara said. She hadn't reached for anything. Her hands were loose at her sides, and her voice was steady as a level bubble, and Odo thought, with a strange floating clarity: *she's been here before. Not this ledge. This moment.* "You walk back down and tell Mr. Vane we said no. Politely. I'll even write it down for you."

"Miz Dunmore." The rope man sighed like a schoolteacher. "We can do this the loud way or the quiet way—"

Bram threw the first stone.

It wasn't dramatic. No shout, no windup. One moment Bram Halloway was leaning against the rock wall looking ancient; the next, a fist-sized chunk of granite was whistling past Odo's ear and taking the pick-handle man square in the knee with a crack like a rail joint under a heavy engine. The man folded with a scream that echoed off the far wall and came back twice.

Everything after that happened fast, and Odo's memory of it stayed broken forever, like a plate dropped on stone.

Maren shouted — actually shouted, the disgraced surveyor who spoke to people maybe forty words a day — and hurled her transit case, which burst open and rained brass fittings across the trail like the world's angriest confetti. Yara went low and forward, not at the rope man but *past* him, cutting the angle, putting herself between the fight and the drop. Fen grabbed Odo's collar and hauled him along the wall toward the bend, toward the only direction that wasn't down.

And the third man — the mule-story man, the table-sharing man — came straight for Odo.

Later, Odo would understand why. Later, Maren would reconstruct it coolly over a campfire: the big man had marked the document case days ago, had watched who carried it, had waited for the moment the carrier was separated. But in the moment itself there was no understanding. There was just a large shape coming down the trail at him with terrible patience, and the case against his ribs, and the drop at his back, and Fen shouting his name from somewhere that was suddenly very far away.

Odo did the only thing his body knew how to do, which was the thing he'd done his entire life when something big came at him.

He ran.

Not down the trail — the big man owned the trail. Odo went *up*, scrambling along the base of the rock wall where the scree was thin and the footing was garbage, putting the wall between himself and the big man's reach, clutching the case with both arms like it was the last plank of a wrecked ship. Stones rolled under his boots. The big man followed, unhurried, herding him — Odo realized with a lurch that he was being herded, funneled along the wall toward the outside edge of the switchback, toward the place where the trail narrowed to nothing and the mountain stopped pretending to hold you.

"FEN!" he screamed, and heard his brother screaming back, and heard fighting — real fighting, grunts and stone and Yara's voice cracking out sharp commands like she'd been born to them — but it was all happening in another country. His country was six feet of crumbling ledge and a patient man walking behind him.

The ledge ended.

There was nowhere else. The trail simply gave up, pinched out between the wall and the void, and beyond it the switchback continued fifteen feet below, connected by nothing but a slope of scree so steep it looked painted on. Odo stood at the end of the world with a satchel full of truth against his chest and a man coming to take it.

"Hand it over, lad." The big man had stopped, too. Even now, even here, he sounded reasonable. That was the horror of him. "Nobody's got to fall today. It's paper. Old paper. Whatever you think it's worth, it ain't worth the drop."

Odo looked down. Four hundred feet. The boulder field. The sugar-spill slope.

He looked at the case.

He thought — absurdly, in the last calm corner of his mind — about the Tollhouse cave, about the story Bram had finally told them around a fire: that the cave charged admission, that everything taken from it had to be paid for, that the sea kept its own ledger. He thought about the dead man whose errand this was, the Lowden Hollow descendant who'd died in the dark trying to carry these same plates into daylight. He thought about a village that wasn't on any map anymore, drinking dust for fifty years because a signature had decided it should.

Some things you don't hand over. Some tolls you've already paid.

"No," Odo said.

The big man shrugged, and came on, and Odo stepped off the edge.

* * *

He didn't decide to. That was the thing he could never explain afterward, not to Fen, not to Yara, not to himself at three in the morning. His boots simply took the step, because the alternative was the big man's hands, and his body understood — faster than his mind, faster than fear — that the scree below was a chance and the man behind him was a certainty.

The world became sliding.

Scree is a lie of a surface. It looks solid until you commit, and then it commits with you, the whole slope deciding at once to go somewhere else. Odo went down on his back with the case locked in both arms and surfed fifteen feet of screaming stone, the sky wheeling, the big man's face shrinking above him, and then the slope delivered him — spat him, really — onto the lower switchback with a force that picked him up and set him down again three feet further along, hard, on his right leg.

The sound his leg made was small and wet and wrong, and Odo heard it from a great distance, the way you hear weather from indoors.

Then it was just pain, arriving all at once like a freight train through a station, and Odo lay on the trail with his teeth locked together and discovered that his arms had never let go. The case was intact. The case was fine. The case was pressed under his fingers, whole and heavy and safe, and through the red roaring haze of the worst pain of his life, Odo Dunmore began to laugh.

Because that was the family disease, apparently. Fen laughed when he was scared. Odo laughed when he was broken. Their father had laughed the day the mine closed, sitting on the porch with his hat in his hands, laughing until he cried, saying *well, well, well* over and over like a man greeting old friends.

Boots hammered down the slope above him. For one black instant he thought it was the big man, and he curled around the case like a snail in its shell—

"—Odo. *Odo.*" Fen. Fen's hands, Fen's face, gray as ash, hovering over him. "You absolute — you *idiot* — you jumped, you jumped off a *mountain*—"

"It was a small mountain," Odo managed.

"Your leg—"

"I know about my leg, Fen, I was present."

Fen made a sound that was half sob and half laugh, which Odo recognized as the family sound, the truest one, and then the others were there — Yara dropping to her knees with her sleeves torn and a welt rising on her jaw, Maren tearing open the supply roll, Bram standing over them both with a rock in one hand and an expression Odo had never seen on him, something old and cold and finished.

"Above?" Yara snapped.

"Gone down," Bram said. "Their friend's knee bought us the time. They'll think on it now." He looked at Odo, at the case still locked in his arms, and something in the old man's face cracked open a degree. "You held it."

"Wasn't letting go," Odo said, and found that the words cost more than the leg had. "It's not mine to drop."

Yara's hand came down on his shoulder, firm and warm. "No," she said quietly. "It isn't. And we're going to get you — and it — off this mountain if I have to carry you both."

"He's heavier than he looks," Fen said wetly.

"So are you," Odo said, and passed out.

* * *

He surfaced once, briefly, to the rhythm of being carried — a litter of tent poles and Turnip's spare blanket, swaying between his brother and the guide, the sky tilting slowly overhead. Someone had splinted his leg with a survey stave. Maren's voice, tight with guilt, saying something about *should have seen it, I should have seen the third man*; Yara's voice answering, low and certain: *you couldn't have. None of us could. That's what men like Vane are for.*

And once, drifting, he heard Bram say, to no one in particular, in a voice like gravel settling:

"Fifty years I've walked these lines. Never once seen a man jump for paper." A pause. The litter swayed. "That boy's got more spine than the company ever built into its trestles."

Odo smiled, and let the dark take him back — a kinder dark this time, with voices in it, and the steady crunch of boots that were all, unmistakably, heading the same direction he was.

The last thing he heard was Fen, close by his ear, whispering the way he used to when they were small and sharing a bunk against the cold:

"Don't you dare die, Odo. I'm not carrying the case *and* the eulogy."

Odo laughed in his sleep, and dreamed of ledgers, and of a tide going out a very long way.
