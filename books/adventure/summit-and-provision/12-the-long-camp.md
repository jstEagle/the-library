## 12. The Long Camp

The storm found them an hour after the switchback, as if it had been waiting politely for the bloodshed to finish.

Yara heard it coming before she saw it—a sound like a freight train that had lost its rails and decided to come anyway, rolling down the high country with its shoulders up. Bram took one look at the sky and said, "Caboose," and that single word carried them the last half mile at a stumbling run, Odo slung between his brother and Maren, the document case strapped across Yara's chest like a child she refused to set down.

The caboose sat on a shelf of track above a frozen creek, one of the old company cars left behind when the line closed—red paint gone the color of dried clay, windows fogged with fifty years of nobody. Its wheels had settled into the ballast like an old dog into a favorite spot. It listed slightly to starboard. It was, Yara thought, shoving the door open onto cold iron darkness and the smell of creosote and mouse, the most beautiful thing she'd seen in weeks.

"Inside," she said. "Everyone. Now."

The storm arrived thirty seconds later and erased the world.

* * *

By lamplight, the wrecked caboose became something almost like a home, if homes could be eight feet wide and tilted.

Fen got a fire going in the little iron stove with the efficiency of a man who had spent his life warming himself in places he wasn't supposed to be. Maren spread her surveying oilcloth over the bunk frame and turned it into an operating table. And Odo lay on it, gray-faced, his left leg bent wrong below the knee, making jokes in a thin voice because that was what Odo did—the way some men bled, he joked.

"The fall," he said, as Maren peeled back the torn trouser leg, "was entirely intentional. I was testing the structural integrity of the mountainside. For the survey."

"Noted," Maren said. "It failed."

"It did rather."

The break was bad but not the worst kind—Maren said so in the flat voice she used for facts she wanted everyone to survive—and between the four of them they had what they needed: a splint made from a section of lath Yara pried off the wall, a roll of linen from Bram's pack that smelled of camphor and decades, and a flask of whiskey that Fen produced from inside his coat with the sheepish air of a man surrendering contraband.

"For pain," Fen said. "I was saving it for a celebration."

"This is a celebration," Odo said through his teeth. "We're all still alive. Pour."

Maren set the bone while Yara held Odo's shoulders and Fen held his hand and told him, rapidly and nonsensically, about a marmot he'd once fought over a sandwich, and Odo screamed into the noise of it, and then it was done, the leg straight and splinted, and Odo lay sweating and shaking and grinning at the ceiling like a man who had won something.

"Well," he said. "That's the worst of my twenties behind me."

"You're twenty-six," Fen said.

"I know what I said."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to a steady orange hum and the storm hammered the roof like a creditor at the door, they ate. Rationing had begun two days early thanks to the rockslide and the sled, so dinner was hard biscuit, a strip of salt pork each, and tea brewed from needles Bram stripped off a sprig he'd pocketed on the climb, which tasted like drinking a forest and was somehow the best thing Yara had ever had.

Odo couldn't sit up, so they propped him against packs and ate around him, knees nearly touching in the narrow space, and the wind screamed, and the stove ticked, and nobody wanted to sleep yet even though everybody needed to.

It was Fen who started it, the way younger brothers start things—innocently, with malice aforethought.

"Bram," he said. "Tell us how you broke your nose."

"I didn't break it. It settled."

"It settled? Noses don't settle."

"Mine did." Bram sipped his needle tea. His face, in the firelight, was a topographic map of everything that had ever happened to him. "Nineteen sixty-one. A mule named Consequence."

"There's no mule named Consequence," Fen said.

"There was. She earned it." And then, to everyone's astonishment, Bram told the story—all of it, the mule, the load of blasting powder she'd objected to, the bridge over Miller's Cut that no longer existed, and how Bram had swum out of a freezing river holding the detonator box above the water like a man raising a toast. He told it dry and slow, and by the end Fen was wheezing and Odo was laughing so hard he had to hold his splinted leg with both hands, and even Maren had put her hand over her mouth.

When it quieted, Bram looked around the little firelit room—at Fen wiping his eyes, at Odo pale and pleased, at Maren, at Yara—and something in his weathered face went soft and far away.

"Fifty years," he said, "since I've told that story to anybody. Funny what you carry."

And that was the door opening, though none of them knew it yet.

* * *

Maren went next, though it took her longer, and she started sideways, the way careful people do.

"I was thirty-one," she said, turning her tin cup slowly. "Chief assistant surveyor on the Corvane Line. Do you know it?"

"Closed line," Yara said. "Through the eastern Karst. Ran six years."

"Ran six years on paper I drew." Maren's cup stopped turning. "There was a grade error. Mine, they said. A misread benchmark, a curve staked too tight, and a cut let go in the spring thaw and buried the work crew's camp. Eleven men." Her voice stayed level the whole time, which was worse than breaking. "The inquiry took my license in forty minutes. The company took everything else slower."

"That wasn't—" Fen began.

"It doesn't matter now whether it was or wasn't," Maren said, which was not the same as saying it hadn't been, and Yara, watching her, thought: *she knows it wasn't. She has known for years and has never once been allowed to say it.* "What matters is I stopped drawing lines after that. Twenty years of other people's numbers, other people's books. Then a woman walks into a staging town hiring strays, and I look at her papers, and I think—well. One more line. One more, drawn right this time."

She looked up, and her eyes were bright and fierce in the lamplight.

"So that's my story. That's what I'm carrying. I'd like it noted for the record that I intend to put it down."

"Noted," Odo said solemnly, and raised his tin cup, and Maren almost smiled.

* * *

Fen and Odo told theirs together, overlapping, interrupting, finishing each other's sentences the way twins do even when they're three years apart, which theirs was—a story of a mother gone young and a father gone meaner, of walking the tracks as boys because the tracks went somewhere and home didn't, of Fen carrying Odo's boots one whole winter when Odo's feet froze and Odo carrying Fen through a fever the summer after, trading debts back and forth until the debts became the bond itself.

"We never owed anybody else anything after that," Fen said. "You can't. Once you've carried a person, you know what carrying is."

"And we decided," Odo added, "that anyone who hires both of us gets the pair. Non-negotiable. Like buying a matched set of hammers."

"Speak for yourself. I'm the good hammer."

"You're the claw hammer. I'm the one that actually drives nails."

They bickered on, comfortably, and Yara listened and felt the strange ache in her chest that listening to other people's families always gave her—not envy exactly, more like standing outside a lit window in the cold. She knew the feeling intimately. She had built her whole life on the far side of that glass.

Which was why, when the silence came around to her, she almost deflected it. Almost.

Bram saved her the trouble of deciding by simply looking at her. Old guide's trick. He didn't ask anything. He just waited, the way the mountain waits, until you understood that the mountain was going to win.

Yara turned her cup in her hands. The fire popped. Outside, the storm leaned on the caboose and the old car groaned and held.

"My father was a roadmaster," she said finally. "On the Meridian & Coast. You've heard of it?"

"Everyone's heard of it," Fen said. "That's the one that went under."

"That's the one that went under because of my family, depending on who tells it." She kept her voice light, which cost her. "He worked for it thirty years. Loved it like a person. When it collapsed—fraud in the financing, mostly, nothing he did—he took it the way some men take a death in the family. Because it was one. He died two years later. Slow, and angry, and convinced the world had proven something to him." She paused. "My mother lasted four months after that. Not illness. Just—stopped. Like a clock nobody wound."

Nobody spoke. Even Odo had gone still against his packs.

"I was nineteen," Yara said. "I'd grown up in depots and section houses. I could read a grade before I could read cursive. And when they were both gone, I did the only thing that made sense to me. I hired onto a crew. And then another. And another." She laughed, short and without much humor in it. "People think I drift because I love the work. I do love the work. But that's not why."

"Why, then?" Fen asked gently.

"Because moving is easy." The words came out of her like something long packed in ice, cold and clean. "Moving requires nothing of you but your legs. Every town is a job and every job is a town, and you learn the shape of it: arrive, measure, draw, leave. Nobody gets close enough to matter, because mattering takes time, and time is the one thing a drifter never stays to spend." She looked into the fire. "My parents taught me the opposite lesson without meaning to. They taught me that staying is where people get destroyed. They stayed—in that house, in that grief, in each other's way—and it killed them both by inches. So I decided I would never give anything enough time to be taken from me. No house. No name on any ledger. No—" she gestured vaguely at the four of them, at the fire, at the small warm world inside the iron walls, "—no *this*."

"And how's that working out?" Odo asked softly.

"Terribly," Yara said. "You're all in my way."

Fen snorted. Maren smiled down at her cup. And Bram said, in his gravel voice, "Took you long enough to notice."

"I noticed in the staging town," Yara admitted. "When I hired you. I told myself it was expedience—four strays, one contract, nobody asks questions. But I stood there counting heads and I caught myself thinking, *this is a crew*, and crews are just families that met on purpose. And I signed you all anyway." She shook her head. "I keep moving so no one can get close enough to leave. That's the truth of it. That's the whole ugly engine of me. And somewhere between the Tollhouse and tonight I stopped moving, and here you all are, and I find that I—" 

Her voice snagged. She was quiet a moment, and the storm filled the pause, and no one rushed her.

"I find that I don't want to pick any of you up and put you down again," she finished. "Not one of you. Not even the loud one."

"I'm the loud one?" Odo said.

"You fell off a mountain today to protect a box of papers."

"Somebody had to. You were busy being heroic elsewhere."

"That's what I'm saying. You're *my* loud one. I've claimed you. There are forms."

Laughter went around the caboose then, real laughter, the kind that hurts a little, and Yara let herself have it, let herself sit in the middle of it like a woman sitting down after a very long walk. The stove ticked. The wind howled and accomplished nothing. Odo's breathing had gone slow and even, half asleep, his head tipped against Fen's shoulder, and Fen let him stay there with the tenderness of a man who had carried him before and expected to again.

Maren banked the fire. Bram took first watch at the window, motionless, listening to the mountain the way other men listen to music.

Yara sat a while longer, watching the flames, and felt the strangest sensation of her adult life, which was the absence of the itch. For fifteen years there had always been an itch—the next line, the next range, the next departure board. Tonight, stormbound in a wrecked caboose with a broken-legged hammer, a disgraced surveyor, an old man and his mule stories, she felt no pull at all toward anywhere else on earth.

*Family,* she thought, testing the word like weight on a new bridge. *So that's what it feels like. It feels like staying.*

She was nearly asleep herself when Bram spoke quietly from the window, without turning around.

"Storm'll blow through by morning. Two days of clear, maybe three, before the next system." A pause. "And Yara—we've got company on our back trail. Lanterns, maybe a mile down. Been there since dark. Holding position."

Yara came fully awake.

Vane's three days were nearly up—and whatever was camped out there in the snow had not come to negotiate.
