## 13. What Vane Wants

The rain came off the mountain in long gray sheets, and Silas Vane sat in his tent with a lamp turned low and his father's ledger open on his knees.

He had brought it everywhere for thirty years. It was not a large book—cloth-bound, water-stained at one corner, the spine mended twice with linen tape gone amber with age. A surveyor's field ledger, 1898 through 1904. To anyone else it would be a curiosity, the kind of thing that sold for two bits in an estate box between a hymnal and a child's arithmetic book. To Silas it was scripture, inheritance, and sentence, all three bound in the same cracked cloth.

His name was written inside the front cover in his father's careful hand. *Property of E. R. Vane, Licensed Surveyor.* Below it, smaller, added years later in a shakier line: *Elias, if you are reading this, burn it.*

Silas had not burned it. He had read it instead, at fourteen, by candlelight in a house that smelled of camphor and disappointment, while his mother slept and the rain did what rain was doing right now against canvas instead of glass.

He turned to the page he always turned to. The figures were neat, confident, professional—the hand of a man who took pride in his work even when he was betraying it. The Karst Range watershed, section lines, flow measurements. And there, in the margin beside the Lowden Hollow entry, a small notation in pencil that Elias Vane had never erased:

*Adjusted per instruction. Payment received in full.*

Four words of pencil. One village's water, signed away. Forty-one families who had watched their wells go salt and their orchards brown while the company's flumes ran fat and silver above them, carrying snowmelt down to the mines and never a drop back.

Silas closed the ledger gently, the way you close a thing that might bite.

Outside, his men were grumbling about the weather around a fire that wouldn't stay lit. He could hear Dree Colton's voice, big and easy, telling some story about a bear or a woman or a bear dressed as a woman; the others laughed on schedule. Good men, mostly. Men who liked clear instructions and cash on delivery and didn't ask what the papers in the oilskin case were worth or to whom. He paid them well because well-paid men were loyal men, and loyal men were the only luxury a man in his trade could afford.

He was not, whatever the railroad towns said about him, a cruel man. He had thought about this a great deal over the years, usually at night, usually alone, and he had concluded that cruelty required appetite, and he had none. What he had was work. There were things companies wanted kept quiet, and Silas Vane was very good at keeping them quiet—not with violence, as a rule, but with money, patience, and the unglamorous genius of knowing which clerk owed which favor. When violence did become necessary, he arranged it the way he arranged everything: minimally, regretfully, and at arm's length.

He told himself this often enough that it had begun to sound true, which is the most any man can hope for from his own conscience.

* * *

The trouble—and he had known it would be the trouble from the moment the telegram came out of the staging town—was the Tollhouse papers.

He spread the inventory on his camp table: a list compiled by a harbor clerk who owed him, describing what a railroad engineer named Dunmore had carried out of a sea cave at extreme low tide. Survey plates. Company seal. Pre-concession dating.

Silas had sat with that last item for a long time.

Because here was the thing his employers did not fully understand, being third- and fourth-generation men who thought of the concession as something like weather—old, natural, theirs. The concession had been built on sand. Not legal sand; paper sand. Elias Vane's adjusted figures were the foundation under everything: the water rights, the mining claims, the spur-line easements, the whole elegant structure of ownership that had made four families rich and one hollow into a ghost. If those original plates surfaced—if anyone competent looked at them beside the filed surveys—the structure would not groan. It would simply come down, quietly, the way a building does when someone finally removes the one beam holding it up.

So they had sent him. They always sent him. And Silas had come up the coast with a case full of buyout instruments and a head full of contingencies, and he had found, to his considerable irritation, that he cared about this one more than he should.

It was the ledger's fault. It was always the ledger's fault.

* * *

His father had been, by every account that survived him, a good man.

That was the part nobody believed when Silas described it, so mostly he didn't describe it. But it was true. Elias Vane had taught Sunday school. He had walked miles out of his way to return a farmer's dropped purse. He had once spent a whole winter rebuilding a widow's fence without payment, and when Silas asked him why, he'd said, "Because a fence is a promise you make to a neighbor, and she hasn't got anyone else to keep it."

And then in the autumn of 1901, a company man had come to their kitchen table with a contract and a figure, and Elias Vane—who was drowning, whose practice was failing, whose son needed shoes and whose wife needed medicine—had picked up his pencil.

Silas remembered the evening his father came home after signing. He remembered it the way you remember a single photograph: frozen, overexposed, permanent. His father had hung up his coat, washed his hands longer than hands need washing, and sat down to supper without eating much. Later Silas had heard him in the study, talking to his mother in a low voice, and caught only one sentence through the door.

"I can put it back," his father was saying. "I can file the true figures. I'll lose everything, but I can put it back."

And his mother—tired, frightened, practical—had answered: "And then what do we eat, Eli?"

His father had never put it back. He had lived another nineteen years, done modest honest work, given to the church, died respected by everyone who didn't know. And every year on the anniversary of the signing, without ever explaining why, he had taken the train partway up the Karst line and stood looking at the mountains for an afternoon, and come home quieter than he left.

Silas had gone with him once, at eleven. He remembered standing beside his father on a windswept platform, watching the old man stare at a dry brown valley below the line, and asking what he was looking at.

"Nothing," his father had said. "There's nothing there."

But his voice had broken on the word nothing, and Silas, who was a child and then a young man and then a fixer, had spent thirty years understanding exactly what his father had meant.

Some truths cost more than they're worth. That was the sentence Silas had built his life inside of. His father had learned it too late to save himself and too early to save anything else. Silas had learned it early enough to make a profession of it. You did not dig up the beam. You did not ask what the village ate. You kept the structure standing, because the structure employed thousands now—miners, railmen, clerks, whole towns that existed only because the lie had been load-bearing. Pull the truth out and you didn't free anybody. You just dropped the building on people who'd never seen the drawings.

That was what he believed. He wanted, very sincerely, to go on believing it.

* * *

Dree Colton put his head inside the tent flap around nine, bringing cold air and the smell of wet wool. "Fire's dead, boss. Weather says we're pinned here till morning."

"Then pin," Silas said, not looking up from the ledger.

Colton lingered, which was unlike him. "That engineer woman," he said. "Dunmore. Word from the waystation is she didn't take your offer."

"She has three days yet."

"She laughed at it, is what I heard. Well—her surveyor laughed. The engineer just looked at you like you were a grade she couldn't be bothered to calculate." Colton scratched his jaw. "Men are starting to ask what's in the case that's worth what you're offering. Questions cost extra, boss. You know that."

"I know that." Silas finally looked up. Colton was a big, simple, decent man in the way of large dogs, and Silas found himself, absurdly, wanting to explain. He did not, of course. Instead he said: "It's land papers. Old ones. Worth more to certain parties than to anyone holding them, provided the holder is sensible."

"And if she isn't sensible?"

Silas turned back to the ledger. "Then she'll have learned what my father learned," he said, "which is that the mountains don't care what's written about them. Only the people do. And the people, Mr. Colton, mostly prefer the story they already have."

Colton considered this, decided it was wisdom because it sounded like it, and went back out into the rain.

Silas sat alone again. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhalation, like the range itself breathing in its sleep.

He opened the ledger to the flyleaf and read his father's last instruction again. *Burn it.* Thirty years, and he had never obeyed it, and he had never disobeyed it either—he had simply carried it, the way you carry a debt you intend to honor someday when circumstances allow. He sometimes wondered whether his father had really wanted it burned, or had only needed to write the words so that some future Vane would know he had known. That the knowledge of the wrong had lived in him, burning him quietly, all those years he'd stood on platforms staring at dry valleys.

If that was what the sentence meant, then Silas was the burning. He was the ledger's interest, compounding.

He hated the woman Dunmore a little for that—for making him do arithmetic he preferred to leave undone. She had the plates. She had a crew of strays and a deadline and, by every report, no sense whatsoever of the forces arranged against her. All he had to do was wait her out, take the papers back, bury the beam one more time, and collect his fee, and the structure would stand another generation, and some other man's son would inherit the ledger and the sentence and the rain.

Unless.

He pulled the waystation report toward him again and read the detail he'd been circling all evening: the engineer's party had engaged a local guide. An old man. Name of Halloway.

Silas set the page down slowly.

Bram Halloway. He knew the name the way you know a scar's origin—secondhand, family legend, a story told once in his father's study with the door shut. A young bore-crew guide, fifty years ago, who had helped cut the sealed tunnel under the pass, and who had been the last man besides Elias Vane to handle the original bore records. His father had mentioned him exactly once, near the end, when the morphine made him loose: *"Halloway saw the true figures. Never said a word. Fifty years, and he never said a word. I never understood whether that made him the best man I ever met or the worst."*

And now Halloway was walking beside the woman who carried the proof, up a mountain Silas knew only from maps, toward a pass Silas had never entered, along routes that existed in exactly one living memory.

Silas rose, went to the tent flap, and looked out at the black shape of the range, invisible now behind the rain, patient as a creditor.

Three days, he had told her. He had meant it as generosity. Standing there, wet wind on his face, he understood with sudden clarity that it had also been a countdown he had no intention of letting run its course—because if they reached the tunnel, if Halloway took them down into the dark where his father's pencil marks still waited in the bore records, then it would stop being a negotiation about papers and become something Silas had spent his whole life making sure never happened: the truth, walking upright, in daylight, with witnesses.

"Dree," he called into the rain. "Wake the men. We move at first light—and somebody find me the old mine road above the switchback. We're done waiting on her answer."

Behind him, on the table, the lamp guttered, and his father's ledger lay open to the flyleaf, and the pencil sentence seemed, in the flicker, almost to move: *burn it, burn it, burn it.*
