## 14. The Tollhouse Revisited

The tide table said they had four hours and eleven minutes.

Yara had checked it three times that morning, the way a gambler checks his cards, as if repetition might change what was written there. The almanac page was soft at the corners from handling, and Bram had watched her fold and unfold it with the particular patience of an old man who has watched many people worry paper.

"Sea doesn't read almanacs," he said now, standing at the top of the shingle with his hands in his coat pockets. "It reads the moon. And the moon, last I looked, keeps her own appointments."

"Then we'd best keep ours," Yara said.

The storm had passed two days before, leaving the coast scrubbed clean and glittering, the air so sharp it felt newly minted. It had also, cruelly or kindly, closed the high pass behind them—snow to the waist on the switchbacks, Vane's party somewhere below or beside or behind, nobody knew—and so here they were, back at the beginning of everything, standing above a beach that only existed for a few hours at a time.

The Tollhouse cave opened its mouth at the base of the headland like something patient. Yara remembered the first time she'd seen it: the sea drawn back farther than she'd thought the sea could go, exposing a floor of dark stone scattered with the debris of decades—shipworm-eaten timbers, a boot sole, bottles worn opaque by salt. The locals called it the Tollhouse because of the stories. Every tollhouse needed admission paid. Nobody agreed on the currency.

Fen came down the shingle first, taking the loose stones at a run because Fen took everything at a run, including meals and opinions. Odo followed more carefully, favoring the leg he'd wrecked on the switchback, still wrapped and splinted but mending the way young things mend—stubbornly and faster than anyone expected. Maren came last, carrying the strongbox's empty twin, which she had insisted on bringing for reasons she hadn't fully explained.

"You're limping less," Yara observed.

"I'm limping *differently*," Odo corrected. "There's an art to it."

"There is not," said Fen.

"There absolutely is. You've got your limp and I've got mine, and mine has better rhythm."

Yara let them argue down the beach ahead of her. It was a sound she'd come to need without noticing—the bickering of the brothers, Maren's dry corrections, Bram's silence that somehow counted as participation. A month ago she would have walked this beach alone, and the loneliness would have fit her like an old coat. Now the coat didn't fit, and she kept catching herself being grateful about it, which was its own kind of strange.

The cave swallowed them one at a time. Inside, the light changed to that underwater green-gray even though the water was a hundred yards off, and their voices went round and hushed, the way voices do in churches and tunnels.

"It's smaller than I remember," Yara said.

"Caves don't shrink," said Bram. "People do."

She shot him a look. He offered nothing back except the ghost of something around his eyes that might, in a generous light, have been humor.

They had left the original plates hidden in the wrecked caboose up-mountain, wrapped in oilcloth inside Odo's spare boot, because Odo had argued—correctly—that no thief alive would steal a boot that smelled like that. But there were other things in the Tollhouse. Things Yara had catalogued hastily on her first visit and then been driven away from by weather and pursuit and the whole accelerating machinery of the journey. She wanted them now. She couldn't have said why, exactly, except that the cave had given her something once, and she had a feeling it wasn't finished giving.

Maren found the second strongbox within the hour.

Not a strongbox, really—a document case, canvas over a light steel frame, the kind surveyors used for field plates when weight mattered. It was wedged deep in a crevice at the rear of the cave, behind a curtain of dried kelp, and it was the kelp that told the story: someone had pulled it aside recently enough that the fronds hadn't fully re-curled. Someone had been here since Yara. Since all of them.

"Bram," Maren said quietly. "Bring the lamp."

Bram brought the lamp. The light fell into the crevice and onto the case, and onto the hand that was still holding it.

Yara heard Fen say "oh" in a very small voice, and then nobody said anything for a long time.

The man had died sitting with his back against the stone, knees drawn up, arms folded over the case as if he'd fallen asleep guarding it. The cold and the salt had done what cold and salt do; he was long past being anything but a shape, a coat, a pair of boots gone stiff as bark. He was maybe fifty. His hands were a workingman's hands, scarred across the knuckles. Around his neck, on a cord, hung a brass tag stamped with a number and a name, the kind rail companies issued to men who worked the lines.

Bram lifted the tag with two fingers and held it to the lamp for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gravel in it that hadn't been there before.

"Hollis Embree."

"You knew him?" Yara asked.

"I knew the name." Bram set the tag gently back against the man's chest. "Embrees were Lowden Hollow people. There was an Embree who drove the water gang on the old spur—my father's era, near enough. This'll be his son, or his grandson." He paused. "This'll be somebody who grew up hearing that his family's land drank dust because of a piece of paper in a company vault."

Odo had turned away. Fen stood very still, which for Fen was remarkable, like watching a river decide to be a lake.

"He came for the plates," Maren said. Her voice was steady in the way Yara had learned meant she was holding herself steady on purpose. "He found them—or found where they were. He got the case open. Look."

She eased the dead man's arms aside, gently, the way you'd move a sleeping colleague, and lifted the case into the lamplight. Inside were more plates—glass negatives in paper sleeves, a dozen of them, and beneath them a leather ledger swollen with damp. And tucked into the ledger's spine, a letter, written in a careful, schoolroom hand on paper gone soft as cloth.

Maren read it aloud. She had to stop twice, not because the words were hard but because they weren't.

---

*"To whoever finds this —*

*I am Hollis Embree, of what used to be Lowden Hollow. My grandmother watched them divert the creek. She was nine years old. She used to tell me how the water went out of the valley in a single summer, and how the company men smiled while it happened, and how her father walked to the county seat three times to file a protest and three times was told the records showed no such village.*

*No such village. As if you could erase a place by losing its paperwork. My grandmother buried two babies the summer the wells went bad, and the company's records say Lowden Hollow never existed.*

*I found where the old surveys went. I have spent eleven years finding it. If you are reading this, I did not get out. The tide is faster than an old man with bad lungs, and I knew that when I came, and I came anyway, because my grandmother deserved to have her village put back on the map before I joined her.*

*Take the case. Take all of it. Don't grieve for me—I had a good life despite everything, and this errand was the best part of it. But take it OUT. That's the whole of my request. Eleven years of looking, and I am asking a stranger for one favor.*

*Pay whatever the toll is. I think I already have.*

*— H.E."*

---

Nobody moved. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself in the faint moving air of the cave, and somewhere far off, the sea breathed against the headland.

"Well," said Fen at last, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, and he didn't try to fix it. "Well, hell."

Odo sat down carefully on a low shelf of rock, favoring his leg, and put his face in his hands for a moment. When he looked up his eyes were wet and furious about it. "Eleven years," he said. "He spent eleven years, and he got all the way to the end, and the *tide*—" He stopped. "That's not right. That's just not right."

"The sea isn't right or wrong," Bram said. "It's just the sea." He crouched by the dead man, slow, joints cracking, and did something Yara didn't expect: he took off his hat. Held it against his chest. "But a man can be right. And this one was."

Yara knelt beside Maren at the case. The plates were intact—every sleeve dry, every image legible under the lamp's edge. Bore records. Diversion schematics. Correspondence with dates and initials she now recognized the way you recognize a signature on a check that bounced. And the ledger, when Maren opened it, was Hollis Embree's own work: eleven years of letters copied, interviews recorded, a grandmother's memories written out in full, dates cross-checked, a whole erased valley rebuilt line by line in ink.

"He didn't just find the proof," Maren murmured. "He *finished* it. This is a complete file. Court-ready. All it ever needed was to leave this cave." She looked up at Yara, and in the green-gray light her face was stripped of all its usual irony. "We can carry it out. We're going through anyway. It costs us nothing but weight."

"That's not the point, though. Is it." Yara heard herself say it and knew it was true. "It's not about whether it costs us anything."

"No," Maren said softly. "I don't suppose it is."

Yara looked at Hollis Embree a long time. At the workingman's hands. At the boots that had walked eleven years of looking. Somewhere in her chest, the old familiar arithmetic was running—the arithmetic of a woman who had spent her whole adult life calculating what things cost, distances, deadlines, the price of stopping versus the price of moving—and for once the arithmetic came out wrong, or rather it came out honest. Some debts you don't get to decline just because the creditor is dead. Some errands transfer.

"We bury him properly," she said. "Above the tideline. With a marker. Then we take the case, and we finish his errand, and we put Lowden Hollow back on the map if it's the last thing any of us does." She paused. "Which, given our luck lately, it may well be."

A ragged little laugh went around the cave, the kind grief makes when it needs somewhere to go.

* * *

Digging in shingle is a fool's occupation, and they were four fools and a guide at it. The soil above the storm line was thin and full of stones, and Odo's leg limited him to supervising, which he performed with the enthusiasm of a foreman who has never once picked up a shovel. They laid Hollis Embree facing the sea, because Bram said the Embrees had always been a water family and would want to hear it even now, especially now. Fen found a flat stone and cut the name into it with his belt knife, crooked but deep:

**HOLLIS EMBREE — LOWDEN HOLLOW — ERRAND FINISHED BY FRIENDS**

"Friends," Odo repeated quietly, after. "We only met him today."

"We've known him longer than that," Fen said. "Everybody on this mountain's known him longer than that. We just didn't have his name till now."

Bram said a few words—not scripture, nothing formal, just names, a list of Embrees stretching back into his own memory, delivered like a roll call until the whole vanished village seemed briefly to stand there on the bluff with them, nine-year-old grandmothers and water-gang drivers and babies lost to bad wells, all of them present, all of them accounted for. Then he put his hat back on, and it was done.

They divided the weight. The case went into a waterproof wrap, and the wrap went against Yara's own pack, because she was the strongest climber and everyone knew it and nobody argued, which was itself a small miracle. The ledger Maren carried inside her coat, against her body, for warmth. The letter they read twice more by firelight that night, and Odo copied it out in his own hand into the crew's logbook, "so it exists in two places, in case the mountain gets ideas."

Yara took the last watch. The others slept in a row like spoons against the caboose's broken stove, and she sat in the doorway with the case between her boots and the stars doing their indifferent wheeling overhead, and she thought about tolls.

Every story about this cave agreed you had to pay. The fishermen said it was a coin. The old-timers said it was a truth you'd rather keep. Hollis Embree had paid with eleven years and, in the end, everything. And she supposed—sitting there, listening to her strange, stubborn, borrowed family breathe in their sleep—that she had been paying too, all along, without knowing the cave had opened for her. Miles. Blood. The bridge. The years of motion. Maybe admission was simply this: you gave the mountain what you'd been hoarding, and it let you pass carrying something heavier than you started with.

Toward dawn, Bram appeared beside her with two cups of something hot and terrible, handed her one, and sat.

"You knew him," she said. "Or you knew of him. You went quiet at the cave's name the day I hired you, and you've known too much about these tunnels from the start. How?"

Bram drank. Took his time. When he answered, he looked at the sea, not at her.

"Because fifty years ago, I was the company man who signed the diversion order," he said. "Young, ambitious, told it was standard practice. I told myself the valley was dying anyway. I told myself a lot of things." He turned the cup in his hands. "Hollis Embree's grandmother spat at my boots once, in the county seat. Rightest woman I ever met. So no—we won't finish his errand because it's convenient, girl. We'll finish it because some of us owe it with interest."

Yara looked at the old man, at the ruin and the repair of him, and found—to her surprise—that she didn't feel betrayed. She felt like she finally understood why he'd come.

"Then we'd better make sure the interest gets paid," she said.

Below them, far down the coast, a light moved along the shoreline road. Small. Deliberate. Stopping at intervals, the way men do when they are searching rather than traveling.

Vane's people. Closer than the storm had promised.

Bram saw it too. He drained his cup and rose, joints announcing themselves.

"Tide turns in an hour," he said. "And it seems we're not the only ones keeping appointments."

Yara wrapped the case tight against her back and woke the others, and the strays came awake the way they always did now—all at once, together, reaching first for each other and only then for their gear—which was, she thought, its own kind of proof of what they'd become.

The errand was no longer theirs alone. It never had been. Somewhere below, the searchers' light swung toward the headland, and the sea began, patiently, to come back for its cave.
