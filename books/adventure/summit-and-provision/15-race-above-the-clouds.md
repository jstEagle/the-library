## 15. Race Above the Clouds

The storm came down off the pass behind them like a debt collector, and Fen Dunmore—no relation to Yara, though he'd started saying *cousin* around the fire two nights back and nobody had corrected him—had never in his life run so fast toward something he couldn't see.

"Handcar!" Bram had shouted it from the mouth of the old ridge spur, and for a moment Fen thought the old man had finally cracked. There was no handcar. There was a derelict line that hadn't seen a wheel since before Fen was born, a ribbon of rust stitched along the spine of the Karst Range with nothing under it but cloud.

Then Odo, limping but game, hauled back a drift of pine needles and there it was: a four-wheeled handcar, tarped under a rock overhang where some track crew had left it fifty years ago, as if they'd only stepped away for lunch. The wood was gray as driftwood. One handle was missing its grip. But the frame was sound, and when Maren pressed down on the lever arm and the whole rig rocked on its axles, she let out a laugh that sounded like it surprised her.

"Well," she said. "Somebody's lucky day."

"Somebody's," said Yara, looking at the sky.

* * *

Fen had walked track his whole working life—the dead spurs, the closed branches, the lines the company starved out one by one until the rails went quiet and the ties went soft. He knew what a bad rail felt like through his boots. This line was bad in the way an old dog is bad: mostly faithful, liable to surprise you, and too stubborn to die.

He rode the front corner of the car with the brake lever in his fist, which was the job nobody wanted and everybody understood. Ahead of him the ridge ran narrow as a knife's back, the rails crowned with frost, and beyond the rails on either side the world simply stopped being there. Cloud filled the valleys like milk in a bowl. Somewhere below and to the east, parallel to them, hidden by that milk, Vane's party was climbing the switchback road with their mules and their rifles and their patience.

"They'll see us against the sky," Odo said. He sat across from Fen, his splinted leg propped on the tool box, holding the spare handle like a lance.

"They'll see weather," Bram said from the rear seat, where he'd installed himself beside the document case with the tenderness of a man watching a sleeping child. "We're a smudge. We're a rumor."

"We're a rumor going downhill at speed," Maren said, "which is my favorite kind of rumor." She took the near handle, Yara the far, and they began to pump, and the handcar woke up with a groan like an old man getting out of a chair he'd sat in too long.

The first hundred yards were argument. The wheels found their song—one flat note, one sharp—and the frost squeaked under the flanges, and then all at once the grade tipped their way and the argument became agreement and the agreement became flight.

Fen whooped. He wasn't proud of it later. Actually, he was.

* * *

Here is what Fen knew about daylight, because every track-walker knows it: you don't get hours, you get a budget. And this high on the range in late autumn, with the sun already leaning west and the storm chewing the pass behind them, the budget was thin. Four hours of usable light, maybe five if the clouds stayed torn open, and after that the ridge would be a place where mistakes were made permanently.

Yara had laid it out at dawn with a stick in the ash of the cookfire. "Vane's party has to come around by the switchback road. That's six miles of climbing for maybe three of our rail. If we hold better than walking pace, we beat them to the trestle by an hour. Maybe two."

"And if we don't?" Odo had asked.

"Then we have a conversation with armed men on a narrow bridge," Yara said, "and I've had enough conversations lately that ended with somebody pointing at me."

So now they pumped, and Fen counted.

That was his other skill, the one nobody hired for: counting. Ties per chain. Seconds between lightning and thunder. He counted their pace aloud without meaning to, a low mutter—"twelve, thirteen, fourteen"—and Maren caught the rhythm of it and matched her strokes to his numbers, and within a mile the four of them had become one machine with four opinions.

"Curve," Fen called, and braked, and the car swung around a shoulder of granite with the cloud boiling past close enough to wet their faces.

"Grade easing," Yara called back, reading the rail the way other people read letters.

"Something ahead," Bram said quietly, and everyone pumped harder instead of slower, which told Fen more about these people than any campfire confession ever had.

* * *

The something ahead was a washout. Not a big one—a bite taken out of the roadbed where a spring had undermined the fill, leaving a gap of maybe eight feet with the rails hanging in air like whiskers.

They stood over it, breathing steam, while the wind tried to take their hats as payment for stopping.

"I can jump it," Fen said.

"You can jump it," Maren agreed. "The car cannot."

"So we carry it."

Odo laughed, then winced, then laughed again anyway, which Fen had learned was just how Odo processed things now. "Carry it. Sure. It weighs as much as a church pew full of deacons."

"It weighs less than regret," Bram said, "and we've all carried that uphill." And he set down the document case, took off his coat, folded it with terrible neatness, and picked up the front end of the handcar like it owed him money.

They carried it across the gap in ninety seconds of grunting, swearing, and one moment when the whole rig tilted toward the void and Yara said, very calmly, "No," as if the mountain were a misbehaving child. Then it was down on good rail on the far side, and they were aboard and moving before their hearts had settled, because the light was spending itself whether they moved or not, and Fen's arithmetic said spend it moving.

* * *

Twice more the cloud tore open, and twice they saw them: Vane's party on the switchback road below, small as toys, mules strung out like beads, and once—this was the part Fen kept turning over afterward—a single figure standing still on the road, looking up at them. Not raising anything. Just looking.

"That'll be Vane himself," Bram said. "Counting us."

"What's he count?"

"Four of us and a case. Same as we count. He's deciding whether the arithmetic favors him."

"And?"

"And the light's going," Bram said, "for both of us. That's the cruelty of it. We're not racing each other anymore. We're racing the same clock, and only one of us gets to be late."

Fen looked at the sun, a pale coin dissolving into the western cloud, and did his numbers, and didn't like them, and said nothing, because Odo was watching him with the look of a younger brother who could read his counting face like a broadsheet.

"We're ahead," Fen said.

"How far ahead?" Odo asked.

"Yes," Fen said.

* * *

The third hour was the best hour of Fen's life, and he'd have said so out loud if anyone had asked, which nobody did, because everyone was busy.

The ridge line straightened along a crest the old builders had cut with more nerve than sense, and the grade fell away in long honest sweeps, and the handcar simply flew. Frost smoked off the rails. The cloud below turned gold underneath where the sunset got into it, so they were running on a road above a sea of burning milk, and Odo—who had been silent with pain for an hour—started singing. Some railroad nonsense about a brakeman's sweetheart, tuneless and joyful, and Maren joined in on a harmony that was technically wrong and spiritually correct, and even Bram's mouth did something at the corner that on another man would have been called a smile.

Fen pumped and counted and thought: *this*. This was the thing he'd never been able to say to anyone, the thing about track-walking that wasn't loneliness. That a line, even a dead one, even a forgotten one, still connected its two ends. That somewhere ahead there was always a station, even if the station was just a question mark. That motion was a kind of hope you could do with your arms.

And for the first time in his life he noticed he wasn't thinking it alone. He glanced back—bad idea, wonderful idea—and saw Yara watching the golden cloud with an expression he'd never seen on her, unguarded as a door left open, and he understood that whatever happened at the end of this rail, she'd already gotten somewhere.

"Water tower!" he shouted instead, because the curve was coming and sentiment doesn't brake.

* * *

The water tower was a skeleton, its tank long collapsed into a wooden ribcage, and beyond it the line bent west along the crest toward a black notch in the ridge that Fen's map-memory said was the trestle. Their trestle. The crossing.

Beyond the notch, the last of the light lay on a distant shoulder of land, and on that shoulder, tiny with distance, sat a blocky shape with a lit window.

"The county road depot," Maren said. "Old stage stop. They'll have gone to ground there for the night."

"Or they're already past us," Odo said, "and that's their lantern."

"No," Bram said. He was looking not at the light but at the road below, where the bead-string of mules had stopped. Where men were making camp in the blue shadow, small cook-smoke rising straight up in the windless cold. "They're done for the day. Cold stops them same as dark. But it won't stop us if we don't let it."

Everyone looked at him.

"There's a section house at the trestle," Bram said. "Stone. Roof's good—I put the roof on." A pause. "In another life."

"Then we sleep warm," Yara said, "and we cross at first light with an hour on them."

"An hour," Fen said, doing the arithmetic, "if nothing goes wrong in the night."

Maren snorted. "You're new here, aren't you?"

But they pumped on into the blue hour, and the stars came out hard and close above the ridge, and the notch with its trestle came on slow and black, and Fen counted the last chains of the day like a man counting his own heartbeat, grateful for every one.

It was Odo who saw it first. He'd gone quiet—not pained-quiet, thinking-quiet—and then he said, "Fen. The rails ahead. Look at the frost."

Fen looked. The rails running toward the trestle gleamed white with frost, all of them, the whole length—

Except one stretch, maybe forty yards out, where the rails shone bare and dark, as if something had recently passed over them. Or as if something had recently been done to them.

He put his hand on the brake and held it, and the handcar sang on into the dark, faster now, downhill, toward the trestle and the stone section house and the forty yards of rail that somebody, sometime today, had touched.

"* * *" he almost said, and didn't, because the real separator was coming whether he spoke it or not: the space between the day they'd had and the night they were about to.

"Fen," Odo said quietly. "How far ahead did you say we were?"

"Yes," Fen said, and braked.
