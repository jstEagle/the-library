## 16. Betrayal on the Trestle

Maren Cole had spent thirty years learning to read the lies in other people's numbers, so it should not have surprised her that she could also read the lie in a man's shoulders. But it did. It always did. That was the curse of being wrong about people once, publicly, catastrophically: you either stopped trusting anyone at all, or you trusted them too hard to prove you'd learned your lesson.

She had chosen the second path somewhere on the mountain, around the time Fen taught Odo to whistle through a blade of grass and Yara laughed for the first time since Maren had known her. She'd chosen to believe the strays were hers.

Which is why, on the morning they reached the Tarnwater trestle, she was watching Hollis Quill's hands instead of his face.

Hollis had joined them at the staging town — or rather, he'd *been there*, already hired by Yara in one of her brisk, don't-think-about-it decisions, because the man claimed to know the ridge lines and owned a mule nobody else wanted. He was competent. He was pleasant. He laughed at Fen's jokes and shared his tobacco with Bram, who accepted it without a word, which from Bram was practically an embrace.

And every night, Maren noticed, Hollis wrote nothing down but somehow always knew exactly where they'd camped the next day before Yara said it aloud.

She told herself she was imagining it. She told herself this was what happened to disgraced surveyors: they saw fraud in every contour line and treachery in every cup of coffee. Then she checked the mule's panniers while Hollis was washing in the creek, and found a company signal lamp, a coil of green fuse wire, and a copy of the Karst concession maps annotated in a neat, expensive hand.

Not Vane's hand. A subordinate's. Which meant Hollis wasn't the fixer. He was the fixer's instrument, and instruments got left behind when they broke.

* * *

The Tarnwater trestle was a hundred and forty feet of blackened timber strung across a gorge that the river had been patiently unmaking since before anyone thought to name it. The ridge line they needed ran along its far side; below it, half a mile down and paralleling their route, Vane's party moved like a line of dark stitches through the snow. Everyone could see everyone. It had become, over two days, a kind of horrible race conducted in full view, both parties pretending not to look.

"Deck's rotten in three places," Yara announced, crouched at the near abutment, tapping boards with her knuckle like a doctor percussing a chest. "We cross single file, light as we can. Odo, you're carrying the case strapped front, not back — if you go down I want the plates landing on top of you, not under."

"That's very touching," said Odo.

"It's practical."

"It's touching *and* practical. I'm going to have it engraved."

Fen snorted. Bram stood at the rail end, looking down into the gorge with the expression of a man greeting an old dog he wasn't sure would remember him. "I helped spike this in 'sixty-one," he said. "Timber came up the lake on barges. Cost two men their fingers and one his whole arm." He paused. "It was good timber then."

Maren heard it then — or rather, she heard the absence of it. The morning wind should have been moaning through the trusses. Instead there was a low, wrong hum, like a struck tuning fork buried in the wood.

"Hold," she said.

Yara looked up. "What?"

"The vibration's off." Maren put her palm flat against the abutment beam. The hum traveled up her arm, thin and continuous. "A trestle this age groans. It doesn't *sing*. Something's changed the tension." Her eyes went down the length of the span, board by board, chord by chord, doing the thing she did — the thing that had made her famous and then infamous, the reading of structures for the story they told about the hands that built or broke them. Two-thirds of the way across, at the third tower bent, one of the lower lateral braces sat a quarter-inch proud of its shoe plate. Freshly proud. Proud the way wood sits proud after someone has driven a wedge behind it and hammered the fastenings loose.

"There," she said quietly. "Third bent. Someone's cut the lateral bracing and wedged it so it looks whole. It'll hold a man walking soft. It will not hold five men and a mule in sequence with any rhythm to them." She turned. "Where's Hollis?"

The answer arrived as a sound: the mule's bell, ringing from upstream, receding.

"He went to water the animal," Fen said slowly. "Twenty minutes ago."

"Before we crossed," Maren said. "He asked to water it *before* we crossed, and nobody thought anything of it, because watering a mule is the most innocent sentence in the world." She was already moving along the bank, anger arriving in her chest like weather. "That's how it works. That's how it *always* works. Nobody sabotages anything with a sinister face on. They do it during chores."

* * *

They caught Hollis a mile upstream, or rather Bram caught him, which surprised no one. The old guide simply walked faster than a fleeing man expected a seventy-year-old to walk, stepped out of the alders onto the trail ahead of him, and held up one hand, and Hollis stopped as though he'd hit glass.

"I'd like my mule back," Bram said.

"She's a good mule," Hollis said. His voice was steady. His eyes went past Bram's shoulder to where the rest of them had come up — Yara with her arms crossed, Fen and Odo flanking, Maren last, carrying his panniers, which she set down between them with a deliberate clank of signal lamp against stone.

"Green fuse wire," Maren said. "Company lamp. Annotated concession maps in a clerk's fist — Third District office, if I had to guess, and I usually do." She crouched and began laying the items out on a flat rock like a merchant displaying wares. "You loosened the third bent's laterals and wedged them. Sloppy work, incidentally. You left the wedge proud. Any first-year could read it."

"I didn't—"

"You did," said Odo, and there was something in his voice Maren hadn't heard before — not the jokester, not the younger brother, but a track-walker whose job had once been walking twenty miles of rail every day and knowing instantly when a single fishplate had been tampered with. Track-walkers died when men like Hollis got lazy. "I walked the section out of Corben Junction for six years. We lost a ganger to a shifted switch some gentleman saboteur thought was funny. So don't."

Hollis looked at them all, and something in him seemed to decide the performance was over, the way an actor's face goes slack when the house lights come up. "Vane pays well," he said simply. "And he only asked me to slow you. Not hurt anyone. The trestle would've held if you crossed one at a time and waited between."

"It would've held until it didn't," Maren snapped. "That's the entire art of your trade, isn't it? Structures that hold *until they don't*. Reports that are true *until they're read closely*. You people deal in the gap between the moment and the failure, and you never have to stand in the gorge when the moment arrives."

"Maren." Yara's voice was quiet. Maren realized her own had risen, and that her hands were shaking, and that thirty years of professional disgrace had just come up her throat wearing today's face. She breathed. She picked up the signal lamp.

"What happens to me now?" Hollis asked. Genuinely curious, almost. Men like him never believed consequences applied to the instrument.

Yara considered him for a long moment. Below them, faint in the gorge haze, Vane's line of dark stitches kept moving, gaining.

"You're going to walk back down the way you came," she said, "and you're going to tell Silas Vane that the survey crew crossed the Tarnwater trestle in good order and gained four hours on him. And then you're going to keep walking south, and if I ever see your face again I'll let Bram decide what happens, and Bram has been very quiet lately in a way that worries even me."

Bram said nothing, which was somehow worse than anything he could have said.

"And the mule stays with us," Fen added. "She likes us better anyway. We've got better personalities."

Hollis looked at the mule. The mule looked at Hollis with the flat ancient contempt of her kind, and turned her head away to browse the alders, which settled the matter more thoroughly than any argument.

He went. They watched him shrink down the trail toward the gorge road, toward Vane, carrying whatever message he chose to carry, because that was the other truth about instruments: you couldn't control what note they'd sound. Maren found her jaw aching and realized she'd been clenching it since the creek.

"Well," said Odo into the silence. "I feel betrayed, vindicated, and slightly hungry, in that order."

"Lunch," said Yara, "and then we cross that bridge like our lives depend on it, because they might."

* * *

They crossed in ninety minutes, one at a time, Maren going first because she could read the deck fastest, calling holds and weight-shifts like a conductor. Bram refused the offer to go last and went second, saying someone ought to check his old work from the inside. At the third bent he stopped, laid his palm on the wedged brace exactly where Maren had, and stayed there a long moment with his eyes closed, as if listening for the two men who'd lost their fingers in 'sixty-one.

"They'd have hated this," he said finally. "Come on."

Odo crossed fourth, the document case strapped to his chest, and it was Odo — light-footed, joking, halfway across and waving at Fen like an idiot — when the mountain decided it hadn't finished with the morning.

The report came from above the far rim: a crack, then a deeper sound, the sound of a slope letting go of something it had held for centuries. Rockfall. Not large — a shoulder of frost-shattered granite the size of a wagon, maybe two — but it took the trail on the far side and, more importantly, it took the trestle's far anchorage with it, or rather shook it, and the whole span shivered like a plucked string.

Maren felt it through her boots and knew before the sound finished. "Off the deck! Get off the—"

The third bent, the one Hollis had gutted, gave up its pretense. The wedged brace snapped with a report like a rifle, and the tower folded inward with a shriek of iron shoes tearing free of timber, and the deck under Odo dropped a foot and tilted, and Odo — quick, lucky Odo, who had survived whiteouts and switchbacks and Fen's cooking — went down onto his knees with the case hugged to his chest and slid.

He didn't scream. That was the thing Maren remembered afterward, the thing she'd carry: he didn't scream. He just said, conversationally, "Oh, bother," and grabbed for a stringer with one hand while the case's strap tore at his shoulder, and the gorge opened beneath him, a hundred feet of gray air and the river a silver thread at the bottom of it.

Fen was moving before anyone could shout. Later, Maren could never reconstruct how — he went out along the tilted deck on his belly, fast, in the exact path she'd have forbidden, and got one wrist around his brother's ankle as Odo's grip tore free of the stringer. The deck lurched again. Fen slid six inches. His boot heel caught the lip of a broken plank and held, absurdly, miraculously, and there they hung: the older brother holding the younger by the ankle above a hundred feet of nothing, both of them silent now, both of them breathing like bellows.

"Rope!" Yara was already throwing the coil off her shoulder, paying it out. "Bram, anchor point — the near stanchion, wrap it twice!"

Bram wrapped it twice. Maren tied the bowline because her hands, traitorous things, were the steadiest ones present, and she understood suddenly that this was why she was here — not the disgrace, not the redemption arc she'd privately been composing for herself, but this: someone had to know knots while everyone else was busy being brave.

They hauled. Fen climbed hand over hand up the rope with Odo's weight dragging at his ankle, and it took four minutes that Maren measured afterward by the marks her fingernails left in her own palms, and then they were both on the intact section of deck, and then both on solid rock, and Fen was laughing and swearing in equal measure and hitting his brother's shoulder, and Odo was saying "bother" again, dazed, with the case still strapped to his chest and one strap torn clean through.

"Let me see it," Maren said, kneeling. "The case, not your ego."

Inside, the plates had held. Forty years of falsified surveys, wrapped in oilcloth, unscratched. She checked each one with trembling thoroughness while Odo's wrist was bound and Fen refused, twice, to let go of his brother's sleeve.

But outside — outside, the arithmetic had changed. The trestle's far side was gone; the ridge trail beyond it was buried under fresh rock; and the detour Bram traced with a stick in the dirt added most of a day. Most of a day, with Vane's party below them no longer pretending not to look, and the storm line sitting on the northern peaks like a patient creditor.

"We've lost the lead," Yara said quietly. She wasn't angry. That was the frightening part. She stood looking at the ruined span with the wind pulling at her coat, and Maren watched her do the same arithmetic and arrive at the same ugly sum.

Then Odo, white-faced, leg bleeding through the bandage, held up the document case and grinned.

"But we've got the plates," he said, "and Vane's got a mule-less informant telling him we're four hours ahead of where we actually are. Hollis doesn't know about the rockslide. As far as his report goes, we're flying." He winced as Fen tightened the bandage. "So really, the betrayal cost him more than it cost us. Somebody write that down. Maren, write that down, you're the one with the pencil."

Maren wrote it down. And underneath it, in the small private ledger she kept behind her ribs, she entered something else: *Trust tested. Repaired by action.* Because that was the lesson of the day, the one thirty years of disgrace had nearly made her miss — that trust wasn't a structure you inspected once and certified. It was a trestle you walked every day, and repaired together, and sometimes carried each other across on your belly with your heart in your teeth.

"Can you walk?" Yara asked Odo.

"I can limp beautifully."

"Then we move now," Yara said, turning north, where the storm had begun, very softly, to snow. "Because Bram's about to tell us about a tunnel, and I have a feeling we're going to need it."
