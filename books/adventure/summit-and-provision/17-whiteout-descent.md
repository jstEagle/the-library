## 17. Whiteout Descent

The whiteout came down on them like a lid being set on a pot, and Bram Halloway, who had spent seventy-one years learning the difference between weather that meant harm and weather that merely meant business, knew at once that this was the first kind.

He heard it before he saw it, which was saying something, because he could barely see his own boots. A sound like the mountain drawing a long breath through its teeth. The wind had been at their backs all morning, pushing them down the shoulder of the pass, and then it stopped pushing and started circling, and the snow that had been falling in polite, individual flakes began falling sideways, and then it stopped being snow at all and became simply *white* — a white that had no top and no bottom, no near and no far, a white you could drown in.

"Hold!" he shouted, and put his hand out behind him, and felt Fen's mitt land in it, and Fen's other hand on Odo's shoulder, Odo on Maren, Maren on Yara. The rope between them went taut. Six souls strung together like beads on a wire, standing still on a slope that wanted very much for them to keep going, in the wrong direction, all the way down.

"Can you see anything?" Yara shouted against the wind. Her voice came from somewhere to his left, or maybe above him. In a whiteout, sound lied too.

"I can see the inside of my own eyelids," Bram said, "and frankly that's more than I want."

Fen made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by terror. Good. A laugh was a rudder. Bram had learned that on these very slopes, fifty years ago, from a foreman named Aldous Pike who could keep a tunnel crew calm in a rockfall by telling jokes so bad the men wanted to live purely out of spite.

"Everyone stay roped," he said. "Nobody moves until I say. And nobody," he added, "asks me how I know what I'm about to do."

There was a pause. Then Maren's voice, dry as old paper: "We were all going to ask that."

"I know," Bram said. "That's why I said it."

* * *

Here was the truth Bram Halloway had been carrying down the mountain in his chest like a stone in a boot: he had walked this descent before. Not this exact slope — no man had walked this exact slope, because the mountain rearranged itself every winter like a restless sleeper — but this *line*. The company had called it the Halloway Route, for eleven years, before they called it nothing at all. He had helped cut it. He had run the first survey stake down it at nineteen years old, with frostbite in two fingers and a head full of wages. He had helped build the snowsheds, the culverts, the switchbacks, and the tunnel. And then he had helped seal the tunnel, and helped bury the survey, and helped erase a village from the record, and after that he had spent fifty years being very careful never to come back, because a man can outrun almost anything except the sound of his own name.

The whiteout didn't care about any of that. The whiteout was simply the mountain's oldest trick: take away the eyes, and see what a man is made of.

He stood in the roaring white and listened.

That was the thing Yara had never quite understood about him, though he'd seen her trying to work it out for weeks, the way she worked out grades and load tables, patient and thorough. Everyone thought the old guide navigated by memory — by maps drawn in the head, landmarks filed away like freight manifests. And he did, some. But memory was a fair-weather friend. What you could trust, what you could trust in a whiteout on a mountain that had spent fifty years trying to forget you, was *water*.

Water remembered everything.

Water had carved these slopes before there was a company, before there was a railroad, before there was a Bram Halloway, and it went on carving, indifferent to permits and concessions and the signatures of important men. Every gully, every culvert, every hidden spring on this descent had a voice, and Bram had spent his boyhood and his manhood listening to them, and fifty years of silence had not made him deaf.

"Untie the sled from Fen," he shouted. "We leave it. Take what you can carry and nothing more."

"We can't leave the—" Odo started.

"We can and we will. The mountain keeps what it's given. Consider it a donation."

There was a scramble behind him, the small desperate sounds of people repacking their lives into pockets. Then Yara's hand found his shoulder and squeezed once. *Lead,* the squeeze said. She had stopped asking him to explain himself somewhere around the third day out, and he had come to love her for it the way you love a door that doesn't squeak.

"Rope tight," he said. "We go down. When I stop, you stop. When I say left, you go left. And if I say run — well. Pray I don't say run."

* * *

He took them down by sound.

The first hour was the worst, because the first hour was all trust and no evidence. He walked with his hood half back — Maren scolded him for it, in the tone of a woman who had lost too many colleagues to frostbite to be polite about it — and he turned his head from side to side like an old hound, listening under the wind.

There. Under the roar: a thin whisper, off to the right. A seep, a thread of meltwater running under the snow crust, finding its old channel. He remembered that whisper. It had run beside the third switchback, and the company had piped it into a culvert, and he had helped lay the pipe, and his foreman had bought the crew a round of whiskey off the savings.

"Left," he said, and turned them left, and thirty paces on his leading boot found the edge of the old roadbed — a terrace in the slope, flat as a table, where no natural slope made tables. The grade of a railroad is a thing water doesn't make. His heart gave a thump that had nothing to do with exertion.

"Roadbed," he called back. "Company cut. We're on the Halloway Route."

"You mean the route named after *you*?" Fen called. "Because that seems like a suspicious coincidence, Mr. Halloway."

"It wasn't named for my good looks, boy."

"It wasn't named for your good looks," Fen agreed solemnly.

Even in the white, even with the cold working into his bones like a debt collector, Bram felt something loosen in his chest. Fifty years he had carried this route like a confession. Now he was walking it in daylight — well, whitelight — with five people who needed him to know it, and every step that went *right* was a small payment on an enormous account.

* * *

They came to the first snowshed at what he judged to be midafternoon, though in a whiteout the sun was a rumor and time went soft. The shed was a timber tunnel built to shrug avalanches off the track, and it was mostly collapsed, but its spine held, and under its broken roof the wind dropped away all at once, like a shout behind a slammed door.

The sudden quiet was enormous. Bram heard his own breath, and five other breaths, and under everything, faint and constant, the voice of water.

Maren pulled back her hood and looked at him with the expression she reserved for survey data that didn't match the official record. "You've been navigating by *sound*," she said. "This whole time."

"Water doesn't lie," Bram said. "Water was here before the company, and it'll be here after. It's the only honest surveyor on this mountain."

Maren stared at him for a long moment, and then she did something he had never seen her do: she laughed, short and real, and sat down hard on a fallen beam. "Fifty years I've been drawing lines on paper," she said, "and the whole time the truth was running around loose under the snow, making noise."

"That's the trouble with the company," Bram said. "They thought a buried survey stayed buried. But water keeps the minutes of every meeting." He looked down at his boots. "I should know. I helped them try to shut it up."

Nobody said anything. He hadn't meant to say it. But the snowshed held them all in its broken ribs like a cupped hand, and the water talked underneath, and the truth came up the way water does — finding the low places.

Yara crouched in front of him, her frost-rimed lashes and her steady eyes. "The tunnel under the pass," she said quietly. "You helped build it. And you helped seal it."

"I did both," Bram said. "In the same year. That was the year I stopped being a good man and started being a paid one." He worked his jaw. "There's a spring above Lowden Hollow that used to feed the village well. The company's tunnel cut it. Diverted it into their flumes. The village went dry, and the survey that would have proved the water was theirs got buried, and I carried the shovel." He met Yara's eyes. "I've been listening to that water for fifty years, Miss Dunmore. Every night, near enough. It doesn't shout. It just keeps saying the same thing, over and over, patient as anything. *Lowden. Lowden. Lowden.*"

Fen, at the mouth of the shed, had gone very still. Odo put a hand on his brother's shoulder.

"So when you found those plates in the Tollhouse," Bram went on, "and you came asking for a guide over the range, and I went quiet at the name of that cave — it wasn't the cave that quieted me." He blew out a breath that steamed and shredded in the wind beyond the shed. "It was the errand. I've been waiting fifty years for somebody to ask me to carry it. I just never had the nerve to volunteer."

Yara put her hand over his mittened fist. Her glove was wet and freezing and he felt it like a brand.

"You're volunteering now," she said.

"I'm volunteering now," he agreed. "At seventy-one. With a whiteout for a ceiling and a cliff for a floor. That's the Halloway way — always early for the train, always late for the reckoning."

* * *

They moved again when the light began to fail — or when the white began to deepen toward grey, which was the only clock they had. The second hour of the descent was kinder to them, because the roadbed held. Bram took them along the old grade, through two more broken snowsheds, past a culvert he had built with his own hands at twenty-three and greeted now like an old friend he'd wronged. "Hello, you ugly thing," he muttered, patting its frozen lip. "Still holding. Better than I did."

The water voices multiplied as they dropped. That was the rule of this mountain, and he had known it at nineteen and known it now: go down, and the water gathers. Threads became rivulets, rivulets became a mutter, the mutter became a conversation, and every tributary had its own accent, its own pitch, and Bram stood in the roaring white with his head cocked like a man at a crowded station, hearing trains that hadn't run in fifty years.

"Left here," he said, at a place where there was nothing to see at all. "There's a cut in the ridge. If we miss it we slide four hundred feet into the Greywater."

"And if we make it?" Odo asked.

"Then we slide four hundred feet into the Greywater on purpose, further down, where there's a bridge. The mountain gives you options. It just doesn't label them."

They made the cut. He knew it before he saw it, by the way the wind changed — a whiteout has a texture, and a cut in a ridge feels like a doorway — and then his boot found the cut floor and the rope went slack with five people exhaling at once.

It was Fen who said it, half to himself, in the doorway of the wind: "He's doing it off the *sound of the water*." A pause. "Mr. Halloway, you're the most useful old man I've ever met, and my grandmother could find a needle in a snowdrift."

"Your grandmother," Bram said, "sounds like a woman I'd have liked to drink with."

"She'd have liked that. She drank with everybody. Once she drank with a bear."

"That was probably my uncle," Bram said. "Family resemblance is strong."

* * *

The last stretch came at full dark, and this was the part Bram had been dreading, and the part he had been waiting for, all at once.

Below the cut, the old grade ended. It had always ended — the company had run out of money and nerve at the Greywater gorge and built the great trestle on the far side instead, and the middle stretch of the Halloway Route was a steep, timbered slope that no map had ever drawn honestly. In clear weather it was a hard descent. In a whiteout, in the dark, it should have been impossible.

But the Greywater was down there. He could hear it.

Fifty years, and it sounded the same. That was the thing that stopped him at the grade's end, rope in hand, snow packing into every seam of his clothing, and made him stand and listen like a boy. The Greywater fell through the gorge in a long, broken cascade — three steps of water, the old crews called them — and each step had its own voice, and the middle step had a hollow *whoomp* where the river went under an overhang of stone, a sound like the mountain's own heartbeat, and Bram had fallen asleep to that sound for six years of his life and had not heard it since, and hearing it now, in the dark, in the white, with five people roped to him and trusting him, he found that his eyes were stinging, and he chose to believe it was the wind.

"Mr. Halloway?" Yara's hand on his arm. Gentle. She knew. She was the kind of person who knew.

"I'm all right," he said. "I'm just — paying a call." He cleared his throat. "Listen. You hear it? Middle step. The *whoomp*."

They all went quiet. Six people standing on a mountainside in the dark, listening to a river none of them could see.

"I hear it," Maren said at last, wonder in her voice. "It's like — it's like something breathing down there."

"That's the mountain's heart," Bram said. "And it beats on the true line. Fifty years I've been away, and it never once skipped." He wiped his face with the back of his mitt, an old man's small vanity, and squared himself. "We go down on the sound. I'll call the line. Left of the cascade there's a timber stair the company built for the maintenance crews — it'll be rotten as old fruit, but it's *there*, and it's a line, and a line is all a body needs. We go slow. We go together. And when we reach the river—" he paused, and let himself say it, the thing he had come down the whole mountain saying in his head like a prayer he didn't believe in until now, when he believed it completely— "when we reach the river, we'll be below the snowline, and below the company's maps, and one night's walk from the county seat."

He took the rope in both fists.

"One more thing," he said. "When we get down — when we file those papers, if we file those papers — there's a spring above Lowden Hollow that's been running the wrong way for fifty years. When it's turned back, I want to be there. I want to hear it come back down the old channel." His voice went rough. "A man ought to hear the thing he helped silence start talking again."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Odo, of all people, the quiet one, the one with the bad leg and the worse luck, put his mitt on Bram's shoulder from behind, the way Bram had been steadying all of them all day.

"You'll be there," Odo said simply. "We'll all be there. That's how this crew works."

Bram nodded, because his voice had gone off to be alone somewhere, and turned down the slope, into the dark, into the white, following the sound of water that had never once stopped calling his name.

He was three steps into the timber stair, calling the line — "left, mind the post, *left*" — when Fen, at the rear, said sharply:

"Mr. Halloway. There's a light."

Bram froze. Below them, far down through the roaring white, down where the Greywater gorge opened toward the valley and the valley ran to the county seat — a light. Moving. Careful, deliberate, swinging the way a lantern swings in a hand that knows the dark and doesn't like it.

And behind it, faint but unmistakable, carried up on the wind: the sound of more than one set of boots on frozen ground.

"Vane's men," Maren breathed. "They came down the gorge. They're *ahead* of us."

Bram stood on the rotten stair in the heart of the whiteout, an old man with a river's voice in his ears and fifty years of debt on his back, and began, quietly, to laugh.

"Well," he said. "Then we'd best hurry. I did promise the mountain I'd be early for something, once in my life."
