## 18. The Last Day of the Permits

The county seat of Harrow's Bend announced itself the way a tired man announces himself at the end of a long shift: with a sigh and a light left on.

Yara saw it first from the ridge above the river valley—a scatter of roofs, a water tower leaning like an old drunk against the sky, and one street lit by gas lamps that someone, somewhere, still paid to keep burning. Below it all sat the courthouse square, and on its corner the recorder's office, a squat stone building with a green door that she had been imagining for eleven days now. Eleven days of scree and whiteout and Odo's leg going bad and Bram navigating by the sound of water. All of it aimed at that green door like a survey line.

"Two hours," Maren said beside her, checking the pocket watch she'd wound every morning without fail even when her fingers were too cold to feel the crown. "Permits expire at midnight. Recorder's office closes at six."

"Then we have four hours to walk two miles," Yara said. "Generous."

"Don't say things like that out loud. The mountain hears you and takes it personally."

They came down off the ridge in the last gray light, seven people moving like one tired animal: Bram leading, Fen half-carrying Odo, who had refused the stretcher twice and accepted it once under conditions of great secrecy, Maren with the document case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, and the two Lowden Hollow descendants—Ruth Calloway and her nephew Tam—who had joined them at the Tollhouse and walked the whole way without complaint, as if carrying a dead man's errand made ordinary hardship seem like small change.

The snow had stopped an hour ago. The air was so clean it felt new-minted. Somewhere below, a church bell rang five o'clock, and Yara's chest did something complicated around the sound, because bells meant towns, and towns meant doors, and doors meant either arrival or refusal, and she had learned over thirty years not to bet on which.

"You're limping," Fen said to her.

"I'm walking with character."

"That's what Odo said about his leg for three days and then he fainted into a snowbank."

"Odo fainted because he's dramatic," Odo said from the sled. "It's my best quality."

* * *

Harrow's Bend smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions, which under any other circumstances would have made Yara weep openly. They came down the main street past shuttered storefronts and one saloon doing brisk business, and people turned to look—the way small-town people look at strangers who arrive muddy, armed with nothing but papers, and accompanied by an old man whom several of the older faces seemed to recognize.

"Bram Halloway," said a woman from a doorway, holding a child by the collar. "I thought you were dead."

"Not yet," Bram said. "Working on it."

"What happened to your beard?"

"It lost an argument with the weather."

The woman laughed, and something in the street loosened. Yara felt the crew relax around her by degrees, shoulders dropping, hands coming out of pockets. She did not relax. Thirty years of railroad work had taught her that the last mile of anything was where the sabotage lived. Switches thrown. Rails pulled. And in this particular case—

She stopped walking.

The recorder's office stood at the corner of the square, green door, brass handle, exactly as advertised. The lights were on inside. She could see the shadow of a clerk moving behind the frosted glass.

And on the steps, in a good coat that had never seen weather, hat tilted at an angle that suggested a man who expected the world to hold still while he adjusted it, stood Silas Vane.

Beside him, a sheriff in a coat two sizes too big, and two men Yara recognized from the switchback ambush—one of them still wearing a plaster on his jaw where Fen had introduced him to a rock.

"There he is," Odo said quietly. "Our favorite recurring villain."

"He's not a villain," Maren said. Her voice had gone flat and precise, the way it went when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Villains want things. He *inherited* wanting. It's worse."

"Four hours to closing," Yara murmured. "He can't just stand there all evening."

"He absolutely can," Maren said. "That's the whole point of him."

* * *

Vane watched them come the way a man watches a train arrive he'd already bought the ticket for. He didn't move from the steps until they were ten feet away, and then he descended slowly, hat in hand, courteous as a funeral director.

"Miss Dunmore." His breath fogged. Up close he looked worse than he had at the waystation—hollowed, sleepless, a man who had been losing arguments with himself for days. "You look terrible. All of you. The mountain's been unkind."

"The mountain's been honest," Bram said. "Different thing."

"So it has." Vane's eyes went to the document case on Maren's chest, lingered there, and returned to Yara. "I'll be brief, since I know you're pressed. Sheriff Ondrell has a warrant for your arrest. Theft of company property from company land—the Tollhouse cave sits inside our concession boundary, as I'm sure your surveyor can confirm." A thin smile at Maren. "The plates are mine by law. You've been carrying stolen goods across my range for two weeks."

"They were never yours," Ruth Calloway said. Her voice cracked on it. "My grandfather drew those surveys. You buried them."

"Madam, I have buried nothing. I wasn't born." Vane said it gently, and somehow that was the cruelest thing he'd said yet. "I'm simply the last man willing to carry what was handed to me. You know how inheritance works. None of us chose it."

"You could put it down," Tam said.

"Yes," Vane said. "I could." For one strange moment he looked like a man standing at the edge of a very high place, considering. Then the sheriff cleared his throat, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"Here is my offer, final form," Vane said. "Walk away, Miss Dunmore. All of you. Leave the case on these steps, sign a statement that you found it empty, and no charges follow anyone. Your bridge record stays closed. Your crew goes home whole. The permits expire at midnight regardless—you cannot file what the office won't accept." He gestured behind him, and Yara saw it then: the green door was locked, and a card was taped to the glass. *Closed this evening for private function — Harrow Consolidated Mining.* Through the frosted window, past the clerk-shaped shadow, she could see lamplight and glasses and the murmur of a party. The company had rented the recorder's office for the evening. Had rented, in effect, the last day of its own concession, and locked the clock inside.

"You bought the office," Yara said.

"I bought the *evening*," Vane corrected. "The office was already for sale. Everything here was always for sale. That's rather the problem, isn't it?"

Behind her, Yara heard Fen shift his weight, and knew without looking that he was calculating distances to the two hired men. She put a hand back without turning, found his sleeve, and pressed it down. Not here. Not with Odo on a sled and Ruth's hands shaking and the whole street watching.

"We'll wait," Yara said.

Vane raised an eyebrow. "For what?"

"For six o'clock. For midnight. For whatever comes after midnight." She looked up at the courthouse clock across the square, its face lit yellow against the darkening sky. "You've had fifty years of other people's patience, Mr. Vane. You can spare us one evening of yours."

Something moved through his face—not anger, something more tired than anger—and he stepped aside, sweeping his arm toward the square like a host showing guests to a bad table.

"By all means," he said. "Wait."

* * *

They waited on the courthouse steps across the square, because Bram said it was warmer there—"stone holds the day's heat longer than wood, same as grudges"—and because from those steps you could see both the recorder's office and the clock, and Yara wanted to see both.

Maren produced tea from somewhere. She always produced tea from somewhere; Yara had stopped asking. They passed one dented tin cup down the line, and the town began, cautiously, to gather. First the woman who'd recognized Bram, then the saloon crowd, then children who should have been in bed, drawn by the rare spectacle of adults sitting still in the cold on purpose.

Fen told them what happened, in his way—which was to make it sound like a story he'd read somewhere and improved upon. The whiteout became a blizzard of biblical proportions. Odo's fall at the switchback became a leap. The tunnel under the pass became, briefly, haunted, until Bram said flatly, "It wasn't haunted, it was badly ventilated," and Fen allowed as how that was probably true but less fun.

Tam laughed for the first time since they'd found his uncle's remains in the Tollhouse, and Ruth put her hand over her mouth like the laugh had escaped custody, and Yara felt the strangest thing in her chest, which was hope, arriving at the worst possible time, the way trains always did.

"Don't," Maren said quietly, beside her. She'd been watching Yara's face instead of the story. "Don't count it yet."

"I'm not counting anything."

"You're counting. You do the thing with your jaw."

"I don't have a thing with my jaw."

"You have a whole vocabulary of things with your jaw. I've surveyed you for two weeks. I know your grades better than you do."

Yara almost smiled. Then the courthouse clock began to strike six, and the sound went through the square like a blade through rope, and everyone stopped talking at once.

Across the street, the party in the recorder's office continued. Laughter, faint and bright. A glass set down on wood. The clerk-shaped shadow hadn't moved.

At quarter past six, the door of the office opened—not the green door but a side entrance—and a man in shirtsleeves stepped out onto the steps, looked at the crowd across the square, and went back in. At half past, Vane himself emerged, spoke briefly with the sheriff, and went back in. At seven, the lights in the office began going out one by one, and Yara stood up so fast the tin cup spilled, because going-out lights meant leaving clerks meant—

But the green door stayed shut. The card stayed taped to the glass. And at seven-fifteen a boy in a company cap came across the square, official as a telegram, and posted a second notice beneath the first:

*Recorder's office closed remainder of day. Filings resume Thursday morning.*

Thursday morning. Nine hours after the permits expired at midnight.

The square went quiet enough to hear the water tower creak.

"They can't do that," Ruth said. "Can they do that?"

"They just did," Maren said. Her voice was steady but her hands weren't. "A filing window isn't a law, it's an office. Offices close. Concessions lapse. And at twelve-oh-one, everything in that case becomes historical curiosity instead of legal fact." She looked at Yara, and Yara saw it hit her in stages, the way bad news arrives in railcars: first the engine, then the weight. "Fifty years, and we lose it to a handwritten notice and a locked door."

"No," Yara said.

Everyone looked at her.

"No," she said again, and heard her own voice come out level, the voice she used on grades she'd already decided were climbable. "We didn't cross a mountain range to be beaten by stationery. Maren—the homestead clause. The one you found in the old territorial code. Read it to me again. Exactly."

Maren blinked. "It says filings touching pre-concession claims may be entered *at the discretion of the recorder*, outside standard hours, provided—"

"Provided what?"

"Provided the claimant presents proof of prior right and pays the applicable fee." Maren's eyes narrowed. "Yara, the discretion belongs to the recorder. And the recorder is in there drinking their wine."

"The recorder is in there," Yara agreed. "But the *office*—" she pointed at the green door, at the brass handle, at the building itself, squat and stone and older than the company that thought it owned the evening— "the office is a public building. And I've been thinking about that cave. About the Tollhouse. About what admission means, and who sets the price, and what happens when somebody tries to lock a door that was never theirs to lock."

Bram had gone very still, the way he went still before saying the thing he'd been not-saying for weeks. "You're talking about the original claim," he said slowly. "The Tollhouse grant. Pre-territorial. The one the company's concession was carved *out of*."

"I'm talking about paying a toll," Yara said, "that nobody's paid in fifty years. And I think—" she looked across the square at Silas Vane, who had come back out onto the steps and was watching them with an expression she couldn't read, part warning and part, impossibly, waiting— "I think he knows it too. I think that's why he's standing there instead of leaving."

"Why would he wait?" Fen asked.

"Because some doors," Bram said, rising stiffly to his feet, "you can't open alone. And some men inherit locks they've spent their whole lives pretending aren't there." He settled his ruined hat. "Get the case, girl. And get ready to say it loud. Whatever happens next, it happens in front of witnesses."

Across the square, the courthouse clock began to strike eight.

Sixteen hours to midnight. One locked door. And Yara Dunmore, who had spent thirty years learning every way a line could be blocked, finally saw the route through—one that ran straight through the middle of a legend, and required her, for the first time in her life, to stop moving and stand her ground.

She picked up the document case herself.

"Toll's due," she said. "Let's go pay it."
