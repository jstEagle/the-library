## 19. Admission Paid

The recorder's office door was oak, and it was locked, and it had a brass plaque on it that read CLOSED FOR PRIVATE FUNCTION, and Yara Dunmore stood in front of all three facts and felt the last day of the permits draining out of the morning like water through scree.

Behind her, the steps of the county courthouse were filling up. Not with lawyers—there wasn't money in this valley for lawyers—but with people. Track families. Widow women who'd walked ties for pin money. Two of the Ferris brothers from the section house at Mile 41, still wearing their high-visibility vests as if they'd come straight off a shift that had ended years ago. Bram Halloway stood at the bottom of the steps with his hands folded over his walking stick, patient as a fence post, and beside him Fen held Odo upright, Odo's splinted leg propped on a borrowed crutch, his face gray but his jaw set like he intended to argue with the building itself if necessary.

Maren came up the steps two at a time with the document case strapped across her chest like a bandolier. "Locked," she said. "I tried the side door too. There's a man inside—I saw him through the frosted glass. Company man. He's sitting at the recorder's own desk drinking coffee out of the recorder's own cup."

"Of course he is," Yara said.

"And there's our friend." Maren tipped her chin toward the base of the steps.

Silas Vane stood at the foot of the courthouse stairs in a wool coat too good for this weather, flanked by the sheriff—a broad, uncomfortable man whose badge looked like it had been polished more often than worn—and by two of Vane's hired men, one of whom Yara recognized from the switchback, which meant she also recognized the shape of the bruise she'd left on him.

Vane held up a paper. "Miss Dunmore," he called, pleasant as a station announcement. "Warrant. Theft of company property from company land. The sheriff here is authorized to take you into custody, and those documents into protective storage, pending review."

"Pending review," Maren muttered. "That's what they called it when they boxed up Lowden Hollow's survey. Pending review for forty years."

The crowd murmured. Yara counted heads without meaning to—an old habit from platform work, estimating load—and got somewhere north of sixty. Rail families traveled light but showed up heavy. Word had gone down the line overnight, hand to hand, the way word always went when the telegraph was company-owned: *the woman with the plates is filing today, last day, come see.*

She had until five o'clock. The permit clock didn't care about warrants or functions or the taste of copper fear in her throat. At five o'clock, if nothing was filed, the concession rolled forward unopposed, and Lowden Hollow stayed dry another forty years, and every mile of blood and whiteout and Odo's leg and the dead man in the Tollhouse cave—all of it curdled into a story nobody would believe.

Yara looked at the locked door. She looked at Vane's pleasant face. And then, because her whole life had been an education in reading other people's lines—the grades they'd run, the corners they'd cut, the old track bed under the new ballast—she looked past both, down into the crowd, and found the face she was looking for.

Old Tom Wickery. Eighty-some years, retired roadmaster, the kind of man who remembered the Karst line before it was the Karst line. He'd ridden the mail car down with them yesterday, uninvited, unasked, and spent the whole trip telling anyone who'd listen about the winter of '09. He was standing near the front now, cap in his hands, watching her with the particular patience of a man who has seen many young engineers arrive at the same wall he hit in 1911.

"Mr. Wickery," Yara said, loud enough to carry. "You worked the coast spur before the concession. Before Harrowgate Consolidated ever stamped its seal on anything up here."

"I did," Wickery said. "Nineteen years old. Walked tie from the Tollhouse landing to the quarry mouth."

"The Tollhouse." Yara let the name sit in the cold air. "Tell these folks what the old-timers said about the Tollhouse."

A ripple went through the crowd. Even Vane shifted his weight.

Wickery smiled the way old men smile when handed a stage. "Tollhouse Cave. Tide cave, below the point. My grandmother's people fished off that rock before there was a rail within a hundred miles. The story goes the cave charges admission. You want to pass through it—or take anything out of it—you pay the toll."

"A story," Vane said smoothly. "Charming local color. Sheriff, if you'd—"

"Hold on." Yara raised her voice. "Because stories are one thing, Mr. Vane, but I'm an engineer, and I don't trust anything until I've seen it in writing. So three nights ago, while you were sending men up a mountain after us, I went back to the Tollhouse at low tide. And I found writing."

She turned to Maren, who was already unbuckling the case. They'd talked about this in the wrecked caboose, in the long dark hours while Odo slept and the storm hammered the roof—talked it through the way you talk through a difficult grade, marking every curve. Maren drew out a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth and handed it over.

Inside was a document. Not one of the survey plates—those were safe, copied, distributed to three separate hands in case of exactly this morning. This was older. Brittle paper, iron-gall ink gone brown, a seal at the bottom that predated Harrowgate Consolidated by eleven years.

"This is a claim record," Yara said. "Filed in this county in 1888, by the Coastal Toll Road and Landing Company. It covers the headland, the landing, and the cave beneath it—the Tollhouse. Eleven years before your founder's concession. Which means everything Harrowgate built after that sits on top of an older claim that was never extinguished. Never bought out. Never revoked. Just—forgotten." She paused. "Or buried. Your founder was good at burying things. We've found several."

Vane's face did something small and controlled. "An expired claim from a defunct company is worth less than the paper it's on."

"It would be," Yara agreed, "if it were just a claim. But claims have clauses, and this one has a clause I've read maybe forty times since we found it. Section four. Listen close, everybody, because this is the part where the story turns out to be true."

She unfolded the page fully and read aloud, slow, so the words could reach the back row:

"*The said cave, known locally as the Tollhouse, shall remain open passage to all persons, and any person may lawfully take from or carry through the lands herein described whatever goods they have honestly paid the toll to obtain; the toll being such cost as the passage exacts of them in body, labor, or risk.*"

She lowered the paper.

Silence. The kind of silence a courthouse yard does well—stone and cold air and sixty people not breathing.

"That's a right-of-way clause," Maren said clearly, from the steps, in her best surveyor's voice, the one that had once certified bridges. "It's archaic phrasing, but it's valid phrasing. It grants public passage and public right of removal across the claimed land, conditioned on payment of toll. And the toll isn't money. Read it again—it never was money. *Body, labor, or risk.* That's why they called it the Tollhouse. The cave charges what it charges."

"You can't seriously—" Vane started.

"I paid it," Yara said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. She held up her left hand, where the rope burn from the second tide window had scabbed and reopened and scabbed again, a raw red stripe across the palm.

"Toll paid in body. Ask my surveyor—she dressed this twice. Ask Fen and Odo Bramble, who carried those plates up four thousand feet of scree on half rations through a whiteout that killed our sled and nearly starved us. Toll paid in labor. Ask Odo what his leg cost on the switchback, buying thirty seconds so the documents wouldn't go over the edge. Toll paid in risk." She stopped, and made herself say the next part plainly, because he deserved plainness. "There was a man in that cave before us. A Lowden Hollow descendant. He tried to carry the proof out alone, and the Tollhouse took his toll in full. We finished his crossing. That's the whole of the transaction."

"So the law says," Yara went on, turning back to the sheriff, "that under a claim senior to Harrowgate's concession by eleven years, I have public right-of-way across this land and public right to carry through it what I've paid for. These documents came out of that cave. Through that right-of-way. Up the mountain and down again, all of it inside the headland's bounds and then beyond them, lawful every step under an older law than yours, Sheriff—one your own county recorded and never repealed, because nobody thought it mattered until this morning."

The sheriff looked at the warrant in his hand. Then at the crowd. Then at Old Tom Wickery, who nodded at him slowly, the way one roadman acknowledges another across a divide.

"My grandmother," Wickery said, to the sheriff, not to Vane, "used to say the Tollhouse never refused anybody who paid honest. And it never let anybody through free. Either way, you don't argue with it. You just settle up." He put his cap on. "Seems to me these folks settled up."

"They're rail families," someone called from the middle of the crowd—Mrs. Aldous, whose husband had died in the tunnel collapse and who had raised four children on widow's pension and stubbornness. "Every family on this line has paid a toll to that mountain. If the Tollhouse keeps accounts, we've all got credit."

Laughter ran through the crowd—not mean laughter, but the kind with iron in it, the kind that starts when fear decides it's done.

Vane stepped forward. "This is theater. A scrap of paper from a dead company doesn't override an active state concession. Sheriff, do your duty."

And here was the thing Yara had counted on, the thing she'd learned drifting between closed lines for eleven years: sheriffs in railroad counties are elected by railroad towns, and railroad towns remember who kept the paychecks coming and who buried the villages. The sheriff turned the warrant over in his big hands like a form he couldn't quite read.

"Reckon I'd need the recorder to countersign an arrest on disputed title," he said at last, heavily. "Recorder's office is closed for a private function."

"Open it," said Mrs. Aldous.

"Company function," said the man at Vane's elbow.

"Then it won't mind company," said Fen, and the crowd laughed again, and began, gently, irresistibly, to move up the steps.

It wasn't a rush. Nobody shoved. It was the way a full train takes a grade—slow, enormous, certain. Sixty-odd people in patched coats climbed the courthouse stairs around Yara and past her, and the two hired men found themselves absorbed into it like stones into a river, and the sheriff followed at the rear looking like a man attending a funeral he approved of. Someone knocked on the oak door. Not pounding—knocking, three polite raps, Mrs. Aldous with her knuckles, the way you'd knock on a neighbor's door.

After a long moment, the lock turned from inside.

The company man behind the desk had the recorder's coffee cup halfway to his mouth. He froze there, a portrait of a man doing arithmetic and disliking the total. Behind him, the great ledger cabinet stood against the wall, brass handles dull with disuse, and above it hung the county seal and the framed photograph of every recorder since statehood.

"Ma'am," the company man said to Mrs. Aldous, because she was nearest and because some instincts outrank employers, "the office is—"

"Closed for a private function," Mrs. Aldous agreed. "Well, it's private no longer. Fetch the recorder."

The recorder, it developed, had been at home two streets over, having been informed his office would be "unavailable" today. He arrived in twelve minutes, coat over nightshirt, escorted by a delegation of children who had appointed themselves his honor guard, and he took one look at the crowd filling his office to the wainscoting and one look at Yara standing at his counter with an oilcloth packet, and something in his tired face eased, as if he'd been waiting years for someone to give him a reason.

"Last day of the permit window," Yara said. "I'm filing a challenge. Senior claim, Tollhouse right-of-way, and enclosed evidence regarding falsified surveys affecting the water rights of Lowden Hollow. I'd like it entered before five o'clock."

The recorder glanced at the clock. Twenty past nine. "We've got time," he said, and unlocked the ledger cabinet himself, and the sound of that key turning was the finest piece of machinery Yara had heard in eleven years of listening to machinery.

Maren laid the copies out on the counter with surgical neatness. The recorder read. His lips moved. Twice he stopped, took off his glasses, cleaned them, and put them back on, and each time the room waited without breathing.

"Lowden Hollow," he said finally. "My grandfather hauled freight for Lowden Hollow. Always said the wells went dry strange—said the maps never matched the ground." He signed the intake register with a flourish that suggested he'd been saving it. "Entered. Challenged. Concession suspended pending review of senior claim." He looked up, and his voice cracked slightly on the ordinary words: "Timestamped eight fifty-one in the morning, October the fourteenth. You made it, Miss Dunmore."

The sound the crowd made then was not a cheer, quite. It was lower than that and longer—the sound of sixty people exhaling forty years.

* * *

They came out onto the steps into hard bright noon, and the crowd had grown—half the town must have walked over, and someone had brought coffee in a wash boiler, and somebody else was telling the Tollhouse story to a ring of children with dramatic gestures that grew more elaborate with each retelling.

Vane was gone from the foot of the steps. His hired men were gone. Only the sheriff remained, leaning against the railing, hat tipped back.

"Warrant's void," he said to Yara, almost apologetically. "Disputed title, resolved by filing. Recorder tore it up himself." He studied her a moment. "You know he'll contest this. Vane. Lawyers, appeals, the whole weight of Harrowgate coming down on a scrap of 1888 paper."

"I know," Yara said. "But a scrap of 1888 paper beat him to the counter by eight hours, and the timestamp doesn't appeal."

The sheriff grunted what might have been agreement, touched his hat brim, and wandered off toward the diner, a man suddenly very interested in lunch.

Bram Halloway came and stood beside her at the top of the steps. For a while neither of them said anything. Below, Odo sat on the courthouse bench with his leg out straight, holding the document case on his lap like a cat that had finally decided to be held, and Fen was explaining the Tollhouse legend to the children with considerably more accuracy than the previous teller, and Maren was deep in conversation with the recorder about pulling the original bore records from the archive, her hands moving fast, her face lit from inside.

"Fifty years," Bram said at last. "Fifty years I helped build their tunnels and kept my mouth shut about what they were for. Thought the debt was too old to pay." He looked out at the crowd, at the widow women and the Ferris brothers and the children. "Turns out it just needed the right engineer to run the line."

"Bram—"

"Don't make it a speech," he said. "I hate speeches. But there's a plate in that case—the last original, the one with my father's mark on the margin. Vane will want it. He'll ask for it quiet, or he'll try for it loud, but he'll come for it, because it's the only thing left with his name on it." The old man's eyes were steady and sad. "When he comes, girl, somebody's going to have to decide what he gets."

Yara watched the street. Wagons, wind, the flag over the courthouse snapping in the cold. Somewhere down the block, a hotel door opened and a wool coat too good for the weather stepped out into the light and stood very still, looking up at the courthouse steps—at the crowd, at the case in Odo's lap, at her.

"He's already here," Yara said.
