## 21. Water for Lowden Hollow

The first thing I did with my restored reputation was almost ruin it again.

It happened on a Tuesday, in the recorder's office at the county seat, three weeks after the permits expired and the world turned over. I had been asked—politely, by a man with ink-stained cuffs and the weary patience of someone who processed other people's paperwork for a living—to verify the provenance of the Tollhouse plates one final time. I spread them across his desk with the care of a surgeon and the pride of a mother, and when he asked me to confirm that the 1889 survey of Lowden Hollow was authentic, I said, "Authentic? Sir, this is the most honest piece of paper in the history of dishonest paper."

He looked at me over his spectacles. "That's not what I asked."

"It's what you meant," I said, and stamped it myself before he could stop me.

Yara was waiting outside on the steps, sitting on the cold stone with her elbows on her knees, watching the street the way she always watched streets—like she was memorizing the exits. Old habit. She'd been doing it less lately, which I counted as progress the way you count a fever breaking: slowly, and without quite believing it.

"Well?" she said.

"Filed. Recorded. Witnessed." I sat down beside her. "Lowden Hollow has its water back. On paper."

"On paper," Yara repeated. Paper was where everything started for people like us. Paper was grades and elevations and the difference between a bridge that stood and one that didn't. But she knew what I meant, and I knew she knew, because she didn't say anything else. She just looked down the street toward the rail yard, where the old spur line ran out of town like a sentence somebody had stopped writing halfway through.

That was the thing about winning. Nobody tells you how much work comes after.

* * *

The spur to Lowden Hollow had been dead eleven years. Eleven years of rust and rot and alder saplings growing up through the ties like the mountain was trying to take the line back into itself, which, in fairness, the mountain had a decent argument for. When we walked it the first week—Yara, me, Fen, Odo, and Bram, all five of us strays with nothing better to do and nowhere else to be—it took us four hours to cover six miles, mostly because Odo kept stopping to pull up saplings and Fen kept stopping to make fun of him for it.

"You're going to throw your back out before we even get paid," Fen called from up ahead.

"I'm going to throw *you* off the embankment is what I'm going to do."

"Promises, promises."

Bram walked at the rear, as he always did, hands clasped behind him like a man inspecting property he already owned. He hadn't said much since the descent through the whiteout—the redemption run, Fen called it, though never within Bram's hearing—and nobody pressed him. Some men process things out loud. Bram processed them with his boots.

It was Yara who stopped, finally, at the point where the line crossed the dry creek bed, and stood there so long that the rest of us gathered around her like iron filings around a magnet.

"This is it," she said. "This is where the water used to come through."

I got out my instruments out of pure reflex. The channel was wide and cobbly and bone-dry, but it had been a real creek once—you could read it in the stones, in the way the banks were cut, in the old scour marks high on the far side. A good-sized creek. A creek worth stealing.

"They diverted it at the headwall," Bram said quietly. We all turned. It was the longest sentence he'd offered in two weeks. "Up in the narrows. There's a tunnel. My father helped dig it." A pause. "So did I."

Nobody said anything clever. For once, nobody wanted to.

"So we reopen the diversion," Yara said at last, in the flat practical voice she used when she was turning grief into a work order. "And we rebuild the spur to haul the materials in. And the company pays for both, because the county says they do, and because Vane signed the settlement instead of burning the last plate."

"He signed it," I said, "and then he walked into the snow."

"And the snow let him go," Yara said. "Which is more than I expected from either of them."

* * *

The offer came the following Monday, delivered by the same ink-cuffed man from the recorder's office, who turned out to be named Mr. Pell and to have considerably more personality outside his office than in it. He found us at the boarding house, all five of us crammed into the parlor because the parlor had the only stove that worked, playing cards with a deck missing the sevens—we'd lost them somewhere on the mountain, along with my dignity and roughly half of Odo's left boot.

Mr. Pell cleared his throat. "The county, in conjunction with the reorganized Karst Consolidated, is soliciting bids for the reconstruction of the Lowden Hollow spur. Water works, roadbed, trestle repair, approximately nine miles of track." He paused. "They've also asked me to extend a personal invitation to a certain crew."

Fen looked up from his cards. "What certain crew?"

"A crew," Mr. Pell said, with the air of a man reading from a list he found amusing, "consisting of one surveyor, formerly of the Karst office, whose disgrace has been formally expunged; one railroad engineer of considerable reputation, whose name appears on no warrant anywhere in this county; two track-walkers described in their previous employment file as, and I quote, 'adequate'; and one guide."

Odo snorted. "Adequate. Eleven years and that's the epitaph."

"You want adequate?" Fen said. "You should see our brotherly affection. It's barely passable."

"The wages are fair," Mr. Pell went on, "the term is eighteen months, and the foreman's position—" here he looked directly at Yara, who had gone very still in the way she did when something mattered too much to react to— "is open. The county would like it filled by someone who knows exactly what happened to the last survey."

The room got quiet. Even the stove seemed to hush.

Yara looked at me. I don't know why she looked at me—I wasn't the one being offered anything—but I understood later. She was checking whether I'd still be there. Whether any of us would. It was the question underneath every question she'd ever asked, the one she'd spent a lifetime making sure she never had to answer: if I stop moving, will anyone stay?

"We'll take it," I said, before she could sabotage herself. "All of us. As a unit. Non-negotiable."

"I haven't actually—" Yara started.

"She speaks for the crew," Fen said solemnly, laying down a card. "It's in our charter."

"We don't have a charter," Odo said.

"That's what makes it binding."

Mr. Pell smiled, tucked his papers away, and said, "Monday, then. Six o'clock. Bring your own coffee; the county provides shovels." At the door he paused. "Oh—and Miss Cole. The record office has entered your restoration into the permanent register. Your name's clean now. Officially."

I waited until the door closed behind him before I let myself feel it. Clean. Twenty years of carrying that scandal like a stone in my coat pocket, and some clerk with a stamp had set it down in an afternoon. I'd have cried, probably, except Fen chose that exact moment to announce that whoever lost the next hand had to tell the story of the time Odo fell asleep standing up in a snowbank, and the moment passed, the way moments do, into laughter.

* * *

We broke ground—or rather, broke rust—on a morning so cold the frost stood up out of the gravel like tiny gray grass. Yara ran the first line herself, sighting down the old grade with the transit balanced on her shoulder, and I watched her face while she worked. I'd watched a lot of faces over instruments in twenty years. Most engineers look at a dead line and see cost. Yara looked at this one and saw, I think, a patient in need of setting.

"Grade's held," she announced. "Eleven years and the roadbed's still true. Whoever built this built it right."

"My father," Bram said, from behind her. "And me. And forty men from Lowden Hollow who were promised jobs on it forever."

Yara lowered the transit. "Then we build it righter."

"There's no such word," I said.

"There is now. I'm the foreman. I say there is."

The first month was mud and muscle. We hired local men—some of them grandsons of the forty—and the townsfolk of the staging town, who had once mockingly christened us *the strays*, now watched our evening arrival at the boarding house with something dangerously close to fondness. Mrs. Aldous, who owned the place, started saving us the corner table without being asked. The butcher started throwing in bones for free, ostensibly for a dog none of us owned. And the children—well. The children discovered that Fen would teach them card games of questionable honesty, and that Odo could be persuaded to balance a spike on his nose if sufficiently dared, and that Bram, impossibly, would tell stories if you caught him at the right hour, sitting on the loading dock with the sunset behind him.

My favorite was the girl, Tansy, maybe ten, who appointed herself my apprentice and followed me around the survey stakes asking questions until my ears bled. Why does the grade matter. Why can't the track just go straight. Why do you look through the little telescope backwards sometimes.

"Because sometimes," I told her, "the only way to see where you're going is to look at where you've been."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge. "That sounds like something you made up after something bad happened to you."

Out of the mouths of babes. "It usually is," I said.

* * *

The headwall tunnel took us until nearly midwinter to reach, and when we finally cut through the last of the collapse and daylight poured into the bore, the whole crew crowded in behind Yara like ducklings. The diversion gate was still there—company iron, company lock, fifty years of rust—and beyond it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound none of us had heard in this valley in our lifetimes.

Water.

Bram put his hand flat against the gate and stood there a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough as ballast.

"Fifty years," he said. "I carried the chain for the man who measured this tunnel. I was sixteen. I thought I was building something." His hand curled into a fist against the iron. "Turns out I was helping bury it."

"Bram," Yara said gently, "we're un-burying it."

"Yes." He wiped his face with the back of his glove, quick and businesslike, pretending it was the dust. "Well. Best get to it, then."

It took three days to swing the gate. On the third afternoon, with the whole valley watching from the creek bed below—men with their hats off, women holding children up on their shoulders, old Mrs. Calloway from the Hollow standing at the front with her arms crossed like she'd personally wait out eternity—the water came through.

It came the way water does: reluctantly, then all at once. A brown tongue feeling its way down the old channel, thickening, quickening, finding stones it remembered. And then the sound—that sound—filling the dry valley the way an organ fills a church, and Mrs. Calloway sat down right there in the gravel, and nobody laughed, because half the crowd was doing the same or close to it.

Fen leaned over to Odo and whispered, not quietly enough, "Bet you can't skip a stone in the new creek."

"Bet you can't reach the bank without crying."

"I'm not crying. It's spray."

"It's a dry valley, you idiot."

I looked at Yara. She was watching the water with the transit case hugged to her chest like a child, and she wasn't crying either, but her eyes were bright, and when she caught me looking she didn't look away. That was new. That was the newest thing of all.

* * *

Spring came early that year, or late, depending on how you counted—the mountain kept its own calendar and shared it with no one. By the time the first true warm week arrived, we had laid track to within sight of the Hollow itself, and the company sent up a small brass band for the occasion, which was optimistic of them, since the "station" at Lowden Hollow was currently a platform, a water tower under construction, and a signboard Fen had painted overnight that read LOWDEN HOLLOW — POPULATION: PENDING.

The night before the first train, we sat on the platform with our feet hanging over the edge, all five of us, passing a bottle of Mrs. Aldous's blackberry wine, which tasted like punishment but went down like tradition.

"To the forty," Bram said, and drank.

"To the girl in the cave," Yara added—meaning the descendant who'd died trying to carry the proof out, whose grave we'd visited on the way up with a spike driven at each corner. "To finishing his errand."

"To adequate," said Fen, raising the bottle grandly. "May we exceed expectations."

"To my leg," said Odo, thumping the scarred knee that had healed crooked and strong. "May it ache on schedule."

"To clean paper," I said, and meant more than the register entry, and everyone knew it.

Yara took the bottle last. She held it a moment, looking down the line into the dark, where tomorrow a train would come—a real train, on real track, to a town that existed again.

"To staying," she said.

Nobody made a joke. That was how we told her we understood what it cost her to say it.

She passed the bottle back, and Fen launched immediately into a completely fabricated account of the whiteout games ("and then Odo had to hop on one leg for a full mile, blindfolded, singing"), and Odo disputed every detail, and Bram made a sound that in a younger man would have been a laugh, and I sat there in the middle of it thinking: twenty years I thought my career ended on a falsified report. It turns out it was just waiting for a better survey.

Down the valley, the new creek talked to itself in the dark, steady as a heartbeat.

And up the line, far off, so faint it might have been wind—though none of us believed that for a second—came the whistle of the morning train, arriving early, the way good news does when you've finally stopped running from it.
