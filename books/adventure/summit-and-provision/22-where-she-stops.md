## 22. Where She Stops

The first run of the Lowden Hollow spur began, fittingly, with an argument about the whistle.

"It's a two-note crossing whistle," Fen said, hand hovering over the cord. "That's regulation."

"It's a celebration," Odo said, "and celebrations get three notes. Maybe four."

"Regulation," Fen repeated, with the flat certainty of a man who had walked track for eleven years and considered the rulebook a form of scripture.

"We were almost killed by a rockslide, Fen. Twice. Regulation can wait a season."

Yara, riding in the cab with her elbow out the window and the cold morning air doing its honest work on her face, let them argue. Bram sat across from her on the fireman's bench, hands folded over his stick, watching the hills come down to meet the sea the way an old man watches a grandchild he's decided to forgive.

"Let them settle it themselves," Bram said. "That's half of what a line is. The other half's ballast."

The whistle, when it finally sounded, was four notes — Odo had won by simply leaning on the cord until Fen gave up — and the sound went rolling down the valley toward Lowden Hollow, where, Maren had reported that morning, half the town had turned out to stand along the rebuilt right-of-way in their good coats. Children had been given flags. Nobody remembered where the flags had come from. That was the kind of town Lowden Hollow was becoming again: the kind that had flags and didn't keep accounts.

Yara had been on first runs before. She'd driven the opening trains on two short lines out east, back when she still believed a completed line was a kind of goodbye — you laid it, you proved it, you handed it over, and you were on the next crew boat before the paint dried on the depot sign. Stopping had always cost her more than moving. Moving, you never had to find out whether a place would have kept you.

She was finding out now. It was terrifying. It was wonderful. Both at once, the way the Karst Range had been both at once, and she was beginning to suspect that was simply the shape of anything worth doing.

The line curved along the coastal shelf below the old cliff road, and Yara knew what was coming before she saw it. She had timed it herself, three nights ago, with Maren's tide tables and Bram's memory, because some things you want to meet prepared.

"The Tollhouse," she said quietly, mostly to herself.

Bram nodded. "Low tide's at half nine. You'll see it bare."

The locomotive came around the headland and there it was: the black slot in the sea cliff, the cave mouth standing open to the morning like a door somebody had finally remembered to unlock. The tide was out far enough to show the shelf of wet rock below it, ribbed and gleaming, the whole approach exposed. In the stories, the Tollhouse charged admission. In the stories, nobody said what the toll was, and that was the whole point of the story — you paid without knowing, and the knowing came after.

The last time she'd passed it, weeks ago now, on the way down from the pass with the document case strapped to her chest and Odo's leg splinted with a rail brace, she had looked away. Not out of superstition. Out of something older — the habit of a woman who had spent her whole adult life not looking at the things she'd left behind. Caves. Towns. People. A bridge. She had a lifetime's practice at the clean, efficient turn of the head.

This time she looked.

She looked the way she'd learned to look at a survey line: steadily, without flinching, taking the whole of it. The cave, the wet shelf, the memory of rusted plates and a dead man's errand finished by strangers. The place where she had stopped being a woman drifting between closed lines and started being whatever she was now, which she suspected the county would eventually need a word for and she did not.

"You're not looking away," Bram observed.

"No."

"Good." He tapped his stick once on the floorboards. "Took me fifty years to learn that trick. You did it in one winter. Wasteful of you, all those years I spent not learning it."

Yara laughed. It came out easier than it used to. Everything did, these days, though she'd noticed she still checked her kit every morning — the transit, the chain, the level — the way some people check for a door they no longer need to bolt. Maren said habits of precision were the last thing to leave a surveyor, and that in Maren's opinion they ought to be the first thing to arrive at a funeral, since they'd certainly outlast everything else.

The cave slid past behind them. She didn't turn around to watch it go. That, too, was new.

* * *

Lowden Hollow came up on them the way towns do when they've decided to like you: all at once and slightly too loudly.

The rebuilt depot was small — one platform, one brick chimney, a water column that Odo had painted blue for reasons he declined to explain — but the platform was full. Rail families, homestead families, the Lowden descendants in a loose cluster near the front, and old Mrs. Tarrow, who was ninety-one and had been a girl in the Hollow when the water was taken, standing at the very edge of the boards with her cane planted like a survey stake.

Yara brought the engine in at a walking pace and stopped it with the precision she'd once reserved for bridges, which is to say she stopped it exactly where the platform sign said to stop it, to the inch, and then pretended she hadn't been showing off.

The crowd made the noise crowds make. Fen stepped down first and was immediately surrounded by children demanding to know if he had really fought off six men on a switchback. "Two," Fen said, with dignity. "Odo fought the ground, and the ground won." Odo, descending carefully on his healed-but-argumentative leg, said, "I saved the documents, which is what history will record," and a child asked him what documents, and Odo said, "Exactly," which the child found deeply satisfying.

Maren came down the steps last, in a coat that had been brushed and a expression that had not, and was immediately claimed by the Hollow's schoolteacher, who had apparently decided that a disgraced surveyor restored to grace was the finest living text in the county and intended to read all of her.

Yara stayed in the cab a moment longer. It was an old habit, letting the others arrive first, being the one who checked the brakes while the world got on with its welcoming. Bram, who was slower off trains than he used to be, paused beside her at the cab door.

"They'll want you at the front," he said.

"They've got Mrs. Tarrow at the front. Mrs. Tarrow is the front."

"Mrs. Tarrow," Bram said, "has been asking after you since Tuesday. She wants to know if you'll take tea. I told her you would. I may have told her you'd be honored."

"You told her what?"

"I told her the truth, which is a thing I've gotten in the habit of." He stepped down, slow and deliberate, and stood a moment on the platform with his hand on the rail, looking up the line they'd come down, and Yara saw him do the thing she'd seen him do at every terminus of this whole long winter: measure it. Fifty years since he'd helped build the tunnel under the pass, fifty years of knowing what was buried in the hills and saying nothing, and now the line ran clean and the water ran clean and the man stood in the morning sun looking at his own work like a debt finally marked paid.

Then he turned and held up his hand, and she took it, and came down onto the boards of Lowden Hollow.

* * *

The ceremony, if it could be called that, lasted eleven minutes and consisted of Mrs. Tarrow cutting a ribbon that Fen had tied and then having to be helped untie it because the knot was regulation. There was a speech by the county commissioner that Maren later described as "geologically accurate, which for a politician is a first." There was tea in the depot office, which had been swept and warmed and smelled of woodsmoke and pencil shavings, because the depot office was also, as of three weeks ago, the survey office of the Lowden Hollow Reconstruction Line, and its chief engineer was Yara Dunmore.

She still hadn't gotten used to the title. She suspected she had perhaps a year before she stopped flinching at it, and she intended to enjoy the flinching while it lasted, because the flinching was honest and the title was true and it was rare in her experience to have both at once.

The offer had come from the commissioner and the Lowden descendants together, in the recorder's office, on the day after everything — the day Vane had set down the last plate and walked into the snow. They'd asked her, all of them at once, in the awkward chorus of people who had rehearsed and then abandoned the rehearsal. Stay. Rebuild the spur. Run it. Not as a contractor passing through. As the line's own engineer.

She had almost said no. That was the truth of it, the part she'd only told Bram, sitting on the caboose steps at the long camp site with the storm gone and the stars enormous. She had almost said no because saying yes meant writing down an address, and she had not written down an address in nine years, and there was a part of her that believed — had always believed — that the writing-down was what made a thing real, and real things could be lost, and she had lost enough.

Bram had listened to all of this with the patience of a man who had spent fifty years listening to mountains.

"You kept moving," he'd said at last, "so no one could get close enough to leave. And then a crew of strays got close anyway, and did we leave?"

"No."

"Did the Hollow leave?"

"No."

"Then your theory's been tested," Bram said, "and it failed. As an engineer, what do you do with a failed theory?"

She'd looked at him for a long moment. "You revise it."

"You revise it," he agreed, and went inside, because Bram Halloway had never in his life lingered on a point after he'd made it.

* * *

The afternoon went the way good afternoons do, which is to say it went without anyone planning it.

There was a walk up the line to the first culvert, because Odo wanted to inspect his paintwork and Fen wanted to inspect Odo's paintwork and both of them wanted an audience. There was an argument about ballast depth that Maren settled with a measurement and a look. There was a moment, standing on the new trestle over the Hollow creek — the water running bright and unimpeded for the first time in forty years — when Mrs. Tarrow put her hand flat on the rail and said nothing at all, and nobody asked her to say anything, and the water said it for her.

Yara stood a little apart and watched her people — her crew, her family, the word still fit strangely in her mouth the way a new boot fits, though it was already beginning to take her shape — and felt the thing she had been running from for nine years catch up with her at last and turn out, on arrival, to be not a debt but a homecoming.

The train would go back up the line at four. The commissioner wanted the engine photographed. Fen had already lost the argument about whether the photograph would include the crew (it would) and whether Odo could hold the document case in it (he could not; the case lived in the county archive now, under glass, and Odo visited it on Thursdays, which everyone pretended not to know about).

At half past three, Yara went into the depot office alone.

The ledger lay on the counter where it always lay — the station ledger, the old one, the one that had recorded every arrival since the spur first opened, its early pages in a copperplate hand, its middle pages thin and official, its last pages blank for forty years until three weeks ago, when Maren had entered the first survey party in a script so precise it looked printed.

Yara opened it to the current page. The rule was simple and old: every person arriving on the line signed the ledger. Name. Party. Address.

She had signed ledgers a hundred times in her life. She had always written, in the address column, some waystation, some crew house, some *care of* that meant *nothing that will hold.* Once, famously, she had written *the next county.* Maren still brought it up.

Yara picked up the pen. Her hand did not shake, because her hand never shook; it was her chest that did the shaking, quietly, the way a bridge does before it settles.

She wrote her name. She wrote *chief engineer, L.H. Reconstruction Line,* because the column asked for a party and she was the party.

Then she came to the address, and she paused, and she thought about a cave that charged an admission nobody could name, and about how she had paid it — in blood and miles, in a dead man's errand carried the last distance, in nine years of motion that had turned out to be a very long, very slow way of arriving — and she wrote, in a clear and steady hand:

*Depot cottage, Station Road, Lowden Hollow.*

She looked at it. It did not vanish. The ink dried. The world continued.

"There it is," said Bram, from the doorway, where he had apparently been standing long enough to see the whole thing and had said nothing, because of course he hadn't. "First address you've written down in years."

"You were watching."

"Someone had to witness it. That's how deeds work." He came into the office and looked down at the page, and something in his old face was softer than she'd ever seen it on the mountain. "Depot cottage, is it? That's the one with the good stove."

"It's the one with the good stove," Yara confirmed. "And a spare room, which I'm telling you about now so you can't act surprised later."

"I'm never surprised," Bram said. "I'm a guide. I see everything coming." He paused at the door. "Supper at seven, then. Maren's making the stew. Fen's been instructed to keep his opinions about salt to himself, which he won't."

He went out. Yara stood a moment longer with the ledger open, the afternoon light coming through the depot window and lying across the page like a level line, true and level, run by a careful hand.

Outside, the four-note whistle sounded — Odo again, unrepentant — and the first train of the Lowden Hollow Reconstruction Line pulled away up its own track, carrying her family home.

Yara Dunmore closed the ledger, hung up her coat on the peg by the door — her peg, the one with her name on it, which Odo had carved and which she had pretended to find excessive — and stepped out onto the platform of the town she was staying in, into the cold bright evening, without looking back at the door to check that it would still be there.

It would. She'd made sure of the hinges herself.
