## 1. Cold Water Ledger

The wreck had been dead for eleven years, and it still had opinions.

"Tell me again why we're diving a barge that sank with nothing on it but navy furniture," Priya said through the comm, her voice arriving in Xanthe's ear the way all Priya's voices did—dry as old rope and twice as reliable.

"It wasn't a barge, it was a coastal transport," Xanthe said, adjusting her grip on the lift line. "And it sank with a full galley fit-out. Brass fittings, copper kettles, a stove you could park a truck in. The navy wrote it off as unrecoverable, which in navy accounting means 'we can't be bothered,' which in our accounting means 'payday.'"

Below her, forty feet down in water the color of strong tea, the *Corvane* lay on its side in the silt like a sleeping animal that hadn't decided yet whether to wake up cross. Eleven years of river current had combed mud over its hull in long, patient strokes. Fish had moved into the portholes the way families move into foreclosed houses—without permission, without apology, and with every intention of staying.

"Navy restricted zone," Tomas put in from the surface. His voice crackled through the comm with the particular satisfaction of a man reading from a document he'd printed out specifically to be annoying about. "Perimeter buoy, warning sign, the whole ceremony. I want it noted that I noted this."

"Noted," said Priya.

"Noted and disregarded," said Xanthe. "Keep the compressor happy, Tomás."

"My name is Tomas."

"On this crew it's whatever keeps the compressor happy."

She let herself sink the last few feet and settled onto the deck of the *Corvane*, boots finding purchase on weed-slick plating. This was the part of salvage nobody romanticized—the first ten minutes of any dive, when your light carved a small yellow room out of all that green dark and everything beyond it was a question you hadn't earned answers to yet. Xanthe liked the questions. She'd built a life out of them precisely because they never asked anything back.

The galley was aft, behind a hatch that had rusted half-open like a jaw deciding whether to complain. She wedged it wider and slid through sideways, exhaling a curtain of silt that hung around her like a bad reputation.

Inside, the stove was exactly where the manifest said it would be, and it was beautiful—a great iron-and-copper beast with brass rails along its top, furred green now, but underneath the fur: metal worth money. She got her pry bar under the mounting brackets and worked. The work was good. The work was always good. There was a simplicity to prying loose something heavy that had no interest in coming loose, a conversation conducted entirely in leverage and patience, and for a while Xanthe Marchetti was entirely, blessedly occupied.

It was on the third bracket that her glove knocked against something that didn't belong.

Not the stove. Not the bulkhead. A flat plate, maybe the size of her palm, screwed to the inside of the cabinet frame above the galley counter—the kind of place a cook tacked up a duty roster, or a reminder, or a name.

She shone her light on it.

Brass. Polished once, long ago; dulled since, but brass holds its letters the way stone holds weather. Someone had stamped it deliberately, deeply, with the care of a person who wanted it found by the right someone and missed by everyone else.

Two initials, in an old-fashioned script, flanked by a small engraved bird in flight.

*E.M.*

Xanthe stopped breathing for a second, which divers are trained not to do, and then made herself start again, slow and even, while her heart did something unprofessional in her chest.

E.M. Elodia Marchetti. Her mother went by Elodia to strangers and Lolly to exactly three people alive, one of whom was currently forty feet underwater holding her mother's initials in a dead navy ship's galley.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, and heard how strange her own voice sounded through the regulator, flattened and far away, like a stranger's.

Because here was the thing: the *Corvane* had sunk eleven years ago off the southern coast, nowhere near Rivergate, nowhere near anywhere her mother had lived or worked or, as far as Xanthe knew, ever been. Her mother had been a cook—had been, among other things, the best cook on the coast and the worst liar, two qualities Xanthe had inherited in reverse proportions. She'd cooked on ferries, on fishing boats, once gloriously disastrously on a research vessel whose scientists still wrote her letters asking for the fish stew recipe. But the navy? Her mother had disliked the navy with a quiet, total, housewifely thoroughness, the way some people dislike wasps. Xanthe had grown up thinking of it as a personality trait, like disliking cilantro.

So what were her mother's initials doing screwed to the inside of a cabinet on a navy transport?

People stamp their things. Cooks especially—kitchens are territories, and galley gear migrates between ships like cats between households. It could have been a pan. A knife roll. Something sold, traded, requisitioned, stolen, carried aboard by some other cook years before the *Corvane* ever sailed. Brass plates travel. That was the reasonable explanation, and Xanthe reached for it the way you reach for a railing in the dark, and it held, mostly, the way railings do.

Except for the bird.

Her mother had drawn that bird. On flour sacks, on the margins of bills, once—memorably—on the forehead of a harbor inspector who'd tried to shut down the family boat over a paperwork error. A gull in flight, wings swept back, done in three strokes. Xanthe had seen it a thousand times. She had never seen anyone else draw it. And now here it was, engraved in brass, bolted inside a cabinet on a restricted navy wreck at the bottom of cold water, waiting.

"Xanthe?" Priya's voice, sharp now. "You've gone quiet. Quiet is a word I don't associate with you. Report."

"Just admiring the stove," Xanthe said. "It has excellent bones."

"You said that about a sunken ferry and then we spent four days pulling eels out of the bilge."

"Eels are protein, Priya."

"I'm coming down."

"Don't." Too fast. She heard it land wrong even through the comm's flattening. She softened it. "I'm nearly through the mounts. Give me twenty minutes and I'll send up the first load. You can admire it when it's on deck and I've claimed bragging rights."

A pause. Priya's pauses were famous; they had texture, weight, moral dimension. This one said: *I noticed that, and I am filing it.*

"Twenty minutes," Priya said finally. "Then I come down anyway, because one of us should get to see the pretty stove, and it isn't going to be Tomas, because if he comes near my dive rotation with that wrench again I'm going to introduce it to the propeller."

"Heard that," said Tomas.

"You were meant to."

Xanthe turned back to the plate.

Here was the other thing—the thing she was already knowing, in the way you know a storm is coming before the barometer admits it, in the pit-of-the-stomach way that doesn't consult the reasonable mind. The plate hadn't traveled. Plates don't unscrew themselves and reattach. Someone had mounted it there, deliberately, hidden where only someone working inside the cabinet would find it. Hidden the way you hide a note. Hidden the way you hide something you mean to come back for—or something you mean to leave behind for whoever comes after.

Her mother had been dead six years. Whoever came after was standing in the galley breathing borrowed air.

Xanthe looked at the screws. They were slotted, standard, marine-grade—and they were bright. Not new-bright, but brighter than everything else in the cabinet, brighter than eleven years should allow. Somebody had maintained them. Somebody had been down here, in this sealed navy wreck, recently enough that the screws still remembered a screwdriver.

Or—

No. She made herself stop. Twenty minutes was fourteen minutes gone already, and her air gauge was giving her the look gauges give you when they know you're not going to like what they say.

She could leave it. That was the smart move, the move she'd have ordered any diver on any crew to make: unknown artifact, unknown provenance, restricted site—log it, report it, walk away. Salvage had rules for exactly this. Rules kept people alive.

But the plate had her mother's bird on it, and her mother had taught her to swim in water colder than this, laughing, saying, *the sea keeps what it loves, so love it carefully,* and Xanthe had never once in her life walked away from a thing with her mother's handwriting on it, including several strongly worded notes about leaving wet towels on radiators.

She took out the multi-tool she wasn't strictly supposed to carry on a survey dive and backed out the first screw.

* * *

On deck, the afternoon had gone gold and the wind had picked up the way coastal wind does when it wants to remind you who's in charge. The *Kestrel's Wake*—their boat, their whole world, currently held together by paint, prayer, and Tomas's opinions about engine mounts—rocked gently at anchor inside the restricted buoy line, flying the flag of professional indifference.

Tomas met her at the rail with a thermos and a raised eyebrow. "You're up early. Air?"

"Air," she lied, taking the cup. "Stove's coming up next run. It's a monster. We'll need the big lift bag."

"And the rest of the run?"

"Survey photos. Priya can have the fun."

Priya surfaced ten minutes later, climbed the ladder streaming, and stood dripping on the deck looking at Xanthe with the expression of a customs officer who has already seen the false bottom in the suitcase. "Galley's clean," she said. "Too clean. Somebody's been through it."

"Somebody salvaged it years ago, probably. Navy writes these sites off and then private crews pick them like crabs."

"Then where are the crab marks? No pry scars on the cabinets. No tool trails in the silt except yours." Priya wrung out her hair and gave her the look again. "Your bubbles went everywhere, by the way. You were down there moving around like a startled octopus. You never move like that. You move like a librarian."

"A very daring librarian."

"Xanthe."

"Tomas," Xanthe said loudly, "how's the compressor?"

"Happy," said Tomas, appearing in the companionway with the timing of a man who has learned when to be elsewhere and failed. "Also, unrelatedly, I ran the numbers on this job while you were down there, and I want to state for the record that the fuel alone puts us at break-even unless the stove sells high, and stoves sell medium, historically, and—"

"We know, Tomas."

"—and also the navy patrol boat came by."

The deck went quiet in the specific way decks do when the sea reminds you it has teeth. Xanthe turned. Out past the point, gray against the gray-gold water, a navy cutter sat at anchor with the unhurried confidence of an institution that owned the water it floated on.

"They've been there an hour," Tomas said. "Didn't hail us. Didn't need to. Just... sat."

"Sites like this, they check on them," Xanthe said. "Standard."

"Since when do we know what's standard for the navy?" Priya asked mildly.

Since never, Xanthe thought. Since we've spent eleven years being too small and too honest and too broke to matter to them. She drank her tea and watched the cutter and felt the brass plate pressing against her chest through two layers of neoprene and one layer of wetsuit lining, where she'd stowed it under her buoyancy vest like a smuggler, which, technically, as of about ninety minutes ago, she was.

She'd wrapped it in a rag. She'd told herself it was to protect the engraving. She'd known, even underwater, even with her pulse hammering in her ears, that she was lying to herself with the fluency of a woman who'd had practice.

* * *

That night, in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and the crew asleep—Priya's snore carrying from the fo'c'sle with the steady authority of a foghorn, Tomas's answering murmur from the engine room where he'd fallen asleep mid-inventory again—Xanthe unwrapped the plate on the chart table.

The brass caught the lamplight and threw it back warm.

Up close, there was more than the initials and the bird. Around the border, tiny, almost worn away, ran a line of stamped text. She tilted it under the lamp, breathed on it, rubbed it with her sleeve the way her mother had rubbed fogged glasses, and the letters came up one by one like fish rising to feed:

*RIVERGATE — MANIFEST SEAL — E.M.*

Rivergate.

She sat back. Outside, the water slapped the hull in its patient, ancient rhythm, and somewhere out in the dark the navy cutter rode its anchor, and the word sat on the table between her hands like a stone.

Everyone on this coast knew the story of the *Rivergate*, the way everyone knows a ghost story—not the details, just the shape of it. Navy transport, lost with all hands' effects twenty-odd years ago crossing the strait in weather it shouldn't have been in. Cargo manifest sealed by court order. Families who waited. Compensation that never quite arrived, or arrived thin, or arrived addressed to the wrong names. Her mother had never spoken of it. Her mother had changed the subject about the navy so completely and so often that Xanthe had assumed, all her childhood, that there was simply nothing to tell.

There had been something to tell. There had been a brass plate with a hidden bird on it, screwed into a cabinet in a dead ship's galley, maintained by careful hands, waiting twenty years in the cold for a pair of eyes that would recognize the gull.

Her mother had been on the *Rivergate*. Or her mother's hands had been. And her mother—who had died with a passbook containing four hundred and twelve dollars and a recipe box worth more than the boat—her mother had left something behind in that wreck that somebody, recently, had been trying to keep safe.

Or trying to keep buried. The two looked identical from outside a locked cabinet.

Xanthe wrapped the plate back in the rag. Then she unwrapped it, because she couldn't stop looking at it, the way you can't stop touching a bruise. Then she wrapped it again, properly, and stowed it in the false bottom of her chart drawer, beneath the tide tables nobody read, and sat there a long moment with her hand flat on the wood.

Moving on wouldn't be simple this time. She'd known it since the moment her glove had touched cold brass instead of cold iron. Some finds you log and sell and forget. Some finds climb out of the water with you and take the spare bunk.

Through the wheelhouse glass, the navy cutter's riding lights burned steady on the black water. Watching. Waiting. Or just doing its job—but Xanthe had her mother's instincts if not her mother's honesty, and her instincts said the cutter wasn't there for the stove.

Tomorrow, she'd tell the crew about the stove and say nothing else. Tomorrow, they'd finish the job and go home and be broke in the usual way, in the comfortable way.

Unless.

She was still telling herself there was no unless when she fell asleep in the captain's chair with the lamp burning and the plate ticking softly against the drawer bottom as the boat rolled—as if it were knocking. As if it had been knocking for twenty years, and had finally decided not to take no for an answer.
