## 2. The Rival's Offer

The Salt Latch smelled of tar, fried onions, and the particular despair of men who had bet on the wrong horse in the morning races. Xanthe liked it for exactly those reasons. A tavern that smelled like hope was a tavern that lied.

She had come in to spend eleven coppers she couldn't spare on a bowl of stew she didn't taste, and to sit where she could see the harbor through the fogged window. The *Kestrel's Debt* rode at her moorings across the gray water, listing slightly to port the way she always did, like a tired woman leaning on a doorframe. Tomas had spent the afternoon under her hull with a hammer and a vocabulary that would have embarrassed a dockside bishop. The sound of it had carried all the way across the water—*tink, tink, tink*—punctuated by words Xanthe chose not to repeat even in the privacy of her own skull.

Three days since the dive. Three days since the brass plate had come up out of the dark with her mother's initials stamped into it, and she still hadn't told anyone. It sat wrapped in an oilcloth at the bottom of her sea chest, beneath a false layer of spare gloves, and every time she walked past her cabin she felt it the way you feel a tooth that's decided to become a problem.

She was halfway through the stew—turnip-heavy, under-salted, honest—when the door opened and let in a slice of cold air and a man shaped like a well-tailored argument.

Cassian Voss.

Of course. Because the week hadn't been difficult enough.

He stood in the doorway a moment, letting the tavern notice him, which was a skill Xanthe had never managed to acquire and had long ago decided not to envy. He wore a charcoal coat that had cost more than the *Kestrel's Debt* earned in a season, and he wore it carelessly, the way he wore everything, as if the world were a coat rack he happened to be standing near. His hair had gone silver at the temples since she'd last seen him, which was four years ago, in a customs office in Marrow Bay, in a shouting match that had ended with her throwing his contract at his head and him catching it, because he caught everything. That was the whole trouble with Cassian Voss. Nothing ever landed on him.

He spotted her immediately. Of course he did. She was the only person in the Salt Latch eating turnip stew on purpose.

"Xanthe Marchetti." He crossed the room like he owned the floorboards and was merely renting them back to the tavern at a friendly rate. "I heard you were working these waters. I told myself, *no*, the sea's too honest for her now. And yet."

"Voss." She didn't get up. Getting up was a concession. "They let you into harbor towns again? I thought there was a warrant."

"There *was* a warrant. There's also a statute of limitations, and a customs clerk with a fondness for brandy." He sat down across from her without asking, which was another thing about him. "You look well."

"I look like a woman eating bad stew. Don't flatter me, it makes me suspicious, and I'm already suspicious of you on general principles."

"Good. Suspicion is the correct posture around me. It's how I know you're paying attention." He raised two fingers at the tavern keeper. "Whatever she's having, and a bottle of the red that you keep under the counter for people who ask twice."

"I'm not drinking with you."

"You're not. I'm drinking. You're going to sit there and watch me do it, because you want to know why I'm here, and you'd sooner chew glass than ask." The wine arrived, dark and dusty-bottled. He poured one glass, then—deliberately, insolently—poured a second and pushed it toward her. "Also, you've been eating that stew for twenty minutes and your spoon hasn't once found a piece of meat. That's not a stew, that's a rumor of stew. Let me buy you dinner."

"You've been watching me for twenty minutes?"

"I've been watching you for three days. You dive on Tuesdays and Fridays. Your engineer limps when it rains. Your dive lead has a scar on her left forearm she got from a winch cable, and she reads survey charts the way other people read letters from home." He sipped. "I also know the *Kestrel's Debt* needs a new sternpost, her bilge pump is held together with prayer and brazing wire, and your last two contracts paid you barely enough to keep the crew in boots."

The tavern noise seemed to recede, the way it does when someone says something you don't want the room to hear.

"You've had me looked into," she said.

"I've had you *looked after*, in my way. There's a difference." He set the glass down. "Xanthe. I'm not here to gloat. I'm here because I have a job only you can do, and because I'd rather offer it to you than to the pack of hyenas who'd otherwise take it and drown doing it."

"There's always a job only I can do. Last time it ended with me throwing paper at your head."

"It was a very well-thrown contract. I kept it, actually. It had a nice crease."

She should have left. That was the sensible move—stand up, drop a coin for the stew, walk out into the cold, and let the past stay where she'd buried it, which was under four years of silence and one brass plate she was pretending she hadn't found. Instead she wrapped both hands around the wineglass, because the tavern was cold and her hands were colder, and told herself the wine was purely thermal.

"What's the job," she said.

Something flickered across his face—satisfaction, quickly leashed. He'd wanted her to ask. He'd known she'd ask. That was the most irritating thing about Cassian Voss: he was almost never wrong about people, and he was never wrong about her.

"The *Rivergate*," he said.

The name went through her like cold water through a wetsuit.

She kept her face still. She'd had practice at that—her mother's face, her mother's stillness, the family talent for looking calm while the floor gave way. But Voss was watching for exactly that, and she saw him see it, and she hated him a little for the gentleness that came into his voice next, because gentleness from Cassian Voss was a tool, and the worst part was that it was a tool he knew how to use *kindly*.

"Navy transport," he went on. "Went down in the strait off Cape Verren, twenty-two years back. Official story is a storm, cargo lost, all hands mostly saved. Unofficial story is longer and less flattering to the Admiralty, but we needn't get into that over dinner." He turned his glass slowly on the table, a small orbit. "There's a strongbox in her hold. Sealed, iron-banded, naval lock. I have a buyer on the other side of the range who wants it recovered before the passes close, and I need a crew that can actually reach her. That's you. That's the *Kestrel's Debt*, and that stubborn engineer of yours, and that dive lead who reads charts like scripture."

"The *Rivergate* is a restricted site. Navy buoys, navy patrol. You know that."

"I know that the patrol rotates on a schedule I've purchased a copy of. I know the site's been left alone for two years because the navy has decided the wreck is embarrassing and prefers it forgotten. And I know," he said, and leaned forward, and here it came, she thought, here's the button, "that you've already been down there. Tuesday last. Fifty meters off the bow section, where the hull's split. You found something, Xanthe. I don't know what. But you came up fast, and you've been walking around for three days like a woman who's seen a ghost, and I would bet my coat—which is, incidentally, a very good coat—that what you found has your family's name written on it."

The stew had gone cold in front of her. The tavern noise came back all at once, laughter and clatter and someone losing at dice, absurd against the ringing in her ears.

"You've had my dive watched," she said quietly.

"I've had the *wreck* watched. You happened to be on it. Which tells me something, by the way. It tells me the sea has a sense of humor, or a sense of justice, and I've never been able to decide which." He spread his hands. "I'm not going to pretend I know what your mother did or didn't do. I know she was from Rivergate village. I know she left. I know you don't talk about her, and I know that when you do talk about her, you do it in the past tense in a way that means the wound's not closed, it's just been dressed."

"Stop."

"All right." He stopped. He was good at that, too—knowing exactly how far to go and then standing one inch short of the line, hands visible, expression open. It had taken her years to learn that his openness was a kind of architecture. It was built. It was load-bearing. And it was, in its way, sincere. That was what made him dangerous. "The number," he said instead. "You want the number. Everyone wants the number first and asks the questions after."

"I want the questions first, and then I want the number, and then I want you to leave."

"The questions: one, the box comes up intact and unopened. Two, it comes to me, and I take it over the pass. You're done at the shoreline—your part is the water, mine is the mountain. Three, no copies, no photographs, no souvenir brass plates." His eyes flicked to her, mild as milk. "Four, you don't ask me what's in it."

"And if I open it?"

His smile was slow and real and it made her chest hurt, which was the unforgivable part. "Then I'd know you were your mother's daughter after all."

She looked at the wine she hadn't drunk. Outside the window the light was going out of the harbor, the *Kestrel's Debt* becoming a shape, then a silhouette, then a suggestion. *Tink, tink, tink* went Tomas's hammer, faint across the water, still at it in the dark, because the boat was dying and he was the only one who'd say it out loud.

"The number," she said.

"Four thousand crowns."

She laughed. It came out wrong, too sharp. "Four thousand. For a restricted dive, a navy patrol, and winter coming. You couldn't get three dock rats to do it for four thousand."

"Five thousand," Voss said, "and two of it up front, tonight, in coin, before I finish this glass. Enough to buy your sternpost, your bilge pump, your crew's boots, and a winter in port with the stove lit and the stew *with meat in it*. The balance on delivery. And Xanthe—" he said her name the way he always had, both syllables, unhurried, like he was setting something down carefully on a high shelf, "—I know what you're going to say. You're going to say no. You're going to say it with excellent posture and you're going to mean it, tonight, at eleven o'clock when you're lying in your bunk staring at the ceiling. And then at two in the morning you're going to get up and look at your account book, and the account book is going to say what it always says, which is *not enough*, and you're going to come find me tomorrow before I leave harbor, because I leave tomorrow, and you know it, because I told you, because I don't lie to you. That's the one rule I've kept."

"You lie constantly."

"To everyone else." He finished the wine and stood, buttoning his beautiful coat. He left both coins and a folded paper on the table—the contract, she realized, written out in his own hand, already signed, already dated, waiting for hers. "Read it tonight. Show your crew. Let them vote. You're democratic that way; it's one of the things I always liked about you, and it's one of the things that's kept you poor." He paused at the door, silhouetted against the last of the harbor light. "The *Rivergate*, Xanthe. Ask yourself why the navy wants it forgotten. Ask yourself why your mother left Rivergate and never went back. The answers are the same answer. It's sitting in a hold under fifty meters of cold water, and you're the only diver alive who can bring it up."

The door closed behind him. The tavern noise rushed back in.

Xanthe sat for a long time. She didn't touch the paper. She didn't touch the wine. Across the water, faint and stubborn, went the sound of Tomas's hammer—*tink, tink, tink*—mending a boat that was dying, in a harbor that was freezing, at the edge of a season that was closing like a hand.

She thought about the brass plate under the spare gloves in her sea chest. *E.M.* Stamped neat and small, twenty-two years old. Her mother's initials, on a navy wreck, in restricted water, and Cassian Voss standing in her tavern telling her the answers were all the same answer.

She picked up the folded contract at last. She didn't read it. She put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where it sat like a second heartbeat, and she walked out into the cold to go and lie to her crew by omission, which was, she was beginning to suspect, the Marchetti inheritance after all.

Tomorrow, before he left harbor, she would find him.

She already hated herself for knowing it.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Debt* at night was a different animal—lanterns low, the smell of engine oil and Tomas's terrible coffee, Priya's charts spread and pinned in the wheelhouse like the wings of moths. Xanthe climbed aboard and found Tomas in the engine room, lying on his back under the bilge pump with a lamp between his teeth, and she stood over him for a moment watching his boots stick out from under the machinery like a punctuation mark.

"Stew?" he asked, around the lamp.

"Rumor of stew."

"Mm." He slid out, sat up, wiped his hands on a rag that was itself mostly grease. He was fifty-one and built like something the shipyard had forgotten to collect, and he had been with her eight years, and he looked at her the way he looked at pressure gauges—reading the needle before it moved. "You've got the face."

"What face."

"The face you had when you came up from the Tuesday dive. The one where your mouth says fine and everything else says *no*." He set the lamp down. "Captain. What's under the spare gloves?"

Her heart did something complicated. "Nothing you need to—"

"Xanthe." He said it gently. He was the only one who used her name like that, like a hand on the shoulder. "I've been patching this boat for eight years. I know a hull that's hiding water. I know a captain that's hiding something. Same sound, if you knock on it right."

She stood there in the engine room with the contract against her ribs and the brass plate thirty feet away in her cabin, and she said the only true thing she could manage.

"There's a job," she said. "Big one. Come to the wheelhouse, both of you. I'll show you the numbers."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and reached for his boots, and said, in the tone of a man filing something away where he would find it later:

"Numbers first, then. But you're going to knock on yourself eventually, Captain. Everything on this boat does, sooner or later. It's how I find the leaks."

Up in the wheelhouse, under the low lantern, she unfolded Voss's contract and laid it on the chart table, and the name at the top of it—*RIVERGATE*—sat there in his neat, expensive handwriting like a word that had been waiting twenty-two years for her to read it.

Priya came down from the fo'c'sle rubbing her eyes, saw the paper, saw Xanthe's face, and said nothing at all, which from Priya was a shout.

"Vote's tomorrow," Xanthe said. "But first—somebody put the kettle on. This is going to take a while."

And somewhere out in the dark harbor, she knew, Cassian Voss was sleeping the sleep of a man who had already counted his money, because he had never once in his life been wrong about her—and for the first time, that was exactly what she was counting on.
