## 3. Crew Vote

Tomas Reyes had a rule about engines and a rule about people, and both rules were the same: listen for the sound they make when something's wrong.

The *Kestrel's Lark* was making it now. A low, unhappy knock from the port engine, deep in the block, the kind of sound that didn't mean "replace a gasket" so much as "replace your savings account." He lay on his back in the engine bay with a mechanic's mirror in one hand and a flashlight clamped in his teeth, listening to the heartbeat of a boat he loved more than some members of his own family, and what she told him was this: we are in trouble.

He spat the flashlight into his palm and sat up, cracking his head on the manifold out of pure tradition.

"Language," he said to himself, because Priya was somewhere above and had opinions about swearing near the waterline. He'd never understood the theology of it—salt water heard worse from the fish—but a crew ran on small courtesies the way an engine ran on oil, and Tomas was a man who kept his fluids topped up.

The numbers were not good. He'd run them three times that morning, once on paper, once in his head, and once at four in the morning staring at the cabin ceiling, which was the least reliable method but somehow always felt the most honest. The port engine needed a rebuild or a replacement; the hull needed scraping and re-sealing below the waterline; the winch hydraulics were weeping fluid like a guilty witness; and the insurance renewal was due in six weeks, which meant the surveyor would come aboard with his little hammer and his little clipboard and find every one of these things and write them down in his little book.

Total: more money than the *Lark* had earned since the harbor authority dredged the channel and accidentally killed their best wreck site.

Total, in fact: roughly the price of one Cassian Voss contract.

Tomas wiped his hands on a rag that had started life white sometime during the previous administration and climbed up into the daylight. The harbor was doing its morning impression of a working port—gulls arguing, cranes groaning, the smell of diesel and brine and someone frying something regrettable on the pier. Xanthe stood at the rail by the wheelhouse, drinking coffee and looking at nothing, which was how she looked when she was looking at something very hard indeed.

"Captain," he said. "We need to talk about the patient."

"She's dying?"

"She's expensive. Which for boats is the same thing, just slower." He held up the rag like a surgeon presenting evidence. "Port engine's knocking from down where the crank lives. That's not a fix, that's a funeral with extra steps. Hull needs work before the surveyor sees her or our insurance goes the way of the engine. And the winch—" he patted the rail beside him affectionately, the way you'd pat a sick friend, "—the winch has developed feelings it expresses through leakage."

Xanthe took a sip of coffee. "Number."

"You don't want the number."

"Tomas."

"It's a big number. It's a number with a comma in an unfriendly place." He watched her face while he said it, because that was the other half of his job description, the part they never put on the hiring notice: watch the captain. "It's roughly, and I want to be clear I'm rounding generously in our favor, the number Voss wrote on that napkin last night."

She flinched.

It was small. If Tomas hadn't spent eleven years learning to read Xanthe Marchetti the way he read gauges—a twitch here, a pressure drop there—he'd have missed it entirely. But he caught it: the half-second hitch when the name came up, the way her thumb pressed flat against the coffee cup as if holding something down.

Not the number. The name. She hadn't flinched at Voss's napkin arithmetic; she'd flinched at *Voss*, or maybe at whatever Voss had been selling, and there was a difference, and the difference mattered.

"Huh," said Tomas, warmly, and filed it away.

"Don't say 'huh' like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you've found a rattle."

"I have found a rattle." He leaned on the rail next to her, close enough to be companionable, far enough to be deniable. "In the boat or in the captain, remains to be determined. But I'll tell you the honest truth, which you hired me to provide along with the swearing-adjacent humor: we can't afford to say no to this job, and I'm not sure we can afford to say yes either. Those are usually the interesting ones."

"I know."

"And you already knew that, which means you called this meeting in your head before I climbed out of the engine bay, which means—" he counted on grease-blackened fingers, "—you're going to call a vote tonight, and you wanted my numbers first so nobody could accuse you of selling them a dream."

Xanthe looked at him then, properly, and there was something tired and grateful in it. "Eleven years," she said, "and you still can't let me have a single mystery."

"Mysteries are for passengers. Crew gets maintenance schedules." He pushed off the rail. "Call it for eight. I'll have the figures written up neat. Well—written up. Neatness is Priya's department."

* * *

By eight o'clock the galley was full in the way it only got for votes: everyone present, door shut, the little brass lamp swinging gently overhead like a metronome counting out the fate of their employment. Tomas had the ledger open on the table between a plate of Priya's flatbread and the crumpled napkin Voss had left behind, which Xanthe had smoothed out and flattened under the salt cellar, and which Tomas regarded with the enthusiasm of a man regarding a live grenade someone had helpfully labeled *opportunity*.

"Right." He turned the ledger around. "The state of the nation."

He gave it to them straight, because that was the deal, the whole deal, the only deal. The *Lark* needed four thousand in repairs to stay legal and another two thousand to stay reliable, and they had nine hundred and change in the account, and the next three contracts on the board paid enough to cover fuel and food and not much else. He drew the columns himself, big and plain, no engineer's shorthand, because he'd learned long ago that a crew vote died in ambiguity the way an engine died in dirty fuel.

"So," he finished. "That's the hole. Here's the rope somebody's offering to throw us."

He nodded at the napkin. Nobody picked it up.

Priya spoke first, arms crossed, dive knife still on her belt because she'd come straight off the afternoon survey. "One dive. One box. Off a restricted wreck, under a navy patrol schedule, in late season water." She ticked each item off on her fingers like she was checking a kit list. "I can make the dive safe. I can't make it legal, and I can't make it boring, and boring is the thing that keeps divers alive."

"The pay's real, though," said Devi from the doorway, where she hovered the way she always did during votes, technically crew, technically cook, officially the conscience with the sharpest elbows. "That's real money. That's the *Lark* whole again and wages through winter and maybe—" her voice did the thing it did when she thought about her sister's school fees, "—maybe a little left over."

"Maybe," said Priya.

"Voss pays," said Devi. "He's a snake but he's a solvent snake."

"There's no such thing as a solvent snake," said Priya. "There's only snakes who haven't bitten yet."

Tomas let them go at it for a few minutes, because a vote that didn't argue first wasn't a vote, it was a mutiny waiting for paperwork. He watched the table instead. Devi wanted the money for reasons that had names and school uniforms attached. Priya wanted the risk priced honestly, which was her way of loving people. And Xanthe—

Xanthe sat with her hands around a cold cup of coffee and listened to all of it without weighing in, and every time the conversation swung near the wreck itself—not the pay, not the risk, but the *Rivergate*, the name of her—something behind her eyes pulled tight like a valve seating.

"Captain," Tomas said finally. "You've been quiet even by your standards."

"I get a vote same as anyone."

"You get the tiebreaker same as anyone. First you get to tell us why you hate this job, because you do—I've watched you look at that napkin like it owed you money and a personal apology—and this crew doesn't sign on blind. Not anymore. Not after Corvo Shoals."

A small silence went around the table at the name of the old disaster, touching it lightly and moving on, the way you touch a scar.

Xanthe set down the cup. For a moment Tomas thought she'd give them the whole story—the brass plate, the initials, whatever history sat coiled under that woman's calm like cable under tension—and he found himself leaning forward despite himself, rag still in his pocket, hands ready for anything.

"We need the work," she said instead. "And I trust this crew to handle hard water better than anyone on this coast. That's my case. Yes or no, honestly given."

It was a good speech. It was also, Tomas noted, a wall where a window should have been.

They voted. Hands, openly, because secret ballots were for parliaments and people with something to hide. Devi, yes, immediately, with the fierce relief of a woman whose problem had briefly acquired a solution. Priya, after a long look at Xanthe that contained an entire unsaid paragraph, yes—with conditions: her dive plan, her call on weather, and her call on aborting, no appeals. Tomas himself, yes, because the numbers didn't leave room for romance and because, though he'd have denied it under oath, some part of him wanted to see what was buried under all that flinching.

Four yeses out of five hands raised, counting Xanthe's own, which came up last and slowest, like something being lowered rather than lifted.

Wait. Four. He counted again. Five crew at the table, four hands.

Devi noticed too. "Fen?"

Fen Okafor, youngest aboard, deckhand and rope-witch and the only person Tomas had ever seen win an argument against Priya, sat with his arms folded and his jaw set. "No," he said.

"Fen—" Devi started.

"My cousin worked the Rivergate route. Dockside, loading. He drowned two years before she went down, different wreck, same navy, same 'unlisted cargo, don't ask.'" Fen's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Every time the navy's involved, somebody poor ends up at the bottom of something. I'm saying no. You can outvote me. But I'm saying it out loud so it's on the record that somebody said it."

Nobody laughed. Nobody argued, either, which was its own kind of respect. Xanthe looked at the boy—young man, Tomas corrected himself, he kept forgetting he'd grown—for a long moment.

"Your no stays on the record," she said. "And if at any point this stops feeling right, you say so, and we stop. That's not a promise I'm making to be kind. It's a condition of the job."

Fen considered this, then nodded once, the nod of a man accepting terms he intended to enforce.

"Carried, four to one," said Tomas, and closed the ledger, and heard the vote land in the room like a hatch sealing. "Congratulations, everyone. We're either saved or doomed, and knowing this crew, probably both."

There was some laughter at that, the careful kind, and Devi got up to put water on for tea because in her cosmology no decision, good or ill, should be made uncaffeinated, and Priya began talking dive depths at Fen in the gentle tone she used for pulling splinters.

Tomas gathered his ledger and paused beside Xanthe at the head of the table. She was looking at the napkin again, at Voss's handwriting, at the figure that was going to save them, and her face was doing the thing faces do over graves.

"One question, Captain," he said quietly. "Engineer to captain. Off the books."

"Off the books," she agreed.

"When Voss said *Rivergate* last night—you flinched. I saw it. So here's my question, and you don't have to answer it tonight." He tapped the table once, softly, the way he'd tap a gauge that read wrong. "Is the rattle in the money, or is it in the wreck?"

Xanthe was silent long enough that the lamp swung twice overhead.

"Both," she said finally. "But mostly the wreck."

Tomas nodded slowly, and decided, in the private office of his own skull where he kept such decisions, that he would be watching his captain closer on this one. Not because he doubted her—eleven years buys a faith deeper than doubt—but because he'd learned that the jobs that saved the boat were rarely the jobs that saved the people, and Xanthe Marchetti carried her wrecks inside her hull where no surveyor's hammer would ever reach.

"Good answer," he said. "Wrong answer, probably. But honest. That's the important part."

He was halfway up the ladder to the engine bay when he heard her behind him, low, almost to herself, almost certainly not meant for his ears:

"Mother, what did you walk away from?"

Tomas stopped on the ladder, one hand on the rail, and smiled without any humor in it at all.

Well, then, he thought. Now we're getting somewhere.
