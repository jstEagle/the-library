## 4. What Mother Swore Off

The bus to Rivergate village smelled of wet wool and diesel, and Xanthe spent the first hour of the ride pretending she wasn't on it.

She'd told the crew she was going to check a lead on tide tables. Tomas had looked at her the way he looked at a bilge pump making a new noise—skeptical, but willing to let it prove itself. Priya hadn't looked at her at all, which was worse. Priya's silences had a way of filing themselves for later.

So here she was, three hours inland along a river valley that narrowed like a throat, wearing her one clean shirt and carrying a brass plate wrapped in a sock at the bottom of her bag like contraband. Which, in a sense, it was.

The village announced itself the way small places do: a church spire first, then a scatter of slate roofs, then the smell of someone burning leaves. The bus sighed to a stop outside a general store whose window display hadn't changed since Xanthe was nine years old—the same tin of boiled sweets, the same postcards of the falls, sun-bleached to ghosts of themselves.

Her aunt's house sat at the end of Ferry Lane, third from the corner, with a blue door her mother had once painted as a birthday surprise and a garden that had long since stopped pretending to be tidy. Xanthe stood at the gate for a full minute, working up the nerve, feeling about twelve years old and forty-two simultaneously.

*Knock*, she told herself. *You've dived wrecks at sixty meters. You can knock on a door.*

She knocked.

Footsteps. A pause. The door opened on Marta Marchetti—her mother's older sister, seventy if she was a day, with her mother's dark eyes set in a face that had decided decades ago to stop softening itself for anyone's benefit.

Marta looked at her for a long moment. "You have her chin," she said finally. "I always hated that about you."

"Hello to you too, Aunt Marta."

"Well." She stepped back from the door. "Are you going to stand in the cold being witty, or are you coming in? I've got tea. It's terrible. It's been terrible since 1998 and I see no reason to change now."

* * *

The kitchen was exactly as Xanthe remembered it from the one visit she'd made at fourteen—a visit that had ended with her mother pulling her out by the wrist before the tea even cooled. Yellow wallpaper gone cream with age. A clock that ticked slightly behind real time, so that living in this house meant living two minutes in the past. Her aunt seemed to have made peace with that arrangement.

"So," Marta said, setting down two chipped cups. "You're diving the *Rivergate*."

Xanthe's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because Cassian Voss came through here Tuesday, asking after the Marchetti name in that voice of his, all silk over gravel. And because you're sitting in my kitchen looking like a woman who's come to ask a question she already knows the answer to." Marta sat, folding her hands. "Ask it."

Xanthe unwrapped the brass plate from the sock and laid it on the table between them. The initials caught the window light: **E.M.**

Elena Marchetti.

Marta went very still. Not shocked—Xanthe realized with a strange lurch—but braced. Like someone who'd been waiting twenty years for a delivery.

"Where," Marta said quietly, "did you get this."

"Off a wreck off Cape Verren. Navy-restricted water. It was bolted inside a crew locker." Xanthe turned the plate over. Stamped faintly on the reverse, almost worn away: a manifest number. "Aunt Marta. What was my mother's name doing on a navy transport?"

Marta reached out and touched the plate with one finger, the way you'd touch something that might still be hot.

"You'd better hear it properly," she said. "And I'd better start further back than you think."

* * *

Xanthe was seven years old, and the summer smelled of cut grass and her mother's cigarette smoke, and they were staying at a rented cottage near the coast because her mother said they needed sea air, which was a lie, because what her mother needed that summer was to be somewhere no one knew her name.

Xanthe remembered it in fragments, the way children do. Her mother on the porch steps at night, not reading, just holding a letter she never opened. Her mother teaching her to skip stones and stopping mid-throw when a car slowed on the lane. Her mother crying once, briefly and furiously, in the kitchen, then turning around and saying, "Right. Pancakes," as though grief were a chore on a list.

And Xanthe remembered the man who came to the door on a Sunday.

He wore a good coat. He spoke softly, the way people speak to animals they don't want to spook. Xanthe was sent outside, but the windows of the cottage were thin, and she heard most of it anyway—or thought she did, for years, until memory and invention blurred into a story she half-told herself: that the man wanted money from her mother, and her mother refused, and that was that.

Now, sitting in her aunt's kitchen with the bad tea going cold, she heard it again—this time whole.

"His name was Aldous Renwick," Marta said. "He was a solicitor. Or he said he was; I never verified a thing about that man in my life. He came representing the estate of your grandfather—my father, Tommaso Marchetti. Your mother's inheritance."

"I didn't know Grandfather had money."

"He didn't. That was rather the point." Marta's mouth twisted. "Tommaso was a dockworker his whole life. But his brother—your great-uncle Silvio—went into shipping during the war years, and Silvio made arrangements. Contracts. Conscripted labor, mostly. Men pulled off these very docks, paid a fraction of what they were owed, their families paid nothing at all when the ships went down. The difference went into accounts under family names. Ours among them."

The clock ticked its two-minutes-behind tick. Somewhere outside, a dog was barking at absolutely nothing, with tremendous conviction.

"How much?" Xanthe asked.

"For our branch? Enough to change a life. Not enough to buy a conscience." Marta turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Your mother refused it. Refused it twice—once when Silvio died, once when Renwick came that summer. She told him she wouldn't take so much as a stamp bought with that money. He warned her the estate would be placed in trust. Sealed. Held against her claim indefinitely." Marta shrugged. "She said fine. Let it rot."

"And the strongbox on the *Rivergate*—"

"Was the trust. Being moved inland for storage when the ship went down. Twenty-two years ago this autumn." Marta looked at her, and for the first time her voice wavered, just slightly, like a floorboard under weight. "You understand what I'm telling you, girl. The money in that box is blood money. Every coin of it. And your mother spent her whole life making sure none of it ever touched us."

* * *

Afterward, Xanthe stood in the garden while Marta "put the kettle on again," which everyone in the family understood to mean *I need five minutes alone.*

The garden was overgrown but the old apple tree was still there, the one with the rope-scarred branch where her mother had hung a swing. Xanthe remembered swinging on it, higher and higher, while Elena stood below with her arms crossed—not worried, never worried, just watching, as if measuring her daughter against some horizon only she could see.

*Why didn't you tell me?*

But even asking it, she knew the answer. Because telling would have meant explaining. Explaining would have meant naming the families, the dockworkers, the drowned men. And her mother had built her entire life on the principle that the Marchetti name should be as light as possible—as unattached, as unclaimed, as invisible as a woman could make herself. Xanthe had inherited that invisibility without ever knowing it was an inheritance. She'd thought she chose salvage because she loved the water. She had, partly. But she'd also chosen a trade where nobody asked where you came from, where names mattered less than hands, where you could be useful without ever being known.

Her mother hadn't sworn off money. She'd sworn off *weight*. And she'd passed the vow down like eye color, without a word.

Xanthe took out the brass plate and held it up to the grey sky.

It made sense now—all of it. Why a plate stamped E.M. had been bolted inside a locker on a navy transport. Her mother had been aboard. Once. Perhaps to see the box with her own eyes, perhaps to sign something, perhaps—Xanthe's breath caught—perhaps to refuse it a third time, in person, at the last possible moment. And the ship had gone down with her refusal bolted to its wall, waiting twenty-two years at the bottom of cold water for her daughter to find it.

*The past has surfaced*, she'd written in her log that night, and felt foolish about it. She didn't feel foolish now.

* * *

They ate soup at the kitchen table—barley and whatever Marta had in the garden that frost hadn't claimed—and Marta talked, and Xanthe listened, and the picture assembled itself piece by piece.

"The navy wasn't stealing the cargo, if that's what Voss implied," Marta said. "The navy was *carrying* stolen cargo. There's a difference, and the difference matters. Someone in the service arranged the transport—quietly, off-manifest. Someone who wanted that trust moved somewhere it couldn't be examined. The wreck was convenient for them. A sealed strongbox at the bottom of the sea settles a lot of questions."

"And now someone wants it raised."

"Now someone wants it raised and *disappeared properly*. There's a difference there too." Marta fixed her with those dark Marchetti eyes. "Cassian Voss isn't paying you to recover an inheritance, Xanthe. He's paying you to make it vanish. And whoever's paying him—" she tapped the table once, sharply, "—is the same kind of person who put it on that ship in the first place."

Xanthe stared into her soup. "The contract says deliver to Voss. Sealed. Unopened."

"And will you?"

"I signed a contract."

"You signed a contract with a man who came through this village asking questions about your dead mother's name and didn't have the decency to mention he knew it was yours." Marta leaned back. "I met his type before he was born, girl. Men like Voss treat loyalty like ballast—they keep it while it keeps them steady, and they jettison it the moment the water gets rough."

"That's not fair. We have history."

"History," Marta said, "is just the part of the wreck you can still see above the silt."

Xanthe laughed despite herself—a short, surprised sound. "You've been saving that one."

"Twenty years. I'm a patient woman. Ask anyone. Actually, don't ask anyone, they'll tell you I'm difficult, which is different."

* * *

Evening came down the valley like slow blue ink. Marta lit a lamp instead of the overhead bulb, and the kitchen shrank to something warm and golden, and Xanthe found herself telling her aunt things she hadn't planned to say—the boat's failing engine, the vote that had passed by one, the way Tomas watched her now, the way Priya's silence filed itself away.

"And the plate," Marta said. "What will you do with it?"

"Keep it. For now."

"Mm." Marta studied her over the lamp. "Your mother kept a photograph of you in her coat pocket for fifteen years after she stopped speaking to me. I found it when we buried her. Worn soft at the edges from handling." She paused. "People who love you badly are still people who love you. Remember that when you're deciding what to do with that box."

"It's not mine to decide. It's not mine at all."

"No," Marta agreed. "It isn't. It belongs to the families Silvio robbed. The dockworkers' children. The widows who were paid in condolences." She said it plainly, without drama, and somehow that made it land harder than any speech could have. "Your mother knew that. It's why she refused. She couldn't give the money back—didn't know how, didn't have the proof, and the proof was locked in a box at the bottom of the sea. So she did the only thing left to her. She made sure she was never worth anything to anyone who might come looking."

Xanthe's throat tightened. "That's a hell of a way to live."

"It's a hell of a way to die, too. Alone, in a rented room, with a name she'd spent thirty years sanding off everything she owned." Marta rose and began stacking cups, her back to Xanthe, her voice carefully level. "Don't you dare do the same thing. I'm too old to lose two Marchettis to the same stupid vow."

* * *

Xanthe walked back toward the village inn in the dark, the brass plate heavy in her bag, her breath fogging in air that already smelled faintly of snow.

At the corner of Ferry Lane she stopped and looked back. The blue door glowed faintly under the lamp. Two minutes behind the rest of the world, that house. Maybe that was the family gift—not the money, not the name, but the habit of living slightly in the past, keeping faith with what was lost.

Her mother had crossed this valley once, heading back empty-handed. Xanthe was about to cross it heading the other way, carrying the thing her mother refused. The symmetry of it sat in her chest like a stone—or like ballast. She hadn't decided which yet.

One thing she had decided, standing in that overgrown garden with the apple tree creaking: she was not delivering that strongbox to Cassian Voss. Whatever the contract said, whatever the pay promised, whatever history lived between them. The money belonged to the families on the ledgers—to people who'd waited twenty-two years already, and whose names she intended to learn.

Which meant she was about to betray a rival, defraud a buyer, and cross a mountain range ahead of the snows carrying evidence that implicated the navy, her family, and quite possibly herself.

She pulled her collar up and smiled into the dark. Her mother would have hated it.

That, more than anything, convinced her it was right.

Behind her, far down the valley road, headlights swung around the bend—slow, deliberate, a car taking its time through a village where strangers drew attention. Xanthe stepped back into the shadow of the wall and watched it pass: a dark sedan, government plates, moving toward the inn where she'd booked a room under a name that wasn't hers.

So. They were already watching the road to Rivergate.

She waited until the taillights faded, then turned and walked the other way, toward the freight yard at the edge of town, where the night train would take her back to the coast—and where, starting tomorrow, she was going to need to be a very good liar indeed.
