## 5. Descent to the Wreck

Priya Anand had a rule about diving wrecks: the water tells you everything before you see anything. Temperature first. Then light. Then that particular hush a dead ship keeps, like a house where everyone left in a hurry and never came back.

The Rivergate was hushed from forty meters up.

She hung on the downline and let the cold climb through her suit, checking her gauges out of habit rather than need—she'd checked them three times on the surface, and Tomas had checked them twice after her, because Tomas checked everything twice and then apologized for it. Below her, Xanthe's torch beam swung slow arcs through the green-black water, patient as a lighthouse that had decided to go looking for ships instead of waiting.

"Bottom's at fifty-two," Xanthe said, voice flattened by the comm unit into something calm and slightly underwater itself. "Visibility maybe eight meters. She's sitting upright."

"She always did have better manners than most of this fleet," Priya said.

The Rivergate had gone down twenty-two years ago with a hold full of official cargo and an official story that didn't survive contact with anyone who'd actually been there. Navy transport, navy wreck site, navy exclusion buoy bobbing cheerfully two hundred meters off the stern like a dog tied outside a shop it wasn't allowed into. Officially they weren't here either. Unofficially, Cassian Voss's contract money was sitting in an escrow account, and Voss's paperwork—which Priya had read twice, looking for the trap she was sure was in there somewhere—was immaculate. That was what worried her. Men with dirty intentions usually had dirty paperwork. Clean paperwork meant someone above him wanted it clean.

"Descending," she said, and let the wreck come up to meet her.

* * *

It came up in pieces, the way big things do. First the masts, or what the masts had become—skeleton spars furred with white anemones, leaning like old men comparing ailments. Then the superstructure, its windows long since gone, giving the wheelhouse the look of a skull wearing a hat. Then the hull itself, vast and brown-green, a hill that had once been a ship, and Priya felt the familiar tightening in her chest that she'd learned, over eleven years of salvage work, not to call awe where anyone could hear her.

"Port side cargo hatch," Xanthe said. "That's our door."

They swam the length of the main deck, torches carving tunnels through the murk. Fish scattered and regrouped, scattered and regrouped, offended by the interruption. A conger eel the length of Priya's leg regarded them from a porthole with the weary expression of a tenant whose landlord has finally shown up.

"Hold on." Xanthe stopped. Her beam fixed on something near the base of the forward mast. "Priya. Come look at this."

Priya finned over. At first she saw nothing but rust and weed, and then her eye sorted it—the way you learn to sort it, the way a doctor sees a fracture in an X-ray that looks like nothing to everybody else.

Marks in the growth. Clean ones.

Somebody had cut through twenty years of marine fouling recently enough that the exposed metal hadn't yet dulled. Tool marks, narrow-gauge, deliberate—a cutting torch or a hydraulic shear, worked along the seam of a deck plate near the forward hold. Not torn open in haste. Opened carefully, by someone who knew ships.

"When were we last on this site?" Priya asked, though she knew the answer. Never. They'd done the survey from the boat only, sonar and drop-camera, and the drop-camera footage had shown the fouling intact along every seam they'd mapped.

"We weren't," Xanthe said quietly. "Nobody was. This is a restricted site."

"Somebody restricted it after they got what they wanted, maybe." Priya ran a gloved finger near—not on—the cut edge. The metal inside was bright. Weeks bright, not months. "This is fresh, Xanthe. I'd say under a month. Maybe under two."

Xanthe was silent for a moment. Through the comm Priya could hear her breathing, slow and controlled, the way she breathed when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Voss said the box was untouched," she said finally. "He said nobody else had the location."

"He also said his paperwork was clean, and I don't trust a man whose filing system is better than mine." Priya straightened. "Let's look at the rest of her before we decide what this means."

* * *

They spent forty minutes on the bottom, which was fifteen more than Priya liked and exactly as many as Xanthe had planned for, because Xanthe planned dives the way other people planned invasions. They photographed the cargo hatch, the hold access, the strongroom door aft—still sealed, still wearing its navy lock, which was either very good news or very bad news depending on how much you trusted locks—and shot reference frames of the whole amidships section until Priya's camera card groaned.

It was on the way back to the upline that Priya saw the second thing.

She almost missed it. A shadow among shadows, just past the break of the poop deck: a shape too regular to be wreckage. She hung in the water and stared until her eyes watered behind the mask, and then she swam closer, and then she wished she hadn't quite so quickly.

A body. Or what the sea leaves of one, after twenty-two years and whatever ate the rest. Navy uniform fragments still clinging—she could see the braid pattern on what remained of a sleeve, the kind worn by a junior officer. Near the hand, half-buried in silt, a small case, watertight, brass corners.

"Priya?" Xanthe's voice. "You've stopped moving."

"There's a body here," Priya said. "Forward of the poop deck. Officer's uniform." She hesitated. Twenty-two years drowned; whoever this was had family somewhere, or didn't, and neither possibility was hers to solve today. "I'm going to look at the case. Just the case. We log the rest and report it—that's not ours to disturb."

"You're sure?"

"No," Priya said honestly. "But the case might be, and I'd rather know what we're carrying out of here than find out later from someone less polite."

The case came free with a soft sigh of silt. It was lighter than it looked, sealed, unmarked except for a stenciled number along one edge that Priya committed to memory and photographed twice. Whatever it was, it wasn't salvage. It was evidence. She could feel the difference in her hands, the way you can feel the difference between a tool and a weapon without being told.

"Surface interval's coming," Xanthe said. "Bring it up. We'll decide on the boat."

They rose together through the darkening water, and Priya found herself glancing back once at the wreck below—the great hush of it, the anemone-furred masts, the small bright wound in its side where someone had already been digging.

Ships keep secrets, she thought. But somebody had come asking questions before them. And whoever it was hadn't bothered to ask nicely.

* * *

The trouble started while they were still at their safety stop.

Priya heard it first through the water—a deep, rhythmic thrumming, felt in the chest more than the ears. She looked up. Fifteen meters above, distorted into wobbling ribbons by the surface, a shape crossed overhead. Long. Gray. Moving with the unhurried authority of something that owned the water it sat on.

"That's not a fishing boat," Xanthe said.

"No," Priya agreed. "Fishing boats don't make my fillings hum."

They completed the stop anyway—Priya insisted, and Xanthe, to her credit, didn't argue, because the one thing worse than a patrol boat finding you at depth was a patrol boat finding you bent. They ascended slow and boring and law-abiding, and broke the surface into a gray afternoon and the flat slap of chop against the hull of the *Magpie*, Tomas's beloved, battered workhorse of a salvage vessel.

The patrol boat sat four hundred meters off. Navy gray, low-slung, a number on its bow that Priya couldn't read at this distance but recognized the style of. It wasn't approaching. It was circling. Slow, wide, patient circles, like a shark that had been to etiquette school.

Tomas met them at the rail, hauling Priya's gear off her with the fussy tenderness he reserved for equipment and people he considered a subset of equipment. "You're late," he said. "You're always late. I've started budgeting for it."

"We found a body," Priya said, and watched all the humor drain out of his face at once.

* * *

An hour later, the *Magpie*'s little cabin smelled of tea and neoprene, and the three of them were crowded around the table with the camera's screen glowing between them, clicking through photographs.

Tomas had run the patrol boat's profile through the registry database on the laptop he treated better than his own children. "Coastal patrol vessel *Kestrel*," he reported. "Based out of Halloway Station, forty klicks south. Standard complement of twelve, standard orders of 'discourage unauthorized activity,' and—" he scrolled, frowned, scrolled again— "huh."

"Huh is not a word I like hearing from you," Xanthe said.

"Their patrol schedule's public. They were logged as running the southern channel today. All week, actually." He tapped the screen. "Someone pulled them off it and pointed them here. That takes authorization above station level."

"So the navy knows we're here," Priya said.

"The navy knew we were coming," Xanthe corrected quietly. "There's a difference. Voss's permits went through channels. Channels have windows in them, and people look through windows." She turned her teacup slowly on the table, not drinking. "The question is whether they're watching us or waiting for us."

"And the other question," Priya said, "is the one you're both pretending isn't on the table." She reached into her dive bag and set the watertight case down in the middle of the table with a click that sounded louder than it was. Brass corners, stenciled number, twenty-two years of patience. "Because I found fresh tool marks on a wreck that's supposed to be untouched, and a dead officer holding a sealed case, and a patrol boat doing figure-eights over our heads, and I am officially saying out loud that this job has more layers than Voss told us."

Tomas looked at the case the way he looked at wiring he suspected was live. "Are we opening it?"

"Not yet," Xanthe said.

"Are we ever opening it, or are we keeping it as a decorative centerpiece? Because it does tie the room together."

"Tomas." Xanthe's voice was gentle, which from Xanthe meant serious. "If that case holds what I think it might hold, opening it is the point of no return. Once we know, we can't unknow. And whatever's in there has been worth killing over for twenty-two years." She glanced at the porthole, where the gray shape of the *Kestrel* slid past, unhurried. "I want us all to choose knowing. Not stumble into it."

Priya studied her captain. Eleven years she'd followed this woman into bad water, and in all that time she'd catalogued perhaps four occasions when Xanthe Marchetti had looked genuinely afraid, and every one of them had involved her family name spoken aloud by someone else. This was the fifth. It was written all over her, in the careful hands and the undrunk tea.

"You know something," Priya said. It wasn't a question.

"I know some things," Xanthe said. "Not enough. Ask me again when I've talked to my aunt."

"You have an aunt?"

"Everyone has an aunt, Tomas. Mine just happens to live in Rivergate village and hasn't spoken to me in nine years."

Priya let it go, because that was the deal between them: Xanthe got her silences, and Priya got to notice them. She picked up the camera instead and clicked back through the day's photos, and that was when she found the third thing, the one she'd missed on the little screen at depth.

She'd shot a frame of the forward hold entrance on their way out—habit, documentation, the thousand small redundancies of a careful diver. In the corner of the frame, half-lost in murk, was the cargo hatch cover. And on the hatch cover, catching her torch beam at an angle, were marks. Not the clean surgical cuts from earlier. These were older, cruder—scrape marks, drag marks, the signature of equipment hauled roughly across steel.

And beside them, faint but unmistakable when you knew what to look for: a stencil. Partially abraded. The letters *R.G.* and beneath them a date fragment, and beneath that, in smaller characters, a code prefix that Priya had seen exactly once before in her career, stamped on crates in a government warehouse she'd never been cleared to enter.

"Tomas," she said. "Pull up the contract file. The inventory manifest Voss gave us."

He did. They compared. The manifest listed the Rivergate's declared cargo: vehicles, rations, mail. Routine. Boring. The kind of list designed to put an auditor to sleep.

"What's the code prefix stand for?" Xanthe asked.

"It doesn't stand for anything public," Priya said. "I saw it once, eight years ago, on a job I'm not technically allowed to discuss, and the man who explained it to me did so in a parking garage." She looked up and met her captain's eyes. "It's a payroll designation. Conscripted labor wages. Money being moved for people who were never meant to be paid."

The cabin went quiet. Outside, the *Kestrel* completed another slow circle, its wake writing and rewriting the same gray sentence across the water.

"So the box," Tomas said slowly, "isn't treasure."

"The box is somebody's wages," Priya said. "Twenty-two years of somebody's wages. And somebody else has been down there recently, cutting at her seams, and I don't think they got what they came for—because if they had, why would Voss be paying us?" She tapped the photo of the scrape marks. "We're not recovering this box, Xanthe. We're racing for it. And I count at least three runners: us, whoever made those cuts, and the navy circling overhead pretending to be weather."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the flat calm Priya had learned to fear more than shouting.

"Then we dive again tomorrow. First light. Before the *Kestrel* finishes its breakfast." She stood, took the watertight case, and locked it in the cabin safe with a care that bordered on ceremony. "And Priya—when we bring that strongbox up, we bring it up quiet, and we move. No harbor. No port authorities. Straight to the mountain road."

"You want to cross the range with it," Tomas said. "In autumn. With the snows coming."

"I want to be somewhere the navy's boats can't follow," Xanthe said. "Yes."

Priya looked at the photograph one more time—the scrape marks, the ghost of the stencil, the bright wound in an old ship's side—and felt the dread settle into her bones, cold and specific, the way it did before the bad jobs. Not the dread of drowning. She'd made peace with that years ago.

The dread of being early to a grave someone else was still robbing.

Above them, engine noise changed pitch. The *Kestrel* had stopped circling.

It was coming toward them.
