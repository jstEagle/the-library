## 6. The Officer Who Waits

Lieutenant Edric Halloway had been awake for nineteen hours, and the coffee on the *Vigilant* tasted like it had been brewed from the anchor chain. He drank it anyway. Discipline, his first commanding officer had told him, was mostly a matter of doing unpleasant things on schedule until they stopped feeling unpleasant.

Twenty-two years in, the coffee still felt unpleasant. He drank it anyway.

The patrol vessel sat two miles off the wreck site, engines idling at a murmur, close enough to watch and far enough to pretend it wasn't watching. Through the wheelhouse glass, the gray morning showed him the salvage boat—a squat, hard-used vessel named the *Magpie*, which was either an honest name or a very honest one, depending on how you felt about magpies—anchored over the coordinates. Divers' bubbles rose in silver strings and burst.

Halloway set his cup on the chart table and opened the signal folder for the fourth time. He had read it four times already. He was hoping, perhaps, that the words would rearrange themselves on the fifth pass. They declined to.

**PRIORITY — EYES ONLY.** *Vigilant to maintain station Rivergate site. Salvage activity to be permitted to proceed unimpeded. All materials recovered to be logged, catalogued, and transferred to Naval Records Division upon crew's return to harbor. No independent reporting. No photographs. Acknowledge.*

There was nothing unusual about the *permitting*. The navy permitted things all the time; it was the navy's second-favorite activity after prohibiting them. What sat wrong was the phrasing. *Unimpeded.* *No independent reporting.* He had been writing and receiving signals for two decades, and he knew the difference between an order and a lid. This was a lid.

He acknowledged it, because he was Lieutenant Edric Halloway, and Lieutenant Edric Halloway acknowledged things. Then he sat down across from it and had the argument he'd been having with himself since the folder arrived at midnight.

"Sir?" Ensign Corliss appeared at the wheelhouse door with a clipboard and the particular brightness of a young officer who had not yet learned that most paperwork was a séance conducted with dead decisions. "The salvage vessel's dive boat is in the water. Third dive of the morning. Shall I log it?"

"You've been logging them."

"Yes, sir. Times, durations, surface intervals. Very thorough." Corliss beamed, then hesitated. "Though I did wonder—should I be logging what they bring up? Nobody's asked me to, and that seems like the sort of thing someone would ask."

"That," Halloway said, "is an unusually good question, Ensign."

"Thank you, sir."

"It wasn't a compliment. Log the times. Nothing else."

Corliss wrote it down, because Corliss wrote everything down, and withdrew. Halloway watched him go and felt the old familiar architecture of the service settle around him: orders descending, obedience ascending, everyone in their proper stratum like sediment in a glass of harbor water. It had been a comfort, that architecture, for a very long time. He had built his whole life inside it the way a barnacle builds its whole life inside a shell—gradually, without ever deciding to, until the shell was the only shape he knew.

The trouble with barnacles, he thought, was that they never got to see the water.

* * *

He had been twenty-six the season the *Rivergate* sailed, a junior signals officer with a good handwriting and a bad habit of noticing things. The transport had been loading at the naval depot for three days—crates, strongboxes, sealed pallets under tarpaulin—and Halloway had been assigned to verify the manifest.

The manifest had been short. The loading had not been.

He remembered standing on the depot quay with his clipboard, watching a crane swing a crate that strained its chains, and looking down at his paper, where the line item read *misc. stores, 40 kg.* The crate had weighed, by his estimate, closer to four hundred. He had asked the loading master about it, a broad man named Dree with forearms like mooring lines.

"Miscellaneous," Dree had said, not unkindly, the way you'd speak to a dog that had barked at the moon.

"It's mislabeled."

"It's *labeled*," Dree had said. "That's the part that matters."

And Halloway—twenty-six, ambitious, aware that the manifest bore a countersignature from an office three pay grades above his ceiling—had signed his name under Dree's. He had told himself a great many things on the walk back to his quarters. That it was above his station. That the quartermasters knew their business. That a discrepancy in weight was a discrepancy in weight, not a crime.

He had been lying, and he had known he was lying, and he had signed anyway, because the signature was expected and the questions were not. It had taken him perhaps four minutes. It had taken the rest of his life, apparently, because here he was, twenty years later, holding a signal that smelled like the same ink.

The *Rivergate* had gone down that winter. Officially: weather, a cargo shift, tragedy, inquiry, closure. Unofficially—well. He had spent two decades not asking the unofficial questions, and he had gotten very good at it. He'd been promoted for it, though nobody had ever said so in those words. The navy rewarded a man who noticed things and then, upon noticing, arranged his face into pleasant blankness.

He looked out at the *Magpie* again. The bubbles kept rising.

* * *

At noon, the signal office forwarded a second message, and this one removed any lingering doubt about what kind of lid they were dealing with.

**ADDENDUM.** *In event salvage crew recovers sealed strongbox or associated documentation, materials are to be secured immediately. Crew is not to be advised of contents. Crew is not to be interviewed regarding contents. Any crew member in possession of materials at time of transfer is to be detained pending review. Acknowledge.*

*Detained.* Halloway read the word three times. It was a clean, bureaucratic word, the kind that existed specifically so that officers wouldn't have to write *arrested* or *made to disappear*. He had written it himself, in other contexts, about other people. It had always looked different from this side of the desk.

He carried the folder up to the bridge, where Sub-Lieutenant Maren had the watch and was conducting a low-grade war with the coffee machine, which had decided, in the navy's infinite wisdom, to be the newest model.

"It's done it again," Maren said, gesturing at the machine, which was dispensing something the color of a storm front. "Sir. I've followed the manual. The manual is wrong. The manual was written by someone who has never been cold."

"The manual is always written by someone who has never been cold," Halloway said. "It's a tradition." He handed her the folder. "Read the addendum. Then tell me what you see."

Maren read. She was a good officer—careful, skeptical in the healthy direction—and Halloway watched her face do the same slow arithmetic his had done at midnight. Her eyebrows went up. Then down. Then into the flat, neutral line that the service taught you to build, brick by brick, over your actual opinions.

"Detained pending review," she said. "Of a salvage crew. On a civilian contract."

"Salvage law is clear. Wreck materials in restricted waters revert to the Crown." Halloway heard himself say it and was briefly disgusted. He sounded like the manual.

"With respect, sir, that's not what this is. If they wanted the materials, they'd board and take them. This is—" She stopped, choosing her words with the care of a woman defusing something. "This is making sure nobody talks."

"Yes."

"And you're asking me what I see."

"I'm asking what you see," Halloway said. "I already know what I see. I'd like to know if it's just me. It's been a long time since I checked."

Maren looked out the wheelhouse windows at the salvage boat, at the patient strings of bubbles, at the gray water doing its gray water business between them. "I see a crew that signed a contract and is doing the work," she said. "And I see us waiting to take it off them and tell them it never happened." She handed the folder back. "That's what I see, sir."

"Thank you, Sub-Lieutenant."

"Is that the answer you wanted?"

"No," Halloway said. "But I suspect it's the answer I needed, which is worth more." He paused. "The coffee machine is broken because the heating element's scaled. Vinegar, half a cup, run a cycle. Don't tell the quartermaster I told you; he'll want a requisition form and the vinegar will have gone off by then."

* * *

The trouble, he thought that evening, standing his watch alone while the sea went dark and the *Magpie* showed a single working light, was that duty was not a thing you could unbelieve on command. He had spent twenty-two years building himself around it the way a tree builds around a fence wire. The wire was still in there. Every ring of him had grown over it. You couldn't cut it out without cutting out the tree.

And yet.

He had a memory he returned to the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The night before the *Rivergate* sailed, he'd had the midwatch at the depot, and a woman had come down to the quay—not a wife, not a dockworker; she had the stillness of someone who owned things and was tired of it. She had stood looking at the loaded transport for a long time, and then she had looked at him, at his lieutenant's stripes, and said:

"You signed for that cargo."

"I did, ma'am."

"Do you know what's in it?"

"Miscellaneous stores, ma'am."

She had looked at him for a long moment—not with anger. That was the part that had stayed with him for twenty years. Not anger. Something worse. Pity, maybe, or the particular grief of watching a young man choose the wrong thing while it was still possible to choose the right one.

"No," she had said. "It isn't." And she had walked away up the quay, and he had stood there with his clipboard and his good handwriting and told himself, once again, that it was above his station.

He'd learned her name later, in the way you learn things you aren't looking for: Marchetti. One of the families the cargo had been *miscellaneous stores* out of. He'd never been sure why she'd come to the quay that night. To look at it one last time. To see if anyone on the navy's side had a face. He had a face. It had just been the wrong one.

Now there was a salvage crew down there in the cold water, pulling up whatever the *Rivergate* had kept all these years, and somewhere in the navy's machinery a signal had gone out with the word *detained* in it, and the machine had expected Edric Halloway to sign his name underneath, one more time, in his good handwriting.

He took out a sheet of paper and, for the discipline of it, drafted the acknowledgment. *Vigilant acknowledges. Will comply.* Six words. He had written harder things. He had written them easily.

He did not sign it. He set the draft under his coffee cup, where it would acquire a ring stain by morning, and went out onto the wing of the bridge to look at the water.

The *Magpie*'s light burned steady over the wreck. Somewhere beneath it lay the strongbox, and the manifest that wasn't, and twenty years of *miscellaneous stores*. Somewhere on that boat was a captain named Marchetti—he'd read the contract filings; the name had gone through him like cold water through a wetsuit—and she had come back to the quay after all these years, and she was diving for the truth he had signed away.

The wind came off the sea and he stood in it and let it be cold, because the cold felt like a kind of honesty, and he was out of practice.

Below, the dive boat began to winch something up. He couldn't see what. He found he very much wanted to, and that wanting—small, insubordinate, human—felt like the first true order he'd received in years.

By morning, he had made his decision, though he hadn't yet admitted it to himself: the acknowledgment would go out, signed, compliant, everything in order. But the daily report he was required to file on the salvage crew's progress—the one that would tell Records Division exactly when to expect the strongbox, exactly when to have the detention orders waiting at the harbor—that report would be late. Delayed. Lost in the signal queue, perhaps. An administrative accident.

He was a careful man. He knew how to lose things properly.

He just didn't yet know how much time the crew would need, or how much trouble they were about to sail into, or that within the week the weather itself would turn against them and force every hand on this coast to choose what it was they served.

The *Magpie*'s light burned on through the dark. Halloway watched it until his watch ended, and then he went below and, for the first time in twenty years, slept without dreaming of the quay.
