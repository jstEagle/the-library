## 7. Storm Warning

The radio at the harbor station had a habit of crackling before it spoke, like an old man clearing his throat before delivering bad news. Xanthe had learned to read the weather in that throat-clearing. Three long bursts of static meant a front. Two meant fog. One meant someone in the coast guard was bored.

This morning it gave her four.

She stood in the doorway of the station with a chipped enamel mug of tea going cold in her hands and listened to the forecaster's voice wind itself up into something apologetic. *An early system, moving faster than models predicted. Significant snowfall above two thousand meters by Thursday. Pass closures likely within the week.*

Thursday. She looked at the mountains across the water, white-capped already, wearing their winter like a coat they'd been trying on for weeks and had finally decided fit.

Behind her, the *Kestrel's Fortune* sat on her mooring lines, patched and listing slightly to port, looking less like a salvage vessel than a dog that had been through a hedge backwards. But she floated. And somewhere beneath the gray-green water off Point Aderyn, the Rivergate waited with her sealed strongbox and her twenty years of secrets.

"You're doing the face," said Priya, appearing at her shoulder with the particular silence of a woman who had spent a decade moving quietly around dive sites and had simply never stopped.

"What face?"

"The one where you're doing arithmetic you don't like." Priya leaned against the doorframe. "I heard the forecast too. Thursday."

"Thursday," Xanthe agreed.

"So we either go up tomorrow with everything we've got, or we sit here until spring and let Voss's money rot in escrow while the *Fortune* rots alongside it."

"That about sums it."

"Mm." Priya sipped her own tea. "Tomas is already in the engine room making lists. He makes lists when he's nervous. Did you know he alphabetizes the spare parts when he's worried? Last time he did that we ended up with the anchor chain filed under 'C' and nobody could find it for a month."

"I remember. We nearly drifted into the fish farm."

"A formative experience for us all."

Xanthe almost smiled. Almost. The other thing—the thing she'd been carrying since the tavern, since the aunt's kitchen, since the brass plate came up out of the cold water wrapped in her mother's initials—sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Tomorrow they would go up the mountain carrying a box full of stolen wages, and every one of them believed they were carrying it for Voss's buyer.

*Let them believe it a little longer,* she thought, and hated herself for it, and did it anyway.

* * *

The planning session happened that evening in the wheelhouse, which was the warmest room on the boat if you stood directly over the heater and didn't mind smelling faintly of diesel. Tomas had covered the chart table with maps of the pass road—a snaking gray line that climbed from the valley floor through switchbacks, past the frozen tollhouse, over the summit saddle, and down the far side toward the river country where, supposedly, Voss's buyer waited.

"Road's passable now," Tomas said, tapping the map with a pencil stub. "Barely. There's a depot at the halfway point—we can shelter there if we have to. After Thursday, forget it. The plows don't run till March, and March is optimistic."

"How far?" asked Devi, their youngest, who had joined the crew eight months ago and still looked at mountain roads the way other people looked at horror films.

"Ninety kilometers as the crow flies. A hundred and forty the way the road goes. Loaded with three sledges and a winch truck, call it four days if the weather holds. Five if it doesn't."

"And if it really doesn't?"

Tomas looked at her over his glasses. "Then we become a very well-equipped footnote in next spring's search-and-rescue report."

"Cheerful," said Priya.

"I'm an engineer. Cheerfulness is above my pay grade."

Xanthe traced the route with one finger. Up through the switchbacks. The tollhouse—she remembered her aunt mentioning it, remembered the name landing strangely, like a word from a language she'd half forgotten. Her mother had crossed here once. Empty-handed. Heading back.

"Cassian comes tonight," she said. "He wants to walk us through the handoff arrangements."

Devi made a noise that might have been a laugh or might have been indigestion. "Does he bring his own chair? He strikes me as a man who brings his own chair."

"He brings his own everything," Xanthe said. "That's rather the problem with Cassian."

* * *

Voss arrived at nine, precisely, because Voss was incapable of arriving at any time that wasn't precise. He came aboard with a leather document case, a bottle of something amber and expensive, and an expression of carefully rationed warmth—the expression of a man who wanted you to know he was pleased to see you but wished you to understand that his pleasure was finite and metered.

"Captain." He set the bottle on the chart table, directly on top of Tomas's map, and Tomas's eye twitched. "Ladies. Engineer. I trust the preparations go well."

"They go," Xanthe said.

"Good. Because I've had word from the other side." He produced a folded paper—not a map, Xanthe noticed, but a letter—and laid it beside the bottle with the flourish of a man laying down a winning card. "My buyer has confirmed. He'll meet us at the river crossing below Kestrel Ford, five days from now. Cash or certified transfer, his choice. And Captain—" he smiled, and the smile had teeth in it somewhere, though it kept them politely hidden, "—the figure has improved."

"Improved how?"

"Twenty percent. Call it a gesture of goodwill."

"Gestures of goodwill usually cost the giver something," Priya observed from her corner. "What's he getting?"

"Certainty," Voss said simply. "He wants this done before the snows seal everything, same as we do. Nobody wants this cargo sitting in a mountain depot all winter with questions circling it like gulls."

*Questions,* Xanthe thought. *Like navy patrol boats. Like officers who circle wrecks instead of searching them.* She kept her face pleasant and her hands busy, straightening the maps Tomas had so lovingly organized.

"And the handoff?" she asked aloud. "How do we know your buyer plays fair?"

Voss's eyes flicked to hers—quick, assessing, the look of a man checking a lock. "You don't. That's what you're paying me for. My reputation is the collateral."

"Your reputation," said Tomas mildly, "is a fascinating document."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—something moved behind Voss's composure. Not anger. Something older and more tired. Then it was gone, smoothed away like a wrinkle under a palm, and he was laughing, warm and easy, pouring drinks nobody had asked for.

"Fair enough. Fair enough. Distrust me professionally; it keeps everyone honest." He handed Xanthe a glass. Their fingers touched briefly on the stem, and she saw him notice, and saw him decide not to comment, and understood that this too was part of the negotiation—the long unfinished conversation between them that neither of them would ever say out loud in front of the crew.

"To the Rivergate," he said, raising his glass. "And to whatever's inside her."

They drank. Even Devi, who was technically underage by two years and morally underage by considerably more, depending on whom you asked.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone and Voss stood at the rail looking out at the black water, Xanthe joined him. The wind had come around from the north and it carried the smell of snow in it, clean and mineral, the smell of the high country closing its accounts.

"Your buyer," she said. "Who is he?"

"A collector."

"Of what? Strongboxes?"

"Of endings." Voss turned the glass in his long fingers. "Some men collect paintings. Some collect ships. He collects... conclusions. Stories that are finished. He pays very well for the last page, Xanthe, and then he locks it in a drawer and no one ever has to read it again."

The words settled over her like the coming snow. She thought of the ledgers her aunt had described—names, wages, families—and felt the stone in her chest shift.

"And you're comfortable with that? Selling him the last page?"

"I'm comfortable with my crew eating this winter." He said it without apology, and that was the thing about Cassian: he never lied about his arithmetic, only about his heart. "Aren't you?"

She looked at the mountains. Thursday's storm was already building somewhere beyond them, patient as a debt.

"Yes," she said. "I'm comfortable."

It was not entirely a lie. It was more like a lie with honest bones inside it. She was comfortable with the crew eating. She was comfortable with the money flowing. What she was not comfortable with—what she had decided in the small hours of the night, alone in her bunk with the brass plate's initials glowing behind her eyelids—was the destination.

The box would go up the mountain. The box would come down the other side. And somewhere between the summit and the river, Cassian Voss's buyer was going to discover that the last page of this story had been rewritten.

* * *

She told no one. That was the discipline of it, the cost of it. That night she lay awake listening to the hull tick and the wind rise, running the route through her head again and again, marking the places where a determined crew with a fast boat on the far river could outrun a buyer's men who expected compliance. It required luck. It required Halloway's navy staying confused a few days longer. Mostly it required her crew trusting her completely right up until the moment she asked them to do something that would look, from the outside, exactly like betrayal.

*I'll tell them,* she promised herself. *When we're past the tollhouse. When there's no turning back and no time to argue. They'll understand.*

She was aware, even as she promised it, that this was what every captain in every disaster story had said to herself, right before the disaster.

Sleep came eventually, thin and dream-flecked. In the dreams her mother stood at a cairn field with her back turned, and every time Xanthe called out, the wind took her voice and scattered it eastward, toward the pass, toward the snow.

* * *

Morning came gray and hard-edged. By seven, Tomas had the trucks warming in the yard; by eight, the sledges were loaded and lashed; by nine, Devi had argued successfully that the emergency rations should include actual chocolate, on the grounds that morale was a load-bearing structure.

"We're really doing it," Devi said, standing in the yard with her breath steaming, looking up at the mountains with an expression caught between terror and delight. "We're actually driving into that."

"People have done stupider things for less money," said Priya.

"Name one."

"The entire history of salvage," said Priya, and swung herself up into the cab.

Xanthe took the wheel of the lead truck. Behind her, the *Fortune* rocked gently at her moorings, patched and patched again, holding. Ahead, the pass road climbed out of the valley in a series of switchbacks that disappeared into cloud, each turn taking them higher into weather that hadn't decided yet whether to be beautiful or lethal.

She checked the mirror once. Voss's black vehicle idled at the yard gate, waiting to fall in behind them, its driver's face unreadable behind glass.

"Everyone ready?" she called.

A chorus of assent, variously enthusiastic.

Xanthe put the truck in gear and drove at the mountain. Somewhere above the treeline, under a hundred tons of waiting snow, the answer to her mother's long silence was riding in a steel box on the second sledge—and somewhere beyond the summit, a man who collected endings was waiting to buy one.

He just didn't know yet that this ending belonged to her.

The first switchback took them. Then the second. The valley fell away below, and the wind rose to meet them, and Xanthe Marchetti drove on into the white with her secret buttoned under her coat like a second heartbeat.
