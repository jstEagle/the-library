## 8. Up the Switchbacks

The axle cracked on the third switchback, which by Tomas's reckoning was the axle's way of filing a formal complaint about the whole expedition.

He heard it before he felt it—a sound like a knuckle cracking in the knee of a giant—and then the sledge behind the truck lurched sideways and dug its runners into the gravel like a sulking child refusing to walk another step.

"Everybody out," he called through the cab window, though Xanthe was already out, and Priya was already out, and Dev had been walking for an hour because he claimed the truck smelled like "burnt ambition and Tomas's cooking."

"That's slander," Tomas said, climbing down into the cold. "My cooking has never once been burnt."

"The stew in March."

"The stew in March was *caramelized*."

Xanthe crouched at the rear axle, her breath fogging, and ran her gloved hand along the housing until she found it: a hairline fracture running diagonally across the weld, thin as a lie. She looked up at him with the particular expression she reserved for machinery that had betrayed her personally.

"Well," she said. "Talk to it."

"You talk to it. You're the captain. It respects authority."

"It clearly doesn't respect *me*, or we wouldn't be stopped at four thousand feet with winter coming down the mountain like a debt collector."

Tomas knelt beside her and pressed his thumb to the crack. It was bad. Not catastrophic-bad—nothing on this truck ever achieved catastrophe, catastrophe required parts that had never been available—but bad enough that they weren't going anywhere until he did something about it, and what he did would have to hold all the way over the pass, because there was no second chance up there. No garage. No parts shop run by a man named Ferro who accepted payment in stories and slightly damp cigarettes.

"Two hours," he said. "Maybe three if the drill bit snaps again."

"If the drill bit snaps again I'm throwing it off the cliff."

"You can't throw my drill bit off a cliff, Captain. That's mutiny of tools."

Priya snorted from where she was re-rigging the sledge harness, and just like that the mood loosened half a notch, which was about as much as it had loosened in two days.

Because that was the thing Tomas had been noticing—the thing he'd been turning over in his head since the harbor, since the vote, since the night Xanthe signed Voss's contract with a pen stroke so quick it looked like she was trying to get it over with before it changed its mind. The crew was fraying. Not badly. Not dangerously. But fraying, the way a rope frays: one strand at a time, quietly, each one too small to notice until you needed the rope to hold.

And every strand traced back to the same knot.

Xanthe knew something about the Rivergate that she wasn't telling them.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the lee of a rock shelf, at the top of the third switchback, where the road bent back on itself like a man checking over his shoulder. Below them the valley floor was already going blue with dusk; above them the pass climbed into cloud, and somewhere past that cloud was the tollhouse, and past the tollhouse the high country, and past that everything else.

Tomas cooked. He always cooked when things were tense, partly because food helped and partly because a man stirring a pot had an excellent excuse not to make eye contact during difficult conversations.

Dev built the fire with his usual ceremony, arranging the kindling like he was setting a table for royalty. Priya checked the dive gear even though there wasn't a body of water within fifty miles that wasn't currently trying to become ice, because checking gear was how Priya prayed. And Xanthe sat apart, on a flat stone at the edge of the firelight, with the route map on her knees and her eyes doing that thing they did when she was reading the same line for the fourth time.

Tomas watched her over the rim of the pot.

He'd known her eleven years. He'd been there when she bought the boat with money she wouldn't explain, and when she sold her mother's ring with money she also wouldn't explain, and when she'd stood on the deck in a storm off Cape Verren and laughed—not the good laugh, the other one, the one that meant something old had hold of her. He knew her silences the way sailors know weather. And this silence had a shape to it. This silence had *initials*.

"So," he said, ladling stew into the first bowl. "The Rivergate."

Xanthe didn't look up. "What about it?"

"Oh, I don't know. Navy transport. Sinks twenty-odd years ago carrying cargo nobody will name. Restricted wreck. And now here we are, hauling a quarter ton of gear up a mountain in November because Cassian Voss—Cassian *Voss*, who once tried to poach our entire crew with a bottle of wine that cost more than my truck—has hired us to fetch a box off it." He handed her the bowl. "I'm mostly curious about the part where you flinched."

"I didn't flinch."

"You flinched like a man touching a stove."

"I did not—"

"Tomas is right," Priya said, without looking up from her harness webbing. "You flinched. I clocked it at the tavern. You flinched at the name and then you got very busy with your drink."

"I was drinking my drink. That's what drinks are for."

Dev, feeding the fire, said nothing, but he said it loudly, in the way Dev could say nothing louder than most people said anything.

Xanthe looked around at the three of them, and Tomas saw her do the arithmetic—he actually saw it happen, the little flicker behind her eyes where she weighed telling them against not telling them and found both options expensive. Then she looked back down at her stew, and the moment closed like water over a dropped coin.

"Eat your dinner," she said. "We've got a long climb tomorrow."

Tomas ate his dinner. So did everyone else. The fire popped. Somewhere far below, a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge complaining about its working conditions. And the fracture in the crew held, the way fractures do, right up until the weight came on.

* * *

The axle repair took the better part of the morning, performed lying on his back in gravel while the wind came down the pass with opinions.

It was not, strictly speaking, a proper repair. A proper repair required a new axle housing, which required a machine shop, which required civilization, none of which were available above the treeline. What Tomas had instead was a welding kit, a length of steel channel he'd salvaged from a bridge railing two summers ago because it "spoke to him," and a philosophy of engineering best summarized as: *make it stronger than it needs to be, then add more.*

He welded a splint along the fracture—two passes, slow and hot, the arc light strobing blue off the rock face—and then boxed it in channel steel and bolted that, and then, because the mountain deserved respect, wrapped the whole assembly in a bandage of steel strap tensioned with shims cut from an old propeller blade. When he was done it looked less like an axle than like a broken leg that had been set by someone with more determination than medical training.

But it would hold. He put his ear against it after it cooled, the way he did with every weld, listening for the tick of cooling metal finding its new shape, and it sounded solid. Sound as anything.

"There," he announced, rolling out from under the truck covered in grit and triumph. "She'll carry us to the moon if we ask nicely."

"She'll carry us to the tollhouse," Xanthe said, "if we don't ask at all."

"Same thing, essentially."

They loaded and moved. The road climbed in long hairpins, each switchback revealing the last one below like a staircase seen from the side, and the truck ground upward in second gear with the engine making the noise of a large animal being asked to do something undignified. Behind it, the sledge followed on its repaired runner, loaded with dive tanks and rope and the salvage crane's disassembled arm and enough canned food to survive a moderately apocalyptic winter.

By midday they'd gained the shoulder of the ridge, and the trees gave out all at once, the way trees do, surrendering not gradually but collectively, as if they'd held a meeting. Above was only rock and scree and the white smear of the high snowfields, and the wind, which up here didn't blow so much as patrol.

That was where they stopped for the noon break, huddled in the truck cab and the sledge's lee, passing around bread and hard cheese and the jar of pickled things Dev had brought for reasons no one had ever successfully extracted from him.

And that was where Tomas caught it.

Not the flinch this time. Something smaller. Priya had asked a question—casual, almost idle, the way she asked questions when she was actually taking a sounding, dropping a weighted line straight down to see what the bottom was made of.

"How well did you know Voss? Before?"

And Xanthe had answered—"Long time ago. Harbor days"—and it was a fine answer, a complete answer, a perfectly reasonable answer.

But her hand had gone to her jacket pocket. Just for a second. Just a touch, the way you touch something to confirm it's still there. And Tomas had spent enough years around Xanthe Marchetti to know that she carried exactly three kinds of things in her pockets: useful tools, useless keepsakes, and secrets. And whatever was in that pocket, she'd touched it like the third kind.

He chewed his bread and said nothing and filed it away, next to the flinch, next to the quick signature, next to the way she'd gone quiet for a full minute on the dock when Voss mentioned the word *strongbox*.

Eleven years of loyalty told him to wait. Eleven years of loyalty also told him that waiting was how ropes snapped—one strand at a time, quietly, each one too small to notice.

* * *

The afternoon went wrong at the fifth switchback, where a rockfall from the summer had left the road shelf half its width and the drop beyond it went down and down and kept going down with the enthusiasm of something that had never once considered stopping.

Tomas drove it himself, because he always drove the bad sections, because Xanthe drove like a woman arguing with the road and Priya drove like a woman negotiating with it, and neither approach was correct on a shelf of crumbling shale above a mile of air. He took it in first gear, wheels a hand's width from the edge, the truck tilted toward the mountain so far that Dev, riding shotgun, spent the entire traverse pressed against his door making a sound like a kettle deciding whether to commit.

"You're fine," Tomas told him. "Look at me. Am I worried?"

"You're never worried. That's not comforting, that's alarming."

"Worry is for people who haven't done the maintenance."

"I have met your maintenance."

The truck cleared the slide with six inches to spare and everyone exhaled in a different key. But the sledge was another matter—it had to be unhitched and winched across separately, and that meant all four of them on the narrow shelf together, hauling on the rope while the wind pushed back, and it was during the worst of it, the sledge halfway across with its runners shrieking on stone, that Priya slipped.

Just a slip. One boot on loose shale, a skid, a lurch—and for one full second she was leaning out over the drop with nothing under her but weather, and Xanthe had her wrist before anyone else had even moved, hauling her back with a grip that would leave bruises, saying nothing, breathing like she'd been the one falling.

They got the sledge across. They got themselves across. They stood on the far side in the wind, hearts hammering, and Dev made a joke about the mountain charging a toll, and Priya laughed, and everything was normal again.

Except Tomas had seen Xanthe's face in that one second. Not fear exactly—or not only fear. Recognition. Like she'd already lost someone to a fall, or a wreck, or a river, and for one second the mountain had shown her the bill again.

Whatever she was carrying, it was heavy. And she was carrying it alone, on purpose, at altitude, in November.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the sixth switchback, out of the worst of the wind, and Tomas lay in his bedroll listening to the truck tick as it cooled and thinking about fractures.

An axle doesn't fail all at once. That was the thing people didn't understand. It fails in slow motion—microscopic cracks spreading a few atoms at a time, load cycle after load cycle, invisible right up until the moment it isn't. And the trick of it, the whole art of it, was catching it early. Listening for the tick. Pressing your thumb to the place where the metal sang wrong.

He turned over. Across the camp, Xanthe's bedroll was empty.

He found her twenty meters up the slope, sitting on a boulder with her knees drawn up, looking north toward the pass, where the clouds had torn open just enough to show the stars and the black serrated line of the summit ridge. He climbed up and sat beside her without asking, because asking would have made it a conversation, and some things you can only deliver as cargo, not conversation.

For a while neither of them spoke. The wind moved through the rocks below, making sounds like the sea, which was either comforting or cruel depending on how homesick you were.

"When my axle cracked," Tomas said finally, "you know why it cracked?"

"Fatigue."

"Fatigue, yes. But fatigue from what? It wasn't overloaded. It wasn't crashed. It cracked because two years ago I hit a pothole outside Ferro's shop, hard, and I noticed the shim rattle, and I thought, *I'll check that next week.*" He picked up a pebble and turned it in his fingers. "Next week became two years. And the crack grew the whole time, quiet, patient, doing its work in the dark. Metal doesn't sulk, Captain. It doesn't hold grudges. It just keeps an honest ledger. Every load you put on it, it writes down. And one day it reads the ledger back to you."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the fire had burned down to a red eye, and Dev's silhouette crossed it once, stoking it, tending it, keeping the family warm whether the family wanted tending or not.

"Is this a speech about the axle," she said, "or a speech about me?"

"Yes," Tomas said.

She almost smiled. It was a near thing, a ghost of a thing, but it was there. "You know I can't tell you yet."

"I know." He tossed the pebble downslope and listened to it click away into the dark. "Here's what I know instead. Whatever it is, it's about your mother. It's about the Rivergate. And it's the reason you said yes to Voss when every sane bone in your body said no—which, incidentally, is the most bones you've ever ignored at once, and I count them professionally." He stood, brushing grit from his knees. "So here's my offer. Tell us when you're ready. Not when you're forced. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and I'll know if you wait too long because the axle always tells you."

"And if I wait too long?"

"Then Priya gets to say 'I told you so,' and believe me, nobody wants that. She's been saving it up for years. It'll be devastating."

This time the smile arrived properly, brief and real, and then she waved him off toward the fire and stayed on her rock with the stars, keeping her own counsel, keeping her own ledger.

Tomas went down and rolled into his bedroll and stared at the underside of the tarp and admitted the truth he'd been avoiding since the harbor: he wasn't worried about the secret. Secrets, in his experience, eventually weighed too much for one person to carry, and the carrier always set them down eventually, usually at the worst possible moment, usually in front of witnesses.

No—what worried him was simpler and colder. Two days ago, at the depot below, he'd seen tire tracks on the service road. Fresh ones. Wide tires, military tread, headed up-valley toward the pass.

Somebody else was climbing too.

And tomorrow, at the top of the seventh switchback, they would reach the frozen tollhouse—and whatever was waiting inside its walls, Tomas suspected, was going to read every secret in the crew right off the walls like a ledger.
