## 9. The Frozen Tollhouse

The tollhouse announced itself the way all good shelters do: reluctantly, and only after you'd stopped believing in it.

Priya saw it first—a dark tooth of a building crouched where the switchbacks bent around a shoulder of granite, its roof sagging under a foot of old snow, one shutter hanging like a broken wing. Twenty years of navy maps had called it a maintenance waypoint. The mountain had clearly been calling it something ruder.

"Home sweet home," Tomas said, hauling his sledge up the last rise. His breath came out in great gray plumes, and there was frost in his eyebrows that made him look like he'd aged forty years since breakfast. "I want it on record that I voted for the southern route."

"You voted for the southern route because it has hot springs," Priya said.

"The springs were a secondary consideration."

"They were the entire consideration."

Xanthe didn't join in. She stood at the tollhouse door with her pry bar already out, working the frozen latch, and Priya watched her the way she'd been watching her for three days now—the way you watch a door left slightly ajar in a storm, wondering what's going to come through it.

The latch gave. Cold air rolled out, smelling of pine pitch and mouse droppings and decades of nobody.

Inside: one room, a stone hearth blackened by ancient fires, two bunks with slats like bad teeth, and a wall of stacked supply crates stamped with an eagle-and-anchor crest that made everyone's shoulders tighten on reflex. Navy property. Out here, three days into the high passes, nowhere near anything worth maintaining.

"Well," said Okafor, dropping his pack and immediately beginning the sacred ritual of unpacking the stove, which he performed every night with the tenderness of a man greeting a firstborn child. "Somebody was expecting company."

"Somebody was expecting somebody else," Priya murmured, but she said it quietly, to herself, and went to help.

* * *

By full dark the tollhouse had transformed. The stove ticked and breathed heat. Melted snow filled every pot they owned. Okafor had produced, from some bottomless pocket of his kit, a bag of dried apricots and was dealing them out like a card sharp, and even Renn—who had not spoken more than six words a day since the pass began—was holding hers up to the lamplight, inspecting them for structural integrity before committing.

Tomas had claimed the corner by the fire and was re-wrapping the axle bearing for the fourth time, muttering to it in the low coaxing voice he usually saved for engines and, occasionally, Priya when she was being stubborn about a dive schedule.

It should have been cozy. It almost was.

But Priya kept looking at the crates.

There were fourteen of them, stacked floor to lintel along the north wall, and they bothered her in a way she couldn't initially name. It wasn't the stamps—she'd seen enough navy surplus to wallpaper a chapel. It was the arrangement. Nobody stacks crates that neatly and then abandons them. These had been squared, aligned, spaced off the damp stone on wooden runners. Maintained. And yet the dust on top was thick as felt, undisturbed, and the seals on the nearest crate were intact—wax seals, official ones, never broken.

Supplies that were stored perfectly and never used. In a tollhouse that wasn't on any route anyone used.

She got up, ostensibly to refill the kettle, and drifted along the wall.

The third crate from the end sat slightly proud of the others—half an inch, maybe less—and behind it, where the wall met the floor, the boarding looked wrong. Newer wood, paler, nailed over older wood. A patch. And patches in walls are either hiding rot or hiding something better than rot.

"Priya." Xanthe's voice, from across the room. Light. Carefully light. "Kettle?"

"In a minute." She set her palm flat against the board. It moved. Not much. But it moved.

Behind her, the conversation faltered. She could feel Xanthe watching her, and she could feel the exact moment Xanthe decided not to say anything, and that—that silence, chosen rather than accidental—was the loudest thing in the room.

Priya took out her knife, worked the nails loose with the patience of twenty years of prying things open in bad places, and pulled the board free.

A cavity, maybe two feet deep, lined with oilcloth. Inside: a metal document case, army green, latched, and cold enough to burn bare fingers.

Nobody spoke. Even the stove seemed to hold its breath.

"Well," said Tomas at last, into the quiet, "that's either treasure or trouble."

"It's paperwork," Priya said. "So both."

* * *

They cleared the table by lamplight, and the manifests came out of their oilcloth wrapping dry and crisp, preserved by cold and care. Supply manifests, requisition forms, cargo tallies—all of them bearing dates from twenty-two years back, all of them bearing the eagle-and-anchor, all of them concerning the *Rivergate*.

And all of them wrong.

Priya read them twice, then a third time, because the wrongness was the kind that hid behind ordinariness. Here was a manifest listing flour, salt pork, coal briquettes, medical stores—fourteen tons of it, loaded at Rivergate harbor, bound downriver. Standard transport run. Except the tonnage didn't match the ship's recorded displacement in any configuration Priya had ever seen, and she'd spent a bored winter once memorizing naval freight specs for reasons she still defended as "research." Fourteen tons of flour does not require a reinforced hold. Fourteen tons of flour does not get a double escort.

Here was a requisition form for the same voyage requesting, in typed block letters: ADDITIONAL HULL BALLAST, SEALED, DO NOT INVENTORY.

Do not inventory. Someone had written *do not inventory* on an official form, signed it, filed it, and buried the file in a wall in the mountains, and had apparently slept fine afterward.

"There's no weight entry," Renn said suddenly. She'd gone very still over the fourth page. "Every manifest I've ever seen lists gross weight. This one just says 'sealed' and gives a hold number."

"Hold three," Priya read. "Reinforced. Same hold we dove."

The word *dove* landed in the room like a dropped tool. Everyone looked at Xanthe.

Xanthe was standing by the fire with her arms crossed, and her face did something complicated—Priya watched it happen, watched her captain decide, in real time, how much of herself to show. It was the same face she'd worn at the harbor tavern when Voss slid the contract across the table. The same face at the crew vote, when Tomas read the wreck's name aloud and Xanthe flinched so fast most people would have missed it.

Priya hadn't missed it. Priya never missed anything; it was her single most annoying quality, according to Xanthe, who said so annually, usually while drunk.

"Captain," Priya said. Not unkindly. But not softly, either. "You've known what's in that box since before Voss ever showed us the contract. Haven't you."

Okafor stopped chewing. Renn set down her apricot. Tomas, in his corner, went quiet in the particular way of a man who has been waiting days for someone else to ask the question he'd been carrying.

The stove ticked. Wind worried the loose shutter outside, a soft irregular knocking, like a polite ghost.

"I knew some of it," Xanthe said finally. "Three days ago I knew less. My aunt filled in the rest." She laughed, short and without any humor in it. "That sounds like the start of a joke. It isn't. It's the opposite of a joke."

"Then tell us the opposite of a joke," Priya said, and pulled out a bench. "Sit down, Xanthe. All of it. We're hauling this box over a glacier together; you don't get to be a stranger on your own crew."

For a moment Priya thought she'd pushed too far—Xanthe's chin came up, that old salt-hard defiance, the look that ended conversations in four languages. But then it broke, or softened, or whatever it is that happens when someone finally stops bracing against a wave and lets it take them. She sat.

"My mother's name was Serafina Marchetti," she said. "Before she was my mother, she was the Marchetti heir. Rivergate family. Old money—not earned money. Money that came down through the harbor trade, and some of it came from contracts with the navy. Labor contracts. Conscripted labor, back when conscription was a polite word for something uglier." Her hands were flat on the table, steadying themselves. "Twenty-two years ago the *Rivergate* went down in the strait with a sealed strongbox aboard. Official story: pay chest and records, lost with all hands, tragic weather. My aunt says otherwise. My aunt says the strongbox held the Marchetti inheritance—and that the inheritance itself was built on wages that were never paid. Families worked the navy yards and the river barges and got ledger entries instead of coin. Promises. Paper. And when the ship sank, the paper conveniently sank with it."

"And your mother?" Tomas asked gently.

"She found out. Young, furious, exactly the way you'd expect. She went to claim it anyway—I think she thought she could take the money and give it back somehow, fix it from inside. She crossed this pass in autumn, heading down to the coast to meet the salvage brokers." Xanthe's eyes went to the wall, to the cavity, to the manifests. "She came back empty-handed. Swore off the whole fortune. Changed her name eventually, married a fisherman, raised me on a boat where we counted every coin twice. She never told me why until the end. She just said: *some money costs more than it's worth, and I couldn't carry it.*"

Priya looked down at the manifest in front of her. ADDITIONAL HULL BALLAST, SEALED, DO NOT INVENTORY. Sealed cargo, hold three, no weight listed, double escort, and a file hidden in a wall where only someone who already knew to look would ever find it.

Someone had hidden these documents deliberately. Someone in the navy, twenty-two years ago, had wanted proof to survive—even if they never had the courage to use it.

"So the strongbox," Priya said slowly, "isn't just money."

"It's the ledger," Xanthe said. "Voss told me it was sealed accounts. He thinks sealed accounts means numbers he can sell. My aunt thinks—" She stopped. Started again. "My aunt thinks it names every family that was owed. Every wage that was stolen. Which means whoever holds that box doesn't just hold an inheritance. They hold the proof of the theft. And the navy has wanted that proof at the bottom of the strait for twenty-two years, and now they want it somewhere quieter than that."

Silence again, but a different species of it. Priya watched it move around the table—Okafor doing arithmetic behind his eyes, Renn staring at the eagle-and-anchor stamp like it might confess, Tomas watching Xanthe with an expression Priya recognized because she'd seen him wear it at engines: assessing damage, estimating whether the thing could be saved, deciding that yes, actually, it could.

"We're being followed," Renn said at last. It wasn't a question. "Patrol boat circled us on the first dive. Somebody's been through the wreck ahead of us. And now there's a file in a wall saying *do not inventory.*"

"Yes," Xanthe said.

"And you were going to tell us when?" Priya asked. "At the summit? At the buyer's? At our funerals?"

"When I figured out what to say that wouldn't sound like I'd dragged all of you into my family's grave." Xanthe's voice cracked, just slightly, on *grave*, and she pressed on through it. "I signed the contract because the pay was real and the boat is dying and I'm not too proud to admit Voss played me—he knows exactly which levers I have. But I'm telling you now because—" She exhaled. "Because I'm not delivering that box to Voss's buyer. Whatever's in it goes to the families. To the descendants. That was my mother's refusal, and I think it's the only thing she ever passed down to me that I actually want."

Priya leaned back. Outside, the wind rose a register, and snow ticked faintly against the shutters like fingers testing the glass.

She thought about the numbers. She always thought about the numbers first—it was safer than thinking about people. The contract pay, split seven ways, would rebuild the boat, replace the compressor, buy two seasons of breathing room. Double-crossing Voss's buyer meant forfeiting all of it, probably. Meant Voss coming after them, meant the navy coming after them harder, meant crossing the rest of this range carrying something every armed and bored institution in the province wanted buried.

Meant all of that. And yet.

"You know what my problem is?" Priya said. "Everyone assumes my problem is danger. It's not. I've dived wrecks in shipping lanes. My problem is lies—specifically, lies between people who are supposed to be breathing the same air in a small steel room." She tapped the manifest. "This is a lie. Twenty-two years old, government issue. Yours was a smaller lie, and you just killed it in front of witnesses, which is the only way to kill a lie properly." She looked around the table. "So. Crew vote. Informal. No proxies, no proxies, Tomas."

"I would never," said Tomas, who absolutely maintained a system of proxies.

"All in favor of finishing the crossing and putting that box in the hands of the families it was stolen from?"

Okafor raised his hand first, mouth still full of apricot. Then Renn, solemn as a swearing-in. Then Tomas, slow, deliberate, holding Xanthe's eyes while he did it—*I see you, Captain, and I'm watching, and I'm in.*

Priya raised her own last. "Carried. Unanimous." She gathered the manifests back into their oilcloth with careful hands. "One condition. No more solo discoveries, Captain. You find something with your mother's initials on it, or your family's name, or a ghost, or a feeling—you bring it to the table. We're past the point where you get to be brave alone. Bravery alone is just drowning with extra steps."

Something moved through Xanthe's face then that Priya had maybe seen twice in nine years of diving together—relief, naked and unarmored, the expression of someone setting down a load they'd convinced themselves they were strong enough to carry forever.

"Deal," Xanthe said.

"Good." Priya stood, stretched until her spine crackled, and jerked a thumb at the bunks. "Now everyone sleep. We cross the col tomorrow, and if the sky over the western ridge is any indication, we're going to want every hour of daylight and both of Tomas's good moods."

"I have one good mood," Tomas said, "and it's asleep."

* * *

Priya took first watch, because she always did, and because she wanted the quiet.

She sat by the banked stove with the manifests beside her, reading them a final time by ember-light, committing hold numbers and signatures to memory. Somewhere past midnight, footsteps padded behind her, and Xanthe lowered herself onto the bench nearby with two cups of cocoa that Okafor's stash had made possible. She handed one over without a word.

For a while they just drank. The wind knocked politely at the shutters. Snow whispered down the chimney and died in the embers.

"My mother stayed in this valley," Xanthe said at last. "Crossing back. Empty-handed. Maybe she sheltered in this exact room." She turned her cup slowly. "Maybe she sat where you're sitting."

"Then she had good taste in seats," Priya said. "Best view of the stove in the house."

Xanthe huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Thank you. For asking the question. For making me answer it."

"That's the job," Priya said. "You keep us alive underwater. I keep you honest above it. Division of labor."

They sat with that, warm and tired, and it might have been the whole of the night—but near dawn, when Priya stepped out to check the sky, she stopped on the frozen doorstep and went very still.

Down the valley, miles off but unmistakable in the starlight, lights were moving. Vehicle lights, running dark-except-for-slits, climbing the lower switchbacks in disciplined sequence. Too many for a mail run. Too organized for smugglers.

Navy, coming up the pass. And behind them, higher up, barely visible against the stars: a single lantern signal, three flashes, pause, two—someone up-slope signaling the convoy, someone who knew the navy's hand codes.

Priya backed inside, closed the door softly, and put her hand on Xanthe's shoulder.

"Wake the crew," she said quietly. "We're not alone on this mountain anymore."
