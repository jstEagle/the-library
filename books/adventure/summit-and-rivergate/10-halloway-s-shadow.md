## 10. Halloway's Shadow

The orders came in at 0400, which was the navy's way of saying they were not proud of them.

Lieutenant Aldous Halloway read them twice by lamplight in the cramped cabin of the *Vigilant*, patrol vessel, hull number painted over so many times it had become more suggestion than fact. The paper was thin, the kind that curled at the edges, and the language was thinner still. *Intercept and assess. Civilian salvage operation suspected of unauthorized recovery within restricted waters. Contain the situation.*

Contain. He'd been in the service twenty-two years, and he had learned to read what the Admiralty meant the way other men learned to read weather. *Contain* did not mean arrest. Arrest required paperwork, charges, a record. *Contain* meant the situation—and everyone in it—should stop existing in any way that required explaining.

He set the orders down on his desk, next to the cold cup of tea he'd made at 0330 because sleep had stopped being a thing his body agreed to anymore, and looked out the porthole at the grey water.

The pass road ran up through the Col de Verren, and the Marchetti crew would be somewhere on it by now, hauling their sledges through country where the weather could kill you as efficiently as any court-martial. His orders said intercept at the depot below the pass. His orders said *contain*.

His orders did not say what he would do about it.

Halloway poured the tea out into the scupper drain—a small ritual of waste he permitted himself, since the tea itself was an act of optimism he no longer believed in—and went up to the bridge.

* * *

The young officer who signed the manifest had been twenty-four years old, with a jaw he hadn't grown into and a belief in the service so complete it was almost a physical feature, like his height or his bad left knee.

It was raining the night the Rivergate took on her final cargo at the military pier, and Halloway remembered the rain because it had gotten under his collar and stayed there, and because Ensign Brandt—his superior by four years and inferior by every measure Halloway would come to understand later—had stood under the warehouse awning checking names off a list that didn't match the crates going up the gangway.

"You're signing for this," Brandt had said, not looking up. "As cargo officer of the watch."

"Those aren't the items on the manifest." Halloway had said it quietly, the way you say things when part of you already knows how the conversation ends. "Manifest says naval stores. That's—" He'd squinted through the rain at the crates being winched aboard. Plain pine, unmarked, heavy enough that the dockhands were moving them two at a time and swearing about it. "That's not stores. Stores don't need two men."

Brandt had finally looked at him then, and Halloway had seen something in the man's face he didn't have a name for yet. Twenty years later he would call it *the look of a man doing arithmetic with someone else's life*. At twenty-four, he'd just thought Brandt looked tired.

"It's a sealed consignment," Brandt said. "Sealed means you don't ask. You sign, Lieutenant, because the signature has to belong to someone with clean hands, and mine—" he'd smiled, actually smiled, rain dripping off his nose— "mine are spoken for."

And here was the thing Halloway could never explain afterward, not to the inquiry boards in his imagination, not to himself at three in the morning: he had known. Not suspected. *Known*, down in the place where knowledge lives before the mind agrees to host it. The crates were wrong. The manifests were wrong. The paymaster's clerk had been dismissed the week before, suddenly, with a commendation nobody could account for. The whole night had the texture of a lie, and he had stood in the rain holding a pen over a document that would make him the last honest name attached to it.

He signed.

Because Brandt told him clean hands were needed. Because refusing would have meant reporting up a chain that ended, he understood even then, somewhere above Brandt, in offices where men kept portraits of admirals and ledgers of favors. Because he was twenty-four and the service was the only architecture his life had, and you don't pull a wall out of a building you're standing inside.

The Rivergate sailed at dawn. Six weeks later she went down in the strait off Cape Merren with all hands, and the loss was attributed to weather, and there was a memorial service, and Halloway stood in the chapel with the rest of them and felt the pen weight in his hand like a stone he'd swallowed.

Twenty-two years. He'd told himself for twenty-two years that he'd been a boy, that he hadn't known, that knowing and suspecting were different countries and he'd merely visited the border. And then, eleven days ago, a civilian salvage crew had pulled a brass plate out of the water with initials stamped on it, and the Admiralty had gone quiet in the particular way the Admiralty went quiet before it started burying things, and Halloway had understood that the border between knowing and suspecting had always been exactly one decision wide.

* * *

"Sir?" Petty Officer Renwick stood in the bridge doorway, holding a signal flimsy like it might bite him. "Priority traffic from Fleet. They want confirmation of our position relative to the Col de Verren depot."

"We're eighteen hours out," Halloway said. "Tell them we're thirty-six."

Renwick blinked. "Sir, the fleet knows our cruising speed. They can do the arithmetic."

"They can," Halloway agreed. "But they'll assume I'm accounting for weather. There's weather coming. Look at the barometer."

There wasn't weather coming. The barometer sat fat and content at 1021, and both of them knew it. Renwick looked at the barometer anyway, because petty officers survive by understanding precisely how much their officers are asking, and what Renwick heard underneath the order was not *falsify our position* but *give me a reason to be slow, and I will be grateful if you don't make me say why*.

"Aye, sir," Renwick said. "Thirty-six hours. I'll cite the front moving down from the Verren massif. It's true there's a front up there somewhere. There's always a front up there somewhere."

"Renwick."

"Sir?"

"If anyone asks, you cited weather on your own initiative. I was in the head."

Renwick's mouth twitched. "That'll be a first, sir. You in the head during priority traffic."

"I'm getting old. It happens more."

The petty officer left, and Halloway stood alone on the bridge of a ship whose hull number had been painted into anonymity, buying a day for people he'd been ordered to contain, and found—to his genuine surprise—that his hands were steady.

* * *

He spent the bought day in the records annex at Port Calloway, which required a shore leave chit he wrote out to himself, which was technically irregular, which was becoming a theme.

The annex smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular despair of paperwork nobody expected to be read again. The archivist, a woman named Osgood who had worked there longer than Halloway had been alive and treated officers with the warm indifference of someone who outlasts everything, waved him toward the transport files without asking what he wanted.

"Rivergate," he said. "Final voyage. Cargo manifests, loading reports, correspondence."

Osgood looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment. "Restricted section," she said. "You'll need clearance above your grade, Lieutenant."

"My grade is sufficient. My conscience is the part that needs clearing, and that's not covered by regulations."

Something moved behind Osgood's face—not approval exactly, but recognition, the look of a woman who had watched forty years of officers come in wanting things they shouldn't and leave either corrupted or cleansed. She pulled a ring of keys from her belt.

"Third stack," she said. "The Rivergate file is thin. That should tell you something right there. Ships don't sink thin. Ships sink fat with paper—cargo disputes, insurance claims, widows' petitions. A thin file means somebody harvested it." She handed him the keys. "Lamp oil's in the cabinet. Don't smoke near my stacks. Don't die in my stacks either; the paperwork's murder."

The Rivergate file was nine pages. Nine pages for a ship, a crew of sixty-one, a cargo worth—by the freight valuation alone—more than the district's annual budget. Nine pages, and Halloway read all of them standing up, because sitting down felt too much like settling in.

Loading report: his own signature, aged twenty-four, in ink that had faded to the brown of old blood. Cargo manifest: naval stores, general. Weather report for the sinking: accurate, damning, complete. And then nothing. No inquiry transcript. No board of survey. No widows' compensation ledger—the families of conscripted laborers' auxiliaries had been listed as *dependents of civilian contractors, claims routed separately*, and the separate routing went to an office that, per the annex index, had been dissolved in the same year, its files transferred to—

Transferred to nothing. The index entry simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the writer looks up and sees who's walked into the room.

Halloway copied what he could by hand, his writing cramped and fast, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the paper beneath. Stolen wages. That was the shape of it, the shape he'd half-glimpsed for two decades without letting it resolve: the crates had been pay chests, mustered wages owed to the families of conscripted laborers—men pulled off their farms and out of their workshops to build naval works, promised money sent home, and the money had been loaded onto the Rivergate instead and sent to the bottom of the strait with a weather report for a eulogy.

Sixty-one sailors had drowned carrying it. Hundreds of families had waited for payments that never came, and been told, eventually, that their claims were routed elsewhere, and the elsewhere was the sea floor.

He put the file back. Osgood watched him from her desk.

"Well?" she said.

"The file's been harvested."

"Mm." She returned to her cataloguing. "Fourth stack, bottom shelf, behind the hydrographic surveys nobody's borrowed since the war. There's a box of duplicates. Clerks used to copy everything twice, back when clerks were thorough and officers were careless. Some of us found that useful." She turned a page. "I never saw you, Lieutenant. But if a man were going to take documents off station, he'd want to do it before the snows close the telegraph relay at Verren. After that, nothing moves for the season except weather."

Halloway stood very still. "Why?"

"Because I've filed papers for forty years," Osgood said, not looking up, "and I know what a harvested file means. It means somebody decided some people didn't count. I've spent forty years making sure the counting survives somewhere." She flicked a glance at him, dry as the dust on her shelves. "Also, you're the first officer in a decade to sign the visitor log with his actual name. Most of them use aliases. As though I can't read handwriting."

* * *

The duplicate box was where she said it would be, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone brittle as biscuit. Inside: carbon copies of the full loading manifests, the paymaster's disbursement schedules, and—a document that made Halloway sit down on a crate despite himself—a requisition authorization bearing the signature of Vice-Admiral Corven. The same Corven whose portrait hung in the corridor at Fleet headquarters. The same Corven whose aide, Halloway happened to know from twenty years of careful, cowardly listening, was now retired to an estate below the snowline in the valley past the pass, where he entertained buyers of various kinds and asked no questions about provenance.

The buyer. The word arrived in his mind fully formed, and he sat with it while the lamp hissed. The Admiralty hadn't sent him to contain a salvage crew. They'd sent him to clear the road for the estate's purchase—delay the civilians just long enough, scare them off just thoroughly enough, and let the strongbox travel the rest of the way to the man who'd ordered it sunk in the first place, buying back the evidence with the same stolen money it documented.

Halloway laughed once, short and awful, in the empty stack.

They'd picked the wrong lieutenant.

* * *

He transmitted his delayed position report at dusk—thirty-six hours out, weather closing, requesting revised instructions—and then he did something he had never done in twenty-two years of service. He opened his personal locker, took out his service revolver, looked at it, and put it back. Whatever was coming, it wouldn't be solved with the weapon he'd been issued. It would be solved with the weapon Osgood had given him: paper, copies, truth in a form that couldn't be contained.

He wrapped the documents in oilcloth, sewed them into the lining of his coat with sail twine and a palm needle, working by lamplight while the *Vigilant* rocked gently on a sea that had no storms in it anywhere, and thought about the young officer in the rain with the pen.

You couldn't unsign a manifest. But you could countersign the truth, late, in different ink. It wasn't absolution. He wasn't fool enough to think it was. It was just the only direction left to walk.

At 2200, Renwick brought him tea—fresh, hot, an act of quiet mutiny in itself.

"Fleet responded to the delay, sir," Renwick said. "They've... amended the instructions." He held out the flimsy, and his hand wasn't quite steady. "New orders. We're to proceed directly to the depot at Verren. And sir—they've assigned a second vessel to assist us. The *Sternward*. She left harbor six hours ago."

Halloway took the flimsy. Read it. Read it again.

Six hours ahead of them, meaning Fleet hadn't believed his weather for a moment—which meant someone above him had counted the hours and decided the lieutenant's shadow needed another shadow to watch it.

"Thank you, Renwick. Log the receipt time honestly."

"Yes, sir." Renwick hesitated at the door. "Sir—the crew's noticed the delay. Nobody's said anything. But Cook's made extra bread, and the boys scrubbed the deck bright this morning, and I just thought you should know that whatever we're slow for, the ship seems... willing to be slow."

Halloway looked at the young petty officer—at the jaw he hadn't quite grown into, at the faith he wore like a uniform—and felt the past fold over on itself like a page turning.

"Get some sleep," Halloway said. "Tomorrow we make for the pass. And Renwick—if anyone asks what's sewn into my coat, you saw nothing."

"Seen nothing all voyage, sir," Renwick said, and grinned, and was gone.

Above the northern horizon, over the Col de Verren, the first clouds of a real storm were gathering—early, ugly, and ahead of schedule. Somewhere under those clouds, a salvage crew was hauling a strongbox through the high country, and somewhere behind them, the *Sternward* was burning coal to catch up.

Halloway climbed to the bridge and rang for full steam.

The race, such as it was, had begun.
