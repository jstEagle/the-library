## 11. Whiteout

The storm did not announce itself. That was the first lie of the day, and Xanthe Marchetti had learned long ago that mountains were liars of the first rank—polite, patient, and utterly without conscience.

One hour it had been a hard blue sky with a wind that only nipped at their collars, the kind of wind that made you feel virtuous for enduring it. The next hour the western horizon had gone the color of dirty wool, and Priya, who could read weather the way other people read menus, had stopped mid-stride and said, "That's not cloud. That's a wall."

They had been above the treeline for two days by then, three sledges and four people strung out along a ridge that the maps called the Shepherd's Shoulder and that Tomas called "the part where I die," in the cheerful tone he reserved for things he intended to survive anyway.

"Time to the pass?" Xanthe asked.

Priya squinted into the rising wind. "Six hours, if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we're not asking about the pass anymore, are we."

Xanthe made the call she would think about for years afterward: they'd push for the saddle rather than drop back down to the last stand of pines, because dropping down meant losing half a day, and losing half a day meant the snows closed behind them like a door. She told herself it was arithmetic. She knew, even as she said it, that it was pride wearing arithmetic's coat.

The wall arrived forty minutes later.

It came without drama at first—just a thickening, a whitening at the edges of things, as if the world were being erased by someone working carefully from the corners inward. Then the wind stood up, found its voice, and began shouting.

"Halt! Halt!" Priya's voice barely carried. She came forward along the rope line, hand over hand, her goggles already crusted white. "We need shelter! Now!"

"There's nothing on the map until—" Tomas began.

"The map is a rumor!" Priya shouted back. "Rope up tight! Xanthe, your left—there's a hollow off the ridge, I can feel the slope change!"

What followed was twenty minutes that Xanthe's body would remember long after her mind agreed to forget: the four of them clawing sideways across a slope that had become a river of moving snow, hauling two sledges while the third tore loose from its anchors with a sound like a sigh and went cartwheeling away into the gray, taking with it most of their food, the spare rope, and—as Tomas would report later, with genuine grief—the good kettle.

"The *good* kettle," he kept saying, even as they dug. "We had history, that kettle."

"Dig," said Priya.

They dug into the hollow until the hollow became a cave, and the cave became a home, of sorts—a chamber barely tall enough to kneel in, walled with packed snow that glowed a faint, sourceless blue when Priya finally got the little camp stove hissing. Outside, the storm screamed like something cheated. Inside, four people sat in a circle of wool and frost, steaming gently, alive.

Xanthe counted heads twice before she let herself feel relief. Three times. It was on the fourth count that she understood what was wrong.

"Priya." Her voice came out strange. "Where's your glove?"

Priya looked down at her right hand as though discovering it belonged to a stranger. The fingers were the color of old candle wax.

The next hour was the longest of Xanthe's life, and she had once spent nine hours in a flooded ballast tank waiting for a pump that may or may not have been coming. They worked by stove-light and stubbornness—warm water from the one pot they'd saved, friction, Tomas reciting an increasingly unhinged catalog of jokes in the low steady voice he used when he was terrified, which was how everyone knew he was terrified. Slowly, painfully, color crawled back into Priya's fingers, from wax to pink to an angry, living red.

"Well," Priya said at last, flexing them, wincing. "I'll keep the hand. Pity about the nails."

"You scared me," Xanthe said, and then, because the words were already out and there was no calling them back, "you scared me badly."

Priya looked at her over the steam. So did Tomas. So did Wren, their youngest, who had not spoken since the sledge went over the edge and who now sat wrapped in every blanket they owned, watching her captain with wide dark eyes.

And Xanthe, huddled in a hole in a mountain with the people whose lives she had just spent so carelessly, felt the story rise in her like floodwater against a door.

She had been keeping it shut for weeks. Through Voss's contract, through the crew vote, through the dive, through the passes—she had kept it shut because keeping it shut was what she did, the way her mother had kept things shut, the way Marchettis survived. But Priya's hand had been wax-colored, and the sledge with the food was gone, and somewhere below them a navy lieutenant was deciding whether to let them live, and suddenly the door would not hold.

"Tomas," she said. "Is there any of that tea left?"

"Aye. It's terrible."

"Pour it anyway."

* * *

He poured it into the dented cup they shared, and passed it around the circle, each of them drinking in turn, which was an old salvage-boat ritual none of them remembered starting and all of them observed like scripture.

"I'm going to tell you why we're really here," Xanthe said. "All of it. Not the contract version."

Tomas raised his eyebrows but said nothing. Wren pulled her blanket tighter. Priya simply waited, the way she waited on dives—patient, level, giving you all the time in the world and no escape from it either.

"My mother's name was Serafina Marchetti," Xanthe began. "You never heard me say it before. There's a reason."

The stove hissed. The storm hammered.

"She grew up in Rivergate village, before the navy bought the valley whole. Her father—my grandfather—worked the transport yards. When the conscription came through, he signed, because everyone signed; refusing meant prison and prison meant his family starved anyway. He was assigned to the Rivergate. He died aboard her—not in any battle. In a cargo accident, hauling freight that was never supposed to be listed on any manifest. Freight that turned out to be wages. The pay chests of every laborer family in the valley, skimmed off the top for years and shipped out under seal, and when the ship went down, the navy declared the loss 'unrecoverable' and paid out nothing. Not to the widows. Not to the children. Nothing."

Wren's breath caught audibly.

"My mother was nineteen. She spent ten years trying to get the money released—petitions, lawyers, a man in the records office who took her savings and gave her nothing. And then, one autumn, someone offered her another way. A private recovery contract. A share of whatever came up. She crossed this very range to meet the buyer." Xanthe paused. "She turned around at the Pass of Names. Came home empty-handed. Signed off on everything—every claim, every inheritance, every scrap of the Marchetti name attached to that money—and never told me why. She wouldn't say. She just said some money costs more than it pays, and then she went quiet about it for the rest of her life, and I grew up thinking my mother was a woman who was afraid of her own history."

Xanthe looked down at her hands. "I used to be angry at her for that. For the silence. For making me inherit a name she seemed ashamed of. And then I dove that restricted wreck, and I brought up a brass plate with her initials stamped on it—her initials, on a wreck she should never have had anything to do with—and I realized I didn't know my mother at all. I knew a story I'd invented to fill the space where she kept her mouth shut."

"So the strongbox," Priya said quietly. "Voss's job."

"Is the pay chest. Or part of it. The original, sealed, unlisted cargo. And Voss has a buyer waiting on the far side of this mountain who wants it exactly as much as the navy wants it buried—which is to say, they both want it to disappear, and neither of them cares what happens to the names inside it."

"The names?" Wren said.

"The ledgers. Every family owed. My grandfather among them." Xanthe swallowed. "My plan, which I should have told you weeks ago instead of letting you sign on blind, is to take that box and give it to the families. Not to Voss. Not to the buyer. To the descendants in Rivergate. Which means double-crossing a man who frightens me, cheating a buyer who frightens me more, and making enemies of the navy, which frightens me most of all. And I let all of you walk into that without telling you, because I was afraid that if I said it out loud, I'd have to admit I've become my mother's daughter after all—someone carrying a refusal she doesn't fully understand."

Silence, except for the storm.

Tomas set down the cup. He looked at it for a moment as if it might offer counsel, then looked up.

"The good kettle went over the cliff," he said, "carrying our food, our spare rope, and my heart. We're trapped in a hole in a mountain eating emergency rations, being hunted by at least two separate organizations, and our captain has just confessed to planning grand theft and fraud." He nodded slowly. "In summary: business as usual, but with better storytelling."

"Tomas—"

"No, hear me out." He held up a finger. "I ran the numbers before the vote. I laid them out honest, and I'd lay them out honest again: this boat was going to sink us all into debt regardless. You gave us a job. It turns out the job is a righteous one. I've worked worse contracts for worse reasons for better pay, and I've never once come home proud. So aye, Captain. You should have told us sooner. And also—I'm in. Fully. Not employee-in. Family-in. There's a difference, and the difference is that family gets told the truth even when it's ugly, and gives it back the same way."

Priya was flexing her recovering hand, staring into the stove flame. "I suspected," she said. "Since the wreck. You hide things badly, Xanthe. You go very still and very polite, like a cat pretending it isn't about to knock something off a shelf." She looked up. "But here's what I know about you. On the Marlow job, when the hull cracked, you went down first. Not because you were the best diver—we both know I am—but because you would not ask anyone to go where you wouldn't. So whatever you're carrying now, you'll carry it first too. That's why I'm in. That, and you owe me a new pair of gloves."

"Wren?" Xanthe asked softly.

The youngest of them had been very still through all of it. Now she spoke, and her voice was small but steady. "My grandmother came from Rivergate valley. Before I was born. She never talked about why they left." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "Maybe her family's in those ledgers. Maybe that's why I always felt like I was looking for something and didn't know what." She wiped her face with the back of her sleeve, angrily, the way young people do when they don't want crying to slow them down. "So don't you dare leave me out of this."

Xanthe felt something in her chest crack open—not painfully, or not only painfully. More the way ice cracks on a river in spring: with a sound, and then with movement underneath.

"We're four people in a snow cave," she said, "with one sledge, half our food, a dead kettle, a stolen inheritance, and two organizations who want us silent."

"And excellent morale," said Tomas.

"And," said Priya, "a route. Because I've been thinking, while you all confessed things. The storm will bury the main trail by morning. But there's an old surveyor's cut on the north side of the shoulder—steep, ugly, nobody uses it. Which means nobody will be watching it either."

Xanthe looked around the circle: the engineer grinning through exhaustion, the dive lead flexing her mended hand, the youngest member of her crew wearing grief and hope in equal measure like a borrowed coat that fit after all.

An hour ago they had been employees. Whatever they were now, it had a different weight to it, and she found she could breathe under it.

"All right," she said. "First light. The north cut."

Outside, the storm went on screaming. But beneath it, faint under the wind, Xanthe thought she could hear the river on the far side of the pass, running toward Rivergate, running toward spring, running whether anyone watched it or not.

They slept in shifts. And in the deepest watch of the night, Xanthe woke to the sound of the stove ticking as it cooled, and to a thought that arrived with total clarity, the way important thoughts do:

Somewhere below them, Lieutenant Halloway was choosing too.

She could only hope he was choosing as honestly as her crew just had.
