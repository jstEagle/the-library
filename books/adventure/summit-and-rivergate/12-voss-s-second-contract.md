## 12. Voss's Second Contract

The satellite phone weighed more than it used to. Cassian Voss was fairly sure this was not a physical fact—the manufacturer had not quietly added three ounces of guilt to the casing since the last contract—but some mornings the thing sat in his palm like a stone he'd personally quarried.

He stood at the window of a rented room above a trading post two valleys south of the pass, watching snow smoke off the ridgeline in long gray banners. Below him, his hired guides were loading mules with the exaggerated care of men who had been paid half up front and intended to be paid the other half never, if they could help it. One of them, a broad-shouldered woman named Ferro who chewed pine resin like it owed her money, caught him looking and gave him a wave that managed to be both respectful and deeply insubordinate.

Voss waved back. He had learned long ago that the best way to manage people who didn't respect you was to act as though you hadn't noticed. It was also, conveniently, the best way to manage himself.

The phone rang. He let it ring twice—appearances mattered even when there was no one to appear for—and answered.

"Mr. Voss." The voice was smooth as bank ice. "I trust the ascent proceeds."

"It proceeds," Voss said. "Slowly. Mountains do that."

"Mountains do. People needn't." A pause, the kind of pause that had been rehearsed in front of a mirror. "My principals have become… attentive. They've asked me to convey an additional opportunity."

Here it is, Voss thought. He sat down on the edge of the bed, which complained under him the way all furniture in rented rooms complained, as if it had known better tenants.

"I'm listening."

"There is a crew on that mountain. Four persons and a strongbox. My principals would prefer that the box arrive without witnesses attached. There is a sum available for the arrangement of that outcome. A considerable sum." The voice softened into something almost paternal. "You have debts, Mr. Voss. We know about the debts. This would clear them twice over, with enough left to start again somewhere the winters are shorter."

Voss looked out the window. Snow smoke. Ridgeline. Somewhere north of that ridgeline, a woman he had loved badly and at a distance for eleven years was hauling a sledge through country that wanted her dead, and someone was offering him money to make sure she stayed dead, or at least disappeared, which amounted to the same thing in the ledgers of men like these.

"How considerable?" he said, because a professional asks. Because asking bought time. Because the voice on the other end needed to believe it had hooked him, and a hook only holds if the fish cooperates.

The number, when it came, was indeed considerable. It was the kind of number that made debts evaporate and futures reorganize themselves. Voss wrote nothing down—he had a good memory for numbers, a curse inherited from a father who'd lost everything by remembering them too well—but he repeated it back slowly, letting the silence after it stretch just long enough to be misread.

"I'll consider it," he said.

"Do more than consider. You have until they reach the depot at Kestrel Notch. After that, my principals handle matters their own way, and I'm afraid their way is less discriminating. They may decide you're a witness too." A dry chuckle. "Good day, Mr. Voss."

The line went dead. Voss set the phone on the bedspread and looked at it for a while, the way one looks at a snake one has decided, provisionally, not to kill.

* * *

He went downstairs and bought tea he didn't want from a postmistress who charged him double because she recognized the coat. It was a good coat. That was the problem with good coats; they told everyone you could afford to be overcharged, and so you were, everywhere, forever, until the coat wore out or the money did. In Voss's case the money had done its wearing first, and the coat was running on reputation alone, which was more than could be said for most of his assets.

He took the tea to the stable, where Ferro was checking a mule's hooves with the tenderness she denied all human beings.

"We move at first light," he said.

"We moved at first light yesterday. And the day before." She spat a thread of resin into the straw. "Your diver crew is ahead of us now. Passed the tollhouse two nights back, according to the boy we pay at the waystation. Weather's turning. If your plan involves catching them on the far side of the bridge at Marrow, we should leave tonight."

"The bridge at Marrow," Voss said carefully, "is not yet settled."

Ferro looked up at him. She had the kind of face that made lying feel like a waste of good material. "Settled how? You told me we were escorting a recovery. Escorting. I don't like the other words that are starting to float around this job. Words like *intercept*. Words like *no loose ends*." She straightened. "I signed on to walk behind a rich man's salvage. I didn't sign on to bury anyone in a snowfield."

"No one's being buried."

"That's what men say right before the digging starts." She went back to the hoof, dismissive, conversation closed, but her shoulders stayed high and hard as fence posts, and Voss understood that he had perhaps four days before Ferro decided on her own what kind of job this was—and Ferro deciding things on her own was a force of nature with a rifle.

He climbed back to his room and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the call.

It was simple arithmetic, insultingly simple. His debts: ruinous. The offer: sufficient, twice. The cost: four lives, one of them—

One of them.

He sat down heavily. Outside, the light was going copper on the snowfields, and somewhere in that copper light Xanthe Marchetti was doing something stubborn and competent and probably ill-advised, because that was the whole architecture of the woman. He had watched her do stubborn and competent across eleven years of harbors and auction houses and the occasional courtroom gallery, always at a distance, always telling himself the distance was tactical. Tactical distance. As if love were a siege and patience a weapon, instead of what it actually was, which was cowardice with a better tailor.

They had been twenty-six and twenty-four, the summer of the Corvella job. He remembered it in fragments the way you remember anything that later becomes unbearable: her laughing at his charts, actually laughing, head back, sitting on the rail of his boat with her boots swinging over water that would have killed them both if either of them had fallen, neither of them caring. Her drawing a route on his chart with a grease pencil and getting it wrong and refusing, magnificently, to admit it. Him standing there thinking, with the dumb clarity of the young: *this one. Whatever this costs, this one.*

And then the Corvella job had gone sideways in the specific way jobs go sideways when two captains both refuse to admit a mistake, and the blame had been assigned—by insurers, by gossip, by Cassian Voss himself, most loudly and least honestly—and she had walked off his boat at Rivergate harbor and he had let her, because apologizing first felt like losing, and he had spent the following decade discovering all the ways a man can lose while winning every argument.

Now the ledger offered him a clean trade. Their silence against his solvency. It was, he had to admit, elegantly priced. Whoever had set the number understood him, or thought they did. That was the part that chilled him: the offer wasn't a temptation thrown at random. It was built. Someone had studied him the way he studied wrecks, found the load-bearing beam, and priced it.

And the terrible, unspeakable truth—the truth he turned over now like a coin worn past reading—was that for about ninety seconds, sitting on the bad bed with the cold tea going bitter, he had considered it. Not seriously. Not with his hands. But the number had passed through him like current through wire, and for those ninety seconds he had seen the arithmetic work, had seen himself debt-free in some southern port with a new boat and no past, and the vision had been quiet, and comfortable, and utterly bloodless, and that was what frightened him. Not that he might do it. That he might do it *calmly*.

He got up and poured the tea out the window, into the snow, where it made a small dark wound that filled in with fresh powder almost immediately. Even the mountain, apparently, kept tidy books.

* * *

The second call came at dusk, and it was not the smooth voice.

"This line is clean?" asked a different voice, older, clipped, with the flattened vowels of a man who had spent his career giving orders to people trained not to notice them.

"It's clean," Voss said, though he had no idea, and suspected nobody did anymore, anywhere.

"Then hear me once. The Marchetti woman is to reach the bridge at Marrow. The box is to change hands there. What happens to the crew afterward is not your concern, and the less you know of it, the longer you'll enjoy your payment." A pause. "There are people above me who remember the Rivergate, Mr. Voss. They have waited twenty years for that box to surface, and they will not permit it to surface *loudly*. Your role is small and profitable. Keep it small."

"And if I decline?"

The laugh was brief and entirely without warmth. "You owe money to people who take calls from my office. Declining isn't among your options. It hasn't been for some time. Good night."

Voss lowered the phone and discovered his hand was shaking—not much, a fine tremor, the kind you could hide in a pocket but not from yourself. So. It wasn't even a choice being offered. It was a script, and he was being permitted to perform reluctance because the performance amused them, or because they needed a deniable instrument, or simply because cruelty gets bored without theater.

He thought about Halloway. He'd never met the lieutenant, but he knew the type from twenty years of navy contracts: the career man with the careful eyes, carrying something heavy and unnamed, walking very straight so it wouldn't slosh. The smooth-voiced intermediary had mentioned, in an earlier call, that a patrol officer was already on the mountain, "managing the file." Managing the file. As if the crew were paperwork. As if Xanthe were a discrepancy to be reconciled.

And underneath the fear, underneath the arithmetic, something older stirred and refused to sit down.

He loved her. That was the fact he had spent eleven years refinancing, deferring, rolling over into new instruments of avoidance, and here at the end of it all the note had come due in the plainest possible currency: a phone call, a number, a choice. Either he was the man who sold her, or he was the man who didn't, and every clever middle position—the delayed warning, the anonymous tip, the engineered accident that spared her while enriching him—was just the old cowardice wearing new weather gear.

Ferro knocked once and put her head in. "Storm's coming down the valley early. Guides say two days, maybe less. If we're crossing to Marrow, we cross tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Voss agreed.

"And the words floating around?" she said. "*Intercept.* *Loose ends.* Are those still floating?"

Voss looked at her. Then he crossed to the window, checked the empty street below with a thoroughness he hoped looked casual, and said, quietly: "Ferro. How would you feel about a change of employer mid-contract?"

Her eyebrows rose a fraction, which on Ferro constituted open astonishment. "Depends on the pay."

"Worse."

"Depends on the job, then."

"Harder. Colder. Possibly illegal in several districts." He heard himself smile, and it felt strange on his face, like a coat borrowed from a better man. "But nobody gets buried."

Ferro chewed her resin for a long moment. "The diver crew," she said. "The Marchetti woman. You know her."

"I've known her since she was young enough to think I was worth knowing."

"Hm," said Ferro, which from her was a treaty, ratified and sealed. "Then we leave at first light. And Voss—" she paused at the door, not turning around, "—whatever you're planning, plan it faster than you're planning it. Men like you always arrive at the right decision one scene too late."

She left. Voss stood alone in the failing light with the satellite phone in his hand, and made the second-to-last call of his old life.

It rang four times before the answer clicked—a familiar voice, wary, wind-scoured, coming through static and distance and eleven years of accumulated silence.

"Who is this?"

"Ferry schedule," Voss said, using the old code from the Corvella summer, the stupid private code they'd invented for no reason except that inventing it had been fun. "There's a change in the timetable. Listen carefully, Xanthe, because I'm only going to say this once, and then you're going to be rightly furious with me for the rest of your life."

* * *

Far to the north, at a cairn field below the high pass, a traveler's name carved in stone waited to be found—and Cassian Voss, packing his kit by candlelight, had no way of knowing that the message he was sending would arrive one valley, one storm, and one betrayal too late to prevent the thing he most feared becoming: the man in the story who sold her after all.
