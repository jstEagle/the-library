## 13. The Pass of Names

Tomas had a theory about mountains, which was that they were the universe's way of teaching humility to people who owned too many wrenches. He'd spent the morning living proof of it, flat on his back under the second sledge with his arms above his head like a man surrendering to a very cold, very rocky god, coaxing a hairline crack in the axle housing to hold with a weld, a clamp, and what he privately called prayer but publicly called "engineering margin."

"Hold," he told the axle. "Just hold until the other side of the pass. Then you can fall apart with my blessing."

The axle, being a machine, said nothing. Machines were the only honest company on a mountain.

Above him, the trail bent around a shoulder of granite and disappeared, and the wind came down it carrying that particular high-altitude smell—stone and old snow and nothing living. They'd been climbing for four days since the frozen tollhouse, since Priya had pulled him aside with the manifests and said, quietly, *she's been carrying this the whole time, Tomas*, and since Tomas had said, equally quietly, *I know, and she'll tell us when she's ready, and until then we carry it with her.*

He was an engineer. He believed in load-bearing structures, and Xanthe Marchetti was the load-bearing structure of his entire life. You didn't kick the beam to see if it was solid. You shored it up and kept walking.

"Tommy." Priya's voice, pitched low over the wind. "You should see this."

He slid out from under the sledge, left a good deal of his dignity and some skin on the granite, and followed her around the shoulder of the pass.

* * *

The cairn field opened out of the trail like a held breath.

Hundreds of stones, stacked by hand, some no higher than his knee, some taller than Priya. They covered the flat shelf of the pass for fifty meters in every direction, gray and patient, dusted with the season's first real snow. And on nearly every flat stone, carved into the rock with knives, nails, chisels—names. Hundreds of names. Travelers had been stopping here for a hundred years, apparently, to cut the names of their lost into stone and leave them where the wind could read them.

Tomas had crossed a lot of passes in his life and never seen anything like it. He stood there with his gloves still smelling of weld smoke and felt the hair on his arms stand up under three layers of wool.

"It's a graveyard," Priya said, "but nobody's buried in it."

"It's a registry," Tomas said, because he was an engineer and couldn't help himself. "Look at the wear patterns. Some of these carvings are decades older than others. People have been maintaining it. Adding to it." He crouched by the nearest cairn and brushed snow from its face. *MARA OKONKWO, 1911–1938. CROSSED IN SPRING, SHOULD HAVE WAITED.* Below that, smaller, in a different hand: *WE REMEMBER.*

"Nobody's buried," he said again, "but nobody's forgotten, either. It's a ledger."

He heard Xanthe come up the trail before he saw her—the particular drag-and-set of her boots when she was tired and pretending not to be. She'd been quiet since the tollhouse, since the manifests, quieter than even her usual brand of quiet, which was already a competitive field. Tomas watched her walk into the cairn field the way you'd walk into a church you hadn't entered since a funeral.

She stopped. She read the stones. And then she went very still, in the specific way she went still when something from her past reached up out of the ground and grabbed her ankle.

Tomas looked at Priya. Priya looked at Tomas. Neither of them said a word, which between the two of them was a full conversation.

Xanthe crossed the field slowly, reading, until she reached a cairn near the center—taller than most, well-tended, its stones fitted with the kind of care that took years of visitors adding one rock at a time. She knelt. She brushed the snow away with her bare hand, which was a stupid thing to do at this altitude, and which she did anyway, because Xanthe.

Tomas couldn't read the carving from where he stood. But he watched his captain kneel in the snow of the Pass of Names with her shoulders shaking, and he didn't need to read it.

* * *

They made camp on the lee side of the field, in the shelter of the largest cairn, because it felt wrong to camp elsewhere and none of them examined why. Priya got the stove going. Rennick—because Rennick was Rennick—had opinions about the trail mix.

"There's raisins in this," Rennick said, holding a handful up to the last light like a jeweler assessing a flawed diamond. "I want it noted, for the record, that I have crossed a mountain range, survived a whiteout, eaten snow to stay hydrated, and I am still being persecuted by raisins."

"Noted," Priya said.

"For the record," Rennick said.

"The record is a cairn field full of the names of the dead, Rennick."

"Then the record will show I suffered with dignity."

Tomas let their voices wash over him and watched Xanthe. She'd taken her rations apart from the others, the way she always did—captain's privilege, she called it, though everyone knew she ate last and least so the crew ate first and most. Tonight she sat with her back against a stone that had a name on it, turning something over in her gloved hands.

He went and sat beside her. Not too close. The distance of a man offering a seat, not demanding a confession.

"Whose stone?" he asked, though he knew.

She was quiet long enough that the wind filled the space. "My mother's."

Tomas felt that land in his chest like a dropped wrench. "She climbed this pass?"

"Once. Heading back." Xanthe's voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant *careful, the floor here is load-bearing and cracked.* "Heading back empty-handed. That's what the carving says. *LUCIA MARCHETTI. CAME FOR WHAT WAS OURS. LEFT IT IN THE GROUND.*" She looked at her hands. "Somebody carved it for her. Or she carved it herself. I don't know which is worse."

"Xanthe—"

"She stood right here, Tommy. Right here, where I'm sitting. She came up over that trail—" she pointed back the way they'd come, toward the Rivergate side, toward the wreck, "—with nothing, because she wouldn't take the money. And then she went home and raised me in a boat with a rusted bilge pump and never told me why, and I spent thirty years thinking she was just... proud. Stubborn. My mother." Her breath fogged. "She was standing in a graveyard of names, holding a refusal, and she carried it home and handed it to me without ever saying a word."

Tomas turned his mug in his hands. He was, by his own assessment, not a wise man. He was a man who could fix an axle in a snowfield and who had once, memorably, fixed a bilge pump with a soup spoon and a promise. But he'd spent thirty years around Xanthe Marchetti, and he knew one thing with the certainty of a plumb line.

"Your mother gave you an inheritance," he said. "It just wasn't the kind that fits in a strongbox."

Xanthe looked at him.

"Money's easy," Tomas said. "Money you spend and it's gone and nobody's the wiser. But a refusal—" he tapped the stone beside them, gently, the way you'd tap a hull listening for a void, "—a refusal you have to *carry*. Every day. Uphill. In the snow. That's not nothing, captain. That's the heaviest cargo there is. And she handed it to you because she trusted you to carry it."

"What if I don't want it?" Xanthe said, and her voice cracked on it, and Tomas understood that this—this, right here—was the real question she'd been hauling up the mountain along with the strongbox. "What if I'd rather have had the money and a mother who talked to me?"

"Then you'd be somebody else," Tomas said, "and we wouldn't be sitting here, and I'd probably be a dock mechanic with a bad back instead of a dock mechanic with a bad back *and* a view." He nodded at the cairn field, at the hundreds of names going blue in the dusk. "Look at it, Xanthe. All these people. Somebody loved every one of them enough to climb up here with a chisel. That's the whole ledger. That's what we're made of—what other people refuse to let go of."

Xanthe laughed, wet and short. "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said, and it's about stones."

"I'm an engineer. I work in stone and steel. It's what I've got."

She leaned her head against the cairn, against the name, and closed her eyes. "She left it in the ground," Xanthe murmured. "And I dug it up."

"Yeah," Tomas said. "And now you get to decide what it is. Money, or testimony. Voss's payday, or those families' names." He paused. "For what it's worth, I know which one you already chose. I've known since the tollhouse. I think you've known since the brass plate."

"Tomas."

"Captain."

"You're a terrible first mate."

"I'm the *only* first mate. It's a very small applicant pool."

She laughed again—properly this time—and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, some plate settling back into true. She looked out over the cairn field, at the last light going off the western peaks, and when she spoke again it was barely above the wind.

"She crossed here to go home and tell herself she'd done the right thing. And she never got to know if she had." Xanthe's jaw set, and Tomas recognized the expression, because he'd seen it on her mother's face, once, from across a harbor, thirty years ago. "I'm going to finish the crossing for her. Both halves of it. The mountain *and* the money. And I'm going to put every name in that strongbox somewhere they can be read."

Tomas raised his mug. "To the ledger."

"To the ledger," Xanthe said, and clinked it.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, because the sky to the west had gone the particular bruised purple that Priya read the way Tomas read pressure gauges, and because Priya had said, over breakfast, "That's a two-day storm arriving in about thirty-six hours, and we are currently twenty hours from the far side, so do the arithmetic and don't do it out loud, it'll only upset Rennick."

"I heard that," Rennick said.

"Good. Panic productively."

They worked their way down through the cairn field in the gray dawn, sledge runners hissing on old snow, and at the edge of it Xanthe stopped. She set down her pack. And while the crew watched, she pulled her knife, knelt at the newest cairn—the one they'd camped against, the one with her mother's name—and added a stone of her own to the top. Fitted it. Pressed it home.

Then, on the flat face of the base stone, beneath *LUCIA MARCHETTI. CAME FOR WHAT WAS OURS. LEFT IT IN THE GROUND*, she carved four small words, her breath steaming, her knife clicking against stone like a tiny, stubborn engine.

Tomas didn't go look. It wasn't his name to read.

But when she stood and shouldered her pack and turned back down the trail, her face was different. Not lighter, exactly. *Oriented.* A structure that had finally found its load path.

They'd made perhaps two kilometers, the pass dropping away below them into the long white descent toward the river country, when Priya, out on point, suddenly raised a fist.

Everyone stopped. The wind carried up the trail to them, faint but unmistakable, threading through the cairn field's silence: the sound of engines. More than one. Coming up the valley behind them, from the direction of the navy depot.

Tomas looked at Xanthe. Xanthe looked at the sky, at the bruise spreading in the west, at the trail below, and said, very calmly, in the voice she saved for the moments when calm was the most dangerous thing available:

"Everyone off the trail. Now."
