## 14. Interception

Priya counted the trucks twice, because the first time she counted them she hoped she'd imagined one.

Four navy vehicles, parked in a loose arc across the depot yard at Kestrel Siding, engines idling in the thin mountain air. Their headlights cut cones through the afternoon gloom, and behind them, soldiers moved with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told *no* by a salvage crew.

"Four trucks," Priya said. "That's not an arrest. That's a parade."

Tomas, crouched beside the sledge with a wrench in his hand, looked up. "Maybe they're lost. Maybe they want directions."

"To us? We're the ones lost."

"Then we're very findable," Tomas said. "Which was always my fear about this job. You can't be ambushed if nobody knows where you are, and apparently everyone knows where we are. We're like a lighthouse, except sad and cold."

Xanthe came around the side of the depot's main shed, where she'd been checking the tarps on the strongbox sledge. Priya watched her take in the trucks, the soldiers, the arc of headlights—and watched something in her captain go very still and very flat, the way it did when Xanthe decided to stop being a person and start being a plan.

"Everyone in the shed," Xanthe said quietly. "Now. Don't run. Running looks like guilt."

"We *are* guilty," Tomas said. "Of something. I've honestly lost track."

"Of salvage," Xanthe said. "Which is legal. Everything else is a rumor."

Priya fell in behind her, one hand resting on the strap of the satchel she hadn't let go of since the tollhouse—the one with the navy manifests hidden inside it, the ones that proved the Rivergate's cargo had never been listed, never declared, never existed on any honest page. She'd confronted Xanthe with them two nights ago, by lamplight, and Xanthe had told her the rest: the Marchetti inheritance, the stolen wages, the mother who'd walked away from a fortune because of how it was bought.

*Stolen wages from conscripted laborers' families.* Priya had turned that phrase over all night, like a stone with something alive under it.

Now the something alive was parked in the depot yard with its engines running.

* * *

The shed smelled of creosote and old rope, and it had one window, which Priya stationed herself at while Xanthe stood in the middle of the floor with her arms crossed, radiating a calm Priya recognized as entirely manufactured.

"They're not coming in," Priya reported. "They're waiting. One of them's smoking. He's got, like, forty cigarettes' worth of posture."

"Posture?"

"Stands like he's being photographed."

"That's a soldier," Tomas said. "They're all like that. My cousin joined up. He stands like that when he's doing dishes."

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three measured raps, the kind that said *I know you're in there and I'm being patient as a courtesy.*

Xanthe opened the door herself.

The officer on the other side was tall, weathered, maybe fifty, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had then been through some things. His uniform was immaculate, but Priya noticed his eyes went past Xanthe immediately—not to the sledge, not to the tarped strongbox, but to the three of them, counting them, and something in his expression when he finished counting looked almost like relief.

"Lieutenant Halloway," he said. "Navy Transport Command. Captain Marchetti, I presume."

"You presume right." Xanthe didn't move from the doorway. "Lieutenant. You've brought four trucks and—what, twenty men? For three salvors and a broken axle. I'm flattered."

"Fourteen men," Halloway said. "And I'm not here for a fight."

"Nobody ever is. That's how fights start."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, quickly suppressed. "I'm here to place you and your crew under detention pending investigation of unauthorized salvage on a restricted wreck. Your equipment will be impounded. You'll be transported down the mountain for processing."

"And if we object?"

"I'd rather you didn't. It's cold, and my men are tired, and honestly, Captain—" his voice dropped, just slightly, just enough— "so am I."

Priya, at the window, felt the hairs on her arms rise. There was something wrong with the speech. It was too rehearsed in some places and too honest in others, like a man reading lines he'd written himself and hated.

Xanthe heard it too. Priya could tell by the way her captain's head tilted, a degree, no more.

"Detention," Xanthe said. "Not arrest."

"Detention."

"Those are different words."

"They are," Halloway agreed. "Arrest requires charges. Detention requires only that someone above me wants you out of the way for a few days. I'm told the paperwork is simpler." He paused. "May I come in? It's freezing, and I'd like to say something without an audience."

Tomas, from his corner, made a small sound that in any other man would have been a whimper.

Xanthe stepped aside.

* * *

Halloway came in, took off his cap, and held it in both hands like a man in church. Up close, Priya could see the exhaustion on him—the gray under his eyes, the way his uniform, immaculate as it was, sat on him like a costume he'd outgrown.

"Captain. Miss—" he glanced at Priya.

"Priya. Dive lead."

"Miss Priya. And the engineer."

"Tomas."

"Tomas." He nodded to each of them, and then, for a moment, he just stood there, and Priya watched a career's worth of discipline fight something older and heavier and lose.

"Twenty-two years ago," he said, "I was a junior signals officer aboard the Rivergate. I signed the cargo manifest on her final voyage. I signed it because I was ordered to, because I was twenty-three, because the officer who gave the order had a reputation, and because I told myself it wasn't my business what was in the crates." He looked at his cap. "It was pay chests. Wages owed to conscripted laborers' families. Mining districts, mostly. People who'd been worked for two years and paid in promises. The navy took the chests off the transport route, listed them as ballast, and let the Rivergate sink on schedule with everything aboard."

The shed was very quiet. Outside, an engine idled. Somewhere, a soldier laughed at something, and the sound was obscene.

"The wreck wasn't an accident," Xanthe said. It wasn't a question.

"No. It wasn't. And the families were told the wages were lost at sea, and the navy paid out compensation at a fraction of a fraction, and the difference—" he finally looked up— "the difference went into accounts I have spent twenty-two years pretending not to wonder about. Until three weeks ago, when a salvage crew with a Marchetti at the helm started asking questions I'd been ordered to ignore, and I stopped being able to pretend."

Priya's heart was doing something complicated. She glanced at Xanthe, whose face had gone carefully blank in a way Priya had learned to read as *devastated and refusing to show it.*

"You were on that patrol boat," Priya said. "The first day. Circling the wreck."

"I was. My orders were to let you dive and then bury whatever you found. I've been following you up this mountain under the same orders. Detain the crew. Secure the cargo. Report nothing." He set his cap down on Tomas's workbench, which Tomas eyed with the expression of a man watching a cat sit on a clean shirt. "Instead, I'm going to do something I should have done twenty-two years ago, and I'd like your word, Captain, that it won't be wasted."

He opened his coat.

Inside, flat against his ribs, was a leather document case—military issue, the navy crest embossed on the cover, the kind of thing Priya had only ever seen in the hands of men who could ruin you with a signature. He held it out to Xanthe.

"Original manifests. Transfer orders. The compensation ledgers—what was paid out versus what was in those chests, family by family, district by district. Names." His hand was steady, but only just. "Four hundred and six families, Captain. The money in your strongbox isn't an inheritance. It's the down payment on a debt the navy has been hiding for a generation."

Xanthe took the case. Her fingers, Priya noticed, weren't steady at all.

"Why?" Xanthe asked. "Why now? Why us? You could have gone to a journalist. A magistrate. Anyone."

"I tried, eleven years ago. The journalist lost his column. The magistrate lost his post. I lost nothing, which told me exactly how high this goes." Halloway's jaw tightened. "But a salvage crew with no name worth ruining, no pension to strip, no rank to bust—no one thought to buy you, so no one thought to guard against you. And you have the one thing I never had." He nodded at the tarped sledge. "The box. Physical proof. Ledgers with the navy's own seal, recovered from the navy's own wreck. They can bury a whistleblower. They can't bury four hundred and six names once they're read aloud."

Tomas cleared his throat. "Sorry. Sorry, I have to ask, because my mother raised a suspicious boy. This isn't a trap, right? Because it's an extremely trap-shaped situation. The four trucks, the polite knock, the tragic backstory—"

"Tomas," Priya said.

"No, it's a fair question—"

"It's a fair question," Halloway said, and to Priya's surprise he didn't bristle. He looked at Tomas with something almost like respect. "If it's a trap, you're all detained and the documents are impounded and I go home and sleep at night, badly, forever. If it's not, then in about a week the truth is in the hands of every newspaper below the snowline and I'm in a cell, and I want you to know I've made peace with the cell. I've had twenty-two years to make peace with it." He paused. "I'd rather have the week."

Priya looked at Xanthe. Xanthe was holding the document case the way she'd once held the brass plate—like something that had surfaced from deep water and couldn't be sent back down.

"My mother," Xanthe said quietly, "knew. Didn't she. That's why she refused the money. That's why she walked away."

"I believe so. There was a Marchetti on the compensation committee. Your mother, I think, though the records are—adjusted. She asked one question too many and then she left the navy's world entirely, and the navy let her, because a woman who walks away is quieter than a woman who talks." Halloway's voice roughened. "She was braver than I was, Captain. She refused to be paid. I took the paycheck and the rank and told myself I'd fix it someday."

"And now?"

"Now the snows close the pass in four days, and my superiors' orders say to seal this valley behind you and lose the paperwork in a records fire, and I have decided," Halloway said, "that I am a very slow reader."

Priya laughed before she could stop herself—a short, startled bark. Halloway almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

They worked fast after that.

Halloway's men, following his orders, "searched" the shed with the thoroughness of people looking for their own gloves—lifting tarps and replacing them, opening crates and closing them, one soldier holding up a coil of rope to the light as if it might confess. The strongbox sledge was inspected, pronounced "inconclusive," and left exactly where it was.

"Detention is lifted," Halloway announced at the shed door, loudly, for the benefit of his men. "Insufficient evidence of unauthorized salvage. The wreck's restricted status was posted improperly. That's a clerical matter for the harbor office." He said it all with the flat boredom of a man reciting a menu, and Priya marveled at it—twenty-two years of pretending, and apparently he'd gotten *good* at pretending, which was its own kind of tragedy.

His men loaded back into the trucks, visibly relieved. Nobody wants to arrest anyone at four thousand feet in November.

Halloway lingered last. He shook Xanthe's hand—formal, correct, two seconds too long. Then, low, only for the four of them:

"There's a convoy road down the eastern face. My report will say you were last seen heading west, toward the old mining tracks. That buys you a day, maybe two, before anyone checks." His eyes moved to the sledge. "Get it to people who'll read the names out loud. Don't come to the navy. Don't come to me. I'm done being useful the moment I file my next report."

"And then?" Priya asked. "What happens to you?"

Halloway picked up his cap and settled it on his head, and for a moment he stood the way Tomas had described his cousin standing—like a man being photographed. Except now Priya understood it differently. Not posture. Armor. The kind you wear when you've decided where the bullets are going to come from.

"Then I write the truest report of my career," he said, "and I let it do whatever damage it can before they take my pen away." He turned to go, then stopped. "Captain. Your mother's name came up in my research. Xanthe—she named you for a river she loved, I think. The records mention it."

"She never told me why," Xanthe said.

"Maybe she was waiting for you to find out on your own." Halloway touched his cap brim. "Good luck on the mountain. And Captain—if a man named Voss is involved in this anywhere, watch him. Men who sell other people's secrets for a living eventually run out of other people's."

He walked to the trucks without looking back. The convoy pulled out of the yard in a growl of diesel and gravel, headlights swinging across the shed wall, and then the depot was quiet except for the wind and Tomas exhaling about a gallon of held breath.

"Well," Tomas said. "I liked him. Which frightens me."

* * *

They broke camp inside the hour.

Priya cinched the last strap on the sledge and found Xanthe standing at the shed's open door, the document case open in her hands, reading. Her lips were moving slightly, the way people's do when the numbers are too big to take in silently.

Priya came and stood beside her. The page was a compensation ledger. *District Nine — Okafor, T. — wages owed: 214 marks. Paid: 11 marks. Balance: unremitted.* And below it, another name. And another. Four hundred and six of them.

"Her name's in here," Xanthe said. "Mom's. I found it. She signed a protest petition in the margin of the transfer order. They kept it. They kept her handwriting in the file like a trophy of something they'd beaten." Her voice didn't break, but it went thin as wire. "She wasn't running from the money, Priya. She was running from them. And she carried it alone her whole life because she thought no one would believe her."

Priya looked out at the yard—the gray sky, the white peaks, the road east that Halloway had bought them with a lie.

"So we don't carry it alone," Priya said. "That's the whole plan. That's the only plan. We get this down the mountain, we get it into hands that read out loud, and we say every name until they can't unhear them."

Xanthe closed the case and held it against her chest, and for a second she leaned—just slightly, just for a moment—into Priya's shoulder. Priya pretended not to notice, in the way friends do, which is to say she noticed completely.

"Voss's buyer is waiting on the other side of the pass," Xanthe said. "And I still don't know who it is. Halloway's right. If Voss is involved—"

The sound came from the ridge road above the depot: an engine, high and thin, moving fast. Priya turned in time to see a single rider crest the rise on a hired mountain bike, no navy gray about him, no uniform—just a lean figure in a guide's oilskin who pulled up at the yard's edge, looked at the empty space where four trucks had been, and smiled at them like a man arriving fashionably late to a party.

Cassian Voss raised one hand in greeting.

"Captain," he called down, cheerful as Sunday. "Wonderful news. I've heard from the buyer. He's moved up the timetable—and he's invited himself to meet us at the bridge over the Whitefall. Tomorrow." He paused, and his smile didn't waver, but Priya had spent enough years reading people underwater to see the current underneath it. "He's very much looking forward to it. He says he already knows you, Xanthe. He says your mother knew him too."

The wind came down off the ridge and carried his words into the yard, and Priya felt the whole plan tilt on its axle.
