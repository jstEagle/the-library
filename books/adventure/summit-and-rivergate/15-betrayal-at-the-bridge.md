## 15. Betrayal at the Bridge

The river announced itself before we saw it—a low, grinding roar under the ice fog, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. We'd been descending for two days since the depot, following Halloway's hand-drawn map down a valley that narrowed and narrowed until the walls pressed close enough that Priya said you could shake hands with both sides of the mountain at once.

"Cheerful place," Tomas muttered, adjusting the sledge harness across his shoulders. "Reminds me of my first marriage."

"You've never been married," Priya said.

"That's what she said too."

I let them have it. After the whiteout, after the snow cave and everything I'd told them there—my mother, the ledgers, the whole rotten architecture of the Marchetti name—the crew had developed a rhythm I didn't dare interrupt. Jokes on the march. Silence when it mattered. Priya walking point without being asked, her eyes doing constant arithmetic on the ridgelines.

The strongbox rode in the middle of the second sledge, wrapped in canvas and rope, and every one of us pretended not to look at it the way you pretend not to look at a sleeping animal that might wake up hungry.

We heard the bridge before we saw that too.

* * *

It was older than anything else in the valley—a timber span strung between two granite shoulders, its planks silvered to the color of old bone, its cables furred with frost. The river ran beneath it black and fast, carrying slabs of ice downstream like a conveyor belt delivering the mountain, piece by piece, to somewhere warmer.

"We cross, we're below the snowline by nightfall," Priya said, crouching at the near end, studying the anchor points. "Halloway's map says there's a road on the far side. Depot town, maybe forty kilometers."

"And Voss's guides are supposed to meet us where?" Tomas asked.

"The far side of the bridge." I checked my watch. "They're two hours late already."

Priya looked up at me. She didn't say anything, which was worse than saying something. Two hours late, in this country, in this season, meant one of three things: dead, lost, or waiting somewhere they hadn't told us about.

"They'll be there," I said, and hated how much I wanted it to be true.

Because here is the thing I hadn't said out loud, not even in the snow cave, not even to Priya with her patient eyes: Cassian Voss had been late to things his entire life, and he had never once been late because he was lost. He was late because he was recalculating. Because somewhere between the harbor tavern and here, the numbers had changed, and Cassian had always been fluent in changed numbers.

I signed the contract anyway. I crossed the range anyway. And now I stood at the edge of a bridge over a black river, telling my family a story I no longer believed, because the alternative was to say it out loud and watch their faces do what mine was doing inside.

"Xanthe." Priya's voice, quiet. "Left bank. Movement."

* * *

They came down out of the trees on the far side—not Voss's guides, or not only them. Four men in good cold-weather gear, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly how many of us there were and exactly where we stood. One carried a rifle slung loose across his back, which was either a courtesy or an insult; a rifle slung loose says *we don't need this yet*, and both meanings landed.

"Toll," the lead man called across the water. His voice carried flat off the ice. "You can put the box on the bridge and walk away, or you can make this complicated. Complicated costs extra, and nobody's budgeted for it."

Tomas shifted beside me. "Four of them," he murmured. "Two rifles visible, probably a third. Bridge is maybe sixty meters. They hold the far side."

"I can count, Tomas."

"Just establishing we're counting the same things."

I stepped forward to the bridgehead. The wind came up the gorge and tried to take my breath as payment. "Where's Voss?" I called.

The lead man smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has been paid not to answer questions. "Ask him yourself sometime. If you catch up."

"He sold us." It came out of me flat, factual, the way you'd report weather. Behind me I felt Priya go very still and Tomas very loud—he started swearing with the fluency of a man who kept a full workshop of profanity oiled and ready—but I couldn't look at them yet. Looking at them meant seeing what this did to them, and I needed another ten seconds of being made of stone.

"Box on the bridge," the man repeated. "Last offer."

Here is what I want recorded, whoever finds this account: I considered it. Not handing over the box—I never considered that, not for one heartbeat—but something adjacent. Charging. Sixty meters of frosted timber with four armed men at the far end, against five exhausted people and a strongbox full of paper. My mother had spent her whole life teaching me that some fights are just ways of dying with better posture. I heard her voice in the wind coming up the gorge: *Live cleverly, Xanthe. Dead heroes make terrible witnesses.*

And then I thought about the families. The names in the ledgers. The conscripted laborers whose wages had been loaded onto the Rivergate twenty years ago and sunk with the navy's blessing, and the officer who had ordered it—who had, if Halloway's documents were true, built a career on top of those sunken pay packets—and who was, according to everything Halloway had told us at the depot, the same man now waiting on the other side of the mountains with money for Cassian Voss.

The buyer was the thief. The thief was buying back the evidence. And Cassian had known.

Cassian had known, and he'd taken the contract anyway, and he'd kissed my knuckles at the harbor like we were characters in somebody else's novel.

"No," I called across the water.

The lead man sighed, genuinely regretful, which somehow made it worse. "Complicated it is."

* * *

What happened next happened fast and mostly badly, which is the honest way to describe most heroism.

The rifleman on the left raised his weapon and fired—not at us, but at the near cable anchor, and the crack of it went through the gorge like the mountain clearing its throat. Splinters jumped from the timber. The whole span shuddered, a long groan rolling through the cables, and frost rained down on us like the bridge itself shedding doubt.

"Down!" Priya hauled me behind the granite shoulder of the bridgehead as the second shot came, then a third, methodical, chewing into the anchor bolts. They weren't trying to kill us. They were trying to drop the bridge behind us once we were committed—or drop it in front of us so we couldn't follow. Either way, the message was the same: *you are not crossing, and you are not chasing.*

"The box," Tomas shouted. "They can't reach the box unless we give it to them—"

He was right, and I saw the shape of it a half-second later, the same half-second the lead man did. They couldn't come across—the bridge was becoming a liability even to them. So they would wait. They would sit on the far side with their rifles and their patience while the storm that had been building all morning came down off the peaks, and they would let the mountain do the work. We'd be stranded on the wrong side of a broken bridge in a closing winter, and eventually, cold does what bullets don't have to.

Unless we gave them a reason to hurry.

"Priya." I grabbed her arm. "The rafting gear. Tell me we still have it."

She stared at me. Then she laughed—one short, sharp bark with no humor in it whatsoever, the laugh of a woman doing math she hates. "You want to run the Gorge of the Damned in pack rafts. In November."

"I want them to think we might."

Understanding moved through our little huddle like current through wire. If we staged at the waterline—if we made it look like we were going to run the river and chase them down the valley—they'd have to move the box *now*, today, ahead of us, instead of letting us freeze at leisure. And men hurrying through a narrowing gorge with a heavy strongbox make mistakes. Drop things. Take routes they wouldn't otherwise take.

"It's insane," Priya said.

"It's a diversion," I said. "We stage it. We don't run it. We just need them to believe we're stupid enough to try."

Tomas was already unroping the gear. "For the record, I am stupid enough to try. I want that noted."

"Noted," Priya and I said together.

* * *

We worked fast, because the sky was working faster. The fog had thickened until the far side of the bridge was a rumor, and the first flakes came down fine and dry, the kind that means serious business later. We dragged the pack rafts to the waterline below the bridgehead, inflated two of them ostentatiously, argued loudly about paddling order—Tomas deserves an award; he argued with himself and lost—and generally performed the opening act of the worst river expedition in history.

Through the fog, we heard them react. Boots on planks. A sharp voice giving orders. The creak and strain of the strongbox being lifted onto a sledge they'd brought for exactly this purpose, which told me everything: they had planned the taking of it down to the transport. This was never a meeting point. It was a pickup.

Which meant Cassian hadn't just sold us out in a panic. He'd drawn them a map.

I stood at the waterline with the fog beading on my face and let myself feel it properly, finally, because there was no more use pretending. Twenty years ago, a girl had loved a boy who could talk his way past any locked door, and she'd believed that charm was a kind of honesty because it was so generous with itself. He'd found her in every port since, always with an offer, always with a reason, always with that look that said *you and me against the arithmetic*. And every single time, when the numbers got tight, Cassian Voss had done the same thing: he'd calculated, and I had been a line item.

The brass plate surfaced in my mind—her initials, green with sea years, sitting in my cabin aboard the boat. *E.M.* Elodie Marchetti. My mother had seen this coming decades before I was born. She hadn't sworn off the fortune because of guilt alone. She'd sworn off it because she'd learned what I was learning on a frozen riverbank: that some inheritances aren't money. Hers was a refusal. Mine, apparently, was a stubbornness with better equipment.

"Xanthe." Priya touched my shoulder. "They're moving. Upstream trail, single file, box on the sledge. Four of them plus—" she squinted into the fog "—a fifth. Someone small. Someone in a coat that costs more than the boat."

My chest went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"Cassian's here?"

"Or I'm looking at a very well-dressed marmot."

He'd come himself. He'd stood on the far side of a river and watched us get shot at, and he hadn't crossed, hadn't called out, hadn't given me so much as his voice. That was the part that would keep, I thought. That was the splinter I'd carry out of this valley. Not the betrayal—betrayal, I could file under business. But the silence. He'd had a chance to say it to my face and he'd chosen fog.

"Then we know where they're going," I said. "Downriver. To the buyer's estate below the snowline. Halloway gave us the location."

"And we're on the wrong side of a dying bridge with two pack rafts and a lot of feelings," Tomas said.

I turned and looked at the bridge—at the shot-up anchor, the groaning cables, the black water sliding underneath with its cargo of ice. Then I looked at my crew. Priya with frost in her eyelashes and murder in her patience. Tomas already eyeing the depot wreckage we'd passed an hour back, because I could see him inventorying it—planking, barrels, rope—doing engineer math on a question nobody had asked yet.

Nobody had asked it out loud. But I watched the answer arrive on his face anyway, and then on Priya's, and I understood that I didn't have to say it, because the whiteout had already said it for me. We were past the part where anyone waited to be asked.

"We're going to need a bigger raft," I said.

Tomas grinned like a man handed a present early. "Captain," he said, "I was hoping you'd say something irresponsible."

Behind us, above the roar of the river, the old bridge gave a long, tired groan and began, plank by plank, to come apart—as if the valley itself had heard our plan and decided to remove the option of going back.
