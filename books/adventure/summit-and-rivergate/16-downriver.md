## 16. Downriver

I want it on the record that I said we should wait.

Not in a cowardly way. In an engineer's way, which is a different animal entirely—a way that involves measurements and load calculations and the phrase "structural integrity" spoken with feeling. I said it while standing on the bank of the river below the broken bridge, looking at what the depot had left us to work with: two shattered supply sledges, a stack of tarred planking that had once been somebody's roof, four empty fuel drums, a coil of rope of dubious ancestry, and one canoe that some depot clerk had clearly been planning to fix "someday" for the last twenty years.

"That," I said, gesturing at all of it like a man presenting a prize-winning garden, "is not a raft."

"It's a start," Xanthe said.

"A start is a first plank. That is a *mood*."

Priya crouched by the drums and knocked on one with her knuckle. It rang back solid. "These'll float."

"These will float *once*," I told her. "The question is whether they float once in the right direction."

Xanthe turned around slowly, the way she does when she's about to win an argument she hasn't started yet. She looked upriver, where Voss's guides had taken the strongbox down toward the valley mouth—two days' travel by road, if the roads were even open, which the snows were doing their best to make a joke of. Then she looked at the river, gray-green and furious, running the same direction at considerably more enthusiasm.

"He has a head start," she said. "But he has to go around."

"And we'd go through."

"We'd go *down*."

I looked at the river again. Somewhere under that surface was water that had recently been snow, and snow that had recently been part of a glacier, and glaciers are famously indifferent to human comfort. The temperature of that water would be measured not in degrees but in seconds—the number of them you'd have before your hands stopped answering questions.

"I want it noted," I said, "that I raised objections. Formally. With diagrams, if anyone asks later."

"Noted," Priya said.

"Minuted," Xanthe said. "You can chair the next meeting."

"There won't be a next meeting if this one goes badly."

"Then let's make sure it doesn't." She smiled—that crooked half-smile of hers that had gotten me into trouble on three continents—and picked up the first drum. "Come on, Tomas. Build me something irresponsible."

And here is the thing nobody tells you about despair, or maybe they tell you and you don't believe it until you're standing in it: there comes a point where fear runs out of fuel. We'd been chased across a mountain range. We'd lost a sledge, nearly lost Priya, been ambushed off our own bridge, and watched the strongbox—all of it, every ledger, every name, twenty years of my captain's family history in a locked steel box—disappear downriver in the hands of men who worked for the man who'd kissed her once and then sold her out. There was nothing left in me that could get more afraid. So the fear packed up its things, and what moved into the empty space was something quieter and much more dangerous.

Purpose.

We built the raft in four hours, and I would put it against anything the navy ever floated, mostly because the navy never had to build anything out of spite.

The drums went in pairs, lashed under crossbeams from the sledge frames. The planking became a deck, laid tight because Priya pointed out—with the particular calm she reserves for being right—that we'd be sitting in meltwater no matter how well it floated, and a deck that shed water beat a deck that kept it. The rope went everywhere. I doubled every knot and then doubled the doubles, because I have been on water that killed people, and the difference between a knot that holds and a knot that doesn't is the whole difference, all of it, everything.

The canoe we broke apart for its good timber and its one excellent paddle, which Xanthe claimed immediately and refused to discuss further.

By late afternoon it sat on the bank looking, honestly, better than it had any right to. Ugly as a quarrel, but sound. I walked around it twice, kicked the drums, tugged every lashing, and pronounced judgment.

"She'll hold," I said. "She won't be comfortable, she won't be dry, and she'll steer like a drunk ox. But she'll hold."

"What do we call her?" Priya asked.

We looked at each other. Naming boats is serious business. You don't rush it.

"*Irresponsible*," Xanthe said.

So that was settled faster than any boat in maritime history.

* * *

We launched at dusk, which every sane person will tell you is madness, and which we did anyway because the river gave us light for another hour and Voss had given himself two days.

The first ten minutes were the worst of my life, and I say that as a man who once spent a night inside a flooded engine room listening to his own air run out. The river took us the moment the bank let go—no negotiation, no courtesy period, just seized us like a dog taking a bone and ran. The world became noise and spray and cold that came through my boots like a verdict. Rocks rose out of the white water ahead of us and slid past close enough to touch, and I touched several of them, briefly, with the oar, making the kind of decisions you make at speed and only understand afterward.

"Priya!" Xanthe shouted over the roar. "Reading!"

"Fast!" Priya shouted back, which was not a reading, but was accurate.

Here is what saved us, and I want to be precise about it because it matters: it wasn't skill. Skill is what you use when you have time. What saved us was that the three of us had spent years working together in spaces too small for secrets—an engine room, a dive deck, a wheelhouse in weather—and we had learned to move like one body with three opinions. When Xanthe leaned left, I leaned left before I knew why. When Priya called a rock, Xanthe was already turning. Nobody argued. Arguing is a luxury of the slow.

We came through the first rapids sideways, spinning, shipping half a ton of freezing water, and shot out the bottom into a long calm stretch where the valley opened up and the mountains stood pink in the last light and none of us said anything for a full minute because we were too busy being alive.

Then Priya started laughing. Just quietly at first, then properly, bent double over her oar, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the fact of it.

"Don't," I warned her.

"You're soaked," she managed. "Your hat is gone."

"My hat was a *good* hat."

"Your hat is in another watershed now."

I checked. She was right. I mourned it for exactly as long as the next rapid allowed, which was not long, because the river narrowed ahead and dropped away beneath us with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Hold on to something that loves you!" I yelled, and we went in.

* * *

We ran the river until dark made it suicide, then dragged the Irresponsible onto a gravel bar and made camp in the lee of a fallen spruce. No fire—we were too visible, and besides, everything within reach was wet enough to have its own tide chart. We ate cold rations and drank tea made with water so cold it barely admitted to being liquid, and huddled together under the tarp like three spoons in a drawer.

It was Priya who said it, into the dark, in the voice people use when they've been thinking something for hours.

"They're not chasing us anymore."

"No," Xanthe said.

"The navy isn't. Halloway bought us a day and then—" She shrugged in the darkness; I heard it more than saw it. "Whatever he did at the depot, he did it knowing it might end him. And Voss isn't chasing us either. He thinks he's done with us."

"So who's chasing whom?" I asked.

"Exactly," Xanthe said, and I could hear the smile in it, and underneath the smile something harder, something that had been forged somewhere above the treeline in a snow cave when she finally told us the whole story—her mother, the money, the families it was stolen from, the name she'd spent her whole life refusing to carry. "Nobody. For the first time since the harbor, nobody is running from anything."

"Toward, then," Priya said.

"Toward," Xanthe agreed.

I lay there after they slept, listening to the river work in the dark, and thought about my father, who repaired fishing boats his whole life and used to say that a hull doesn't fight the water, it just refuses to stop being itself, and eventually the water goes around. I'd always thought it was a line about boats. Lying on a gravel bar with frozen feet and a stolen inheritance somewhere downstream, I understood he'd been talking about people the entire time.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and mean, with a wind straight off the icefields, and the river had opinions about it.

The second day was harder than the first. The valley narrowed, the current sharpened, and twice we had to line the raft through stretches I wouldn't have ridden in a steel tub—hauling her along the bank on ropes, hip-deep in water that bit like a dog, Xanthe and Priya pulling while I steered against the push from behind. My hands went past numb into a strange burning brightness, and I caught myself laughing at the pain of it, which is either a sign of extraordinary morale or extraordinary damage, and I've decided not to investigate which.

Midday, Priya spotted it: smoke, thin and far, threading up from the tree line near a bend where the river slowed around a gravel island. A camp. Maybe a village. Maybe—

"Road meets river here," Xanthe said, studying the map we'd lifted from the depot, her finger on the bend. "If Voss's guides stopped last night, this is where."

"If they're still there," Priya said, "we outnumber them."

"If they're still there, they're watching the road," I said, "not the water. Nobody watches the water. Nobody is insane enough to come down the water."

All three of us looked at the raft. The raft, dripping, listing slightly to port, held the silence of a vessel that knows exactly what it is.

"So we go past quiet," Xanthe said. "Tomas, can you keep her slow?"

I could. It nearly killed me. Running a river fast is instinct; running it slow is surgery—backwatering, feathering the oar, letting the current do the work while pretending to resist it, all while three armed men sat fifty meters away on a bank having breakfast. We slid past that camp so close I could smell their woodsmoke and hear one of them complaining about his boots. I saw a rifle leaning against a log. I saw a canvas-wrapped shape the size and stubbornness of a strongbox sitting between two bedrolls, guarded by a man who was looking at the road.

We went by like ghosts. Like gossip. Like a rumor the river was telling itself.

A kilometer downstream, Xanthe let out a breath she'd apparently been holding since the harbor tavern, and Priya whispered, "I have never been so frightened and so pleased at the same time," and I said, "Welcome to my entire career."

* * *

The gorge found us in the late afternoon.

We heard it before we saw it—a change in the river's voice, from roar to something deeper, a bass note you feel in your teeth rather than hear with your ears. The walls closed in, black rock streaked with old flood lines, and the channel threaded down to maybe six meters of churning white between walls that went up and up.

There was no lining through it. No bank to stand on, barely room to exist. It was ride it or climb out and lose two days climbing around, and Voss didn't have two days to give us, and neither did the truth.

"Talk to me, Tomas," Xanthe said, and her voice was steady, and I loved her for it and hated her a little, the way you do.

I read the water. That's the honest description of what happened next—I read it the way I read engine noise, the way my father read weather, looking for the line. Every rapid has one: the thread of water that wants to carry you through instead of onto the rocks. Most of the time you find it by luck. Once in a lifetime, if you're lucky about your luck, you see it whole.

I saw it whole. Left of center, past a fanged rock, where the current folded over itself and ran clean.

"Left of the splitter," I said. "Hard left, then whatever happens, happens. Don't fight the river after the drop. Let it have us. It knows the way better than we do."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong in very dramatic fashion and you can put it on my stone: *He Was Wrong in Very Dramatic Fashion*."

"Tomas."

"Captain." I met her eyes. "I'm not wrong."

She believed me. That's the thing I'll remember longest—not the drop, though God knows the drop deserves remembering, but the moment before it, when she looked at me across the pitching deck of a raft named Irresponsible and simply believed me, without a vote, without a diagram, without a single objection minuted. Years of engine rooms and bad weather and shared rations, cashing themselves out all at once.

"Left of the splitter," she said, and took the bow.

We hit the entry dead on. The rock went by close enough to read its geology. Then the world stood up.

I have been thrown from horses, blown off roofs, and once ejected from a diving bell by my own faulty valve work, and none of it compares. The gorge swallowed us whole. Water came from everywhere at once, from below, from the sides, from directions water has no business coming from. We spun, dropped, surfaced, dropped again. I heard Priya yell something that was either a prayer or a technical observation. The drums groaned under us like living things. At the worst of it—airborne, genuinely airborne, the whole raft and its idiot crew hanging above a chute of white—I had one thought, clear as a bell:

*Toward. Not from.*

Then the river set us down, gentle as a mother, in a long green stretch below the gorge, and the three of us lay gasping on the deck among the wreckage of our dignity, and the raft rode easy, and not one lashing had given.

I sat up. I checked my hands—they still worked, more or less. I checked the oars—one gone, snapped at the throat, but the canoe paddle remained, and Xanthe was holding it aloft like a trophy.

"Still with us?" she asked.

"Barely," Priya said. "Ask me again in a year."

Downstream, the valley opened wide and gold in the evening light, and far off—so far it might have been imagination, except that Priya saw it too, and then Xanthe, all three of us pointing like children—there was a smudge of chimney smoke rising from the trees. A settlement. The valley mouth. The estate country, where a buyer waited with ledgers he hadn't earned and a guest of honor he didn't know was coming.

Voss had a day on us, maybe less now. He thought the river was a wall at his back.

He was about to learn it was a door.

"Get the spare oar," Xanthe said, already moving to the bow, the paddle across her knees, the last light catching her face. "We're not stopping tonight."

I shipped the oar, and the Irresponsible gathered herself and ran, and for the first time in longer than I could measure, none of us was looking back.
