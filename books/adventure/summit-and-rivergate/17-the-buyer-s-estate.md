## 17. The Buyer's Estate

The valley opened below them like a held breath.

Xanthe lay flat on the ridge with her binoculars cupped to keep the lenses from fogging, and watched the estate arrange itself in the last of the afternoon light. It was the kind of place that pretended it had always been there—grey stone, slate roof, a boathouse at the water's edge with its own private dock—but she could see the seams. The generator shed that was too large for a house this size. The antenna mast disguised as a flagpole, badly. The new gravel on the drive, laid since the snows began, because someone important wanted his road passable even when the world wasn't.

"That's not a gentleman's winter residence," Priya murmured beside her. "That's a filing cabinet with chimneys."

"Which means he keeps records," Xanthe said. "Records mean paper. Paper burns slow enough for us to read."

Tomas, prone on her other side with his chin propped on his fists, made a low whistle. "You say the most romantic things."

Below the treeline now, and the air was almost gentle after six days of the pass—the wind gone soft, smelling of pine resin and woodsmoke from the village three kilometers downriver. The crew had come off the mountain looking like something dragged behind a sledge: frost-nipped, half-starved, Priya's hands still wrapped where the whiteout had taken skin off her knuckles. But they'd come off it together, which was more than the mountain had expected to allow.

The strongbox was gone. Voss's guides had taken it two days ago at the bridge, along with their spare rope, their reserve fuel, and any illusion Xanthe had been carrying about her old rival's better nature. What they hadn't taken—and this was the mistake Xanthe intended to make expensive—was her knowledge of Cassian Voss himself. Twenty years of watching him across harbors and auction rooms and the occasional regrettable evening. She knew how he negotiated, how he traveled, how he slept badly in unfamiliar beds and worse in familiar ones.

And she knew he would be here tonight, because the buyer would want to inspect before paying, and Voss never let a buyer sleep on a decision. He'd learned that lesson young, in some back room she'd never asked about, and he'd built an entire career on never giving a man a night to reconsider.

"He'll do it in the study," she said. "Ground floor, facing the water. Men like this always put their money room where they can see what they bought it with."

"And you?" Tomas asked quietly. "What are you going to see?"

She lowered the binoculars. "I'm going to see an old friend about a betrayal."

* * *

They came down through the trees in the blue hour, when the estate's lights made the windows gold and blind—anyone inside looking out saw only their own reflection, warm and satisfied. Priya took the boathouse end with Halloway's stolen radio handset and a schedule of the guard rotations Xanthe had memorized off the patrol boat's noticeboard weeks ago, back when the lieutenant was still an obstacle instead of whatever he was now. Tomas took the generator shed, on the grounds that a man who could fix anything could also, given ten minutes and a screwdriver, make sure nothing worked quite on time.

Xanthe went in through the kitchen, because kitchens were honest. There was always a side door, always a cook who stepped out for smoke or gossip, always a corridor that connected the working heart of a house to its money rooms without passing through the front hall where the important people performed themselves.

This one had all three. The cook—a broad woman singing something tuneless over a stockpot—never saw the shadow that crossed the pantry; Xanthe was through and into the service corridor before the song reached its second verse. Her boots were wrapped in wool. Her breath she kept shallow and slow, the way her mother had taught her, once, in another life, holding her hand under cold water: *the diver who panics uses twice the air, little fish. Be patient. The bottom is not going anywhere.*

The bottom was not going anywhere. Cassian Voss was in the study.

She heard him before she saw him—that voice, warm as brandy and just as flammable, doing the thing it did. "Of course the box is sealed. That's rather the point of a strongbox, my friend. If I could open it, so could the navy, and then neither of us would be having this conversation." A pause. A laugh, low and genuine-sounding, which meant it wasn't. "No. Payment first. You've seen the manifest copy. You know what's inside is worth more than what I'm asking."

Xanthe pressed herself against the wall beside the study door, which stood open a hand's width, spilling lamplight into the corridor. Through the gap she could see a slice of the room: firelight, a desk, the edge of a second man—older, heavy-shouldered, wearing civilian clothes that fit him like a uniform anyway. And on the desk between them, squat and salt-scoured, the strongbox. Her crew had bled for that box. Priya had nearly gone under the ice for it.

"You understand my position," the older man said. His voice was dry, unhurried, the voice of a man who had signed things. "An item of this provenance attracts attention. Attention is the one commodity I cannot purchase."

"Attention is exactly what you're purchasing," Voss said pleasantly. "Silence, delivered to your door. I'm told it's the family business."

Something moved in the older man's stillness—not a flinch, nothing so honest, but a settling, the way ice settles on a hull before it cracks it. Xanthe filed the face away. She'd seen it somewhere before, or its father, or its type: navy portraits, commissioning photographs, the grey men behind the grey men. The officer who ordered the original theft, Halloway had said. The one whose name was on the orders that turned conscripted wages into somebody's inheritance.

"I'll have my people verify the seal's integrity," the man said at last. "Tomorrow morning. We conclude tomorrow."

"We conclude tonight," Voss said, and there it was—the old reflex, the no-night-to-reconsider rule. But his voice had a hairline crack in it, and Xanthe, who had spent twenty years learning to hear the flaws in Cassian Voss the way other people heard music, heard it plainly. He was afraid. Not of the buyer. Of something else.

The man rose. "Tomorrow, Mr. Voss. My house, my hour." Footsteps, unhurried, moving away through some inner door. And then the study was quiet except for the fire, and Voss alone with the box, and after a long moment the sound of him sitting down heavily in the chair like a man setting down something heavier than a strongbox.

Xanthe counted to sixty. Then she pushed the door open and walked in.

He didn't startle. That was the thing about Cassian—he never startled, he simply reorganized. One heartbeat of absolute stillness, and then he leaned back in the chair and looked at her with an expression she might have called fond if she hadn't known better.

"Elodie Marchetti's daughter," he said softly. "In the flesh. Do you know, I gave those guides very specific instructions not to hurt anyone. I hope—"

"They left us stranded above the snowline with no fuel, Cassian."

"Yes." He looked at the fire. "That was the specific instruction part I paid extra for."

She crossed the room slowly, letting him watch her come, and stopped at the corner of the desk, one hand resting near the strongbox. Up close it was worse than she remembered—salt-welded, scarred, the navy stamp half-eaten by twenty years of cold water. Somewhere inside it was a ledger with names in it. Hundreds of names. Families who had waited decades for someone to read them aloud.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been told I age like wine."

"Wine doesn't sweat."

His smile flickered and died honestly, which was rare enough that she almost pitied him. Almost. "You shouldn't be here, Xanthe. Tomorrow morning this is finished, and you walk away, and nobody has to—"

"Nobody has to what? Learn your buyer's name?" She tilted her head toward the inner door. "Because I already have. And I have documents in a very safe place that say what's in this box isn't treasure, Cassian. It's testimony. Stolen wages. Money taken from laborers' families by the man whose house we're standing in, and every year it sat in that wreck, his interest compounded and their children went without."

Voss closed his eyes. When he opened them, the charm was gone entirely, and what was underneath was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the climb. "I know what's in the box."

"Do you?"

"I've known since the harbor tavern." He stood, restless, and paced to the window where his own reflection hung ghost-gold against the dark water. "Why do you think I came to *you*? Of all the salvage crews on this coast, all the hungry, competent, discreet crews—I walked into that tavern and hired the one captain whose mother's initials are stamped on the Rivergate's cargo manifest." He laughed, short and bitter. "God, you thought it was a coincidence. You voted on it. You took a *crew vote*, Xanthe, like it was a job."

Her hand had gone cold on the desk. "Then why?"

"Because I hoped—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because twenty years ago I watched you choose the sea over everything else, and I understood it, and I hated it, and I did the same thing myself, and I told myself we were the same. And then your mother died, and you disappeared for three years, and I told myself you were running from grief. And when I found the Rivergate listing—her name on the cargo papers, the Marchetti inheritance sealed in a navy hold—I thought, here it is. Here is the thing she ran from, sitting at the bottom of the sea, and if I put it in front of her daughter, maybe she'd finally stop running. Maybe she'd take it. Maybe she'd take it and stay somewhere, with me, and be—" He gestured vaguely at the fire, the room, the whole gilded trap of the estate. "*Settled.*"

"So you sold us at a bridge."

"So I *panicked*," he said, turning, and his voice cracked clean through the middle this time. "When Halloway's name surfaced—when I realized the navy wasn't chasing the box, they were chasing the *witnesses*—I did the arithmetic every salvage man does. My debts, your lives. And I chose wrong. I knew I was choosing wrong while I was choosing it. That's the worst part, Xanthe. It wasn't malice. It was cowardice with good bookkeeping."

The fire popped. Out past the window, on the dark river, she could see the boathouse lights where Priya was, and beyond it the black shoulder of the mountain they'd crossed, where her mother's name sat carved in a cairn among strangers. *Empty-handed,* the crossing had said. Her mother had come back down this same valley carrying nothing, because carrying the money would have meant agreeing with how it was gotten.

"Cassian," Xanthe said, and her voice surprised her by being steady, "I'm not going to run. That's the joke. That's the whole stupid joke. You dangled this box in front of me to make me stop running, and I *did* stop—I stopped on a mountain, in a snow cave, in front of my crew, and I told them everything. My mother didn't abandon the fortune because she was afraid of it. She abandoned it because keeping it would have made her complicit. And I spent my whole life thinking that refusal was a wound she handed down to me." She shook her head. "It was an instruction."

Voss stared at her. "You're going to give it away. All of it. To the families."

"To the families whose names are in that ledger. Yes."

"And me?" Something raw moved under the question. "Twenty years, Xanthe. There were nights in Hong Kong, nights in Valletta—you remember them differently than I do, fine, but you remember them. Was any of it—"

"Don't," she said gently. "Don't make me answer that tonight, in this house, standing next to this box. Ask me again someday when I'm not furious with you. Ask me when you've earned it."

He held her gaze for a long moment, and she watched him weigh it—survival against something older, the same scale he'd misused at the bridge. Then he exhaled, and his shoulders dropped, and Cassian Voss looked, for the first time in her memory, like a man who had set down a weight voluntarily.

"The verification team comes at eight," he said quietly. "Four men, armed, professional. The buyer keeps the box in his study overnight because he believes no one alive knows it's here." He glanced at the window, at the boathouse lights. "Your engineer is in my generator shed, by the way. Tell him the third fuse from the left is wired to fail—he'll appreciate knowing it's intentional."

"How—"

"I've known Tomas for eleven years, Xanthe. Who do you think sold him his first welding rig?" He straightened his cuffs, and just like that the armor came back up, polished and charming, the rival of legend. But his eyes stayed honest a moment longer. "Eight o'clock. After that, this house fills with men whose job is making sure the Rivergate stays silent forever. Whatever you're going to do—do it before dawn."

She was halfway to the service door when he spoke again, softer.

"Xanthe. The ledger. When you read the names." He paused. "Read them loudly."

She looked back at him—at the man who loved her in the only language he'd ever trusted, which was leverage, and who had just spent his last piece of it buying her a head start.

"Loudly," she promised, and slipped into the dark of the corridor.

Outside, the first flakes of the coming storm were starting to fall, fat and slow, and down by the water Priya's signal lamp blinked twice from the boathouse: *in position.* Above the treeline, the sky was closing like a fist.

Eight hours until dawn. Eight hours until the house filled with grey men and guns.

Xanthe pulled her hood up and started for the boathouse, where a strongbox, a radio mast, and the rest of her life were waiting—and behind her, in the lit study window, Cassian Voss poured himself a drink and sat down to wait out the longest night of either of their lives.
