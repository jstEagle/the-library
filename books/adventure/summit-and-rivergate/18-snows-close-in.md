## 18. Snows Close In

The orders arrived at 0400, which told Halloway everything he needed to know about them. Honest paperwork kept office hours. Only the shameful kind came in the dark.

He read them twice by the red lamp in his cabin aboard the *Corvane*, the patrol vessel riding at anchor in the rivergate estuary, and then a third time because his hands were not quite steady and he wanted to be sure it was the cold and nothing else.

*Directive 11-C. Effective immediately: seal all approaches to the Kestrel Valley. No civilian transit. All communications traffic in the valley to be monitored and, where deemed operationally necessary, suppressed. All records pertaining to transport vessel Rivergate (decommissioned) to be consolidated under naval custody for review.*

Consolidated. That was a lovely word. It had the same relationship to *destroyed* that *reassigned* had to *disappeared*. He had been in the service long enough to speak fluent bureaucratic, and Directive 11-C was practically poetry.

There was a signature block at the bottom. Not the Admiral's — the Admiral at least signed things like a man who expected to answer for them someday. This one bore the countersign of Vice-Commodore Aldous Renwick, Logistics Directorate. Renwick. The name sat in Halloway's chest like a swallowed stone, because twenty-two years ago, as a lieutenant junior grade with ink still wet on his commission, he had carried a sealed manifest from Renwick's office down to the Rivergate's quartermaster and been told, in a voice kind as a hand on the shoulder, *"You didn't see what was in the crates, son. You saw crates."*

He had seen crates. He had signed off on them. And every promotion since had arrived with the faint smell of that signature on it.

Halloway set the directive down, went out onto the deck, and looked up at the sky over the mountains, where the snows were coming in like a curtain being drawn across a stage.

"Well," he said to no one, because the deck watch was aft and out of earshot. "Here we are again."

* * *

The trouble with a career built on small compromises, he reflected over breakfast, is that each one is so reasonable. Nobody wakes up one morning and decides to become the sort of officer who buries stolen wages under a mountain. You simply sign one manifest, and then the next one, because refusing the second would mean admitting the first meant something. Twenty-two years of reasonable decisions, stacked neatly, and the stack turned out to be a wall.

He'd tried to tell himself, back at the pass, that delaying his own report by a day was just inefficiency. Bureaucracy being bureaucracy. A man could lose a day in triplicate and no one would blink. But he knew what it was. He'd known since the moment Marchetti's crew came down off the mountain looking half-frozen and entirely unrepentant, and he'd handed her the documents instead of the handcuffs, and she'd looked at him with those level eyes and said, *"You're going to be in trouble for this,"* and he'd said, *"I've been in trouble for twenty years. This is the first time it's the honest kind."*

She'd almost smiled. It had been, he decided later, worth the court-martial risk on its own.

Now the navy was moving to seal the valley, and somewhere up past the snowline a woman named Xanthe Marchetti was carrying a strongbox full of other people's money toward an estate owned by — if his suspicions were right, and they were, they had been for weeks — Vice-Commodore Renwick himself. The buyer Voss had arranged. The same office that had ordered the theft. The circle closing after two decades, and the only question left was whether it closed on the truth or on everyone who knew it.

His aide, Ensign Okonkwo, knocked and entered with the morning signals folder. She was twenty-four, sharp as a tack, and had the particular gift of reading her captain's silences like other people read charts.

"Anything interesting?" she asked, nodding at the directive still lying open on his desk.

"Housekeeping," Halloway said. "Records consolidation."

"Ah." She set down the folder. "The Rivergate records?"

"The Rivergate records."

"And we're consolidating them where?"

"A place with excellent security and no windows."

Okonkwo considered this. "Sir, may I say something insubordinate?"

"You may. You always do eventually."

"I filed the survey photos from the first dive last month. Routine archive. This morning I went to pull them and the file had been re-indexed under a restricted classification that didn't exist yesterday." She folded her arms. "Somebody is erasing things faster than I can file them, and I'd like to know whether my captain is going to let them."

Halloway looked at her for a long moment. There were perhaps three people in the world whose trust he could still afford to spend, and he was about to spend one of them.

"What do you know about the Rivergate's last cargo?" he asked.

"That officially it was engineering equipment and rations. Unofficially, sir, I grew up in Rivergate village, and my grandmother used to say the navy took the valley's wages to build its road and never paid a soul." Her voice didn't waver. "I used to think that was old-woman talk."

"It wasn't." He slid the documents across the desk — the ones he'd taken from the depot safe, the ledgers, the wage rolls, the names. Four hundred and eleven families. "These are the names. Every one of them."

Okonkwo read. He watched her face do what his had done: the slow tightening, the moment where the abstract became a woman named Ferro who was owed eleven months' pay for work on a road she'd been conscripted onto at nineteen, or a boy listed as *dependent, age 4* because the ledger recorded even the children who'd gone hungry while the money sat in a naval account accruing interest nobody claimed.

"My grandmother's name will be in there," Okonkwo said quietly.

"It might be. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry." She closed the ledger with great care, the way you close something holy. "Be useful. What's the plan, Captain?"

And there it was — the question he'd been circling for days, wearing a uniform now, waiting for him to salute it.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, formed itself over the next six hours in the gaps between duties, the way plans made by honest men in dishonest institutions always have to.

Directive 11-C gave him perfect cover and a perfect deadline. Seal the valley: that meant establishing the communications checkpoint at the lower relay station by 1800, personally, per the order's insistence that a senior officer oversee signal suppression. Signal suppression meant cutting the valley's links to the outside world before anyone inside could transmit anything inconvenient.

Which meant that whatever was going to be transmitted had to go out before 1800, from the relay station, by him, using the very authority the directive handed him like a loaded weapon wrapped in a bow.

"They'll know it was you within the hour," Okonkwo said, when he laid it out in the chart room with the door shut. "Renwick will make sure of it. They'll want a scapegoat more than they want the story buried quietly, once the story's loose. A leak you can prosecute. A silence you just have to live with."

"Yes," Halloway said. "I've done the arithmetic."

"Arrest. Tribunal. They'll take your pension. Your rank. Possibly your freedom, depending how frightened they are."

"All accurate."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

He found he was smiling, which surprised him. "You know, when I was your age I wanted a command. A real one — a ship, a crew, a flag. Somewhere along the way I got a desk instead, and I told myself the desk mattered, that keeping the machine running was its own kind of service." He tapped the ledger. "This is the machine I was running. Four hundred families, ground up and filed away. So no, Ensign, I don't suppose I get to keep the pension. But I might finally get the command. Small one. One transmission. Best crew I ever had, too — just you."

Okonkwo's jaw did something complicated. "For the record, sir, I think this is insane."

"Noted for the record."

"For the further record—" she pulled the relay station's access codes from her breast pocket and set them on the chart table "—the maintenance window for the transmitter runs until 1750, and I logged a spurious fault report this morning that should keep the regional supervisor from monitoring our frequency until at least 1830."

Halloway stared at her. "Ensign."

"Insane, sir. But well-prepared insanity. There's a difference, and I intend to be able to tell my grandchildren the difference mattered."

* * *

The snows arrived at 1500, early and angry, exactly as the weather service had promised and exactly as Marchetti must have known they would. Halloway watched them come down the valley from the relay station's window — a grey-white wall swallowing the peaks, then the treeline, then the road — and thought about her, up there somewhere, driving toward Renwick's estate with a strongbox and a crew and no idea that the navy was about to slam every door behind her.

No. That wasn't true, and he was tired of flattering himself. She probably knew. She'd known about the patrol boat, about the marks on the wreck, about the whole rotten architecture of it. Xanthe Marchetti struck him as a woman who assumed doors would slam and planned her routes through windows.

The relay station was a squat concrete building staffed by two technicians who took one look at a lieutenant's directive and retreated gratefully to the mess for the authorized break the directive also provided. Halloway sat down at the main console with the ledgers in a canvas satchel at his feet and Okonkwo standing watch at the door, and began to type.

It was slower than he'd imagined, this. Not the technical part — the technical part was trivial, attach files, address the distribution list, send. What was slow was reading the names one more time. Because once he pressed the key, they stopped being evidence and became testimony, and testimony deserved to be read aloud at least once by someone who'd helped bury it.

*Ferro, M. Conscripted laborer, road works, Sector 4. Owed: 11 months' wages.*
*Ferro, dependent, age 4.*
*Duvall, T. Owed: 9 months.*
*Marchetti, R. Clerk, Rivergate freight office. Refused disbursement; flagged for review.*

He stopped at that one. Rosa Marchetti. Xanthe's mother, right there in the ledger — the clerk who'd seen the numbers and refused to touch them, who'd carried her refusal home like a stone and passed it to her daughter instead of the money. He thought of the brass plate, of the story Priya had told him at the depot in a low furious voice, and understood suddenly that the family name hadn't been stained by this at all. It had been the only clean thing in the file.

"Captain." Okonkwo, at the window. "Vehicle coming up the road. Navy staff car. Fast."

"How fast?"

"Fifteen minutes, maybe less, if they don't care about their suspension."

He looked at the screen. Three hundred and nine names entered. A hundred and two to go. His fingers hovered.

"Ensign," he said calmly, "how attached are you to the idea of a long, unhurried conversation with whoever's in that car?"

"Deeply unattached, sir."

"Good. Go stand in the road and have one."

She went. Through the window he watched her plant herself in the middle of the track with a clipboard and the magnificent, immovable boredom of a junior officer enforcing a checkpoint, and he watched the staff car brake, and argue, and brake again, and he put his head down and typed.

*Duvall, T.* Gone.
*Kessler, A.* Gone.
*Okafor, P., and dependents, ages 2 and 6.* Gone.

The engine noise outside rose and fell. A door slammed. Voices — Okonkwo's, crisp, citing Directive 11-C subsection four with the relish of a woman weaponizing her enemy's own paperwork. Whoever was in the car was not happy, but they were checking their orders, because everyone always checked their orders, and the orders said the relay station was sealed to all personnel without direct authorization from —

From Renwick. Of course. The car was Renwick's people, come to supervise the suppression personally, which meant Renwick already suspected the truth was a live grenade and wanted his hand on the pin.

*Ninety-one names left.* His hands were steady now. Whatever happened after this, the shaking was finished with.

*Seventy.*
*Fifty.*
*Thirty.*

Outside, a new voice cut through the wind — higher, angrier, a voice used to being obeyed. Okonkwo's reply drifted back, polite as frost: *"Sir, unless you have written authorization countersigned at fleet level, I'm required to detain you at this checkpoint. Would you like to step inside where it's warm while we verify? It may take some time. The lines are down, you understand. Everything's down."*

God bless her. He typed faster.

*Twenty.*
*Ten.*
*Marchetti, R. Clerk. Refused disbursement.*

Last line. Last name. He read it once more, aloud, quietly, the way you'd speak at a graveside — *"Rosa Marchetti, who refused"* — and then he addressed the transmission to every news bureau, every journalist, every public records office his twenty-two years of knowing where bodies were buried had taught him to trust, attached the ledgers, the manifests, the directive itself with Renwick's countersignature sitting on it like a confession, and pressed send.

The progress bar filled. The confirmation chime sounded, absurdly cheerful, like a kettle announcing tea.

Somewhere beyond the snows, four hundred and eleven families' names were loose in the world, multiplying down every wire and satellite link, and no vice-commodore alive could call them home.

Halloway sat back. Outside, the staff car's occupant had apparently produced authorization after all, because Okonkwo was stepping aside with a salute, and boots were crunching up the path toward the door, several pairs, moving with purpose.

He saved nothing, wiped nothing, ran nothing. Let them find the console exactly as it was. A man who hid the evidence invited doubt; a man who sat beside it in plain view invited certainty.

The door opened. Colonel Vasik — Renwick's ferret of an adjutant, whom Halloway had last seen smiling at a function five years ago — stood in the frame with two military policemen behind him, snow melting on his shoulders, his expression cycling rapidly from triumph to confusion as he took in the scene: the console, the open ledgers, the old lieutenant rising from the chair with his hands loose at his sides and, he was fairly sure, an expression of profound relief.

"Lieutenant Commander Halloway," Vasik said. "You are relieved of duty and placed under arrest pending investigation into the unauthorized disclosure of classified—"

"Yes, yes." Halloway held out his wrists, then reconsidered and offered his belt and sidearm instead, which seemed more traditional. "Before you read me the rest — and do read all of it, Colonel, I've always enjoyed thoroughness — you should know the transmission completed fourteen minutes ago. Distribution list of forty-seven recipients on three continents. Your countermand window closed before your car cleared the switchbacks."

Vasik's face performed a small, private catastrophe.

"So," Halloway went on pleasantly, shrugging his coat off and folding it over the chair — no sense making the MPs carry it in this weather — "you can arrest me, which you're going to do regardless, or you can arrest me *and* waste an hour confiscating equipment that no longer matters. I recommend the former. The storm's worsening, and I'd hate for anyone to drive these roads angry."

They took him out through the snow toward the staff car, Okonkwo watching from the checkpoint with her clipboard clutched to her chest and her eyes suspiciously bright. As they passed her, Halloway paused between his escorts — a liberty no one thought to deny a man walking so willingly — and said, "Ensign, the station log needs completing. Fault reports, maintenance window, the usual. Make it boring. Make it *very* boring."

"Aye, sir," she said, and saluted, and held it until the car door shut.

Through the frosted glass, as the vehicle swung onto the valley road, Halloway caught one last look at the mountains — white, immense, indifferent — and allowed himself, for the first time in twenty-two years, to feel like a man going home rather than a man going down.

Then the radio crackled, and Vasik leaned forward from the front seat with a fresh sheet of paper and a smile that had recovered its footing, and said:

"Change of destination, Commander. The Commodore requests your presence at his estate. Tonight. He says—" Vasik consulted the note with theatrical care "—he says there's a situation developing on his property involving a salvage crew, and he'd like you to explain, in person, why his guests keep receiving documents with your fingerprints on them."

Halloway looked out at the snow hammering the windshield, at the road climbing toward the estate below the snowline where a strongbox, a rival, a buyer, and a woman named Marchetti were converging like weather fronts.

"Driver," he said, settling back comfortably, "step on it. I wouldn't miss this for the world."
