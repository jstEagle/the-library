## 19. Climax: The Strongbox Opened

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rope and the particular cold of water that never quite freezes. Xanthe crouched behind a stack of canvas-wrapped dinghies, her breath coming in slow, deliberate pulls, and listened to the snow bury the world outside.

It had been falling since dusk—fat, windless flakes that came down like the sky was trying to smother the valley. By now the road to the estate would be gone. The drive. The gates. Every route out, erased under a foot of white and gaining an inch every hour. Whoever wanted this strongbox would have to come to it on foot, and so would she.

Which suited her fine. She'd spent thirty years learning how to be hard to find. Tonight she intended to be impossible to miss.

Through the gap between two hulls she could see the boathouse doors, tall as church gates, and beyond their frost-furred windows the grey gleam of the lake. Somewhere out there, Priya and Tomas were doing what she'd asked of them—making noise, making tracks, being gloriously visible along the shoreline path so that anyone hunting her would have hunters of their own. She'd told them to stay away from the boathouse. She'd told them if she wasn't at the tree line by midnight to take the documents Halloway had given them and run for the valley villages without her.

Tomas had looked at her like she'd suggested they all learn to fly. But he'd gone. That was the thing about Tomas—he argued with his whole face and then did exactly what you asked, because underneath all that grumbling he trusted her, and trust like that was a debt you paid back or died owing.

She meant to pay it tonight.

The strongbox sat where Voss's guides had stashed it when they'd carried it up from the depot: beneath the workbench, under a false floor panel, wrapped in oilcloth. They'd thought they were clever. They hadn't counted on the fact that Xanthe Marchetti had been crawling through other people's hiding places since before she could swim, and that men who stole things always hid them the same way—close, low, and within reach of a quick exit. Thieves planned for flight. She planned for return.

The panel came up without a sound. The oilcloth fell away. And there it was—the strongbox from the *Rivergate*, twenty years at the bottom of cold water and another twenty in the dark, its brass fittings green with age, its lock a mechanism of beautiful, spiteful complexity.

Xanthe set it on the workbench and took out her tools.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her a little. Her heart was going like a drum in a parade, but her hands—her hands remembered every lock her mother had ever taught her to pick, sitting cross-legged on the deck of a salvage boat while other children learned piano. *A lock is just a question,* her mother used to say, guiding her small fingers along the tumblers. *And most people never change their answers.*

*Most people never change their answers.* God, Mum. You could have warned me about this one.

The first tumbler gave. The second fought her, then surrendered with a click she felt in her teeth. The third—

Boots on gravel outside. More than one pair. Voices, low and urgent, muffled by the snow that was already drifting against the boathouse walls.

Xanthe went still, lamp off, one hand flat on the cool brass lid.

They were here. The buyer's men—or the buyer himself; she'd heard enough through the study door earlier to know Admiral Kessler traveled with an escort these days, old men surrounded by young ones with rifles. They'd found the tracks along the shore. Of course they had. Priya and Tomas had made wonderful noise, and wonderful noise draws everyone.

*I'm sorry,* she thought at them, not for the first time tonight. *I'll make it worth it.*

The door hinges groaned. Lamplight swept the interior in a long yellow blade, cutting across the stacked dinghies, across the hanging nets, across the water-stained floorboards—and stopped.

"There," said a voice she recognized. Not Kessler. One of the guides. "Bench. Someone's been at it."

*Someone has been at it,* Xanthe thought, pressed into the shadow between hulls, *and someone is about to be very disappointed in your security arrangements.*

Three of them came into the light. She counted by sound: heavy boots, a rifle stock knocking a hull, the wet rasp of a man breathing through a broken nose—she'd done that, on the bridge, and felt no remorse whatsoever. They gathered around the workbench like men around a poker table, and the tallest reached down and lifted the strongbox lid.

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Xanthe knew that texture intimately. It was the silence of a man looking into an empty box.

"It's empty," the guide said, and his voice cracked on the second word. "Where is it—where's the—" He spun, lamplight swinging wild. "Search the boats. Search everything. She's here somewhere. *Find her.*"

Xanthe moved.

Not away—toward. While their backs were turned and their lamps pointed at the far end of the boathouse, she slipped from her hiding place, crossed six feet of open floor in a crouch, and pulled the main breaker for the boathouse lights. Darkness dropped over them like a lid. Then she was moving again, along the wall she'd memorized an hour ago, toward the small auxiliary room at the water's end where the estate kept its radio equipment—a relic from the days when the admiral liked to call across the lake for his dinner, back before he had things to hide.

Behind her, the guides blundered and shouted, their lamps sweeping blind arcs through the dark. A rifle discharged; the shot cracked off the tin roof and rang like a struck bell, and somewhere outside a dog began to bark, and then several dogs, and then the whole frozen evening was awake and shouting.

The radio room door was unlocked. Estate people never locked anything—they'd never needed to, until tonight.

Inside, it was cramped and smelled of dust and warm vacuum tubes. An old transmitter sat on the desk beside a receiver, both of them older than she was, both of them built in an era when machines were made to be repaired rather than replaced. Beside them, in a leather folder stamped with the Kessler crest, lay the frequency log.

Xanthe set the ledgers on the desk.

She'd taken them from the strongbox an hour ago, in the dark of the tree line, with shaking hands and tears she hadn't let fall. Because the box hadn't held money. Money could be stolen twice. The box held something better and worse: ledgers. Twenty years of them, in a navy clerk's careful hand. Names. Every family conscripted to build the river works. Every wage deducted, every payment "withheld for the war effort," every promise made and broken, entered in columns so neat they looked like music.

The Marchetti name was there. Third page. Her mother's father, owed eleven years of wages, dead before the navy admitted the debt existed. Her mother had been nineteen when she'd walked away from the fortune Voss had offered her—the fortune that was really blood money laundered through three shell companies—and she'd walked away because taking it would have meant agreeing the theft was settled. Agreeing it was over.

It wasn't over. It was in her hands.

Xanthe keyed the transmitter. The tubes glowed orange, warming, humming, waking up like old dogs hearing the word *walk*. She'd spent the climb over the pass listening to Priya recite frequencies the way other people recited poetry—emergency bands, weather bands, the regional relay that every village kitchen radio in the valley was tuned to on winter nights when the roads closed and the world shrank to a circle of lamplight and voices.

The valley was listening tonight. Snowbound, cut off, bored, frightened—and listening.

Outside, boots pounded toward the radio room. She had perhaps a minute. Maybe less.

She keyed the mic.

"This is Xanthe Marchetti." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'm broadcasting from the Kessler estate on Lake Verenne. I'm holding the cargo manifests of the transport *Rivergate*, lost twenty years ago, and the wage ledgers of the River Works conscription. If your family worked the river works—if your family was ever told their wages were 'withheld'—your name is in these books. I'm going to read them."

She opened the ledger to the first page and began.

*"Abelard, Tomas. Fourteen years' service. Owed: 4,120 crowns."*

*"Abelard, Marta. Eleven years. Owed: 3,300 crowns."*

Boots in the corridor. Fists on the door. *"Open this door!"*

*"Alcott, Ren. Nine years. Owed: 2,875 crowns."*

The door burst open and lamplight flooded in, and Xanthe didn't turn around. She kept reading, voice level, names rolling out of her like water out of a breached dam, while behind her a man shouted her name and another shouted about shooting the equipment and a third—older, colder, a voice like a slammed drawer—said, *"No. Don't touch it. Don't you dare touch it."*

Because the buyer understood, even if his men didn't. A destroyed transmitter was a story. A live broadcast, interrupted mid-name, with witnesses in every farmhouse from here to the pass—that was evidence. That was a confession with a body count. Shoot the radio and every listener would know exactly what the Kesslers were willing to kill to keep quiet.

*"Marchetti, Elena,"* Xanthe read, and her voice caught, just once, on her mother's name. *"Owed: 6,400 crowns. Claimant status: deceased. Heir: one daughter."*

She swallowed. Kept reading.

*"Marchetti, Xanthe. Heir. Owed: 6,400 crowns."*

She turned around then, slowly, the mic still live in her hand, and faced Admiral Kessler across the little room. He was older than his photographs—of course he was; the photographs were twenty years old too—but the eyes were the same: pale, patient, entitled. A man who had signed a theft and called it logistics.

"Miss Marchetti," he said quietly. "You understand what you've done."

"I understand exactly what I've done." She lifted the ledger so the microphone could hear the pages turn. "I've read your handwriting. It's very neat. You should be proud of it—it's going to hang you."

"The courts will take years—"

"The courts aren't listening." She gestured, with the hand holding the mic, at the window, the night, the buried valley beyond. "Four hundred farmhouses are listening. Every kitchen radio from here to Rivergate. You can't arrest a frequency, Admiral. You can't buy it back, sink it, or lose the paperwork. It's loose. It's *loose*, and it knows all your names."

For a moment—one long, glittering moment—something moved behind his eyes that might have been respect, if respect could curdle. Then he said, softly, to the men behind him: "Seal the estate. Nobody leaves. Nobody arrives. We'll sort this out when the snow stops."

"And when the snow stops," Xanthe said into the mic, cheerfully, "everyone will already know. So I'd get comfortable, Admiral. I've got nine more ledgers, and I read fast."

He stared at her. Then, without another word, he turned and walked out, and his men went with him, and the door banged shut, and the bolt rattled home—from the outside.

Xanthe stood alone in the humming orange light, heart hammering, and realized she was smiling so hard her face hurt.

She keyed the mic again. "Still there? Good. Next page. And if anyone out there is near the tree line north of the estate—there are two friends of mine freezing, and they're very fond of soup."

* * *

They came at midnight, as promised. All of them.

Priya first, shouldering through the service door Halloway had left unchained—because of course Halloway had left it unchained; the man had been defecting in installments for a week and this was simply the final payment. Behind him came Tomas, snow-crusted and furious, carrying a soup pot he'd apparently liberated from the estate kitchen with the same moral seriousness he brought to axle repairs. Behind *him* came Lieutenant Halloway himself, out of uniform, looking like a man who had finally set down a weight he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it was there.

"You absolute—" Tomas began.

"I know," Xanthe said.

"—reckless, self-important—"

"I know."

"—magnificent, impossible woman." He set down the soup pot and pulled her into a hug that cracked a rib she was fairly sure had been fine before. "We heard your voice coming out of a farmhouse radio in the middle of a blizzard and Priya cried."

"I did not cry," said Priya, crying. "There was snow in my eyes. There was a lot of snow everywhere, if you'll recall."

Halloway stood by the door, awkward as a man at a funeral who isn't sure whose side he's on. "The admiral's sealed the roads," he said. "But he can't seal the airwaves, and he knows it. By morning, half the valley will be at his gate asking about their grandparents' wages. By noon, the regional papers will have the story—I sent the documents ahead when we crossed the pass. And by tomorrow night..." He paused. "By tomorrow night, the navy will need someone to blame. They won't choose me. I've made sure of that. Which means they'll choose someone convenient."

"Voss," Xanthe said.

"Voss," Halloway agreed. "His guides talked. His debts are public knowledge now. He sold you to cover himself, and the sale is the only part of this the navy can prosecute quickly and cleanly. There's a warrant with his name on it already."

Xanthe looked out the frosted window, toward the guest wing of the estate where Voss had been held since the bridge—not quite prisoner, not quite guest, a man waiting to find out which he was. She thought about a harbor tavern, years ago, and a younger Cassian Voss buying her a drink with money he couldn't afford and telling her, laughing, that the difference between them was that she ran from her name and he ran toward his.

He'd been wrong about which way he was running. So had she.

"He'll want to see you," Halloway said. "Before they take him. He asked."

"Of course he did." Xanthe picked up the ledger—her mother's page—and folded the corner down to mark it. "Tell him tomorrow. Tonight—" she looked around the little room, at her crew, her family, her improbable, stubborn, loyal people, all of them crammed into a radio shack smelling of dust and victory and Tomas's stolen soup, "—tonight we finish the list. Four hundred families are waiting to hear their names. The least I can do is say them properly."

She keyed the mic. The tubes hummed. Outside, the snow went on falling, soft and endless, burying the roads, the fences, the old order of things—and inside, warm as a lit window, Xanthe Marchetti began to read.

*"Ashford, Wren. Twelve years' service. Owed: 3,980 crowns..."*
