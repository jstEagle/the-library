## 20. Aftermath at Rivergate

The village smelled of woodsmoke and thawing river mud, and Priya thought it was the best smell in the world.

She'd spent three weeks in a hospital in the lowlands learning to walk properly again on a frostbitten foot, and the whole time she had dreamed of smells that weren't antiseptic. Woodsmoke. Bread. The particular green rot of a riverbank in early spring. She stood on the ferry dock with her duffel over one shoulder and her cane—temporary, she kept telling everyone, including herself—and watched Rivergate resolve out of the morning mist: slate roofs, crooked chimneys, the church spire leaning like a man listening hard.

Tomas was already unloading crates from the ferry's hold with one arm, because Tomas considered two arms an extravagance when one would do. Xanthe stood at the rail beside her, very still, watching the village come closer.

"You could wait on the boat," Priya said. "Circle the lake a few times. Very dignified. Mysterious."

"They're expecting us."

"That's what worries you."

Xanthe didn't answer, which was answer enough. She wore her good coat—the one without salt stains—and she had done something with her hair that suggested she'd asked someone for help, which meant she'd asked Priya, who had pretended not to notice the significance of being asked. Below them, on the dock, a small crowd had gathered. Not a welcoming party exactly. More like people who had heard a thing and needed to see it with their own eyes before they believed it or decided what to feel about it.

Priya counted maybe forty faces. Some she recognized from the estate, from the weeks after Halloway's transmission went out—families who had arrived with folders of photographs and letters twenty years old, names they'd been saying into the dark all this time. An old woman named Marta Ostrander who had hugged Priya so hard her ribs had creaked. A boy of about ten who had asked if the strongbox was heavy, and when told it was, had nodded gravely, as though weight were proof of honesty.

Others were strangers. Others were here to be angry, and Priya couldn't blame them. Twenty years was a long time to wait for money that had been stolen from you, and no amount of radio broadcasts made up for the winters people had gone without.

"Captain," Tomas called up, "you planning to disembark today, or are we establishing a new life aboard?"

"We're coming," Xanthe said.

* * *

They had taken rooms above the tavern because the tavern was where everything happened in Rivergate, and also because its landlady, Mrs. Ferro, charged fair rates and fed them too much, which Priya suspected was Xanthe paying extra without anyone admitting it.

The first week was paperwork. There was so much paperwork. Halloway, awaiting court-martial in a cell he described by letter as "spartan but honest," had arranged for an independent commission to oversee claims, and the commission sent forms, and the forms required signatures, and the signatures required witnesses, and every witness required tea, and every cup of tea required Mrs. Ferro to comment on how thin everyone looked.

Priya handled the technical side—coordinates, salvage manifests, the chain-of-custody record for the strongbox itself, which sat in a bank vault in the county seat under more seals than it had ever had aboard the navy transport. She was good at this kind of work. Order out of chaos. It was why Xanthe had hired her originally, back when "dive lead" had sounded glamorous and turned out to mean "person who fills out dive logs at midnight."

Xanthe handled the people. That was the part Priya watched.

Because the families came, day after day, and not all of them came gently.

There was Mr. Aldous Wren, whose father had been a conscripted laborer on the mountain road project, who stood in the tavern's back room with his hat in his hands and said, quietly, "My mother died waiting. You understand? She died. And now there's a form."

There was the Delacroix family, three siblings who did not agree among themselves about anything except that the delay was an outrage, and who said so loudly enough that Mrs. Ferro appeared twice with the teapot held like a shield.

And there was a woman named Ilsa Brandt, who didn't shout at all. She sat across from Xanthe with her hands folded and said, "You knew. Your mother knew. Your family name is on half these documents. Why should we trust you to hand us what's ours?"

Priya, sorting manifests at the side table, stopped sorting.

She expected Xanthe to deflect. She'd seen her captain deflect customs officers, rival salvagers, a customs officer who was also a rival salvager. Deflection was practically a maritime skill.

But Xanthe set down her pen and said, "You shouldn't. Not on my word alone. That's why the ledgers matter—they don't need me to vouch for them. Every name, every amount owed, every family. It's all written down by the people who took it, in their own handwriting, because they were arrogant enough to keep records." She slid a copy across the table. "Check it against your own papers. Check everything. If I've made one error, say so publicly, and I'll stand corrected in front of whoever you like."

Ilsa Brandt looked at her for a long moment. "Your mother refused this money. Ran from it. And you crossed a mountain range in winter to bring it back. What does that make you?"

"Tired," Xanthe said, and something in the room loosened—a breath, almost a laugh—and Ilsa Brandt signed her claim form.

That evening Priya found Xanthe on the tavern's back step, staring at the river, holding a cup of tea she hadn't drunk.

"You know," Priya said, sitting down carefully, favoring the foot, "most people would have argued back."

"With Brandt? She'd have eaten me alive. She's right, anyway." Xanthe turned the cup in her hands. "Twenty years, Priya. They're not angry at me, really. They're angry at the waiting. I'm just the person standing closest when it boils over."

"Doesn't make standing there easier."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "But it makes it necessary. Somebody has to be the person standing closest. Might as well be me. I've got practice being blamed for things my mother did."

It was the closest thing to a joke she'd managed in days, and Priya took it as a good sign, and drank her own tea while the river went on doing what rivers do, which is keep moving regardless of what humans put in them or take out.

* * *

The middle weeks brought the hearings—not trials, exactly, but public sessions in the church hall where the commission read findings and families responded, and where the word "navy" was spoken aloud by civilians without anyone flinching, which Priya understood was historically unprecedented in this valley.

Tomas attended every single one. He sat in the back row with his notebook—he always had a notebook, usually full of axle specifications—and he took notes on testimony like a man studying engine tolerances. When Priya asked him why, he shrugged.

"Somebody should write down what actually gets said. Commissions write down what they want remembered." He tapped the pencil against his chin. "Also, if anyone ever accuses our crew of anything, I intend to produce documentation so thorough their lawyers cry."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm enjoying the crying lawyers part. Hypothetically."

It was Tomas who noticed, on the fourth day of hearings, the man in the gray coat who never spoke. He sat near the back, took no notes, and left before each session ended. On the fifth day Priya followed him out, at a distance, with the unhurried gait of a woman with a bad foot who therefore had a perfect excuse for loitering.

He walked to the telegraph office, sent a wire, and bought a paper. Ordinary. Except that when he passed her on the street, he gave her a nod of recognition—not hostile, not friendly. The nod of a man confirming an inventory.

She reported it to Xanthe that night, expecting alarm. Instead Xanthe just nodded slowly, as if ticking a box.

"Halloway warned us. The inquiry goes wider than the wages. There were officers involved in the original theft who aren't in custody yet, and some of them have friends who'd rather this all stayed quiet." She said it flatly, the way she reported weather. "We're not in danger. We're being counted."

"How is that comforting?"

"It isn't. But panic is expensive and we can't afford it." Then, softer, with the ghost of a smile: "Besides, we survived a whiteout, a betrayal, a raft built from depot wreckage, and Tomas's cooking. A man in a gray coat doesn't move the needle."

"Heard that," Tomas called from inside.

"You were meant to!"

* * *

The hardest day came in the third week, when the Ostranders arrived en masse.

Marta Ostrander had been at the estate, warm and grateful. Her son, Gerhardt, was another matter. He was a big man with a quarry worker's shoulders and a voice that filled the church hall without effort, and when the commission confirmed his family's claim—the largest single sum, his father having labored longest—he stood up and did not look at the commissioners at all.

He looked at Xanthe.

"Twenty years," he said. "My father broke his back building their road, and when he came home they told him the pay was 'deferred.' Deferred. He deferred himself into an early grave waiting for it. And where was the Marchetti family all that time? Comfortable. Comfortable and quiet, on money that wasn't even theirs—it was ours, and they wouldn't touch it, oh no, too principled, so it sat in a box at the bottom of a lake while we went hungry. Principles don't feed children, Captain Marchetti."

The hall went very quiet. Priya's hands curled around her cane.

Xanthe stood. She didn't rush it. She let the silence sit until it belonged to her, and then she said, "You're right."

Gerhardt blinked. It was clearly not the response he'd loaded.

"Everything you said is right. My mother knew what the money was and she chose to disappear rather than fight for it, and disappearing was easier for her than it was for your father, because she had options he never did. Her refusal cost your family nothing directly—but it cost this valley twenty years of truth, and truth was worth money, and the delay has a price nobody's ledger accounts for." She paused. "The commission will tell you the interest calculations. They won't tell you it isn't enough. So I will: it isn't enough. Nothing they can pay is enough. What I can do is make sure every name in those ledgers gets read into the public record, so that no one ever gets to pretend this didn't happen, and so that the next family—because there's always a next family—has somewhere to point."

Gerhardt stared at her. The hall stared at her.

"My father," he said finally, gruffly, "would have hated being a cause. He'd have wanted the money and a quiet life." A rough sound, half laugh. "But he'd have liked that you didn't apologize your way out of it. He hated that most. People apologizing instead of fixing."

"I'm told fixing takes longer," Xanthe said.

"It does." He sat down. And that was that—or seemed to be, until the session ended and he sought her out in the churchyard, and Priya, pretending to examine the memorial stones, saw the two of them talking for nearly an hour, and saw Gerhardt Ostrander shake Xanthe's hand at the end like a man closing a deal he intended to honor.

That night Priya said, "You could have been angry back. He was rough on you."

"He earned the right to be. I didn't earn the right to dodge." Xanthe was re-rigging a dive lamp at the table, hands busy, eyes down. "My mother taught me everything about running away, Priya. I'm teaching myself the other thing. It's slower."

"Everything worth learning is."

"Did you just quote Mrs. Ferro at me?"

"She quotes everyone. Eventually it cycles back around."

* * *

On the last day of the month, the commission released the first payments.

Priya stood in the bank square in the cold bright morning and watched people collect envelopes that contained, in paper form, two decades of deferred lives. It was not a celebration. Some people wept. Some people laughed in the wrong places. One elderly man held his envelope up to the light as if checking whether hope could be counterfeited. Marta Ostrander kissed the bank manager on both cheeks, scandalizing him thoroughly.

And some people simply took the envelope and walked home, and Priya understood that too. Money returned is not time returned. The boy who'd asked if the strongbox was heavy was there with his grandmother, and he tugged Priya's sleeve and said, "Is it true you ran the rapids on a raft made of a broken boat?"

"Made of several broken boats, technically."

"Cool," he said, with the gravity of a judge passing sentence, and Priya felt, absurdly, that she had been awarded a medal.

Xanthe stayed through all of it, last to leave the square, answering questions, signing nothing that mattered and everything that did. When the crowd thinned, Priya limped over and stood beside her.

"Well," Priya said. "You traded invisibility for a name. How's it wearing?"

"Heavy," Xanthe said. Then she smiled, tired and real. "Good heavy. Like the strongbox."

"The strongbox was mostly ledgers. Paper's light."

"Don't ruin my metaphor. I only get one per season."

They were still laughing when Tomas came around the corner of the bank at a pace just short of running, which for Tomas constituted open panic. He had a telegram in his fist and an expression Priya had seen exactly once before—aboard the raft, when the bridge came into view downstream.

"What?" Xanthe said. "What is it?"

Tomas caught his breath. "Wire from the prison administration. Voss." He handed it over. "He's asking for you. By name. Says—" Tomas squinted at his own memory of it— "says he's found something else. Something your mother left behind. And he'll only tell you."

Priya watched Xanthe read the wire twice, watched her jaw set in the way it did before a dive into black water, and thought: here we go again.

"He's in custody," Priya said. "Whatever he's found, it can wait."

Xanthe folded the wire along its crease, once, precisely, and put it in her coat pocket over her heart, which was either sentiment or strategy, and with Cassian Voss it was never possible to tell which.

"No," she said. "With him, it never waits. That's the whole problem with him."
