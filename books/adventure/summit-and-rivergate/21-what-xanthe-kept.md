## 21. What Xanthe Kept

The package arrived on a Tuesday, which Xanthe would later decide was the most insulting part of the whole affair.

Tuesdays were for engine oil and inventory lists. Tuesdays were when Tomas counted washers like a miser counting coins and Priya tested the air lines until they sang. Nothing dramatic was supposed to happen on a Tuesday. Dramatic things came on Fridays, wrapped in weather warnings or navy paperwork. A Tuesday parcel in brown paper, tied with actual string, sitting on the dock beside the *Kestrel's* gangplank like an ordinary delivery of rope or rivets—that was a violation of the natural order.

"It's got your name on it," Priya said, not touching it. She had the particular stillness of a woman who had spent too many years defusing things underwater to touch anything suspicious on land.

"Has it." Xanthe looked at the label. Her name, yes—Xanthe Marchetti, written in a hand she recognized with a jolt that went through her ribs like cold water. Cassian Voss had beautiful handwriting. He'd always had beautiful handwriting. It was one of the many infuriating things about him, right up there with his jawline and his talent for surviving situations that should have ended him.

"Voss?" Tomas asked, coming up the dock with a coil of hose over his shoulder. He'd lost weight on the mountain and gained it back wrong—all of it apparently migrating to his forearms—and he now had the permanent squint of a man recalculating every load limit he'd ever trusted. "The one who sold you to the buyer? That Voss?"

"The same."

"And he's sending you mail."

"Apparently."

"Through the post," Priya said. "Like a person."

"He is technically a person," Xanthe said. "The courts are still deciding what kind."

She took the parcel below, into the wheelhouse, because some things you didn't open on a dock in front of your crew no matter how much they pretended not to hover. They hovered anyway. She could see Priya's reflection in the wheelhouse glass, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman prepared to perform the Heimlich maneuver on a parcel if necessary.

Inside the brown paper: a smaller box, wooden, old, its corners worn soft. Inside that, nested in wood shavings like something precious—or something someone wanted very badly to seem precious—the brass plate.

Her mother's initials caught the light. E.M., stamped deep, the way stamps go when someone presses hard enough to mean it. The plate was tarnished at the edges but someone had polished the center recently, recently enough that Xanthe could see her own warped reflection looking back at her, thin and silvered and slightly appalled.

There was a note beneath it. One page. Beautiful handwriting.

*Xanthe —*

*They let me have my effects before the trial. Most of them were confiscated. This one they couldn't figure out how to classify, so eventually a very tired clerk gave up and put it in the "personal" pile, which I suspect is where everything true ends up eventually.*

*I found it in the wreck's purser's cabinet, third dive, while your people were hauling the box. I've been carrying it since. I told myself I was holding it as leverage. Then insurance. Then a bargaining chip. Then, somewhere over the pass, I noticed I was just holding it.*

*Your mother pressed those initials herself—I checked the records after everything came out. She did the fitting on the Rivergate's cabin fittings the spring before she left Rivergate village for good. She was nineteen. Nobody ever mentions that she worked, only that she refused. But refusal is work too. Harder work than brass.*

*I'm not asking for absolution. I've read enough court documents lately to know what absolution costs and I can't afford the down payment. I'm asking you to keep this, because keeping things is what you do—you kept your crew together through a winter that should have scattered them, you kept a promise to strangers who've been dead twenty years, and you even kept me alive at the boathouse when leaving me for the snow would have been tidier and, frankly, better for your reputation.*

*Some loves survive being chosen second. I chose survival. You chose everyone else. We're both still here, which means one of us was wrong about the other, and I've decided it was me.*

*Fair winds. Or whatever wind you'll accept.*

*C.V.*

*P.S. — The trial begins in March. If you come, sit behind me. It confuses prosecutors wonderfully.*

Xanthe read it twice. Then she sat down on the wheelhouse bench, which creaked in protest, and held the plate in both hands and did not say anything for a long time.

Outside, Priya's reflection waited. Eventually the wheelhouse door opened—not waiting for permission, because Priya never had—and Priya leaned against the frame with two mugs of coffee, handing one over without ceremony.

"Well?" she said.

"He sent me my mother's brass plate."

"The one from the wreck."

"The same."

"The one you hid from us for half a season and then lied about for another quarter of one."

"When you put it like that—"

"I always put it like that." Priya sipped her coffee. "It's my whole personality."

Xanthe turned the plate over. On the back, faint, almost gone, there was a maker's mark and a date. The spring her mother was nineteen. The spring before everything. Xanthe tried to imagine her—Elena Marchetti, young, hands stained with polish, pressing her own initials into a fitting for a navy transport she had no reason to think would matter. No reason to think it would sink. No reason to think that twenty-some years later her daughter would hang off its hull in black water and find her name waiting there like a question.

"She touched this," Xanthe said. "Before any of it. Before the money, before the refusal, before she walked out of our lives and took the silence with her. It's just... a piece of a ship. She made a thing, and it survived, and now it's mine."

Priya considered this with the gravity she brought to all matters, including lunch. "So it's not really about him at all."

"No."

"Good. Because if a man could buy his way back into your good graces with tarnished hardware, I'd have to reevaluate my entire opinion of sentimentality, and I've built a lot on that opinion."

"You think I should throw it in the harbor."

"I think," Priya said, "that what you do with it is between you and your mother, and that Cassian Voss gets exactly zero credit either way. He didn't give you a gift. He returned stolen property. There's a difference, and the difference has his name on it."

Xanthe almost laughed. "You've been talking to the lawyers."

"I've been talking to everyone. It's a small harbor. Everyone talks to everyone. Did you know the fishmonger has opinions about your love life? Strong ones. He's pro-Voss, which surprised me, because I had him down as anti-everyone."

"The fishmonger is pro-Voss?"

"He says a man who sells you out once knows exactly how he did it, which makes him safer than a man who might sell you out someday. I don't follow the logic but he delivered it with great confidence while weighing my mackerel, so it felt authoritative."

* * *

The *Kestrel* came out of the yard in February, three weeks late and twice over budget, which Tomas announced as "on time and under budget, adjusted for reality." She gleamed. Not yacht-gleam—nothing about the Kestrel would ever gleam properly—but the honest gleam of fresh paint over patched steel, new welds ground smooth, a rebuilt axle assembly that Tomas showed off to anyone who stood still long enough, including, on one memorable occasion, a seagull.

Xanthe supervised the refit the way she supervised everything: by being present, asking questions, and pretending not to notice when the crew rearranged her plans entirely. The wheelhouse got new glass. The chart table got a proper drawer, which meant that for the first time in the boat's history there was a place where important papers could live without being stuffed under a fender.

And there was a blank spot above the chart table. A small one, at eye level, where a previous owner had once mounted a barometer and Xanthe had never gotten around to replacing it. Every few days during the refit, someone asked about it.

"What goes there?" Tomas asked.

"A clock, maybe," said Priya.

"A photo of the crew," suggested Dev, their newest hand, who had joined after the pass and still treated everything with the wide-eyed reverence of a man who'd heard the stories secondhand and believed none of them.

"Nothing," said Xanthe. "It's nothing."

But she caught herself looking at it. Standing at the helm in the evenings with the harbor lights coming on, she'd find her eyes drifting to that small bare patch of bulkhead, and her thumb would move against her fingers the way it did when she was holding something that wasn't there.

The plate lived in the chart drawer for six weeks. Wrapped in cloth, at the bottom, under the tide tables. She told herself she was deciding. She was very good at telling herself she was deciding; she'd had years of practice. Her mother had been excellent at it too, according to Aunt Rosa—a woman who could deliberate over a decision for a decade and call it principle.

In March, the trial began. Xanthe did not go. She told the crew it was because the season was starting, which was true. She told herself it was because she had nothing to say to him, which was also true, in the way that most comfortable lies are true—true adjacent, true-adjacent-adjacent, circling the truth like a patrol boat circling a wreck.

Dev went instead, on his own initiative, and came back with a full report delivered over dinner with the enthusiasm of a man describing a football match. "He wore a gray suit. Very restrained. His lawyer is terrifying—he made a navy commander apologize in open court, actually apologized, I thought the man was going to cry. And Voss—" Dev paused, savoring it. "When they asked him why he hired your crew in the first place, knowing what the cargo was, he said—" Dev consulted a napkin on which he'd written it down, because of course he had. "'Because she was the only diver I knew who'd bring it up whole, and the only person I knew who wouldn't keep it.' Direct quote. The courtroom went very quiet."

"Hm," said Xanthe.

"That's it? 'Hm'?"

"What would you like me to say?"

"Something! Anything! Priya, tell her to say something."

"Priya," said Priya, buttering bread, "believes people are entitled to their 'hm's."

But that night, Xanthe climbed to the wheelhouse alone, unwrapped the plate from its cloth, and held it up to the bare spot on the bulkhead.

It fit. Of course it fit. The mounting holes from the old barometer lined up almost exactly, as though the space had been waiting for it, as though the boat had known before she did—which, knowing boats, it probably had.

She didn't screw it in that night. She held it there, arms raised, looking at her mother's initials hanging in the lamplight above the chart table where every course she'd ever plot would pass beneath them, and she thought about what it meant to keep something.

Keeping wasn't forgiving. She understood that now, in the marrow-deep way you understand things after a winter in the mountains. Keeping the plate didn't mean her mother's choices were right, or that the silence had been kind, or that Elena Marchetti hadn't broken things that stayed broken. Keeping it meant saying: this happened, and it was mine, and I am not afraid of it anymore. It meant standing behind her name even the parts of the name that hurt.

And keeping Voss's note—she'd kept that too, folded in the same drawer, and she was honest enough with herself to know why. Not because it excused him. Selling them at the bridge had nearly killed Priya; there was no version of events where that became a charming anecdote. But because he'd been right about one thing, in the end, and it was the thing nobody else had the nerve to say: she had stopped running. Somewhere between the frozen tollhouse and the boathouse, between the cairn field and the radio broadcast, she had traded invisibility for testimony, and she had done it in front of witnesses, and she was still here.

Some loves survive being chosen second. She read that line again in her head, and this time she didn't flinch. It wasn't a plea. It was a diagnosis. He'd loved her the way a man loves a lighthouse—from a distance, mostly, and mainly when he needed saving. And she'd loved him the way you love a riptide: knowing exactly what it was, going in anyway, swimming hard afterward.

Neither of those was the love she wanted. But both of them had been real, and real things deserved to be kept, even the ones you'd never choose again.

She screwed the plate to the bulkhead with four brass screws, taking her time, seating each one properly. When she finished, she stepped back, and the wheelhouse looked different—not finished, exactly, but inhabited. Like a room where someone had finally unpacked.

"There," she said, to the plate, to the boat, to her mother, to whoever was listening. "Kept."

* * *

Spring came to the harbor the way it always did: sideways, arguing with itself, one warm week followed by a cold snap that made Tomas mutter about climate and fate in equal measure. The crew took small jobs—survey work, a drowned dinghy, a favor for the harbormaster that involved retrieving a ceremonial cannon nobody could explain. Money was tight but honest, and honesty, Xanthe was discovering, had a flavor. It took getting used to, like strong tea.

Aunt Rosa visited in April, first time aboard, gripping the rail like it owed her money. She inspected the wheelhouse, approved of the new glass, disapproved of the coffee, and then stopped dead in front of the brass plate.

For a long moment she said nothing. Xanthe watched her sister-in-law's face do several complicated things in sequence, like a lock turning through tumblers.

"Elena fitted that," Rosa said finally. "I was there. She was so proud. She said, 'Rosa, when I'm rich and famous, this will be worth more than the whole ship.'" A short laugh, wet at the edges. "She was wrong about the rich and famous. She was right about the worth."

"Yeah," Xanthe said. "I think she was."

Rosa reached out and touched the edge of the plate, gently, the way you touch a headstone. "Your mother would have hated all of this, you know. The attention. The newspapers. Her name in the ledgers, in the articles, spoken out loud in courtrooms." She turned to look at Xanthe, and her eyes were fierce and fond. "And she would have been so proud of you for doing it anyway. Both things. At the same time. That was Elena—two true things, held together, refusing to pick."

"Sounds exhausting."

"It was. Loving her was exhausting. So is loving you, frankly, but here we are." Rosa patted her cheek with a hand that smelled of rosemary and engine grease—she'd insisted on inspecting the bilge on her way through—and left the wheelhouse the way she entered most rooms: mid-conversation, victorious.

That evening, Xanthe stood at the helm while the crew argued below about whether Dev's new hammock constituted a fire hazard or an improvement. The harbor lights came on. The water went gold, then violet, then dark. Above the chart table, E.M. caught the last of the light and held it.

Her mother's name, kept. Her mother's refusal, inherited. Her own name, carved into a cairn on a pass she'd cross again in a month, in daylight, with a crew she trusted and a contract she'd actually read.

The mail boat chugged past, heading for the dock, and Xanthe saw Priya wave up at her and hold something aloft—an envelope, white, official-looking, with a crest on it.

"Government seal!" Priya called up, grinning. "Either we're being sued or we're being hired!"

Xanthe looked at the plate one more time. Then she rang down for the crew to assemble, because whatever it was, they'd face it the way they faced everything now—together, in daylight, with the truth already told and nothing left to hide behind except each other.

"Bring it up," she called. "Let's see which."
