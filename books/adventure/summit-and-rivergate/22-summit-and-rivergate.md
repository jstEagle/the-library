## 22. Summit and Rivergate

The ice went out of the river in the third week of April, and with it went the last excuse Xanthe Marchetti had been carrying around like a stone in her pocket.

She hadn't realized she was carrying one until it was gone. All winter, while the investigations ground forward and the lawyers traded letters and the families of Rivergate waited for their money like people wait for a tide that is late but not absent, Xanthe had told herself she was staying put because of the boat. The *Kestrel* needed her hull replated below the waterline, needed new seacocks, needed six weeks of Tomas's undivided attention and a supplier who could actually deliver bronze instead of promising it. That was true. It was all true.

But it was also true that she had not left the harbor since November, and that some part of her had been waiting to see whether the world would punish her for what she'd done before she risked going out into it again.

The world had not punished her. The world had, if anything, been embarrassingly kind. A journalist from the coast had come through in February and written a piece that called her "the salvage captain who chose the ledger over the lockbox," which was the sort of sentence that made Priya snort into her coffee and made Xanthe want to crawl under the wheelhouse bench. Strangers sent letters. One of them was from a woman whose grandmother appeared on page forty-one of the ledgers — conscripted, unpaid, two sons lost to the works — and the letter said only: *You gave us back her name. We didn't know we'd lost it.* Xanthe read it four times and then put it in the drawer with the brass plate, and did not take it out again for a month, because taking it out meant feeling it, and feeling it meant admitting that the whole terrible winter had been worth something.

Then, in the last week of April, a contract came in. An honest one. She checked twice.

A geological survey outfit out of the capital wanted core samples pulled from three sites along the northern shelf — legitimate work, properly insured, payment terms in plain language with no clause anywhere requiring discretion about the cargo. The route ran up the old trade road, over the pass, down to Rivergate on the far side. Which meant crossing the range again. In spring this time, when the switchbacks were mud instead of blue ice, when the tollhouse stood open instead of drifted shut, when you could stop at the top of the world for as long as you liked and nobody was chasing you and nothing was buried in your hold that could hang you.

Tomas read the contract aloud at the galley table, as was tradition, holding the paper at arm's length because his reading glasses were somewhere in the engine room among the spare gaskets, where they had lived since October. He got to the compensation figure and whistled. He got to the liability section and read it twice more, slowly, like a man savoring a meal he didn't trust.

"There's no trap in here," he said finally, sounding faintly disappointed. "I've been looking for an hour. There's no trap."

"Disappointing," Priya agreed. "I'd grown fond of traps. They kept things interesting."

"The last contract had a trap," said Devi from the doorway, where she was coiling line. "The trap was the entire mountain range, plus a navy lieutenant, plus Cassian Voss, plus winter. I'm fine with boring."

Xanthe looked at the vote tally forming itself in the air above the table — everyone's hand already halfway up before she asked, which was new, which was its own small miracle — and signed.

* * *

They crossed in daylight.

That was the whole plan, and it felt almost indecent in its simplicity. Leave at dawn. Drive while the light held. Stop when they were tired. Sleep somewhere with a roof, or without one, whichever suited them. Nobody watching the horizon. Nobody counting the hours until weather or soldiers or betrayal caught up.

Xanthe had made that crossing four times now, and the first three times she had learned it the way you learn a face in a mirror you're running past — in fragments, in glimpses, in whatever you could see while also watching the road behind you. The whiteout had taken the upper meadows entirely; she knew those slopes as a blankness that nearly killed Priya. The night run to the depot existed in her memory mostly as headlight-panic and Halloway's quiet voice on the radio. Even the good day — the day after the tollhouse, when Priya stood with her against the coming storm — was stitched together out of exhaustion and dread with a few bright patches sewn in.

Crossing it slowly, in full sun, with nothing in the hold but survey equipment and a crate of oranges Tomas had insisted on, was like meeting someone you'd only ever seen bleeding, and finding them well.

The lower valley smelled of meltwater and turned earth. The river ran high and loud and brown-green, doing its annual work of rearranging every boulder it disapproved of. They passed the depot where Halloway had handed over the documents, and it was just a depot — corrugated roof, a dog asleep on the loading ramp belonging to nobody and everybody, a woman selling fuel who remembered them, vaguely, as "the ones the army was hassling," and who charged them fair prices and threw in a bag of boiled sweets for the altitude. Xanthe bought fuel and did not explain anything, because there was nothing left that needed explaining, and the strangeness of that nearly undid her. For two years there had always been something that needed explaining, some story to manage, some version of herself to maintain depending on who was asking. Now a woman sold her diesel and asked how the roads were up top, and Xanthe said muddy, and that was the entire conversation.

Priya drove the second stretch, up through the pines, and talked the whole way — about dive sites she wanted to survey in summer, about a wreck off the southern cape rumored to be a smuggler's brig, about whether Devi would finally admit that card games aboard were rigged. The crew listened the way crews listen to Priya, which is to say with half an ear each and total affection. Xanthe watched the treeline climb past the windows and thought about how, last autumn, silence in the cab had meant fear, everyone listening for engines that weren't theirs. Silence now was just silence. You could fill it or leave it empty. Both were allowed.

They stopped at the overlook where the whiteout had caught them — Xanthe recognized it by a leaning pine and the particular shape of the ridge beyond, though without snow it looked gentler, almost apologetic, a slope that had never in its life tried to kill anyone. They stopped because Priya asked to stop, walked out to the edge, and stood looking down at the clouds filling the valley below them like milk poured into a bowl.

"I spent a bad hour right here," Priya said. "Face-down in a drift, thinking about my mother's kitchen."

"And now?"

"Now I'm thinking the view is wasted on emergencies." She turned around, squinting in the sun, grinning. "We should have a rule. Any time we cross this pass, we stop here. Ten minutes minimum. No exceptions for schedule, weather, or act of navy."

"Act of navy," Tomas repeated, delighted. "Put it in the crew charter."

There wasn't a crew charter. There was a grease-stained page in the wheelhouse listing whose turn it was to cook, which functioned as their entire legal framework. But everyone agreed anyway, solemnly, hands shaken all around, and so it became law.

* * *

The cairn field sat just below the summit saddle, where the trail bent north and the wind came through the notch with a long low note like a bottle being blown. Hundreds of stones, stacked by hundreds of hands across who knew how many years. Travelers' names cut into flat rocks and wedged into the stacks — some fresh, some worn to ghosts, some just initials, some names followed by dates, some names followed by nothing at all, which somehow said more.

Xanthe had found her mother's name here in the worst week of her life, wedged deep in the third stack from the trail, half-shadowed: **M. MARCHETTI**, and beneath it, smaller, in a hand that shook slightly: *going home empty.*

Her mother had crossed this pass once, heading back from Rivergate with nothing. Refused the fortune. Turned around. Carved her name into a stone on the way so that somebody, someday, would know she'd been here and chosen the empty-handed road.

Xanthe had stood in front of that stone in November with the strongbox strapped to a sledge ten meters behind her, and understood, finally, what had been passed down to her. Not money. A refusal. And she had spent the rest of that winter learning what her mother must have known on this exact spot: that refusing stolen things isn't loss. It's the first honest weight you can carry.

Now she climbed up to the third stack in the afternoon light, and the others let her go alone, which was its own kindness — they knew, the way family knows, when to be present and when to be elsewhere. She heard them down at the trucks, Tomas showing Devi how to stack stones so they actually stayed stacked, Priya narrating geology at anyone who'd listen.

Her mother's stone was still there. Winter hadn't taken it. Someone had re-wedged it, in fact — pushed it deeper into the stack, protected it, maybe read it, maybe understood it. There were other new stones nearby. The cairn field had grown over the winter. Of course it had. People kept losing things and choosing things and passing through.

Xanthe had brought a chisel and a flat gray rock she'd picked out of the riverbank below the harbor in March, carried it aboard, carried it up the whole pass without telling anyone why. She knelt in the gravel beside her mother's name, set the stone on her knee, and took her time.

**X. MARCHETTI**

She considered adding a line beneath it. *Going home full*, perhaps. Or *stayed*. Or nothing at all. She turned the stone over twice, held it up to the light, and settled on nothing — because the name alone was the whole statement, wasn't it? Her mother had needed the explanation. Xanthe didn't. The name beside the name said everything: *I came back. I finished it. I'm still here.*

She wedged it into the stack directly below her mother's, pressed it firm, tested it with her thumb the way Tomas had taught Devi an hour earlier without knowing he was teaching her too. It held.

She stayed a while longer than she meant to. The wind played its bottle-note through the notch. Somewhere below, a marmot whistled at nothing. She thought about her mother, young and furious and frightened, walking south with empty hands, believing she was protecting her daughter from a name. She thought about how protection and hiding look identical from inside and completely different from outside. She thought about the drawer on the boat with the brass plate in it, waiting to be hung in the repaired wheelhouse, M.I.M. catching the lamplight every evening for the rest of the *Kestrel*'s days.

"You would hate how I did it," she said quietly, to the stone, to the mountain, to the woman who'd been dead eleven years and present for every minute of the last six months. "All the noise. All the witnesses." She wiped her nose with the back of her glove. "But you'd be glad I did it. I think you'd be glad."

The wind gusted through the notch, and the cairn field hissed softly, thousands of stones shifting a millimeter against each other, the sound the mountains make when they're listening politely.

When she came back down, nobody asked what she'd carved. Priya handed her an orange. Tomas pretended to be deeply concerned with a wheel bearing. Devi said, "Ten-minute rule applies here too, captain. We're stopping on the way home regardless of what you say."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say 'we should make distance.' You always say that."

"I'm retiring the phrase," Xanthe said, and peeled her orange, and for a while the five of them — her, Priya, Tomas, Devi, and the mountains — just sat in the sun at the top of the world with nowhere to be.

* * *

Rivergate village had changed in the way places change when they stop bracing for impact.

The investigation office occupied the old customs house now, and there was a queue outside it most mornings — real people, real folders, real claims, the descendants working patiently through the ledgers, page by page, name by name. Some of them nodded to Xanthe in the street. Some of them didn't. One old man, whose family's claim was on the last page of the last ledger, stopped her outside the bakery and held her hand in both of his for a long moment and said nothing at all, and that was harder and better than any speech.

Not everyone had forgiven the delay. Not everyone would. A woman at the general store told her, flatly, that thirty years was thirty years and no dramatic mountain crossing changed the arithmetic. Xanthe agreed with her. Out loud. The woman seemed briefly wrong-footed by this, having clearly prepared for an argument, and eventually muttered that the bread was fresh if she wanted some, and Xanthe bought two loaves, and that was the whole of it. Accountability, she was learning, wasn't a door you walked through once. It was a street you walked down daily, and some days people were glad to see you on it, and some days they weren't, and you kept walking it anyway because it was yours now.

The crew spent three nights in the village. Aunt Rosa — her mother's sister, the keeper of the family's silences, the one who had told her about the inheritance in a cold kitchen last autumn — came down from the hill house both evenings. The first evening she was stiff and formal and asked careful questions about the trial, the estate, the officer awaiting sentencing. The second evening she brought a bottle of the harsh plum brandy she distilled for medicinal purposes and got loose-lipped and funny and told stories about Xanthe's mother at nineteen, stealing a rowboat, losing a shoe in the mudflats, laughing so hard she couldn't row straight.

"She laughed like that," Rosa said, "right up until she didn't. Until the money. After the money, she was kind, and she was tired, and she was careful. You have her carefulness, girl. You had her tiredness too, for years." She refilled Xanthe's glass with a hand that did not tremble at all. "The laughing's back, though. I noticed. Don't lose it again."

On the last morning, the survey outfit's local liaison met them at the bridge — the same bridge where Voss's guides had ambushed them, though it bore no scars now except the ordinary scars of any old bridge, patched planks, moss, a railing someone had repainted green. The liaison was a cheerful young man named Ferro who spread maps on the tailgate and pointed out the three sample sites and said the words "flexible timeline" and "weather holds" and "no rush whatsoever," and Xanthe felt Tomas physically relax beside her, vertebra by vertebra, a man unclenching from a year of deadlines that were really ambushes.

"No rush," she repeated, testing the words like new rope.

"No rush," Ferro confirmed. "The mountain's not going anywhere."

No, Xanthe thought. It isn't. And neither, as it turned out, was she.

* * *

They started back over the pass two days later, samples secured, contracts satisfied, and stopped at the overlook per the newly ratified charter, all five of them standing in the wind looking down at the clouds.

It was there, on the return crossing, with the sun dropping gold behind the western peaks, that Devi said the thing.

She'd been quiet most of the trip, in the way she got quiet when she was turning something over, and she finally set down the rope coil she was pretending to inspect and said, "There's a letter came for you at the harbor office. Forwarded twice. I signed for it Tuesday because you were under the boat." She paused. "It's from him."

Nobody had to ask who him was. There was only one him left in the story.

"Cassian," Priya said, not a question.

"He's out on remand pending sentencing. Cooperating. Reduced charges." Devi shrugged, elaborately casual. "Anyway. It's in the wheelhouse. I didn't read it. Much."

"Devi."

"One corner. The envelope opened itself in transit. These things happen."

Xanthe read it that night in the wheelhouse, alone, with the brass plate already mounted on the bulkhead above the chart table — she'd screwed it in place in March, and every evening the little stamped initials caught the lamp and glowed like a coal. The letter was short. Voss wrote the way he negotiated, which was to say precisely, economically, and with the emotional content hidden in the structure rather than stated.

He thanked her, first, in the flat manner of a man performing an obligation. Then a paragraph about the trial, the officers, the machinery of accountability grinding along. Then, near the end, the actual letter:

*I took the contract because I thought if I put you in front of the thing you were running from, you'd have to stop running. I told myself it was business. It was the truest thing I did and I dressed it as the worst one. The sale was panic. The ambush was cowardice. You know all this; you watched me do it. What I don't know — what I've been sitting in a cell working out — is whether the man who loved you at twenty-three gets any credit for recognizing, at fifty-one, that you were the best thing he ever failed to keep.*

*No answer required. No answer expected. Cross the pass in daylight. Stop whenever you choose.*

*— C.V.*

Xanthe read it three times. Then she folded it along its original creases and put it in the chart drawer, underneath the tide tables, in the place where things go that are finished but not disposable.

She didn't write back. She suspected she might, someday, years from now, when the sentence was served and they were both older and the whole story had settled into sediment. Or she might not. Both futures seemed livable, and that — she understood, turning the lamp down — was the difference between her mother's life and hers. Her mother had been given one choice and it had cost her everything soft. Xanthe had been given the same choice and somehow, impossibly, come out the far side with the soft parts intact. The crew. The boat. The name. Even, in some ruined and unfixable form, him.

She went out onto the deck. The harbor was dark and quiet, the *Kestrel* riding easy on her new plates, the survey gear lashed and ready, the northern shelf waiting with its honest work and its flexible timeline. Summer was coming. There were wrecks to survey, a smuggler's brig off the southern cape, a pass to cross again in July with the charter's ten-minute stops honored at every sacred point.

Staying used to be the thing she feared most. Staying meant being known, and being known meant being found, and being found meant being caught. She had spent twenty years moving before anyone could get a good look at her.

She leaned on the rail and looked at the water and realized the fear was simply gone — not conquered, not faced down in some dramatic moment on a mountainside, just gone, worn away like a stone in a current, the way fears actually leave. Somewhere between the cairn field and the bakery, between the broadcast of the names and the old man holding her hand, between her aunt's plum brandy and the crew voting with their hands already raised — somewhere in all that ordinary accumulated daylight, staying had become the easiest thing in the world.

Behind her, in the wheelhouse, the brass plate caught the last of the lamp. Ahead of her, the season opened like a road.

Xanthe Marchetti stayed exactly where she was, and slept soundly, and in the morning they sailed north in daylight, stopping whenever they chose.
