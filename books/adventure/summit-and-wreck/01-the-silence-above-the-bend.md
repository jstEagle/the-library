## 1. The Silence Above the Bend

The river told you everything, if you listened, and it lied about half of it anyway.

I'd spent eleven years learning the difference, running the Kestrel up and down the Serapine in low-water season, when the channel narrowed to a brown ribbon and the sandbars moved like slow animals with somewhere to be. That October morning the water sat at its meanest and most honest: a hand's depth over the Barrow Shoal, willows dragging their fingers, the kind of morning where a boatman either respected the river or fed it.

I respected it. Mostly.

"Sandbar, port side, fifty meters," I said, mostly to myself, partly to the Kestrel's wheel, which I talked to the way other people talked to God—regularly, without much expectation of an answer.

The answer came anyway. It came through the radio, which had been hissing static at me since dawn, and it came in my father's voice.

Or what was left of it.

*"—Kestrel, do you copy, Kestrel—"* A burst of static like gravel poured down a tin roof. *"—above the bend, we've—"* crackle, hiss, and then, faint and wrong, *"—Rosie, if you can hear—"* 

And then nothing. Not silence exactly. The radio kept breathing, that wet static wheeze, but the voice was gone the way a fish is gone when it slips off the hook—present a half-second ago, now just a memory of weight on the line.

I stood at the wheel with my hands doing their job without me, easing the Kestrel through the shoal while the rest of me stood in that static, listening to a dead man's frequency. Not dead. I corrected myself. Missing. There was a difference, and the difference was eleven years and one abandoned daughter, and I intended to keep the difference.

My father, Edmund Blackwood, salvage magnate, owner of more dredges than friends, had gone upriver six weeks ago chasing a story old enough to have barnacles: the Meridian Star, a colonial treasure ship that had come up the Serapine in 1789 with a hold full of gold and a crew full of secrets, and had never come back down. The official record said storm. My father's record—thirty years of charts, letters, and what my mother used to call his "obsessions dressed as research"—said something else entirely. He believed the ship was scuttled deliberately, up past the Third Bend, and that its manifest named the men who stole it.

And that one of those men was a Blackwood. And that the theft, in some baroque legal sense I never bothered to follow, had made the Blackwood fortune itself the stolen goods.

He'd asked me to come with him. Once. Over the phone, in that careful voice he used for things he'd rehearsed. I'd said what I'd had eleven years of practice saying: *No.*

Now his voice was in my radio, cut to ribbons, saying my name.

I tied off at Corley's Landing at noon, because my hands were shaking and because Corley's had the only fuel pump above the delta and the only bartender who didn't ask questions. I was on my second coffee, which the bartender had generously upgraded to coffee with rum in it, when the seaplane came down the valley.

I knew it before it touched the water. White hull, black trim, the kind of machine that cost more than the Kestrel, the dock, and my entire future combined. Vane's plane. Vane's everything.

Julian Vane came up the dock steps like a man ascending a stage he'd built himself. Silver at the temples, pressed despite the humidity, a smile you could bank with. He'd been my father's partner for twenty years, which in the salvage trade meant he'd spent twenty years holding the shovel while my father dug and taking half of whatever turned up.

"Rosalind." He said my whole name, unhurried, like he'd paid for the privilege. "You look well."

"Vane." I said it the way you spit out a fishbone. "You're a long way from your office."

"My office is wherever the work is." He sat across from me uninvited, which was his way everywhere. "You heard it too, I take it. The broadcast."

My face, I'm told, is good at nothing. "I heard static."

"Of course you did." He smiled wider, and I understood that the smile was the message. "Edmund's expedition has gone quiet. Six weeks of silence, then one garbled distress call this morning. I'm mounting a recovery operation. I need a boatman who knows the river above the Third Bend, and there is exactly one, and she's sitting in front of me drinking rum in her coffee at noon."

"It's medicinal."

"It's triple rate." He set a folded paper on the table between us, and I didn't touch it, and he knew I wouldn't, and he left it there anyway, which was the whole art of him. "Triple rate, plus a bonus on recovery of personnel. I want your boat, your channel knowledge, and your discretion. We leave tomorrow at first light."

I looked at the paper. Triple rate. The Kestrel's hull needed work I'd been postponing since spring. My account had the kind of balance you keep in your head so it sounds bigger.

And underneath the money, in the place I kept the things I didn't say out loud: *Rosie, if you can hear—*

Eleven years of silence from him, and then one sentence with my name in it, and here I was, ready to row up a flooding river after him. That was the thing about fathers who leave. They leave a door open a crack, just enough, and you spend the rest of your life telling yourself you're not going to walk through it.

"What's the operation, exactly?" I asked. "Recovery, or salvage?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to me."

Vane folded his hands. "The Meridian Star is an asset, Rosalind. Edmund understood that. Whatever he found up there, whatever he thought he found—it belongs to the partnership. To the company. I'd have thought you'd want it handled by professionals rather than left to the river."

There it was. Under the smile, under the triple rate: the wreck wasn't a grave to Vane. It was inventory.

"You said recovery of personnel," I said. "You didn't say how many."

"Edmund, and whatever crew he took." A fractional pause, the kind a man makes when he's deciding how much truth a scene can hold. "Six men went up with him."

"Six men, six weeks, one garbled call." I turned my coffee glass a slow circle on the table. "And you waited until this morning to mount your operation."

"I waited until I had a broadcast to follow. Rivers are expensive, Rosalind. I don't spend money on guesses."

No. He spent it on certainty. And that was the thing that snagged in me, the hook under the bait, because I'd grown up around Vane's certainty and I knew what it cost other people. My mother, for one. My father's half of the company, for another, signed away in a bad year with Vane holding the pen and the sympathy.

But triple rate was triple rate, and *Rosie, if you can hear* was *Rosie, if you can hear*, and I have never once claimed to be a woman who resists her own worst ideas.

"Tomorrow at first light," I said. "My boat, my rules on the water. And Vane—if we're recovering people, they come back alive, all of them, or there's no bonus in the world that covers what I'll do."

"Spoken like your father," he said, and stood, and offered a hand I didn't take.

He paused at the top of the dock steps, silhouetted against the afternoon, and said, almost gently, "He'd have wanted you along, you know. He asked me to ask you. Years ago."

The seaplane lifted off the river and climbed away south, and I sat a long time with my cold coffee and the paper of numbers, watching the water carry the plane's shadow downstream toward the sea.

He'd asked. Years ago. And Vane had sat on it, or buried it, or decided I wasn't worth the fuel.

I went down to the Kestrel in the blue hour and started loading fuel and rope and the medical kit I kept for other people's accidents. Above the Third Bend, the river narrowed into country I'd only run twice, country the charts called *impassable at low water* in the tidy handwriting of men who'd never tried.

The radio hissed on its bracket. I turned it up all the way, and let the static fill the wheelhouse like rain.

"Hold on," I told it. "I'm coming up."
