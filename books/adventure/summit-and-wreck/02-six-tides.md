## 2. Six Tides

The first tide took the anchor chain.

I heard it go at dawn—a shriek of metal over stone, then nothing, and when I came up on deck the *Kestrel* was swinging loose above a gravel bar that hadn't been there last night. The river had dropped another foot in the dark. Low-water season didn't creep; it peeled, like paint off an old hull, and every morning the channel I'd trusted yesterday had narrowed into something with opinions.

"Chain's gone," said Okonkwo from the bow, not looking up from his coffee. He'd been Vane's deckhand for eleven years and had the emotional range of a bollard. "Snapped clean."

"Chains don't snap clean."

"No," he agreed, and drank his coffee.

I crouched at the break point. The links were bright where they'd parted—fresh metal, no corrosion, no wear. Cut. Sometime in the night, while my crew slept, someone had taken bolt cutters to my mooring on a river where the nearest settlement was two days downstream and hostile to visitors of all kinds.

Vane's crew slept aft. All six of them, including the one called Marsh, who smiled too easily and whose hands were wrong for a salvage man—too soft, too careful, the hands of someone who'd learned to handle things that went bang.

I stood up slowly and looked downriver, the way we'd come, and let myself think the thought I'd been refusing since the charter office: *You knew this would happen. You signed anyway.*

My father had taught me one useful thing before he taught me how to leave: never run a river you haven't read. So why had I run this one, for this man, at triple pay?

Because triple pay was a year of dry dock, a new engine, and freedom from the bank manager's smile. Because the distress call had been my father's voice, or close enough—*Kestrel, if anyone—Blackwood expedition—we're at the—* and then static, and then nothing, and eleven years of silence doesn't prepare you for how fast you'll move when it breaks.

And because some part of me, the part I kept below the waterline, wanted to see what kind of man chased a ghost ship up a dying river and got himself lost doing it.

* * *

That night I read his journal again by lamplight. I'd found it in his stateroom aboard the *Meridian Star*'s sister wreck three years ago—no. Wrong. I'd bought it at an estate sale, from a man who'd salvaged it from a flooded camp, and I'd told myself it was research. It was obsession with a receipt.

> *Day 9. The manifest is real. I have held it. The Blackwood fortune did not begin in shipping—it began in theft. My great-grandfather signed away heirs who were owed everything, and the ledger names them. Names them, Rosalind. If you are reading this, I am sorry I was a coward twice: once on the river, once at your door.*

He'd written that last line and then, apparently, gone back to hunting treasure. That was my father in a sentence: capable of devastating honesty, incapable of acting on it.

> *Day 11. Vane knows more than he says. He asked today whether I'd reached the bend at Kilometre 340. I told him no. He drew me a chart anyway. From memory. A man does not memorize a river he has never run.*

I closed the journal and listened to the night sounds of the boat—the tick of cooling engine, Okonkwo's snore, the river chewing at the banks—and made myself add up what I knew.

One: the radio relay at Halfway Point had failed, and when I'd climbed the tower to fix it, the cable hadn't frayed. It had been cut, neatly, with the same kind of tool that had opened my anchor chain.

Two: Vane's charts marked the wreck site at Kilometre 347, give or take nothing. Salvage charts are estimates, educated guesses dressed in contour lines. These were coordinates. He hadn't sent me to search. He'd sent me to confirm.

Three: Marsh had asked me, casually, over dinner, how many rounds I carried for the flare gun. Nobody asks about flare guns unless they're thinking about other guns.

I sat with all of it until the lamp burned low, and dread did what dread does when you feed it facts: it hardened. It stopped being a fog and became a keel. I wasn't racing the tides anymore. I was racing Vane, and the finish line was whatever my father had found at Kilometre 347.

* * *

The rapids at the Devil's Elbow nearly ended it on the third tide.

Low water turned them from white water into white teeth—boulders standing proud of the current like a jaw mid-snap, the channel threading between them narrower than my beam. I ran the wheelhouse alone because I run the Elbow alone, always have, and for forty minutes the world was noise and spray and the particular prayer of a woman talking to a boat she loves.

We came through with the port rail kissed by rock and the propeller singing a note I didn't like. Behind us, Vane's tender—the faster, shallower boat he'd insisted on bringing—sheared a rudder on the same line I'd threaded and limped to shore.

That evening, while Vane's men argued about repairs, Marsh came to my wheelhouse. He didn't knock. He stood in the doorway with his soft hands jammed in his pockets and said, "How much do you actually know about why we're here?"

"I know your boss draws rivers from memory."

Marsh laughed, but it came out wrong, like something breaking on the way up. "I was military police before this. Twelve years. You learn to read a crew." He glanced over his shoulder at the firelight on the bank. "They're not carrying tool kits, Rosalind. The long cases? Those aren't sounding poles."

"What are they?"

"You know what they are." He stepped inside and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "There's no rescue plan. There's a recovery plan. Vane needs Blackwood's find confirmed and everyone who's seen it accounted for. You're the confirmation. The rest is accounting." He swallowed. "I signed on for salvage. I didn't sign on for this. And I've been watching you run this river, and I've decided you're the only person on this expedition who might get me home."

"Why tell me?"

"Because my mother raised me better than whatever Vane's paying." He pulled a folded paper from his jacket—Vane's master chart, stolen from the strongbox, and I could see why Marsh had access because Marsh was the one who guarded it. "Look at the annotations."

I spread it under the lamp. The wreck site at Kilometre 347 was circled, and beside it, in Vane's small precise hand: *Confirmed by E.B., Day 12. Manifest intact. Proceed to acquisition.* Dated nine days ago.

Nine days ago, my father had still been transmitting. Nine days ago, Vane had already known exactly what the *Meridian Star* held and exactly where it lay, and he'd chartered me anyway—not to find it, but to be a witness who could later be subtracted.

"He always knew," I said.

"He's known for twenty years," Marsh said. "Your father figured out how. That's the problem."

Outside, the wind changed. I felt it before I understood it—that flat, green smell rolling down from the highlands, the smell I'd grown up with and dreaded, the smell that meant the monsoon had left its mountains early this year and was coming for the valley.

Okonkwo appeared at the rail, coffee in hand, and looked at the sky with the expression of a man revising his odds. "Six tides," he said. "Maybe five. After that, this river stops being a river."

I folded Vane's chart into my jacket, next to my father's journal, and put my hand on the wheel.

Five tides to Kilometre 347. Five tides to reach my father before Vane's accounting reached him first.

"Okonkwo," I said, "wake the engine."

* * *

We slipped the mooring on the fourth tide, in the dark, without running lights. Behind us, Vane's tender sat broken on the bank, and I allowed myself one cold minute of satisfaction before the river bent north and the rain began—fat, warm drops, then sheets, the monsoon arriving like a debt collector.

Ahead, through the murk, the water was already rising. And somewhere up that drowned channel, at a coordinate a greedy man had memorized twenty years ago, my father was waiting—alive, I told myself, alive—or the river was about to teach me the difference between hope and salvage.
