## 3. What the River Kept

The channel wasn't on any chart I owned, which is a polite way of saying it was on none at all. It existed only as a rumor the river told itself twice a year, when the spring tide came up fat and generous and drowned the sandbars that otherwise guarded the cut like paid sentries.

I'd found its mouth by accident three years ago, running low and desperate with a cargo of roofing tin, and had spent every season since half-hoping I'd never need it again. Now I needed it more than I'd ever needed anything, and the tide would hold for perhaps ninety minutes.

"Tell me we're not doing this," said Marisol from the wheelhouse doorway. She'd been my deckhand for two seasons and my conscience for one, and she knew exactly what we were doing.

"We're doing this."

"The chart says—"

"The chart says nothing. That's rather the point."

She looked at the water ahead—a brown silk sheet pulled taut over hidden teeth—and then at me, and did the thing she always did when I chose badly: she tied her hair back and went to stand by the engine hatch, where she could shout at me and the machinery simultaneously.

I took us in on the flood tide's shoulder, letting the sea push us upriver faster than the river wanted to run down. The channel narrowed until the banks were close enough to touch, close enough that overhanging branches combed the wheelhouse roof like fingers checking for loose change. Twice the keel kissed something soft and yielding—sand, thank God, sand forgives—and once it kissed something that was neither soft nor forgiving, and the whole boat shuddered like a dog shaking off rain.

"Depth!" Marisol called.

"Enough!"

"That's not a number!"

"It's an attitude!"

But she was grinning when she said it, because there is a particular joy in cheating death that only people who do it professionally can appreciate, and we shot through the last narrows into water so still it looked embarrassed to be moving at all.

And there it was.

The bend above the wreck hadn't changed since the survey charts of 1887, which showed a deep-water anchorage where colonial ships once loaded what the manifests politely called "produce." The river had eaten most of the old jetty, but its pilings still stood out of the water like the ribs of something that had died standing up. Beyond them, half-sunk in the silt of a century and a half, lay the Meridian Star.

Not much of her left. A black spine of timbers breaking the surface at low water, ribs fanned out like a whale's carcass picked clean. But enough. Enough to know that everything my father had chased upriver—the silence, the garbled distress call, the money Vane dangled like bait on a hook—had led here, to a rotting hull full of other people's stolen lives.

We anchored in the lee of the jetty ruins. Marisol stayed with the boat; I went ashore alone, because some conversations you have to have by yourself.

* * *

The cave was where his journal said it would be—I'd been reading the pages Marisol's defector had smuggled off Vane's ship, reading them over and over in the dead hours of the night watch until I knew my father's handwriting better than he'd ever let me know his face. *Above the third piling, where the rock wears a scar like a lightning bolt.* He'd written it like a man leaving directions for someone he hoped would come, and feared wouldn't.

The cave mouth was barely shoulder-width. Inside, it opened into a chamber lit by a single crack in the ceiling, where afternoon light fell in a dusty column like something holy or theatrical. And in the middle of that light sat Edmund Blackwood.

He was thinner than the man in my memory, beard gone white at the edges, hands wrapped in filthy bandages. His eyes took a long time finding me, and longer believing me.

"You're not real," he said. "Vane sends ghosts now."

"I'm real." I crouched at the edge of the light, keeping my voice level the way you do with a spooked animal—or a spooked father. "Rosalind. Your daughter. You remember me. I'm the one who used to sit on your knee while you read shipping manifests instead of stories."

Something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe, or just the memory of recognition.

"They erased them," he whispered. "Do you understand? They didn't lose the papers. They didn't misplace them. They *erased* them. Your great-great-grandfather signed away his claim for thirty pounds and a barrel of salt pork, and then they burned the true manifest and wrote a new one, and everyone who could say otherwise died of fever within the year. All of them. Conveniently. Thoroughly." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "Thirty pounds, Rosalind. We were bought for less than a good horse."

On the stone beside him lay a leather tube, wax-sealed, stamped with a crest I'd seen carved over the door of Blackwood House my whole childhood without ever asking what it meant. The true manifest. The names of the rightful heirs. The proof that everything my family built—every shipyard, every warehouse, every cold elegant room I'd grown up dusting—was founded on theft dressed up as salvage.

"Take it," he said, pushing it toward me with both hands, reverent, trembling. "Take it home. Take it to the lawyers, the newspapers, anyone. Let them choke on it."

"And what happens to you?"

"I'm finished here." He said it lightly, the way he'd once said *I'll be back by Friday*, and I felt nineteen years collapse into a single moment on a riverbank.

"No," I said. "You're coming with me."

"The boat can't carry—"

"The boat will carry what I decide it carries."

Outside, the light changed. Not gradually, not the way evening comes, but all at once, the way a door closes. I crawled to the cave mouth and looked up, and my stomach dropped through the bottom of the world.

The horizon was gone. Where the sky should have been there was a wall of cloud, purple-black, stacked to the top of heaven and moving toward us with the patience of something that has never once been late. The monsoon. Early. Of course it was early. The river had been generous with the spring tide; it intended to collect.

Downstream, around the bend, came the sound I'd been dreading: engines. Deep ones. Vane's vessel, heavy with armed men and certainty, coming up the main channel while I'd been threading the needle.

"Rosalind." My father's voice behind me, suddenly clear, suddenly sane. "Whatever you're thinking—don't. Vane doesn't want witnesses. He wants the manifest and quiet. In that order."

"Then he can be disappointed twice."

I helped him to his feet. He weighed almost nothing, which frightened me more than the storm. Together we came out of the cave into the first fat drops of rain, each one landing like a thrown stone, and below us the river was already rising, already changing its mind about everything, and Vane's boat rounded the bend into view with men on her decks and rifles in their hands.

My father gripped my arm. "You can't save both," he said—not the papers and himself, but something else, something I wouldn't understand until the water made me choose. "No one ever can. That's the lesson of this river. That's the lesson of our name."

The rain came down like a verdict.
