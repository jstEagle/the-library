## 4. Landfall

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, all at once, as if the sky had been holding its breath for a century and had finally given up on dignity.

We went down to my boat through water that was already ankle-deep and rising. My father leaned on my shoulder, and I learned quickly not to look at his legs, where the rock had caught him in the cave—because looking meant thinking, and thinking meant doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. The boat. The manifest. The man. The river. Pick two, and the river would pick for you if you hesitated.

The *Kestrel* sat low at her mooring, and she would sit lower still. I'd come upriver with spare fuel, tinned food, rope, and the particular optimism of a woman who believed she was going to be paid. Now I began throwing that optimism overboard, crate by crate. Fuel stayed. Food stayed. The spare anchor went into the flood with a splash that sounded almost apologetic.

Vane's boat was coming on behind us, her engines growling against the current, men moving along her rail with the unhurried confidence of people who believed the outcome was a formality.

"Rosalind." My father's voice was thin, but the old iron was still in it somewhere, rusted and bent and present. "The manifest. You understand what it is. What it proves."

"I understand exactly what it is."

"Then you understand what Vane will do to get it. And what the world will do once it's read. Every Blackwood debt called in. Every name on that list dragged into the light. Including mine. Including yours."

I looked at the oilskin packet under his arm, swollen with rain, heavy with a hundred and sixty years of other people's theft. Somewhere in those pages was a fortune with my name on it, or near enough. Somewhere in those pages was also the answer to every question I'd carried since I was nine years old and watched his boat disappear upriver without me: *why*. Why he left. Why he came back hollow. Why the name Blackwood sat on our family like a stone on a grave.

I didn't get the answer. Not cleanly. What I got instead was the look on his face as he watched me weigh the packet in my hand—a look that said he already knew, and that knowing had broken him more thoroughly than the rock in the cave had broken his leg.

Because he'd made the same calculation once, years ago, and he'd chosen the papers over everything, and it had cost him a daughter.

"Get in the boat," I said.

* * *

The arithmetic got worse.

The *Kestrel* drew too much water with the two of us and the fuel and the flood already shoving at her hull. I stood at the gunwale with the manifest in one hand and my father's arm in the other, and the river made its argument in the only language it knows, which is force.

"Take it," my father said. "Leave me. I'm half a man on my best day and this is nowhere near it. The papers are worth more than I—"

"Don't." I said it quietly, and he stopped, which told me something about how I'd said it.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about inheritance: you spend your whole life believing you're owed something, and then the moment comes to collect, and you find out what the debt actually costs. The manifest was proof. Proof meant ruin—Vane's, yes, but also the ruin that follows ruin, the lawyers and the claims and the vultures in good suits, years of my father's name dragged through channels narrower and crueler than this river. I could carry the truth downriver in an oilskin packet, or I could carry the man who'd spent his life drowning in it.

I had spent thirty years wanting him to choose me.

It seemed only fair that I go first.

I struck the match on the second try, because my hands were shaking, and I'd like the record to show they were shaking from the cold.

The oilskin caught beautifully. Oilskin always does; that's rather the point of it. My father made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—half protest, half something that might, in a kinder telling, have been relief—and I held the burning packet until the heat reached my fingers, and then I gave it to the river, and the river took it the way it takes everything, without comment.

"There," I said. "Now we're nobody. Isn't that a relief?"

He looked at me through the rain, and for the first time since the cave, since the bend, since possibly since 1972, my father laughed. It was a wreck of a laugh, waterlogged and rusted, but it was a laugh.

"You are," he said, "your mother's daughter after all."

* * *

We went downriver in the dark with the flood at our backs, which is the only way to go downriver in a flood—fast, and frightened, and steering more than sailing.

The channel I'd found on the way up, the uncharted one I'd shot on the spring tide with my teeth clenched and my affairs in order, was gone. Not hidden—*gone*, erased, a sandbar where a channel had been, the river redrawing its own map the way it had been redrawing this coast since before anyone drew maps at all. So we took the main channel instead, the wide one, the one Vane would take, because Vane had charts that were twenty years old and a certainty that was older still.

We heard him before we saw him. Engines, upstream, driving hard.

"He'll want to pass us," my father said. "He'll want to be first to the wreck. He doesn't know about the fire."

"He knows the papers existed."

"He knows you had them. Which means he thinks you still do. Which means, Rosalind—" and here my father's voice did something I hadn't heard since childhood, something almost like mischief, almost like the man who'd taught me to read the water before he taught me to read—"he thinks the treasure is on your boat. And he is going to come after it with everything he has."

"Which is?"

"A boat older than mine, loaded heavier than yours, and a certainty that the river will behave."

I cut the *Kestrel* toward the bank, into the slack water under the drowned willows, and killed the engine, and we waited.

Vane's boat came down on the flood like a man late for his own funeral. She was overloaded—I could see it even in the dark, the way she sat, the way the water came at her freeboard with an appetite—and she was driving hard, because Vane had never in his life driven at anything less than full throttle, and the river rose to meet him the way the river rises to meet everything, which is to say: it rose.

I heard the sandbar before I saw it. A change in the water's voice, a long low hiss where the flood poured over the shoal it had built that afternoon out of the channel it had erased. Vane's helmsman heard it too—I'll grant him that, he heard it a full two seconds before impact, and those two seconds contained a great deal of engine noise and at least one word I won't repeat.

Then the sound. Not an explosion—a *stop*. The whole river's argument interrupted mid-sentence. The boat struck, slewed, and began, with the terrible patience of water, to come apart.

Men went into the flood. I saw them—headlamps, flailing arms, the small human business of drowning made large by rain. Vane stood at his rail with a rifle in one hand and, God help him, a lantern in the other, and he was looking at *me*, at the *Kestrel* in the willows, and I watched him understand it all in a single lightning-flash of clarity: the papers were gone, the wreck was worthless, the river had beaten him to the only prize he'd ever wanted, which was being right.

"Rosalind!" he shouted, over the water, over the rain. And then, because he was Vane, and Vane to the last: "You'll regret—"

The river took the rest of the sentence. It took the lantern too. It took the boat, eventually, with a sound like a long slow sigh, and it gave back three of his men, whom I pulled out of the current one at a time because I am my mother's daughter and my mother would have done it, and it did not give back Vane at all.

My father watched from the stern, shivering, saying nothing, until the last light went under. Then: "The river keeps what it keeps."

"And gives back the rest," I said. "Eventually. Changed."

* * *

We came down the last reach on the third morning, when the rain had spent itself and the sky had that scoured, embarrassed look it gets after a monsoon, like a man who's said too much at a party. The flood was falling. The town was wet and whole and smelling of woodsmoke and river mud, and the *Kestrel* slid into her berth at the charter dock with three rescued Vane men sitting sheepish on her crates and a broken old man wrapped in every blanket I owned.

I did not go to the salvage office. I did not file a claim on the wreck, which by every law of the water was now mine—found, abandoned, and unclaimed, the Meridian Star and whatever her flooded hold still held. The lawyers could have her. The historians could have her. Let the river keep its dead and its ledgers both.

What I did was sign the *Kestrel* over to no one, which is to say I kept her, which is to say I kept myself. I took my father home—not to the house I'd grown up hating, but to my house, the small one, the one with the leaky roof and the good light—and I sat with him on the porch while the river went by below, brown and swollen and already forgetting.

"They'll ask you what you found up there," he said.

"Nothing," I said. "I found nothing. I found my father, which is a different thing entirely, and not for sale."

He was quiet a while. The river went on being the river.

"Your grandfather used to say we were owed something," he said finally. "Owed by the world. I spent forty years collecting on a debt that didn't exist. And the whole time—" his hand found mine, old and cold and steady—"the whole time, the thing I actually owed was sitting at a dock, running a charter boat, waiting for me to come home and apologize."

"Take your time," I said. "I'm told the river's patient."

He laughed again, that wrecked, waterlogged laugh, and I realized I'd been waiting thirty years to hear it, and that it had been worth more than the manifest, more than the Star, more than every coin in every flooded hold from here to the sea.

Below us the water was falling. By the next tide it would be down. By the one after that, the channel above the bend would be open again, and the *Kestrel* would need a crew, and there were three men on my dock who owed me their lives and knew these waters better than anyone.

I finished my tea. I looked at the river, and the river, for once, looked back like it had no argument with me at all.

"Get some rest," I told my father. "Tomorrow we take on crew."
