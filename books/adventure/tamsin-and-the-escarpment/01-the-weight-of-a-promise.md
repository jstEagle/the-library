## 1. The Weight of a Promise

The river country in late April was a country of noise — meltwater shouting down every gully, the Kettle itself running high and brown with glacier flour, willows hissing when the wind came off the icefields. Marguerite Yarrow had learned to read that noise the way other people read print: a rising pitch meant water where there had been none yesterday; a sudden silence meant a slide somewhere upstream, holding its breath.

She did not love the noise. But she trusted it, which in her experience was worth more.

"Is it always like this?" asked Gerald Pemberton, from the stern of the canoe, in the tone of a man who hoped it was not.

"Always like what, Mr. Pemberton?"

He gestured with the hand not clamped white-knuckled around his paddle — a small, circular gesture that took in the river, the scree slopes, the ragged line of spruce, the clouds tearing themselves apart on the peaks. "All this. The *scale* of it."

Marguerite considered the question with the professional patience she billed at two hundred dollars a day. Pemberton was her third client of the season, a software fortune inherited rather than earned, forty-one years old, recently divorced, recently convinced by an expensive magazine spread that wilderness would fix him. He was not a bad man. He was simply a man who had never before been somewhere that did not care whether he lived or died, and he found the indifference both terrifying and intoxicating, like a first cigarette.

"It's like this," she said. "Some years louder."

They ran the bend below Caribou Narrows, the current taking the canoe by the shoulders and swinging it, and for a moment there was no room for talk — only the brown water shouldering past, the spray cold on her wrists, the far bank sliding. She felt the old animal pleasure of it, the thing that had kept her in this work after everything else fell away: the river asking everything of you and giving back nothing but the fact that you were still here, still answering.

By late afternoon they made camp on a gravel bench above the confluence, under a stand of black cottonwood just breaking bud. The buds smelled resinous and sweet, the first green smell of the year. Marguerite got the fire going while Pemberton stood at the water's edge photographing the light with great seriousness, and she let him, because the light deserved it and because a client absorbed in photography was a client who wasn't asking questions.

It was Pemberton who saw the smoke.

"Is that — is someone else out here?"

Marguerite followed his arm. Across the river, up in the timber, a thread of grey rose through the spruce, thin and wrong. Not a campfire's smoke — too little of it, too steady, too patient. A signal fire, or the remnant of one, tended by someone with nothing better to burn and no strength to burn more.

"That's the old Hollis cabin," she said slowly. Trapper's cabin, abandoned thirty years, roof half-collapsed, listed in no guidebook because there was nothing there to guide anyone to. "Nobody camps there."

"Maybe they don't know that."

"Maybe." She looked at the smoke a long moment. The river said its loud brown say behind her. Everything in her wanted to stay on this side of the water, where the map of her responsibilities ended at the edge of Pemberton's paid-for itinerary. But she had spent eleven years guiding in this country, and in eleven years she had learned one rule above all others: smoke in a place where no one should be was never nothing.

"We'll cross in the morning," she said. "First light."

Pemberton looked disappointed, then relieved, then disappointed at being relieved. "Shouldn't we go now? Someone might be hurt."

"They've kept that fire going since at least midday," she said, nodding at the sun's angle. "If they're hurt, they're hurt slow. And I don't cross the Kettle at high water with four hours of light and a client who's held a paddle for three days." She met his eyes. "Morning. I promise."

* * *

They crossed at dawn in flat grey light, the river running a foot higher than the evening before, the canoe ferrying hard across the current while Pemberton bailed with a coffee pot. The far bank took them into old burn timber, snags silvered and leaning, the ground soft with wolf-willow and last year's grass. Marguerite climbed with the rifle she carried for bears slung across her back and told herself the whole way up that she was being thorough, not superstitious.

The cabin sat in a fold of the hillside where a spring trickled out of the rocks, its sod roof caved at one corner, its door hanging by one leather hinge. The smoke had stopped sometime in the night. That frightened her more than the smoke had.

"Hallo the cabin!" she called, and the words went flat into the trees and came back to her smaller.

Nothing. Wind in the snags. Somewhere above, a hawk turning over its one thin note.

She pushed the door open with her boot.

Inside, the dark smelled of mouse droppings, old iron, woodsmoke — and under all of it, sweetish and foul, the smell she knew from a bad season on the Skeena ten years ago, when a logger had come up the trail holding himself together with both hands. Blood gone bad.

"Hallo," she said again, softer.

A voice answered from the corner, dry as paper tearing. "Took you long enough."

Her eyes adjusted. In the corner farthest from the collapsed roof, propped against the wall on a bed of spruce boughs and a canvas pack, lay a man. Old — seventy at least, maybe older, the kind of old the bush makes faster than calendars do. Grey stubble, cheeks hollowed, hands resting on a belly wrapped in duct-taped clothing stained so dark it was nearly black. Beside him, a tin pail half-full of melted snow, a hunting knife, and a grease pencil worn to a stub.

"I heard your canoe last evening," he said. "Figured you'd come over. Most don't." He studied her with eyes gone yellow at the rims but still sharp underneath, still weighing. "You're not police."

"No."

"Not a doctor."

"No."

"Good." He almost smiled. "Don't need either. Need something rarer." He lifted his chin toward the door. "Close that. Cold comes in like a debt collector."

Marguerite closed the door. Behind her she heard Pemberton retching quietly outside among the willows — he'd looked in over her shoulder, seen what there was to see, and done the sensible thing. At least he hadn't run. She'd give him that in the tip calculation.

She knelt by the spring seeping through the floor stones, filled her water bottle, and brought it to the man along with the field kit from her pack. He drank in small, disciplined sips, the way people drink when they've been rationing themselves and intend to keep rationing even now.

"Aldous Crane," he said.

"Marguerite." She didn't give the surname. Habit.

"Well, Marguerite-no-surname." He set the bottle down. "Let's not waste each other's time. I opened myself up on a deadfall two days back, coming down fast from the high country. Gut wound. I know what a gut wound does, and I know I'm not walking out of here, and I'm not fool enough to think you can carry me." He said it without drama, the way he might have reported the weather. "So here's what happens instead."

"Mr. Crane—"

"You'll get your say. Let me have mine first, because mine has less of it left." His hand moved from his belly to the pack beneath him, and came out with a folded sheet of heavy paper, soft as cloth at the creases, covered in lines and numbers and small dense handwriting. On top of it lay the grease pencil. "I spent nine seasons looking for something up on the escarpment above the Kettle Glacier. Found it last summer. Found it, and understood I couldn't take it, and couldn't leave it, and couldn't tell anybody — because the wrong anybodies are already looking."

Marguerite said nothing. Outside, Pemberton's footsteps crunched away toward the river, giving them privacy or fleeing; with clients it was often the same motion.

"The melt comes early this year," Crane went on. "I've watched this country forty years; I can feel it in my knees, and my knees are never wrong. Three weeks, maybe four, and the calving starts, and the lower corridor closes, and nobody goes up there till August — and by August, if what I found is still sitting there unclaimed, it won't be unclaimed long." He pressed the paper into her hands. It was warm from lying against him, and heavier than paper had any right to be. "I need it delivered. To the escarpment. Before the melt seals the route."

"Deliver it to *whom*?"

"That's the rare thing I mentioned." He leaned his head back against the wall and looked at her, and for a moment the sharpness in his eyes wavered and she saw how much the steadiness was costing him. "There's a man named Edmund Yarrow. Archaeologist. Used to be a good one. He'll be up in that country inside the month — there's an expedition forming, funded out of Vancouver, and Yarrow's name is on the survey permit. You deliver that map to Edmund Yarrow, and to no one else, and you make sure it's *him*, because there are men working for the money behind that expedition who would pay you more than you'll earn in ten years to hand it over, and worse men who wouldn't pay at all."

The name went through her like cold water through a wader's boot.

She kept her face still. Eleven years of guiding wealthy strangers through dangerous country had taught her that too — the still face, the voice that gave away nothing. But inside, everything tilted. *Edmund Yarrow.* Nine years since she'd spoken the name aloud except to refuse it. Nine years since the trial, the disgrace, the reporters on the lawn, and her father walking out of all their lives as though they were a camp he'd struck in the rain and decided not to return to.

"You know him," Crane said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know *of* him," she said.

Crane watched her for a long moment. Dying men, she thought, have nothing left to spend but attention, and they spend it well. "Everyone in this country knows of him," he said at last. "Half of them hate him. Doesn't matter. What matters is he's the only man alive who'll understand what's on that map, and the only one whose name still carries enough weight to get the place protected once it's known." He coughed, wet and deep, and pressed his lips together until it passed. "The escarpment isn't empty, girl. There's a site up there — pre-contact, old, *old*, carvings and a longhouse under the scree, a trade culture nobody's ever written down. I found it by falling into it, near as anything. And I'm the last person who knows exactly where it is, because I never wrote it down anywhere but this."

"Why not go public? Report it, call the ministry—"

"Because reports get filed, and filings get read by the wrong clerks, and clerks have bosses, and bosses have buyers." His mouth twisted. "I've seen what happens to sites that get 'reported' in this province. They get protected right into private collections. This way—" he tapped the paper in her hands "—this way it goes to one man who owes the truth a debt, and he pays it, or I've misjudged him for forty years."

Marguerite looked down at the map. Grease-pencil lines over a base survey — contour lines, elevations, route notes in a cramped sure hand. And along the margins, in the same hand, notations in a shorthand of symbols: strike-and-dip marks, stratigraphic ticks, a system of little coded letters beside every waypoint.

She knew that shorthand.

She had learned it at a kitchen table at the age of eight, tracing symbols in flour spilled from a sack while her father laughed and told her she'd be reading rock strata before she could read novels. *Delta for dip, Maggie. Sigma for the seam. Never guess what the stone is doing; ask it.*

The cabin seemed to tilt. She put her hand flat on the floorboards until it stopped.

"You're pale," Crane observed.

"I'm fine." Her voice came out level, which felt like a small miracle performed by someone else using her throat. "This notation — where did you learn it?"

"From the man who invented it." Crane's yellow-rimmed eyes glinted, and there was something in the glint that was almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened her more than the blood had. "Edmund Yarrow and I worked three seasons together, back before any of it went bad for him. Best field partner I ever had. He taught me to read stone; I taught him which rivers lie." He paused. "You've seen this shorthand before. Don't tell me where. I can see you deciding not to tell me, and I respect the economy."

Marguerite knelt there, the map in her hands, the smell of blood and bud-time sap in her nose, and understood that she was standing at one of those places the river country makes — a fork with water running high in both channels. She could put the map down, walk out, catch the charter at the lake, and never think of Aldous Crane again except at three in the morning for the rest of her life. Or she could take it, and be pulled back into the gravity of the one man whose abandonment had shaped her more than any mountain had.

"What do you want from me, exactly?" she said. "Say it plainly."

"Three things." Crane held up fingers, gnarled and trembling slightly. "One: carry this to Edmund Yarrow, into his own hand, before the melt closes the corridor. Two: don't sell it, don't copy it, don't lose it. Three—" the third finger came up slower "—when you meet the men backing his expedition, look at them closely. Look at what they've packed. If I'm wrong about Yarrow, you'll want to know it early. And if I'm right, you'll want to know who's standing behind him, because money like that doesn't fund archaeology. It funds acquisition."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll ask the next guide who comes up this valley," he said placidly, "and probably die before one does. But you won't say no. I watched you come through that door. You checked the corners before you checked me. People who check the corners don't leave things unfinished."

Outside, the wind moved through the burn timber with a sound like long exhalation. Pemberton called something faintly from the riverbank — a question about timing, about the day's mileage, about the ordinary arithmetic of a guided trip that had already, irrevocably, stopped being one.

Marguerite looked at the dying man in the ruined cabin, at the duct tape and the tin pail and the grease pencil worn to a stub by hands that had drawn this map in whatever light he could beg from the broken roof. He had been alone up here for two days, keeping a signal fire going with a gut wound, waiting for a stranger, betting a stranger's character on the contents of a doorway. It was the most extravagant act of trust she had ever witnessed, and it had been spent on her.

"I'll need supplies," she heard herself say. "And I'll need to finish my contract — three days downriver with a client. After that I can be at the Kettle Glacier approach inside a week."

Something eased in Crane's face — not relief exactly; more like a ledger being balanced. "That'll do."

"And Mr. Crane — I should tell you I'm not the person you think I am. I'm not some saint of the backcountry. I take money to walk rich people around. That's the whole of my virtue."

"Girl," he said, and smiled with cracked lips, "I've been trusting the wrong people my whole life so I'd recognize the right ones when they showed up. You're the right ones." He closed his eyes, opened them. "Now help me sit up straighter. And then get out your notebook, because there are things about that route the map can't hold — wind patterns on the col, where the cornice lies, which moraine lies about being solid. Forty years of it. I'll talk while I've got breath, and you'll write while I've got breath, and between us we'll get it all."

She helped him upright against the pack, arranged the spruce boughs, gave him water. Then she took out the battered Rite-in-the-Rain book she used for trip logs, clicked her pen, and for the next two hours wrote down everything Aldous Crane knew about the high country above the Kettle Glacier while his voice grew thinner and thinner, as though he were spending himself deliberately, coin by coin, buying with each word a little more certainty that the promise would hold.

Near the end he drifted, surfaced, drifted again. Once he caught her wrist with surprising strength and said, "Tell Yarrow — tell him Crane found it where we always said it would be. Tell him the old joke finally paid off." He didn't explain the joke. Perhaps he forgot to. Perhaps there was no longer a difference.

* * *

He died an hour past noon, in the grey interval between one breath and the next, without ceremony, the way the country takes everything. Marguerite closed his eyes, folded his hands over the duct-taped ruin of his middle, and covered him with the canvas from his pack because there was nothing else and no time to dig properly — though she dug anyway, badly, in ground still frozen a spade-depth down, until Pemberton appeared at the door white-faced and said, "Miss — the river's rising. We should go. We really should go."

She marked the spot with a cairn of river stones and Crane's own hunting knife driven into the earth at its head, because a man who planned routes for forty years would want to be findable.

On the gravel bar, pushing the canoe out into the loud brown water, she felt the map against her chest where she'd stowed it inside her jacket, in the waterproof sleeve, over her heart like a letter. Pemberton paddled in silence for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man handling something breakable that wasn't his.

"What was it? What did he give you?"

Marguerite watched the far bank slide past, the snags, the snowline, the white shoulder of the peaks where the Kettle Glacier lay hidden beyond the ridges, already beginning its long slow surrender to the season.

"A job," she said.

But that night, camped below the narrows with the fire low and her client asleep, she took the map out and held it to the light, and traced with one finger the grease-pencil line climbing north and east toward the escarpment — and at the third waypoint, beside a symbol she had known since she was eight years old, she found a second notation, fainter, older, in different ink: a date, and initials, *E.Y.*, and beneath them a single word in her father's hand that she had not read in nine years and would carry now, like a stone in her boot, all the way back into his country:

*Kettle. Come see.*
