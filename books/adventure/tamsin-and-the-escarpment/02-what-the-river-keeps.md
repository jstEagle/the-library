## 2. What the River Keeps

The river took three days to forgive me for leaving it.

I'd left Aldous Crane in that cabin with a fire banked high and the last of his whiskey poured into a tin cup he could reach without sitting up, because sitting up was beyond him now. I'd buried what was left of the gut-shot man's dignity under a wool blanket and a promise, and I had walked out into the grey morning with a grease-pencil map folded against my heart like something stolen.

By the second evening I had my client — a retired dentist from Calgary who wanted to "rough it" within earshot of his satellite phone — delivered back downriver to his floatplane, his photographs of elk safe on his memory card, his tip generous and forgettable. By the third day the river country had closed behind me like water over a dropped stone, and I was alone in my cabin at the edge of Meridian with the stove lit and the map unrolled on the table.

Outside, the Kettle ran high with early melt. It made the windows hum. Inside, I sat with my hands flat on either side of the map as if it might bolt, the way a bird bolts, the way promises do when you look at them too directly.

It was drawn on the back of a surveyor's linen, the kind that doesn't rot, and Crane — Aldous Crane, who had pressed my hand so hard I could still feel the ghost of his grip two days later — had worked over it in grease pencil in a hand that shook toward the end. Rivers in blue-black. The glacier tongue in a hatched white. And above it all, climbing the escarpment in careful switchbacks, a route annotated in another hand entirely.

That other hand stopped me cold.

I knew it before I knew I knew it. That is the obscenity of handwriting — it bypasses every defense you've built. It goes straight to the child you were, sitting cross-legged on a tarp in some sun-scalded trench while a man with dust in his beard taught you to read contour lines like scripture. *The land always tells the truth, Maggie. It's people who fudge their elevations.*

My father's notations. Edmund Yarrow's small, precise capitals, the 4s open at the top like little doors, the way he crossed his sevens twice — once in haste and once, deliberately, as if to say *I meant this*. *ELEV. CORRECTION +3M — BAROM. DRIFT.* A benchmark symbol I had watched him draw ten thousand times: a triangle in a circle, patient as a signature.

I hadn't seen that handwriting in eleven years. I had told myself I'd forgotten it. I had told myself a great many things in eleven years.

The stove ticked. The river hummed against the glass. And I sat there in the smell of woodsmoke and old canvas while a dead man's map opened a door I had nailed shut from the inside.

* * *

Here is what the world knows about Edmund Yarrow, if it knows anything: that he was, once, the most promising pre-contact archaeologist in the northern cordillera; that in the autumn of a certain year he announced the discovery of a trade complex in the high country that would rewrite everything we believed about the peoples of the escarpment; and that six months later a graduate student found the artifacts in his flagship paper did not match the artifacts in his field photographs, and the whole edifice came down in a season. Papers retracted. Name struck from the institute. Funding evaporated like meltwater. His wife had already been dead two years by then, which meant the only person left to watch him fall was me.

He didn't watch me watching. That was the thing. He packed a duffel, said something about needing to be somewhere he wasn't known, and walked out of my life at nineteen with less ceremony than he'd have given a broken trowel. No forwarding address that held. A postcard, once, unsigned, a mountain range I couldn't identify. Then nothing.

Eleven years. Long enough to build a life on the far side of a name. I guided tourists up rivers and down trails under my mother's surname — Yarrow was a brand, and the brand was fraud — and I told myself the wilderness had no use for reputations, which is true, and that therefore neither did I, which is not.

And here is what the world does *not* know, what I did not know until I spread a dead prospector's map across my table and read it like a letter: that somewhere in those lost years, Edmund Yarrow had come back to the high country. Had surveyed the approach to the escarpment above the Kettle Glacier with the same meticulous hand, the same doubled sevens, the same stubborn corrections in the margins. Had stood, at some point, close enough to the ground Aldous Crane died believing in, to measure its elevations to the metre.

The route on the map was his. The annotations were his. Which meant one of two things: either my father had found Crane's site and walked away from it honestly — or he had found it, and someone had paid him not to walk away.

* * *

I slept badly, which is to say I slept in the chair with the map weighted under the cast-iron pan, dreaming of trenches filling with water. Toward dawn I gave up, fed the stove, and dug out the box of clippings I keep the way some people keep cigarettes — knowing they're killing me, unable to throw them out.

It's a biscuit tin. My mother's, originally, painted with wrens. Inside: every article ever printed about Edmund Yarrow's disgrace, plus the thin sediment of mentions since — a symposium panel where he was listed as "withdrawn," a journal correction, a footnote in someone else's retrospective. Eleven years of a man being erased, collected by his daughter with the devotion of an archaeologist and the discipline of none.

The newest clipping was eight months old. I'd cut it out in a diner outside Fort Nelson, and I remembered the strange double feeling of it even now — the lurch of seeing his name in print again, followed immediately by the name printed beside it.

**VANE MINING BACKS CONTROVERSIAL RETURN OF DISGRACED ARCHAEOLOGIST**

*Corliss Vane, the B.C. extraction magnate whose Vane Consolidated has drawn fire from heritage groups across three provinces, has quietly funded a new high-country expedition led by Dr. Edmund Yarrow, whose 20— survey of the Kettle headwaters was formally discredited—*

I had read it four times in that diner and put it away, and I read it five more times now at my table while the light came up grey over the river. The article was mostly about Vane — they always were, even then. Corliss Vane photographed at a gala, silver-haired, laughing, one hand resting on a marble plinth as though the plinth owed him money. Corliss Vane quoted saying that "history and resource development are not enemies; they are neighbours." Corliss Vane's foundation donating a wing to a museum whose board included two of his own directors.

And at the bottom, one paragraph, small as a burial:

*Yarrow declined to comment on the nature of the expedition's objectives, stating only that "the work will speak for itself." Sources suggest the team will deploy via the Kettle Glacier corridor in advance of the spring calving.*

The work will speak for itself. He used to say that too, in the good years, kneeling in the dirt with his hands full of somebody's ancient world. Hearing it quoted in a newspaper bought by a mining man was like hearing a prayer recited by a debt collector.

I set the clipping down. My tea had gone cold. Outside, the Kettle kept up its low argument with the banks, and somewhere in that sound — I'd swear it, some mornings — was my mother's voice, telling me the river keeps everything, eventually gives everything back, but never in the order you lost it.

* * *

What do you do with a map that is also a wound?

I spent the morning doing the practical things, because practical things are how I pray. I re-waterproofed the linen. I transcribed the route into my own notebook in my own hand — elevation gains, water sources, the places where Crane's shaking line suggested bad going, a gully marked with three heavy strokes and the single word *NO*. I measured distances with a thumb and a scale bar and built the trip in my head the way I build all trips: day by day, camp by camp, risk stacked on risk.

Nine days in, if the melt behaved. Twelve if it didn't. Calving season on the Kettle usually opened in earnest by late spring, and after it opened, the lower corridor became a shooting gallery of falling ice, and the escarpment route sealed itself until autumn. Crane had known that. It was why he'd needed me to go *now*, why he'd spent his last coin of strength making a stranger swear. *Before the melt takes the road,* he'd said. *Before Vane's people get there first.*

Vane's people.

I sat back and looked at the word sitting there in my memory, and then I went back to the biscuit tin and pulled the clipping again and forced myself to read it not as a daughter but as a guide, which is to say as a person who assesses parties for a living. Who funds them. Who equips them. What does a legitimate archaeological survey need? Permits, packers, a cook, a geologist maybe, radios, rope. What does it not need?

Helicopter fuel caches. Armed security. A financier with extraction leases staked across half the cordillera and a public habit of calling heritage "neighbours."

Crane had been a prospector forty years. Prospectors know mining men the way prey knows predators — by silhouette, by gait, by the particular quality of the attention. Whatever Aldous Crane had found up there, whatever he'd understood about who else was looking for it, he had understood it well enough to die with a map in his fist and a stranger's hand in his, extracting a promise instead of trusting a phone call. He could have gone to the authorities. He could have gone to the band office, the university, the heritage branch. Instead he'd gambled everything on a woman who happened to stop at a ruined cabin, because — I thought about his eyes, fever-bright and utterly certain — because he'd looked at me and seen something he trusted.

Or someone. God help me, the thought arrived uninvited: maybe he'd recognized the training. Maybe competence leaves a scent, like smoke, like my father's cologne of dust and notebook graphite, and a dying man had smelled it on me and bet the site on it.

If that was true, then Crane hadn't just handed me a map. He'd handed me my father — the real one, the one under the disgrace, the one who drew doubled sevens and corrected his barometers — and dared me to decide which Edmund Yarrow had drawn that route. The honest one, documenting a wonder. Or the bought one, surveying a theft.

* * *

By afternoon I had talked myself into walking away. It was easy. It's always easy; that's the trap of it. I laid it out like gear on a tarp: Burn the map. The site stays secret, which protects it better than any promise. Vane never learns the route through me. My father remains a stranger, which hurts exactly as much as it already hurts, no more, no new wounds. In the fall I take paying clients up the ordinary trails and forget the whole thing, and someday an old woman tells herself she kept her word in spirit, and the river keeps the rest.

I almost believed it. I got as far as folding the map along its old creases.

Then I unfolded it again, because of the margin.

Down in the lower corner, in grease pencil under Crane's shakier hand, was a note I'd taken for a surveyor's joke the first time through: *E.Y. says the stone remembers who listened. Tell him he was right.*

Tell him he was right.

Crane had known him. Not known *of* him — known him. Sat with him. Listened to him say the thing about stone remembering, which I had heard a hundred times at a hundred firepits, which I had carried through eleven years of pretending to forget it along with everything else. And Crane, dying, had written it down like a message he was afraid the post wouldn't deliver.

You cannot burn a letter addressed to your father. You can tell yourself it's not really addressed to him. You can tell yourself the address is symbolic, metaphorical, a literary flourish of a dying man. I stood at my table with the map half-folded and the stove hot and the river loud, and I understood with total clarity that I was capable of any amount of self-deception except the amount required to burn this.

Because underneath the anger — eleven years settled and compacted into something like bedrock — there was the other thing, the thing I hated: I wanted to see his handwriting on the land itself. I wanted to stand where he had stood and find out whether the man who taught me to read contours had been reading them honestly all along, or whether he'd been lying to the world and to me since before I could spell *stratigraphy*. Either answer would cost me. But I had spent eleven years paying interest on a question I refused to ask, and the principal had just come due, carried up a river by a dying stranger.

There was also, I'll admit it here where no one can hear, the simpler arithmetic. If Vane's men reached that site first, carvings older than any nation would end up in a private vault in Vancouver, and the history of the people who made them would be sold by the pound. Someone was going to go up that escarpment this spring. The only variable I controlled was whether it was me.

I am not a brave woman. I want that on the record, wherever records go. Brave people run toward things. I stood at my table for a long time, weighing, the way you weigh a crossing — testing the current with a stick, counting the seconds between the big waves.

Then I took my mother's surname off the shelf where I keep it, blew the dust of disuse off it, and began, methodically, to plan a lie.

Marguerite Yarrow would stay home. But the river keeps everything, and gives it back changed — and a route-finder named something else, someone with steady hands and no famous father, might just talk her way onto Corliss Vane's expedition before the week was out.

I sharpened a pencil. Outside, the Kettle ran high and cold, carrying the mountain down toward the sea one grain at a time, keeping what it keeps.

Nine days to the escarpment, if the melt behaved.

I started packing.
