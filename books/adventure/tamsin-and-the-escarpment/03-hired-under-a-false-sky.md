## 3. Hired Under a False Sky

The hiring hall in Fort Meridian smelled of diesel, wet wool, and other people's ambitions. Marguerite stood at the edge of it, pack between her boots, and watched the expedition's agents work through the morning's line of applicants like butchers working through a carcass.

There were four of them behind the folding tables: a logistics man with a clipboard and the patience of a man counting bolts; a young woman checking certifications with the flat efficiency of someone who had already rejected forty people today; a mountain guide Marguerite half-recognized from seasons past, now wearing a Vane Resources windbreaker as if it had always been his skin; and at the end, half-turned toward the window, a compact woman with iron-grey hair and reading glasses pushed up into it, who was not looking at the applicants at all but at a topographic map spread flat, her finger tracing the drainage above the Kettle.

Marguerite had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it on the river, in the cabin with Crane's blood drying into the floorboards, on the two-day walk out to the rail spur, and again on the bus north, the map folded against her sternum like a second heartbeat. *Yarrow*, she had told herself, is a name that opens doors and burns down houses. *Thackeray* is a name nobody has ever heard of. Her mother's surname. Her mother, who had taught her that a lie told cleanly is kinder than a truth told cruelly, and who had died before Marguerite could ask her whether she'd believed that.

"Name?" The logistics man didn't look up.

"Thackeray. M. Thackeray."

"First name."

"Marguerite." She let it sit there, plain as a stone in a streambed. The lie was not in the name. The lie was in the silence that followed it, the silence where *Yarrow* should have been, and she found she could carry it the way she carried a heavy pack — by refusing to think about the weight until it was already on her shoulders.

"Qualifications."

"Ten years guiding. Class four whitewater, glacier travel, crevasse rescue, avalanche II. Route-finding in the Kettle drainage three seasons." All true. That was the trick she'd settled on: build the false name out of true things, so that any single answer could survive being checked.

The logistics man looked up at last, and his eyes did the arithmetic guides' eyes do — her hands, her shoulders, the scuffed mountaineering boots she'd resoled twice rather than replace. "Vane's expedition is fully staffed."

"Vane's expedition is hiring a route-finder," she said. "I watched you turn away two this morning who couldn't read the icefield map you've got pinned behind you. You've got the terminus marked on the wrong side of the medial moraine. If your man up there navigates by that, he'll walk your whole team into the icefall."

A silence. The woman with the certifications looked up. The guide in the windbreaker looked at Marguerite with the expression of a man watching someone else's dog misbehave beautifully.

The iron-haired woman at the end of the table took off her glasses.

"Show me," she said.

Marguerite came around the tables. The map was a commercial survey, twenty years out of date, and she put her finger on the terminus, then on the moraine, then on the faint pencil ghost where the glacier's edge had been a generation ago and wasn't anymore. "Your survey's pre-retreat. Everything east of here has changed. The lower tongue's thinner — which means more meltwater, which means your crossing windows are shorter and your crevasse bridges are rotten from underneath. You'll want to cross at first hard freeze of the morning, not at midday when everyone wants to move. And you'll want someone on the rope who's crossed this tongue before, because the guidebooks are all lying about where the safe line is."

The woman studied her for a long moment over the rims of her glasses. "You've been up there."

"Three seasons. Guiding rich men to fish and poor men to mine claims."

"And you want to guide rich men to dig." Something flickered in her face — not quite a smile. "I'm Priya Nair. I'm the expedition geologist, which means I'm the person who has to live with whatever route you choose. Can you start Thursday?"

Marguerite had expected more resistance, and the ease of it rang a small warning bell somewhere under her ribs. "Thursday."

"Base camp's at the scree line above the Kettle. You'll fly in with the second lift." Priya Nair extended a hand, and her grip was dry and strong and entirely without warmth, which Marguerite found herself trusting more than any smile she'd been given in a year. "One condition. You work for the expedition, not for Mr. Vane personally. He'll try to make it personal. Don't let him."

"I've worked for worse men than Mr. Vane," Marguerite said, and heard how it sounded, and Priya Nair heard it too, and filed it away somewhere behind those glasses, and Marguerite understood she had just made her first mistake of the expedition: she had been interesting.

* * *

Base camp at the scree line was a village of orange and yellow nylon pitched on a shelf of glacial till, with the Kettle Glacier below it like a frozen river deciding, slowly, over centuries, to leave. Marguerite arrived with the second lift on a morning so clear the peaks threw blue shadows across the ice, and the first person to meet her at the landing was a broad, cheerful man in a cook's apron who took her pack off her shoulder before she could refuse.

"You're the new route-finder. Thackeray." He pronounced it carefully, like a word he intended to get right. "Tomás Reyes. I feed people. You look like you haven't eaten since the railhead, which is an insult to my profession. Sit."

"I just got off a helicopter."

"And the first thing a helicopter does is make you forget you have a body. Sit. There's soup."

There was soup — barley and caribou and something green he'd coaxed out of a cooler with what Marguerite suspected was outright bribery — and it was the best thing she had eaten in a month, and she told him so, and Tomás Reyes beamed like she had complimented his children. He talked while she ate, an easy river of talk: the state of the galley tent, the quality of the coffee Vane had shipped up (excellent, which Tomás found suspicious — "a man who spends that much on coffee is buying something with the other hand"), the geologist who was the only person who thanked him properly, the mountaineers who were competent but joyless, and the boss.

"Mr. Vane," Tomás said, and his voice changed — not darker, just more careful, the way a man's voice changes when he's walking on a frozen lake and hears it settle. "He'll want to meet you. He meets everyone. That's not a compliment."

"What's it like, working for him?"

Tomás wiped the counter that didn't need wiping. "He remembers everything," he said. "Your name, your sister's name, what you said you liked for breakfast in March. And he uses it. Like — you know when a card player shows you a kindness and you can't tell if it's the kindness or the play? It's always both. But the soup is good, and the pay is real, and my sister needs a surgery, so." He shrugged, a big shrug that carried a lot. "You eat. You work. You watch."

Marguerite watched him, this stranger feeding her, and felt the first small dangerous warmth of it — the warmth of a table, of a man who fed people because feeding was what he was *for*. She had been alone a long time. She had forgotten how much a galley tent could feel like the center of a world.

* * *

She met Priya properly that evening, out at the survey stakes above camp, where the geologist was arguing with a rock.

"You're standing on my outcrop," Priya said, without turning around.

Marguerite stepped off it. "Sorry."

"Don't be sorry, be useful. Tell me what you see."

Marguerite looked. Strata, folded and faulted, the limestone above the shale, a seam of something darker running through it like a vein of ink. "Limestone over shale. The dark band's been metamorphosed — contact rock. Something hot came through here."

"Something hot came through here," Priya repeated, with the satisfaction of a woman whose student has passed an unexpected exam. She turned around. Up close, in the low gold light, her eyes behind the glasses were sharp and tired and amused all at once. "Most guides see a hill. Most *archaeologists* see a hill. You saw the contact."

"My father taught geology," Marguerite said, which was true, and which was also the closest she had come to the truth since the hiring hall, and she felt it cost her something, and watched Priya notice that it cost her something.

"Mm," said Priya, and let it go, and Marguerite understood that this was a courtesy, and that courtesies from Priya Nair were rarer than clear mornings, and should be treasured accordingly.

They walked the stakes together as the light went long and copper. Priya talked about the survey — the ground-penetrating radar, the grid they'd cut into the benchland below the escarpment — and Marguerite listened with half her mind and spent the other half on the map folded against her sternum, on the grease-pencil lines that matched, feature for feature, the drainage below them. Crane's map was real. The site was real. And she was inside the expedition that was walking toward it.

"Thackeray." Priya stopped at the last stake. "Why does a guide with ten years of river work take a contract in the ice?"

"The money."

"The money's good, but it's not *that* good." Priya looked at her, and Marguerite held the look the way you hold a crevasse fall — steady, breathing, giving up nothing. "Everyone up here is running from something or toward something. I'm running toward. Tomás is running toward — his sister, don't ask, he'll tell you when he's ready. Vane is running toward." A pause. "You I can't read yet. That's rare. I don't like it."

"I'll try to be more legible," Marguerite said.

"Do." Priya turned back toward camp. "Because the ones I can't read are usually the ones the mountain eats first."

* * *

Corliss Vane came to her tent on the third morning, alone, without announcement, the way weather comes.

She heard him before she saw him — boots on the scree, unhurried, and then the tent flap lifted and a man stood silhouetted against the white morning, tall, silver at the temples, dressed in expedition gear that fit him the way a tailored coat fits a man who has never been cold in his life. He had the kind of face that photographs well and forgives itself easily.

"Marguerite Thackeray." He said the name as if tasting it. "Our new route-finder. The one who embarrassed my logistics man in Fort Meridian." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was, Marguerite thought, a professional smile, the smile of a man who had learned early that being liked was cheaper than being feared and more profitable than both. "May I?"

He didn't wait for the answer. He came in, looked at her gear with an assessor's eye, at the maps rolled at the foot of her sleeping bag, and sat down on her gear crate as if he owned it, which, she reminded herself, in every way that mattered, he did.

"You crossed the lower tongue twice," he said. "Nair speaks well of you. Reyes feeds you too much. And you looked at my terminus map for eleven seconds and found the error." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the watchfulness underneath the charm came up to the surface like a fish rising. "I like competence, Ms. Thackeray. I collect it. Tell me — what do you want?"

"Excuse me?"

"Everyone wants something. The cook wants his sister well. The geologist wants her name on a paper. The mountaineers want the summit and the story. You — you turned down two river contracts to come to the ice, which pays worse and kills more often. So." The smile again, warm as a stove, and just as consuming. "What do you want, Marguerite Thackeray? Because I find that if I know what a person wants, I can make sure they get it. It's the only management technique I've ever needed."

And there it was — the thing under the thing, the hook inside the bait. Marguerite felt the old instinct, the guide's instinct, the daughter's instinct, rise in her like water finding its level: *give him nothing true.* She thought of her father saying it once, years ago, over a camp stove, about a funding committee: *they can't buy what they can't name.* She had hated him for a decade and here was his voice in her head, keeping her alive.

"I want a season's pay," she said. "And to not be on a team that loses anyone."

Vane studied her for a moment, and something in his face suggested she had given an answer he found either boring or interesting, and that he hadn't decided which.

"Reasonable," he said, rising. "Unambitious, but reasonable." At the tent flap he paused, silhouetted again, and looked back at her with the expression of a man doing arithmetic. "We move to the benchland in four days. There's a site above us, in the escarpment, that my — that Dr. Yarrow believes may be significant." The name landed in the tent between them like a dropped tool. Vane watched her face as he said it, watched it the way a man watches a gauge. "Do you know him? Yarrow? He was famous, once. Before."

"I've heard the name," Marguerite said. Her voice came out level. She would remember that, later, as the hardest thing she had ever done — harder than the crevasse, harder than the cabin, harder than the promise itself: to hear her father's name in the mouth of the man buying him and keep her hands still.

"Mm," said Vane, and smiled, and left, and the tent flap fell, and Marguerite sat on her sleeping bag in the cold and discovered she was shaking.

He had watched her face. He had *watched her face.* And she had given him nothing — but he had asked, and men like Vane only asked when they already suspected, and the suspicion would keep, like a crevasse under snow, patient and invisible, until the day it opened under someone's weight.

* * *

That night the galley tent was full — mountaineers, surveyors, the joyless competent — and Tomás fed them all and Marguerite washed pots, because it was something to do with her hands, and Priya sat at the end of the bench with her tea and her glasses pushed up and watched the room like a woman auditing it.

"Vane came to your tent," Priya said, quietly, under the noise.

"This morning."

"He doesn't do that. He sends people. He makes you come to *him* — it's a thing he does, a hierarchy thing." Priya turned her tea mug slowly. "He came to you."

"He wanted to know what I want."

"And what did you tell him?"

"A season's pay and no dead teammates."

Priya looked at her over the mug for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, real laugh, quickly folded away, like a document returned to a drawer. "No dead teammates. He'll have written that down somewhere. He writes everything down." She drank. "You know he's not here for the geology."

Marguerite kept her eyes on the pot she was scrubbing. "No?"

"The manifests don't match the dig. Cargo crates rated at four hundred kilos. Chain. A private fuel cache for a helicopter nobody's supposed to have. I've been a geologist on eleven expeditions and I've never seen a dig provisioned like a mining claim." Priya said it lightly, conversationally, a woman discussing weather, but her eyes were not light at all. "I'm telling you because you looked at that terminus map for eleven seconds and found the error. I'm telling you because I think you notice things."

The galley noise went on around them — Tomás laughing at something, the mountaineers arguing about rope lengths, the stove hissing — and Marguerite stood with her hands in grey water and felt the shape of the thing she had walked into become, for the first time, fully three-dimensional. Crane's map. Her father. Vane's crates. The promise, and the deadline, and the guns she hadn't seen yet but could now, suddenly, feel the weight of, out there in the dark along the eastern moraine.

"Tomás," Priya said, raising her voice, "your sister — the surgery — when?"

"Spring, God willing." Tomás came over wiping his hands, and the three of them were briefly a triangle around the stove, the cook and the geologist and the false-named guide, and Tomás looked between them and said, with the directness of a man who fed people for a living, "You two are talking about the boss. You get the same look the boss gets."

"We're talking about soup," said Priya.

"You're talking about the boss." Tomás hung the towel on its nail with a snap. "Look — I don't need to know. But whatever it is, when it breaks, and it'll break, because things like this always break —" he looked at Marguerite, and his eyes were kind and entirely unfooled, "— nobody eats alone. That's the rule in my galley. Nobody eats alone."

Marguerite stood in the warmth of the stove light, in the smell of barley and diesel and woodsmoke, between a woman who noticed things and a man who fed people, wearing her mother's name like borrowed clothing, and felt something she had not felt since she was a child at her father's field camps: the dangerous, unearned, unearned-*able* warmth of belonging to a table.

She thought of Crane dying alone in a cabin with his blood in the floorboards and a stranger's promise in his fist. She thought of the map against her sternum. She thought of her father somewhere up the valley, in a tent of his own, and of Vane's arithmetic, and of the four hundred kilo crates waiting like open mouths.

"Nobody eats alone," she said, and Tomás nodded once, satisfied, and went back to his stove, and outside the tent the wind came down off the icefield carrying the first smell of the coming storm, and above them, unseen in the dark, the escarpment waited with its buried longhouse and its erased history, and four days out, along the eastern moraine, something was already moving toward it.
