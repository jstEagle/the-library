## 4. The Man Who Taught Me Stone

The morning the expedition left base camp, Marguerite learned that her father had a habit she had forgotten: he ate standing up.

She noticed it from across the cook tent, over the steam rising off Tomás's oatmeal, and the recognition landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well — no sound for a long time, and then the deep cold splash of it arriving at the bottom. Edmund Yarrow stood at the edge of the canvas with his bowl in one hand and a topographic map weighted on the supply crate beside him, and he ate in small absent bites, the way he had eaten at every dig site of her childhood, as though food were a courtesy he paid to his body between more important appointments. He was grayer than the man in her memory. Thinner through the shoulders. But the stance was identical: weight on the balls of his feet, head tipped toward the ground as if the earth were about to say something worth hearing.

*You taught me that,* she thought, and hated him for it, and hated herself a little for the warmth underneath the hate, which was worse.

"More?" Tomás asked, appearing at her elbow with the pot.

"No. Thank you." She made herself smile. "It's good."

"It's glue," Tomás said cheerfully. "But it's hot glue, and out there, hot glue is breakfast." He ladled another half-spoonful into her bowl anyway, unasked, and moved on down the bench, and Marguerite looked back at her father and found that Priya Nair had joined him at the map, and that the two of them were talking with their hands, geologist and archaeologist arguing happily about till deposits and terminal moraines, and that neither of them looked like a man who had sold his daughter's childhood or a woman who would soon learn what the crates in the manifest were really for.

Four days, Priya had said. Four days along the eastern moraine before anyone legitimate should be thinking about the escarpment. Marguerite turned her spoon in the glue and told herself, again, that she was here for Crane's map and nothing else. That the man eating standing up was simply the expedition's consulting archaeologist, hired by Corliss Vane, and that whatever he had been to her belonged to a country she had emigrated from twenty years ago and could not go back to without papers.

The route out of base camp climbed through the river country first — the same braided grey water she'd guided clients through all summer, though from this side, upstream, it looked like a different animal entirely, younger and angrier, all white noise and grinding stones. The trail was a suggestion rather than a fact: cairns every few hundred meters, then not even that, just the logic of the land itself, which said *here the footing holds; here it does not.* Vane rode most of the first day on a horse that didn't like him, smiling at everyone, learning everyone's name, asking questions that sounded generous and felt like inventory. *Where did you learn route-finding, Ms. Ash? Your mother's people are from the coast, I understand? How long have you worked the Kettle?*

Marguerite answered each question with a small true thing wrapped around a large false one. Her mother's surname had opened doors for twenty years; it had never once felt like armor until now.

Edmund walked. Of course he walked. He carried his own pack and refused the horse twice, politely, and by mid-morning he had drifted to the front of the column where the route-finder was, because that was where the interesting ground was, and Marguerite understood with a sinking professional clarity that she had four days of trail ahead of her and he would spend all of them within ten meters of her boots.

"So," he said, falling into step. Not looking at her. Looking at the ground, reading it. "You cut the switchback above the creek instead of taking the game trail."

"The game trail washes out every spring."

"Mm. And you read the washout from the far side before committing." He nodded slowly, as if confirming something to himself. "You test slope with your heel first. Left heel. You don't trust a handhold until you've loaded it twice. And yesterday, when we crossed the scree fan below the bluff—" he finally turned his head, and his eyes were pale grey and terribly attentive— "you sent the pack string across on the diagonal, uphill of the fall line, which is textbook, except nobody teaches that anymore. It's in maybe two books, and one of them is mine."

Marguerite kept walking. The river roared below them. Her pulse was doing something complicated that she refused to acknowledge.

"Lucky guess," she said.

"No," Edmund Yarrow said. "I know that style. I've watched someone move through mountains like that for years. She learned it before she was tall enough to see over a survey table." He paused. "She used to count her steps out loud when she was tired, so she wouldn't sit down."

The trail bent around a stand of black spruce, and Marguerite took the bend slowly, giving herself the length of it to put her face back together. When she came out the other side she was Marguerite Ash again, route-finder, unremarkable, mildly bored.

"You're mistaken about who you're talking to," she said. "But it's flattering that your lost person walks like me. Must make the mountains feel crowded."

Something moved behind his eyes — hurt, maybe, or its close cousin, respect — and then he did the thing she remembered worst about him, the thing that had ended arguments in her childhood before they began: he withdrew. Folded himself back into the work like a letter into an envelope.

"Of course," he said. "My mistake, Ms. Ash." And he dropped back to walk with Priya, and Marguerite led the column onward with her jaw aching from clenching and the whole gray sky pressing down like a thumb.

* * *

They camped that night below the tree line, in a shallow bowl the map called nothing and Marguerite's memory called the Hare Grounds, because her father had once told her that hares wintered there and seven-year-old Tamsin Yarrow had believed this was the most important fact in the world.

She pitched her tent apart from the others, the way guides do, and then sat outside it in the blue dusk cleaning the grit out of her rope while the camp sounds assembled themselves around her: Tomás's stove ticking, the horses shifting, Priya's laugh, low and genuine, from the direction of the fire. Above the bowl the escarpment wasn't visible yet, but she could feel it the way you feel weather coming — a pressure, a held breath, forty miles of icefield and moraine between her and a promise made to a dying man in a cabin that smelled of blood and kerosene.

*Deliver it before the melt.* She turned Crane's grease-pencil map over in her mind for the thousandth time, the wobbly contour lines, the little skull-and-crossbones he'd drawn at the crevasse field because prospectors have a sense of humor even about the things that kill them. Somewhere under that ice lip lay a longhouse, carvings, a trade culture the history books had decided never existed. And somewhere behind her, four days east along the moraine, men with guns were already walking toward it.

"You're doing the step-counting."

She hadn't heard him approach. That was the other unforgivable thing about Edmund Yarrow: for a big man he moved like snowfall. He stood at the edge of her lamplight with his hands in his pockets, not intruding, not retreating either, occupying exactly the space a colleague might occupy.

"I don't know what you mean."

"When you're working a problem," he said, "you count. Under your breath. You've done it since you were six. You counted your way through the entire crossing today — I could hear you at the rope anchors. Forty-one, forty-two." He tilted his head. "You stopped at sixty-three both times. The same number. You always stop at sixty-three."

The rope lay coiled in her lap, and Marguerite became aware that she was gripping it hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go, finger by finger, deliberately, the way her father had once taught her to let go of anger too: *feel it leave, Tamsin. Don't throw it. Set it down.*

"There is no Tamsin here," she said quietly. "There's a route-finder named Ash, and she's very good, and she doesn't know you. If you keep confusing us, Mr. Yarrow, I'll speak to Vane about reassignment, and you can find your own lines through the crevasse field."

He absorbed that. She watched him absorb it — watched the blow land somewhere behind his sternum and get filed away, unpaid, like everything else he'd ever owed her.

"All right," he said.

"That's it? All right?"

"What would you like it to be?" And now there was something raw under the professionalism, the first crack in the glaze. "You've built a wall with a door in it, Marguerite, and the door only opens outward. I can knock until my hands break. So yes. All right. I'll be your colleague. I'll call you Ash. And I'll watch you cross the ice the way I taught you to cross ice, and I will know, every single day, exactly whose daughter is keeping me alive out here, and I will say nothing about it, because that is apparently what you need." His voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Is that the arrangement?"

The fire popped across the bowl. Somewhere upslope a hare — or the ghost of one — moved through the dwarf birch.

"The arrangement," Marguerite said, "is that you don't know me."

"Yes," he said. "You mentioned." He turned to go, then stopped, and she saw him decide against a dozen sentences and choose, finally, the smallest one. "Sixty-three was the number of steps from our tent to the mess tent at Kluane. The summer you were seven. In case you ever wondered whether I remember anything."

And he was gone into the dark, walking softly, like snowfall, and Marguerite sat alone with her lamp and her rope and discovered that her eyes were wet and that she could not, for the life of her, have said whether it was grief or rage, because at some point in the last twenty years those two things in her had grown together like roots under a sidewalk, lifting and cracking everything built above them.

*He remembers,* she thought. *Of course he remembers. Remembering costs nothing. That's the trick of it.*

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, efficiently, the way you'd wipe grit off a compass, and made the decision she had probably made the moment she'd seen him eating standing up: she was not leaving. Walking away had been the plan — take the map, take the money, deliver the promise, never stand in the same valley as Edmund Yarrow again. But Vane's advance party was already on the moraine, and the site was undefended, and the only person in this expedition who might be turned was the man who knew exactly what he'd sold. She couldn't fight Vane from the river country. She could fight him from inside his own tent line, one rope-length from the man whose conscience, if any remained, was the sharpest tool she had.

Stay close precisely because it hurts. Use the hurt. It was the only honest inheritance he'd ever given her.

* * *

The second day they climbed out of the trees for good, and the world went white and enormous.

The glacier appeared all at once around a shoulder of granite — the Kettle, vast and ridged and faintly blue in its depths, running down from the icefield like a frozen river that had changed its mind halfway. The air thinned and sharpened. Sound carried strangely: a shout a mile off arrived crisp as a whisper at your ear, and the ice itself spoke in random groans and reports, like distant artillery, like a house settling, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Marguerite worked. That was the mercy of the terrain — it demanded all of her, and what it demanded left no room for the man walking behind her. She read the moraine for stable ground, set the pace, called the rests, fixed the rope lengths for tomorrow's crossing of the lower tongue. Priya ranged alongside her collecting samples, chattering happily about striations and debris transport, and Marguerite found, to her surprise, that she liked the geologist enormously — liked the quick generosity of her attention, the way she asked questions to learn rather than to audit. Tomás brought up the rear with the horse train, singing something low and Mexican and mournful to animals who clearly preferred it to silence.

Vane observed it all from the middle of the column, and Marguerite caught him watching her twice — not the way he watched the others, with appetite and calculation, but with a stillness that raised the hair on her arms. As if he were comparing her to something. As if a file existed somewhere with her name in it, spelled correctly.

At the noon halt, Edmund crouched at the edge of the lateral moraine and lifted a stone out of the rubble, and Marguerite watched, despite herself, despite everything, as her father turned it in his fingers and his whole face changed — the guardedness dropping away like a cloth pulled off a birdcage, leaving pure light. Wonder. The thing she had loved him for first, before she had learned to hate him for everything else.

"Look at this," he said, to no one, to everyone, holding up a river-worn cobble that had no business being thirty miles from any river. "Glacial erratic. Carried here from the headwaters. This stone has traveled further than any of us will in our lifetimes, and it did it asleep." He laughed — a short, helpless, delighted sound — and set it back exactly where it had lain, aligned with the marks its weight had pressed into the gravel. "God, I love this country."

Priya grinned at him. Tomás, passing with a pannier, said, "You are all crazy, you rock people," with deep affection.

And Marguerite stood six feet away with her heart in ruins, because that laugh was the sound of every good year of her childhood, and the man making it had still signed whatever he'd signed, and had still stayed gone, and wonder, she reminded herself, wonder was not the same as goodness. She had learned that lesson at fourteen, at a funeral, alone. She could learn it again.

But she picked up her pack, and found that her hands had counted — *sixty-one, sixty-two, sixty-three* — before she noticed, and stopped.

* * *

On the third evening, at the last camp before the tongue, Edmund found her checking anchors by lamplight and did not pretend it was coincidence.

"The eastern moraine," he said.

Marguerite's hands went still on the piton. "What about it?"

"You've rerouted us twice to keep sightlines on it. You check it with binoculars more often than the route itself warrants. You're watching for something." He crouched opposite her, forearms on knees, and in the small circle of lamplight his face was stripped of everything but seriousness. "So am I."

The glacier groaned out in the dark, a long tectonic complaint, and Marguerite weighed the moment — the risk, the opening, the terrible temptation of it. He was offering her something. She could feel the shape of it through the words, the way you feel a handhold through a glove: *I know something is wrong, and I am telling you I know.*

"Mr. Yarrow," she said carefully, "if you have concerns about route security, raise them with Vane."

"I did." A pause. "He told me the advance party is a mineral survey. Four men, two weeks of rations, and a satellite phone, walking a line that has no mineral signature, three days before any survey schedule, toward a location that appears in exactly one document I've ever seen." His grey eyes held hers, steady and bleak. "A document drawn by a man named Aldous Crane, who was my partner once, twenty-five years ago, before either of us had anything left to lose."

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere behind them, Tomás laughed at one of Priya's jokes, warm and ordinary, a world away.

"He gave the map to someone," Edmund said quietly. "Before he died. I don't know who. But whoever it was has been carrying it toward my escarpment for two weeks, and I would very much like to know if she's sitting in front of me."

Wind moved through the camp, snapping canvas, and Marguerite looked at her father — at the man who had taught her stone, who remembered sixty-three steps, who had just said *my escarpment* as if the site already belonged to grief — and understood that the wall with the door in it had just developed a crack from the other side.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we cross the tongue. Get some sleep, Mr. Yarrow. You'll want your strength."

He held her gaze a moment longer, and then he nodded, slowly, accepting the deferral the way he accepted everything — filing it, unpaid — and rose and walked back toward the tents through the blue dark, and Marguerite sat alone with the anchors and the wind and the knowledge that the distance between them was no longer measured in miles, and closing.
