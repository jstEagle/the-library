## 5. Corliss Vane's Ledger

The manifests arrived on the third morning, folded into a weatherproof pouch and dropped by the supply runner at Vane's tent without so much as a knock. Priya Nair saw it happen because she was awake — she was always awake by five-thirty, a habit of fieldwork that had outlived every reason for it — and because the runner's dog paused to drink from the melt-pool beside her tent, and where the dog went, she watched.

She told herself it was none of her business. She was the expedition geologist, hired to read ice and stone, not ledgers. Her contract said nothing about curiosity. But curiosity was the whole reason she was here, three hundred kilometres past the last paved road, sleeping on a foam pad that smelled of other people's sweat, eating Tomás Reyes's excellent bread and pretending she hadn't noticed that the bread was the only honest thing about this operation.

So when Marguerite — *Ruth*, the woman called herself, Ruth Ashford, though Priya had already begun privately doubting even that — came off the morning watch and found Priya sitting cross-legged in the lee of the cook tent with a mug of tea going cold in her hands, Priya made a decision she would later understand was the first true decision of her life on this mountain.

"Ruth," she said. "Walk with me."

* * *

They walked the perimeter, which was less a perimeter than a suggestion: a loop of flagging tape strung between boulders above the scree line, close enough to camp to be safe, far enough to feel like privacy. The Kettle Glacier lay below them like a river that had been told to hold still and had obeyed for ten thousand years, its surface crazed with blue fractures, breathing cold up the slope in slow exhalations. Somewhere under all that white, water was moving. It always was. That was the first thing Priya had learned about glaciers and the last thing she'd forgotten: nothing frozen is ever truly stopped.

"I've been reading the manifests," Priya said, when they were out of earshot of Tomás's radio.

Marguerite glanced at her sideways. Up here her face was always slightly wind-scoured, always slightly guarded, like a house with the lights on behind drawn curtains. "That seems above your pay grade."

"It is. Everything about this expedition is above my pay grade. That's rather my point." Priya turned the mug in her hands. "I did my doctorate on glacial transport dynamics. I've worked four commercial surveys and two academic digs. I know what an archaeological expedition looks like on paper. I know what it weighs."

"And?"

"And this one weighs wrong." She had rehearsed this on her walk down from the supply tent, ordered her evidence the way she ordered stratigraphy, oldest certainty first. Now she laid it out. "A legitimate dig at this scale — twelve people, six weeks, helicopter-supported — needs rope, anchors, tents, sample bags, sieves, trowels, brushes, conservation foam, field lab equipment, satellite uplink time. What came in on yesterday's drop includes two hundred metres of cargo netting rated at two tonnes. Three pallets of industrial ratchet straps. A crate marked *agricultural* that, if you stand downwind when it's opened, smells unmistakably of chain grease. And forty drums of aviation fuel cached at the lower moraine — enough, at our stated flight plan, to run the helicopter for eleven weeks. We're contracted for five."

Marguerite had stopped walking. The wind moved her hair across her face and she didn't push it back, and Priya understood suddenly that she wasn't surprised. Not by the numbers. By being asked about them.

"You knew," Priya said.

"I noticed things." Marguerite's voice was flat, careful. "Route-finders notice things. It's the job."

"What kind of things?"

"Vane's personal kit came in on the first lift. Before the science equipment. Four hard cases he wouldn't let anyone else touch." A pause, measured out like rope through a belay device. "He carries a sidearm on a heritage survey in a park where firearms are prohibited. He asked me twice how many days' travel between here and the escarpment approach, and both times he asked it like a man checking a lock, not planning a hike."

The glacier breathed below them. Priya felt the cold come up through her boots, patient as arithmetic.

"So you've been counting too," she said.

"Someone has to."

* * *

They walked on, and Priya let the silence do its work, because she had learned long ago that people tell you the truth in the gaps between questions, if you can bear to leave the gaps open. The sun climbed. Light came off the icefield in sheets, so bright it made sound seem far away, and the flagging tape ahead of them snapped and fluttered like small orange birds trying to decide whether to fly.

Priya thought about how she had come to be here at all. Two years of grant rejections; a department that wanted her teaching load doubled and her research buried; then Corliss Vane at a conference in Vancouver, silver-haired and warm-handed, saying *the Kettle escarpment, Dr. Nair, untouched pre-contact stratigraphy, full funding, full autonomy, publish whatever you find.* She had known even then that money like that came with gravity. She had told herself gravity was just physics. She had needed the work so badly that she had mistaken being used for being trusted.

"The thing that bothers me most," she said finally, "isn't the fuel or the chains. It's the crates."

"Crates?"

"Custom-built. Foam-lined. Dimensional lumber, museum grade. You build crates like that when you already know what you're putting in them — their size, their weight, their fragility." She looked at Marguerite. "Nobody builds conservation packaging for artifacts they haven't found yet. Unless someone has already told them exactly what's up there. Dimensions and all."

Marguerite stood very still. Below them the glacier ticked and settled, the sound carrying up the slope like something shifting in its sleep. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the flatness, and underneath it something that sounded almost like grief.

"There's a survey," she said. "Old one. Pre-war era, never published properly. If someone got hold of the field notes — the real ones, with measurements — they'd know what's carved into that ledge before they ever saw it."

"Do you know who has those notes?"

Another silence. Longer. Priya watched Marguerite look north along the valley, toward the escarpment, invisible from here behind the shoulder of the mountain but present anyway, the way a wound is present through clothing. When she answered, she chose each word like a foothold.

"I know whose handwriting is on them."

It was not an answer, and it was entirely an answer, and Priya — who had spent her career learning to read what rock says beneath what it shows — decided not to press. Not yet. Alliances are built the way cairns are built: one stone at a time, each one chosen, none of them asked to carry more than it can.

"Watch rotation tonight," Priya said instead. "You and me, twenty-two hundred to oh-two hundred. Cold work."

"Cold work," Marguerite agreed. And then, so quietly Priya nearly missed it under the wind: "Bring the manifests. The copies you made."

Priya smiled despite herself. "I knew you'd know about the copies."

"I told you. Route-finders notice things."

* * *

Night came down the valley like water filling a basin. By twenty-two hundred the temperature had fallen to something that made breath a small private weather system, and the sky had cleared to that high alpine blackness where the stars don't twinkle so much as burn, fixed and merciless, and the glacier below gave off its own faint grey light, the way snow does on the edge of moonrise.

They took the watch from Tomás, who handed over the thermos with the solemnity of a man passing a relic and whispered, "There's ginger in it. My mother's cure for everything including heartbreak and bad employers," and shuffled off to his tent already talking in his sleep, or perhaps to his sister, three thousand kilometres south, in the low murmuring way he talked to people who weren't there.

For the first hour they said little. Watch duty has its own liturgy: walk the line, check the tape, listen to the mountain. The glacier spoke in its night voice — cracks like distant doors, groans like a ship in old water — and once, far down-valley, something calved or collapsed, a boom that rolled up to them flattened into almost-silence, and both women stopped and listened until the echo finished dying.

"He'll move early," Marguerite said at last. They were standing at the northern end of the line, looking toward the escarpment's dark mass. "Vane. Before any survey schedule justifies it. The advance party won't be scientists."

"How do you know there *is* an advance party?"

"Because of the fuel. Eleven weeks of fuel means flights that aren't on any manifest we've seen. Because of the crates. And because—" Marguerite stopped. Priya waited. Out here, in the dark, waiting cost nothing.

"Because he asked me about the eastern moraine," Marguerite said finally. "Not the main approach. The eastern moraine. Which is longer, steeper, and worse in every measurable way — unless you want to arrive somewhere unobserved."

Priya felt the pieces settle into place with the ugly click of a mechanism working as designed. "Armed men, days ahead of us, taking the route nobody honest would take."

"That's my read."

"And the site itself? Whoever put those carvings there — their descendants are still in the valley settlements. If Vane strips that ledge—"

"He won't strip it." Marguerite's voice had gone very quiet, which Priya was learning meant very dangerous. "Stripping implies it was ever public. He'll take it the way a thief takes a room — quietly, completely, and before anyone knows the room existed. Private sale. The site will exist afterward only in the inventory of people who will never publish, never share, never admit it was found. Five thousand years of somebody's history, gone into a vault in Geneva." She turned. Even in the dark Priya could see the shape of her expression, the way you can see the shape of a mountain at night. "I made a promise about that place. To a dying man. I intend to keep it."

"Aldous Crane," Priya said.

Marguerite went still. "How do you know that name?"

"Because Edmund Yarrow talks in his sleep." Priya wrapped both hands around the thermos cup. "Or rather, he talks when he thinks no one's listening, which is worse. Third day out, at the crossing, he stood next to me looking at the ice and said, 'Crane always said the Kettle would outlive us both.' He didn't explain. He saw me hear it, and he walked away." She paused. "Who was Crane to him?"

For a long moment Marguerite said nothing, and the glacier filled the silence with its slow grinding, the sound of enormous patience.

"I think," she said at last, "he was the man who kept the map."

* * *

They sat after that, backs against the boulder, sharing the thermos in shifts, and the alliance between them stopped being a word and became a fact — quiet, structural, load-bearing. Priya laid out what she could offer: her photographs, her instruments, her habit of writing everything down in a notebook Vane had laughed at and dismissed as academic fussiness. Marguerite offered what she could offer: the terrain, the timing, and a competence with rope and anchor that Priya had watched save Tomás's life on the crossing and had not yet found words adequate to describe.

"We document everything," Marguerite said. "Every crate, every flight, every man on the eastern moraine. If it comes to a fight, we fight with records. Paper and photographs. That's how you beat a man like Vane — not by out-muscling him, but by making the world look at what he's done."

"And if the world doesn't look?"

Marguerite was silent for a moment. Down on the ice, something shifted — a serac leaning further into its long surrender — and the sound came up to them like a held breath being released.

"Then we look," she said. "However long it takes."

Priya nodded slowly. She thought of her rejected grants, her buried research, the warm handshake in Vancouver, and understood that she had crossed some line in the last hour and could not locate exactly where. It didn't matter. On a glacier, the crevasse doesn't announce itself; you simply look back and see the bridge you crossed is gone.

"One more thing," she said, as the watch neared its end and the east showed the faintest bruise of coming light. "Yarrow. Whatever history you have with him — and I'm not asking what it is — he's Vane's man. However reluctantly. If it comes to choosing sides on that ledge, can we count you?"

Marguerite looked north again, toward the hidden escarpment, and Priya watched her weigh the question with the care of someone placing protection on rotten rock.

"When the time comes," Marguerite said, "you'll know which side I'm on. I promise you that much."

It was not quite the answer Priya wanted. But before she could press, Tomás's radio crackled from the cook tent — the night-frequency squawk that meant the runner's dog was moving, or a message had come in, or the world had simply decided to interrupt. Then footsteps. Then Tomás himself, half-dressed, holding the handset out into the dark with an expression Priya had never seen on his easy face.

"For the geologist," he said. "Priority traffic. From the lower cache."

Priya took the handset. The voice on the other end was thin with distance and static, a supply pilot reporting routine status — fuel counts, weather — and then, appended almost as an afterthought, the sentence that turned the night cold in a new way:

*"Be advised — four unidentified persons observed moving up the eastern moraine at first light yesterday. Armed. No manifest match. Over."*

Priya lowered the handset. Beside her, Marguerite had gone absolutely still, and when she spoke, her breath hung in the air between them like something written and immediately erased.

"They're already moving," she said. "We're three days behind, and the melt is early."
