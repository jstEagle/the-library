## 6. The Kettle Crossing

The glacier announced itself before we saw it — a cold breath rolling down the valley like something exhaling from the throat of the world. By midmorning we stood at its margin, and I understood why the old maps called it the Kettle: a great grey cauldron of ice, dented and scarred, its surface pocked with meltwater pools that caught the sky and held it hostage.

"Lower tongue," Edmund said, crouched at the edge with his field notebook open, sketching the route in quick strokes. He didn't look at me when he spoke. He hadn't looked at me directly since the trail confrontation three days ago, and some stubborn part of me had begun to prefer it that way. "We cross here, where the ice is compressed against bedrock. Two rope teams. Marguerite leads the second."

Vane stood apart, gloved hands clasped behind him, surveying the glacier the way a man surveys property he intends to own. "How long?" he asked.

"Four hours if the ice holds," I said. "Six if it doesn't."

"And if it doesn't?"

I met his eyes. They were pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had never once been told no by weather or by woman. "Then we turn back and try again tomorrow."

He smiled as though I'd said something charming. "Tomorrow," he repeated, tasting the word. "Very well."

* * *

The first hour was honest work. The lower tongue rose in shallow terraces, each one scalloped with melt channels that sang as we crossed them — water running somewhere beneath the ice, a sound like fingers drumming on hollow wood. I placed wands every fifty meters, orange flags snapping in the wind off the icefield, and behind me came Tomás with the heavy pack, then Priya, then Edmund's team strung out along their own line.

Tomás talked the whole way, because Tomás always talked. He narrated the glacier as though introducing us to an old friend of dubious character. "She's got a temper, this one. You can hear her thinking." He tapped his boot on the ice. "All that water underneath, going about her business. She's not asleep, she's just quiet."

"You've crossed glaciers before?" Priya asked.

"Lots. In Patagonia, with my uncle. He taught me everything I know about ice and half of what I know about cooking, which is why I'm the cook and not the guide." He grinned at her over his shoulder. "My uncle also said the glacier always takes a toll. You pay going across, one way or another."

"What kind of toll?" Priya asked.

"That's the thing. You don't get to choose."

I heard him say it and felt the words settle into my chest like a stone dropped into a well. I kept walking.

By the second hour we were on the blue ice proper, and the glacier showed us its teeth. Crevasses opened on either side of our route like mouths mid-sentence, some narrow enough to jump, others wide enough that their far walls were lost in shadow. The light came down into them in shafts and died there. Once, crossing a snow bridge that flexed under my boots, I heard the ice groan — a long, low note, like a ship settling — and I stopped, tested the bridge with my axe, and moved us thirty meters east to where the compression ridges ran cleaner.

Edmund watched me do it. I felt his attention the way you feel sun through cloud — diffuse, but there. When I glanced back he was looking at the bridge, not at me, making a note. But I knew what he'd seen: the way I'd read the sag in the snow, the way I'd counted the crevasse spacing before committing. Techniques with a familiar accent. Techniques he'd taught a girl of nine on the gravel bars of the Skeena, drawing cross-sections in the dirt with a stick while her mother called them both in for supper.

I put it out of my mind and kept placing wands.

* * *

The trouble came at the hour mark past noon, on a broad bench of ice below the serac field, where the glacier bent around a rib of exposed rock and the meltwater pooled in turquoise lenses. It was beautiful country, which should have warned me. Beautiful country on a glacier is usually country under tension.

I probed ahead with my axe, found good ice, and waved the second team forward. Tomás came across the bench with his usual rolling gait, pack swaying, mid-story about his uncle and a mule named Consuelo who had opinions about bridges. Priya followed. Behind them, Edmund's team held at the last wand, waiting for my signal.

Tomás was ten meters out when the glacier cleared its throat.

It wasn't loud. That was the horror of it — no crack, no boom, just a soft structural sigh beneath our feet, the sound of a snow bridge over a hidden void deciding it had carried enough. I saw the surface dimple. I saw Tomás feel it through his boots, saw his head come up, his story dying in his mouth.

"Down!" I shouted. "Get flat—"

The bridge went. Not all at once — it slumped, the way a roof slumps, and Tomás went with it, sliding into a hole that opened beneath him like a mouth. His axe skated off blue ice. His arms windmilled once, catching the lip, and then his body swung into the crevasse and the rope snapped taut and the whole world narrowed to a single screaming line.

He hung at maybe eight meters, spinning slowly, his boot tips knocking against the far wall. The rope ran from his harness up over the broken lip of the bridge, and the lip was cutting it. I could see the sheath fraying even from where I stood, white filaments lifting off the core like thread from a wound.

Every voice in my head spoke at once. My instructor at the guiding school: *the lip will kill your rope before the fall kills your man.* My father's voice, older, from a kitchen table covered in topo sheets: *never trust the edge you fell over; build your anchor where the ground is honest.*

I dropped to my knees two body-lengths back from the lip, where the ice was old, compressed, striped blue and white like butcher's marble. Two screws. That was the arithmetic. I drove the first ice screw in at an angle, my breath coming hard, my wrist screaming with the effort, and clipped the sling. The second screw went in lower, opposite angle, load-sharing. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Behind me someone was shouting Tomás's name and Tomás was shouting back that he was fine, he was fine, and nobody was fine, but his voice was steady and that mattered.

"Marguerite!" Edmund's voice, closer than it should have been. He'd left his team and run forward along the rope. "Back up the anchor — I'll take the load—"

"I have it!" The words came out of me like something fired rather than spoken. "Slack off your line — you'll pull the lip down!"

And here was the moment I would think about later, in the dark, in the weeks after: he obeyed. Edmund Yarrow, who had not taken instruction from anyone in twenty years, backed off three steps and belayed me without another word.

I built the haul system with hands that knew the work better than my mind did — prusik, pulley, progress-capture, the mechanical advantage stacking 3-to-1 until the rope ran through my hands like something alive. "Tomás! Feet on the wall! Walk yourself up as I take slack!"

"I'm walking," he called up, and there was a laugh in it, ragged but real. "Consuelo would be proud."

I hauled. The rope burned through my gloves. Eight meters became six, six became four, and then his hands came over the lip and Priya was there, dragging him onto solid ice, and the three of us collapsed in a heap of packs and axes and shaking breath.

For a long moment nobody said anything. The glacier sang its water-song beneath us, indifferent. Somewhere above, a serac shed a block of ice with a sound like a rifle shot, and nobody flinched, because after almost losing a man, a falling serac seemed a small and reasonable thing.

Tomás sat up first. He looked at the hole he'd nearly gone into — a slot of deep blue narrowing downward into black — and then at me, and something passed over his face that I couldn't read at the time and understood only much later. Gratitude, yes. But also recognition. The look of a man filing away a fact about someone he intended to keep.

"My uncle," he said finally, "would say the glacier took her toll today."

"We paid it," Priya said. Her voice wasn't quite level. She was looking at my hands, at the raw red stripes the rope had printed across my palms through the gloves. "Marguerite. That anchor. That was — I've seen guides freeze on less."

"It's just work," I said.

"It isn't, though." She said it gently, but she said it. "That was fast. That was *practiced*. Where did you learn to build a load-sharing anchor in fifteen seconds flat?"

"A girl learns things," I said, "growing up where I grew up."

Across the broken bridge, Edmund was coiling the damaged rope with precise, economical movements. He hadn't joined the heap of gratitude. He stood slightly apart, the way he always stood, and when he finished the coil he set it down and looked at me across the distance — really looked, for the first time since the trail — and whatever was in his face, he made no attempt to hide it from me.

It wasn't pride exactly. Pride would have been simpler. It was more like a man watching a door he'd painted shut swing open on its own, and finding the room behind it unchanged.

He crossed the ice toward us, stepping carefully around the hole, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close I could see the windburn on his face and the grey coming into his beard, and how tired he looked, tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix.

"The second screw," he said. "You set it opposing. Most people forget."

"You taught me," I said. "On the gravel bar. I was nine. You used tent pegs and a log."

Something moved through his expression like weather. "You remember that."

"I remember everything," I said. "That's the problem."

He held my gaze a moment longer, and I braced for the argument, the retreat, the cold professional mask sliding back into place. Instead he nodded — a small, slow nod, the nod of a man conceding a point in a debate he'd been having with himself for years — and turned to Tomás.

"Can you walk?"

"I can dance," Tomás said, getting up, testing his legs, wincing at nothing worse than bruises. "Though perhaps not today."

"Then we move to the far bench and camp early," Edmund said. "Nobody crosses the upper section tired." He looked at me again, and this time the words came slower, dragged up from somewhere deep. "Good work. Marguerite." A pause, the name handled like something fragile. "Your mother would have—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Good work."

He walked back to his team. I stood there with my raw hands and my hammering heart, and Priya touched my shoulder lightly, saying nothing, because she was learning already what needed saying and what didn't.

* * *

We camped on the far bench with the serac field hanging above us like a frozen wave deciding whether to break. Tomás, true to form, cooked anyway — a thick barley soup with too much pepper, served with the ceremony of a man who had cheated death and intended to celebrate it nightly. He told the story of the fall twice, improving it each time, giving himself a longer slide and a more dramatic rescue, until Priya threw a crust of bread at him and he caught it in his mouth and bowed.

But later, when the light went copper on the ice and the others drifted toward their tents, Tomás came and sat beside me on a supply crate at the camp's edge. For a while he said nothing, which was unlike him, and I learned that his silences, rare as they were, were worth waiting through.

"You had me," he said at last. "The whole time. From the first screw to the last pull, you never wavered. I watched your face when they pulled me out." He turned the tin cup in his hands. "You weren't scared for me. You were angry."

"I was angry at the ice."

"No." He shook his head. "You were angry at something older than the ice. I know that anger. I carry it myself, for my sister, for the doctors who cost more than I make in five years." He looked at me sideways. "Whatever name you're using, whoever you actually are — you saved my life today, and that makes you mine. My people. So one day, when you're ready to tell me the truth, I'll be ready to hear it. No rush. No questions."

He clinked his cup against mine and went off to his stove, humming.

I sat a long time after dark, watching the seracs glow faintly under the stars, listening to the glacier breathe beneath us. My palms throbbed. My father's voice was still in my ears — *you set it opposing; most people forget* — and beneath the anger, beneath twenty years of abandonment, something had shifted, some plate grinding against another plate deep underground, the way plates do before anything changes on the surface.

Across the camp, I saw Edmund's tent lamp burning late. He was awake too. Writing, probably. Or staring at the ceiling of his tent the way I stared at mine, doing arithmetic neither of us wanted to finish.

Because I had seen something today that I hadn't let myself see before. When Vane's men had checked the route that afternoon — the four of them, moving ahead along the eastern moraine with ropes coiled wrong and rifles slung like tools instead of weapons — Edmund had watched them go with an expression I recognized now, because I'd worn it myself for years in mirrors.

Not surprise. Calculation. A man measuring a distance he'd already agreed to walk.

And I lay in the dark wondering, with the glacier singing under me, whether the man who'd taught me to build anchors that hold had built his whole life on ice that was about to give — and whether, when it did, he would reach for my rope, or cut it.
