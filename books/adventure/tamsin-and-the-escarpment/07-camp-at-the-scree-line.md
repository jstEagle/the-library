## 7. Camp at the Scree Line

The scree line was where the mountain stopped pretending to be gentle.

Tomás Reyes felt it in his knees first, then his shoulders, then somewhere behind his sternum — that low, grinding ache of a body asked to carry more than it had been built for. Below them, the valley unspooled green and forgiving, the river a bright thread stitched through it. Above, the escarpment waited under its rind of ice, gray and patient as an unpaid debt. Between the two, the camp: six tents pitched on a shelf of broken stone where nothing grew and nothing forgave, but at least nothing slid.

He'd cooked at altitude before. Three seasons on the coast range, one bad winter in the north passes he did not talk about. But this was different. Here the wind came off the glacier like something with intent, and by four in the afternoon it had already begun to strip the heat from the rocks, so that the whole shelf ticked and settled around them like a cooling stove. Tomás worked the camp stove with his sleeves rolled up despite the cold, because a cook who flinched at weather fed people food that tasted of fear, and fear was the one ingredient he refused to serve.

Tonight it was lentils and chorizo, flatbread blistered on the skillet's lid, and the last of the fresh peppers sliced thin over everything — a small extravagance he'd hoarded from the resupply cache like a miser. The smell went out across the scree like an invitation. He watched them come to it the way moths come to anything honest: Priya first, folding herself down onto a rock with her notebook still in hand; then the survey crew, loud and grateful; then Vane, who ate standing, charming, watchful, wrong — Tomás had decided that about the man on the second day and seen no reason since to revise it. A man who thanked you for every meal while counting your spoons.

Last came the two Yarrows, though only one of them carried the name.

Edmund took his plate with a murmured thanks and withdrew to the far edge of the firelight, the way he always did, a man practicing exile even in company. And Marguerite — *Wren*, she was calling herself, Wren Ashford, her mother's surname worn like a borrowed coat — sat down cross-legged near the flames with her plate balanced on her knee, close enough to warm and far enough to leave.

Tomás watched her the way he watched all his people, which was to say too closely, and with love he hadn't yet been given permission to feel. There was something about the guide. She moved like water finding its level — economical, unhurried, never once caught off balance. She read terrain the way he read a simmering pot, by small signs, by smell, by the sound things made just before they changed. And she was carrying something. He'd fed enough hungry people to know the difference between hunger for food and hunger for absolution, and Marguerite Ashford was starving for the second thing, and doing her best to pretend she didn't know it.

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, ice shifted in the dark with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"So," said Danny Okafor from the survey crew, tipping his cup toward the flames, "someone tell a story before we all start hearing the mountain talk back. It gets into your head otherwise. First week up here, I swear I heard my mother calling me for dinner."

"Your mother called you for dinner?" said Ravi, grinning. "At eight thousand feet?"

"She has reach."

Laughter went around the circle, easy and bright, and Tomás let it wash over him while he scraped the skillet. This was the part of expedition life nobody put in the brochures — this hour after the plates were clean when strangers became, if not friends, then at least fellow prisoners of the same beautiful, indifferent place. He had learned on his first season that the fire was not for warmth. The fire was for this. For making a circle of light small enough that the dark couldn't get a word in.

"Tell us why you're here, Tomás," Priya said. She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. She had a way of asking questions that looked casual and landed deep, like stones dropped down a well. "You've heard all ours. Okafor's mother. Ravi's disastrous love life. Even the boss gave us the mining-magnate-who-loves-science speech." She glanced toward Vane's tent, and something flickered across her face — quick, quickly gone. "But you. You cook like a man feeding a purpose."

"Flattery," Tomás said, "gets you seconds."

"I'm serious."

"He knows you're serious," Okafor said. "Everyone knows. Come on, Reyes. Story tax. You've been dodging it since the Kettle Crossing."

*Since the Kettle Crossing.* Tomás kept his eyes on the coals. Four days ago the glacier's lower tongue had tried to swallow their cook whole, and it would have, too — the snow bridge had gone soft and blue and treacherous beneath him, and the world had tilted, and there had been one long white instant in which Tomás Reyes understood with total clarity that he was going to die in a hole in the ice and his sister would never know what happened to the money. Then a rope had snapped taut, an anchor had held, and a voice he now thought about more than he should have had cut through the roar: *Down! Weight off! Now!* Marguerite had hauled him out with hands that didn't shake until afterward, and Edmund Yarrow had stood at the crevasse lip watching his daughter work with an expression Tomás still couldn't file anywhere — pride and grief wearing each other's clothes.

He owed her. Not just the rope. The afterward, too — the mug of tea she'd pressed into his shaking hands without a word, as if she knew exactly what the body does when it comes back from the edge and doesn't want to be talked at about it.

"Fine," he said. "Story tax. But someone else goes next time, and it has to be true."

"Deal," said three voices at once.

He set down the skillet. He looked into the fire for a while, gathering it, the way you gather dough — patiently, letting it come together on its own terms.

"My sister's name is Lucía," he began. "She's nineteen. She wants to be a doctor, which is a joke in our family, because the only medicine we could ever afford was my grandmother's — rum and prayer, mostly prayer." A ripple of laughter, soft. "Two years ago she started getting sick. Tired all the time, bruising like a peach. My mother thought it was nerves. My mother thinks everything is nerves." His voice stayed level; he'd practiced this part. "It wasn't nerves. It was her blood. Something in the marrow, they said. The word they used sounded like a country I couldn't afford to visit."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke. Above them the wind worried at the tents, patient as a customs officer.

"There's a treatment. Of course there's a treatment — there's always a treatment, that's how they get you. It costs more than everything my family has earned in three generations, combined, doubled, and then some. So I did the math every poor man does. What can I sell? I sold the truck. I sold my grandmother's ring, may she forgive me. And then I found out what expedition cooks with high-altitude certification make on a private contract, and I stopped doing math and started packing."

"That's—" Okafor started.

"Not finished," Tomás said, holding up a spoon like a conductor. "The story tax includes the embarrassing part. When I signed on, I told myself: one season. In and out. Hard cash, no questions. And then the Kettle Crossing happened, and I was hanging in a hole in the world thinking about Lucía's face, and do you know what I thought?" He shook his head at himself. "I thought, *at least the insurance pays out double for death on contract.* That's what I thought. In the actual moment. My sister's surgery priced against my own body, and me doing the arithmetic while the ice chewed on my boots." He looked up, and his grin was crooked and real. "So yes. I came for money. I stay because the food here needs me, and because—" he gestured around the circle with the spoon, taking them all in, "—because apparently I collect people. It's a sickness. Ask anyone."

Silence, the good kind, the kind that means something landed where it was meant to land. Priya reached over and squeezed his wrist once, hard, saying nothing, which from Priya was a paragraph. Ravi muttered something about needing a minute and inspected the sky very closely.

And Marguerite — Wren — was looking at him with an expression he'd begun to catalog: the stillness she wore like armor, except right now the armor had a crack in it, and through the crack he could see something raw and young and drowning. As if his story about Lucía had reached down inside her and pulled on a rope attached to something heavy.

"You'd sell anything for family," she said quietly. It wasn't quite a question.

"To the last spoon," Tomás said. "Why do you think I guard the kitchen?"

That got a laugh, but hers came out strange, half-strangled, and she turned her face to the fire, and Tomás saw her jaw work once, twice, like a woman chewing back words that wanted out.

Later, when the survey crew had stumbled off to their tents and the fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, it was the three of them left — Tomás washing the last skillet with water heated on the coals, Priya sitting wrapped in her bag like a skeptical caterpillar, and Marguerite across the flames, turning her cup slowly in both hands without drinking from it.

"The stars are different up here," Priya said, to no one, to everyone. "Closer. Like they're checking on us."

"They're the same stars," Tomás said. "You're just higher up. Less between you and them."

"That's what worries me. Less between."

Marguerite laughed — a real one this time, short and surprised out of her. "You two are going to make me soft."

"We can only hope," said Priya.

Tomás set the skillet upside down on a rock to dry and came back to the fire, and because the hour was late and the coals were kind and secrets keep worse in silence than in company, he said, "Your turn, Wren."

Her cup stopped turning.

"Turn's passed," she said.

"Story tax doesn't expire. It accrues interest." He sat down across from her, elbows on knees. "One story. Doesn't have to be the big one. Just a true one. You've heard all of ours. Fair's fair."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the fire filled the silence with small sounds — the tick of cooling stone, the whisper of ash settling, the wind combing the scree above camp. Tomás had spent a lifetime reading kitchens and the people in them, and he knew every species of silence, and this one was the dangerous kind: not empty, but full. Overfull. A pot about to go over the boil.

"Why I'm here," she said finally, and her voice had changed — lower, careful, each word placed like a foot on rotten ice. "All right. Why I'm here."

Priya had gone very still inside her bag. She was watching Marguerite the way she watched data that didn't match the model.

"I had a father who taught me stone," Marguerite said. "Not masonry — reading it. How it lies and how it tells the truth. Where water goes, where ground holds, where it gives way. I was good at it. Better than good. It was the only language we ever really spoke, him and me." Her thumb traced the rim of her cup, around and around. "Then he chose the work over everything else. Over us. And I swore I'd never follow him up a mountain again." She looked up, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "And here I am. On a mountain. Following him."

The words dropped into the circle like stones into still water. Tomás felt Priya go rigid beside him.

"Following *him*?" Priya said carefully. "Who's—"

"A man I used to know," Marguerite said, too fast, too smooth, and Tomás watched the door swing shut behind her eyes even as it opened. "A long time ago. Before any of you. It's an old story and it doesn't have an ending yet, so I'll spare you the middle."

She smiled. It was a good smile. It was also, Tomás thought, the loneliest thing he'd seen in weeks, and he had spent those weeks looking at glaciers.

"That's not a story," he said gently. "That's a receipt. We gave you the whole till. You gave us a receipt."

"Tomás—"

"Lucía's marrow," he said. "I told you the word the doctors used. I told you about the ring. You don't owe us the big one, Wren. But you owe yourself better than that receipt, or it'll eat you before the mountain gets a chance. I've seen it happen. The mountain loves a secret. Keeps it fed. Lets it grow."

Something moved behind her face — he watched it happen, watched the truth rise in her like water in a locked well, watched her almost, *almost* open her mouth and say it. Say *Yarrow*. Say *he's my father*. Say *I'm here under a name that isn't mine and every one of you trusts a ghost*. Her lips parted. The fire cracked. High above, the ice groaned along the escarpment, long and slow, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"I came here," she said at last, each word laid down with terrible care, "to finish something. For someone who couldn't. And if I do it right, none of you will ever need to know more than that."

Priya exhaled slowly through her nose. "That," she said, "is the most honest dishonest answer I've ever heard."

"It's the only kind I have."

Tomás nodded slowly. He looked at her across the coals — this woman who had pulled him out of the ice with hands that didn't shake, who ate last and slept least and checked everyone's ropes without being asked, who was walking around with a name like a knife hidden in her boot — and he made a decision he didn't fully understand yet and wouldn't examine for a long time.

"All right," he said. "Receipt accepted. For now." He pointed the spoon at her, mock-stern. "But when you're ready to pay the full tax, the kitchen takes payment in truth, and the exchange rate is generous."

"And if I never pay?" she said.

"Then I'll feed you anyway," Tomás said. "That's the sickness I told you about. I collect people. Even the ones traveling under false pretenses." He held her gaze, mild and steady, and saw her understand exactly how much he understood, and saw her decide — visibly, the shoulders dropping a quarter inch — to let it stand.

Priya snorted softly. "God help us all. He's adopted her."

"Somebody had to," Tomás said. "She eats like a heron."

Marguerite laughed — really laughed, helpless and surprised, the sound cracking open the night — and for a moment the circle held: three liars and a fire and ten thousand tons of ice listening overhead, warm as anything.

*

She turned in early, and Priya followed not long after, pausing at her tent flap to look back at Tomás with an expression he couldn't read — a question forming behind her eyes, some suspicion taking shape in the dark of her mind like a photograph developing. He'd ask her tomorrow. Tonight the coals needed banking and the bread needed covering against the frost.

He worked alone in the red glow, humming, content in the way that comes only after a hard day honestly spent among people you'd take a rope for. Above the camp the escarpment stood black against the stars, and somewhere up there, in the dark, whatever Marguerite had come to finish was waiting — and whatever Corliss Vane had really come for was waiting too, patient as the ice, counting its own spoons.

Tomás banked the fire, and the last coal pulsed once, like a heartbeat deciding whether to keep going.

He didn't see the figure detach itself from the shadow of the supply tent and move away eastward, toward the moraine, quiet as falling snow.
