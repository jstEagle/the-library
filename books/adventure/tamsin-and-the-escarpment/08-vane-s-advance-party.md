## 8. Vane's Advance Party

Priya Nair had learned to read a man by his paperwork.

It was a habit from her first season in the field, when she'd been assigned to audit the logistics of a survey in Ladakh and had discovered, buried in the freight manifests, that the "geological sampling equipment" was actually a portable core drill meant for a mining concern that had no business near a heritage site. Her supervisor had laughed at her for making trouble. The site had been drilled anyway. She had never laughed at paperwork again.

So it was that on the fourth night above the Kettle Crossing, while the others slept off the long pull up the lateral moraine, Priya sat in the supply tent with Vane's manifests spread across her knees and a headlamp turned low enough to read by without bleeding light through the canvas, and let the numbers tell her what they always told her: that Corliss Vane was not funding an archaeological survey.

The expedition's official manifest — the one filed with the territorial permitting office, the one Edmund Yarrow had signed with the careful, old-fashioned hand she'd seen on his published papers — listed rope, tents, dehydrated rations, survey instruments, sample bags, trowels, brushes, photographic plates. A dig. An honest, underfunded, slightly desperate academic dig, the kind Priya had known all her working life, where the budget line for *miscellaneous* covered bandages and pencil sharpeners.

Vane's private manifest — the duplicate set he kept in the green aluminum case chained to the inside of his own tent, which Tomás had been instructed to ferry between camps and which Priya had photographed, page by page, during the chaos of the river crossing two days ago — listed something else entirely.

Cargo netting rated at four tonnes. Aircraft-grade tie-downs. Six collapsible crates lined with closed-cell foam, each crate stenciled with a serial number belonging to a logistics company registered in a jurisdiction whose name Priya had to look up twice because she didn't believe it the first time. A fuel cache manifest for a helicopter — not chartered through any of the regional operators who flew the glacier corridor, but a private machine out of Vancouver, tail number scrubbed from the public registry. And, itemized with the bland precision of a man ordering stationery: four satellite phones, four rifles chambered in .308, and four thousand rounds of ammunition.

*Four*, she thought. Not six. Not eight. Four men, four phones, four guns. A number small enough to move quietly along a moraine and large enough to hold a ledge against anyone who objected.

She sat very still in the cold of the tent, listening to the mountain breathe. Wind came down off the icefield in long exhalations, dragging grit across the nylon overhead like fingers drawn over skin. Somewhere below camp, the glacier groaned — a deep, structural sound, the sound of something enormous shifting its weight in its sleep — and Priya felt the sound travel up through the ground, through her folded legs, into her chest, where it settled beside the dread already growing there.

Because here was the thing about the numbers. Here was the thing she couldn't stop turning over.

The manifests were dated.

The rifle requisition was dated eleven days before the expedition's permits were approved. The helicopter fuel cache was scheduled for placement at a point on the eastern side of the escarpment — coordinates she recognized now, having spent three evenings with Marguerite's maps, as lying within two kilometers of the site itself — *before* the survey route had been finalized. Before Edmund Yarrow had stood up at the pre-departure briefing in Whitehorse and laid out the phased approach with his chalk and his careful voice: reconnaissance first, geophysics second, excavation third, and no team ascending to the upper ledges until the meltwater channels had been mapped and pronounced safe.

Corliss Vane had known where he was going before the archaeology gave him a reason to go there.

Priya pressed her thumbnail against the edge of the photograph she'd taken of the final page — the page with the delivery schedule — until the laminate bit her skin. The advance party's departure date was written there in Vane's own handwriting, or in the handwriting of someone who imitated it well: a date four days ago. Which meant that right now, tonight, while she sat among the sleeping tents with her heart going hard and quiet, four armed men were somewhere ahead of them on the eastern moraine, moving toward the escarpment through country no legitimate survey would enter for another week.

*Days before any honest reason would take you there,* she thought. *You sent your thieves ahead of your alibi.*

Outside, a ptarmigan startled from the scree with a whirr of wings, and Priya flinched — actually flinched, her whole body jerking — and then made herself breathe, made herself fold the photographs back into their oilcloth sleeve, made herself think like the scientist she was instead of the frightened woman she was becoming.

Evidence first. Evidence, then allies, then action. In that order, always in that order, because evidence without allies got you disappeared into a crevasse on a glacier nobody patrolled, and allies without evidence got you called hysterical in front of the man who owned the helicopters and the guns and, apparently, the reputation of Edmund Yarrow.

She killed the headlamp and waited for her eyes to adjust, and then she went looking for Marguerite.

* * *

Marguerite Yarrow — or Marguerite Hale, as the payroll ledger still insisted, though Priya had stopped believing that fiction around the time she'd watched the woman read a glacial moraine the way other people read a familiar street — was on watch at the eastern edge of camp, sitting on a boulder with her back straight and her binoculars resting in her lap, watching nothing so far as Priya could tell. Watching everything, more likely. That was the thing about Marguerite: she had the stillness of a person who had spent years being paid to notice what the wilderness intended to do next.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Marguerite said, without turning.

"So are you."

"I'm on watch."

"I'm on conscience." Priya climbed the boulder's flank and settled beside her, close enough that their shoulders could share warmth, far enough apart that neither of them had to look at the other to talk. That was another thing about Marguerite: she talked better sideways. Priya had noticed it on the trail — how the woman's truths came out at a slant, offered to the horizon rather than to a face, as if direct eye contact were a kind of debt she'd stopped agreeing to pay.

Below them the camp lay in its blue dark: five tents, the cook fire banked to a red memory, Tomás's tarps fluttering their small insomnia. Beyond the camp the glacier descended in frozen catenaries toward the lake, white going grey going black, and beyond the glacier the world simply ended — dropped away into the escarpment's shadow, a wall of rock a thousand meters high holding up the sky.

"The eastern moraine," Priya said. "How long would it take a fit party to reach the escarpment approach from here?"

Marguerite's profile didn't move. But Priya saw her hands go still on the binoculars, the way a person's hands go still when they've understood that a question is not idle.

"Two days, if they know the line," Marguerite said. "Three if they don't and they're careful. Why?"

"And if they don't care about being careful? If they've got satellite comms, modern gear, and no interest in leaving a trace?"

"Then faster." Marguerite turned her head at last. In the starlight her eyes were pale and level and entirely awake. "Priya. What did you find?"

Priya told her.

She told it quietly, in the flat register she used for data, because the flat register was the only container strong enough to hold what she had to say without spilling it everywhere. She described the manifests. The crates. The ammunition. The dates. She watched Marguerite's face as she spoke, and what she saw there was not surprise — that was the terrible part. Marguerite listened the way a person listens to a diagnosis they have already suspected, nodding once at each confirming detail, her jaw tightening not at the revelation but at the confirmation.

"You knew," Priya said. "Some of it."

"I knew Vane wasn't here for scholarship." Marguerite looked back out at the dark. "You don't fund a dig like this out of love. I told myself maybe he wanted naming rights. Maybe he wanted a documentary. People buy vanity in stranger shapes." She exhaled through her nose, a short, humorless sound. "I didn't let myself think about rifles."

"Four men are already on that moraine." Priya nodded east, toward the escarpment, toward whatever slept up there under the snow — carvings, walls, a buried longhouse, a history somebody had gone to great lengths to erase once and that Corliss Vane apparently intended to erase again, this time into private collections. "They left four days ago. By tomorrow night they'll be at the base of the approach. By the day after, they'll be on the ledge."

"Before Edmund's survey schedule puts anyone legitimate within ten kilometers."

"Yes."

Marguerite was silent for a long moment. The wind moved; the tents muttered; somewhere in the ice below, the glacier ticked and settled like a house at night. When Marguerite finally spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its trail-camp casualness, carrying something old and heavy that Priya hadn't heard in it before.

"When I took this job, I made a promise. To a dying man." She said it without looking at Priya, offering it to the escarpment. "He gave me a map. He asked me to carry it up there before the melt sealed the routes. I said yes because he was bleeding out in a trapper's cabin and it cost me nothing to say yes." Her hands flexed once on the binoculars. "It isn't costing nothing anymore."

"Aldous Crane," Priya said.

Marguerite's head came around sharply. "How do you—"

"He was my father's partner. Thirty years ago. Before the disgrace." Priya kept her voice even. "Edmund talks about him sometimes. Or he used to. He talks about very little these days, but Crane is one of the things that gets past the lock." She paused. "You didn't know that either, did you. That the man who gave you the map knew your father."

For a moment Priya thought Marguerite was going to deny it — deny the name, deny the map, deny the whole architecture of lies she'd built her passage onto this expedition with. Priya watched her decide not to. It happened in her shoulders first: a lowering, a setting-down of weight, like a climber unclipping from a pitch she could no longer lead alone.

"No," Marguerite said. "I didn't know. And I don't know what that means yet. Whether it means anything." She looked east again, at the invisible wall of the escarpment. "But I know what it costs if those men get there first. Whatever's up there has been sitting intact for five hundred years because nobody found it. Crane found it. Crane drew the map. And Crane, dying, trusted a stranger to keep it safe." Her voice roughened. "I'm not going to be the stranger who failed him because I was busy protecting a fake surname."

Priya felt something shift in her chest — the click of an alliance forming, that specific gravity that gathers between two people who have decided, independently and almost simultaneously, that the truth matters more than their safety. She had felt it before, exactly twice in her life. Both times it had cost her something. Both times she had never regretted it.

"There's a problem with acting on this," she said. "Several problems. The largest one is that we can't go to Edmund."

Marguerite made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were capable of frostbite. "No. We can't."

"Vane owns him. I don't mean that metaphorically. I've watched them together — the way Edmund waits for Vane's reactions before he commits to a route, a campsite, a word. That's not a financier respecting his academic. That's a man on a leash checking the length of it." Priya hugged her knees. "If we tell Edmund, and Edmund tells Vane — whether from weakness, loyalty, or fear — then we've announced ourselves to the one person on this glacier who can make us vanish."

"And if we don't tell him," Marguerite said slowly, "and we act alone, and it goes wrong, we're two members of a six-person expedition conducting vigilante operations against our own employer on a glacier with no law within two hundred kilometers." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Tomás."

"What about him?"

"Tomás sees everything. He counts cases, he weighs loads, he knows what every pack in this expedition contains because he packed half of them himself. If Vane's advance party carried rifles up the eastern moraine, they didn't carry them through camp — which means the rifles came in with the resupply, which means Tomás either handled them or knows who did." Marguerite lowered her hands. "And Tomás has a sister in Guadalajara whose surgery is being paid for out of this expedition's payroll. He's got the most to lose of any of us, and he's also the bravest person here, and I hate putting those two facts in the same sentence."

Priya looked at her. "You've thought about this already. Before tonight."

"I've thought about everything already," Marguerite said. "It's the only thing I'm good at besides walking." She was quiet a moment, and then, sideways, to the horizon rather than to Priya's face, she added: "My father taught me that too. Thinking ahead. He used to say the field doesn't forgive improvisation — you win in the planning or you lose in the doing." Her mouth twisted. "He taught me nearly everything I use to survive. Funny, the things you inherit from people who leave."

The wind rose a little, worrying the tent fabric, and far below them — so far below that Priya felt it more than heard it — the glacier released some internal tension with a boom that rolled up the valley like distant artillery. Both women went still. Both women looked east.

"They'll calve early this year," Marguerite said. "I can feel it. The meltwater's running loud at night. The seracs on the lower tongue are leaning." She said it the way a doctor says *the prognosis is poor* — not to frighten, but because the truth was the truth and dressing it up helped no one. "When the calving starts, the exit corridor closes. Everything that happens on this mountain has to happen fast, and it has to happen in order, and right now the order is: those four men reach that ledge in two days, and nobody who cares about stopping them knows they exist except us."

"Then we make them exist," Priya said. "Carefully. Tonight, I'll finish cataloguing the manifests — every date, every serial number, photographed twice and hidden in two places. Tomorrow I'll find a way to ask Tomás about the resupply without asking. And tomorrow night—" She hesitated, because the next sentence was the one that crossed the line from observing to conspiring, and she wanted, just for a moment, to feel the weight of it before she picked it up. "Tomorrow night, you and I need to know exactly where that approach route begins, and how long it would take two people moving fast and quiet to beat four people moving loaded."

Marguerite turned and looked at her fully then, and in the starlight her expression was not gratitude, exactly, and not relief. It was recognition — the look of one solitary animal meeting another at a waterhole and understanding, without negotiation, that from now on they drank at the same hours.

"You understand what we're doing," Marguerite said. "If Vane finds out — if any of this touches daylight before we're ready — he doesn't fire us. There's no HR department on a glacier. There's just weather, and distance, and men with rifles, and a lot of places a body doesn't get found until the melt."

"I've audited monsters before," Priya said. "They always have better paperwork than you expect and worse judgment than they think. We'll manage."

Something flickered at the corner of Marguerite's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first Priya had seen there since the river crossing. Then it faded, and Marguerite lifted the binoculars and trained them east, into the black, and held them there for a long, long time.

"Priya," she said at last, and her voice had gone very quiet. "Come look at this."

Priya leaned in, cheek almost touching cheek, and followed the line of the lenses up the eastern moraine to where the last of the starlight silvered the crest of the ridge.

There — brief, unmistakable, gone — a light had blinked. Not a star. Stars didn't move in a horizontal line along a moraine at midnight, and stars didn't come in fours.

One. Two. Three. Four. Headlamps, shielded, disciplined, spaced like soldiers, climbing steadily toward the escarpment through the dark.

The deadline, Priya thought, her stomach dropping through the cold air of the mountain, has already started.
