## 9. Night Run

The camp slept the way a held breath sleeps — shallow, listening, ready to break.

Marguerite lay in her tent with her boots on and her pack already buckled, watching the orange glow of the dying fire through the nylon. Beyond it, the glacier muttered to itself all night, that deep grinding conversation of ice against stone, ice against ice, as though the mountain were turning over in its sleep and dreaming of the sea it had been. She had learned to sleep inside that sound years ago, guiding clients who paid her to keep them alive in country that did not care whether they lived or died. But tonight the sound had teeth. Tonight she kept hearing Priya's voice from watch duty two nights past, low and urgent over the hiss of the stove: *Crates, Marguerite. Chains. Helicopter fuel cached at the moraine. Nobody brings chains to an archaeological survey.*

And this morning, before first light, she had watched four men with rifles slung across their backs walk east along the moraine toward the escarpment, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where they were going and exactly what was waiting there.

Days ahead of any legitimate survey schedule. Days ahead of anything Edmund Yarrow had announced around the map table with his careful, scholarly cadence.

She had stood by the cook tent with a tin mug going cold in her hands and done the arithmetic no one wanted done: Vane's advance party would reach the site in three days. The main body, following the mapped route, would take five. If anyone guarded that ledge — if Crane's map meant what she believed it meant, if there were people, descendants, caretakers, anyone at all who had kept that place through five hundred years of erasure — then armed men were about to arrive unannounced at their door, and the only person in this expedition who knew they were coming was a woman lying under a false name in a borrowed tent, trusted by no one, belonging to nothing.

*Deliver it,* Crane had said, his blood soaking through the blanket she'd pressed against him, his hand closing around hers with the last strength he owned. *Before the melt. Before the calving seals it. Promise me.*

She had promised a dying man. She had not promised Corliss Vane.

Marguerite waited until the fire burned down to coals and Tomás's snores settled into their familiar rhythm, punctuated by the small sounds he made in his sleep — half words, half prayers, fragments of Spanish she'd never asked him to translate. She waited until the sentry rotation turned over and heard Priya's footsteps pass her tent on the way to the eastern post, unhurried, professional. Priya knew. Not everything — Marguerite had given her only the shape of it, not the name at the center — but enough. Enough that when Marguerite had said, earlier, *if I'm not in my tent at dawn, don't come looking until you've checked the supply manifest*, Priya had looked at her for a long moment across the stove and said only: *Take the good rope. And come back.*

Now she unzipped the tent with the slow patience of a woman defusing something, slid out into the cold, and stood a moment letting her eyes adjust. The moon was a thin paring, three days off full, enough light to travel by and not enough to be seen by. Perfect. The mountains stood around the camp like a congregation of grey giants, their summits catching the faintest silver, and below them the Kettle Glacier lay in its valley like a frozen river caught mid-flood, blue-white and ancient and utterly indifferent to the small human dramas unfolding on its flanks.

The rope line was fifty meters beyond the camp's perimeter, strung between two boulders as a marker for the route down to the lower tongue — the line every member of the expedition had been briefed to clip onto when crossing the icefall's approach. She reached it without incident, crouched behind the second boulder to check her gear one final time: headlamp, spare batteries, the grease-pencil map sealed in a waterproof sleeve against her sternum, a week's rations compressed into a day's worth of weight, her ice tools, the anchor kit she never traveled without since the Kettle Crossing.

Since the crevasse. Since Tomás falling, and the rope going taut, and her hands doing what her father's hands had taught them to do twenty years ago in a quarry outside Yellowknife, building snow anchors out of nothing while a man's life swung on the other end of the line.

*You build anchors the way I taught you,* Edmund had said afterward, standing apart from the celebration, his voice carefully neutral, his eyes anything but. *Nobody else builds them that way.*

*I had a good teacher,* she had answered, and walked away, and hated herself for how much it had cost her to say even that much.

She clipped onto the rope line now, and that was when the voice came out of the dark behind her.

"You'll want the second anchor point, not the first. The first is buried under a meter of fresh snow. It won't hold a fall."

Marguerite went absolutely still. The voice was quiet, cultured, worn smooth by decades of lecture halls and field camps, and she knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat — with her whole body, involuntarily, before thought could intervene.

"I've been watching your tent for two hours," Edmund Yarrow said, stepping out of the shadow of the far boulder. He was dressed for travel, fully roped, pack on, ice axe in hand, and in the thin moonlight his face was older than she remembered it being that morning, carved deeper, as though the last few days had taken years off his life expectancy and spent them all at once. "You're good. Better than I was at your age. But you checked your left crampon twice and your right once, and you always look at the route before you look at the sky. Your mother did the same thing."

"Don't." The word came out flat and cold, a blade laid on ice. "Don't talk about her."

"Then let's talk about where you're going." He came closer, stopping just outside arm's reach, close enough that she could see the frost forming in his eyebrows, the exhaustion banked behind his discipline. "East, along the high moraine, ahead of Vane's men. To warn whoever's up there. To keep a promise to a dead prospector whose map you carry against your chest right now — because I can see the outline of the sleeve through your jacket, and I know exactly what a waterproof document sleeve looks like under fleece, because I've carried one the same way for forty years."

Every instinct she had screamed at her to deny it. Instead she found herself saying, with a calm that surprised her: "How long have you known?"

"That you're my daughter?" He almost smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had ever seen. "Since the Kettle Crossing. Maybe before. Since the moment you tied off that anchor and didn't look at anyone for praise. You looked at the anchor. I used to do that. My father beat it into me that the work speaks for itself, and I passed it to a six-year-old girl in a quarry, and she grew up and became someone who builds anchors in the dark for strangers and takes no credit." His breath fogged between them. "I've known since the crossing. I said nothing because I was a coward. I'm saying something now because I'm done being one."

"You're done being one," she repeated. Her voice stayed level but her hands, hidden in her gloves, had closed into fists so tight she could feel her nails through the lining. "Four armed men are walking toward that site right now because the man who funds this expedition wants to sell five hundred years of someone else's history by the pound. You've known about Vane for — how long? Years? You took his money. You drew those survey notations on that map — I recognized your handwriting before I recognized the coordinates. And you're telling me tonight, at a rope line, in the dark, that you're *done being a coward*?"

"Yes."

"One word."

"It's more than I deserve and less than you need, but yes. All of it. Every piece." He stepped closer still, and now she could see that his eyes were wet, though his voice never wavered. "I can't give you back twenty years, Marguerite. I can't give you back your mother, or the birthdays, or the reason you flinch when someone says the word 'father.' I can't undo any of it. What I can do is stand between Vane's men and that site tonight, with whatever's left of my reputation and my body, and I am asking you — not telling you, asking — to let me come with you."

She should have said no. Every rational part of her, every scarred and self-protective part, knew that the correct answer was no — that taking him along meant trusting him, and trusting him meant handing him the power to hurt her again, and that the smart move, the guide's move, the survivor's move, was to knock him down, tie him to a boulder, and leave him a note explaining why.

Instead she heard herself say: "Can you keep pace? Because I'm not slowing down for you."

Something moved through his face — relief, grief, gratitude, all tangled together, none of them allowed to finish. "I climbed the Columbia Icefield at fifty-eight," he said quietly. "I can keep pace with you."

She unclipped from the rope line, moved ten meters down, and clipped in again beside him rather than ahead of him. It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even trust. It was logistics — two climbers on one rope travel better than one climber alone on rotten ice — and she told herself that was all it was, even as some younger, hungrier part of her whispered that it was the first time in twenty years she had stood on the same rope as her father, and that the rope between them felt like both a lifeline and a fuse.

They moved out into the dark together.

* * *

The moraine at night was a different country.

By day it was brutal and grey, a highway of broken stone dumped along the glacier's flank by centuries of slow violence. By night, under the paring moon, it became something older — a spine of the world, black rock silvered at the edges, the glacier below breathing its long cold breath, and above them the stars so dense and close that Marguerite felt she could reach up and trail her fingers through them like a hand through water. They climbed in silence for the first hour, rope slack between them, boots crunching on scree that shifted and sighed beneath each step, and the only sounds in the world were their breathing, the ice, and occasionally the far-off crack of the glacier settling, like knuckles cracking in a giant's fist.

It was Edmund who finally spoke, and when he did, his voice was pitched low, conversational, the register of a man talking across a campfire rather than across a chasm of twenty years.

"The notation you recognized. On Crane's map."

"What about it."

"The elevation figures were mine. The contour intervals. Crane couldn't read a contour to save his life — bless him, he could find gold in a granite wall but he drew elevation lines like a drunk drawing rivers." A pause, filled only by their breathing and the crunch of scree. "He came to me eleven years ago, after the disgrace, when I was living in a basement suite in Whitehorse doing contract surveys for mining companies that wanted to strip the north bare. He had sketches. Fragments. Stories from the old trappers about carvings above the Kettle, about a longhouse older than anything south of the permafrost line. He wanted it documented properly. Protected. He knew what would happen if word got out wrong — knew it the way only a prospector knows, having watched a dozen sites get chewed to gravel by men with permits and no conscience." Another pause. "So we worked on it together. Quietly. For years. And then Corliss Vane bought my debts, and my silence, and my expertise, and I told myself I could steer the expedition from the inside. Keep the site safe by controlling the survey. And then Vane showed me his manifests, and I understood what I had actually sold."

"And you still stayed."

"And I still stayed." No excuse in it. No flinching. Just the flat, terrible honesty of a man laying his own bones on the table. "Because leaving meant losing what little access I had, and staying meant pretending, and I chose pretending, because pretending is what cowards do best. Until tonight."

Marguerite said nothing. The scree rose steeper now, and she saved her breath for the climb, but her mind was working fast and cold, sorting his words like a geologist sorting core samples — what was ore, what was waste rock, what was pyrite glittering prettily and worth nothing. He had given her truth freely, but truth from Edmund Yarrow came mixed with twenty years of practice at shaping narratives, and she would be a fool to swallow it whole.

Still. When he reached back without being asked to steady her over a slick section of ice-glazed stone — a small, automatic gesture, the gesture of a partner, of a rope-mate — she found she did not pull away.

* * *

They stopped to rest in the lee of a shattered ridge at what her watch told her was 2:40 in the morning, chewing frozen energy bars that tasted of sweetened cardboard, sharing a thermos lid of tea that Edmund had somehow had the foresight to bring. Below them the glacier stretched away pale and vast, and somewhere on its far side, hours behind them now, the camp was a smudge of darkness they'd never find again from here.

"They'll notice by dawn," Marguerite said. "Two missing. Vane will know."

"He'll know," Edmund agreed. "And he'll assume the worst — that we've gone to make contact with the site's guardians, or to document the site independently, or simply to steal the discovery for ourselves. Any of those stories serves us poorly. None of them changes what we have to do."

"We get there first. We warn whoever's up there. We—" She stopped. In the distance, faint but unmistakable, carried on the still night air from somewhere ahead and below: voices. Human voices, more than one, moving in the dark.

Edmund went rigid beside her. Slowly, carefully, he killed his headlamp, and she killed hers, and they sank down behind the ridge stones and listened.

The voices were coming from the east. From the direction of the escarpment approach. Moving *toward* them, not away — which meant Vane's advance party had pushed harder and faster than anyone had estimated, traveling through the night, and were now closer to the site than Marguerite and Edmund were, perhaps by hours, perhaps by less.

Marguerite closed her eyes and did the arithmetic again, and this time the numbers came out wrong. Badly wrong. Three days had become one. One had become *now*.

"They're ahead of us," she breathed.

"No." Edmund's voice, when it came, was very quiet, and very certain, and it was the voice she remembered from childhood — the lecture-hall voice, the certainty-of-stone voice, the voice that had made her believe, once, that her father knew everything. "Listen. Count the voices. Four men, Vane's party — you'd hear four sets of boots on scree, four voices at minimum, probably more if they're talking. I count two. Two voices, and they're not moving like soldiers. They're moving like people who know this ground the way I know a library." He paused, and in the dark she heard him exhale slowly. "That's not Vane's advance party, Marguerite. That's the guardians. Coming *down*. Toward the route. Toward whoever's coming up."

Toward them.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Marguerite reached into her jacket, drew out the waterproof sleeve, and held the map between them in the thin moonlight — her father's own contours, Crane's grease-pencil lines, the escarpment marked in a hand eleven years dead. Two liars on one rope, crouched in the dark above a glacier, holding between them the only honest thing either of them had touched in years.

"Whatever happens next," she said quietly, "we tell them the truth. All of it. Who we are, why we're here, what's coming. No more lies. Not tonight."

Edmund nodded once. "No more lies."

Below them, out of the dark, a lantern flickered to life — and began to climb toward them through the night.
