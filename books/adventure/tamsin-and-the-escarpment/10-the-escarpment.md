## 10. The Escarpment

They climbed through the last of the dark, and the mountain gave them the dawn as payment.

Marguerite felt it before she saw it — a thinning of the cold, a grey that crept up from beneath the horizon like water rising in a hold. Above them the escarpment stood black against it, a wall of limestone and older things, its face streaked with the rust-bleed of iron seams. The wind had died somewhere below the scree line, and in its absence the silence was enormous, the kind of silence that made her own breathing feel like an intrusion.

Edmund Yarrow climbed ten feet behind her on the fixed line, and she was aware of him the way she was aware of weather — constantly, unwillingly, by the sound of his crampons and the rhythm of his breath. He had not spoken since the rope line at camp. Neither had she. There was nothing to say that could be said while moving over ground that wanted to kill them both, and so they had climbed together through six hours of night like two strangers sharing a lifeboat, each pretending the other wasn't there.

But he had come. That was the thing she kept turning over as her boots found the next ledge. She had expected to be stopped. She had built the whole argument in her head on the rope line — *you don't understand what's up there, you're in over your head* — and instead he had looked at her for a long moment with his headlamp throwing shadows into the hollows of his face, and said only: "Give me two minutes to write a note in case we don't come back."

Two minutes. As if his disappearance were the thing requiring explanation.

The ledge system began where the moraine gave out, a staircase of tilted slabs cut into the cliff by some patient violence of ice long gone. Marguerite led, reading the rock the way her father had taught her to read it when she was small enough to ride his shoulders across talus fields — *the stone tells you where it wants to break, Tamsin; your job is only to listen.* She had hated that name once. She had spent eleven years making sure no one used it. And yet here, alone with the mountain and the man who'd given it to her, she let herself hear it again, faintly, like a song from a house that had burned down.

"Chockstone," Edmund said behind her, low. "Left side. Don't trust the right."

She had already seen it. She moved left without acknowledging him, and heard the almost-sound he made — not quite approval, not quite anything — and hated how much she wanted it.

The sun cleared the eastern ridge as they gained the final pitch, and the escarpment caught fire. Limestone went gold, then rose, then a white so bright it hurt behind her glacier glasses. The Kettle Glacier lay far below and behind them now, a river of dirty silver winding down from the col, and beyond it the whole drowned valley of their approach, blue with morning shadow. Somewhere down there Vane's advance party was moving along the eastern moraine with rifles and crates. Somewhere down there Priya was watching the manifests and Tomás was keeping his hands busy and his eyes open. The thought of them pulled at her like current. But the promise pulled harder.

Aldous Crane had bled out in a trapper's cabin with her hand pressed over his wound and his fingers locked around her wrist, and the last coherent thing he'd said was not about pain or God or even his own name. It was: *before the melt. Before the melt, girl, or it won't matter what I found.* She had promised a dying stranger she would carry his map to this wall of stone, and she had learned the hard way that promises made to the dying are the only ones that can't be renegotiated.

"There." Edmund's voice, just above her shoulder.

She saw it and her heart stumbled.

A cleft in the escarpment face, invisible from below — she understood now why Crane's map had been drawn in grease pencil on waxed paper, why the coordinates were approximate, why no one had found this in a century of prospectors swarming these ranges. From the valley floor the cleft read as nothing, a shadow among shadows, a fold in the rock. You had to be standing nearly on top of it, at the precise angle of a dying man's memory, to see the seam at all.

And at the lip of the cleft, half-buried in lichen and two hundred years of gravel wash, stood a carved post.

Marguerite knelt beside it without deciding to. Her gloves came off before she could think better of it, bare fingers finding the grooves despite the cold, because some part of her — the part trained before she was ten, the part that never died no matter how many rivers she guided rich tourists down — needed to know if it was real.

It was real.

The carving ran the full height of the post and continued, she realized, down into the gravel. Interlocking figures — not the crude pictographs of the tourist brochures, not the scattered petroglyphs that dot the southern valleys and get photographed and forgotten. These were composed. Deliberate. Figures joined hand to hand in a chain that spiraled around the wood, and interspersed among them, unmistakably, were forms she recognized from museum cases three thousand miles away: copper shields. Trade goods. A record of wealth moving along routes no one had ever mapped here.

"This is Coast-and-interior style," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange in the vast quiet. "Mixed. This is— nobody mixed these traditions. Not this far north. Not in the literature."

"No," Edmund said quietly. "They didn't."

She looked up at him. He had removed his own glove and was standing with his palm hovering an inch above the carving, not touching, the way a man hovers his hand over a sleeping child. His face — the face she had studied across courtroom sketches and newspaper columns for a decade, learning hatred like a language — was doing something she had no category for.

He looked young. He looked like the man in the photographs from before, kneeling in a trench with dirt to his elbows, laughing at something off-camera.

"The literature is wrong," he said. "Or incomplete. Or—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Tamsin. Look at the stratigraphy at the base there. Tell me what you see."

She should have flinched at the name. Part of her did — a small cold shock, like stepping into meltwater. But the site was pulling at her too hard, and some exhausted, honest corner of her let it go, just for now, just on this ledge.

She knelt at the base of the post. The gravel wash had slumped away from the cleft mouth in recent years — she could see the fresh scar of it — and beneath the surface gravel the matrix changed. Darker. Organic. Compacted.

"Midden," she said slowly. "Occupation layer. And that—" she pointed to a lens of pale material threading through the dark soil, "—that's crushed shell, but it's not local. Wrong chemistry for this drainage." She sat back on her heels, and her pulse was loud in her ears. "This isn't a campsite. This is a settlement. A permanent one."

"A trade entrepôt," Edmund said. "At minimum." He crouched beside her, careful of the deposits, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register she remembered from a hundred childhood evenings — the lecture voice, except that back then it had been aimed at her like a gift, the one time he ever talked to her as though she were a colleague rather than a child. "Look at the position. Command of the corridor between the glacier route and the river systems. Defensible, watered, south-facing. They weren't passing through here. They were *stationed* here. This was a node. Possibly *the* node."

"For what?"

"For everything we've been told didn't happen." He gestured at the valley below, the whole blank space on the maps. "Coastal goods moving inland. Interior materials moving coastward. Obsidian, copper, dried fish, dentalium — the trade networks everyone insisted stopped at the passes. Someone maintained a settlement here, at altitude, season after season, generation after generation, and the histories wrote it out of existence because it was easier to believe the mountains were empty."

The wind came back then, a single gust off the col, carrying the smell of ice and stone. Marguerite shivered and didn't know if it was the cold.

*Before the melt,* Crane had said. He had known. Some old prospector with a grade-school education had walked into this cleft decades ago and understood, in his gut, what entire faculties had failed to imagine. And he had spent the rest of his life carrying the secret like a coal in his chest, waiting for someone he could trust with it, and at the end he had chosen a stranger on a river because she'd bandaged him and hadn't flinched.

"We have to see the rest," she said.

Edmund nodded. "Carefully. Nothing moves without documentation first. We do this right, or we don't do it at all."

*We*, again. She let it stand.

* * *

The cleft opened inward like a throat.

They went in single file, Marguerite first with the map held open against her chest, following grease-pencil lines that Aldous Crane had drawn with a dying man's economy. The passage narrowed, turned twice, and then the walls fell away and they stepped out onto a shelf the size of a church floor, walled on three sides by the escarpment itself and open to the south — sheltered, hidden, catching every hour of daylight the latitude allowed.

Marguerite stopped walking and forgot how to start again.

Carved posts stood across the shelf in rows — dozens of them, some fallen, some still upright after centuries, their surfaces worked edge to edge with figures. Behind them, breaking the shelf's far end, a long mound ran the width of the ledge, grass-grown and deceptively gentle, its outline too regular, too straight, too long to be anything natural. Roof-fall. Collapsed beams. The buried skeleton of a structure that must have run sixty feet if it ran a foot.

A longhouse. At four thousand feet. Behind a door in solid rock.

"Oh," Edmund said. Just that. A single syllable pushed out of him like a rib breaking.

He walked forward and Marguerite watched him change. She had seen men transformed by mountains, by storms, by near-death — she had thought she knew every shape awe could take. She had been wrong. This was different. This was a man walking into the answer to a question he had given his whole life to, and finding it larger than the question, larger than the life, larger than anything he had left room inside himself to receive it.

He knelt at the nearest upright post and did not touch it. He took out his notebook — the same brand of notebook, she noticed with a jolt, that he had carried all through her childhood, the same green cloth cover — and he began to draw. His hand shook. She watched him steady it against his knee and keep working, sketching the spiral chain of figures, annotating, measuring by eye, and the scratch of his pencil was the only sound on the shelf besides the wind.

"Tamsin." He didn't look up. "Come look at the threshold."

She crossed the shelf slowly, giving every feature its due distance, cataloguing as she went because her body remembered training her mind had disowned. At the mound's western end, where the gravel had washed thin, a flat stone lay exposed — and it was worked. Pecked and ground, its face covered with a composition unlike the posts: a central figure with arms extended, radiating lines, and around the border a running pattern of what were unmistakably canoe forms. Canoes, inland, at altitude, carved into the very threshold of the buried house.

"They brought the sea up the mountain," Marguerite whispered.

"They brought the sea up the mountain," Edmund agreed, and his voice cracked on it. He sat back on the gravel, heedless of the cold coming up through his knees, and looked at the whole shelf — the posts, the mound, the hidden door, the valley of erased history spread out below — and Marguerite saw the moment the wonder curdled.

She watched it happen. Watched the light go out of his face by degrees, replaced by something greyer. His eyes went to the south, down-valley, toward the moraine where four armed men were moving with crates and chains toward a site they had been sent to strip days before any legitimate survey could reach it. Toward *this*. Toward the posts and the threshold and the longhouse under the grass, all of it crated and chained and lifted out by helicopter into Corliss Vane's private world, to hang in some climate-controlled gallery where no descendant would ever stand in front of it and feel what she was feeling right now.

Edmund put his face in his hands.

"I signed the supply orders," he said, into his palms. His voice came out flattened, deadened, the voice of a man reciting his own autopsy. "I told myself it was survey equipment. Crates for core samples. I knew. God help me, Tamsin, I knew by the second manifest, and I kept signing because the alternative was—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "Because I had nothing left, and he knew it, and he bought what was left."

The wind moved through the carved posts and made a sound Marguerite would remember the rest of her life — a low, hollow resonance, as if the shelf itself were breathing through a hundred throats.

She should have said something cruel. She had rehearsed cruelties for eleven years; she had a whole armory of them, honed on long solo nights, and every one of them rose to her lips now and found no purchase. Because the man kneeling in the gravel with his face in his hands was not the man from the newspaper columns. He was smaller than that man. And more frightened. And still, underneath the ruin of him, recognizably the person who had taught her to listen to stone.

"You knew what Vane sent those men for," she said at last. Not cruelty. Just the fact, laid down between them like a chockstone.

"Yes."

"And you came anyway. Last night. With me."

He lowered his hands. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I came because my daughter was walking into the dark alone, and because—" He exhaled. "Because I have been looking away from things for eleven years, and I am so tired, Tamsin. I am so tired of looking away."

Above them the escarpment caught the midmorning sun and burned white and gold, indifferent and magnificent. Below, far down the eastern moraine, something glinted — metal, movement, small as a flea at this distance but unmistakable. Men. Moving fast now, as if they had finally found what they were climbing toward.

Edmund saw it too. She watched him track it, watched his jaw set, and when he spoke again the wonder was still in his voice but it had fused with something harder, something that sounded almost like the father from the old photographs.

"They'll reach the switchbacks by midafternoon," he said. "Which means we have until midafternoon to decide what we are going to do about it. Together, I think. If you'll allow it."

Marguerite looked at the carved threshold, at the sea-borne figures climbing toward a center that had waited centuries for someone to read it. Then she looked at her father — kneeling in the gravel of the greatest discovery of his ruined life, asking her, of all people, for terms.

"We document everything first," she said. "Every post, every stone. Photographs, drawings, measurements. If they're going to take it, the world will know exactly what was here."

Edmund nodded slowly. Then he reached into his pack and drew out the satellite phone, and his thumb hovered over the power button — and stopped.

"Tamsin," he said quietly. "There's something you need to know about Aldous Crane before we make that call."
