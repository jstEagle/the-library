## 11. What Edmund Buried

The night before the escarpment, Edmund Yarrow did not sleep. He sat at the edge of the fire's dying light with his notebook open on his knee and wrote nothing, because there was nothing left to write that the mountain had not already written in him. Around him the camp breathed — Tomás snoring with the untroubled rhythm of an honest man, Priya's tent zipped against the cold, and beyond them, in the dark, his daughter's empty sleeping bag, still holding the shape of her departure.

She had gone into the night without him knowing, and he had followed anyway. That was the thing he could not stop turning over: that after twenty years of silence, of letters unsent and a name spoken like a curse, all it had taken to put him beside her was a rope line and moonlight and the old reflex of his body — *follow the one who walks toward danger* — which was, he understood now, the only inheritance he had ever given her that was worth anything.

He should have slept. Tomorrow they would need everything. Instead he let the fire burn down to its red arithmetic and let the past come for him, as it always did when he was too tired to bar the door.

* * *

It began, as most ruin begins, with a man who believed he was being careful.

Edmund had been forty-six the year the world decided he was a fraud. He remembered the season of it precisely — the wet spring, the smell of the university's old radiators waking after winter, the way the committee room had smelled of burnt coffee and carpet glue while three men and a woman explained to him, with the gentle cadences people use on the dangerous and the grieving, that the Kerman tablets were modern forgeries and that the isotopic evidence pointed, regrettably but conclusively, to his own field laboratory.

He had not forged them. He wanted, even now, even here, at fifty-eight, with the glacier groaning below him like something dreaming — he wanted that sentence to stand somewhere in the record. He had not forged them. But wanting it had never once made it true in any room that mattered.

What he had done was worse, in a way, though no tribunal had ever found a name for it. He had been careless. He had left the tablets accessible during the two weeks his graduate assistant, Daniel Osei, had been struggling through a personal catastrophe — a brother's arrest, a mother's hospital bills — and Daniel had been approached, and Daniel had been paid, and Daniel had copied the tablets so skillfully that only the isotopes gave the lie away. And when the inquiry came, Daniel had named Edmund as the author of the scheme, because Daniel had been promised leniency for a name, and Edmund — proud, furious, certain that the truth would out — had refused to name Daniel first.

The truth had not outed. The truth had sat in a filing cabinet while Edmund's career was carried out of the building in boxes.

Marguerite had been eleven. He thought about her at eleven the way a man thinks about a wound he can see the scar of but not the moment of cutting. She had come to the university that last afternoon, waiting outside the committee room with a library book on her knees — he could still see the book, blue cloth, gold lettering worn to ghost, some novel about ships — and when he came out she had read his face before he could arrange it, the way she always had, and she had said, "Papa?" in a voice that asked him to contradict what she already knew.

He had not contradicted it. He had knelt down on the corridor floor, in front of the committee members and the janitor and God, and told her that Papa had to go away for a while, and that Mama would explain, and that none of it was her fault — which was the one true thing he managed to say in that entire season, and the only one anyone believed.

* * *

Corliss Vane came to him fourteen months later, in a hotel bar in Whitehorse, and Edmund would spend the rest of his life understanding that this was the moment he actually lost everything — not the inquiry, not the boxes, not Marguerite's face in the corridor, but the whiskey glass in the Whitehorse bar, condensation sliding down it like the glacier itself arriving early to warn him.

Vane had chosen the table by the window. Vane always chose the table by the window; Edmund learned later it was so that whoever sat across from him was always backlit, always half silhouette, always slightly harder to read than the man reading them. He was younger then than the photographs suggested, broad through the shoulders, with the easy handsomeness of a man who had never once been interrupted. He ordered for both of them without asking.

"Dr. Yarrow." He said the title deliberately, and Edmund felt it land like a coin dropped into a dry well. "I've read your work. All of it. Including the parts everyone else stopped reading."

"The parts everyone else stopped reading are the parts that ruined me."

"No." Vane turned his glass a quarter-turn on the coaster, precisely, a man who calibrated everything. "The parts everyone else stopped reading are the parts that prove you were right. The trade-route thesis. The northern dispersal pattern. Everyone remembers the scandal. Nobody remembers that your original survey data was never discredited — only you."

"That's a distinction without a market."

"On the contrary." Vane smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and Edmund would come to know that warmth the way you come to know the particular stillness of air before a slide releases. "It's the entire market. I've been buying up the northern mineral leases quietly for six years. Your survey work maps onto my holdings almost perfectly. You found things, Dr. Yarrow, twenty years ago, that the province doesn't know are there. I'd like you to find them again."

"I don't dig anymore."

"You will." Vane said it without menace, which was the frightening part. There was no threat in him at all, not that night. There was only patience, the patience of a man who had read Edmund Yarrow more carefully than any review board ever had, and knew exactly which hunger had survived the disgrace. Not money. Not vindication, exactly. It was the third thing, the one nobody names: the need to be *right where everyone could see it*. To stand in a trench with the cameras running and hold up the thing that proved the whole world had been wrong about you.

Vane offered him an expedition the way other men offer a drink to a drowning swimmer — generously, warmly, with no mention of the current underneath.

And Edmund took it. He took it with both hands.

* * *

The first two seasons had been clean. That was the part he could hardly forgive himself for, looking back — how good it had been. Real funding, real permits, real students who called him *Professor* without the flicker of irony that had followed him through his exile years teaching night courses to hobbyists in Prince George. They had surveyed the eastern moraine. They had pulled charcoal samples that dated beautifully. His hands remembered the trowel the way a musician's remember the frets, and for two years he had allowed himself to believe that Vane was simply a patron — that magnates had patronized archaeology since Carnarvon, and that a man was permitted one resurrection.

Then, in the third autumn, Vane had flown up unannounced, walked the site with his hands behind his back like a landowner inspecting orchards, and stood for a long time at the cut where Edmund's team had exposed the corner of a carved threshold stone.

"There," Vane said. "That one. And anything associated with it. I want those handled personally."

"Handled?"

"Crated. Flown out. My buyers have been asking." Vane's tone did not change at all; it was the tone of a man discussing freight schedules. "Private sale. Discreet. The provenance paperwork will be... creative, but that's not your department."

Edmund had looked at the threshold stone — at the interlocking spirals some carver had cut four hundred years before any European name touched this coast, at the wear-polish on its lip where generations of feet had crossed it — and he had felt the exact temperature of the choice in front of him, the way you feel the temperature of water before committing to the crossing.

He could refuse. He could walk to the radio, call the ministry, end everything — the expedition, the funding, the slow rehabilitation of his name, the small apartment in Whitehorse where he had begun, God help him, to be happy.

Or he could say yes, and tell himself the lie that he would spend five years telling himself: *I'll stay inside it. I'll document everything. I'll control what leaves. Better me than someone worse.*

He said yes.

He had told himself, in the years since, that he had said yes for Marguerite — and there was a sliver of truth in it, which is what makes a lie load-bearing. Because within a month of that conversation, Vane had shown him the other ledger. Not the expedition accounts — the *personal* ones. Photographs of Daniel Osei, living comfortably in Toronto under a name that was not quite his own, a name Vane had helped arrange back when Daniel was still useful. Bank records. A daughter of Daniel's enrolled in a private school whose tuition appeared, in the filings, to pay itself.

"You're a careful man now, Edmund," Vane had said, in that same window-lit voice. "But care goes both ways. If the inquiry ever reopens — if anyone ever asks you to testify about the tablets — well. Daniel's situation becomes complicated. Yours becomes fatal. And hers—" and here Vane had paused, and looked at Edmund with something that was almost tenderness, which was the cruelest instrument in his case, "—hers becomes collateral. Children always are, in these matters. Best keep them far from the field."

Marguerite had been nineteen that year. Living with her mother in Vancouver, not speaking to him — the letters he sent coming back unopened, or worse, opened and returned. He had told himself the silence was her anger, and her anger was her right, and that distance was the safest country he could give her.

But Vane's words had rearranged the silence into something else. Into architecture. Every unanswered letter became a wall he had built on purpose. Every year of estrangement became proof of his diligence. He had let her believe she was abandoned — had *needed* her to believe it, because a daughter who hated her father would never write to him, never visit the dig, never appear in a photograph, never become the soft place in Edmund Yarrow where Corliss Vane could press a thumb.

He had traded her love for her safety, without her consent, and called it protection, and known every single day that it was also cowardice wearing protection's coat.

* * *

The fire had gone to embers. Edmund became aware of the cold working through his coat, and of the glacier below the moraine making its long interior sounds — the deep percussion of ice adjusting itself, like a house settling, like a conscience.

Here was the arithmetic he could not make come out even:

If he had stayed away, Marguerite would have grown up whole, hating a father she was better off hating, and Vane would have stripped this site alone, and the spirals on the threshold stone would be hanging in some Zurich apartment above a sofa, their meaning amputated, their context a rumor.

If he had told her the truth at nineteen — *stay away from me, stay away from my work, Vane is watching* — she would have demanded reasons, and reasons were testimony, and testimony was the gun Vane held to Daniel's head and, through Daniel, to Edmund's.

So he had buried it. All of it. Under permafrost, under silence, under the one story she would believe because it required nothing further from him: *he left, and he did not come back.*

And now she was here. Under a false name — he had known within a day; she dug the way he had taught her to dig, left-handed, cleaning backward toward herself, and no stranger on earth moved through a section wall with that particular inherited grammar — and she was here because a dying prospector had put a map in her hands, and the map was drawn in Edmund's own notation system, because Aldous Crane had been his partner once, his first partner, before any of it, before the tablets, before Vane, back when the two of them had surveyed this very escarpment on a shoestring grant and sworn, drunk on cheap rye and youth, that whatever they found here would belong to the descendants of the people who made it.

Crane had kept the faith for thirty years. Crane had died keeping it, gut-shot in a trapper's cabin, pressing a grease-pencil map into a stranger's hands because he had heard Vane's men were on the river and trusted no one closer.

And Edmund had spent the same thirty years as Vane's instrument, telling himself each morning that today he would find the seam in the arrangement, the loose thread, the moment to pull — and each evening that the moment was not yet safe, not yet right, that Marguerite's safety and Daniel's family hung on his compliance, that patience was strategy.

It was not strategy. He saw that now, sitting in the cold with his unwritten notebook. It was the oldest lie in the ledger, the one every compromised man tells himself at altitude: *later.* Vane had never needed chains or threats after that first year. He needed only to let Edmund supply his own.

Tomorrow — or tonight, if her head start held — they would reach the ledge together, and she would see the site, and she would see it cleanly, with eyes he had not yet taught to flinch. And she would ask him, sooner or later, with those eyes that had read his face across a committee-room corridor when she was eleven: *what did you agree to?*

And for the first time in twenty years, Edmund Yarrow found that he wanted to answer. Not to be forgiven — he had done the accounting on forgiveness and found himself insolvent. But because the site deserved a witness who told the whole truth, and because she deserved a father who finally handed her the shovel instead of the story.

He closed the notebook. Far below, the glacier boomed — once, twice — a sound like a door being tested in its frame.

Up-slope, faint on the wind, came the scrape of boots on scree. Someone was moving in the dark, climbing fast toward the escarpment, and Edmund was already reaching for his rope before his mind finished the sentence: *she didn't go alone, and neither will I.*
