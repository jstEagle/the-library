## 12. The First Theft

Priya had learned to read mountains the way other people read faces — by the small betrayals of light and shadow, the way a slope held its snow or shrugged it off, the particular silence a ridge made when it was hiding something. So when she saw the four figures moving along the eastern moraine at first light, tiny as punctuation against the grey sweep of stone, she knew before anyone told her that the survey schedule had been a fiction all along.

She was on the high bench above camp, ostensibly taking permafrost cores from the exposed till. The core drill sat beside her, cold and patient. Her binoculars were in her hands, and through them the four men resolved into detail: hard-shell jackets in colours no geologist would choose, packs riding high and light, rifles slung across their backs with the casualness of men who had carried them long enough to forget they were there.

They were moving fast. Too fast for science. Science stopped to look at things.

* * *

She counted what they carried. That was the habit of her training — count, measure, record, doubt yourself later. Two of them hauled sleds, and the sleds were wrong: not expedition sleds with their lashed cargo and repair patches, but low, rigid platforms with webbing straps and corner fittings, the kind used to pre-rig loads for helicopter lift. She knew those fittings. She had inventoried the supply manifests herself, back in the valley, because Vane had asked her to — asked her charmingly, over coffee that tasted of burnt sugar, handing her a clipboard like a gift.

Crates rated for two hundred kilograms. Cargo netting. A second fuel cache marked at coordinates that made no sense for a surface traverse but perfect sense for an aircraft staging point. She had told herself then that Vane was simply thorough, that wealthy men liked redundancy the way other people liked watches. She had wanted to believe it. Belief was cheaper than suspicion, and suspicion, once unpacked, could not be repacked.

Now she watched the sleds move up the moraine toward the escarpment's eastern shoulder, toward the approach Marguerite had traced on the map with one finger three nights ago, and belief packed its bags and left without saying goodbye.

The morning was doing that thing alpine mornings did — burning off its own beauty, mist lifting off the glacier tongue in slow ribbons so the whole valley seemed to be exhaling. Somewhere below, Tomás was arguing cheerfully with the camp stove. Somewhere lower still, Edmund Yarrow knelt at his excavation grid with the tenderness of a man touching something holy. And Corliss Vane stood at the edge of camp in his immaculate shell jacket, watching the same four men Priya was watching, and he was smiling.

That smile was the worst part. Not hidden. Not ashamed. Pleased.

* * *

She waited until midday to find Marguerite, because waiting was its own discipline. She took the long way around the meltwater pool, checked the weather line twice, invented reasons to pass the cook tent. Tomás pressed a tin mug of something sweet and terrible into her hands and studied her face with those dark, quick eyes of his.

"You look like my sister when the test results come early," he said. "Like you already know and you're deciding how much to say."

"Tomás—"

"I'm not asking." He lowered his voice, and the cheerfulness drained out of him like water out of a cracked pot, leaving something older and harder underneath. "I'm telling you I've been counting too. The crates. The chains. The way Vane's men don't ask questions about the route east. Whatever this is, I'd rather know than not know. Even if knowing is bad."

"It's bad," Priya said.

He nodded slowly, unsurprised, and turned back to his stove, and she understood that he would carry it the way he carried everything — quietly, competently, for other people's sake. Her chest ached with something that felt dangerously like love. Three weeks ago these people had been strangers' names on a payroll sheet. Now they were the only family she had within five hundred kilometres, and she was about to put all of them in the path of whatever Vane was really doing.

She found Marguerite checking anchor points on the rope line above the lateral moraine, gloved hands moving with that economy Priya had come to admire — no wasted motion, no wasted words. The wind came down off the icefield and pulled at Marguerite's hood, and she didn't bother fighting it, just let it whip her hair across her face while she worked.

"The advance party," Priya said. "Four men, eastern moraine, moving since dawn. Sleds rigged for heli-lift."

Marguerite went very still. It was a specific stillness — not surprise, Priya realized, but confirmation. The stillness of someone who has been dreading a sound and finally hears it.

"You knew," Priya said. "Or you suspected."

"I suspected." Marguerite coiled the last of the rope with unnecessary precision. "Vane's manifests never matched a dig. You noticed it weeks ago. I noticed it before we ever left the valley." She looked up, and her eyes were the flat grey-green of glacial ice, and behind the flatness something moved, quick and pained. "Priya, if I tell you something, can you hold it? Not lie for me. Just hold it."

"You're holding something already," Priya said. "You've been holding it since Hired Under a False Sky, or whatever you want to call the day you walked into base camp with a name that doesn't match your boots." She said it gently. She hoped. "Yarrow. Your mother's surname is Yarrow, isn't it? Or your father's is Thackeray, or — I don't know which way it goes. But I've watched you and Edmund argue stratigraphy for three weeks like two people reading the same book from opposite ends. Nobody argues like that with a stranger."

For a long moment the wind filled the silence between them, hissing across the scree like sand through an hourglass.

"My father," Marguerite said at last, "is Edmund Yarrow."

It landed the way she must have known it would land — not as shock, exactly, but as the final piece of a picture Priya had been assembling without admitting it. The resemblance in profile. The shared gestures: the way both of them tapped stone twice before trusting it, the way both fell silent in front of old work.

"And Crane?" Priya said. "The prospector whose map started all this?"

Marguerite's jaw tightened. "Is a story for another day. Today the story is four armed men heading for a site that isn't ours, carrying rigging for aircraft that aren't supposed to exist on this expedition." She turned to face Priya fully. "I'm going up tonight. Ahead of them. There may be people at the site — descendants, guardians, whoever has kept that place for however many centuries. They deserve warning. And if there's no one, then the site deserves warning, and I'm the only one who can give it."

"Then I'm coming with you."

"No."

"Priya—"

"No," Marguerite said again, but softer. "Someone has to stay in camp and watch Vane. Someone he trusts enough to leave unwatched. You're the geologist. You're useful. Usefulness is camouflage." A ghost of a smile. "Trust me. I've been using it for weeks."

* * *

But it was Priya who went up first, in the end — not to the escarpment itself, but to the shoulder of the eastern moraine where the terrain folded into a shallow bowl of shattered rock, a place she'd noted on her first survey walk as ideal for a seismic monitoring station and therefore ideal for a woman with a camera and a conscience.

She reached it in the blue hour before full dark, when the snowfields turned the colour of old bruises and the wind dropped into that eerie alpine hush that always felt less like peace and more like held breath. Below her, maybe six hundred metres down-slope, the advance party had made camp. She could see their fire, small and arrogant in the vastness, and the sleds laid out beside it under tarps, and — as the last light failed — the shapes of the crates they had already begun to build.

They were fast. That was what frightened her most. They weren't exploring, weren't surveying, weren't doing any of the careful, reverent, tedious work that real archaeology demanded. They had gone directly to the site — which meant they had known exactly where it was, which meant the map Marguerite carried was not the only map, which meant—

Which meant Edmund. Or Vane's own sources. Either way, the knowledge had been sold before the expedition ever broke camp, and everything since — the permits, the surveys, the patient grids and trowels — had been theatre performed for an audience of one government liaison who would visit next month and see exactly what he was meant to see.

Priya lay on her stomach among the rocks, camera braced on her pack, and photographed everything.

The firelight helped and hindered — it silhouetted the men beautifully but washed out detail, so she waited, freezing by degrees, for the moon to clear the ridge. When it did, the scene turned silver and legible. She shot the sleds. The crates, stencilled with a logistics company name she recognized from Vane's valley warehouse. The padded cradles inside the crates, cut to shapes that were unmistakably anthropomorphic — head-forms, torso-forms, the negative spaces of carved figures waiting to be stolen. One man walked the perimeter with a rifle and a flashlight, and she photographed him too, and the flashlight beam swung past her position close enough that she pressed her cheek to the frozen ground and breathed through her mouth and thought, absurdly, of her mother's kitchen, of cumin and cardamom, of safety defined as warmth and noise and other people.

The beam moved on.

She photographed the site itself, visible beyond the party's camp as a darker seam in the escarpment's face — the ledge, the fallen lintel stone, the corner of the buried longhouse where the melt had exposed carved wood that should have been documented by brush and daylight and respect, and instead was being measured by men with tape measures and crowbars. Through the telephoto she caught one figure prying at a carving with a flat bar, and the sound reached her a moment later, thin with distance: a crack like a knuckle popping, then another, patient, methodical. Eight hundred years of weather had done less damage in eight hundred years than these men would do in a night.

Her hands shook. She made them stop. Anger was fuel; shaking wasted it.

* * *

She descended at two in the morning, taking the route she'd memorized on the way up, moving by moonlight and memory. Camp was asleep except for the watch rotation, and she slipped past the cook tent where Tomás, unable to sleep, sat wrapped in a blanket drinking tea. He looked at her face, at the frost in her eyelashes, at the camera bag hugged to her chest like a child, and he asked nothing. He poured a second cup and set it on the crate beside him, and that small act steadied her more than any speech could have.

Marguerite was awake, of course. Marguerite was always awake now; Priya had begun to suspect she slept the way glaciers moved — imperceptibly, and only under pressure. She met Priya at the equipment cache, and when Priya showed her the photographs on the camera's small screen, Marguerite's breath came out slow and white and shaking.

"The cradles," she said. "They cut cradles. They knew the forms before they ever saw them."

"Which means someone gave them drawings," Priya said. "Measurements. Edmund's survey data, probably — his original notes from years ago, the ones that got him disgraced. Vane didn't fund an expedition. He funded a retrieval."

Marguerite stared at the screen a long time. In the moonlight her face seemed carved from the same material as the escarpment — pale, striated, bearing marks of old force. Then she closed the camera and handed it back, and when she spoke her voice was stripped down to bare wire.

"I'm leaving within the hour. The eastern approach takes me twelve hours; the advance party will reach the ledge tomorrow afternoon. If I push, I arrive first." She paused. "Radio check at noon tomorrow. Channel four, the frequency we agreed. If I don't answer, assume the worst and get Tomás and yourself out down the western corridor before it softens."

"And do what afterward? Report to whom? Vane owns the satellite phone contract, the liaison, the permits—"

"Then document. Everything. You're good at documenting." Marguerite almost smiled. "It's why I trust you."

She melted into the dark, and Priya stood alone among the crates and ropes with the taste of fear like copper in her mouth.

* * *

Noon came grey and windless, the kind of sky that meant weather was deciding something. Priya climbed to the high bench with the radio, checked her sightlines, and keyed the handset.

"Base to Yarrow. Radio check, channel four. Over."

Static. The long, granular static of high-altitude emptiness, like the mountain sifting sand.

She tried again at ten past. Again at twenty. On the fourth attempt, a voice answered — and for one soaring half-second she thought it was Marguerite, and then the voice resolved and it was wrong, it was all wrong: male, unhurried, amused.

"Base, this is Vane. Lovely day for radio checks." A pause, filled with the faint crackle of a man settling comfortably somewhere. "Interesting frequency you're using, Dr. Nair. Not on our approved allocation. Almost as though someone set up a private channel." Another pause, longer, letting the silence do its work. "Almost as though someone had something to say to someone else. Something she wouldn't want the rest of us to hear."

Priya's mouth went dry. She keyed the transmit button and said nothing, because anything she said would be a confession.

"You're quiet," Vane observed pleasantly. "Geologists usually love explaining things. Here's something I'll explain instead, free of charge: there's a repeater on the ridge above the eastern moraine. Installed it myself, years ago, for mineral surveys. Everything on channel four comes through it. Everything." A soft laugh, like gravel poured slowly. "So thank you for the confirmation, Doctor. I wasn't entirely certain which of my staff was the leak. Now I am. And I know exactly where our friend Yarrow is headed."

The click of the transmission ending was the loudest sound Priya had ever heard.

She stood on the high bench with the dead handset in her hand and understood, with terrible clarity, that the quiet phase was over. Whatever happened next would happen fast, and loud, and to people she loved. Below her, camp stirred with lunch-hour motion — Tomás at his stove, Edmund at his grid, Vane emerging from his tent already giving orders, already turning toward the equipment cache where the radios lived.

And far to the east, beyond the ridge, beyond sight, a woman was climbing alone toward a ledge full of carvings and four men with guns, unaware that her route was known, her channel was open, and the clock had just started running.

Priya began to run.
