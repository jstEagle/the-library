## 13. Taken

The trouble with a camp, Tomás Reyes had learned long ago, was that it told on you. A camp was a confession written in canvas and rope. Anyone who knew how to read one could walk through it like a diary — who slept badly, who ate alone, who had been crying behind the cook tent. He had spent twenty-two years feeding people in places where the wind tried to peel the roofs off the world, and he had learned to keep his own corner of any camp clean of evidence: no letters home pinned inside the flour bin, no photographs tucked into the spice tin, nothing that a stranger could hold up to the light and use.

He should have known Vane would read the camp.

It began, as bad weather does, at the edges. The supply runner came down from the pass two days early, a lean Saulteaux man named Étienne Desjarlais who ran the line between the glacier and the railhead with a mule team and a face that gave away nothing but frost. Tomás fed him first, always — coffee before questions, that was the law of the trail — and Étienne drank it standing at the cook fire's edge, eyes moving over the camp the way a man counts sheep he doesn't own.

"Quiet trip," Tomás said.

"Quiet enough." Étienne turned his cup, not drinking. "Lot of crates going up this time."

"Crates go up all the time."

"Not like these." He set the cup down half-finished, which from Étienne was the same as shouting. "Chained. Sealed with wire and lead. And the manifest says *survey equipment*." His eyes came up, flat and dark as river ice. "I hauled survey equipment for eleven years, Reyes. Survey equipment doesn't rattle when you drop it."

Tomás kept his hands moving — wiping the griddle, banking the coals — because hands that stopped moving invited questions. "You want eggs?"

"I want you to tell the woman with the camera to be careful." Étienne said it quietly, without looking at him, the way men passed dangerous things along trails where they might be watched. "There were two men at the railhead asking about names. Not about cargo. About *names*. Who signed on, who dropped off, who the route-finder was." He straightened, stretched as if his back were the only thing on his mind. "One of them had a list."

* * *

Tomás carried the words through the rest of the day like a stone in his boot. He watched Priya Nair work her grid squares above the scree line, small and precise, her camera bag riding her hip the way other people wore sidearms. He watched Marguerite — *Tamsin*, she was Tamsin here, Tamsin Cole, a name worn like a coat buttoned wrong — read the terrain with her father's eyes and pretend she didn't know whose eyes they were. He watched Corliss Vane stand apart from everyone at the edge of the light, tall and silver-templed and patient as a man watching an auction, and Tomás understood that the patience was the worst thing about him. Greedy men grabbed. Patient men waited until grabbing cost nothing.

That evening the order came down through the expedition secretary, a soft-handed man named Pell who never met anyone's eyes: all personnel would present their documents and personal effects for inventory at the command tent at eight o'clock. *Logistical review,* the notice said. *Standard procedure for the final phase.*

"There is no standard procedure," Priya said, standing at Tomás's cook fire with her arms crossed against the cold and something colder underneath. "I've been on nine expeditions. There is no such thing as a logistical review of personal effects. That's a search."

"It's a confiscation," Tomás said. He kept his voice low, kept his ladle moving through the pot of barley and caribou he'd made because hot food was the only sermon he trusted. "Pell didn't even try to make it sound real."

Marguerite stood a little apart, downwind, the way she always did — close enough to hear, far enough to claim she hadn't. But tonight there was no pretending distance in her face. "They're looking for something specific," she said. "Or someone."

"You," Tomás said, and then wished he hadn't, because saying it out loud made it a fact instead of a fear.

Her jaw tightened. Around them the camp murmured its evening sounds — the generator's drone, the flapping of frozen canvas, Edmund Yarrow's tent glowing amber at the far end like a lamp in a museum case. "Then we don't give them anything to find," Marguerite said. "Priya, your photos —"

"Are on two cards and a drive. One card stays on my body. One goes somewhere no one will look."

"The drive goes in the barley tin," Tomás said. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, embarrassed by his own cleverness, which had never once done him any good until now. "Nobody searches a cook's pantry. It smells too bad and it's too heavy. Half the world's secrets are probably buried in flour sacks."

Something almost like a smile touched Priya's mouth. Then it went, because across the compound, four of Vane's men — the ones who had come up the eastern moraine days ahead of any honest schedule, the ones with rifles slung under tarps that fooled nobody — walked past the mess tent in step, unhurried, the way wolves walk when they are not hungry yet.

* * *

Eight o'clock came gray and windless, the kind of stillness on a glacier that old-timers called *the held breath*. Tomás joined the queue outside the command tent with his kit bag over his shoulder and his heart going like a fist on a door. Ahead of him the line moved slowly. Inside, under the hiss of a propane lantern, Pell sat at a folding table with a ledger open, and beside him sat a man Tomás had not seen before — broad, quiet, with the flattened knuckles of someone who had used them, taking notes in a hand so neat it looked printed.

"Name," Pell said, when Tomás reached the table.

"Tomás Reyes. Cook."

"Nationality."

"Mexican. Papers are in the bag."

The broad man took the bag without asking and turned it out onto the table with a tenderness that was worse than roughness — every item lifted, weighed in the palm, set down in its place. Knife. Spare gloves. The photograph of his sister Lucía he kept folded inside his hat brim, and Tomás's stomach dropped when the man's fingers paused on it, smoothed it flat against the table, studied the girl's crooked smile, and wrote something in the ledger.

"That's my sister," Tomás said. "She's sick. That's why I'm here. That's the whole story."

"I'm sure it is," the man said, in a voice like a closed drawer, and set the photograph face-down.

They took the radio handset. They took his satellite phone. They took the little notebook in which he tracked stores, flipping through it twice, and Tomás felt sweat start between his shoulders despite the cold, because in the back pages of that notebook, disguised as supply arithmetic, he had been keeping a count of Vane's crates — weights, times, which ones rattled. The man's finger stopped on those pages. Stayed there a long moment. Then he tore them out, all six, folded them once, and put them in his breast pocket without a word, and wrote in the ledger: *cook, cooperative.*

"Cooperative," Tomás repeated, when they handed him back a bag lighter by a phone, a radio, and six pages of his own handwriting.

"You'll be compensated for the inconvenience," Pell said, not looking up. "All personnel are restricted to the main camp pending the final phase. Meals will be taken in the mess tent. No one goes beyond the flag line without escort."

"And if I need to check the cache at the moraine? Food stores don't inventory themselves."

"Someone will accompany you."

*Escorted to his own kitchen.* Tomás walked back out into the gray light and found Priya in the queue behind him, chin high, eyes furious and afraid in equal measure, and behind her Marguerite, whose face was smooth as poured wax and whose hands, at her sides, were shaking. When the broad man reached into Marguerite's pack, Tomás drifted closer, ostensibly to argue about coffee rations, and heard everything: the map gone from where she'd hidden it — or said it was; the false-bottomed tin opened with a pocketknife; the name *Tamsin Cole* written in the ledger while the broad man's pen hovered, just barely, as though the ink itself were suspicious.

"Route-finder," the man said. "Cole. Where were you trained?"

"Nowhere you've heard of," Marguerite said pleasantly.

"Everyone's trained somewhere."

"Then I suppose you'll have to look everywhere."

It was bravado, and beautiful, and doomed. Tomás saw it land in the man's eyes like a hook in a fish's lip — the small tightening, the note made, the page turned. They let her go. They let all of them go, back into the camp that was suddenly not a camp but a holding pen, the flag lines strung with new rope, the escorts already walking their rounds. As Tomás passed her, Marguerite murmured, without moving her lips, "The runner. Tomorrow. Can he—"

"I'll get word out," Tomás breathed, "if it's the last pot I ever boil."

* * *

He did not sleep. He lay in his bunk listening to the glacier talk to itself — that deep interior groaning, like a giant turning over in bed — and he thought about Lucía, fourteen years old and growing a spine of titanium where her vertebrae were failing, waiting in Guadalajara for the money that was buying her surgery, money that came from Corliss Vane's payroll and was therefore, Tomás understood now, soaked in whatever this was. Theft. Worse than theft. He thought about how a man could be bought without ever signing anything — how the signature was just the paycheck arriving, month after month, and the silence you kept because your sister's spine was crumbling and Vane's checks cleared.

He got up at three, when the camp was at its stillest and the escort rotation changed — he had counted the change for two nights now, because a cook notices everything or dies of not noticing. In the mess tent he built a fire in the stove and began, deliberately, loudly, to bake. Bread for the morning. If anyone asked, and someone always asked, he was simply a cook who couldn't sleep and might as well work.

While the dough rose he took a fresh notebook from his stores and wrote, in the shorthand of kitchens, a message that would mean nothing to anyone who seized it:

*Menu change for Thursday. The lamb is off — send goat instead. Tell the butcher the T. C. order is cancelled; we'll take the standard cut. Confirm by return.*

*T. C.* Tamsin Cole. *Cancelled.* Any reader would see a cook fussing over meat orders. Étienne Desjarlais, who had run supplies for eleven years and missed nothing, would see a warning: *they have her name, they're checking it, get word to her before the interrogation starts.* And beneath it, folded separately into the same envelope, a second slip for Priya's contact at the university, because Priya had told him once, over watch duty, that if everything went wrong he should send one sentence north: *the ledger is photographed, send the auditors.* He copied that sentence in his careful, schoolroom English, the best handwriting he owned, reserved for the truest things.

At dawn the mule bells sounded below the moraine. Étienne came up through the blue shadow of the ice with frost in his beard and his eyes already reading the camp — the new ropes, the escorts, the way the tents stood too neat, like a stage set after the play has been cancelled. Tomás met him at the cache with the day's manifests and a sack of mail, and under the cover of arguing about flour weights he pressed the envelope into the runner's glove, deep in the wool, the way you'd press a coal into snow.

"Thursday's menu changed," Tomás said. "Lamb's off. Goat instead. You'll tell the butcher personally?"

Étienne's face did not move. Only his eyes, flicking once toward the escort trudging the flag line, then back. "Goat keeps better anyway," he said. "In this heat, lamb turns fast." He swung up onto the sled, gathered the reins, and added, so softly the wind nearly ate it: "Tell your people to stay near the fire. Weather's coming that won't wait for anyone."

Then the bells faded down the white corridor of the glacier, and Tomás stood alone with his flour and his fear, watching the empty trail.

Behind him, boots crunched. He turned. Two of Vane's men stood at the edge of the cache, rifles unslung now, no longer bothering with tarps, and the broad man from the inventory table smiled at him with terrible patience.

"Mess tent, Reyes," he said. "Mr. Vane would like a word about your correspondence."
