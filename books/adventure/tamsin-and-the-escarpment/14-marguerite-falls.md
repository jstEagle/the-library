## 14. Marguerite Falls

The mountain gave her three hours of grace and then took them back with interest.

Marguerite descended the eastern flank in the grey hour before dawn, when the rock was still holding the cold of the night and nothing moved but the wind and her own shadow sliding over the scree. Below her, the glacier lay pale as a dead thing, its surface creased with the blue shadows of crevasses, and beyond it the moraine ran like a scar toward the valley. Somewhere down there were Vane's men. She had seen their fire at midnight, a smudge of orange in the black throat of the eastern gully, four silhouettes moving against it with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been afraid in the mountains.

They were between her and the escarpment approach. Worse — they were between her and camp, and she had no doubt they were climbing toward her, because Vane did not send men out to look at views.

She moved fast, the way her father had taught her before he was anyone's employee, before the disgrace, before the long silence that had swallowed her childhood whole. Weight on the uphill foot. Test every hold. The mountain forgives nothing twice. She could hear his voice in her skull as clearly as if he walked beside her — *the rock will lie to you, Marguerite; the ice never does* — and she hated how much comfort there was in it, how the voice of the man who had abandoned her had become the voice she trusted most in the world.

The gully she chose was a seam of broken stone running down between two ribs of granite, choked with old snow gone rotten at its heart. It looked passable. It looked, in fact, like the only passable thing, because the alternatives were the open moraine where Vane's men would see her against the pale ice, or a backtrack of six hours she did not have. Calving season was coming early. She could feel it in the way the glacier had begun to groan at night, a deep tectonic complaint, like a giant turning over in a sleep it would not much longer keep.

She was a third of the way down when the first shout came.

It rolled up the gully ahead of its meaning, a flat human sound bouncing off stone, and she froze with her hand flat against the rock and her breath held in her chest. Below and to the left, two figures had broken from the treeline of the lower moraine, moving with the efficient, ugly speed of men who did not care what they kicked loose behind them. A third held back near the rocks where they'd stashed gear. She counted the way Priya had taught her to count — not objects but intentions — and understood that this was not a patrol. It was a net. They had known someone would come down this way, or they had guessed, and either way the guess had been good enough.

"Yarrow's girl!" one of them called up, and the name hit her like a thrown stone.

Not her name. Her father's. Which meant they didn't know who she was — they knew only what she was, the daughter, the leak, the complication — and there was a thin mercy in that, or so she told herself, already moving again, faster now, dropping through the broken ground with the map pressed against her ribs inside her jacket where it had lived for a week, warm as a second heartbeat.

The grease-pencil map. Crane's map. The promise.

*Deliver it to the escarpment before the melt.* She had made that promise to a dying man in a cabin that smelled of blood and woodsmoke, and she had kept it, and now the keeping of it had become the thing that would kill her, because Vane's men were not climbing to talk.

A stone whined past her ear and struck the rock beside her head with a flat crack, spitting chips. Not a warning shot. A miss. The difference mattered, and her body understood it before her mind did; she stopped thinking in words and started thinking in holds, in angles, in the grammar of descent that had been drilled into her so deep it was less a skill than an organ.

The gully narrowed. The rotten snow lay across it in a long ramp, sun-cupped and hollowed, its surface crusted but its core gone to slush and air. She knew what it was the moment her boot broke through the crust to the ankle — knew it the way you know a bad floor in an old house — but the men were fifty metres below and closing, and another stone came up the gully and burst against the wall above her, and there was no time to be wise.

She went across the ramp anyway, fast, weight distributed, ice axe held flat to spread the load, and for ten steps the rotten snow held. For ten steps she believed in it.

On the eleventh, the mountain exhaled.

The whole slope moved at once — not an avalanche, nothing so honest, just the slow structural failure of snow that had spent a summer dying in place — and Marguerite went with it, sliding, the world tilting, the grey sky swinging past like a door. She got the axe in and it held for a heartbeat, two, long enough to feel hope, and then the snow beneath the axe sheared away in a slab and she was falling, truly falling, the gully walls strobing past, and she had time for one thought that was not about herself at all — *the map* — before the slope funneled her into the choke at its base and the world went white, then black, then a ringing grey full of the sound of her own blood.

* * *

She came back to herself in pieces.

First the cold, seeping through her jacket where the snow had soaked it. Then the taste of blood in her mouth — bitten cheek, nothing worse, she catalogued it automatically, the way Tomás had taught her, *triage the body like it belongs to a stranger, you'll be kinder to it that way*. Then the pain in her left wrist, arriving last and loudest, a white-hot wire running from her hand to her elbow, and she lay still in the rubble at the base of the gully and breathed through it in long, disciplined pulls until the wire dimmed to something she could think around.

Broken. She knew broken. She had set a broken wrist on a client in the Selkirks with a tent pole and duct tape and the client's own belt between his teeth. She knew exactly what it meant: her left hand was, for the foreseeable future, a club. She tested the fingers — they moved, all five, sluggish and screaming — and felt something in her chest unknot slightly. A club was survivable. A dead hand was not.

The gully walls rose on either side of her, steep and clean, showing her exactly how far she had fallen: thirty metres, perhaps more, the rotten ramp sheared away above her into a fresh scar of exposed ice. No climbing back up. Not with one hand. Not before the men arrived, and they would arrive, because the fall had been loud and the mountain had announced her like a town crier.

She got up.

It took three attempts and cost her a sound she was not proud of, a small animal noise she bit down on and buried. She stood in the rubble with the world swaying gently around her and did the inventory that mattered. Axe: gone, somewhere up the slope, buried or shattered. Rope: still coiled at her hip, mercifully. The map—

The map.

Her right hand went to her ribs and found the jacket torn open along the seam, the inner pocket hanging like a gutted fish, and for one full second she felt nothing at all, a perfect and terrible blankness, and then she was on her knees in the rubble tearing through the scree with one good hand, flinging stones, digging through the slush, saying *no* out loud in a voice she didn't recognize, low and continuous, a litany, a prayer with no god attached to it.

She found her compass. She found her knife. She found a scrap of the jacket lining, chewed and grey.

The map was gone. Carried down the slope inside the failing snow, or flung free somewhere in the fall, lying in the open somewhere on this mountainside like a leaf — and even if she found it, she had no time, because the voices were coming now, real and close, bouncing up the gully from below.

She looked at the descent ahead of her. Steep ground, rotten, unroped, one-handed, pursued. And below it the moraine, and below that the glacier, and beyond the glacier the escarpment, where a dead man's promise waited for her with the patience of stone.

*Deliver it before the melt.* The melt was not waiting either.

Marguerite Yarrow stood at the bottom of the gully she had fallen down and let the despair come, because she had learned long ago that despair was like weather — you did not beat it, you did not argue with it, you stood in it and you let it pass through you, and if you were still standing when it passed, you moved. It moved through her now like the glacier's cold, filling every hollow, and she let it, and she counted her breaths, and on the fortieth breath it began, very slowly, to thin.

The map was gone. But Crane had drawn the map from memory, which meant the memory existed, which meant the route existed, which meant — she closed her eyes and saw it, the grease-pencil lines, the survey notations in her father's hand, the escarpment's profile like a broken tooth against the sky — which meant it was still in her. Not on paper. In her. In the childhood afternoons at the kitchen table with a man who had taught her to read terrain the way other fathers taught daughters to read hymnals. They could take the paper. They could not take the kitchen table.

She turned downhill.

* * *

They nearly had her at the moraine.

She heard them before she saw them — the particular crunch-and-slide of men moving fast on broken rock — and went to ground behind a slab of granite furred with orange lichen, pressing herself into its shadow as the two climbers from the gully came down the rib to her left. Up close they were younger than she'd guessed, hard-used, one of them carrying a coil of rope slung like a weapon. They stopped ten metres away and she watched their breath hang in the cold air and listened to them talk about her in the flat, bored way men talk about a job.

"Blood on the rocks down there. She's hurt."

"Hurt's good. Vane wants her talking, not walking."

"Vane wants a lot of things." The younger one spat. "He wants the paper. Old man Crane's paper. That's the whole errand."

Marguerite's heart stopped and restarted, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that they didn't know the map was lost. They were hunting the paper because Vane had told them the paper mattered — and the paper mattered because Vane believed it was the only key. She pressed her bleeding cheek against the cold stone and made herself memorize their faces, their gear, the direction they moved when they moved on: east, toward the escarpment approach, walking straight toward the site with the confidence of men carrying a key they did not yet hold.

She let them get twenty metres down the moraine before she moved, and then she moved the other way, north, along the glacier's flank, one-armed, half-climbing and half-falling through the broken ground, putting distance between herself and the net with the blind mechanical persistence of an animal that knows only that stopping is death.

The light was failing by the time the ground gentled. The glacier had begun its evening complaints — deep reports from the icefall, like artillery in another war — and the lake at its toe had gone the colour of pewter. Marguerite found a hollow under an overhanging slab, out of the wind, and sat in it and finally, finally let herself shake.

Her wrist had swollen to the size of a grapefruit inside her sleeve. She splinted it with the axe handle she'd recovered from the scree — the axe head gone, the shaft intact, the mountain's idea of a joke — and two wraps of the rope, and she bit down on her own collar and did the work one-handed and did not make a sound, because sound carried, and she was alone, properly alone for the first time since the night run, and the aloneness was a country with no borders and no end.

*You lost it.* The thought arrived without mercy, in her own voice, in the voice she used for truths she couldn't dodge. *You had one thing to carry and you lost it. He was dying and he gave it to you and you dropped it down a mountain, and now Vane's men are walking to the escarpment with a head start and a net and guns, and the only person who might have stopped this is sitting in a hole in the rock with a broken wrist, talking to herself.*

She let it finish. Then she looked at her splinted hand, at the crude lashing, at the axe handle that had once been a tool and was now a prop, and she thought about Tomás in the camp below, telling his sister's story by firelight. She thought about Priya, precise and furious, photographing what should not exist. She thought about her father on the rope line in the dark, saying *I'm coming with you*, and meaning it, and her not knowing yet what to do with a man who meant a thing twenty years too late.

And she thought about Aldous Crane, gut-shot in a trapper's cabin, pressing a grease-stained paper into her hands with fingers that could barely close, and the look in his eyes — not gratitude, not hope, but *trust*, the terrible unconditional trust of a man spending his last currency on a stranger.

"He trusted the wrong person," she said aloud, to the hollow, to the glacier, to the dark coming down. Her voice cracked on it and she let it crack. "No." She shook her head, slow, deliberate, forcing the correction through her teeth like a splinter. "He trusted the right person. I'm still here."

The map was gone. The promise was not a piece of paper. The promise had never been a piece of paper. She had confused the object with the oath, and the mountain had just corrected her at the price of a wrist, and the correction was this: the route lived in her head, and the promise lived in her chest, and neither of them could be confiscated.

She would get to camp. She would find Edmund, or Priya, or Tomás — whoever the net had not closed over yet — and she would tell them everything, her name, her blood, the whole architecture of her lies, because the time for false skies was over. And then they would race Vane's men to the escarpment, and she would stand on the ledge where Crane had stood, and she would keep the promise with her voice and her testimony and her body if it came to that, because a promise made to a dying man in the wilderness was not a contract.

It was a debt the mountain itself had witnessed.

She ate half a ration bar, drank the meltwater seeping at the back of the hollow, and watched the first stars come out over the icefall. Somewhere below, a helicopter she could not hear was being fueled. Somewhere east, four men walked toward a site older than any nation, carrying crates and chains and the confidence of men who had never lost anything that mattered.

Marguerite pulled her jacket tight around the splinted wrist, rose out of the hollow, and turned toward camp.

She did not see, until she was halfway down the last slope in the full dark, the single light moving along the glacier's flank to meet her — a headlamp, steady, unhurried, swinging with a gait she knew better than her own heartbeat — and she stood on the slope with her broken hand against her chest and waited for her father to reach her, not knowing that he was carrying the one thing she had lost, and the one truth she had never guessed.
