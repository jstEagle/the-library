## 15. The Prospector's Second Gift

I had spent thirty years learning to read stone, and in all that time the mountain had never taught me the thing I needed most: how to be seen by my own daughter without flinching.

She stood on the slope above the glacier's flank with her broken wrist cradled against her chest, and the headlamp's beam caught her face in slices — the set of her jaw, which was her mother's; the way she held her weight back on her heels, ready to turn and run, which was mine. Snow came down between us in that fine, indifferent way snow has, settling on her shoulders, on mine, erasing the distinction between us one flake at a time.

In my left hand I carried her map. Crane's map. Grease pencil on waxed canvas, creased where she had creased it, torn where Vane's men had torn it from her in the gully — I had followed the signs of her descent the way I would follow any trail, reading scuffed stone and snapped heather, and had found the canvas snagged on a dwarf birch not twenty feet from where she had fallen, half-buried in the slide debris, its corner bloodied where her hand had gripped it.

In my right hand I carried something heavier. A truth I had kept for eleven years, and which had grown, in the keeping, into the exact shape of my ruin.

"Marguerite," I said.

She did not answer at once. The wind moved through the col above us with a sound like a door being drawn shut somewhere far off in the mountain's interior. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in the way that a blade laid flat on a table is level.

"You followed me."

"I followed the map." I held it up. "It followed me, really. It's been following me for a long time."

Her eyes went to the canvas, and I watched her understand — watched the arithmetic move through her face: the gully, the fall, the men, the loss, and now this. Her mouth opened and closed once. In the lamplight her breath came out white and was taken by the wind.

"You went down after it." Not a question. "Into the slide zone. Alone. At night."

"I've done stupider things," I said. "I once let a twelve-year-old girl believe her father had chosen a dig site over her. That was stupider."

The words landed the way I had known they would land, which is to say badly, and I did not look away to spare either of us. That was the discipline of it. I had spent eleven years practicing the art of not looking away from what I had done — in mirrors, in hotel windows in towns where no one knew my name, in the long blue hours before dawn in base camps on three continents. I was, whatever else could be said of me, very good at looking.

"Give me the map," she said.

"No."

Her whole body changed. I saw her weight shift, saw the calculation of the slope, the distance, her one good hand. God help me, I almost laughed — not from cruelty but from recognition. She was going to fight me for it. She was going to fight her father, with a shattered wrist, on a forty-degree slope, at midnight, in a storm, for a piece of waxed canvas.

"It's mine," she said. "He gave it to me. I made a promise."

"I know what you promised." I folded the map once, carefully, and did not put it away, but held it where she could see it. "I was there when Aldous Crane drew it. That's what I came to tell you. That's why I followed you down the mountain like a thief instead of walking into camp and saying it in front of the fire, where it should have been said years ago."

The wind gusted. Somewhere above us, deep in the icefall, the glacier spoke — a groan, long and tectonic, the sound of a thing too large to hurry deciding, all the same, to hurry. We both looked up. Old habit. You learn to listen to ice the way sailors listen to rigging, and she had learned it from me, in the years before, and I could see her listening now, cataloguing the note of it, filing it away.

"That note's early," she said quietly.

"I know."

"Calving's not due for nine days."

"It's not going to wait nine days." I turned the beam of my lamp toward the glacier's lower tongue, where the corridor ran — the lane of blue ice and lateral moraine that was our only way out, the way I had promised myself we would all go home by. "Look at the seracs on the east flank. They've leaned since Tuesday. The meltwater's running under the tongue at night — you can hear it if you put your ear to the ice. The whole corridor is a held breath."

She looked. She was silent for a long moment, and I let her be silent, because the silence was doing work that words could not.

"And Vane's helicopter lifts start in four," she said.

"Three. He moved the schedule this afternoon. He told the camp we'd had a weather window."

"He told the camp a lie."

"He tells the camp a great many lies. They've become very efficient." I took a breath, and the cold of it went all the way down, and I understood that there would not be a second chance to say what I had come to say. There never is. There is only the one breath, and what you do with it.

* * *

"Aldous Crane was my partner," I said.

She went very still. Not the stillness of surprise — I would learn later that she had already begun to suspect something of the kind — but the stillness of a person setting down something heavy in order to lift something heavier.

"Before Vane," I said. "Before the disgrace, before any of it. Aldous and I worked the northern ranges together for six years. He was the one who found the first trade bead on the Kettle moraine, back when I was still a graduate student with more ambition than sense. He couldn't read a stratigraphy to save his life, but he could read a mountain like other men read a newspaper. He found things because he paid attention to where the land itself was paying attention. Aldous used to say the ground remembers what people want forgotten, and all you have to do is notice where it's remembering."

"Then why," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she steadied it with an effort I could see cost her, "did I grow up thinking you worked alone? Why did every story you ever told me — the river country, the first surveys, all of it — why was there never an Aldous in any of them?"

"Because by the time you were old enough for the stories, Aldous and I had sworn never to speak of what we'd found. And because after Vane, I had sworn never to speak of Aldous at all." I looked down at the map in my hands. The grease-pencil lines were smeared now where the meltwater and her blood had touched them, and still legible, because grease pencil is a stubborn medium, made for marking things that must survive weather. "He drew this eleven years ago. Not last month. Eleven years ago, the spring after the inquiry, when the world had finished with me and I had finished with myself."

"Eleven years," she repeated. "He's had this for eleven years, and he waited until he was dying to send it up the mountain with a stranger."

"He wasn't waiting. He was hiding it. There's a difference." I turned the canvas over. On the reverse, faint as breath on glass, were more grease-pencil marks — not lines but numbers, a grid of them, and letters, and one small symbol like a trident that she would have recognized if she had ever turned the map over, which she had had no reason to do, because the front had told her everything she thought she needed to know. "The front of the map is a decoy, Marguerite. It's good — it's Aldous's work, it would get a person to the escarpment, eventually, by a hard and dangerous route, which was the point. It would keep out anyone who wasn't willing to suffer for it. But the true coordinates aren't drawn anywhere on it. They're keyed."

She leaned closer despite herself. I saw her good hand rise toward the canvas and stop, hovering, as if the numbers might burn.

"The numbers on the back mean nothing without the key," I said. "And the key was never written down. Aldous memorized it. And he gave it to one other person, thirty-one years ago, on a night very much like this one, on a ridge above a glacier, because we had found something up there that we agreed the world was not ready to have, and because one of us had to be able to find it again if the other one died."

The wind dropped. In the sudden hush the glacier's voice came through clean — that groan again, deeper now, and beneath it a ticking, like a clock, like ice under strain keeping its own time.

"You," she said.

"Me. Aldous Crane and I shared a key to the escarpment site before you were born. And when the inquiry destroyed me, and Vane came to me with his money and his patience and his leash, Aldous was the only man alive who knew what I had agreed to help him find. So he drew a map that would lead a good person to the site and a greedy person into the worst country on the mountain, and he kept it, and he waited, and he watched what I became." I made myself look at her. "And when he was gut-shot in a trapper's cabin with the end coming up his throat, he didn't send for me. He gave it to you. Because he judged, correctly, that you were the one of us who could still be trusted with it."

She stepped back. Not far — half a pace on the slope — but it was the farthest she had been from me in eleven years and the farthest I had ever been from anyone.

"You knew," she said. "When I walked into camp under my mother's name. You knew I had the map. You knew what Crane had done, and you said nothing, and you let me — you let me believe you didn't know me. You let me dig beside you. You watched me work and you said nothing for — how many days?"

"Nine."

"Nine days." She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "You recognized my digging style before you recognized my face. You told me that. On the trail. You stood there and told me that like it was a fond memory, and the whole time you knew I was carrying the thing you'd sold to Vane."

"I didn't know you had the map until the night you left camp," I said. "I knew Crane was dead — the runner brought word with the resupply. I didn't know what he'd done with the last of his life until I saw you slip the rope line, and I followed, and I found this in the gully, and I understood it all at once, the way you understand an avalanche after it's already moving." I held the map out between us, flat on my palm. "I have spent nine days not saying any of this. I have gotten very good, in my life, at not saying things. It's the one skill I have perfected. And I'm telling you now, on a slope, in a storm, because in three days the helicopters come and in nine the corridor closes, and if I die on this mountain with this still unsaid, then everything Aldous did — everything you did — was for nothing."

She didn't take the map. She looked at it, and then at me, and her eyes in the lamplight were her mother's eyes, and they were asking the only question that had ever mattered.

"Why did you do it?" she said. "The original time. With Vane. Why?"

And there it was. The door I had spent eleven years walking around. I had rehearsed a hundred answers in a hundred cold tents — the debts, the disgrace, the leverage, the fear — and every one of them was true and every one of them was a wall built to keep out the truest thing, which was small and shameful and simple.

"Because he asked me to look away," I said, "and looking away is the easiest thing in the world, and I had just learned that the world was willing to take everything from me, and I told myself that looking away from one crate, one shipment, one season, would buy me back the work. And then it was two crates. And then it was the whole site. You don't sell your integrity all at once, Marguerite. Nobody does. You rent it out by the day, and one morning you wake up and realize you can no longer afford to buy it back."

"And me?" Her voice was very quiet now, and very dangerous, the way deep water is quiet. "Where did I fit in the looking away?"

I had known this question was coming since the day she walked into camp with her mother's surname and her father's hands. I had thought I was ready for it. I had been wrong every time I'd rehearsed it, and I was wrong now, and I said it anyway, because the whole point of this night, of this slope, of the map bleeding grease and blood in my palm, was that I was done being wrong in the old way.

"I let you believe I chose the work over you," I said, "because the truth was worse. The truth was that Vane knew about you. Knew where you lived, what school, what name. He never said it as a threat. He never had to. He'd mention you, in passing, the way you'd mention weather. And I told myself that if you hated me — if you hated me cleanly, completely, with nothing left over — then you would never come looking. You would never follow me into his orbit. You would be safe from him in the one place he couldn't reach, which was inside your contempt." My voice went, then. I let it. "I made you the instrument of my cowardice and called it protection. I have no defense. I am not asking for one to be made for me."

The snow thickened between us. Above, the glacier groaned again, and this time there was a crack in the middle of it, sharp as a rifle shot, and both of us turned toward the icefall, and in the beam of the lamps we saw it — a serac on the east flank, one of the leaning towers, shed a shoulder of itself. It fell without sound at this distance, a white exhalation, and the report reached us four seconds later, flat and final.

"That's the second one this week," she said.

"The third."

She was silent. I watched her work, the way I had watched her work a hundred times over nine days and a thousand times in memory — methodically, from the evidence outward, never from the wish inward. It was the best thing about her, and I had given it to her, at least, if nothing else.

"Crane's key," she said at last. "If it's memorized, not drawn, then Vane can't have it. Even with the map."

"No. The map alone gets his crews to the decoy approach — the eastern moraine, the hard route. It will cost them days they don't have, and it will not get them to the site. That's why he needs me. That's why he's kept me on a leash for eleven years instead of simply discarding me. I'm not his archaeologist. I'm his key."

"Then the site is safe as long as you don't talk."

"The site is safe as long as I don't talk," I agreed. "And I am about to talk. To you. All of it. The key, the coordinates, the approach, everything Aldous gave me and everything I gave Vane — the ledger of my whole disgrace, laid out, so that you can use it. Against him. Against me, if it comes to that. I'll testify. I'll sign anything. I'll stand up in whatever room they put me in and say it all out loud." I turned the map over one more time and held it out, and this time I did not hold it out to give it to her. I held it out to show her the numbers, the trident, the key that was not a key. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking you to come back to me, or to call me anything but Yarrow, or to stand next to me at the end of this. I am asking for one thing, and it is the only thing I have any right to ask."

"Which is?"

"A chance to fix it. Not to fix us. There may be no fixing us, and I'll deserve whatever that turns out to be. But the site — Aldous's site, the longhouse, the carvings, the people whose history Vane intends to sell by the pound — that can still be fixed. With your route knowledge and my key and whatever Nair and Reyes are worth, which I suspect is a great deal. We have three days before the helicopters and maybe four before the corridor closes, and I am asking you to spend them with me, not because I'm your father, but because I'm the only man on this mountain who can open that door."

She looked at the map for a long time. The snow gathered on her shoulders and did not melt, and I understood that she was cold through, that her wrist was bad, that she had been falling and running and hiding for the better part of two days, and that she was still standing here listening to me, which was more than I had any right to expect and everything I had no right to hope for.

"If I do this," she said finally, "you follow my lead on the mountain. Not the other way around. Your key opens the door; my routes get us there and back. And when this is over — if we're all alive, and the site is documented, and Vane is finished — you tell the inquiry everything, first, before I say a word, so that it's your name on the confession and not mine on the accusation."

"Yes," I said.

"And you never again decide what I'm strong enough to know."

The old wound opened in me, clean and deep, and I let it bleed. "I promise," I said, and for the first time in eleven years the words did not taste of ash.

She took the map from my palm. Her good hand closed over it, over the bloodied corner where her own blood was, and she folded it with her teeth and her fingers and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where it had been before, and I understood that she had just decided something, and that I would not learn what it was until it was too late to change it, and that this, too, was my inheritance.

Then her head came up, and she was looking past me, down the glacier's flank toward the black line of the moraine, and her whole body went rigid.

"Father," she said — the first time she had said the word in eleven years, and no warmth in it, only urgency — "the camp lights. Count them."

I turned. Far below, strung along the bench above base camp, the tents made their small constellation of yellow. But at the eastern edge of the camp, where the supply cache stood, where the fuel drums stood, there was a light moving. Two lights. Men with lamps, moving fast, toward the helicopter pad.

And on the pad itself, out of schedule, out of any weather window, a machine was warming up, its rotor blades catching the lamplight in a slow, silver blur.

"He's lifting tonight," I said. "He's not waiting for the crates. He's going for the site now — with the decoy map and every man he has."

Above us, the glacier answered, a long rolling detonation from the icefall, and the first snow-slurry hissed down the flank a hundred meters to our left, and Marguerite was already turning uphill, into the storm, her broken hand pressed to the map over her heart.

"Then we run," she said.
