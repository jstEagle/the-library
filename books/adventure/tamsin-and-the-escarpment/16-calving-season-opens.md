## 16. Calving Season Opens

Priya Nair had learned to read ice the way other people read weather, and the glacier was shouting.

She stood at the edge of camp before dawn, breath crystallizing in the grey light, and listened to the Kettle speak. It was not the deep, patient groaning she'd catalogued over three weeks of survey work — the sound of a river of ice settling into its ancient habits, groaning like an old house in wind. This was different. This was a series of sharp cracks, percussive, arriving out of the white silence like gunshots from a war being fought somewhere below the surface. Between them came a new sound entirely: a hissing rush, water moving where no water should be moving yet, meltwater finding channels through the glacier's guts weeks ahead of any season she'd ever recorded.

The calving had begun early.

She knew what that meant. She'd spent her first night on this expedition with a thermos of terrible coffee and Tomás's battered copy of a glaciology monograph, teaching herself the vocabulary of the corridor they'd have to leave by. The eastern tongue of the Kettle terminated in a face above a proglacial lake, and every summer it shed itself into that lake in thunderous slabs — seracs the size of apartment buildings, collapsing without warning, sending waves across the water that could capsize a crossing party or simply erase the low route along the moraine. The corridor between the icefall and the lake wall was passable for perhaps six more days. Maybe four. The meltwater she could hear now was the glacier opening its own veins.

She turned back toward camp and saw Vane already awake, standing outside his command tent in a quilted jacket that probably cost more than her annual grant money, watching the ice face with binoculars. Not with alarm. With impatience.

That was the moment Priya understood the shape of the next few days, and felt the cold go through her that had nothing to do with altitude.

* * *

The manifests had told her first, weeks ago. Crates rated for two hundred kilograms when nothing in a legitimate dig needed crates rated for anything — you wrapped artifacts in fleece and carried them down on human backs, carefully, because care was the whole point. Chains. A fuel cache marked on Vane's private map with coordinates that matched nowhere near the survey grid. And now, this morning, the helicopter.

It came up the valley at seven-forty, a silver-and-black machine riding the katabatic wind off the ice, and set down on the flattened pad Vane's advance crew had blasted out of the moraine with charges nobody had authorized for anything but "route preparation." The rotors threw grit in a hundred-meter circle and everyone turned their backs and covered their faces, and when the wash died down, Vane was standing there smiling like a man greeting a taxi.

Priya watched from the survey tent's doorway, pretending to check core samples, and counted.

Four lifts planned today. Four tomorrow. Six the day after, weather permitting. The pilot — a lean, sun-weathered man who spoke to Vane with the easy familiarity of long employment — walked the pad, kicked a stone, pronounced it acceptable. Vane clapped him on the shoulder.

"Load's ready at the site," Vane said, loud enough to carry. "Crates eleven through nineteen. Mind the balance sheet, Gerald, I want us heavy on the return legs."

*Crates.* Not samples. Not documentation. Crates eleven through nineteen, numbered, chained, waiting on the escarpment ledge where a thousand years ago people had carved their lives into living stone so that someone, someday, would know they had been here.

Priya wrote *Gerald* in the margin of her notebook, underlined it twice, and went to find Marguerite.

* * *

She found her at the cook tent, which had become, over the past weeks, the unofficial heart of whatever this expedition actually was. Tomás was frying something that smelled miraculous given the ingredients available at four thousand meters, and Marguerite sat hunched over the map — Crane's map, or what remained of it after the gully took the original and Edmund's memory gave it back. Her left wrist was splinted and strapped against her chest, and she was annotating with her right hand alone, awkward as a child learning to write again.

Priya slid onto the bench beside her and said, quietly, "He's flying them out."

Marguerite didn't look up. "I know. I heard the rotors."

"Not just today. He's got a schedule. Gerald says Vane wants everything off the mountain before—" she stopped herself, glanced at Tomás, who was very deliberately stirring a pot and listening with both ears, "—before the window closes."

"The window doesn't close," Marguerite said. "The window *collapses*. There's a difference, and he either doesn't know it or doesn't care."

"He knows." Priya kept her voice flat. "I've seen his notes. He has the calving projections. He's gambling that he can beat the ice."

Tomás set down his spoon. "And if he loses?"

"If he loses, the last lift goes into the lake with the seracs, and he'll have spent other people's lives buying cargo." Priya looked at the two of them, at the small conspiracy they had become around this table without ever formally agreeing to it. "He's already spent them. Every hour those crates sit on the ledge is an hour his crew spends under the ice face hauling them to the pad. You've seen the men he hired. They don't ask questions. They don't know enough to ask questions."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. Outside, faintly, the glacier cracked again — a report like a rifle, then the long rolling sigh of something enormous shifting its weight.

"There's a fuel cache," Priya said finally. "North lateral moraine, under a blue tarp, camouflaged. Jet-A in drums, plus the auxiliary pump and filters. It's how he keeps the helicopter cycling without flying drums up from the valley strip every run. Without it, Gerald can make maybe two round trips a day instead of five, and he has to stage fuel from below, which means weather delays, which means—"

"Which means the math stops working," Marguerite said. "The schedule slips past the corridor."

"Yes."

"And if the schedule slips far enough, Vane can't get the crates out at all, and he has to choose between the cargo and his own skin."

Tomás wiped his hands slowly on his apron. "You're talking about burning his fuel."

"I'm talking about contaminating it," Priya said. "Water in the lines. Grit in the filters. Something that looks like failure, not sabotage. Something a maintenance inspection would blame on bad storage at altitude." She had thought about this for two nights, lying in her tent listening to the ice crack, running scenarios the way she used to run lab protocols — step one, step two, contingency. "A cup of fine sediment in each drum's filler neck. The pump draws it up on the first cycle, the engine ingests it, Gerald grounds himself rather than fly dirty. Nobody dies. Nobody gets hurt. We buy four days, maybe five, and by then the corridor is gone and the only way off this mountain is the way we came in — on foot, together, with nothing but what we can carry."

"And Vane?" Marguerite asked.

"Vane rages. Vane suspects. But suspicion isn't proof, and if it's done clean, there's no proof to find." Priya heard her own voice steadying itself into the register she used for defending dissertation chapters, and hated how much courage it was costing her to keep it there. "I can do it tonight. The cache is forty minutes from camp along the moraine. I've mapped it. I know the watch rotations — I made the watch rotations, that's the joke of it, I built the schedule myself three weeks ago when I still believed this was a survey."

Marguerite reached over and put her good hand flat on Priya's forearm. Her grip was stronger than the splint suggested.

"You don't have to do this alone."

"I do, though." Priya said it gently, and meant it as mercy. "If two of us go missing from camp on the same night, that's a pattern. If one geologist takes a walk to check a seismometer she happens to have installed near the north moraine — well. Geologists wander. It's practically our defining feature." She almost smiled. "Besides. If it goes wrong, someone has to be clean enough to finish it. To get the documentation out. The photographs, the manifest copies, the recordings of Vane's own mouth telling Gerald to fly heavy. That was always the real weapon. The fuel is just the delay that gives the weapon time to work."

Tomás said nothing, but he pressed a tin mug of coffee into her hands, black and sweet and scalding, the way she liked it, and that was its own kind of signature on the plan.

* * *

She waited until 23:40, when the watch changed and the new sentry — a broad, incurious man named Dekker whose primary talent was falling asleep upright — settled into his chair by the fire. The sky had clouded over, which was a gift; a high overcast diffused the moon to nothing, and the temperature had dropped hard enough that the snow crust would hold her weight without crampons if she stepped carefully.

She told herself she wasn't afraid. She inventoried the fear anyway, the way her mother had taught her — name it and it becomes data. Fear of the dark moraine, minimal, she'd crossed worse in Ladakh. Fear of the ice sounds, moderate, irrational, the glacier couldn't reach her here. Fear of Dekker waking, low. Fear of Vane, absolute, total, the true number, the one she kept trying not to look at directly.

Because she had seen what Corliss Vane did to obstacles. She'd read the clipping Marguerite showed her, the one about the Yukon claim and the Cree family whose trapline had mysteriously burned the month after they refused to sell. She'd heard him at dinner, charming the room, and caught the half-second lag between his smile arriving and his eyes agreeing to it. She knew exactly what she was.

She went anyway.

The moraine at night was a country of loose stone and buried bones of rock, and she moved through it the way she imagined the old traders had moved through it, by feel and patience, letting her boots find the stable ground before committing her weight. Twice the glacier fired its distant guns and twice she stopped, crouched among boulders, and let the echoes roll over her like weather. Once something else happened — a longer sound, a groan that climbed in pitch until it became almost musical, and then a crash she felt through the soles of her boots, and far off toward the terminus, a white plume rising against the black sky where a piece of the ice face had let go into the lake.

Early. Ahead of every projection. The corridor was dying faster than anyone's math.

She found the cache exactly where Vane's private map promised: a hollow behind a glacial erratic, floored with pallets, draped in a blue tarp weighted with stones. Eight drums of Jet-A, the transfer pump in its case, a crate of filters, a toolbox. She worked by red light, headlamp hooded, hands slow and deliberate in the cold until they stopped being her hands and became instruments.

Water first — melted snow, poured in a thin stream through each filler neck, half a liter per drum, enough to pool at the bottom where the pickup tubes drank. Then the sediment: she'd brought it from the stream below the survey line, sieved fine as flour, and she dusted it into each neck and stirred the top inch with a length of sterile tubing so it would look like drift contamination, the natural filth of a badly maintained cache. The filters she left sealed but swapped two of them with units she'd deliberately scored inside — hairline cuts in the element paper that would pass a visual check and fail under pressure. Last, she loosened the pump's intake fitting a quarter turn, just enough to suck air, just enough to make the whole system behave erratically in ways no single fault could explain.

An hour. She gave herself permission to take ninety minutes and finished in seventy-five, and stood back in the dark looking at eight drums that now carried, invisibly, the power to stop a helicopter.

That was when she heard the boot.

Not close. Forty meters, maybe fifty, on the moraine trail — the particular rhythm of a man walking with purpose and a rifle carried casually, which is to say not casually at all. She killed her light and folded herself down behind the erratic, into the cold shadow between stone and tarp, and made herself small, and made herself breathe.

Two men. She could hear them now, voices carrying in the thin air, conversational, unhurried. Coming to the cache. Coming *to the cache*, at midnight, on the same night she had chosen, out of all the nights—

No. Not coincidence. She understood it even as her stomach dropped: Vane checked his assets. Randomly, unpredictably, the way a man checks a thing when he's spent his life surrounded by people with reasons to steal from him. She had rolled dice against a man who loaded them.

The boots came closer. Light bloomed — an unhooded flashlight sweeping the tarp, the stones, the ground. She pressed her cheek against frozen rock and watched the beam swing past her boot toe by a hand's width.

One of them knelt at the nearest drum. She heard the filler cap turn. Heard him dip something — a stick, a gauge — and draw it out, and hold it up to the light.

"Clean," the man said, in the tone of a report. "All eight?"

"All eight," said the other, farther off, already turning away.

The cap screwed back down. The light swept once more, lingered a half-second on the tarp's edge where her knee had brushed a stone out of alignment — a stone now sitting wrong, a stone she hadn't noticed disturbing, a stone that was going to kill her — and then swung away, and the boots receded down the trail, and the darkness closed back over the moraine like water over a drowned thing.

She lay there for ten minutes, counting her own heartbeats, before she allowed herself to move. Before she allowed herself to think about the stone.

She fixed the stone. Reset it precisely, photographing it first with the red light so she'd remember its original angle. Then she packed her tools, erased every trace she could find — footprints brushed with a pine bough, fibers from her sleeve collected with tweezers, breath-melt on the drum caps fanned dry — and began the walk back to camp with the specific, humming clarity of a person who has just learned the exact thickness of the ice she's standing on.

* * *

She was two hundred meters from the tents when the searchlight hit her.

It came from the direction of the helicopter pad — a handheld unit, brutally bright, pinning her to the moraine like a specimen to a board. Behind the light, silhouettes. Three of them. Four. Rifles slung, not raised, which somehow was worse, because rifles raised would at least have been honest.

"Priya Nair." Vane's voice, from behind the light, warm as ever, which was worst of all. "Out for a walk?"

"I'm checking my seismometer," she said, and her voice held, and she marveled at that even as she heard it. "Station N-2. On the north moraine. I logged it in the survey plan—"

"You did." The light lowered slightly, and she could see his face now, and the smile was in place, and the eyes were doing the lagging thing they did. "You're thorough, Dr. Nair. That's one of the things I admire about you. Here's another thing I admire: you came back. From your station. At one in the morning. Past the helicopter pad." He tilted his head. "Rather than approaching camp from the east trail, which is shorter, which is what your own route card says."

She had forgotten about the route card. She had written the route card. She had written it three weeks ago, in another life, believing in the fiction she'd been hired to inhabit, and she had forgotten it tonight because fear narrows a person to a tunnel and her tunnel had pointed home.

"Seismic stations aren't always where you want them relative to trails," she said.

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "They're wherever the science needs them. That's the beautiful thing about science. It's so difficult to argue with." He stepped closer, out of his own light's glare, and now she could see the two men flanking him — Dekker, awake after all, and beside him a hard-faced man she'd never been introduced to, whose presence answered a question she'd been asking for weeks. "Walk with me, Dr. Nair. There's some equipment I'd like you to look at. In my tent. It seems to have developed a fault overnight — radio transmission on frequencies it shouldn't be using. Very strange. Almost as if someone were sending signals off this mountain." He smiled at her, and for the first time since Ladakh, since the beginning, Priya saw him stop performing.

"Unless," he said softly, "you'd like to tell me who you've been talking to."

Behind him, far off across the dark valley, the Kettle Glacier fired another shot into the night, and another, a drumroll now, continuous, the ice face shedding itself into the lake in pieces too many and too fast to count — and Priya Nair, standing in the searchlight with her hands open at her sides, understood that the corridor was closing, and the crates were still on the ledge, and whatever happened next would happen without her.

She lifted her chin and said nothing at all.
