## 17. The Longhouse Stand

The dawn came up grey and slow, the way it does above the Kettle, light spilling over the escarpment's shoulder like water finding a crack in a dam. Marguerite lay on her belly at the lip of the moraine ridge, her broken wrist strapped tight against her chest beneath her parka, and watched the camp below wake itself into motion. Four of Vane's men moved between the tents with the loose, bored confidence of men who believed no one was coming for them. The fifth — the one called Hessler, wide and deliberate, with a rifle slung across his back like a tool rather than a weapon — stood at the edge of the longhouse trench, smoking, his breath pluming white in the cold.

Beside her in the shadow of a granite boulder, Edmund Yarrow crouched with a pair of binoculars pressed to his eyes. He had not spoken in twenty minutes. His hands, she noticed, were shaking — not from the cold.

"They're loading the last of the crates at first light," he said finally, his voice low and scraped raw. "Hessler wants them staged at the landing zone before the helicopter comes up. That gives us maybe an hour. Maybe less."

"Then we have an hour," Marguerite said. "Maybe less."

She felt him flinch at the echo of her own words thrown back at him — the same flat arithmetic he'd used on her all those years ago, when she'd asked why he had to go, when he'd told her that expeditions ran on schedules and families didn't. She hadn't meant it as a wound. Or perhaps she had. With fathers, it was hard to know what you meant until it landed.

Tomás Reyes lay flat on her other side, breathing hard, his face still grey from three days in Vane's improvised holding tent. They had gotten to him an hour before dawn — Marguerite cutting the guy-lines while Edmund talked the night watchman into a stupor of boredom and bad coffee — and Tomás had come out of that canvas prison moving like a man who had decided fear was a luxury he could no longer afford. His wrists bore the red welts of zip ties. His eyes held something harder than anger.

"They took my knife," he said now, for the third time since the rescue, as though the knife were the sum of the injury. "And Priya's camera bag. And—" He stopped himself. "They did not take what I know."

"No," Marguerite said. "They didn't."

Because Tomás knew the ground. That was the thing Vane's men had never understood about the people they'd detained — that a cook who had fed trail crews for fifteen years carried the terrain in his body the way a surveyor carried it in his notebooks. Tomás knew which gullies drained toward the glacier and which drained toward the scree. He knew where the meltwater ran loud enough to cover footsteps. He knew, better than any of them, that the eastern approach to the longhouse site funneled through a single defile no wider than a truck — a defile Vane's advance party had walked through carelessly, twice a day, for a week, because nothing had ever threatened them there.

"Here is what we have," Marguerite said, and she heard her own voice take on the clipped cadence of her father's, and hated it, and used it anyway. "Four armed men below, one rifle between them that I've seen. Hessler is competent; the others are hired muscle. They expect trouble from the mountain, not from us. The helicopter is due at midday — Vane radioed the schedule before Priya cut the antenna, so they'll be watching the sky, not the ground. And the site itself—" she tapped the moraine with two fingers, "—the site itself is ours. Every rock, every drainage, every fall-line. We've been walking this ground for two weeks. They've been guarding it for five days."

Edmund lowered the binoculars. For a moment he looked at her — really looked, the way he used to look at a stratigraphy profile when the layers told him something he hadn't expected — and she saw something move behind his eyes that might have been pride, or grief, or both wearing the same coat.

"You sound like your mother," he said quietly.

"My mother never sounded like you."

It was cruel, and she knew it, and she said it anyway, because the hour was too short and the stakes too high for tenderness, and because some part of her understood — had understood since the rope line, since the night run, since the rotten gully where her wrist had broken and her map had fallen — that this day would not leave room for anything unresolved between them. Whatever they were to each other after dawn, they would be it fully.

"We go down through the western drainage," Edmund said, and the professional in him reasserted itself like a spine straightening. "The meltwater channel runs shallow until it hits the talus. It'll mask our approach to within eighty meters of the trench. Then we split — Tomás takes the high ground above the defile with the signal mirror and the air horn from the med kit. Marguerite, you and I come up through the trench spoil heaps. If Hessler posts a man at the LZ, Tomás pulls him with noise. If he doesn't—"

"If he doesn't, we're standing in their camp before they've finished their coffee," Marguerite finished.

* * *

They went down through the water.

That was the part Marguerite would remember afterward, in the years when the whole expedition compressed itself into a handful of images: the three of them wading the meltwater channel in the blue half-light, boots filling, the current shoving at their calves with a force that seemed absurd for water two inches deep, the roar of it swallowing every footfall. The cold climbed her legs like something alive. Her broken wrist ached inside its splint with a dull, rhythmic insistence, a pulse beneath the pulse. Ahead of her, Edmund moved with a sureness that surprised her — the old field man surfacing through the ruined years, reading the channel bed the way he'd once taught her to read it, choosing each foothold by the color of the stone, avoiding the algae-slicked granite without appearing to think about it.

*That's how you know where the water runs in June,* he'd told her once, crouched beside a creek in some other lifetime, some other range. *Not by looking at the water. By looking at what the water has polished.*

She had been nine years old. She had forgotten everything else about that summer except this.

At the mouth of the defile they stopped, pressed against the canyon wall where the spray had built a rind of ice, and waited for Tomás to climb away above them. They heard him moving — a scrabble of boot edges on scree, then silence, then nothing, which was the best possible sign. A man unseen makes no sound. A man making no sound is either dead or doing his job.

Then Edmund touched her shoulder, once, and they came up out of the water into the spoil heaps behind the longhouse trench, and the world changed.

The site opened around them like a held breath. Even now, even with armed men fifty meters away and a helicopter hours out, the place reached up and took hold of her the way it had the first time, on the ledge, in the chapter of this story that belonged to wonder instead of war. The carved threshold stones caught the early light and threw it back in low relief — spirals, ship-forms, figures with elongated hands raised toward a sun-disc that archaeology had no name for. The trench cuts ran through midden and floor-lens and posthole, the ghost-geometry of a longhouse that had stood here four hundred years before anyone had thought to write this coast onto a map. Trade beads from a continent away. Smelted copper. A culture that had read the seasons off this glacier and built its calendar into stone.

Her father had spent his life trying to prove such places existed. And then he had spent his ruin helping a man who wanted to strip-mine one for auction.

Marguerite pushed the thought down and moved.

* * *

Hessler had posted one man at the landing zone — a big, sleepy kid with a shotgun and a thermos — and Tomás pulled him exactly as planned. The air horn went off above the defile at 5:40, one long blast, and the kid went up the slope at a jog with his shotgun unslung and his coffee still steaming on a crate lid, chasing a noise that Tomás was already relocating two hundred meters further along the ridge. By the time the kid realized the horn had stopped, Tomás had circled behind him and was herding him gently, relentlessly, away from the LZ and toward the far end of the moraine, the way a sheepdog moves a flock — never threatening, always present, always just outside the range where a frightened man with a gun might do something irreversible.

Which left three.

Marguerite and Edmund came up through the trench spoil with their hands empty and their voices ready, and that was the gamble — the whole mad architecture of it rested on the fact that Vane's crew were guards, not soldiers, men paid to watch crates and intimidate cooks, men whose violence was calibrated for the unarmed. You did not beat them with force. You beat them with the one weapon they had no answer for: the truth, arriving faster than they could contain it.

They stepped out of the trench at the exact center of the site, side by side, in full view of the three remaining men, and Marguerite felt the strangest sensation of her life — the feeling of standing on ground she loved, in front of men with guns, and being entirely calm.

"Hessler!" Edmund called, and his voice carried across the trench the way a professor's voice carries across a lecture hall, pitched to fill space without shouting. "Aldous Crane sends his regards."

Everything stopped. The two younger men froze with crate-straps half-looped. Hessler turned slowly, cigarette stubbed out under his heel, and looked at the two figures standing in the middle of his work site, and Marguerite watched his face perform the small, rapid arithmetic of a man deciding whether this was a problem or an inconvenience.

"Dr. Yarrow." Hessler's voice was flat. "Vane said you'd gone soft. He didn't say you'd gone insane."

"Vane says many things," Edmund said. "Most of them are lies. This morning I intend to stop repeating them."

He raised the recorder.

It was Tomás's idea originally — no. It was Priya's, passed along in the coded note the supply runner had smuggled out of camp before the detentions began: *If you can't stop them, document them.* Marguerite had carried the little machine against her chest for two days, wrapped in a wool sock, its batteries warmed by her own body heat. Now Edmund held it up in the grey morning light, its red indicator steady, recording, and everything about the gesture was wrong for a battlefield and right for a reckoning.

"What is that?" Hessler asked.

"A confession," Edmund said. "Mine."

And then, with three armed men watching and his daughter standing beside him and the carved threshold stones at his back, Edmund Yarrow did the thing he had spent eleven years failing to do: he told the truth out loud, on the record, in front of witnesses, with nothing held back and nowhere left to hide.

"My name is Edmund Yarrow. In 2019 I was disgraced — rightly — for fabricating survey data. Corliss Vane bought my debts and bought me with them. He financed this expedition on the condition that I locate saleable artifacts and look away while his crews removed them. I agreed. I told myself it was salvage, that the site was doomed anyway, that the science would survive the sale. None of that was true. I let his advance party arm themselves and move ahead of any legitimate survey. I let them detain my colleagues. I let my daughter believe she was abandoned because telling her the truth would have given Vane leverage over both of us. Everything Corliss Vane intends to do to this site, he does with my knowledge and under my name. I am recording this statement freely, and I will sign it, and I will repeat it under oath to any inquiry that will have me."

The silence afterward had weight. It pressed down on the trench like snow-load.

One of the young guards — the one called Ferro, barely twenty, with a pimpled jaw and a rifle he clearly resented carrying — lowered it halfway and did not raise it again. Marguerite saw the moment land on him: the moment a hired man realizes the man who signed his paychecks has just testified against the paycheck. Hessler saw it too. That was the real battle, and it lasted four seconds, and no shot was fired.

"You understand," Hessler said carefully, "that none of that changes what happens at midday. The helicopter comes. The crates go."

"The helicopter comes," Edmund agreed. "But the crates stay, and here is why—"

"That's enough." Hessler's voice hardened. He raised his rifle — not aimed, but leveled, a warning posture, the grammar of a man who preferred compliance to bloodshed. "Both of you step out of the trench. Now."

"He will shoot," Marguerite said to Edmund, conversationally, as though discussing weather. "Or he won't. Either way, the recording exists. It's already backed up — Tomás has the duplicate stashed in the supply cache at the scree line, and Priya's photographs are in a second cache Vane hasn't found. You can shoot us, Hessler. You cannot shoot the truth fast enough."

It was a bluff, half of it. The duplicate cache was real — Tomás had made it during his detention, hiding materials in the food stores where no one searches a cook's domain — but the second cache was imagined, and Marguerite delivered the lie with the steady eye of a woman who had learned bluffing from the mountains themselves, where everything pretends to be more solid than it is.

Hessler hesitated. And in that hesitation, the morning tilted.

Because that was when Tomás came down off the ridge.

He did not come down quietly. He came down loudly, deliberately, crashing through the scree on the eastern slope with the air horn sounding again and again, and behind him — visible now against the skyline, unmistakable — came the sleepy guard with the shotgun, walking beside him, unarmed, unharmed, and visibly no longer interested in being part of whatever this was. The kid set the shotgun down on a boulder and sat down next to it and put his head in his hands, and that image — more than any argument, more than any confession — broke the crew's cohesion like ice going out of a river.

Ferro shouldered his rifle and stepped back from the crates. The fourth man, seeing the guard sit down, seeing Ferro step back, seeing a professor and a guide and a cook standing together on the trench lip with the morning rising behind them — the fourth man simply walked away, downhill, toward the lake, and kept walking.

Which left Hessler alone with a rifle, a helicopter schedule, and a decision.

He looked at the recorder. He looked at Edmund. He looked, last and longest, at Marguerite — and something in her stance, something in the way she stood slightly forward of her father with her splinted wrist cradled like a shield, made him recalculate whatever he had calculated about her before.

"Vane will come himself," Hessler said. "You understand that. When the helicopter lands and the crates aren't on it, Vane comes up the mountain, and he doesn't send cooks to talk."

"Good," Marguerite said. "I'd like to see him."

"You'll see him sooner than you'd like." Hessler unslung his rifle and laid it on the crate lid, next to the cold coffee, in the universal gesture of a man surrendering an argument rather than a fight. "Midday. The LZ. Bring your recording. Bring your nerve."

He walked down toward the camp tents, and the morning, incredibly, was theirs.

* * *

They stood afterward among the threshold stones, the three of them plus a guard sitting on a boulder nursing his head, and the light came up gold across the carvings, and for a few minutes nobody spoke, because there are moments the voice would only diminish.

Edmund set the recorder down on the trench ladder, gently, the way a man sets down something fragile and irreplaceable. Then he crouched at the edge of the excavation and put his bare hand — bare, in that cold, because some things must be touched skin to stone — flat against the carved spiral of the threshold.

"I spent thirty years arguing this culture existed," he said, so quietly Marguerite had to lean close to hear. "Thirty years, and every journal laughed, and every grant died, and then I lied my way into disgrace and let a thief buy the ruins of my name. And the whole time, the proof was here, waiting, patient as stone always is. It didn't need me. It never needed me. It only needed someone to refuse to sell it."

"You refused," Marguerite said. "Eleven years late. But today, on the record, with witnesses." She paused. "That counts, Dad. I need you to hear me say that it counts."

He looked up at her then, and his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them, because a field man learns there is no shame in weather.

"It counts," he said. "And it isn't enough. Because Vane is coming, and he doesn't negotiate with recordings. He negotiates with leverage, and money, and men like Hessler who haven't yet decided which side of history pays better." He stood, joints cracking, and turned to face the south, where the glacier lay white and enormous under the strengthening sun. "We have until midday. We should spend it getting Priya free, fortifying the caches, and deciding what we do when a helicopter lands carrying a man who thinks everything on this mountain is for sale."

Tomás was already moving — checking the cache, counting the food, doing the quiet logistics of a man who has decided that survival is a craft. Marguerite stayed by the threshold a moment longer, her splinted hand resting above the carved sun-disc, feeling the old cold of the stone rise into her like a message.

Below them, far down the valley, the Kettle Glacier gave a sound like a cannon.

Then another.

Then a long, rolling series of them, echoing off the escarpment walls, and the serac field at the glacier's throat — the corridor, the only way out — shrugged and shed its first tower of the season, and the ice-dust rose in a white plume against the morning sky.

Marguerite straightened. The calving had begun — days early, just as Priya had warned, just as Crane's map had promised it would.

Midday was coming. So was Vane. And now the mountain itself had entered the negotiation.
