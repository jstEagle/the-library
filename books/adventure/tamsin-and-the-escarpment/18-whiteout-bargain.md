## 18. Whiteout Bargain

The storm came down the escarpment like a held breath finally released.

Marguerite felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in her ears, a taste of iron in the wind, the light going strange and flat across the snowfield as though someone had turned a dimmer on the world. She had been raised on mountains that lied about their moods, and she knew this one was done negotiating with weather.

"Whiteout inside the hour," she called to Edmund, who stood at the lip of the longhouse excavation with his hands still buried to the wrists in the soil he'd refused to leave. "Maybe less."

He looked up at the sky, then at the crates.

Four of them now — Vane's men had managed two lifts before Priya's sabotage grounded the helicopter for good, and the remaining carvings sat lashed to cargo sleds near the ledge's eastern edge, wrapped in tarps that snapped and cracked like whips in the rising wind. Two of Vane's crew stood guard over them, rifles slung but hands never far from the stocks. The other two had gone down-slope after Tomás when he'd slipped his bonds in the night, and had not come back.

"Those sleds aren't going anywhere," Edmund said. He said it carefully, the way a man says a thing he wants to believe.

"Neither are we," Marguerite answered, "if we're still standing here arguing about them when it hits."

* * *

It hit the way an avalanche hits — not gradually at all, but all at once, the horizon simply ceasing to exist.

One moment there was the grey smear of the glacier below and the black teeth of the serac field; the next there was nothing but white, white moving, white with weight behind it, wind-driven snow that stung like flung gravel and erased the difference between ground and sky so completely that Marguerite, roped to Edmund with ten metres of line between them, could not see her own boots unless she looked straight down.

They crawled to the overhang above the longhouse threshold — the carved lintel, the spiral-and-fish motif she had touched with bare fingers two days ago and felt something move in her chest that she had no name for — and they waited out the worst of it in the hollow of stone while the storm screamed past the ledge mouth like a freight train that never finished passing.

Edmund rigged a shelter half from the tarp he'd cut free of one of the abandoned sleds. His hands were steady. His hands had always been steady; it was everything else about him that shook.

"You should have taken the descent route yesterday," he said, shouting under the wind. "Before the corridor started closing. You and Tomás."

"And left you here?"

"I'm the one who signed the arrangement. I'm the one who owes the debt." He wouldn't look at her. Snow-light came through the tarp and made his face the colour of old paper. "You don't owe me a rescue, Marguerite. You owe Crane a delivery, and you've made it. Everything else is interest on my account, not yours."

She almost laughed. It came out of her as something between a breath and a wound.

"Do you know what I thought, all those years? That you'd found something better than us. A dig that mattered more. A woman, maybe. A life where nobody asked you to explain yourself at dinner parties." She pulled her mittened hand through the space between them, not quite touching him. "I never once thought you were protecting me. Because protecting someone means staying, Dad. Protecting someone means being boring and present and answering their letters."

"I wrote—"

"You sent money. Money isn't letters."

The wind took the silence and stretched it. Somewhere below them, muffled by a hundred metres of screaming air, the glacier fired another shot into its own throat.

Then, through the white, a voice.

* * *

It came first as a shape — a dark suggestion against the whiteout, wavering, wrong, like a hole in the storm — and then as a man on a rope, dragging one leg, half-carried by the last of Vane's crew who was still ambulatory. They came out of the murmurless roar at the edge of the overhang and Marguerite had the rifle up off her shoulder before her mind caught up with her hands, because that was what the last three days had taught her body to do.

"It's me," the shape rasped. "For God's sake, Yarrow, it's me."

Corliss Vane had been a handsome man in the way that glaciers are beautiful — from a distance, and on his own terms. Up close, in the flat white light, with the blood frozen black along his temple and his left leg hanging at an angle the leg had not been designed for, he looked like what he was: sixty years old, out of his depth, and furious about it.

"The icefall," he said, letting himself be lowered against the rock. His voice was still smooth. That was the terrible thing about him; even now, even bleeding, the voice ran like oil. "Took two of my people. Took the forward cache. My pilot says the corridor has maybe thirty-six hours before it's a chimney full of falling ice, and the helicopter is dead until the fuel lines thaw, which they won't, because your geologist put water in the lines and sand in the filters, and I would shake her hand if I could find her."

"She's alive?" Marguerite kept the rifle level. "Tomás?"

"Your cook is hiding somewhere on this mountain with more sense than all of us combined. And yes, the geologist is alive — she was helping pull my man Kessler out of the crevasse when the second serac went. She's got a broken arm and a conscience, apparently, because she's been treating my injured whether they deserve it or not." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; Marguerite watched the pain go through him like a fishhook. "We are all of us, Ms. Reyes — or should I say Ms. Yarrow? — in the same boat now. And the boat is on fire."

The name landed like a slap. Edmund stiffened beside her.

"You knew," Edmund said quietly.

"I've known since Fort Nelson. Do you think I fund expeditions without knowing who walks onto them?" Vane tipped his head back against the stone, and for a moment the charm dropped away entirely and Marguerite saw the arithmetic underneath, the cold ledger running behind his eyes. "You used your mother's surname and your father's route-finding instincts and you thought a man who made his fortune reading ground surveys wouldn't recognize the handwriting? Please. I've been waiting weeks for you to tell me yourself. I confess I expected better timing."

"What do you want, Vane?" Marguerite said.

"What I have always wanted. What I paid for." He gestured, a small precise movement, toward the tarped sleds at the ledge's edge — invisible now in the white, but present, weighted, real. "And here is the situation, since neither of you seems to grasp it. The storm will pass tonight. The corridor closes tomorrow. My aircraft is dead, my advance team is dead or scattered, and I am—" he looked down at his leg with distaste, as though it were a subordinate who had failed him, "—compromised. Which means the only way any of us leaves this escarpment is together, on foot, through the serac field, in a window measured in hours."

He let that sit. The wind filled it.

"So here is my offer. We walk out together — all of us, your geologist, your cook, my surviving men, your father, you. I have a satellite beacon and a standing contract with a bush pilot out of Atlin who will lift us from the toe of the glacier regardless of what the paperwork says afterward. Everyone goes home. No one else dies on this mountain." His eyes came up and fixed on hers, and they were pale and patient and utterly without warmth. "In exchange, the crates come with us. All four. And when we reach the lake, they go into my charter and fly south, and none of this — none of it — ever becomes a matter of record. Not the site. Not the theft. Not your father's signature on my ledger. Not your little masquerade. Everyone lives, everyone keeps their reputation, and history stays exactly as ignorant of this place as it has been for five hundred years. That is the bargain. Take it or freeze beside it."

* * *

Marguerite looked at her father.

She had spent twenty years building a version of Edmund Yarrow out of absence — a man assembled from other people's gossip, from newspaper clippings, from the single memory of his hands teaching hers to read a strike-and-dip in a road cut outside Kamloops. In the last week the real man had been slowly replacing the imagined one, and it had not been simple. He had signed Vane's ledger. He had looked away. He had also, two nights ago, stood in front of a loaded rifle and recited his confession into a recorder while Vane's foreman shouted at him to stop, and Marguerite had heard in his voice something she recognized from her own mirror: the sound of a person choosing, late and at cost, to be the thing they'd claimed to be all along.

Now he met her eyes across the hollow of stone, and he did not tell her what to do. He just nodded once, small, and looked away, and gave her the decision like a man laying a heavy tool down on a table between them.

Vane watched the exchange with the expression of a buyer who knows the auction is already won.

"You're thinking about it," he said. "Good. Sensible. Sentiment is a luxury of people who aren't trapped above a calving glacier. Say yes, Ms. Yarrow, and in forty hours you're drinking coffee in Atlin and this whole season becomes a story you never have to tell."

"No."

The word came out of her smaller than she intended — swallowed a little by the wind — so she said it again, louder, into the white roaring dark.

"No. Here's the bargain. You get exactly what you offered everyone else: safe extraction. Every living person on this mountain walks out through that corridor together, and your pilot takes whoever's standing at the toe of the ice, no questions, no charges pressed by you, no reprisals against Priya for the fuel lines or Tomás for running or me for lying my way onto your payroll. The man goes free. All the men. That part I'll give you, and I'll keep it, because I don't leave people on mountains and I don't sell promises once I've made them."

Vane's smile thinned.

"But the artifacts stay."

"They're worth nothing to you dead," he said. "You understand that. A crate of stone in a warehouse in Zurich is worth millions. A crate of stone photographed and reported is worth a footnote."

"They're not worth anything to you at all," Marguerite said. "That's the thing you've never understood, and it's why you'll lose. You look at that longhouse and you see inventory. Crane looked at it and saw a map he was willing to die carrying. My father looked at it and remembered why he became an archaeologist before men like you taught him what things cost. There are descendants — living communities — who have stories about this place, and Priya has documented every centimetre of it with attribution protocols that will hold up in any court on earth. The site is already saved, Corliss. It was saved the moment we found it and she started shooting photographs. All that's left to negotiate is your pride."

"My pride," Vane repeated softly, "is the only reason either of you is still breathing. Don't confuse restraint with weakness. I have men—"

"You have two men, one of whom is holding you upright, on a ledge that's about to be sealed off from the world, in a storm that doesn't care how much money you have." She leaned closer, and the wind tore her words away as fast as she spoke them, so that only he received them, only he carried them. "You want to test whose terms survive up here? Go ahead. Walk out alone. Drag your sleds down to the serac field and see how far a broken leg gets you through collapsing ice. The mountain has already voted, Mr. Vane. I'm just telling you the tally."

For a long moment the only sound was the storm, and beneath the storm, far below, the glacier working at its own throat like an animal worrying a bone.

Then Corliss Vane began, very quietly, to laugh.

"There it is," he said. "There's the Yarrow in you. I wondered. Your father folds — I bought him for less than the price of this expedition — but you." He shook his head, wincing, still laughing. "You'd haggle with God on a melting ice floe. Fine. Fine. The crates stay. Documented, reported, donated to whatever museum makes you feel righteous. And I walk out of here a free man with nothing but my life and my losses, and you will spend the rest of yours knowing you traded a fortune for a feeling." He extended his hand, mitten and all, grotesque and formal. "Do we have terms?"

Marguerite looked at the hand. Then she reached past it and gripped his forearm instead — the mountaineer's grip, the honest one, flesh to flesh through two layers of cloth.

"We have terms. And Vane — if anyone dies in that corridor tomorrow because you slow us down, the bargain dies with them. Free man or not."

"Understood," he said, and for the first time since Fort Nelson, Marguerite believed he was telling the truth, because fear had finally gotten underneath the oil of his voice and found bedrock.

* * *

They spent the rest of the daylight preparing, because the storm would break in the night and the window would open with the morning, and windows like that do not wait.

Edmund and Marguerite hauled the sleds — empty now, stripped of their stolen cargo, the carvings reburied under cairns of scree that Priya had GPS-tagged within an inch of their lives — to the top of the descent gully and cached them for the traverse. Vane's surviving man, a silent, frostbitten Swede named Lindqvist, worked beside them without complaint, and Marguerite decided that men like Lindqvist were how empires actually ran: not on monsters, but on tired people following orders in bad weather.

At dusk the storm began to die in stages, the way big storms do — the roar dropping to a moan, the moan to a whisper, the whiteout thinning until the world reassembled itself in greys and blues. And what the world revealed stopped Marguerite's breath.

The serac field below had transformed. Where two days ago there had been a corridor — a winding lane of blue ice between leaning towers, dangerous but passable — there was now a battlefield. Half the eastern wall had come down in the night's shaking. New crevasses yawned across the route like unhealed wounds. And at the field's narrowest point, the pinch above the terminal lake, a single tower remained standing across the path: a serac the size of a church, leaning perhaps fifteen degrees off true, shedding a constant veil of spindrift, holding — for now — the way a held breath holds.

Priya appeared at the overhang as the light failed, one-armed, filthy, her splinted forearm bound across her chest, and she looked down at the corridor for a long time without saying anything. Then she took out her notebook — the fourth notebook, the one she'd sewn into her parka lining — and began, by headlamp, to sketch the route from memory.

"It's passable," she said at last. Her voice was hoarse. "Barely. One line, single file, no loads heavier than a man, no stopping in the pinch. If the tower holds for ninety minutes, we make the lake. If it lets go early—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to.

From the shadows of the overhang, Tomás emerged — gaunt, snow-crusted, grinning like a man who had cheated at cards with death itself and gotten away with it — and put a pot of snow-melt on the stove, because that was who Tomás was, and if the world ended tomorrow it would end with hot food in it.

Marguerite stood at the ledge's edge as full dark came down, looking at the leaning tower, and felt Crane's compass in her inner pocket like a second heartbeat.

Below her, in the deep of the glacier's throat, the ice groaned — a long, low, thoughtful sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, deciding whether to wake.

Tomorrow, she thought. Ninety minutes. One line.

Behind her, Edmund began checking knots.
