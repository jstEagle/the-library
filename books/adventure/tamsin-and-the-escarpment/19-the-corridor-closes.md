## 19. The Corridor Closes

The mountain gave us one hour of grey light and no more.

I felt it before I heard it — a change in the air pressure, the way the glacier breathed differently when it was about to speak. I had grown up in a country of earthquakes, where the ground told you things in its own grammar, and I had learned to listen. The Kettle Glacier spoke in groans and cracks, in the deep wet sigh of meltwater running beneath us like blood under skin. All morning it had been talking. Now it was shouting.

"Tomás." Priya's voice came from behind me, tight but level. She was moving well for a woman whose arm hung in a sling of rope and duct tape, her collarbone cracked where Vane's man had thrown her against the fuel cache she'd sabotaged. She had done it anyway — drained the heliport dry with a wrench and a prayer — and she wore the injury like a medal she refused to let anyone polish. "How far to the narrows?"

I checked the map in my head, because the paper map was gone, lost down a gully with Marguerite's wrist, and everything we knew now lived in memory. "Two kilometres. Maybe less if the corridor hasn't closed from the east side."

"And if it has?"

"Then we climb," I said. "And we pray the climbing is kinder than the falling."

Marguerite was ahead of me, setting the pace, her broken wrist strapped flat against her ribs so she could still use the ice axe in her right hand. She hadn't slept — none of us had, not after the longhouse, not after Vane went down screaming into the whiteout with his confession recorded on Edmund's battered recorder and his crates of stolen centuries abandoned on the ledge above. But she moved like sleep was a country she'd renounced citizenship in. Every few minutes she stopped, turned, counted us with her eyes: Edmund. Priya. Me. Four. Always four.

"No one left behind," she said, not for the first time. It wasn't a slogan anymore. It was arithmetic. It was law.

The storm had spent itself in the night, leaving the glacier scoured and glittering, a river of glass frozen mid-flood. The corridor — Crane's corridor, the route the old prospector had drawn in grease pencil for a woman he'd never met — ran between two walls of blue ice that leaned toward each other like conspirators. In summer it was a highway. Today, with calving season open early and the whole massif shrugging off its winter, it was a throat deciding whether to swallow.

We roped up at the entrance. Marguerite tied the knots herself, left-handed, refusing help, and I watched Edmund watch her hands. There was something in his face I had never seen on the trail — not the cold professionalism of the first weeks, not even the raw wonder of the escarpment. It looked like a man reading a letter he'd written long ago and finding, line by line, that every word was true.

"Tomás leads," Marguerite said. "You know why."

I did know why. Because I was the lightest, and because I had crossed glaciers since I was twelve years old hauling supplies for my uncle's outfitting business, and because — though she would never say it — she wanted someone between her and whatever came next whom she could hear breathing. We had become that for each other on this mountain, the four of us. A family assembled out of lies and loyalty, held together with rope.

I stepped into the corridor.

* * *

The ice walls rose thirty metres on either side, sculpted by a century of wind into shapes that made my skin prickle — faces, wings, the ribcages of enormous animals. Meltwater ran down them in silver threads, feeding a stream that chattered along the corridor floor, and everywhere there was sound: the creak of stressed ice, the tick and pop of expanding air pockets, the distant artillery of seracs calving off the upper glacier, rolling down slopes we could not see.

"Keep moving," Priya said. "Don't stop to look at anything."

"I'm not looking," I lied. "I'm admiring."

"Admire faster."

But even Priya slowed once, at a place where the morning sun struck through a gap in the eastern wall and lit the ice from within, turning it the colour of deep water, of cathedral glass, of something older than colour. For three seconds none of us spoke. Then the glacier groaned — a long, low note like a ship's hull in a storm — and we moved again.

It was maybe forty minutes in that the first serac fell behind us.

I didn't hear it fall. I heard it *land* — a concussion through the ice under my boots, a white breath of powder rolling up the corridor from behind, and then Marguerite's voice, sharp as a whip-crack: "RUN. Don't look. RUN."

So I ran. The rope pulled taut and slack and taut as we all found our own rhythms, boots punching into the softening surface, the stream spraying up around our ankles. Behind us — I couldn't help it, I glanced — a tower of ice the size of a church had come off the upper wall and shattered across the corridor's mouth, and the powder cloud was rising like a ghost deciding whether to follow us.

"It missed," Edmund panted beside me. He was sixty-one years old and keeping pace with men half his age, and there was blood on his temple from a fall an hour ago that he had not mentioned and would not mention. "It missed us."

"It's not aiming," Marguerite called back. "That's what frightens me. Keep moving."

We passed a place where the corridor narrowed to barely four metres, walls pressing close enough that I could touch both at once, and the ice here was rotten — dark, aerated, full of bubbles like black bread. My uncle had taught me a word for this: *deglacé*, sugar ice, ice that had given up being ice. I tested each step with the axe before committing my weight, and I called the hazards back down the rope, and Priya repeated them forward to Edmund, and that was how we moved — a chain of voices, a family made of callsigns.

Halfway through the narrows, the glacier spoke again, closer this time.

"Priya." Marguerite's voice had changed. "How far to the exit?"

Priya closed her eyes. I saw her do it — saw her go somewhere inside herself, back through the weeks, to the day she'd studied Crane's map over a camp stove while Tomás fried plantains and complained about the altitude. Her memory was the map now. Her memory was all we had.

"Six hundred metres," she said. "Maybe seven hundred. Then the corridor opens onto the lateral moraine, and the moraine runs down to the lake margin. Crane marked a safe crossing point — a gravel bar. If the lake hasn't risen past it."

"And if it has?"

"Then we swim," I said, "and I remind everyone I never learned."

Nobody laughed. That frightened me more than the seracs did.

* * *

Five hundred metres from the exit, the corridor began to die.

It happened the way a sentence dies — word by word, collapse by collapse. First a block the size of a truck came off the western wall and slammed into the floor ahead of us, and we climbed over it, Marguerite going up one-handed with a grace that made my chest ache. Then another. Then the ceiling itself lowered, and the light changed, and the air grew cold and dead-smelling, trapped, ancient — air that had been sealed in that ice before any of our countries had names.

"We're under the jumble," Edmund said quietly. "This section collapsed years ago and refroze. It's stable."

"You hope," said Priya.

"I estimate," said Edmund, and for the first time in days there was something almost warm in his voice, the ghost of the professor he must have been before disgrace and debt and Corliss Vane got their hands on him. "There's a difference."

They argued like that the whole way through the jumble — small, dry exchanges, a married couple who had never been married — and I understood that this was how they were surviving it, the two of them. Not forgiveness. Not yet, maybe not ever. But a shared language, which is sometimes the same thing.

Then the glacier ended the argument.

The crack came from directly overhead — not a groan but a *crack*, sharp as a rifle, and a seam of daylight appeared in the ceiling ten metres ahead, widening as we watched. Marguerite didn't shout this time. She did something worse. She went very quiet, and very fast, and we followed her because quiet from Marguerite Yarrow was the loudest alarm on this mountain.

The seam became a fissure. The fissure became a falling.

I heard it coming and I did the only thing my body knew: I threw myself flat against the eastern wall and pulled the rope slack toward me, toward Priya, dragging her out of the fall line with my good arm and her bad one, and the world went white. Ice hammered down where Edmund had been standing. Powder filled my mouth, my eyes, the space between heartbeats. When it cleared — seconds, hours, I don't know — Edmund was fifteen metres further along, on his knees in the debris field, alive, coughing, his axe snapped in half.

"Edmund!" Marguerite was already moving to him.

"I'm here." He waved her off with the broken axe, absurdly, like a king declining a crown. "I'm here. Go."

"The rope—"

"Cut it if you have to. *Go.*"

She didn't cut it. Of course she didn't cut it. She hauled him up with her right hand and her fury and her strapped wrist, and we ran the last two hundred metres of the corridor with the whole western wall shedding its shoulders behind us, serac after serac, the thunder continuous now, the glacier coming apart the way a story comes apart when you tell the truth too late.

And then — light. Open sky. The corridor spat us out onto the lateral moraine like a stone from a sling, and we stood there gasping in the grey noon, watching the entrance we had just come through shudder, settle, and begin, block by grinding block, to close.

* * *

The lake was worse than we'd feared.

Crane's gravel bar was still there — a pale ribbon of stone running from the moraine toward the far shore — but the lake had climbed. Meltwater, weeks of it, weeks of warm wind and collapsing winter, had raised the surface until the bar showed in only three places, islands now, stepping stones with drowning gaps between them. And the water was not the gentle turquoise of postcards. It was grey with rock flour, opaque, and along its edge the lake ice was breaking up in plates the size of doors, grinding against each other with a sound like slow applause.

"The crossing is two hundred metres," Priya said. She had her eyes closed again, walking Crane's map in her head. "The bar holds if we move fast. Water's knee-deep at the deepest gap, maybe thigh. Current's negligible — it's a lake, not a river. Cold is the enemy. Cold and time."

"How much time?" Marguerite asked.

Priya opened her eyes and looked back the way we'd come. The corridor's mouth was half-swallowed now; even as we watched, a final tower leaned, hesitated, and folded into the rubble with a boom that rolled across the lake and came back to us off the far shore. Above the whole face of the lower glacier, the seracs were marching — the icefall letting go all at once, the way it does when calving season truly opens, when the glacier decides it has carried its burdens long enough.

"Less than we need," Priya said. "More than zero. Let's spend it."

We crossed.

I will not pretend I remember it clearly. Memory in extreme cold is a lantern with a dying battery — flashes, fragments. I remember the first step into the water and the way it took my breath like a hand closing around my lungs. I remember Marguerite going first, always first, testing each stone with her axe. I remember Edmund carrying Priya's pack and his own and half of mine without being asked, saying nothing, doing it, as if cargo were the only language left to him. I remember Priya, hip-deep in the second gap, crying out once when the current — stronger than she'd guessed, fed by a channel under the bar — knocked her bad shoulder against a boulder, and I remember reaching her and holding her upright, the two of us braced together, and her saying through chattering teeth: "If I drop the camera, leave it."

"I'm not leaving your camera."

"Tomás. The documentation. Everything on the site. It matters more than—"

"It matters *because* of you," I said, and I don't know where the words came from, but they were true, and they shut us both up for the rest of the crossing.

Behind us, the glacier kept falling. The sound followed us across the bar like a tide — boom, boom, boom — and spray from the impacts misted the lake, and once a wave set loose by some enormous collapse lifted the plates of ice and sent them grinding toward the bar, and we ran the last fifty metres with the water chasing our heels, and I laughed, actually laughed, because terror and joy are neighbours on this mountain and share a fence with a hole in it.

We came up the far bank on our hands and knees, four soaked, shaking, half-frozen people on a gravel shore, and behind us the corridor closed.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible part. No final explosion, no cinematic curtain. The mouth of the corridor simply filled — serac by serac, slide by slide — until there was no corridor, no route, no Crane's passage, nothing but a fresh scar of rubble down the glacier's flank and a dust cloud drifting out over the lake like breath on a mirror. The way we had come was gone. The way anyone had ever come was gone. Whatever else happened — whatever inquiries, whatever trials, whatever reconciliation or refusal waited in the valleys below — the escarpment was alone now, sealed in ice and stone, keeping its carved threshold and its buried longhouse and its five thousand years of erased history safe from every hand but memory's.

Priya sat down hard on the gravel and started laughing, wet and ragged, and then Edmund sat down beside her, and then I did, and finally Marguerite — Marguerite, who had not sat down since the longhouse, who had carried all of us on a broken wrist and a dead prospector's promise — Marguerite knelt in the shallows and put her good hand flat on the water, and stayed like that a long moment, saying goodbye to something. To Crane, maybe. To the route. To the version of herself who had walked into camp under a false name, carrying a map and a grudge.

Then she stood up and turned to face us, and the wind pushed her hair back, and for the first time since I'd met her — hired under a false sky, a stranger with a stranger's name — she smiled. Small, exhausted, real.

"Four," she said, counting us with her eyes one last time. "No one left behind."

"No one left behind," we answered, and it sounded like a vow, because it was one.

We started down the valley, toward the tree line, toward the radio station at the ranger outpost, toward testimony and consequences and the long work of telling the truth. Priya walked between Edmund and me, navigating by memory even now, calling the route — *moraine crest, then the goat track, then the creek ford* — and Edmund listened to her the way students listen, and I thought: this is what survival is. Not escaping the mountain. Carrying each other down it.

We were two kilometres from the tree line when Marguerite stopped mid-stride and turned back toward the glacier, and I saw her face change.

"Tomás," she said quietly. "Look. On the ridge. Do you see movement?"

I looked. High above the ruined corridor, on the skyline of the eastern moraine where Vane's advance party had first marched, something glinted — metal, or glass, or a signal mirror catching the sun. Once. Twice. Deliberate.

Someone was up there. Someone had watched us come out.

And they were signalling.
