## 20. Testimony

The hearing room smelled of floor wax and cold coffee, of radiators ticking against October's first hard frost — a smell so ordinary it felt like an insult after the glacier, where every breath had been a negotiation with the thin air and the mountain's ancient patience. Marguerite sat in the corridor outside Room 4 with her broken wrist in a cast gone grey at the edges, turning Crane's compass over in her good hand. The needle swung, settled, swung again. North was north no matter what room you waited in.

They had given her a folder. Inside it: her statement, typed by someone who had never stood on the scree line, never heard the seracs groan like old ships, never watched a man she loved choose a ledger over a living site. She had read it twice on the train north and found it bloodless. So she had written her own in longhand the night before, in the hotel where the pipes knocked all night, and now that version sat inside the folder like a stone inside a loaf of bread.

Priya sat beside her, her arm still in a sling from the fuel cache sabotage, her hair cut short and uneven where the medics had shaved around a gash above her ear. She wore her one good blazer and looked, Marguerite thought, like a woman who had already decided how this day would go.

"They'll try to make it about you," Priya said quietly. "The false name. They'll say it compromises everything."

"It does compromise things."

"It doesn't compromise *this*." Priya tapped the folder. "Photographs don't lie because the photographer used her mother's surname. Neither do you."

Through the double doors, Tomás caught her eye from across the corridor. He'd driven up overnight from the coast, still smelling faintly of diesel and his sister's kitchen, and he raised two fingers to his brow in a little salute that made her throat tighten. His sister was out of surgery now. The money Vane had promised him for silence had instead become evidence; the money the heritage trust had advanced him had become bone grafts and plates and a future. He mouthed something through the glass — *we're here* — and Marguerite nodded and looked away before her face could do anything embarrassing.

A clerk opened the doors. "Ms. Yarrow."

Not Ms. Thorne. Someone had already unsealed the false name; there was no point pretending otherwise. She stood, and the compass needle trembled with the motion, and she put it in her pocket where it lay against her hip like a small cold heart.

* * *

The room was smaller than television had taught her to expect. A table of five commissioners under fluorescent light, water glasses, a stenographer whose fingers hovered like birds. Corliss Vane's lawyers occupied one side of the gallery in charcoal suits, and Vane himself was not present — remanded, charged, his empire of crates and chains and helicopter fuel being inventoried somewhere by people with clipboards. His absence made the room feel both emptier and heavier, as though the chair where he should have sat were itself a kind of testimony.

Edmund Yarrow sat at the witness table when she came in.

She had known he would be there — they had told her, and she had said *fine*, and meant it, mostly — but knowing is not the same as seeing. He was thinner than the man who had recognized her digging style before her face on the moraine. His hands, those hands that had taught hers to read sediment and strike a trowel true, lay flat on the table as if pinned. He did not look at her as she took her seat three metres away. That, too, was a kind of discipline. Or mercy. With her father it had always been impossible to tell the difference.

"Ms. Yarrow," said the chairwoman, a dry-voiced woman named Halloran with reading glasses on a chain. "You've provided a written statement. We've read it. For the record, I'll ask you to begin at the beginning, in your own words."

Marguerite unfolded her own pages. Her handwriting looked suddenly childish on the official paper, loops and slashes, the script of a girl who had learned letters from a father who wrote survey notes in grease pencil on glacier maps.

"The beginning," she said, "is a cabin on the Stellako River, in April. A dying prospector named Aldous Crane gave me a map and asked me to keep a promise."

She told it plainly. She had decided on the train that plainness would be her weapon — no lyricism, no self-pity, none of the novelist's music her mother had loved. Just the facts laid out like artifacts on a sorting table: the gut-shot man, the grease-pencil lines, the promise extracted from a stranger who trusted her because she was Edmund Yarrow's daughter even before she knew she was.

Halloran let her run nearly ten minutes before interrupting. "You joined the Vane expedition under the name Thorne. Why?"

"Because I intended to stop what Vane intended to do. And because I didn't want my father to know I was coming." She paused. "I was wrong about which mattered more. But I wasn't wrong about either."

One of Vane's lawyers — a woman with a voice like poured concrete — leaned forward. "Commissioner, if I may. The witness admits to deception, falsified credentials, unauthorized presence on a licensed expedition—"

"I admit to all of it," Marguerite said. "I'd do all of it again. You can charge me if you like. But don't dress it up as the scandal here. The scandal here is four armed men moving toward an intact pre-contact site days ahead of any legitimate survey, and crates ordered before a single artifact was documented, and a financier who gambled six lives on a helicopter schedule during calving season. Start there."

The concrete voice started again — *"the witness is hardly impartial, given her relationship to Dr. Yarrow"* — and Marguerite turned and looked at the woman fully for the first time.

"You want to talk about my relationship to Dr. Yarrow?" she said. "Fine. He's my father. He left when I was nine. I spent twenty years learning his handwriting without wanting to, because he taught me to read survey notes before he taught me to ride a bike. When I saw his notations on Crane's map I knew them the way you know your own name. And when I found him on that glacier, I stayed close to him precisely because it hurt — because staying close was the only way to protect the site, and maybe him, and I couldn't always tell those two jobs apart." She looked back at Halloran. "So yes. I'm not impartial. I'm the most partial witness you have. Ask me anyway."

There was a silence in which the radiator ticked twice, and Halloran said, "Proceed."

* * *

They kept her on the stand for four hours.

She gave them the crevasse crossing and Tomás falling into the blue dark and the anchor she built out of everything she'd been taught by a man she hated and needed in equal measure. She gave them the night run, the rope line, Edmund catching her and choosing — God help them both — to come along. She described the escarpment ledge in careful, reverent detail: the carved threshold stones, the buried longhouse, the trade goods that proved a sophisticated culture history had written off as transient. She watched one of the commissioners, a young archaeologist herself by the look of her, stop taking notes and simply listen.

Then she gave them the rest. The advance party. The crating. Priya's photographs. The intercepted radio call. The gully where her wrist broke and the map changed hands. She did not soften any of it, and she did not inflate it, and when she reached the part where Edmund had stood in front of Vane's crew at dawn and recorded his own confession aloud — *"My name is Edmund Yarrow, and I agreed to look away"* — her voice finally cracked, just once, and she let it.

"The commission has Dr. Yarrow's sworn confession," Halloran said. "Why do you think he gave it?"

Marguerite considered lying, or at least decorating. She was tired enough that the truth arrived before she could stop it.

"Because I testified against him before anyone asked me to," she said. "He heard me do it. I think — I think it was the first honest thing anyone had shown him in fifteen years, and he wanted to match it." She folded her pages closed. "It doesn't absolve him. I want that in the record too. What he agreed to with Vane was monstrous, and the years he let me believe he'd abandoned us — that was its own theft. Knowing why doesn't give me back the time. Testifying against him isn't betrayal and it isn't forgiveness. It's just the truth, and the truth is the only thing I have left to give either of them — him or the mountain."

Halloran removed her glasses. "Thank you, Ms. Yarrow. No further questions from the commission." A beat. "Does counsel wish—" The concrete-voiced lawyer began gathering papers, declining, and Marguerite understood: they had come hoping to bury her in procedure and had instead handed her four hours of open microphone.

"You're dismissed," Halloran said, and then, quieter, almost off the record: "For whatever a chair's opinion is worth — the site will be protected. People like you are the reason."

* * *

The corridor afterward was emptying, the day's witnesses dispersing into the grey afternoon. Priya squeezed her good arm and went to file the photograph originals with the registrar. Tomás pressed a paper bag of his sister's empanadas into her hands and pretended not to see her eyes.

Edmund was waiting by the window at the end of the hall, where the light came down flat and silver off the parking lot.

She could have walked past. Every version of her that had survived the last twenty years voted for walking past. Instead she stopped, three metres away, the same distance the hearing room had enforced between their chairs, and found that she couldn't speak.

"You were extraordinary in there," he said.

"Don't."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Not — I'm not praising you. I'm thanking you. There's a difference, and I only just learned it." His hands were shaking; she could see it, the fine tremor of a man who had held himself rigid for weeks and was now, at last, allowed to come apart. "When you stood up today and told them what I did — all of it, without flinching, without sparing me — I sat there and I thought: this is the daughter I made. Not the one I abandoned. The one the abandonment made. And she is better than anything I ever dug out of the earth."

"Edmund—"

"You testified against your own father," he said, "because the truth deserved it more than I did. Do you know how long I've been owed exactly that? Since Vane bought me. Since before. Everyone protected me — Crane tried to, in his way, with that map; God knows what it cost him. Nobody ever simply told me the truth and let me stand in it." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, worn down to the studs. "You did. Thank you."

Her eyes were burning. "It ends things," she said. "Whatever we might have — it ends it. You know that."

"Yes," he said simply. "I know."

And that was the whole of it, and it was unbearable, and it was clean. She had thought the moment would feel like breaking. It felt instead like setting a bone — the brief grinding agony, and then the long slow knowledge that what was mended would be stronger than what had been whole.

He reached into his coat pocket and held something out. Crane's duplicate — no. She looked closer. It was her mother's field notebook, the one from the river country summers, the one Marguerite had believed lost with everything else of her mother's. Water-stained, spine cracked, alive.

"You kept it," Marguerite said.

"I kept everything," Edmund said. "That was always the problem." He set it on the windowsill between them, not quite offering, not quite withholding, and stepped back. "The commission reconvenes next month on access permits. There's talk the escarpment will be designated heritage land. If it is — someone will need to lead the first permitted team up. Someone who knows the route." He looked at her, and for the first time since the moraine his eyes held no claim on her at all, only a question. "I've applied as consultant. Nothing more. If you'd rather go without me, I'll withdraw tonight."

Marguerite picked up the notebook. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"I'll let you know," she said.

"That's more than I hoped for." He buttoned his coat, an old man's careful gesture, and walked toward the stairs. At the door he paused. "Marguerite. The truth you told in there — it's the finest thing I've ever been part of. Even as the villain of it."

Then he was gone, and she stood alone at the window with her mother's notebook in her hands and Crane's compass in her pocket, the needle swinging, swinging, settling at last toward north.

Outside, the first snow of the season was starting to fall on the parking lot — early, the way the calving had come early, the way everything true in her life arrived ahead of schedule and demanded she move. She thought of the escarpment under its new white silence, the carved threshold waiting, the longhouse breathing under its blanket of stone. She thought of a small permitted team, and a compass that belonged to a dead man who had trusted her, and a door that forgiveness leaves open — not a debt settled, but a threshold crossed whenever one is ready.

She opened the notebook to the first page. Her mother's handwriting bloomed up at her like flowers pressed for twenty years.

Marguerite smiled through the last of her tears, took out a pen, and beneath her mother's name wrote her own — Yarrow, no alias, no shame — and began, at last, to plan the journey home.
