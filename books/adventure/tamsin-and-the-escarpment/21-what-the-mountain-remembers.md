## 21. What the Mountain Remembers

The letter came on a Tuesday in October, in an envelope heavy enough to feel like stone.

Priya Nair stood at her kitchen window in Vancouver, rain silvering the glass, and read it three times before she let herself believe it. *The Kettle Escarpment Archaeological District has been granted interim heritage protection under provincial order, pending formal designation.* Below the legalese, someone had handwritten a note in blue ink: *Dr. Nair — your documentation made this possible. The Elders' Council asks that you attend the designation ceremony in the spring. — R. Whitehorse.*

She set the letter down on the counter beside her cold coffee and pressed both palms flat against the cool laminate, as if she needed to anchor herself to something that would not move. Outside, the city went on being the city — buses sighing at stops, a gull arguing with the weather — and none of it knew that a ledge of limestone four hundred kilometres north had just been given back its name.

*Its name.* That was the part that undid her. Not the protection itself, though she had spent eleven months of her life fighting for it, drafting reports at 3 a.m. with her wrist still stiff from the glacier, answering the inquiry's questions until her voice went hoarse. It was the name. The descendant communities of the upper river valley — the Ktunaxa-affiliated families who had survived the smallpox years and the residential schools and a century of being told their ancestors had never built anything worth remembering — had known, in the way oral knowledge knows things, that there was a place above the Kettle Glacier where the old trade road ended. They had called it *the house on the shoulder of the world*. The survey maps called it Site Ke-114. Priya had spent the winter making sure that henceforth, in every register and every paper, it would be called what its people called it.

She picked up the letter again and read the handwritten line once more. Then she did what she had learned to do on the ice, when the seracs were falling and there was nothing to do but keep moving: she put one foot in front of the other. She showered. She dressed. She caught the bus to the university, and in the geology building's third-floor office she shared with two other postdocs, she opened the folder marked FINAL and began, for the hundredth time, to check.

* * *

The monograph was finished. That was the sentence she kept returning to, turning over like a stone found on a beach. *The monograph is finished.*

*The House on the Shoulder of the World: Trade, Ceremony, and Continuity at the Kettle Escarpment, c. 1100–1650 CE.* Two hundred and forty pages. Fourteen plates of the carvings photographed in raking dawn light — Marguerite had held the reflector boards for six hours without complaint while Priya worked the camera, the two of them breathing steam in the cold, saying almost nothing, needing to say nothing. Forty-one figures. Radiocarbon dates from the hearth layers. The trace-element analysis of the copper ornaments that proved a trade network stretching from the coast to the plains, a commerce no colonial historian had ever credited to the people of this valley because it had suited everyone, for a hundred and fifty years, to believe they had none.

And on the title page, beneath her own name, the attribution she had fought the publisher hardest for: *In collaboration with, and at the direction of, the Whitehorse Lake Elders' Council and the descendant communities of the upper Kettle River.*

Her editor had wanted her name alone. "It'll carry more weight," he'd said. "Single author, first monograph, dramatic circumstances — you know how these things work." She did know how they worked. That was precisely why she had refused. Every artifact Vane had tried to pry off that mountain was worth money because someone, somewhere, had decided that knowledge belonged to whoever could pay for it. The whole architecture of the theft — the crates, the chains, the helicopter fuel cached on the moraine — rested on the assumption that a thing could be owned by taking it. She would not write a book that repeated the assumption in softer language.

So the book carried four names on its acknowledgments page that mattered more than hers: the Elders' Council; Roberta Whitehorse, who had walked the escarpment approach with them in June and stood silent before the carved threshold for so long that Priya had checked, quietly, whether she should be worried; Tomás Reyes, whose photographs of the longhouse interior were better than hers and who said so with a grin that dared her to argue; and Edmund Yarrow, listed last, as consulting surveyor, his contribution noted in exactly fourteen words — the same number of words, Priya suspected, that he would have chosen himself.

She thought of Edmund often that autumn, though she saw him rarely. He had pleaded guilty to two counts — obstruction and failure to report — and accepted a suspended sentence and a lifetime ban from unsupervised fieldwork in the province. His testimony at the inquiry had been the hinge on which everything turned: forty minutes, unbroken, in which he laid out the whole rotten arrangement in a voice like a man reading his own autopsy. How Vane had come to him after the disgrace, when no university would return his calls. How the money had arrived with conditions folded inside it like knives in cloth. How he had told himself, month after month, that he was protecting the site by staying close to it, the way an alcoholic tells himself he is protecting the family by staying in the house. And then — Priya had watched the transcript a dozen times, frame by frame, looking for the seam and never finding it — the moment on the escarpment when he had looked at the carvings and understood, finally and completely, what he had agreed to sell.

Marguerite's testimony had come after his. Priya had sat in the public gallery and listened to her name the false name, the promise to a dying stranger, the broken wrist in the gully, all of it — and then, without pausing, without mercy, she had named her father's complicity too, in the same clear voice she used for everything. There had been a sound in the hearing room when she did it, a kind of collective intake, as if the whole room had leaned toward her and been refused. Edmund had thanked her afterward. Priya had seen it through the doors: father and daughter standing in the corridor, not touching, his head bowed, her face unreadable, and his mouth moving around words that were, whatever else they were, true.

That was the thing about truth, Priya thought now, watching the rain write and rewrite itself on the window. It didn't heal anything. It just made repair possible. Whether the repair happened was another question entirely, and not hers to answer.

* * *

The charges against Corliss Vane were announced on a Wednesday: trafficking in cultural property, conspiracy, smuggling, and — the count that had made even the jaded courtroom reporters sit up — criminal negligence causing bodily harm, for the helicopter lifts he had ordered into calving season, gambling the lives of his own crew against the closing corridor. Priya read the news on her phone standing in the grocery aisle, and she bought a bottle of wine she couldn't really afford, and that night she drank half of it and cried, though she couldn't have said exactly what for. Relief, probably. And grief for the version of events where none of it had been necessary — where the site had simply been left alone, the way it had been left alone for four hundred years, because leaving things alone is also a form of wisdom and the modern world has very little of it.

Vane's lawyers fought everything, of course. But the evidence was iron: Priya's photographs, timestamped and hash-verified; the manifests she had copied in the supply tent with cold fingers while pretending to inventory core samples; the intercepted radio log; Edmund's confession, recorded on a battered handheld recorder with a cracked case that now sat in a display at the provincial museum like a relic from some earlier, braver age. The advance party's four men had turned on their employer within a week, each certain the others had already talked. Men who follow money for a living, Priya reflected, understand loyalty as a market, and markets crash.

Tomás called her the night the charges were announced. She could hear the noise of a restaurant kitchen behind him, the clang and rush of a Saturday service, and over it his voice, bright as a struck pan.

"They approved it," he said. "The surgery. Full coverage — there's a fund, some victim-assistance thing out of the seized assets, can you believe it? Marisol's consult is in November. November, Priya. She cried. My sister never cries, she once watched our whole house burn down and didn't cry."

"Congratulations," Priya said, and meant it down to the marrow. "Tell her the wine I'm drinking is in her honour."

"Tell her yourself. Come to the wedding."

"The what?"

"The wedding! When she's better. Spring. You're invited, obviously. Marguerite's invited. Even the old man's invited, if he behaves." A pause, the kitchen roaring behind him, and then, quieter: "He asked about you, you know. Yarrow. Asked if you were publishing the whole record or holding things back. I told him you'd sooner cut off your own hand."

"He was right to ask," Priya said. "Tell him the answer anyway."

She hung up and stood a long time at her window with the glass cooling in her hand. Restoration, she thought. People used the word as if it meant putting things back the way they were. But nothing went back. Marisol Reyes would walk again, but she would always know what the walking had cost. The mountain would keep its carvings, but it would keep them behind permits and fences and the particular sadness of places that have been fought over. And Edmund Yarrow would spend whatever years remained to him knowing exactly what he had almost done, which was a heavier sentence than any court hands down, and possibly a more useful one.

The world was not set right. It was set slightly more right, which was the only setting the world ever offered anyone.

* * *

In February, the Elders' Council invited her north.

Not for the ceremony — that was spring, and formal, and there would be speeches. This was different. Roberta Whitehorse's letter was short: *We are going up to the escarpment while the trail is still snowed in, before the tourists and the cameras. We would like you there. Bring your notebook. Leave your camera.*

Priya flew to the staging town and rode up with Roberta and two of her nieces in a truck that smelled of woodsmoke and dog, and they skied the last day along the frozen river with the light going amber and long, and camped below the scree line in tents that glowed orange in the dusk like lanterns. Nobody spoke much. The mountains spoke enough for everybody.

They climbed to the ledge at dawn.

Priya had seen the site a dozen times — in summer, in fall, in the archival photographs, in her dreams, which had lately taken to featuring crevasses with inconvenient regularity. But she had never seen it like this: under snow, the carvings furred white, the buried longhouse a smooth swell beneath the drifts, the whole place returned to silence. The only marks on the ledge were animal tracks, a fox's neat stitching across the threshold stone.

Roberta Whitehorse walked to the carvings and brushed the snow from one panel with her bare hand — a figure with arms raised, the one the Elders called *the one who holds the sky open* — and began, softly, to speak in her own language. Priya understood not one word and understood all of it. It was a greeting. It was a report: we are still here; the ones who tried to take you are answered for; your name is spoken again. It was, Priya realized, with a tightness in her throat, the same thing her documentation had been trying to say for a year, in a language far less equipped to say it.

Afterward, over tea melted from snow, Roberta looked at her across the little stove and said, "You wrote it all down. Everything."

"Everything I could verify. Nothing I was asked to withhold."

"And the book — people will read it?"

"Some. Scholars first. Then others, maybe."

Roberta nodded slowly, looking out at the icefields, blue-white to the horizon. "My grandmother said the mountain remembers everything. She said the rock keeps the stories even when we can't." She turned back. "But rock doesn't argue with a government. Paper does. So we needed your paper." A pause, and then, with the ghost of a smile: "Don't let it go to your head. The mountain did most of the work. It kept the site hidden from men like Vane for four hundred years. All you did was arrive after it got tired."

Priya laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the glacier — and wrote in her notebook, because Roberta had told her to bring it: *Feb. 14. Ledge under snow. The mountain remembers; we are permitted to take notes.*

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way it does in the north, all at once and then everywhere. The designation ceremony was held at the foot of the glacier lake in June, on the gravel flat where the outlet stream ran milky with rock flour, and it was smaller than Priya had expected and larger than it looked: perhaps sixty people, Elders and officials and a scattering of journalists kept politely at a distance, and among them, at the edges, her strange scattered expedition-family. Tomás in a shirt with actual buttons, waving. Marguerite in her guide's canvas, sunburned, watchful, standing slightly apart the way she always did, as if belonging were a country she held a visa for but hadn't yet cleared customs. And Edmund Yarrow, greyer than Priya remembered, standing further apart than that, invited and uncertain, a man attending the resurrection of something he had nearly helped bury.

The speeches were long. The Elders spoke first, in their language and then in English, and a young woman sang a song that Priya was told had not been sung in that valley in living memory, having survived only in the memory of one old woman and now, thanks to a recording, in many. When the protection order was signed — actual paper, actual pen, witnessed — there was applause, restrained and real.

Afterward, drifting between clusters of people with a cup of bad coffee, Priya found herself beside Edmund at the water's edge. For a while neither spoke. The glacier groaned out on the lake, a deep tectonic complaint, the sound she had once believed she would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life. Maybe she would. Maybe it was a fair price.

"You've read the page proofs," she said at last. "Your section."

"I have." He turned the coffee cup in his hands. "Fourteen words. You were generous."

"I was accurate. Accuracy was the job."

He almost smiled. Out on the lake, with a crack like a rifle shot, a small piece of the glacier tongue calved and went under, and the wave came ashore against the gravel a minute later, lapping at their boots, and receded. Neither of them stepped back.

"She's coming back in August," Edmund said, not looking at her. "With a permitted team. Small. She asked me to consult on the survey grid." His voice did something complicated on the word *asked*. "I don't know what I am to her, Dr. Nair. Consultant, father, cautionary tale. Perhaps all three. Perhaps that's more than I deserve."

"It's not mine to sort out," Priya said. "But for what it's worth — she reads your field notes. All of them. She thinks I don't know."

Edmund looked at her then, and there was something in his face that had not been there at the inquiry, nor in the courtroom, nor on the escarpment in the terrible dawn of the stand: something loosened, tentative, like ice going out of a river.

Then Marguerite was crossing the gravel toward them, and Priya saw her father straighten, and saw Marguerite see him straighten, and saw her decide — visibly, deliberately, the way she decided everything — to pretend she hadn't. She stopped a few feet away and held out a small object on her palm: a brass compass, dented, its glass clouded, its needle swinging lazily north.

"Aldous Crane's," she said. "I've been carrying it since the cabin. I think it belongs up there now. On the threshold stone." She glanced at her father, and then at Priya, and something passed across her face that was not quite a smile and not quite an invitation. "I go up in August. Both of you — if you want."

Out on the lake, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a door opening very slowly in a very large house. The needle of Crane's compass steadied, trembling, and pointed home.

Priya reached out and closed Marguerite's fingers over the brass. "August," she said. "I'll bring the notebook."
