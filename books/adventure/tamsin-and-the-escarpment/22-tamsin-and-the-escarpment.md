## 22. Tamsin and the Escarpment

The mountain let them come back in the last week of August, when the Kettle had finished its tantrum and lay blue-green and exhausted in its valley, calving quietly into the lake like an old man muttering in his sleep.

Marguerite felt the change before she saw it — the way you feel a house you grew up in before you round the corner to it. The air off the glacier carried that same mineral cold she'd breathed as a child on her father's shoulders, that smell of stone and ancient ice and something almost sweet beneath it, lichen maybe, or the memory of lichen. Her wrist ached where it had broken, though the bone had knit months ago. Some pains kept their own calendar.

Behind her, the permitted team came up the moraine in single file: two heritage surveyors from the university, a young archivist named Dev who talked to his own camera more than to people, Priya walking with the easy roll of someone whose hip still remembered the corridor collapse but refused to complain about it. And at the rear, half a rope-length back from everyone else, Edmund Yarrow.

Consultant, the permit said. Nothing more. She had written those words herself, in her own careful hand, and she had watched him read them when she handed him the paperwork — watched him take the small wound of *nothing more* without flinching, because he understood it was the toll for the road and he was willing, at last, to pay what things cost.

"Route's good," Priya said, coming up beside her. "You picked the season right."

"I had help picking it." Marguerite nodded back toward the glacier. "It's quieter than it was."

"Glaciers are like people." Priya shaded her eyes against the low sun sliding along the escarpment's eastern face. "They do their worst damage in silence, and then they apologize all over the landscape."

Marguerite laughed despite herself. It was the kind of joke they'd traded across watch fires and interrogation rooms and hospital corridors, the currency of their friendship now, worn smooth by handling. Below them the lake lay flat as hammered tin, strewn with the small white fleet of bergs the summer had calved. Somewhere under that tin surface was the place where the corridor had closed behind them, the seracs folding down like a curtain after the last act. Tomás had been the last one out. He liked telling it that way, and no one ever corrected him.

* * *

They made camp at the scree line, in the old place, and Marguerite found she could not look at the fire ring without seeing Tomás standing in it with his sleeves rolled up, telling the story of his sister's surgery with flour still on his hands from bread he'd baked at four thousand metres because altitude was no excuse for bad food. He wasn't here this time. Someone had to run the restaurant while Rosa learned to walk again, he'd said, and he'd made them all promise to bring him back a stone — not a carving, just a stone, one honest rock from the ledge, so he could put it on the bar and point at it whenever anyone asked about the mountain.

Priya had already chosen hers. Dev was cataloguing everything that didn't move and some things that did.

Edmund sat apart, as he had sat apart for eleven days of travel, close enough to be part of the circle and far enough that no one had to decide whether to include him. He was mending a bootlace with the concentration of a man performing surgery. His hands were older than she remembered them being in the spring. Or perhaps she simply hadn't been looking closely enough then; there had been too much else to look at — the map, the guns, the ice, the ledger of everything Corliss Vane had bought and she had come to reclaim.

Vane was in remand now, awaiting trial. The crates were in a government warehouse, inventoried, photographed, each item tagged with its findspot and its story. The descendant communities of the river country had been at every table since the second week, and it was their names that would go on the publications — Priya had insisted, had fought for it in rooms where fighting was done with footnotes and committee minutes, and had won. The site would become protected land in the spring. There would be a shelter, eventually, and a protocol, and school groups in a decade who would never know how nearly the longhouse carvings had gone into a private vault in a city none of their ancestors had ever seen.

That was the world set slightly right. Marguerite had learned not to ask for more than slightly.

She banked the fire and went to her tent, and took out the compass.

* * *

Aldous Crane's compass was a brass thing gone soft with handling, its case dented where a falling rock or a careless boot had marked it decades ago, its needle still true. She had carried it since the cabin, since the gut-shot man breathing in fish-smelling dark who had looked at her — a stranger, a woman with a client's credit card and a guide's calluses — and decided she was the one to trust. She had never fully understood that decision. She understood it better now. Dying men don't choose carefully; they choose *toward* something. Crane had chosen toward the escarpment, toward the carvings, toward the truth of a trade culture that had mapped these rivers with grease pencil and memory long before any survey notation. He had chosen toward the thing itself, and away from himself, and he had needed hands to carry the choosing.

Her father's partner. That was the other gift Edmund had given her on the rotten gully's edge, when she lay with her wrist broken and the map lost: the duplicate key, the coordinates memorized rather than drawn, and the whole sad architecture of the years between — Vane's money, Vane's leverage, the disgrace manufactured and then exploited, and the abandonment arranged like a surgical procedure to keep her out of reach of a man who collected people the way he collected artifacts. She had listened to all of it with her arm cradled against her chest and the snow coming down, and she had believed him, mostly, and she had told him: *believing is not the same as forgiving.*

He had said, "I know. I'm not asking for the second."

He had kept that bargain for five months now. It was, she thought, the first bargain he had ever honored completely.

The needle swung and settled, pointing north-northwest, toward the ledge. Of course it did. Compasses only know one direction. People were supposed to be more complicated, and mostly were, and mostly used the complication as an excuse.

She wrapped the compass in its cloth and slept, or half-slept, listening to the glacier talk to itself in the dark.

* * *

They reached the escarpment on the third morning, up the gully that had broken her wrist, now dry and benign in the high summer light, and along the goat track below the overhang, and then around the shoulder of rock where the world opened.

The carved threshold stood exactly as they had left it.

Marguerite had braced herself for the feeling — she'd been warned by Priya, who'd read the literature on return visits, on recontact, on the strange grief of places that stay the same while the people who found them are changed past recognition. But nothing quite prepared her. The threshold stone rose from the ledge like a doorframe with no door, its lintel carved with the running spiral-and-fish pattern the community elders had identified within a week of seeing the photographs — *river patterns*, Elder Sam Alook had said, touching the print gently with one finger, *our people drew the water moving*. The longhouse depression beyond it held its green shadow. The scree slopes above caught the sun and threw it back gold.

Dev was crying quietly and filming anyway. The surveyors were setting up their instruments with the hushed efficiency of people in a cathedral. Priya stood at the threshold with her hand flat on the stone, not reading it, just greeting it.

Edmund hung back. He always hung back now, at thresholds. Marguerite watched him from the corner of her eye — the great Edmund Yarrow, whose name had once opened doors and then closed them, standing three steps below the carved lintel with his hands in his pockets like a man outside a house he'd been evicted from.

"You can come up," she said. "It's a permitted site. You're on the permit."

"I know what I'm on." But he came up, slowly, and stood beside her under the lintel, and looked at the spirals the way he had looked at them the first time — that transformation she remembered, wonder burning through the man like sun through fog, and then the shame arriving right behind it, on schedule, because wonder and guilt had shared a room inside him for twenty years and knew each other's habits.

"The water moving," he said softly. "Sam Alook's phrase. I've thought about it every day since. All my career I read stone as text — stratigraphy, sequence, deposition. Data. And here it's just—" He stopped. Started again. "Here it's a sentence someone said to the river, and the river kept it."

"Yes," Marguerite said.

That was most of what they said to each other anymore. Short sentences, true ones. They had burned through the long speeches in the storm, in the inquiry, in the letters that crossed the country twice a month — his longer, hers shorter, both getting briefer and more honest as the months went on, until what remained was this: two people who could stand under a carved door together and agree that the water moved.

* * *

The work filled three days. Grids and photographs, the gentle excavation of a test pit under the surveyors' protocols, the recording of everything with attribution spoken aloud before each entry — *site of the river peoples, documented with the authorization of the Alook family council* — until the words became a rhythm, a liturgy, and Marguerite found herself saying them in her sleep.

On the third evening, Priya found her at the ledge's western end, where the drop fell away sheer to the glacier's tongue, blue and crevassed and beautiful from this distance, the way dangerous things are.

"Tomorrow we descend," Priya said. "Weather window holds through Thursday."

"I know."

"You've been carrying that cloth bundle for eleven days." Priya nodded at her pack. "I don't pry. But I notice things. Occupational hazard."

"It's a compass," Marguerite said. "It belonged to the man who started all of this. Aldous Crane."

"The prospector." Priya sat down on the rock beside her, careful of her hip, and looked out at the ice. "Tomás told me about him. The cabin. The promise."

"He made me promise to carry the map here before the melt. I did. Late, and badly, and at cost. But the map got here, and the site got here, and here they both are." She turned the cloth bundle over in her hands. "I keep thinking about what you owe a dead man who trusted you. There's no invoice. There's no settlement. You just keep carrying it until you find the place to set it down."

"And is this the place?"

Marguerite looked at the threshold stone, gold-lit now, the spirals deepening into shadow as the sun dropped. "I think it might be."

Priya stood, pressed her shoulder briefly — the whole conversation, in that gesture — and walked back toward camp, leaving her alone with the compass and the enormous evening.

* * *

She waited until the others had turned in. Even Dev, even his camera. The light went from gold to copper to that thin blue the north does at midnight in late August, when the sun dips below the ridge and the world runs on stored brightness. The glacier glowed faintly, self-lit, like something thinking.

Edmund was sitting up when she passed his tent. He came out without being asked, pulling his jacket on, and fell into step beside her, and neither of them spoke on the short walk to the threshold. This, too, had become a language between them: the willingness to be present without explanation.

At the carved lintel she stopped, and unwrapped the compass.

"Aldous Crane found this site forty years ago," she said. "Before you. Before the disgrace, before Vane, before any of it. He found it and he told no one, and he kept the secret so long it killed him keeping it. He gave me the map because he couldn't carry it anymore." She held the compass up so the last blue light caught the brass. "I testified that he was the original discoverer. It's in the record now. His name comes before yours in the site documentation, and mine comes after his, and I want you to know I did that on purpose."

"I know," Edmund said. "I read the record twice." A pause. "Three times."

"He deserves to have something of his here. Not in a warehouse. Here. Where the needle points at the thing he spent his life pointing at."

She knelt at the base of the threshold stone, in the hollow where the roots of the mountain gripped, and set the compass down in a nest of gravel, needle swinging, settling, finding north the way it always had, the way it would tonight and tomorrow and in ten thousand tomorrows after, pointing home at a home it could never leave. She built a small cairn over it — three stones, four, five — not to hide it, just to hold it, the way you hold something you're giving away.

When she stood, Edmund was looking at the little cairn with an expression she couldn't name and didn't try to.

"He was my friend," Edmund said. "For one winter, before I got ambitious. He taught me which berries lie. I forgot that for thirty years. I'm not going to forget it again."

"No," Marguerite said. "You're not."

They stood together under the lintel while the glacier murmured below, and the stars came out in the east first, the way they always do, arriving like scouts ahead of the full night. Somewhere far off, a serac calved with a sound like a door closing very far away — or opening; at this distance, she thought, it was impossible to say which.

"Next season," Edmund said, "the council wants to bring youth groups up. Sam Alook asked whether I'd teach the stone-reading. The real kind. The kind that listens." He glanced at her, quickly, the way he'd glanced at her across twenty years of silences. "I told him I'd have to ask my daughter if she'd permit it."

Marguerite looked at the cairn, at the compass sleeping under its stones, at the carved water moving forever above it.

"She'll consider it," she said. "Tell her she'll consider it."

And high on the ridge behind them, where the trail would open south come morning, the first wind of autumn moved through the pass — carrying, faint and unmistakable, the smell of the river country, the smell of going home.
