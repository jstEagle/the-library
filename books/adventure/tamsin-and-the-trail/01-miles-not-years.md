## 1. Miles, Not Years

The morning Wren Vane's old life came looking for him, he was measuring the distance between two rocks that did not matter.

That was the ritual of it. Fifteen years in the borderlands had taught him that a man could survive almost anything if he refused to count time. Time was the enemy—time reminded you of birthdays missed and promises broken, of a woman's face going soft-focus in memory until you could no longer swear to the color of her eyes. So Wren counted miles instead. He measured his days in the space between landmarks: from the split granite at Redgate Wash to the leaning sentinel stone the drovers called the Old Man's Tooth, eleven miles if the sand held firm, fourteen if the wind had been working the dunes overnight. From the Tooth down to the river terraces, another nine. He kept a ledger of distances in a waterproof notebook, columns of neat figures, and when anyone asked him how long he'd been out here—and no one asked, not really—he would have answered honestly: four thousand two hundred and some-odd miles, give or take.

Not years. Never years.

He knelt now in the gravel of a dry tributary, boot heel beside a stone no bigger than a skull, and sighted north along the wash toward the smudge of the escarpment. The air was doing that thing it did before dawn fully committed—cool as well-water, smelling of dust and creosote and the faint mineral breath of the river country far below. A jackrabbit watched him with one liquid eye and decided he was boring. Wren took this as a professional compliment. A ranger who interested the wildlife was a ranger doing something wrong.

He marked the rock, logged the interval—*3.1 miles to the cairn, ground hard, no sign*—and stood, knees cracking like green kindling. Thirty-eight years old and he creaked like a man of sixty. The desert did that. It wrung the water out of you slowly, year by year, until what remained was mostly sinew and silence.

*Year by year.* He caught himself, scowled, and corrected the accounting. *Mile by mile.*

His patrol that day was routine, which meant it was perfect. He walked the eastern rim where the smugglers' trail threaded down toward the water, found nothing but the cold ash of a drover's fire three days old and the desiccated corpse of a snake that had lost an argument with a hawk. He checked the rain gauge at the Middle Station—a quarter inch since last month, the sky hoarding itself—and drank tepid coffee from a tin cup while the sun climbed and the heat rose off the flats in visible sheets, the whole world shimmering like a mirage of itself. At noon he lay in the strip of shade behind the station and did not sleep, because sleeping in daylight invited dreams, and dreams were years wearing a disguise.

At the Middle Station there was a radio.

Wren had a complicated relationship with that radio, the way a man has a complicated relationship with a scar. It was a heavy old set bolted to a shelf of raw pine, its casing sun-bleached, its dial glowing a sickly amber when powered. Twice a week it crackled with Command's voice—Sergeant Okonkwo, usually, her tone flat as the salt pans—and twice a week Wren reported his mileage, his findings, his need for nothing. That was the covenant: *need for nothing.* He'd negotiated it fifteen years ago, when he first signed on. Send me to the empty places, he'd told them. Don't send anything after me.

So when the radio hissed alive at four in the afternoon, an unscheduled hour, Wren knew before he touched the handset that the covenant was broken.

"Middle Station, come in." Okonkwo's voice, stripped of its usual flatness. Underneath it, something urgent. Something almost apologetic.

He let it hiss for three full seconds. A man can do a lot of living in three seconds. He thought about walking out the back into the heat and simply continuing his patrol, letting the call dissolve into static, becoming one more unlogged mile. But duty was the only religion he'd kept, the single thread he hadn't burned, and so he keyed the handset.

"Middle Station. Vane."

"Vane." A pause. Paper shuffling on her end, or maybe just wind against her microphone. "We've got a situation upriver. The Ellery survey expedition has gone silent."

The name went through him like a fishhook through soft flesh.

He kept his voice level. Fifteen years of practice made level voices cheap. "Define silent."

"Missed three scheduled check-ins. Last transmission was eight days ago, position somewhere above the Kettle Narrows. Weather service is calling a monsoon system stacking over the highlands—biggest in a decade, they're saying. If the expedition's in trouble up there, the window to reach them closes when the river does." Another pause, longer. "Command wants you on it."

"Command has forty rangers," Wren said. "Half of them know boats."

"None of them know *that* water." Okonkwo's voice hardened, taking on the tone she used when she'd already argued the fight upstream and lost. "You ran supply runs through the Narrows for six years before you transferred. You're the only man on the roster who's read the river charts, and half those channels aren't on any chart anyway. It's your reach, Vane. I'm sorry. I argued for you to stay out of it—I know your history with—"

"There's no history," Wren said, too fast, too clean, the way a man slams a door.

The static filled the space where his denial should have convinced someone. When Okonkwo spoke again, she was gentle, which was worse than being sharp. "Boat's fueled at the Lower Depot. Charts are in the locker. Storm's thirty-six hours out, maybe less. I'll log your departure at dawn unless you tell me otherwise."

He told her otherwise. He told her nothing at all. He sat on the bench outside the Middle Station as the light went long and copper, and he looked north toward the river country he had spent a decade and a half pretending didn't live inside him, and he did not key the handset again until full dark, and then all he said was:

"Vane copies. Departing dawn."

* * *

He did not sleep either. Instead he sat cross-legged on the station floor with his distance ledger open on his knees, reading backward through fifteen years of numbers, and understood—for the first time, with total clarity—that the ledger had never been about measuring the land at all. It had been a wall. Every mile a brick. Eleven miles to the Old Man's Tooth, nine to the terraces, three-point-one to the cairn, thousands upon thousands of small honest distances stacked into a fortification with a single purpose: to keep one name, one face, one summer on the water from getting back in.

Tamsin Ellery.

He hadn't spoken her name aloud in fourteen years, and even in the privacy of his own skull it landed like a dropped anchor. He'd gotten good at the other trick—the one where you don't think of a person, you think of the weather around the place where they used to be. He knew the exact shape of the hole. He'd built his life around its contours the way a river builds around a boulder, flowing past, never touching.

But the river always comes back to the boulder eventually. Ask any cartographer.

Because that was the bitter geometry of it: her father had been a cartographer, old Ellery with his ink-stained cuffs and his laugh like a landslide, the man who'd taught Wren to read water when Wren was nineteen and hungry for a trade and a family that wasn't his own. Two summers Wren had worked the survey boats. Two summers of charting channels and eating fish burnt on tin skillets and learning that Tamsin Ellery laughed with her whole body and argued with her whole soul and looked at him some evenings across a campfire like he was a country worth mapping.

And then old Ellery had put his hand on Wren's shoulder under the stars above the Narrows and said, *When Tamsin takes over the survey house, I want you standing beside her. Family isn't blood, boy. It's who you'd drown for.*

And Wren—who had a family, God help him, a family with a great house in the lowlands and a name older than the river towns and secrets underneath the floorboards that he'd only begun to suspect—had run. Not walked. Run. He'd taken the borderland posting before the engagement announcement could be printed, before he could be named heir to a survey house built on honesty while his own inheritance was built on something else entirely. He'd told himself it was protection. He'd told himself she was better off. He'd told himself every lie a coward tells at speed, and then he'd come out here and started laying bricks.

Now Command wanted him to row straight back into the middle of the hole he'd dug.

*The Ellery expedition has gone silent.* Eight days. Above the Kettle Narrows. With a monsoon coming down like the sky meant to erase the whole watershed. And somewhere in that drowned country was a woman he had abandoned so thoroughly that his apology, if he ever managed one, would need its own supply line.

Wren closed the ledger. He packed by lamplight with the economy of long habit—rope, med kit, dry rations, the river charts he'd sworn he'd never open again, their folds gone soft as cloth from a decade of disuse. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He'd expected them to shake.

Outside, the southern horizon had gone strange. The stars along it were dimmed, blurred, as though the night itself were sweating. Somewhere up in the highlands, beyond the edge of seeing, a storm the size of a country was gathering its skirts, and every drop of it meant to come down the river at once.

He thought of Tamsin—eight days silent, upstream, in the path of all that water—and felt the wall inside him tremble, brick by brick, mile by mile.

Dawn found him at the Lower Depot, standing on the dock beside a borrowed boat with hairline cracks spiderwebbing up from the waterline, looking at charts he'd promised himself he'd never read again.

The river waited, brown and patient, running north toward everything he'd fled.

Wren stepped aboard.
