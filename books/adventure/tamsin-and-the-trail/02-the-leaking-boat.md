## 2. The Leaking Boat

The boat was called *Charity*, which Wren Vane took as a bad omen from the moment he saw her.

She sat low in the brown water at the Fort Meridian dock, a flat-bottomed river skiff borrowed from the supply office, her paint gone chalky with sun and her gunwales scarred by years of careless hands. The quartermaster, a one-eyed man named Bratch who had known Wren since before either of them had gray in their beards, watched him run his palm along the hull the way other men ran their palms along horses' flanks.

"She'll hold," Bratch said. "Mostly."

"Mostly is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

Bratch shrugged, which for him was an entire weather report. "Hairline cracks above the waterline. She weeps a little when you load her heavy. Bail every hour and she'll carry you to the moon and back." He spat thoughtfully over the rail. "You know the water better than anyone on this side of the divide, Vane. That's why they're sending you."

That was what command kept saying. *You know the water.* As if the river were a map he'd memorized instead of a debt he'd been dodging for fifteen years. The Ellery expedition had gone silent eleven days ago—last transmission from somewhere past the Serrated Bend, where the channel braided into a dozen lying threads—and every ranger within two hundred miles had suddenly discovered urgent business elsewhere. So the order had come down to the one man who knew the upper reaches, the one man whose knowledge of that country was not professional at all but personal, and personal in the worst way.

Wren loaded the *Charity* without speaking: crates of hardtack, a medical kit, rope, the signal lamp, his rifle wrapped in oilcloth against the coming damp. He worked in the old rhythm, each item placed with intention, because intention was all he had left of discipline some mornings. Fifteen years of measuring his life in miles rather than years. A man could survive almost anything if he counted it in distance.

The storm was already stacking on the horizon when he cast off.

It built itself out of the southwest in great blue-black terraces, cloud upon cloud, silent and enormous, like a city being erected by something that did not intend its inhabitants to survive the winter. The air ahead of it had gone strange and sweet, that green metallic taste the desert borderlands got before the monsoon broke—a taste Wren associated with every bad decision of his life. He had learned to read storms the way literate men read letters, and this one said: *you have three days, maybe four, and then the river will stop being a road and start being a wall.*

He took the *Charity* upriver anyway.

* * *

The first night he made camp on a gravel bar below the Serrated Bend and did not sleep.

Fires were allowed up here—the country belonged to no one officially, which meant it belonged to whoever was strongest, which meant it belonged to no one honestly—and he built a small one anyway, more for the sound than the warmth. Fire talking to itself. Company that asked nothing.

He sat with his knees drawn up and let the memory come, because out here there was no outrunning it. The river had a way of pulling things out of a man like splinters.

Tamsin Ellery at nineteen, standing in the shallows below her father's survey camp with her boots slung around her neck and her trousers rolled to the knee, laughing at him because he'd fallen in trying to look dignified. She had a laugh that started in her chest and came out sideways, and she used it on him without mercy, and he had loved her the way young men love: completely, stupidly, with no contingency plan.

Her father, Aldous Ellery, the cartographer, had been kind to him in that particular way kind men are kind to useful ones. He'd taught Wren to read a sextant, to correct for refraction, to love the difference between a place and a map of a place. *A survey is a promise,* Aldous used to say, tapping the brass rim of his instruments. *Every line you draw says: I stood here, I saw truly, and I will not lie about it.* Wren had believed then that such promises were possible. He had been twenty-three and full of the belief that names could be escaped by effort alone.

Then Aldous had begun saying other things. Quieter things. Things about partnership, about the future of the survey company, about a daughter who looked at Wren across the cookfire in a way that made the future seem less like a threat and more like a door standing open.

And Wren had understood what was coming. The naming. The heirship. Old Ellery intended to fold him into the family—not the Vane name, never that, but the *work*, the legacy, the standing in front of rooms full of investors and magistrates and saying *this land is thus, and here is my proof.* And Wren had known, with the cold certainty that arrives all at once or not at all, that the day Aldous Ellery spread his surveys on a table in the capital, the truth would come spilling out of them like water from a cracked hull. Because the Ellery maps covered the riverlands. And the riverlands were Vane land. Had been Vane land for three generations, deeded and sealed and defended in courtrooms by lawyers whose fees had hollowed out half the family fortune.

Except it wasn't true. Wren had found the original documents when he was seventeen, in his father's locked cabinet, hunting for nothing in particular. He had read them twice, understanding arriving slowly and then all at once, the way dawn comes up behind a ridge. The boundary lines had been moved. The witnesses were dead men's signatures forged by a clerk long since paid and pensioned. Three generations of Vane wealth rested on paper lies, and everyone who mattered knew it, and everyone who mattered had agreed to keep knowing it quietly.

His father had called it *consolidation*. His uncle had called it *the price of civilization*. Wren, at seventeen, had called it nothing at all, because he had learned early that in his family the correct response to an unbearable fact was silence, polished daily until it shone.

So when Aldous Ellery began talking about heirs, Wren had done the only thing his upbringing had equipped him to do. He had run. Not dramatically—no letter, no scene, no goodbye. He had simply taken the morning boat downstream one day in the wet season and joined the rangers under a name so common it might as well have been no name at all, and he had let fifteen years pass over him like water over a stone.

He told himself he hadn't loved her enough. That was the version he'd sanded smooth over the years, the story he could carry. *I didn't love her enough to stay.* It was cleaner than the truth, which was that he had loved her exactly enough, and staying would have meant watching her father discover, line by line on his own honest maps, that the man courting his daughter was the son of thieves. That Tamsin would learn it too. That everything good in his life would curdle into evidence.

Better to be a coward early than a liar late. That was what he'd told himself. Some nights, even now, he almost believed it.

The fire popped. Out on the black water, something large turned over and sank again. The storm-city on the horizon had grown another district.

* * *

He reached the mouth of the Serrated Bend on the second day, and that was where the *Charity* began to die.

It started as a sound—a faint ticking from beneath the floorboards, rhythmic as a clock—and Wren, who knew boats the way priests knew scripture, went still and listened. Then he pulled the supply crate off the starboard seam and found the hairline crack weeping exactly as Bratch had promised, except that the river here ran fast and cold with snowmelt off the high country, and fast cold water finds flaws the way gossip finds ears. A thin silver thread of water pulsed through the crack with every flex of the hull.

He bailed, wedged a rag into the seam, redistributed the weight, and ran the bend slow, hugging the inside channel where the current forgave mistakes. Twice the *Charity* ground over submerged rock with a shudder he felt in his teeth. Once he had to pole her off a sandbar that hadn't been on any chart, because of course it hadn't; the river redrew itself every season, and the charts were always obituaries of last year's water.

By late afternoon the sky had come down close and greenish, and the air had the pressure of a held breath. The rain, when it finally started, came not as drops but as a decision—one vast horizontal sheet of it, marching up the channel toward him, bending the surface of the river flat.

Wren got the canvas up over the cargo and himself under it, and steered by feel and memory while the world outside went white with water. And in the drumming dark under the tarp, with the bilge sloshing around his ankles and the pump handle worn smooth under his hand, he let himself think the thing he had refused to think since the radio call.

*Tamsin.*

Eleven days silent. Two crew, maybe three, a season's worth of supplies, and a woman who had inherited her father's stubbornness along with his instruments. If the expedition had met trouble—and expeditions that go silent have met trouble; silence is how the river says *no*—then she was up here somewhere in this same rising water, in a camp that might be flooded, might be burned, might be nothing but ash and absence.

Because there had been something else in the radio traffic, buried in the routine bulletins like a bone in a fish. A report, third-hand, of smoke seen on the upper water two weeks back. A campfire burning wrong. Burning *big*.

Wren pumped bilge and stared at the gray curtain beyond the canvas and felt dread settle over the old grief like fresh snow on old stone. Because if Tamsin Ellery was alive—if he found her, and finding her was the entire point of him being here—then he would have to stand in front of her and be the answer to a question she had surely spent fifteen years asking: *why?*

Why had he vanished between one wet season and the next. Why no letter. Why her father had aged ten years in a single autumn, going quiet in the way quiet men go quiet, checking the river road every morning for a boat that never came back.

The *Charity* groaned beneath him. Water ticked up through the starboard seam, patient as anything. Ahead, through a tear in the rain, the channel split around a wooded point, and on the point—so faint he might have invented it—there was a smudge of darkness against the gray that was not cloud and not tree smoke and not mist.

Smoke. Low and deliberate, the kind fed by hand, the kind built to be seen.

Wren's hands went still on the pump.

Someone was signaling.
