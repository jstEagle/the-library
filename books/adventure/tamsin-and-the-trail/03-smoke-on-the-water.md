## 3. Smoke on the Water

The rain came first as a rumor—a smell of wet stone and green rot rolling down from the highlands, thick enough to taste on the back of the tongue. Tamsin Ellery stood at the edge of what had been their camp and watched the clouds stack themselves against the western escarpment like slate being loaded onto a barge, layer over layer, until the sky had no color left in it but iron.

Behind her, the camp still smoked.

Not burned entirely—she'd saved that much. The thief's fire had taken the mess tent, the supply crates, half of the survey equipment her father had spent thirty years assembling, and very nearly taken Okafor with it. But the cook shelter stood, and the medicine box had been dragged clear, and the theodolite was wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of the pirogue where she'd slept on it for two nights like a mother over an egg. She counted her losses the way her father had taught her: not in grief, but in inventory. What remained. What could be rebuilt. What was gone beyond any river's power to return.

The maps were gone beyond returning.

She turned that thought over for the hundredth time since the attack, and it cut the same way it had cut the first time—clean and deep, right through the muscle of her chest. Not the paper itself; paper could be redrawn, though it would take years she did not have. It was what the paper *meant*. Eleven seasons of her father's life, every sounding and bearing and hand-inked contour of the upper riverlands, all of it proving what the Vanes had spent three generations denying: that the boundaries on their land grants had been drawn by men who never set foot above the falls, that the river's true course ran miles east of every line their lawyers had inked into the colonial registry. Her father used to say a map was a moral document. That whoever held the map held the truth, and truth was the only inheritance worth passing down.

Now Corvus held it. A man with a knife scar splitting his chin and eyes like coins left too long in water, who had come into their camp smiling, eaten their salt, and struck the match while they slept.

Tamsin crouched beside the signal pyre and checked it again, though she had checked it eleven times already today. Dry palm fronds beneath, stacked in a lattice so air could move through. Green branches bundled on top to make smoke, not flame—the smoke mattered more than the fire, because fire only said *here*, and smoke said *distress*, and distress was what she needed someone upstream or downstream to read. Beside the pyre sat the drum of pitch and the bundle of rags she would soak and light the moment anything moved on the water.

*Anything* was doing a great deal of work in that sentence. In four days, nothing had moved on the water except leaves and once, heartbreakingly, a heron.

* * *

"Ellery." Okafor's voice came from the shade of the cook shelter, thinned by fever but still carrying the dry authority he used on junior surveyors. "You're wearing a groove in the mud. Come sit before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You've said 'I'm fine' so many times the word has lost its meaning, like a coin rubbed smooth." He was propped against a crate with his bandaged leg stretched out before him, and beside him, younger Ferro lay gray-faced under a blanket, the burn salve shining on his forearms. Two injured men. One sound boat, cracked along one seam. No radio—that had gone into the river with the first mate, poor Sefu, when Corvus's men capsized the longboat on the first night. And no father.

Her father had gone upriver alone nine days ago to close out the last survey station at the old mission, taking the skiff and one guide. He should have been back in five. Every hour past five was a stone added to the weight she carried behind her ribs.

"He's alive," Okafor said, watching her face do what her face always did—everything, immediately, like weather. "Aldous Ellery has survived forty years of rivers, warlords, fevers, and his own cooking. One delayed return is not an obituary."

"One delayed return plus an organized arsonist is a pattern," she said. "Corvus didn't burn us for banditry, Okafor. Bandits take the strongbox and go. He took the maps and burned everything that could prove what they were. Someone paid for this." She fed a strip of bark to the small cooking fire, watched it curl. "Someone with a name old enough to need protecting."

Okafor was quiet a moment. "You think Vane."

"I think the land grant office has a Vane signature on every disputed parcel from here to the delta. I think my father wrote to me last spring saying he'd found something in the mission archives that would 'end the family's little empire,' and I think nine days after he went to fetch the rest of the proof, a professional came down the river looking for exactly that proof." She shrugged, and hated how tired the gesture felt. "But thinking isn't knowing, and knowing isn't proving, and none of it matters if we die on this bank waiting for a rescue that isn't coming."

"So the fire."

"So the fire."

* * *

She worked because working was the only prayer she trusted. By early afternoon she had re-lashed the pirogue's cargo net, boiled water for Ferro's dressings, rendered more pitch from the resin stores, and re-surveyed, by eye and dead reckoning, the stretch of river visible from camp—which was to say she had done nothing useful at all, and known it, and done it anyway, because her hands required occupation or they would start shaking and she could not afford shaking. Not yet. Shaking was for later, for the dark hours when Ferro moaned in his sleep and the jungle made its sounds and she lay awake composing letters to a dead man or a living one, she didn't know which, each version worse than the other.

In the letter she composed most often, her father told her not to be angry at the world for being dangerous. Anger was fuel, he said, but fuel burned hot and fast; better to be the slow coal, the one that lasted the night. She had inherited his patience the way some people inherited land—unwillingly, and with obligations attached.

Fifteen years ago she had not been patient. Fifteen years ago she had loved a man with desert dust in his hair and his family's coldness hidden under his charm, and she had believed—she had actually believed—that love was stronger than blood. Wren Vane had stood in her father's study with an offer of partnership in the survey company in his mouth and terror in his eyes, and then he had simply... gone. No letter. No explanation. A horse missing from the stable and a hole in her life shaped exactly like him, which she had spent fifteen years refusing to look at directly, the way you refuse to look at the sun.

She did not let herself think of him often. But tonight, feeding the signal fire its first green branches as the clouds finally broke, she thought of him, and hated how easily he surfaced, like a drowned thing rising.

The rain arrived not as drops but as a decision—one vast sheet of it sweeping across the river toward her, hissing on the water, hammering the broad leaves. She pulled the oiled tarp over the pyre's heart, keeping the coals alive beneath, and stood in the downpour letting it wash the soot and sweat off her while the temperature dropped ten degrees in as many minutes. Above the river, the world went white with rain.

And through that white, faint as a memory, came a sound she had almost stopped believing in.

An engine.

Small. Strained. Fighting the current the way a swimmer fights it, stroke by stubborn stroke.

Tamsin's whole body went still, the way it went still over a critical measurement—as if motion might introduce error. She wiped the rain from her eyes and stared downriver into the gray curtain. For a moment there was nothing. Then the curtain tore, just slightly, and a shape resolved: a flat-bottomed patrol boat, government gray, riding low, its single running lamp smeared into a halo by the rain.

Rescue. Command had sent someone. Or a trader blown off course. Or—

Or Corvus coming back to finish the arithmetic.

She moved without deciding to move. Pitch-soaked rags onto the coals; the pyre caught with a whump that she felt in her teeth; green fronds piled on top until the smoke rose fat and white and unmistakable, standing straight up even in the rain, a column saying *here, here, here* to anyone with eyes. She took up the machete from the crate where it lived and stood between the fire and the shelter where her men lay, feet planted in the mud, rain streaming off her jaw.

The boat came on. Slower now. Its pilot had seen the smoke—she saw the bow swing toward the bank, saw a figure at the wheel lean forward into the rain, squinting up at her column of smoke, at her.

The figure wore a wide-brimmed hat and a ranger's slicker, and even at two hundred meters, even through rain and fifteen years, some animal part of her brain—the part that had memorized the set of shoulders, the angle of a head bent over charts, the particular way he stood when he was trying to seem calmer than he was—began, horribly, hopefully, to recognize him.

No, she told it. No. The world was not so small, and God was not so cruel, and the odds against it were astronomical, and anyway he was in the desert, he had chosen the desert, he had chosen it over—

The boat's engine died. Silence rushed in, huge and wet. The hull kissed the mud of the bank, and the figure stepped down into the shallows, hat brim dripping, and looked up at her through the rain.

Tamsin Ellery did not lower the machete.

"Wren," she said, and the name came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh, "you have got to be joking."
