## 4. Fifteen Years of Silence

*Tamsin*

The boat came out of the rain like something remembered rather than seen.

Tamsin stood at the edge of the burned camp with her signal fire hissing under the first fat drops of the storm, one arm raised, and watched it nose through the brown water—a narrow river skiff, patched canvas over its frame, a lone figure at the stern oar. Relief hit her first, hot and stupid, before her mind caught up to what her eyes were telling her: she knew that stroke. She knew the economy of it, the way the man let the current do half the work, shoulders loose, wrists easy. Fifteen years and two thousand miles of river between them, and her body still recognized him before her heart could put up its defenses.

*Wren Vane.*

Of course. Of course it would be him. The desert sent its rangers when expeditions went silent, and the desert had always kept Wren Vane like a coin in its pocket.

She lowered her arm slowly. Around her, the camp told its story in charcoal and ash—the collapsed mess tent, the black ribs of the survey crates, the shallow graves she'd dug for Ruiz and Okafor with a broken entrenching tool while the others watched from the shade cloth. Two injured still breathing in the lean-to she'd lashed together from pole and tarp: Ferro with the arrow wound through his shoulder, and little Adaeze, feverish, her burned hands wrapped in boiled bandages Tamsin had changed twice a day because there was no one else to do it.

She had not survived nine days alone on this bank by being soft, and she was not going to start now.

The boat ground against the shallows. He stepped out into knee-deep water, hauling the bowline, and even soaked to the waist he moved like he had at twenty-two—all that careful strength, all that terrible gentleness. His hat was gone. Rain flattened his dark hair against his skull, and when he looked up and saw her standing among the ashes, his whole face did something complicated and then went carefully blank, the way it always had when feeling got too large for him.

"Tamsin." Her name came out of him like something he'd been carrying a long way.

"Ranger Vane." She made it official. Made it cold. "You're late."

Something flickered behind his eyes—hurt, maybe, or recognition of exactly how much he deserved that—but he only said, "I came as fast as the river allowed."

"The river's been rising for three days." She turned on her heel, already walking, because if she stopped moving she'd have to look at him, and looking at him was a luxury she couldn't afford until everyone was safe. "Ferro took an arrow. Adaeze has burns. We have food for four days and no boat worth the name. If you're here to file a report, file it walking."

He followed her through the wreckage without asking questions, which was one kindness, and she hated herself a little for noticing it as a kindness at all.

* * *

*Wren*

The camp was worse than the radio silence had suggested. Command had given him the bare bones—Ellery expedition, no contact in eleven days, last known position above the Serrano narrows—and he had filled in the rest himself during four days of hard rowing, and every version he'd imagined had been bad. None of them had been this: the sweet-rot smell of burned canvas, the two mounds of fresh-turned earth already greening with silt, the woman moving through it all with a field medic's brisk certainty and a general's authority, as though grief were simply another supply to be rationed.

He helped her lift Ferro onto a stretcher of poles and tarpaulin. The man was gray-lipped but conscious, and he looked at Wren with the flat assessment of a soldier counting weapons.

"You're the ranger," Ferro rasped.

"I'm the ranger."

"Then you can row." It wasn't gratitude. It was arithmetic.

Wren almost smiled. He liked this man already.

Adaeze was worse—fever-bright, mumbling about fire, her bandaged hands curled like scorched leaves against her chest. Tamsin knelt beside her and fed her water from a tin cup with a patience so unguarded it hurt to watch, and Wren understood, watching her, that this was the woman she'd become in his absence: someone who ran expeditions and dug graves and held dying men's heads and never once asked the sky why. Someone who had learned to need nothing.

Especially not him.

"We move at first light," she said, standing, wringing out her sleeves. "The trading post at Puerto Bravo is two days downstream if the water holds. Corvus will have passed through there."

"Corvus?"

She finally looked at him straight on, and the look had fifteen years of interest accruing on it. "You really don't know. You came all this way and you don't know anything."

"Command told me the expedition went silent. That's all. I swear it."

"Your swearing," she said quietly, "has a history."

The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream thunder rolled along the canyon walls like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor. Wren stood in it and took the blow, because there was nowhere to put it down.

"Tell me what happened," he said. "All of it. I'm listening."

* * *

*Tamsin*

She told it in the lean-to, by lamplight, while the storm worked itself up to something serious outside. Ferro slept. Adaeze muttered. The tarp overhead drummed like a second heartbeat.

Her father had led them upriver to finish the Ellery Survey—the great unfinished work, the maps her grandfather had started and her father had bled thirty years to complete. They'd made camp below the old mission bend. And on the ninth night, men had come out of the dark with knives and torches, quiet as malaria.

"They didn't rob us," she said. "That's what I keep turning over. They burned the camp, they shot Ferro, they took Papa's survey chest and nothing else—not the payroll, not the instruments, not the rifles. Just the maps. And their leader—" she wrapped her hands around the tin cup, needing something solid, "—he wore a black feather stitched at his collar. Corvus. Papa warned me about him years ago. A thief who works for people who can't be seen hiring thieves."

"And your father? Where is he?"

"Gone." Her voice cracked on it and she hated that too. "When the shooting started, he pushed me into the brush with the wounded and went back for the duplicate plates. I haven't seen him since. Nine days."

Wren was silent a long moment. Rain hammered the tarp. When he spoke, his voice was strange—careful, like a man walking on ice over deep water.

"What was in those maps, Tamsin? What was worth killing for?"

She laughed, and it came out bitter as quinine. "You're asking me that? *You?*" She set the cup down hard. "Fine. Since you've apparently forgotten your own family's business. The Ellery Survey doesn't just chart the riverlands, Wren. It dates them. Boundary stones, land grants, the old colonial registry—it proves where every claim begins. And my father spent thirty years confirming what my grandfather suspected before him. That the Vane holdings—the entire lower basin, forty miles of the best water and soil on this river—rest on a forged grant. Your great-grandfather bought a clerk and backdated a signature, and every deed, every title, every acre your family has paraded for four generations is built on paper that was never legal. Papa's maps prove it. Prove it beyond appeal. That's what Corvus stole. That's what someone paid to make disappear."

The silence after was total except for the rain.

She watched it land on him. Watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out. He had gone very still—that particular stillness she remembered from their one year together, the stillness of a man absorbing damage rather than showing it.

"My name," he said at last, barely above the rain. "My family's name is the crime scene."

"Now you understand why I don't want your apologies." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, armor over the wound. "Every kind thing you ever did me, every hour we had—I can't trust any of it anymore, because I have to wonder if you knew. If you were sent. Command doesn't send a desert ranger upriver because he knows the water, Wren. Command sends him because command wants a report that says *no survivors.*"

"That's not why I came."

"Isn't it? Then tell me your orders. Word for word. Can you?"

And there it was—the flinch. Small, quickly mastered, but she had spent fifteen years learning to read rivers and she read him just as easily. He looked at the lamp flame instead of her.

"They told me to locate the expedition," he said slowly. "Assess. Report."

"Report what, Wren? Report *what?*"

Thunder cracked close enough to shiver the lamp. Outside, the river had begun to speak in a new voice—deeper, hungrier, the sound of a flood gathering its argument. Ferro stirred and groaned. Adaeze whimpered in her fever.

Wren stood abruptly, needing to move, and went to the mouth of the lean-to where the rain sheeted silver in the lamplight. For a long time he just watched the water rise over the graves she had dug, and she watched his back, that familiar broad tired back, and felt the old treacherous ache underneath all the anger, the part of her that had never once stopped—

No. She shut that door. She'd bricked it over years ago; she wasn't going to let a flooded river knock it down.

"At first light," she said, "we load your boat. Ferro and Adaeze first. Then us. Puerto Bravo before the post closes for the storm season, or we lose Corvus's trail for six months."

He didn't turn around. "And after Puerto Bravo?"

"After Puerto Bravo," she said, "you and I are going to have a much longer conversation about your orders. And about fifteen years ago. In whichever order you fear most."

His shoulders moved—almost a laugh, mostly not. "Fair enough."

"And Wren." He turned then, rain-light on his face, and she made herself hold his eyes because looking away would cost more than looking. "Whatever you are to me now—stranger, enemy, ghost—I need your hands and your boat. That's all I need. Don't confuse it with anything else."

"No," he said quietly. "I won't."

But as she rolled into her bedroll with her back to him, listening to the river climb and climb, Tamsin thought she heard him say something else, so low the rain nearly ate it—something that might have been *I'm sorry*, or might have been *I found her*, or might have been only the water.

By morning, the lowland trail was gone. By morning, the choice of routes was gone too. There was only the river, risen brown and roaring through the drowned willows, running fast toward Puerto Bravo—and somewhere ahead of them, riding that same flood, a man with a black feather at his collar who thought every witness to the Ellery Survey was dead.

He was about to learn otherwise.
