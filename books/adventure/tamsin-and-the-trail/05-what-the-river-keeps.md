## 5. What the River Keeps

The river had changed its mind overnight.

Wren stood at the water's edge in the grey half-light and watched it, the way he'd watched it for fifteen years without ever quite learning to read it. Down here, where the desert gave way to scrub and the scrub gave way to green, the water ran brown and muscular, swollen with rain that had fallen a hundred miles away in country he'd never see. It carried whole trees past him, roots first, like drowned men swimming for their lives.

"Two feet higher than yesterday," Tamsin said behind him. She was coiling rope with quick, economical hands, not looking at him. "Maybe three."

"You've been measuring?"

"I measure everything." She said it flatly, but something flickered under it—pride, or grief, or both. "My father taught me. A river is just arithmetic moving too fast to count."

He remembered her father then, unbidden: Aldous Ellery, broad-shouldered at a surveyor's table, tapping a brass instrument and saying *measure twice, boy, because the land doesn't forgive*. Wren had been nineteen and desperate to be useful. He'd measured everything too, back then. He'd stopped when he learned what his family's measurements were worth.

"We run it today," he said.

Tamsin's head came up. "In this boat? With two men who can barely hold a paddle?"

"The trading post closes its ledger at month's end. Corvus will want to sell before the flood cuts the road." Wren turned to face her fully. Her hair was tied back, her jaw set in a line he recognized from a younger version of her face, and it hurt in an old, unhealed way. "If we wait for the river to drop, we wait a week. If we wait a week, the maps are gone."

"And if we run it and capsize, we're all dead, and the maps are still gone."

"Yes," he said. "But dead people don't have to watch them go."

Something almost like a laugh moved through her—not warmth, exactly, but the ghost of it, the shape of a woman she'd been before he'd taught her to guard herself. She looked at the river for a long moment, doing her arithmetic.

"Portage around the worst of it," she said finally. "You take the tiller through the middle channel. I'll call the water."

"You'll call the water," he repeated.

"I know this river better than you do, Vane. I've spent three years on it." She shouldered the coil of rope. "You know boats. I know this water. That's the trade. Don't make it more complicated than it is."

It wasn't complicated. It was the simplest thing in the world, and the hardest: standing next to her again, dividing labor like they'd never divided anything else, as though the silence between them were just another stretch of unmapped country they'd agreed not to enter.

* * *

They portaged the first bend, hauling the boat over slick red stone while Ferro limped ahead and Pell sat propped against a boulder, his bandaged arm cradled against his chest, watching them with the weary patience of a man who had decided not to die today and was sticking to it.

"The middle channel," Tamsin said, crouching at the lip of the rapids. Below them the river funneled between two shelves of rock, white water stacking up in long diagonal ridges. "There's a hole on the left side about halfway down—a stopper, it'll hold anything that goes in. Stay right of the seam where the brown water meets the green."

Wren studied the line. Fifteen years of desert patrols, and his eye still went to the water the way it had when he was young, reading current like text. The seam she meant was visible even from here—a clean line drawn down the channel, calm on one side, chaos on the other.

"You've run this?" he asked.

"Twice. Once going up, once coming down fast with Corvus shooting at us." She said it the way another woman might report the weather. "He's a good shot from a moving boat. Remember that."

"I remember everything you tell me," Wren said, before he could stop himself.

She didn't answer. She got into the bow and picked up her paddle, and the conversation was over, and the river waited below them like a debt coming due.

* * *

The first mile was manageable. The second was a lie.

The channel narrowed without warning where a slide of boulders had come down since her last passage—new rock, raw and pale, the river still deciding how to go around it. Wren felt the boat's response change under his hands, the tiller going light, the current grabbing the hull sideways with a casual, enormous strength.

"Right!" Tamsin's voice cracked across the water. "Hard right, there's a strainer—"

He saw it a heartbeat later: a fallen tree wedged between the rocks, branches combing the surface, the kind of trap that held a boat and drowned everyone in it. He threw his weight against the tiller. The boat slewed. Ferro shouted something. And then the world tilted, because the current found the seam after all, the brown water folding over the green, and the starboard gunwale dipped toward the white ridge of a standing wave with the slow, terrible certainty of a door closing.

"Tamsin—" 

"I see it!" She was already moving, shifting her weight forward and left, paddle braced against the water like a lever. "Lean! Lean left, all of you, NOW—"

Wren threw himself across the thwart. For one suspended moment the boat lay on its side in the air of its own intention, water pouring green and cold across his shoulders, Ferro's boots skidding past his face—and then Tamsin's brace caught, her paddle biting deep, and the hull swung back beneath them with a groan, slamming flat onto the wave's shoulder and riding it down.

They came out the other side in a chaos of spray and shouting, spinning, shipping half a ton of brown water, alive.

Alive.

Wren's hands were shaking so badly he could hardly hold the tiller. He bailed with his free arm, scooping frantically, and heard Tamsin doing the same in the bow, and above the roar of the water he heard Ferro laughing—a high, unhinged, grateful sound—and Pell saying quietly, over and over, "Thank God, thank God."

"That was my fault," Wren called forward. "I was late on the—"

"It wasn't your fault." Her voice cut through the noise, hard and clear. "That rock wasn't on any chart. Nobody could have seen it. You corrected fast enough to save us." A pause, and then, grudgingly, as if each word cost her something: "Your line through the chute was good. Before that. Clean."

He stared at the back of her head. Water dripped off his nose. Somewhere inside his chest, a door he'd bricked over fifteen years ago developed a hairline crack, and warm light came through it, and he hated how much he wanted to stand in that light.

"You always did call water well," he said.

"My father said I read rivers like other girls read letters." She didn't turn around. But her shoulders had come down from around her ears, just slightly. Just enough.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar high above the flood line, a tongue of pale stone in the dark throat of the valley. Ferro and Pell slept by the fire, exhausted into honesty, their breathing long and even. The river talked to itself in the dark, endless, patient.

Wren took the first watch because it was easier than sleeping near her. He sat with his back against a drift log and let the cold come up through him, and after an hour he heard her move, and didn't pretend not to.

She sat down three feet away. Close enough to talk. Far enough to keep the account balanced.

"Fifteen years," she said, to the fire, not to him. "I used to draft letters to you. In my head. On the trail, at night, when the work was done. Angry ones, mostly. Then, later, just... reports. As if you were a colleague. As if I could reduce you to data." She turned a stick over in her fingers. "Do you know what the strange thing is? Today, in the rapids—I didn't think. I called the line and you took it, instantly, no argument, and for half a second it felt like nothing had ever happened. Like muscle memory. Like trust."

"It was trust," Wren said. "Whatever else it is now."

"Don't." The word came out sharp. Then softer: "Don't say things like that until I know what you're for, Wren. Command sent you upriver. Men like you don't get sent by accident."

"I'm not a man like me," he said, and heard how it sounded, and almost laughed. "I don't know what I'm for either. That's the truth. I've spent fifteen years being nobody's son, nobody's heir, nobody's anything, and it turns out the name I ran from followed me anyway. It's waiting downstream at that trading post, probably, in whatever Corvus sells and whoever buys it."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Downriver, thunder muttered, the monsoon gathering itself for the real work.

"When we find those maps," she said at last, "and we will—they name your family, Wren. Every forged boundary stone. Every bribed clerk. My father spent thirty years proving it. If you help me publish them, you lose everything you have left. Your pension. Your commission. Whatever's left of your family that isn't rotten."

"I never had any of that," he said. "I had miles. Miles and years, and neither of them ever added up to anything." He looked at her—at the firelight on her face, at the exhaustion and the iron underneath it. "The maps are worth more than my name. They always were. I just needed someone to show me the line through the chute."

Something shifted in her expression—too small to name, too large to ignore. She reached out and took the stick from his hand, feeding it to the fire, and her fingers brushed his, brief and deliberate, and neither of them acknowledged it, and neither of them forgot it.

"Sleep," she said, standing. "You take second watch. Wake me at moonrise."

"There's no moon tonight. Storm coming."

"Then wake me when the rain starts." At the edge of the firelight she paused, her back to him. "And Wren. Tomorrow, at the post—if it comes to violence—you watch my left side. I'll watch yours."

"Like old times," he said quietly.

She didn't answer. But she didn't deny it either, and that, he thought, watching her settle into her bedroll with her back straight and her hand near her knife, was the closest thing to forgiveness the river would give them today.

He woke her when the first drops fell, fat and warm out of a black sky, and she rose without complaint and took the watch, and he lay listening to the rain thicken on the canvas and thought: *the trading post tomorrow, and whatever waits there.*

He didn't yet know that what waited there wasn't only Corvus.

It was a ledger with a Vane signature in it, fresh ink, dated three days ago—and a man at the post who knew Wren's face, and had already sent word downriver that the last Ellery was still alive.
