## 6. The Trading Post Ledger

The trading post announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke, river mud, and the sweet rot of fruit no one had bothered to sell. Tamsin saw it first from the bow, a huddle of buildings on stilts above the flood line, tin roofs patched with canvas and hope. A dock of blackened pilings jutted into the brown water like broken teeth.

"Kestrel's Landing," Wren said behind her. His voice had gone flat in that way she remembered too well—the ranger's voice, the one that filed everything under terrain and threat and left nothing over for feeling.

She didn't answer. Fifteen years of silence had taught her how to let him hang in his own quiet.

The two injured crew lay amidships, Pak wrapped in blankets against the fever climbing through him, Sefa pale but conscious, her splinted arm propped on a coil of rope. Tamsin had changed the dressings twice since dawn with hands that only shook when she let herself think about her father. She did not let herself think about her father. She thought about ledgers instead. Ledgers were safe. Ledgers told you exactly what was owed.

* * *

Kestrel himself was a broad woman of sixty-odd with forearms like mooring line and eyes that had priced every soul who ever walked up her dock. She took one look at their boat—one look at *Wren*—and spat over the rail.

"Ranger Vane." The name landed between them like a dropped anchor.

"You know me?" Wren asked.

"I know the name." She jerked her chin at Tamsin. "This one I know by face. Ellery's girl. You came through three weeks back with your father and eight others, bought fuel and rice, drew charts on my counter." Her gaze sharpened. "Then Corvus came."

Tamsin's heart stopped, started again, harder. "You've seen him? Recently?"

"Seen him." Kestrel laughed without any humor in it. "He sat where you're standing and paid me gold to forget he'd been here. Which is more than the other one offered."

"The other one," Tamsin said slowly. "What other one?"

* * *

Inside, the post smelled of lamp oil and dried fish. Kestrel unlocked a cabinet and brought out a ledger bound in cracked leather, its pages soft as cloth from years of wet thumbs. She laid it open on the counter and turned back through the entries while rain began ticking on the tin roof overhead.

"Three weeks past. Ellery expedition. Fuel, rice, quinine, chart paper." Her blunt finger moved down the column. "Two days later, this." An entry in a different hand, cramped and precise: *Corvus. Passage downstream. One crate, sealed. Paid triple for discretion.*

"One crate," Tamsin whispered. "Sealed."

"Maps don't weigh much," Wren said quietly.

"No," she agreed. "But they're worth everything." She looked up at him and found him already watching her, something grim settling over his features like weather. "Keep reading," she said to Kestrel. "There was another name you mentioned."

Kestrel turned one more page. "Four days after your father. This gentleman." She tapped an entry: *Aldous Renwick, agent. Passage upstream. Retainers disbursed: three names struck from record. Paid for silence, all accounts closed.*

Tamsin read it twice. *Retainers disbursed.* Three names. Three people paid to un-know what they knew.

"Renwick," she said. "That's a Vane name if I've ever heard one. Your family's man of business. He used to come to the house when I was a girl, all ink-stained cuffs and smiles. Father called him the Vane family's broom." She looked up. "Sweeps things under carpets."

Wren had gone very still. "Read it again," he said. "All of it."

Kestrel read it aloud, flat as a tide table. And Tamsin watched the color leave Wren's face degree by degree, and understood that whatever he'd been carrying upriver beside her, it had just gotten heavier.

* * *

They got the crew onto cots in the post's back room. Kestrel's wife—a silent, capable woman who communicated entirely in gestures—brought willow bark tea and clean bandages and asked no questions, which Tamsin suspected cost extra. Pak's fever broke near dusk. Sefa slept.

Tamsin found Wren on the covered porch, sitting on the top step with his elbows on his knees, watching the river rise. The rain had thickened from ticking to drumming, and beyond the dock the water had climbed the second piling. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled like furniture being moved in a giant's house.

She sat down beside him. Not close. A hand's width of space, maintained with the precision of a surveyor.

"So," she said. "Tell me why you're really here."

He didn't pretend not to understand. That was one thing about him she'd never hated—Wren Vane had never learned to lie well, which made his silences worse, not better.

"Command sent me," he said.

"To rescue us."

The pause lasted three seconds. In those three seconds, fifteen years rearranged themselves inside her chest.

"They sent me to confirm the expedition dead," he said. "All hands lost. Report back, close the file. There was—" His jaw worked. "There was a secondary instruction. Sealed orders. If survivors were found, I was to assess whether they possessed documents. If they did, I was to secure them and report to Command directly. Not to headquarters. To a specific office. Aldous Renwick's office."

The rain filled the silence after that, drumming on the tin roof, streaming off the eaves in silver ropes.

"You were sent," Tamsin said, and her voice came out low and even, which surprised her, because inside she was anything but even, "to bury us. My father. My crew. Me. You were sent to find our bodies—or our papers—and make sure neither ever surfaced."

"Tamsin—"

"Were you going to do it?" She turned to face him fully now. "If we'd been dead on a sandbar, would you have reported it and ridden home? And if we'd been alive—if you'd found us with the maps—what then? Would you have taken them? Would you have—" She stopped, because the next question was the one that mattered, and she made herself ask it. "Would you have left us for Corvus?"

"No." It came out fast, raw. "No. I burned through that question somewhere around the second rapid. I don't—I haven't decided what I am yet, Tamsin. But I know what I'm not. I'm not the man who leaves you twice."

"You left me once."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, and that undid her more than any argument could have. "Yes, I did. And I've spent fifteen years telling myself it was duty, and it wasn't. It was cowardice wearing duty's coat. But this—" He pulled something from inside his jacket. Folded paper, oilskin-wrapped, travel-worn. "These are my orders. Signed by Command. Countersigned by Renwick. They sent a ranger whose family owns the lie to bury the proof of the lie. They thought I'd be grateful. They thought I'd want to protect the inheritance."

"Do you?" she asked. "Want it?"

He looked out at the river. The water had swallowed the third piling now, and the current carried whole trees past the dock, torn loose somewhere upstream, riding the flood like passengers.

"My father made me memorize our claim boundaries before I could write my own name," he said. "Every fence line, every survey stone. He called it the family scripture. I recited it at fourteen and felt sick the whole time and didn't know why until I was old enough to read the original grant myself and see the dates didn't match the surveys. The riverlands were never ours, Tamsin. We wrote ourselves onto them." He laughed, short and bitter. "And then I ran away to the desert to get away from it, and fifteen years later Command hands me sealed orders and asks me to carry the same lie one more mile. Because who else? I know the water. And I'm a Vane. Who would suspect a Vane of drowning the truth?"

Tamsin sat with it. The betrayal was real and it deserved its weight; she let it sit on her chest and press. But underneath it, another thing pressed too—the memory of a young man who'd gone gray-faced reciting boundary lines at a dinner table, who'd once traced the river's course on her father's drafts with a fingertip and said, *I wish someone would map it honestly.* She had loved that boy. She was beginning to suspect the man still contained him.

"You should have told me on the boat," she said finally. "Day one."

"I know."

"You should have told me before the rapids."

"I know."

"You should have told me last night, in the cave, instead of—" She stopped. Last night, in the cave, there had been a moment. His hand finding hers in the dark when the thunder came. Neither of them had named it. She wasn't going to name it now, not with this between them.

"If I'd told you day one," Wren said, "you'd have put me off the boat."

"Would I have?"

"Yes," he said. "And you'd have been right to."

Something in her cracked—not broke, cracked, the way ice cracks before it becomes navigable. She exhaled.

"What happens now," she said, "depends on what you do with those orders."

Wren looked down at the oilskin packet in his hands. Then he rose, crossed to the brazier Kestrel kept burning on the porch against the damp, and stood there a long moment, the firelight moving on his face.

"Fifteen years ago I wouldn't sign their claim," he said, half to her, half to the fire. "So I ran. Running was the only refusal I knew. This is the other kind." He unwrapped the oilskin. The folded pages caught the light—Command's seal, Renwick's countersignature, the neat bureaucratic language of erasure. *Confirm loss of all hands. Secure any cartographic materials. Report to R. Renwick, Esq.*

He fed them into the coals.

The paper curled, blackened, lifted. Ash rose through the rain-holes in the porch roof and was snatched away downwind, over the river, gone. Wren watched every bit of it burn, and when the last corner flared and died, his shoulders came down from around his ears for the first time since she'd found him on that sandbar.

"There," he said quietly. "Now I'm just a deserter with a boat."

"Now you're a witness," Tamsin corrected. "There's a difference. Deserter runs. Witness testifies."

He turned to look at her, and in the brazier light his eyes were wet, though his voice held steady. "Testify to what? I have no proof. Renwick has the ledger entry, Corvus has the maps, and my word against my own family isn't worth the breath it takes."

"It will be," Tamsin said. "Because we're going to get the proof back."

"How? Corvus went downstream three weeks ago with a sealed crate. He could be anywhere."

"He's not anywhere." She stood, brushing damp from her trousers, and felt the old certainty rise in her—the surveyor's certainty, the one that had kept her alive in the canyon country her father taught her to read. "Corvus doesn't keep what he steals. He sells it. And there's exactly one buyer in these territories who'd pay for Vane-family secrets—and one broker who moves goods between them. Kestrel said Renwick paid three retainers. Struck from record. But Kestrel's ledger wasn't struck." She tapped the doorway behind them, where the old woman's book lay open on the counter. "Corvus sold the crate here or passed it to a courier here. Either way, there's a trail, and Kestrel knows more than she's selling. Everyone on this river sells eventually. We just have to meet her price."

From inside the post, a floorboard creaked. Kestrel stood in the doorway, arms folded, having heard every word, and her mouth curved into something that was almost a smile.

"Meet my price," she said. "Clever girl. Like her father." She stepped out into the rain-shadow of the roof and lowered her voice, though there was no one for a hundred miles to hear it. "You're right that Corvus passed through. Wrong about the crate. He kept it sealed because he hadn't found his buyer yet. The buyer's coming to him. Upstream. Day after tomorrow, maybe sooner with this flood." Her eyes flicked to Wren, then back. "And the man coming to buy your father's life's work is Aldous Renwick himself. Coming up the river in a steam launch, flying no colors, with four armed men and a strongbox full of gold."

Tamsin felt the plan assemble itself in her chest, sudden and complete, like a chart snapping into register. "Then we don't chase him downstream," she said. "We wait. We let the buyer come to the merchandise."

"And when he comes?" Wren asked.

She looked at him—at the ash still on his fingers, at the man who had just burned fifteen years of obedience in a brazier—and gave him the ghost of a smile.

"Then we take back what's ours," she said. "Both of ours."

Out on the black water, lightning stitched the horizon, and beneath the thunder, faint but unmistakable, came the sound none of them wanted to hear yet: a steam whistle, far upstream, cutting through the rain.
