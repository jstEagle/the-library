## 7. Monsoon

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, a gray wall sweeping up the gorge like a door slamming shut behind them.

Wren heard it before he saw it—a sound like the whole sky tearing—and then the world beyond the cave mouth dissolved into water. The river below had been rising all afternoon, brown and muscular, shouldering through the lowlands with a patience that frightened him more than violence would have. Now it climbed in earnest. He stood at the lip of the cave and watched it take the tamarisk flats, the old landing, the dead campfire they'd abandoned an hour earlier. It swallowed them without ceremony, the way a ledger swallows figures.

Behind him, Tamsin was working by lamplight, re-splinting Okafor's forearm while Ruiz held the man's shoulder steady. She'd done this three times since the rapids, each time faster, her hands sure even when her voice wasn't. The injured crew lay on bedrolls against the far wall of the cave, above a tide line of old flood silt that told Wren everything he needed to know about how high the water could reach.

"Higher than this," he said quietly, to no one, and went to check the back of the cavern.

The cave ran deep—forty feet, maybe fifty, narrowing toward a chimney of cracked stone that breathed cold air down on them. High enough. Dry enough. For now. He'd run storms like this before, alone, counting hours by the sound of the rain. But he'd never run one with four other heartbeats depending on his judgment, two of them broken, and one of them belonging to a woman who hadn't spoken to him except in orders for three days.

He came back to the fire ring they'd built from driftwood and stone. The flame threw Tamsin's shadow huge against the wall, her silhouette bent over Okafor's arm like a question mark.

"How is he?"

"Healing," she said, not looking up. "Or pretending to. Same thing out here." She tied off the splint, sat back on her heels, and only then looked at him. Her eyes were rimmed red—not tears, he realized, but days without sleep. "How's the river?"

"Still climbing. It'll crest sometime tonight or tomorrow morning. We're above the line if we stay back."

"If."

"I checked the chimney. It's climbable, if it comes to that."

She nodded once, filed it away, turned back to her patient. Conversation over. That was how she managed him now—inventory and logistics, nothing that required looking at him longer than necessary.

Ruiz cleared his throat, gathered the cook pot, and said something about checking the stores, which was a lie so transparent that Wren almost smiled. The crew had developed a talent for vanishing whenever the two of them occupied the same ten feet of space. Even Okafor, half-dazed with fever, had learned to sleep loudly at the wrong moments.

The rain thickened. Thunder rolled down the gorge and came back off the cliffs doubled.

Tamsin finally sat, wrapping her arms around her knees, staring out at the gray curtain where the world used to be. The lamplight caught the map case strapped across her chest—she hadn't taken it off since the trading post, wouldn't let anyone else carry it, slept with it buckled to her like a second spine. Inside were the pages Corvus hadn't gotten: fragments, copies, her father's field notes. Enough to prove the shape of the theft, not enough to end it.

"You should sleep," Wren said.

"So should you."

"One of us keeping watch makes sense."

"Both of us keeping watch makes more." She didn't look at him. "I've spent fifteen years learning not to trust anyone's watch but my own. Forgive me if I don't break the habit tonight."

The words landed harder than she probably intended—or exactly as hard as she intended; he could never tell anymore. He sat down across the fire from her, close enough to talk, far enough to pretend otherwise. Water drummed on stone outside. Somewhere in the dark, the river made a sound like grinding teeth.

For a long time neither of them spoke. Then Tamsin said, almost to herself:

"My father used to say monsoons were honest. Everything else in the territory lies—the maps lie, the land claims lie, the people lie—but a flood just tells you what's true. What's solid. What holds." A short, bitter breath. "I used to think that was wisdom. Now I think he just liked the way it excused him from ever saying anything directly."

Wren fed a stick to the fire. "You're angry with him."

"I'm furious with him. He took me on this expedition because I'm the best surveyor he knows, and he still didn't tell me what we were really hunting until we were three weeks upriver." She shook her head. "The forged claims. Your family's name on land that was measured and mapped and *belonged* to other people long before any Vane ever drew a boundary line. He knew. He'd known for years. And he waited until there was no turning back before he told me why it mattered."

"Why does it matter?" Wren asked, and heard how flat his own voice came out. "To you. Beyond the justice of it."

She looked at him then, really looked, and the lamplight made her face older than fifteen years should have allowed. "Because my grandfather surveyed those lands honestly. Because his name is on the original charts, and your family's name got stamped over his like a boot print on a letter. Because I grew up watching my father fight a war of paper against men who owned every court and clerk in the territory, and lose, and keep fighting anyway." Her jaw tightened. "And because someone burned our camp and left two of my crew bleeding and stole fifteen years of my father's work in a single night, and I would like very much to know whether the man sitting across from me was sent to finish the job."

The question hung between them, and the storm filled the silence after it.

Wren had rehearsed answers for three days. Command's version: he was dispatched to confirm casualties, assess conditions, report. The true version—the one he'd been carrying since the trading post, since he'd burned his written orders page by page in the cook fire while she watched with an expression he couldn't read—that version had no rehearsal. It had only weight.

"The truth," he said, "is that I didn't know what I was sent to do until I read my own orders twice."

"And what did they say?"

"They said: confirm the Ellery expedition lost. All hands. Report findings to territorial command." He met her eyes. "There was no line about rescue. There was no line about survivors. Someone in command wanted the expedition officially dead, and I was the instrument they chose because I know the water and—" he stopped, started again, "—because I carry a name that opens doors your father's name can't."

Tamsin was very still. "Whose order?"

"Warrant Officer Dree. Who reports to Commissioner Halloway. Who dines twice a season with my father." He said it plainly, laying each fact down like a card he couldn't take back. "I burned the orders. You saw me do it. Whatever I am now, I'm not their instrument anymore. But I won't insult you by claiming I figured that out on my own. I figured it out standing in front of you, wanting to be a man you could look at without flinching."

The fire popped. Rain hammered on. Tamsin's expression didn't soften, but something in it shifted—a hairline crack, like the ones in his borrowed hull.

"That's not the whole truth," she said.

"No," Wren agreed. His heart was suddenly loud. "It isn't."

* * *

The storm reached its full voice around midnight. Lightning turned the cave mouth into a strobing frame of white water, and inside, the lamplight seemed to shrink, huddling closer to them. Okafor slept in true fever now, muttering; Ruiz dozed sitting up with a rifle across his knees; the fourth crew member, Pell, kept a silent vigil near the injured, pretending not to listen, failing completely.

Tamsin moved closer to the fire. Not beside Wren—there was still a careful arm's length of stone between them—but closer. Close enough that he could see the rain-reflections moving in her eyes.

"Tell me why you ran," she said.

Not *why you left*. Why you *ran*. She'd always known the difference, even at nineteen, even sobbing on the dock while his boat shrank downstream. He had spent fifteen years building a story about ambition, about restlessness, about a young man who loved the desert more than any woman. He'd told it so many times he'd nearly believed it himself.

But the monsoon was honest. His own words came back to him, and he found he had no appetite for lying in front of it.

"My father called me into the study the winter before the wedding," Wren said. The memory rose whole, undimmed, as if it had been waiting fifteen years in the dark for exactly this weather. "Cedar paneling, brandy, the Vane crest over the mantel—you remember the house."

"I remember being shown the garden and not the study."

"He put a document on the desk. The land claim. The original—the forged one, the one your grandfather's surveys contradict. My great-grandfather's signature at the bottom, and a space beneath it." Wren's hands had gone cold; he watched them as though they belonged to someone else. "One space. One name. Mine. Heir apparent, affirming and ratifying the claim, binding myself to it in law and blood. He'd had the clerks prepare it years in advance. He'd been waiting for me to come of age, marry well, and make the family legitimate forever."

Tamsin had gone very quiet.

"He said, 'Sign it, and everything is yours. The riverlands, the holdings, the name entire. Sign it, and bring your bride into it.'" Wren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He knew about you. Of course he knew. He'd investigated you the way he investigates everything—your father's debts, your grandfather's charts, the whole lineage. And he thought—" Wren pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "God help me, he thought offering me the choice was generous. Sign and inherit everything, or refuse and inherit nothing. He genuinely believed I'd choose the name. Everyone chooses the name. No one walks away from the Vane name."

"But you did."

"I walked out of the study. I walked to the boatyard. I took the patrol skiff and I went upstream in the dark, and I told myself I'd come back in a week with some compromise, some third path." His voice dropped. "I never found one. Every week I didn't go back made going back harder. Every year made it impossible. And somewhere in there I stopped telling myself I'd left to find a third path and started telling myself I'd left because I wanted to. Because the desert was simpler than love. Because distance was cleaner than choosing."

"And was it?"

"No." The word came out of him like something extracted. "It was the coward's arithmetic. I traded one grief for a smaller one and called the trade freedom. I measured my life in miles because miles don't ask questions. Fifteen years, Tamsin. Fifteen years of patrols and reports and empty stations, and every single day I knew exactly what I'd done, and every single day I told myself the story that made it bearable." He looked up at her across the fire. "I didn't leave because I stopped loving you. I left because loving you meant losing everything else, and I was too much my father's son to believe I could survive the loss. That's the whole truth. Ugly, small, and fifteen years late."

The storm roared. Water sheeted past the cave mouth in silver ropes. Tamsin didn't move for a long moment, and Wren braced himself—for anger, for contempt, for the cold efficiency she'd worn like armor since Smoke on the Water, since the burned camp, since the moment she'd recognized his boat and her face had closed like a door.

Instead she said, "I signed something too."

He blinked. "What?"

"After you left. My father put papers in front of me—deeds, actually. Our claim to the survey lands, the honest one, the one your family's forgery buried. He said, 'Sign, and spend your life fighting for it, or don't sign, and live free of it.' And I signed." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I was so angry—at you, at him, at the whole rotten architecture of names and inheritance—I signed out of spite. I thought, *I will become the thing the Vanes cannot buy.* I gave my life to those maps because you taught me exactly what a name costs."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." She raised a hand, not unkindly. "Let me finish. I'm not telling you this to wound you. I'm telling you because I need you to understand what I worked out on the dock, crying, fifteen years ago, and have spent every year since confirming." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "I hated you for leaving. I hated you properly, thoroughly, for years. And then one day I was thirty, and good at my work, and free in every way I'd chosen to be free, and I realized the hate had gone quiet. Not forgiven. Just... quiet. Like a river under ice." She paused. "And then Corvus burned our camp, and my father went missing, and a ranger came upriver in a leaking boat with your face and your hands and your infuriating competence, and the ice broke, and I discovered the hate had been holding something down all along."

The lamplight flickered. Neither of them moved.

"What was it holding down?" Wren asked, though he already knew. He'd known since the rapids, since her hand locked around his wrist in the white water and for one suspended moment they moved as a single animal, and something in his chest had torn open along an old seam.

"You know what it was holding down."

"Say it anyway."

She crossed the arm's length of stone between them—not all at once, but the way she did everything, deliberately, surveyor's precision, closing the distance by degrees until her shoulder rested against his. Warm. Solid. Real.

"I never stopped," she said simply. "Fifteen years, and I never stopped. I just built a life tall enough to hide it in."

Wren exhaled, a breath he felt he'd been holding since the study, since the dock, since the first mile of a life measured in miles instead of years. He didn't kiss her. Not yet. He turned his hand palm-up on the stone between them, an offer, a question, and after a moment her fingers found his and laced through, calloused against calloused, and held on.

Outside, the monsoon hammered the cliffs and the river rose and rose, erasing the lowlands, erasing the old landing, erasing every path back to who they had been. In here there was only firelight and rain-sound and the small astonishing fact of her hand in his.

"Forgiveness isn't a door," Tamsin murmured, half-asleep against his shoulder as the fire burned low. "My father says—it's a river. You don't cross it once. You just keep wading."

"Then I'll wade," Wren said. "As long as it takes."

She was quiet so long he thought she'd drifted off. Then, in the smallest voice:

"Wren. Listen."

He listened. Beneath the rain, beneath the river's grind—another sound. Faint. Rhythmic. From downstream, where no boat should be.

An oar creak. Once. Twice.

Then a light, far off in the drowning dark, sweeping the cliff face like a searching eye.
