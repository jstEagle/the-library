## 8. Corvus

The rain had found a voice by the second night, and it was not a kind one.

Wren heard it first as rhythm—the monsoon hammering the cliff above the cave mouth, running down the rock face in ropes of water, pooling in the lowlands until the river they'd fled had become a brown sea swallowing trees whole. But beneath that rhythm, under the thunder and the groan of the flooded forest, there was another sound. A sound he knew from fifteen years of patrols along a border where men went missing and were never asked about.

A boat. Oars muffled with cloth.

He lay still on the stone shelf at the back of the cave, listening past the storm. Beside him, Tamsin slept sitting up, her head tipped against the wall, one hand curled around the recovered ledger pages they'd taken from Corvus's abandoned camp—proof enough to hang half the gentry of the riverlands, if either of them lived long enough to deliver it. Further in, near the small fire they'd dared behind a screen of rock, old Ferro dozed with his splinted leg propped on a pack, and the boy Sef breathed shallow but steady through his cracked ribs.

Wren's hand closed around the grip of his knife without his mind deciding it should.

* * *

He'd felt it coming all day, the way you feel weather in an old wound. The birds had gone quiet at noon. The water birds especially—herons, spoonbills, the whole chattering parliament of the shallows—had lifted off downstream in one silent gray exodus, as if something had walked through their church. He'd told himself it was the flood. Floods moved birds too.

But floods didn't move them *upstream*.

Now, in the black hours before dawn, he heard the oars again, closer. Then silence—the deliberate, total silence of a man shipping them. Then nothing at all, which was worse.

Wren rose without sound and crossed to Tamsin, touching her shoulder. Her eyes opened instantly, fully awake, the way they always did, as if sleep were just another country she passed through without stopping. She read his face in the firelight's last coals and said nothing, only reached for her knife and stood.

"He's here," Wren breathed.

"You're sure."

"I've been listening to this river my whole life. It just stopped talking about us." He glanced toward the cave mouth, a ragged arch of stone beyond which the flooded world churned, silver-black under lightning. "Water's deep right up to the entrance. He'd have come by boat. Which means he's already out of it."

Tamsin moved to wake Ferro, pressing two fingers to the old man's lips before he could speak, then signing to him and Sef with quick, economical gestures: *quiet, back wall, stay down*. They obeyed like crew who had already learned what she was made of.

Wren drew her aside into the dark behind the rock screen.

"He's not here for the maps," he whispered. "If he wanted them, he'd have taken them from your camp when he burned it. He left the ledgers behind because paper wasn't the job. The job was people."

"Then why burn the camp?"

"To make it look like bandits. Like mischance." His throat tightened around the next words. "Like the Ellery expedition drowned. That's what command sent me upriver to confirm—that everyone was dead. Not to rescue anyone. I told you that."

"You did." Her voice was flat, but her eyes weren't; they held a banked fury that had nothing to do with him anymore. "So who sent *him*?"

"I've been turning it over for two days." Wren exhaled slowly. "The agent at the trading post. The man buying silence with Vane money. He doesn't work alone—he travels with muscle. I've seen this blade before, Tamsin. Years ago, at my father's estate, standing behind a man who signed things. He had a different name then. Everyone in my family's employ has a different name eventually."

"And now he calls himself Corvus."

"Crows follow battlefields," Wren said. "It's not a name. It's a trade."

Lightning flared at the cave mouth, and in its white instant Wren saw the water outside—and the boat, or what had been a boat, wedged against a drowned sapling thirty yards out, riding empty. Riding *empty*, and listing, and taking on the river.

Which meant Corvus had swum the last stretch. In floodwater. At night.

"He's good," Wren murmured, and hated the respect in it.

"Then we don't wait for him to be better." Tamsin was already moving, pulling the coil of rope from her pack, checking knots with fingers that never fumbled even soaked to the bone. "Ferro can't run. Sef can barely stand. There is no running anyway—it's water in every direction. So we hold this cave. One entrance. We know the ground. He doesn't."

"It's pitch dark in here once the coals die."

"Good," she said, and there was something in her voice that made Wren understand, suddenly and completely, why her father had trusted her with a survey crew at twenty-two. "I grew up mapping caves. I know every inch of this floor. He'll be walking in blind."

She pressed the rope into Wren's hands. "Low line, knee height, across the entrance shelf. Anchor it there and there. If he comes in fast, it takes his legs. Then we take everything else."

They worked in silence, and the silence between them had changed since the trading post, since he'd burned his orders in front of her and watched the ash spiral into the river. It was no longer the silence of two people deciding whether to hate each other. It was the silence of two people loading a weapon together.

* * *

Corvus came an hour before dawn, and he came like the rain came—everywhere at once, patient, certain of gravity.

Wren heard him first as a shift in the drip-pattern at the cave mouth: the water falling from the arch had a new interruption, a body's worth of shadow breaking its rhythm. Then the faint scrape of a boot finding wet stone. Then nothing, a long nothing, while Wren crouched behind the rock screen with his knife low and his heart going like a paddle wheel.

The man entered the way smoke enters a room.

Wren caught only fragments in the lightning flashes—a broad silhouette, low, moving with a strange gliding economy; the dull gleam of a blade held reversed along the forearm; a face that was less a face than a scarred topography, all ridges and hollows, the eyes catching light like an animal's. He crossed the outer shelf without a single wasted motion, and his foot found the rope exactly as Tamsin had promised.

The world turned over.

Corvus went down hard, and to his credit he made it look almost intentional, rolling with the fall, coming up—but Tamsin was already there, out of the dark like something the cave itself had produced, and her knife-hand struck his wrist with a crack that Wren felt in his own teeth. The blade skittered away across wet stone. Corvus lashed out blind, caught Tamsin across the shoulder, sent her staggering—

—and Wren hit him from the side with everything fifteen years of solitude had built into his shoulders.

They went down together, grappling in the flooded dark, and it was ugly and close and utterly unlike any fight Wren had ever trained for. The man was strong the way driftwood is strong—dense, waterlogged, impossible. A forearm closed around Wren's throat and began to squeeze with the dispassionate pressure of a machine doing arithmetic. Lightning lit the cave: he saw the scarred face inches from his own, expressionless, professional, *patient*, and understood with perfect clarity that this man had done this many times before and would do it many times after, unless—

Unless.

Wren's vision went to sparks. His knife was pinned. His hands clawed at the arm and found nothing but muscle and rain-slick leather. The roar of the flood seemed very far away now, very gentle, almost kind—

Then a shape rose out of the darkness behind Corvus, and Tamsin brought the iron pry-bar from the survey kit down across the base of his skull with a sound like a surveyor's stake driven home.

The pressure vanished. Wren rolled free, coughing, dragging air in great burning gulps, and watched Corvus crumple sideways into the shallow water, twitching once, twice, then lying still except for the rise and fall of breath.

Alive. Unconscious. Alive.

Tamsin dropped the pry-bar and knelt beside Wren, her hands hovering over him, not quite touching, as if she needed permission. "Breathe," she said. "Just breathe."

He breathed. When he could speak, his voice came out scraped raw. "You saved my life."

"You saved mine first. Fifteen years ago, when you taught me to read water." Her mouth twisted. "I've been repaying the debt ever since. It seems we're going to be even forever."

"That sounds about right."

Behind them, Ferro struck a match and lit the lantern, and the cave filled with trembling gold light. Sef was sitting up despite his ribs, wide-eyed. And Corvus lay bound now—Tamsin worked fast, ferociously competent, trussing wrists and ankles with the same rope that had felled him—while the storm outside threw itself against the cliff like something denied entry.

* * *

They searched him by lantern light, because they had to know.

In the oilskin wallet inside his coat they found what Wren had feared and Tamsin had demanded: papers. Letters of commission, sealed with the signet Wren had grown up watching his father press into wax—the double-V of the Vane holdings. Instructions in a clerk's precise hand. *Confirm the fate of the survey party. Ensure no testimony reaches the inquiry. Payment upon completion.* And beneath it, a list. Four names. Two struck through.

Tamsin read the list and did not cry, though Wren saw her jaw set hard enough to crack stone. "My father's name is third," she said quietly. "Yours is fourth."

"They meant to erase me too." Wren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I spent fifteen years running from that name, and the whole time it was a death sentence waiting for me to come home and claim it."

"Then don't claim it." She looked up at him, lantern light moving in her eyes, and took his hand—soaked, shaking, alive—and pressed the letters into it. "Carry these instead. Names are what other people call you. This—" she tapped the papers, the proof, the whole rotten architecture of her family's ruin and his family's crime, "—this is what you *are* now. A witness."

Outside, the storm was changing. The rain slackened by degrees, the thunder retreating upstream, and through the cave mouth Wren could see the flood beginning its slow surrender, the drowned trees lifting their heads, the water dropping a hand's width off the rocks. Dawn was coming up gray and washed and honest.

And far off, barely visible through the thinning rain, a light flickered on the high ground across the valley. Not lightning. Steady. Deliberate. A signal lamp, blinking some patient message into the ruined morning—from the mission station upstream, if Wren had to guess, or from someone else entirely.

Tamsin saw it too. She went very still.

"That's the mission ridge," she whispered. "That's where—" She couldn't finish it.

Wren put the letters safely inside his coat, over his heart, where a name used to be. "Then we'd better go find out," he said, "who's still alive to receive them."
