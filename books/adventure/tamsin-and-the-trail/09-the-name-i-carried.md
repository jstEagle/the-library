## 9. The Name I Carried

The river went down the way a fever breaks—slowly, and leaving everything it touched changed.

Tamsin stood at the mouth of the cave on the third morning and watched the water surrender its stolen country. The cottonwoods reappeared first, their trunks striped brown to the height of the flood, then the fence posts of some drowned homestead, then a long sandbar that had not existed before, smooth as a fresh page. The air smelled of silt and rot and, faintly, impossibly, of green things growing. The monsoon had spent itself against the cliffs in the night, and now the sky hung rinsed and pale, like linen washed too many times.

Behind her, Wren was binding Corvus's wrists with the last of their good rope. The thief sat where they'd left him, slumped against the cave wall, his broken nose crusted black, watching them with the flat patience of a man who had already counted every exit and found them all underwater. The Vane agent's letters were folded into Tamsin's shirt, over her heart, where she could feel them with each breath—the confession of a family written in another man's hand.

*Two witnesses,* Corvus had said in the dark, before the fight. *That was the price. Two.*

He hadn't gotten his price. He'd gotten a cracked skull from a surveyor's mallet swung by a woman he'd assumed was half-dead with exhaustion, and Tamsin intended to think about that for the rest of her life whenever she needed reminding that fear was not the same thing as weakness.

"We can't take him downriver," Wren said, coming to stand beside her. His knuckles were split. There was a bruise blooming along his jaw like a storm cloud seen through smoke. "Not with the crew hurt and the boat what it is. But the mission upstream has a radio. And walls."

"You're assuming the mission is still there."

"I'm assuming," he said quietly, "that if your father made for high ground, he'd make for the one place on this river with stone foundations."

She looked at him. Fifteen years ago she would have read his face like one of her father's charts—every contour familiar, every depth sounding known. Then there had been the long blank space where he should have been, and now here he was again, legible in ways that hurt. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking upriver, at the water sliding past, and his hands were very still at his sides, the stillness of a man holding something shut.

"The mission," she said. "St. Brigid's. My father drew it into the margins of his first survey. He said the brothers kept the only honest records on the river." A breath. "You knew him, back then. You knew he'd go there."

"I knew he'd go somewhere the Vane name couldn't follow."

* * *

They left at midmorning, poling the leaking boat through water gone shallow and confused, braiding around drowned fences and the carcass of a paddock gate. Marta and Osei lay propped among the cargo with their splinted limbs cushioned on rolled canvas, and Corvus rode bound in the bow under Osei's rifle, silent as ballast. The current, having done its damage, now did them the courtesy of carrying them north without argument.

Tamsin took the first shift at the pole because she did not trust herself to sit still. Her arms burned. Her shoulders burned. Somewhere beneath the burning was a feeling she couldn't name yet, a pressure behind the sternum, because they were going to find her father—Wren said *if*, but she heard *when*, and she had decided to let herself hear it that way—and she did not know in what condition finding would leave her.

Fifteen years she had imagined this river journey ending. In the early years, the imaginings had been cruel: she'd find him drowning, and she'd decide whether to throw the line. Later, the imaginings had simply stopped, the way a limb stops aching after amputation. She had built an expedition instead, and a reputation, and a life measured in soundings and contour lines, and she had told herself the blank space on the map where Wren Vane used to be was just that—a blank space, unmapped, unmissed.

Then three days ago he had come out of the smoke on the water with his hair longer and his face harder and his eyes doing that thing they'd always done when he lied to himself, looking slightly left of her, and she had wanted to shoot him and hold him in the same breath, and she had settled for neither.

Now, watching him work the pole beside her—reading the water the way she read terrain, his body remembering what his pride had tried to forget—she felt the old ache shift its weight. Not forgiveness. Not yet. But something adjacent to it, something that might, given distance and honesty, become forgiveness's rough draft.

"Sandbar ahead, port side," he said.

"I see it."

"I know you see it. I'm—" He exhaled. "Habit. From before."

"Keep the habit," she said, and didn't look at him, and heard him breathe out something that might have been relief.

* * *

St. Brigid's rose out of the flood plain in the late afternoon like a promise kept: whitewashed walls stained to the shoulders by brown water, a bell tower standing in six inches of retreating river, cypress trees bent but unbroken. A brother met them at the landing—an old man with a rope belt and forearms like dock pilings—and took one look at their boat, their wounded, their prisoner, and asked no questions, which told Tamsin he had already been asking them for days.

"He came up on the second night of the rain," the brother said, walking them fast across the flooded courtyard. "Half-drowned. Carrying a oilskin packet above the water like it was a child. We've had him in the infirmary since. He's—" The brother hesitated, and Tamsin's whole body went cold. "He's been asking for you, miss. Every hour, he's asked the hour, so he'd know how much light was left."

The infirmary smelled of lamp oil and camphor and wet stone. Her father lay in a narrow bed beneath a window, and for one terrible moment Tamsin thought they were too late, because he seemed smaller than the man in her memory, diminished, a chart of himself with most of the detail washed away. His skin was gray. His breathing made a sound like paper tearing.

But his eyes found her, and they were exactly, ferociously, unchanged.

"There she is," Aldous Ellery said. "My cartographer. Late."

Tamsin crossed the room in four steps and took his hand, and it was nothing like she remembered—papery, light, the bones like a bird's—but his grip closed on hers with the old stubborn strength, and she bowed her head over it and let the tears come without shame, because there was no one in this room worth performing for.

"I looked for you," she said. "After the fire. I searched the banks for two days."

"I know. Corvus told me. That's how I knew you lived—he came back to gloat, and I was already gone over the falls side." A ghost of his old smile. "Sloppy of him. Never send a thief to do a surveyor's arithmetic." His eyes moved past her, and Tamsin turned to see Wren standing at the foot of the bed, hat in his hands, looking like a man at a door he had no right to knock on.

"Aldous," Wren said.

"Wren Vane." Her father's voice was dry as old ink. "Fifteen years, and you finally row upstream. Most men run downstream; it's easier." He coughed, a long terrible tearing, and when it passed he waved off Tamsin's alarm with two fingers. "Sit down, boy. Both of you. I haven't the strength to say this twice, and I haven't the time to say it gently."

They sat. The lamp hissed. Outside, the water whispered against the walls, going down, going down.

Her father reached beneath the pillow and brought out the oilskin packet—water-stained, corner-burned, but intact—and laid it on the blanket between them like a relic on an altar.

"My surveys," he said. "Thirty years of them. Every sounding, every benchmark, every deed comparison from the source to the delta. And the originals of the land office filings, which I copied from the archive at the capital before certain parties knew I'd been reading." He looked at Wren, and his voice gentled in a way Tamsin had almost forgotten it could. "Your grandfather's claim, boy. Your father's signature renewing it. The dates don't match the ink. The ink doesn't match the paper. The paper doesn't match anything but a lie told well enough to hold for sixty years."

"I know," Wren said. "I've read them."

"And?"

"And my family stole a river," Wren said. "And everyone who depended on it."

"Yes." Aldous closed his eyes briefly, as if even the truth weighed something he could barely lift. "Now. Here is what remains of me, and we will not pretend otherwise—I have seen enough dying men to recognize the arrangement. So listen." He pushed the packet toward the middle of the blanket, equidistant between them, and put one hand on either side of it.

"Tamsin. These maps are yours by blood and by labor. You'll finish the survey. You'll publish it, all of it, the honest lines and the dishonest ones both, with the fraud named plainly. That is your inheritance, and I am sorry it comes wrapped in grief."

His hand moved.

"Wren. These maps are yours by debt. Not guilt—debt. There's a difference, and your generation will be the ones to learn it. Guilt paralyzes. Debt moves." His grip tightened on the oilskin. "I watched you at eighteen, boy, refusing to sign what your father put in front of you, and I watched you run rather than sign it, and I have spent fifteen years being angry at you for the cowardice of it while knowing, in the part of me that reads character the way I read contours, that running was the only clean thing available to you. It isn't anymore. Now you can stay, and stand, and speak. The inquiry convenes in the capital within the season. I want you in that room. I want your voice saying what these papers say. Not because it costs you—it will cost you everything—to say it. Because it's true, and truth spoken by the guilty kin is worth ten truths spoken by strangers."

Wren was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and steady and stripped of everything Tamsin had spent fifteen years resenting in it.

"I'll testify. All of it. On the record, under my own name, once—so the record shows a Vane confessing. And after that, I'm finished with the name. Let the family keep it. It was always more theirs than mine." He looked at Tamsin's father. "I never wanted the inheritance, sir. I want the debt."

Aldous Ellery smiled then, and it was the smile from her childhood, the one that preceded the best lessons, the ones learned in mud up to the knees.

"Good," he said. "That's the first correct answer anyone's given me in sixty years." He lifted the packet and held it out—not to one of them, but between them, requiring four hands to receive it. "Then it's settled. Take it. Both of you. A map belongs to whoever will walk the ground it describes."

Tamsin reached out, and Wren reached out, and together they took the weight of thirty years of honest work, and her father's hands fell away empty and easy, as though he had set down something carried a very long way.

"Now get out," Aldous said, settling back against the pillows, suddenly, enormously tired. "All of you, out, except my daughter. I have fifteen years of questions about her mother's grave and whether she finally learned to splice properly, and none of them are any of your business."

* * *

Later, in the blue hour, Tamsin found Wren in the bell tower, the recovered maps open on the stone ledge before him, Corvus locked below in the crypt cell with a brother standing guard and the mission's radio crackling its report toward the capital. Downriver, the last of the flood slid seaward, and the land emerged inch by inch, raw and new, waiting to be surveyed.

"So," she said, leaning on the parapet beside him. "No name."

"No name." He glanced at her sideways. "Does it trouble you? Marrying—or working, or whatever this is—with a man who doesn't officially exist?"

"It troubles me," she said, "that you think those are the options." She pulled the top map toward her, smoothing it flat against the stone: the upper river, the reach they'd just survived, drawn in her father's young hand forty years ago, every line still true. "Because I've been thinking. Everything from the falls to the delta was surveyed under a lie. Which means it has to be done again. Properly. Honestly. Years of work. Dangerous water, hostile claim-holders, weather." She tapped the blank margin beyond the last benchmark. "And no expedition I know of is equipped for it."

Wren looked at the map, then at her, and something in his face opened like the sky after rain.

"Tamsin Ellery," he said, "are you offering me a job?"

"I'm offering you a debt," she said. "It moves."

He laughed—a real one, startled out of him—and for a moment the bell tower held nothing but the sound and the river-light. Then the radio below them crackled louder, and a brother's footsteps came quick up the stairs, and the old man appeared in the doorway with his face grave and his voice careful.

"Forgive the interruption. The capital acknowledges the report." He swallowed. "There's a reply. It concerns the Vane family—and it seems they already know you're alive, Mr. Vane. They've sent someone to meet you at the falls crossing. Three days' travel." He hesitated. "They say he's your father."

The laughter died in Wren's eyes. Tamsin put her hand flat on the map, over the blank space waiting to be filled, and felt the whole next chapter of the river gather itself like weather.

"Three days," Wren said quietly. "Then he's early. I was going to come to him."
