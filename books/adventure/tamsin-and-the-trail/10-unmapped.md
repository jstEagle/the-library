## 10. Unmapped

The courthouse at Meridian Bend had been built by men who believed that stone could outlast the river, and for a hundred years the river had been proving them wrong. Water stains climbed the foundation like slow green fingers. The steps had settled unevenly, so that every person who entered leaned slightly, as if the building itself were tipping them toward confession.

Wren Vane climbed those steps on a morning in late autumn, when the flood season was a memory and the desert beyond the town had turned the color of old brass. He wore his ranger's coat, brushed clean, and nothing else that belonged to his family. No signet. No papers bearing the Vane seal. He had left all of it in a drawer at the fort, along with the letter of resignation he'd written three times and finally sent with only one line: *I am done measuring my life in miles from home.*

The inquiry chamber smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where important things are decided slowly. Commissioners sat behind a long table under a ceiling fan that turned with the weary patience of a mule. Clerks scratched. A stenographer's keys clicked like rain on tin.

He had rehearsed this. In the cave, during the monsoon, with water roaring past the entrance and Tamsin sleeping against the far wall with her father's maps rolled beneath her head like a pillow, he had rehearsed every word. But standing now before the commissioners, he found the rehearsal unnecessary. The truth did not need staging. It only needed saying.

"My name is Wren Vane," he began. "For fifteen years I tried to make that name mean nothing. I want to say now what it has always meant."

He told them about his grandfather, who had commissioned surveys and then buried them. About the forged claim filed in the year of the great drought, when the riverlands were worth stealing because no one else wanted to measure them. About the agent named Halloway who had bought silence at the trading post, and Corvus, who had been paid not to steal maps but to erase the people who made them. He laid out the recovered documents—the originals Tamsin's father had drawn line by honest line, and the forgeries that had overlaid them like a lie laid over a face.

His voice did not shake until he reached the part about himself. The signature his father had demanded. The night he rode out instead, and the fifteen years of distance he had mistaken for freedom.

"I thought running was refusing," he said. "It wasn't. Refusing would have been staying and saying no out loud. I'm saying it now, thirty years too late for my grandfather's victims and fifteen years too late for the woman whose father's work my family stole." He looked at the commissioners, and then—because she wasn't there, because she was waiting where she'd said she'd be—he said it anyway, to the room, to the record, to her. "Tamsin Ellery deserves back every acre, every mile of river, every truth that was taken. And if my testimony costs me everything the Vane name ever bought, then the price is fair, and I pay it gladly."

The senior commissioner, a dry woman with ink-stained fingers, asked him questions for two hours. Where had the maps been hidden. How had Corvus been captured. Who else knew of the forgery. He answered everything, and each answer came easier than the last, as though the words had been stones in his chest and he was setting them down one at a time on the table between them.

Near the end, she asked him one question no clerk had written down. "Vane," she said. "After this, what will you call yourself?"

He had expected it. He still took a moment.

"Nothing yet," he said. "Something new. Something that hasn't been decided for me."

She nodded slowly, as if that were the most honest answer given in that chamber in years, and struck the bell that ended the session.

* * *

Outside, the light hit him like open water.

He stood on the courthouse steps and breathed, and discovered that breathing could be a kind of astonishment. For fifteen years he had carried his name the way a man carries a stone in his pack—unnoticed in its weight until it's gone, and then staggered by how far he can walk. He felt lighter than he had since he was nineteen years old. Lighter, perhaps, than he had ever been.

Tamsin waited at the bottom of the steps.

Not at the foot of them, exactly—she was leaning against a mooring post down by the landing, arms crossed, hat tipped back, watching the courthouse door with the expression of someone who has been patient for months and intends to be patient exactly as long as necessary and not one minute more. When he came down the steps she didn't move to meet him. She just watched, letting him close the distance himself, which he understood was its own kind of courtesy. She had spent fifteen years waiting for him to come toward her. She wasn't going to do it for him again.

"Well?" she said, when he reached her.

"They believed me." He heard his own voice and barely recognized it—unguarded, unarmored. "Or they will. It'll take time. There'll be appeals. My cousins will fight it through every court they can afford."

"They can afford a lot of courts."

"Not enough of them." He shook his head. "The maps are real, Tam. Your father's lines are true lines. Truth holds up better than money, in the end. It just takes longer, and it doesn't buy anyone dinner on the way."

Her mouth twitched. That was as much celebration as she allowed herself in public. Then she pushed off the mooring post and led him down the landing to where a boat sat tied in the brown-gold current of the afternoon.

She had built her. That was the thing that stopped him—not the clean lines or the fresh-caulked seams or the new paint gone silver-gray, but the fact that Tamsin Ellery had built her with her own hands over the winter, plank by plank, in a yard above the flood line, while the lawyers argued and the inquiry crawled and Wren testified day after day in a stone room. She had built a boat the way other people built arguments: as proof that she intended to go on.

"She's called the *Honest Measure*," Tamsin said. "My father helped with the keel, before—" She stopped, and Wren put his hand over hers on the rail, and she let it stay there. Her father had lasted until spring. Long enough to see the inquiry opened. Long enough to press the recovered maps into both their hands and say, in a voice worn thin as survey paper, *Finish the chart. All of it. Every mile they lied about.* Long enough to know, before he went, that the river would be measured honestly again.

"He'd have liked her," Wren said quietly, looking at the boat.

"He'd have criticized her stern angle and then secretly loved her." Tamsin untied the line. "Which is the same thing."

In the stern, wrapped in oilcloth against weather that hadn't come yet, lay the charts. Blank ones. New paper, unruled except for the faintest grid, waiting. Wren had seen them that morning and had to look away, the way you look away from something too bright. Blank charts were an accusation and an invitation at once. They assumed nothing. They forgave nothing. They simply waited to be filled with what was true.

"The upper reaches first?" he asked. "Past the old Vane markers?"

"Every false marker comes down," Tamsin said. "And every true line goes up in its place. Father's originals cover the lower hundred miles. The rest—" she patted the oilcloth bundle— "the rest we draw ourselves."

*We*. She said it easily, without ceremony, the way she said *port* and *starboard* and *mind the seam*. As though it had always been a word with both of them in it, and the fifteen years between had merely been a long eddy the current had carried them through.

They loaded the boat in the last of the light. Wren worked in silence beside her, handing her coils and crates, and found that even silence with her had changed texture—it was no longer the silence of things unsaid but of things so thoroughly said they no longer needed repeating. Once, passing her a water cask, their hands met on the rope and stayed a half-second longer than rope required. Neither remarked on it. Some currents don't need sounding.

The town lamps came on behind them as they cast off. Somewhere upriver, the first cold stars were coming out over water that had drowned his family's claims and nearly drowned them both and kept, in the end, only what was true.

"Where does the *Honest Measure* sleep tonight?" he asked, taking the tiller.

"Nowhere," Tamsin said, settling into the bow with the charts across her knees. "We run the night reach while the water's calm. There's a bend forty miles up where the old charts show an island that isn't there anymore—or maybe it is, and nobody's bothered to check in sixty years. We check."

Wren laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and put the bow upstream.

The river took them. It didn't care what he had been called, or what he would be called next; it cared only for hull and current and the honesty of the line they would draw. Behind them the courthouse shrank to a smudge of lamplight, and ahead the dark water opened wide and unmapped, and Wren Vane—who was not, as of this morning, entirely sure he was Wren Vane at all—steered into it without a single mile of regret behind him.

At the first bend, Tamsin unfolded a blank chart across her knees, dipped her pen, and drew one small, certain mark at the place where they began.

"There," she said. "That's us. Everything after this, we find out together."
