## 1. Miles, Not Years

The morning Ulyana Crane turned thirty-four, she measured out eleven kilometers of dead track along the Vorsa River and did not think about her birthday once.

She thought about the track. She thought about the frost heave at kilometer 6.2 that had lifted the old bed forty centimeters out of true, and about the culvert at 8.7 where the river had spent decades quietly eating the embankment from underneath, patient as a dentist. She thought about how the rails themselves had been pulled up in '61 — she could see the scars where the spikes had been, a double row of small dark wounds running off toward the hills like stitches removed too soon — but the ballast remained, gray stone laid down by men whose names were not recorded anywhere, holding its line through sixty years of nothing.

That was the thing about closed lines. Everyone assumed that when the trains stopped, the railway ended. But a railway was not trains. A railway was a decision made permanent: this much stone, this much grade, this exact path through the world. You could pull up every rail and tear down every station and the decision would still be there under the moss, still legible to anyone who knew how to read it.

Ulyana knew how to read it. It was, at present, the only language she trusted herself to speak.

She set her level on the tripod at kilometer 9.0, checked the bubble, and sighted down toward the river bend. The Vorsa was high already — pre-monsoon swell, the water the color of milk tea, carrying whole trees past her at walking pace. In three weeks, maybe four, it would come up over the embankment and take another bite of the old line, and the regional transport authority would send her a slightly more urgent version of the same letter they'd been sending for two years: *Final assessment required before seasonal decommissioning of Sector 12 assets.*

*Assets.* The word always made her want to laugh. Sixty kilometers of somebody's life's work, filed under the same heading as surplus office chairs.

"Nine point one," she said aloud, to no one, marking the chainage in her field book with a pencil worn down to a stub. Her voice came out rusty. She hadn't spoken since yesterday evening, when she'd said "one moment" to the woman at the fuel depot in Kessel, and before that — she actually had to think — Tuesday, to a farmer asking if the survey crew was coming back through his pasture. There was no survey crew. There hadn't been a crew since March, when funding dried up and her two assistants went back to the city and their lives. There was only Ulyana, her instruments, her truck, and four hundred and twelve kilometers of ghost railway between here and the delta.

Four hundred and twelve. She knew it the way other people knew their own age.

* * *

Her father used to say she counted everything because she couldn't stand anything that couldn't be counted. He'd meant it as an accusation, delivered across a kitchen table five years ago, in the last conversation they'd had that wasn't conducted through lawyers or silence. *Your grandfather dies and you drive eight hundred kilometers to a survey job the same week. You don't feel things, Ulyana. You measure them so you don't have to.*

He'd been wrong about the feeling part. That was the unfairness of it. She felt plenty; she simply refused to let her feelings run the schedule. Grief was weather. You didn't cancel a survey for weather. You noted it in the log — *overcast, wind NE 15, visibility good* — and you worked through it, because the work was real in a way that grief was not, and because a man who died owing answers owed them whether or not anyone stayed angry enough to collect.

Emil Crane had been a railway engineer too. Third generation. The Cranes didn't have a coat of arms; they had chainages. Her grandfather had surveyed half the network in this region, including lines that officially did not exist, and when he died fourteen months ago he left behind a house full of boxes, a bank account nobody could explain, and a lawyer's letter addressed to Ulyana specifically, which she had carried unopened through three countries and two contract renewals and which now lived in the glovebox of her truck, wedged under the registration papers like something contraband.

She knew what it said. Of course she knew what it said. Lawyers' letters about estates all said the same thing: *You are named as executor and sole beneficiary of certain effects. Please contact this office at your earliest convenience regarding disposition of the remaining materials.* The *remaining materials* were the problem. Her father wanted them burned. Her aunt wanted them donated to the historical society, which amounted to the same thing with better lighting. And Ulyana — Ulyana wanted to open the boxes, and that want frightened her more than anything on any river she'd ever crossed, because opening the boxes meant admitting there was something in her grandfather's life she needed to understand, and needing things from dead men was how you ended up like her father: fifty-eight years old, furious at a ghost, drinking coffee at seven in the morning in a kitchen that smelled of someone else's absence.

So the letter stayed in the glovebox. Five years of exile, measured precisely: one thousand eight hundred and twenty-six days since she'd last stood in that kitchen. She kept the count without meaning to. Some numbers you don't choose; they choose you.

* * *

By noon the heat came up off the ballast in visible waves, and Ulyana retreated to the truck to eat bread and tinned fish and check the radio. The monsoon bulletin was the same as yesterday's, delivered in the flat voice of a man reading someone else's bad news: low pressure forming over the gulf, expected intensification, river levels above seasonal mean by forty centimeters and rising. *Communities along the lower Vorsa are advised—*

She turned it down to a murmur and got out her maps instead.

This was the ritual, and rituals were the load-bearing walls of her life. Every day at noon: bread, fish, radio, maps. She spread the sector sheets across the passenger seat — hand-annotated originals, some of them traced from her grandfather's own surveys, because the regional archive's copies were garbage — and reviewed the morning's work against the afternoon's plan. Kilometer 9.0 to 11.0 today, then the long walk-in tomorrow to the abandoned bridge site at 14.3, where the piers still stood in the river like the femurs of something enormous.

On the margin of the sheet, in her grandfather's handwriting — she'd found his annotations baked into these maps years ago and never erased them, though she told herself she would — was a note she'd read a hundred times:

*Vorsa bend, km 13–16: bed unstable, do not trust 1948 soundings. E.C.*

Do not trust. As if the river were a witness who might lie under oath.

She ran her thumb over the ink. His handwriting was beautiful in the way of men who'd learned penmanship when it mattered — upright, economical, every numeral identical to every other numeral of its kind. She had inherited the hand but not the patience behind it. Or maybe she'd inherited the patience too, and what she lacked was whatever he'd had that made him write warnings in margins instead of filing reports.

There were a lot of things Emil Crane hadn't filed.

Ulyana folded the map along its worn creases, ate the rest of the bread standing up, and went back to work.

* * *

The afternoon went the way afternoons went: stadia readings, rod shots, the small arithmetic of elevation accumulating in her book page by page. This was the part of the work outsiders never understood, the part she'd have trouble explaining even if she had anyone to explain it to. Surveying looked like boredom. It was actually the closest thing to prayer she knew — a repeated, disciplined attention to exactly what was, foot by foot, with no room for what should be or might have been. The land didn't care about her family. The land didn't lie, though it kept secrets. A benchmark set in 1953 was either where the record said it was or it wasn't, and finding out was the whole job, and the whole job was enough.

At kilometer 10.4 she found the wreck of a signal box, collapsed into itself like a burnt matchstick, and spent twenty minutes documenting it because the record showed it decommissioned in 1975 and the construction suggested 1940s, and discrepancies were the raw ore of her trade. Inside, under a slab of roofing tin, she found a ceramic insulator still bolted to its arm, intact, glazed the deep brown of strong tea. She held it a while. Somewhere behind her eyes, uninvited, arrived a memory of being nine years old, in her grandfather's workshop, him setting a piece just like this in her palm: *"Glass and porcelain, Ulyana. Everything else rusts, rots, or burns. That's why the old lines used it. Men knew what would last."*

What lasts. She put the insulator in her pack, telling herself it was a sample. Samples got logged. Logged things behaved.

The light was going gold and long when she finished the sector, and she drove back down the maintenance track to her camp — a lean-to, a cot, a stove, a rain barrel, a generator she babied like a sick animal — parked on a gravel shelf above the river with a view of the water doing its slow, muscular work. She washed in the barrel, cold enough to make her gasp, and cooked rice with the last of the dried chilies, and sat outside as the dark came down and the river turned from milk tea to black glass.

Frogs started up in the reeds. Somewhere far off, thunder that wasn't thunder — a barge engine, probably, working the channel at night. She checked the radio again. The bulletin had upgraded: system strengthening, tracking west-northwest, landfall estimate six days, *all river traffic advised to complete downstream passage within seventy-two hours.*

Seventy-two hours. She wrote it in the log. Numbers first, always numbers first, and then the fear could file in behind them in an orderly queue.

She was thirty-four today. She allowed herself, as an annual concession, to acknowledge it: thirty-four years, which was — she did the arithmetic without wanting to — roughly four hundred and eight months, or one hundred and seventy-seven weeks since the funeral, or eleven kilometers of dead track surveyed since dawn. All true. None of it sufficient. There was a remainder after every calculation, a quantity that wouldn't convert into distance no matter how many times she tried the exchange, and the remainder had a name she didn't say, and a glovebox letter, and a grave she hadn't visited because visiting meant standing still, and standing still meant the counting stopped, and if the counting stopped—

She banked the stove fire. She lay down on the cot with her boots off and her field book on her chest like armor, and listened to the river.

* * *

It woke her at some black hour, the sound wrong.

Not the river — the river had a voice she knew the way she knew her own pulse, and this was underneath it, a knocking, irregular, wooden. Knocking against something. Against pilings, she thought, before she was fully awake, because the old bridge at 14.3 was upstream and anything loose on this reach eventually found those piers, the way lost things find fences.

She got up with the flashlight and walked the gravel shelf to the edge of the water. The moon was down; the river was a moving darkness with a sound like breathing. The knocking came again — knock, drag, knock — rhythmic but broken, arrhythmic, alive-sounding.

Anything loose on this reach. Driftwood mostly, whole trees this time of year. But trees knocked and then swept past. This had been knocking for — she checked her watch — how long had she been awake? Ten minutes. Fifteen. Too long for driftwood unless it was snagged, and snagged things at flood stage on the Vorsa were worth knowing about, because a snagged tree could dam a channel overnight and drown a camp.

Professional interest, she told herself. Purely professional.

The boat was a twelve-foot aluminum skiff she'd bought off a fisherman two seasons ago, and she rowed it badly but adequately, out onto the black water with the flashlight clipped to her collar and the current immediately taking hold of the hull like a hand closing. The knocking grew. Fifty meters upstream of camp, near the old ferry landing whose pilings still stood in a crooked row — she'd documented them in week one, *timber, untreated, section loss approx. 40%* — the flashlight beam found it.

A chest. A trunk, rather — iron-banded, the size of a sea chest, riding half-submerged and jammed against the pilings, turning slowly against its own mooring of rope and river. Water poured off it each time the current swung it, and the wood gleamed wetly, dark, dense, some hardwood that had no business floating at all except that air was trapped inside it, keeping it stubbornly, absurdly buoyant.

And on the lid, where the beam steadied, a mark. Stamped deep, painted once in gilt that the water had reduced to a shadow of gold: a crowned tower flanked by two stars.

Ulyana knew heraldry the way she knew rail gauges — professionally, from archives, from the crests on old station facades. She knew that mark. Every engineer who worked this region knew it, the way Americans knew the bald eagle. It was the seal of Vessar, the border state that had existed for ninety-one years and then, after the war and the treaties, had existed no longer, dissolved so thoroughly that its railways were re-gauged, its stations renamed, its records scattered among three successor governments like ashes divided among urns.

Nations didn't float down rivers. Documents did.

The chest swung against the piling again — knock — and in the flashlight beam she saw that the lock plate was torn away, ripped clean, recent damage, bright metal showing through the patina. Something had been taken out, or something had gotten in. The rope trailing from the handle was cut, not frayed.

Ulyana sat on the oars for a long moment, the current worrying the skiff, the frogs gone silent around her, and felt the first hairline crack run through the ordered architecture of her days — the noon rituals, the numbered kilometers, the unopened letter sleeping in its glovebox vault. Because she recognized the feeling arriving in her chest, and it was not professional interest. It was fascination, naked and hungry, the exact appetite she'd spent five years starving.

The river pushed the skiff sideways. The chest knocked the piling again, patiently, like a visitor who intended to wait.

"All right," Ulyana said aloud, to the river, to the chest, to the year she was now living in. She shipped the oars and reached out with the boathook. "All right."

The rope was cold and heavier than it should have been, and the chest came toward her through the black water like something answering a summons.
