## 3. The Ledger of Names

Ulyana did not sleep. She told herself it was the storm — the river still slapping at the pilings of the survey shed like something impatient to be let in — but that was a lie, and she was too good a surveyor to tolerate bad data. The truth was the chest, sitting on her worktable where she'd left it, oilcloth-wrapped bundles stacked inside like organs waiting for a transplant.

She had measured everything else in the room twice. She had not touched the chest again since hauling it up from the piling.

At 0400 by her watch — she kept railway time even out here, where no train had run in forty years — she lit the camp stove, set water on for coffee, and pulled the chest toward her across the scarred planks. The seal of Vessar stared up at her from the lid: a double-headed crane standing in water, wings half-raised, stamped deep enough into the iron-banded wood to have survived decades of silt and flood. She knew the crest the way she knew the shape of her own hand. It had been on the spine of every atlas in her grandfather's study. It had been embossed in gold on the cover of the timetable book he kept beside his chair, its pages soft as cloth from handling.

A nation that had lasted less than a century and dissolved so completely that most maps now printed the region with a dotted border and an apologetic note. And here was its seal, fished out of a river like a drowned man's watch.

The lock hung broken. Whatever had battered the chest loose from wherever it had been hidden — a collapsed boathouse, a flooded archive, a grave — had torn the hasp open years ago. The river had gotten there first. That thought bothered her more than she could articulate: the idea that all this time the water had been reading what she was about to read.

She unwrapped the first bundle with a surveyor's hands, folding the oilcloth back along its creases rather than tearing it. Inside were shipping manifests, bound in twine that had gone the color of tea. The paper was heavy stock, official, the kind that outlasts the people who sign it. She untied the twine — it crumbled slightly, shedding rust-colored flakes — and opened the top document flat under the stove light.

*Vessar State Railways. Freight Manifest, Line 7 North. Season: Autumn.*

Her breath went shallow. Line 7 North. She knew that line. Not from history books — from her own maps. She crossed the shed to the steel trunk where she kept her working surveys, thumbed the latches, and drew out the rolled mylar sheets of the closed-lines project, five years of walking dead track through wet grass and second-growth forest. She pinned the relevant sheet to the wall board with two magnets and stood back.

There it was. *LN-7N, decommissioned 1949, status: never constructed.* The regional authority's records claimed the line existed only as a proposal, a pencil ghost on a planning map, abandoned before grading began. But Ulyana had walked its right-of-way anyway, because proposals leave scars. She had found the graded bed, the culverts, the cut through the ridge where the rock had been blasted and never reused. Someone had built most of a line and then erased it from the paperwork, and Ulyana had spent three weeks documenting the erasure without ever asking herself the obvious question: why would a state build a railway in secret and then pretend it hadn't?

Now she held one answer in her hands, and it weighed four kilos and smelled of river mud.

The manifest listed freight in the bland vocabulary of logistics: *livestock cars, 14; ballast, assorted; personnel, escort detail.* Livestock. Her eyes moved down the columns, looking for the actual cargo — cattle, she assumed, or timber equipment — and found instead entries like this:

*Car 3: 41 units, origin Kessel, destination Marovets Station. Condition at loading: fair. Rations: nil (per directive).*

Units. Forty-one units, condition fair, rations nil per directive. She read it three times before her mind would accept what the grammar was telling her. Livestock got rations. These were people, written down as tonnage, and the man who wrote *rations: nil* had known exactly what he was doing, because starving cargo doesn't complain, doesn't stamp, doesn't need feed stops.

Ulyana sat down slowly on her stool. The coffee water had begun to hiss; she ignored it until it boiled over and spat on the stove plate, and even then she only moved because the smell of scorching made her nauseous.

* * *

She worked through the manifests until dawn came gray through the shed's single window, methodical because methodical was the only way she knew how to hold herself together. Fourteen manifests, autumn season, one year — the year Vessar stopped existing, though these documents predated the formal dissolution by eight months. Every manifest followed the same route: collection points along the river towns, consolidation at Kessel, then north on Line 7 to a terminus called Marovets Station.

Marovets. She checked her survey sheet. *MN-4 junction, unofficial designation "Marovets," decommissioned 1951, structures demolished, status: site unrecorded.* Unrecorded. She had been to that site. She had eaten lunch on the concrete pad where the platform used to be, watching herons fish the flooded cutting, and filed a report noting anomalous foundation footings consistent with a structure larger than any proposed station on the planning maps. Her supervisor had written back: *Not our problem. Log and move on.*

She had logged and moved on. Everyone had logged and moved on, for eighty years.

The second bundle was worse. It was a ledger — an actual ledger, ledger-ruled, leather-spined, the kind of book meant to be kept forever — titled in faded ink: *Register of Transported Persons, Line 7 Operations.* Not freight. Persons. Someone, at some point, had drawn a line between the bureaucratic fiction and the human fact, and this book lived on the human side of it.

Page after page of families. Names in careful clerk's handwriting, grouped by household: *Ostrevik, Mikel — wife, Anna; daughters, Lena (9), Petra (6); mother-in-law, Dvorak, Ilsa.* Occupations. Addresses. A column headed *Allocation* filled in later, in different ink, with place names she didn't recognize and numbers that looked like labor-camp designations. Some entries had a final annotation in red: *deceased en route,* with a date. The dates clustered. Three days here, five days there — whole trains' worth of deaths inside a single week, as if the line itself had a season for killing.

Ulyana turned the pages with a growing horror that had nothing to do with surprise. This was the machinery she had spent her adult life studying — gradients, load limits, bridge ratings — turned inside out. Every technical decision she understood instinctively had a moral twin. The ruling grade on Line 7 was steep; that's why they needed water stops. Water stops meant halts, and halts meant the guards could open the doors and count. She caught herself doing it — calculating, engineering, understanding — and hated how fluently her mind served the material. The past she had fled had apparently been waiting for someone with her exact skills to come along and appreciate it properly.

Near the back of the ledger, the handwriting changed. Looser, faster, a different clerk or the same clerk coming apart. The allocations column emptied. The red annotations multiplied until entire pages bled. And then, on the last used page, a single entry with no allocation at all:

*Marovets Station: 212 persons, disposition: received, no further record.*

No further record. Two hundred and twelve people delivered to a station that officially never existed, at the end of a line that officially was never built, and the empire's paperwork simply — stopped. As if the ledger itself had walked off the end of the rails into the flooded cutting where Ulyana once ate lunch among the herons.

She closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which struck her as obscene. Outside, the river had dropped a hand's width overnight; she could see new mud on the piling where the chest had hung, a dark tideline like a bruise.

* * *

The third bundle was thin. One document, folded in thirds, on heavier paper than the rest, with a letterhead she recognized before she finished unfolding it: the Ministry of Transport, Vessar State Railways, the double-headed crane embossed at the top in gold that had dulled to brass.

It was a requisition order. Allocation of rolling stock and personnel for special transport operations, Line 7 North, effective immediately, exempt from standard audit. There were signatures — a director of operations, a ministry undersecretary whose name Renner-style dynasties would later build fortunes on, she'd learn that soon enough though she didn't know it yet — and then, at the bottom, in the space marked *Surveying Authority: certification of line capacity and routing,* a signature she knew better than her own.

*E. Crane.*

Edvard Crane. Her grandfather. His handwriting — she could see it with her eyes shut, the particular forward lean of his capitals, the way he crossed his t's like he was striking them through, impatient even with ink. He had taught her to draft with that hand hovering over hers. *Always certify what you've walked, Ulyana. Never sign for a line you haven't measured yourself.* Which meant, if he'd followed his own rule, that he had walked Line 7 North. He had measured the grades. He had certified the capacity. He had put his name at the bottom of a document authorizing trains with *rations: nil,* and his signature had ridden at the head of every manifest in this chest like a figurehead on a ship of drownings.

Ulyana set the requisition down on the table very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Five years ago she had walked out of her grandfather's house mid-argument and never gone back — over the estate, over her father, over the whole suffocating question of what the Crane name was supposed to mean and to whom. She had chosen a life of distances precisely because distances didn't argue back. A chain didn't care whose granddaughter you were. A benchmark didn't have expectations. She had told herself exile was neutrality, that measuring closed lines was a kind of honest forgetting, giving useful work to useless history.

And all that time, the history had been riding beside her. In her maps. In her surveys. In the foundations she'd photographed and the anomalies she'd logged and moved on from. She had spent five years tracing her grandfather's pencil lines without knowing they were his, walking his secret railway mile by mile, sleeping in culverts he had specified, and calling it freedom.

The vertigo hit her standing up. The shed seemed to tilt — the river, the pilings, the whole delta reorienting around a new axis — and she gripped the edge of the table and breathed until the floor agreed to stay where it was. When she could think again, the thought that arrived was small and terrible and precise, the way her worst thoughts always were:

He signed for a line he'd walked. So he knew. He stood where I stood. He saw the same cut in the ridge, the same platform pad, the same water in the cutting. And he wrote *capacity certified* anyway.

Or — and here her mind, traitorous, loyal, offered the alternative like a hand extended over a drop — maybe he hadn't known. Maybe a surveyor sees grades and gauges and not the cargo. Maybe certification is just numbers, and numbers are innocent, and innocence is a thing you can claim the way you claim mileage, one honest measurement at a time.

She wanted, with a physical ache, to believe the second version. That want frightened her more than the ledger did, because it was exactly the kind of want that built systems like this one: everyone along the line choosing the version they could live with, one signature, one log-and-move-on at a time.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Instead she did what she always did when the world refused to hold still: she measured something. She took down the chest's dimensions — 82 by 51 by 44 centimeters, iron-banded pine, 31 kilograms dry weight, though it wasn't dry, the wood still weeping river at the joints. She recorded the tide marks on the piling. She entered the coordinates of the find in her logbook in her own neat hand, and then, below the data, in a field meant for remarks, she wrote: *Chest recovered from flood debris bearing Vessar State Railways seal. Contents: manifests, register of transported persons, requisition order. Signature of E. Crane present.*

She looked at those last words for a long time. Then she added: *Relationship: granddaughter.*

It was the first time in five years she had written the word in anything but past tense.

Dawn came up the color of tin. The river ran high and brown and patient, carrying whatever else it intended to give back. Downstream, maybe twenty kilometers as the crow flew, lay the port town of Serevan, where the delta pilots drank, where boats loaded for the lower river, where a chest like this one might be sold, or studied, or sunk. Ulyana had no plan yet. But she had learned one thing from her grandfather besides drafting, and it surfaced now with the smooth inevitability of something long submerged:

Never move what you haven't first surveyed.

She wrapped the documents back in their oilcloth, repacked the chest, and began, with steady hands, to make a copy of everything — because whatever this was, whoever had lost it, the river had given it to *her,* and Ulyana Crane did not sign for a line she hadn't measured herself.

She was halfway through photographing the first manifest when she heard the outboard motor, far off, coming up the channel toward her camp.
