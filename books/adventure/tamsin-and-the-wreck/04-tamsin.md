## 4. Tamsin

The delta taught Tamsin Adeyemi to read water before it taught her to read words.

Her grandmother used to say the river had three moods and never told you which one was coming. Morning glass, noon chop, evening treachery — and somewhere in between, the brown flood that came down from the high country carrying everything the land didn't want anymore: fence posts, dead cattle, roofs, boats, bodies, secrets. Tamsin had been piloting cargo through the delta since she was sixteen, long enough to know that the river gave back only what it wanted to give back, and always at a price.

She was running the *Marrow* — forty feet of riveted stubbornness her father had bought wrecked and she'd rebuilt twice — up the Sable Cut with a deck load of diesel drums and cement when she saw the surveyor's skiff for the third time in two weeks.

That alone would have been enough to make her notice. Survey crews came through the delta every few years with their tripods and their orange vests and their government paper, measuring channels that hadn't shifted since before the war, drawing maps nobody needed of lines nobody ran. They came in threes and fours, they stayed at the Port Authority hostel, they drank one beer each at the Anchorline and left by nine. They were furniture.

This one came alone. This one drank nothing. And this one asked questions.

Tamsin brought the *Marrow* alongside the fuel dock at Corren's Landing with the easy reverse-throttle kiss that took ten years to learn, and watched the woman on the quay out of the corner of her eye while her deckhand, a gap-toothed boy named Fitch who was mostly elbows, made fast the stern line.

The woman stood at the rail of the Anchorline's veranda, not sitting, holding a folded map the way other people held prayer books. She was tall, sunburnt across the cheekbones in a way that said she'd spent years outdoors without bothering about vanity, and she wore her hair pulled back so tight it looked like discipline rather than style. When she spoke to the barman she leaned forward slightly, and though Tamsin couldn't hear the words over the diesel idle, she could read the shape of them: *Have you heard of this? Do you know this name? Has anyone come asking?*

Dangerous questions. Tamsin knew dangerous questions when she saw them, because she'd grown up watching her grandmother ask them.

* * *

Her grandmother's name had been Bintou Adeyemi, and she had come down the river in 1943 in the hold of a barge, along with ninety-one other people whose names had been written in a book by men who thought writing a name in a book was the same as owning the person attached to it.

Bintou had never talked about the war itself. What she talked about was the ledger — because there had been a ledger, she was sure of it, a shipping manifest with families listed like freight: *heads of household, dependents, tonnage.* She'd seen it once, she said, in the office at Vessar Junction, held by a clerk with grey gloves who checked names off as the trains came in. She'd been eleven years old. Her mother had gripped her wrist so hard it bruised and whispered, *Remember your name. Whatever happens, remember your name,* and Bintou had remembered it for eighty-one years, through three countries and two marriages and the raising of a granddaughter who now drove boats through the exact water those trains had been routed beside.

Vessar didn't exist anymore. The nation had been dissolved after the war, its borders divided between neighbors who agreed very publicly to never speak of what had happened inside it, and the agreement had held for seventy years because shame makes excellent mortar. The delta towns still carried Vessari names in their street plans. The old rail embankments still shouldered up out of the reeds like the spines of buried animals. And every family of delta descent — the ones the river people called the Remnant, without much irony and without much kindness either — kept some version of Bintou's story: a name remembered, a document imagined, a justice postponed so long it had become folklore.

Tamsin had inherited the remembering the way other people inherited land. It sat in her chest like ballast. It kept her steady and it kept her low.

So when she saw a stranger at the Anchorline asking after Vessar — actually saying the word aloud, in public, where anyone could hear — she did what her grandmother had trained her to do with anything valuable floating past on the current: she watched it, and she waited.

* * *

She got her first real look at the woman that evening, when the surveyor came down to the dock to look at the *Marrow.*

Not at Tamsin — at the boat. Specifically at the old brass fitting bolted to the wheelhouse bulkhead, a salvaged railway marker plate Tamsin had mounted there years ago because it was pretty and free. The woman stopped in front of it like she'd been shot.

"That's Vessar Junction," she said. Not a question. Her voice was low and even, an engineer's voice, a voice used to being right about measurements. "Station marker. Third class platform. That plate hasn't been manufactured since—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Since the war," Tamsin finished, from the deck. She kept coiling a line around her fist, unhurried. "You know your plates."

"I survey closed lines." The woman looked up, and her eyes were the color of river water in flood season — brown going on grey, something moving underneath. "Ulyana Crane."

"Tamsin Adeyemi." She let the surname sit there a beat longer than necessary. In the delta, among the Remnant, *Adeyemi* was a name with weight, and Ulyana Crane was either too polite or too preoccupied to show whether she felt it. "You've been asking at the Anchorline. About Vessar."

Something flickered across the woman's face — the quick recalculation of someone who'd just realized she'd been observed. "Routine historical work," she said. "Decommissioned routes. The Port Authority archives are incomplete."

"The Port Authority archives are incomplete," Tamsin repeated, "because the Port Authority burned them. In '61. My grandmother watched them do it." She set the coiled line down on the deck with deliberate care. "So whatever routine historical work you're doing, you're doing it without a library. Which means you're doing it from something you found."

Ulyana Crane went very still. It was a particular kind of stillness, Tamsin thought — not the stillness of guilt but the stillness of a person standing at the edge of a bridge they haven't decided to jump off yet.

"I don't know what you mean," Crane said.

"Sure." Tamsin climbed down to the dock, closing the distance to a conversational range, and noted with professional interest that the woman didn't step back. Most people stepped back. "Look, I'm not the police and I'm not the press and I'm not anybody's cousin in the Interior Ministry, which puts me in a rare category of harmless. But I'll tell you something for free, since you're new on my river." She nodded back toward the town, toward the veranda of the Anchorline where the barman was already talking on the telephone — she'd clocked that an hour ago; barmen at Corren's Landing talked on the telephone whenever anyone said the word *Vessar*, it was practically a municipal service. "There are people who get nervous when strangers ask about Vessar. Rich people. Old-family people. And this river is narrow, and news travels on it faster than boats do. So whatever you found — and you found something, because nobody surveys closed lines with that face unless they found something — you want to be careful who sees you carrying it."

Crane studied her for a long moment. The flood-season light was going amber behind her, catching the dust on her jacket, the salt lines on her boots, the exhaustion she wore like a second skin.

"You said your grandmother watched them burn the archives," Crane said finally. "Why were they burning them?"

"Because paper is the only thing that outlives an empire," Tamsin said, "and somebody finally noticed."

* * *

That night Tamsin sat in the wheelhouse of the *Marrow* with the diesel generator humming and her grandmother's tin box open on the chart table.

The box wasn't much. A ration card with a hole punched through the name. A photograph of a woman in a headwrap standing in front of a building that no longer existed, squinting into a sun seventy years gone. A scrap of oilcloth, stiff with age, that Bintou had torn from a crate on the deportation platform and kept her whole life as if it might someday be evidence of something. And a page torn from a school notebook, in Bintou's careful hand, listing the names of the families from her village as she remembered them — thirty-one names, spelled phonetically, some guessed, some half-forgotten, all of them carried like embers cupped in bare hands for eight decades.

Tamsin had grown up on that list. She could recite it. As a child she'd thought everyone's grandmother made them memorize the dead.

She turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil gone silver with time: *Bintou Adeyemi, Vessar Junction, before.*

Before. The whole war compressed into a single preposition.

Fitch knocked on the wheelhouse doorframe and stuck his head in. "That surveyor's still at the dock," he said. "Been standing there twenty minutes. Just looking at the water."

Tamsin glanced out the window. Down at the quay, Ulyana Crane stood at the edge with her map folded under her arm, staring at the black flood-current sliding past the pilings. She looked, Tamsin thought, like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

"She's not fishing," Fitch offered.

"No," Tamsin said. "She's not."

"You think she's trouble?"

Tamsin considered it. Trouble, on the delta, was usually easy to spot: it came loud, or armed, or drunk, or official. But there was another kind of trouble, rarer and worse — the kind that arrived quiet and alone and carrying something heavy, and the only warning you got was the way your own chest tightened when you looked at it. Her grandmother had a word for that feeling. She'd called it *the tide pulling*, and she'd said when you feel it, you can either tie off or go with it, but standing in the middle deciding will drown you faster than either.

"She's carrying something," Tamsin said. "And it's carrying her."

Fitch frowned. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got." She closed the tin box. "Get some sleep. We run the Sable Cut at first light, and I want us well clear of this town before the Port Authority opens."

"Why?" Fitch said. "What happens when the Port Authority opens?"

Tamsin looked out at the quay again. The barman from the Anchorline had come out of his door and was walking toward Crane with a phone still in his hand and two men behind him wearing the kind of jackets that tried very hard to look like they weren't uniforms.

"That," Tamsin said, already reaching for the ignition, "is exactly what I'm afraid of."

Down on the dock, Ulyana Crane turned at the sound of approaching footsteps, and even at this distance Tamsin could see her hand close around the folded map — close around it the way you close your hand around the only thing in the world that matters — as the three men fanned out across the planking with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been told no.

Tamsin gunned the generator, threw the wheelhouse lights on full, and hit the *Marrow*'s horn — one long blast, the universal river signal for *look here, look now* — because whatever else that stranger was, she was carrying half of Tamsin's history in whatever was hidden in that map, and Tamsin Adeyemi had promised her grandmother's ghost, eighty-one years late, that no one carrying those names would ever be taken quietly again.
