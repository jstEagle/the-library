## 5. The Man from the Consulate

The rain had followed Ulyana into Harrow Bend, which suited her fine. Rain emptied the port bars, and empty bars made for honest surveying — she could spread her charts across a corner table without anyone leaning over to ask what the closed lines were for.

She'd been in the town two days, long enough to dry the oilcloth bundles by the stove of her rented room and long enough to learn that the chest's lock, broken as it was, told its own story. Not rusted through. Pried. Someone had opened it before the river did, and recently — the brass around the hasp still showed bright metal where the crowbar had bitten. She turned that fact over on the walk down to the Waterline each evening the way she turned a bad rail joint over in her mind: not to solve it yet, but to feel its weight.

The Waterline smelled of diesel, fried fish, and wet rope. Ulyana took her usual seat at the end of the counter, back to the wall, charts rolled beside her boot, and ordered the stew that tasted like whatever the cook had dragged out of the river that morning. She'd asked her questions carefully all week — *who handles salvage out of the old Vessar reach, who'd want papers off a drowned barge* — and gotten the shrugs she expected. Port people forgot fast when forgetting paid better than remembering.

She was halfway through the stew when the door opened and the room changed temperature.

It wasn't anything dramatic. The man who came in was ordinary in the way of well-kept things: fifty or so, silver at the temples, a gray coat that had never seen weather, shoes too clean for Harrow Bend. What changed the room was that every conversation near him dipped half a tone, the way water dips around a hull. He scanned the bar once — a slow, cataloguing sweep, like a man taking inventory — and then walked directly to her.

"You'll forgive the intrusion," he said. "You're the surveyor working the decommissioned lines upriver. Crane."

Not a question. Ulyana set down her spoon. "I don't recall advertising."

"Your permit is public record. So is your route." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced to exactly the right wattage. "Ivo Renner. Cultural attaché, Consulate of the Meridian Compact." He laid a card on the counter between them, face up, square and heavy stock. "May I sit? I won't take much of your evening."

Ulyana looked at the card. Embossed lettering, a crest she didn't recognize, an address in the capital three hundred miles downstream. Everything about it said money pretending to be paperwork.

"The stool's free," she said.

He sat with the ease of a man who had never been refused furniture. He ordered nothing. That, more than anything, told her he wasn't here to drink.

"I'll be direct," Renner said, folding his hands. "I understand you've recovered certain materials from the flood line. Documents. Historical materials relating to the former nation of Vessar."

The stew went cold in Ulyana's stomach. She kept her face where she always kept it — level, unreadable, the face she wore for county officials and landowners who wanted to argue about easements. Five years of solitary work had built that face like ballast.

"I recover a lot of things," she said. "Fence posts. Signal lamps. Once a piano."

"A strongbox," Renner said pleasantly. "Stamped with the seal of Vessar. Recovered from a piling near the old Kiln Reach trestle, approximately nine days ago. You brought it downriver on the *Marta K* under a cargo manifest listing it as machine parts."

The bar noise came back to her slowly, like blood returning to a numb limb. She had told no one the contents. She had listed the chest falsely on the manifest precisely so no one would look twice. Which meant whoever sent this man hadn't heard it from her — they'd known what the chest was before she ever pulled it from the water.

"Interesting story," she said. "Whoever told you."

"It doesn't matter who told me." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was worse than any threat would have been. "What matters is what happens next. Those documents are the property of a defunct state. Under the succession treaties, archival materials of the Vessar government revert to the Compact's custody. Technically, Ms. Crane, you are currently in possession of stolen government records."

"Technically," Ulyana said, "I'm in possession of salvage, pulled from public water, under a salvage claim I filed in Kessel parish."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Recalculation. He adjusted his smile by a degree, the way a helmsman adjusts for current.

"Which is why I'm not here to seize anything. I'm here to make you an offer." He produced an envelope from inside his coat and set it beside the card. It did not slap down; it settled, like something that wanted to be unnoticed. "The Compact maintains a historical archive. We acquire private holdings all the time — fair market compensation, full documentation, a receipt signed by my own office. Your find would be preserved, catalogued, studied by scholars. Honored. Isn't that what a discovery like this deserves? To be *kept properly*?"

Ulyana looked at the envelope. She didn't touch it. "How much?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters what number you think makes me stupid."

Renner laughed — a real laugh, brief and warm, and somehow that was the most frightening thing he'd done yet. "A practical woman. Good. I despise haggling." He named a figure.

It was eleven years of survey contracts. It was a new truck, new instruments, the lawyer's unopened letter paid off, the whole gray ledger of her exile squared and closed. He watched her do the arithmetic — he was very good at watching people do arithmetic — and when she reached the total, he nodded as if she'd agreed.

"You clearly know what's in there," Ulyana said.

"I know what such chests generally contained. Manifests. Ledgers. Requisitions. The administrative sediment of a wartime state." His voice stayed smooth as ballast gravel. "Dull reading, mostly. Numbers. Names. The kind of paper historians yawn over."

"Names," Ulyana repeated.

"Names," he agreed, "of people dead seventy years. Whose grandchildren live comfortable lives and have no desire to be handed grief in a box. Consider them, Ms. Crane. Consider what it does to a family to learn its history secondhand, from strangers, in a courtroom. Some doors are better left shut. This isn't a threat. It's concern. Rivers give up dangerous things, and people who carry them tend to have accidents — not because of the things, but because of the *carrying*. I'd hate to read about you."

The word *accidents* went through the room like cold water through a cracked hull. Ulyana realized her hand had drifted toward the rolled charts at her boot and stopped it, deliberately, the way you ease off a brake you don't want noticed.

"I'll consider your offer," she said.

"That's all I ask." He rose, buttoned his coat, and left the envelope where it lay. "Keep the advance whether you accept or not. A gesture of good faith. My card has a number that reaches me at any hour. And Ms. Crane—" he paused, halfway turned, and for one unguarded instant the courtesy dropped and she saw the thing underneath, dry and patient as a foundation stone, "—the monsoon closes the lower river soon. After that, travel becomes very difficult. Decisions made in comfort are always wiser than decisions made in storms. Goodnight."

The door swung shut behind him. The bar noise resumed. At the far end of the counter, a deckhand who had been staring into his glass the entire time got up, left coins, and walked out into the rain without finishing his drink.

Ulyana sat a long while. Then she picked up the envelope, weighed it, and didn't open it. She put it in her coat pocket, untouched, because opening it would mean counting, and she wasn't ready to know how many years of her life a man thought her grandfather's signature was worth.

Back in her rented room, she moved the oilcloth bundles from under the bed to inside her survey duffel, and the duffel to beside the door, and propped the chair under the knob out of habit rather than belief. A chair wouldn't stop men like that. Nothing stopped men like that except distance, and distance was the one thing she knew how to measure.

Sleep came late and thin. In the morning, the envelope was still in her pocket, and the card was gone from the counter where she'd left it — she found it later, she couldn't say how, tucked into her chart case, between the sheets for the closed lines, face up, like a bookmark placed by someone who wanted her to know he could reach any page of her life he pleased.

Tamsin Adeyemi saw the gray coat come out of the Waterline and clocked it the way she clocked everything on the waterfront: automatically, professionally, the way a pilot reads a snag before it reads her.

Nobody wore a coat like that in Harrow Bend unless they were lost or hunting. This one wasn't lost. He crossed the mud-planked lot to a black car that had no business surviving the road in, and there was a driver waiting with the engine running, and neither of them hurried. Men who hurry are men who fear being seen. These two wanted to be seen.

Tamsin finished her coffee on the *Marta K*'s stern rail and let her eyes do quiet work. She'd noticed the surveyor woman days ago — the tall one with the river-gray eyes who asked careful questions in careful places, the kind of questions that get people remembered. *Who salvages out of the old Vessar reach. Who buys papers.* Tamsin had filed her under *trouble, possibly interesting*, the way you file weather building on a horizon.

Now trouble had found her wearing a consulate coat, and the horizon had a name.

She drifted down the gangplank, ostensibly to check a mooring line, actually to pass close enough to the car to hear. The driver had the window down. The man in the gray coat was speaking low and even, and Tamsin caught fragments the way you catch fish with a net that leaks.

"—she'll run it downriver, obviously. Nobody keeps a thing like that ashore once you've shown them the price of keeping it. Watch the boatyards. If she charters, I want the pilot's name within the hour."

"And if she burns it?" the driver asked.

"She won't burn it." A pause, and Tamsin heard the smile in it, the same warm terrible smile. "People don't burn inheritance. They only bury it deeper. Get me the harbor master's night log."

Tamsin walked past the car without turning her head, thumbed a mooring cleat that didn't need thumbing, and went back aboard with her heart beating in the flat, controlled rhythm she used for rapids. She stood at the stern rail and watched the black car pull out onto the levee road, and she thought about a name in a ledger she had never read but had always known existed — her grandmother's name, written down somewhere by someone in an office with a seal on the door, reduced to a line in a manifest, a weight in tons, a destination.

Her grandmother had survived. Tamsin knew that much the way you know a scar's origin story — secondhand, half-heard, never fully told. What she didn't know was the *how*, and the *how* lived in paper like the paper the gray-coated man wanted buried in an archive with a receipt.

So the surveyor had found something. Something worth a consulate's attention, worth a number that made hardened dockhands whistle, worth a driver waiting with an engine running. And the surveyor was asking about the Vessar reach, which meant sooner or later she'd need the river, and the lower river in monsoon season belonged to maybe six pilots fool enough to run it.

Tamsin was one of the six.

She looked down at the brown water sliding past the hull, carrying silt and secrets at the same speed, and she made the pilot's calculation she'd been making since she was sixteen: read the water, respect the water, and when the water brings something to you — a log, a body, a chest, a stranger — decide fast whether to dodge it or take it aboard, because hesitating mid-channel is how boats die.

The surveyor came down to the docks that evening. Tamsin watched her from the wheelhouse, watchful, coiled, saying nothing yet. Two people carrying different halves of the same drowned history, standing forty meters apart on the same mud, both pretending to look at the water.

The storm was four days out. The gray coat would be back. And whatever the surveyor decided, she'd decide it soon, and she'd need a boat, and Tamsin intended to be the one holding the tiller when she did — close enough to see what was in that chest, and to learn whether her grandmother's name was in it, and what the word *survived* had cost.

Downriver, the sky over the delta had gone the color of wet slate. First thunder, very far off, like freight rolling on a line that hadn't been used in seventy years.
