## 6. Closed Lines

Ulyana did not sleep. She told herself it was the heat, the storm pressure sitting on the valley like a hand on a scale, but that was a lie she'd been telling herself for three nights now, and lies were not something her life had much room for. Her life ran on measurements. Distances, gradients, load tolerances, the exact number of meters between a piling and a channel marker. Things that could be checked twice and trusted.

The ledger could not be checked twice. It kept changing under her hands.

She had spread it out on the folding table in her survey tent, weighted at the corners with rail spikes — the irony of that had occurred to her and been dismissed as sentiment — and by lamplight she worked through it page by page the way she worked through any document: systematically, without flinching, letting the numbers do the talking until they couldn't anymore.

The manifests were straightforward in the way atrocities are always administratively straightforward. Dates. Consignment numbers. Headcounts that were never called passengers because passengers came back. Origin stations, destination stations, tonnage figures where human beings had been entered as freight. The handwriting was neat. Someone had been paid to be neat.

It was the route column that finally made her put down her pen.

She copied the station sequence onto a fresh sheet of survey paper, because copying was what she did with information — it made it hers, made it manageable, turned history into data. Eleven names. *Vessar Halt. Kessel Yard. Drenthe Siding. Ostrava Cut. The Fen Junction.* And then four more names she didn't recognize at all, stations with no codes attached, listed only as *via* points on a line designated by a single letter and number: **L-9**.

Ulyana sat back and looked at L-9 for a long time.

Then she got up, unhooked the oil lamp from its bracket, and went out into the wet dark to her equipment chest, past the survey stakes and the theodolite wrapped in canvas, to the flat steel case at the bottom where she kept her reference maps. Not the working maps — those lived in her head and in the field books. These were the archive copies, the closed-line surveys, the ones no one asked for anymore because no one remembered the lines existed.

She knew them all. That was the joke of her profession, if you could call it a profession: she was an expert in things that had been deliberately forgotten. Railways decommissioned, de-listed, erased from the national atlas. Lines built during the Vessar years and abandoned after, their ballast swallowed by brush, their bridges left to rust into the rivers they crossed. Five years of walking them. Five years of measuring dead track through country that wanted nothing measured.

She found the map she was looking for on the third try. Sheet 14, quadrant northeast, drawn in her own hand from field observation two years ago, titled in her own block capitals: **UNNAMED SPUR, APPROX. 31 KM, ORIGIN UNKNOWN.**

Origin unknown. She had written that herself, standing in the rain at the northern end of a cutting full of sixty-year-old sleepers, unable to find any record of the line's construction anywhere in the regional archives. She had assumed clerical loss. Archives lost things; that was normal. She had noted it and moved on, because moving on was the whole architecture of her life.

Now she laid the ledger's route beside her map and matched them point by point.

Kessel Yard — there, at the southern terminus, where her spur met the main line. Drenthe Siding — kilometer nine, the collapsed loading platform she'd once sheltered under during a squall. Ostrava Cut — the deep rock cutting at kilometer seventeen, blasted through a ridge, still bearing drill marks. The Fen Junction — kilometer twenty-four, where the line split around a marsh that had since eaten half the embankment.

And the four unnamed *via* points. She found them too. Not as stations — there had never been stations — but as features. A water tower foundation at kilometer nineteen. A passing loop at kilometer twenty-eight, long enough for eleven cars. A concrete ramp descending to the riverbank at kilometer thirty-one, the northern end, where the line simply stopped, as if it had been built to deliver something to the water and nothing else.

A line officially never built. No record, no designation, no name. But surveyed, graded, ballasted, fitted with a passing loop sized for mass transport and a ramp sized for loading at a riverbank — and used, according to this ledger, forty-one times between March and November of a single year, carrying 6,204 people one way.

Ulyana's hands were steady. She noticed that about herself with a kind of clinical detachment: her hands never shook, not even now. It was everything behind her sternum that shook, some deep structural member groaning under a load it had never been rated for.

Because she knew who had surveyed L-9.

Not from the ledger. The ledger said nothing about engineers; ledgers never did, they recorded only what moved and what it weighed. She knew it from the letter. The lawyer's letter, still sealed, still riding in the bottom of her duffel bag for five weeks now, forwarded twice, growing soft at the corners. *Regarding the estate of Emil Crane.* She had read the envelope a hundred times without opening it. She knew her grandfather's estate would contain his papers, because he had been a man who kept papers, who annotated everything, who believed — as she had been raised to believe — that anything worth doing was worth documenting.

Emil Crane had been a railway engineer. That was the whole inheritance, or so she'd thought: the profession, the precision, the habit of measuring the world instead of feeling it. He had worked the borderlands in the Vessar years. He had come home after the war quieter than he'd left, and he had spent the rest of his life walking old lines with a notebook, and when young Ulyana had asked him why, he had said, *because someone has to know where things actually are,* and she had thought it was a lesson about surveying.

She looked at the ramp at kilometer thirty-one, at the passing loop at twenty-eight, at the water tower at nineteen, and understood that it had been a confession. He had walked that line for fifty years because he had helped build it, and he could not say so, and so he had measured it instead, over and over, the way a man touches a scar.

Her grandfather's name was in the ledger. She had seen it two nights ago on the requisition order — *authorized, E. Crane, survey & works* — and she had felt the vertigo of it, the ground tilting. But this was worse. This was not a signature on a page. This was the line itself, running under her boots for two years while she mapped it lovingly, feature by feature, into her own careful hand. The past hadn't just been waiting for her. She had been tracing it, drafting it, titling it *origin unknown* with her own pen.

She sat down on the equipment chest in the dark and let the rain start on the canvas above her, first scattered, then steady, the monsoon's outriders arriving early.

* * *

By morning the rain had settled into the long gray drumming that meant the front was close, and Ulyana had made a decision, which was itself a relief, because decisions were the only currency she had ever trusted.

She dug the letter out of the duffel.

It had traveled far. The postmark was from the capital, the forwarding stickers from two towns she'd passed through last season, and the paper had gone soft as cloth at the folds. The lawyer's name was Halvorsen & Associates, and beneath it, in smaller print, *Executor for the Estate of E. Crane (dec.).*

She opened it with a surveyor's knife, one clean stroke, because tearing it would have meant hesitation and she had decided against hesitation somewhere around dawn.

*Dear Ms. Crane,*

*I write further to the death of your grandfather, and apologize for the delay in reaching you — your forwarding arrangements being, I am told, of a peripatetic nature.*

*Under the terms of the will, the residue of the estate — the house having been sold to cover debts — passes to you in full. This consists principally of his professional papers: field notebooks, correspondence, drawings, and maps, comprising some fourteen crates currently held in storage at our offices. There is also a sum of money, modest, and a personal item he specifically directed be delivered to you and no one else.*

*Mr. Crane's instructions regarding the papers were unusual, and I feel bound to reproduce them verbatim, as he insisted:*

*"To my granddaughter. She will understand the measurements even if she hates me for them. Everything is in order. Except one thing, which is out of order, and she'll find it, because finding things is what she does. When she finds it, she should decide for herself what it's worth. Not what I thought it was worth. What it's worth."*

*I confess I do not know what this refers to. Mr. Crane declined to elaborate, saying only that "she'll know."*

*There is one further matter. In cataloguing the papers we identified a gap: the field notebooks run continuously from 1938 to 1987 with the exception of a single volume, referenced elsewhere in his correspondence as "the ninth book," which is absent. One letter in the collection, dated late in his life, refers to a page removed from this book and kept separately — quote, "with the thing it belongs to" — though what thing, again, he does not say.*

*I remain at your disposal, though I appreciate that disposal may be the wrong word given recent events. Please contact us at your convenience, or don't; Mr. Crane predicted you wouldn't.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*A. Halvorsen*

Ulyana read it three times.

The rain filled the silences between readings. Somewhere down at the landing, Tamsin Adeyemi's boat knocked against the pilings — she'd heard the pilot arrive yesterday evening, heard the easy loud voice carrying across the water, and had pretended not to listen, and had listened anyway.

*Fourteen crates.* Fourteen crates of her grandfather's mind, sitting in a lawyer's storeroom in the capital, and every road to the capital ran through the delta, and the delta was about to close for the monsoon, and a man in a gray coat had offered her a great deal of money for a chest she hadn't asked for and couldn't put down.

*The ninth book.* Missing. Referenced everywhere and present nowhere, like the line it described. Like Vessar itself, a country that had existed on stamps and timetables and then been voted out of the atlas, leaving only infrastructure behind — rails, grudges, ledgers.

*With the thing it belongs to.*

She looked at the ledger on the table. At the requisition order with her grandfather's signature, *E. Crane, survey & works*, authorizing — what? She had gone over it a dozen times. Authorizing the extension of Line L-9 to the riverbank. Authorizing the passing loop. Authorizing, in dry bureaucratic language, the construction of the apparatus of deportation, itemized down to the cubic meters of gravel. His signature sat at the bottom like a nail driven flush.

But the ledger also referenced documents it did not contain. She had noted the gaps the first night, because gaps were her specialty: entries marked *see annex*, consignment numbers keyed to a supplementary register, and one entry — the final one, dated November, the last train of the season — whose destination column read simply *per Book 9, p. 41.*

Book 9, page 41. Removed and kept separately, with the thing it belongs to.

Her grandfather had taken a page out of his own notebook and hidden it inside something, and he had told a lawyer she would find it, because finding things was what she did. And then he had died and left her a chest's worth of evidence that he had helped murder six thousand people — except that the missing page might say otherwise, or might say worse, and she would not know which until she held it, and she could not hold it because it was in a crate in the capital behind a locked door, and the man in the gray coat wanted the chest burned before anyone ever reached a crate at all.

Grief and anger arrived together, the way weather fronts do, and she could not tell where one ended and the other began. Grief for the old man walking dead lines for fifty years, measuring his guilt kilometer by kilometer, too cowardly or too careful or both to burn the evidence and too haunted to destroy it — leaving it to her, the way you leave a debt to family. Anger at him for exactly that. For making her inherit it. For teaching her to love railways and then hiding a slaughter inside the love. For writing *she'll hate me for them* and dying before she could tell him he was right, and wrong, and that she needed him to explain, and that there was no one left to ask.

She realized she was standing. She didn't remember getting up.

Outside, the river had come up another half meter overnight, brown and muscular, dragging whole trees past the camp. Down at the landing, a figure in a yellow oilskin stood on the deck of the cargo boat, watching the water with the particular patience of someone who reads it for a living. Tamsin. Who had appeared in her orbit the same week the chest surfaced, asking careful questions in port bars, and who had looked at Ulyana two nights ago with an expression Ulyana now recognized, because she'd been wearing it herself since the ledger: the look of someone doing arithmetic with the dead.

Ulyana thought about the ramp at kilometer thirty-one. Concrete, descending to the riverbank. Built to load people onto boats? Or off them?

She thought: *I need a pilot.*

She thought: *and she needs something from me, and neither of us has said yet what it costs.*

The rain thickened. On the table, the ledger lay open to the final entry, and Ulyana looked at the small notation one more time — *per Book 9, p. 41* — and then took up her pen and wrote, beneath it, in her own block capitals, the way she titled every map she drew:

**LINE L-9. ORIGIN KNOWN.**

Then she capped the pen, folded the lawyer's letter into her breast pocket, pulled on her oilskin, and went down through the rain toward the boat, and the woman waiting on it, and whatever the truth cost that neither of them had named a price for yet.
