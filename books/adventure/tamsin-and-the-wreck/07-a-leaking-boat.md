## 7. A Leaking Boat

Tamsin Adeyemi knew every sound her boat made, the way a mother knows a child's breathing in the next room. The *Marrow* was forty years old and honest about it: the diesel's uneven idle, the groan of the cargo boom under load, the particular knock in the port engine that only appeared above twelve hundred RPM. So when she heard footsteps on her deck at four in the morning — footsteps that were not hers — she was awake before the thought finished forming.

She lay still in the narrow cabin behind the wheelhouse, listening. Two sets of feet, maybe three. The careful kind of walking, weight rolled heel to toe, the walk of men who had done this before. Rain ticked against the deck plates. Downriver, Port Anselm's lights smeared yellow through the drizzle, and somewhere between here and those lights, Ulyana Crane was asking questions that got people followed.

Tamsin had watched it happen for three nights running. The surveyor with the mud-colored jacket and the mile-counter eyes, sitting too straight at the corner table of the Salt Barrel, asking about old lines, closed stations, the Vessar years. Asking like someone who already knew the answers and wanted confirmation of something worse. Tamsin had seen that look before. She'd worn it herself, once, at nineteen, in an archive in a city whose name she no longer said aloud.

The footsteps stopped outside her cabin door. A flashlight beam swept the gap beneath it, thin as a blade.

*They're not here for me,* she realized. *They're checking whether she came here.*

The beam moved on. Boots crossed toward the stern, toward the hold where Tamsin kept her tools and, more usefully, her father's old river charts — the ones with the seasonal channels inked in pencil, updated by hand across thirty years. Those charts were worth more to her than the boat. She'd have killed for them once. Nearly had.

The men talked in low voices. Not local accents. The clipped consonants of the interior, consulate country.

"—says check everything. If the chest came downriver, it comes through her."

"Crane's not stupid enough to—"

"She paid you to be thorough, not opinionated."

A pause. Then, quieter: "The pilot knows the delta better than anyone. If Crane runs, she runs with Tamsin Adeyemi."

Tamsin's blood went cold and then hot, which was how fear always arrived for her — a cold front, then the storm behind it.

The men finished their search in six minutes. Professional. They left nothing disturbed except, when she finally slid the cabin bolt and stepped out into the rain-smell dark, a single calling card on her wheelhouse console, weighted down with a brass washer so it wouldn't blow away.

Ivo Renner. Consulate of Vessar-in-Exile. Beneath the printed name, in fountain-pen ink gone slightly feathered from the damp: *Passage is a service. Services can be arranged. — I.R.*

Tamsin stood holding the card while the rain thickened around her, and understood that she had just been conscripted into somebody else's war without firing a shot.

* * *

By seven she'd made her decision the way she made all her decisions: alone, quickly, and with a strong cup of ginger tea to argue against.

Loading day. The *Marrow* was taking on freight for the delta run — rice in sealed bags, machine parts for the fishery co-op at Lowmoor, drums of stove oil lashed amidships. Her two deckhands, Ferro and the boy they called Sparrow, moved crates up the gangplank while Tamsin supervised the stowage with one eye and kept the other on the road along the levee.

Ulyana Crane appeared at half past eight, and she did not look like a woman making a calm decision.

She came down the levee path at a pace just short of running, surveyor's satchel slung crosswise over her chest the way soldiers carry ammunition, and Tamsin noted — filed away — that the satchel was heavier than any set of instruments needed to be. The surveyor's face had the gray look of someone who had slept badly or not at all. Behind her, up on the road, a gray sedan sat with its engine running and its wipers going, patient as a heron.

"You're loading," Ulyana said, arriving at the gangplank. Not a greeting. An assessment.

"I'm loading." Tamsin didn't stop working. "You're being followed."

"I know."

"They were on my boat last night."

Something flickered across Ulyana's face — guilt, Tamsin decided, or its close cousin, responsibility. "I'm sorry. That's because of me."

"It is." Tamsin set a crate down harder than necessary. "So let's not waste each other's time. You need passage downriver. Past Kessel Locks, at minimum, before the front closes everything. You can't take the road because they're watching the roads. You can't take the ferry because the ferry keeps manifests, and manifests are exactly what your new friends are good at reading. Which leaves me." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "And I haven't said yes."

"No," Ulyana agreed. "You haven't."

"And you haven't told me what's in the bag."

For a long moment the only sounds were rain on canvas, the diesel idling, Sparrow whistling something tuneless in the hold. Ulyana looked out at the brown water moving fast with the pre-monsoon swell, and Tamsin watched her decide — watched the calculation happen behind those distance-measuring eyes, the same arithmetic Tamsin had seen her do over beers: risk weighed against risk, miles against miles.

Then Ulyana unslung the satchel, opened it, and showed her the ledger.

Tamsin had expected the chest itself. What she saw instead was a single volume wrapped in oilcloth, water-stained at the corners, its spine cracked from swelling. Ulyana peeled the cloth back just far enough to expose a page. Columns of names. Ages. Origins. Destinations written as station codes.

Tamsin's throat closed.

She didn't touch it. She wanted to touch it more than she had wanted anything in years, and because she wanted it that much, she didn't. "Vessar," she said. Her voice came out flat.

"The deportation ledgers. There were three volumes in the chest. This is the second." Ulyana rewrapped it with hands that weren't quite steady. "There's a requisition order signed by my grandfather. There are station names on routes that officially never existed. And there's—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a page referenced but missing. Page forty-one. Everything else in the ledger points to it and it's been cut out."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because last night I read the name Adeyemi in that book." Ulyana met her eyes. "Twice. A family from the marsh parishes, deported east on the spring trains. I don't know if they're yours. But you've been watching me for three days like someone waiting for a name, and I thought you deserved to hear it from me instead of finding it the way I found mine."

The whistling in the hold had stopped. Sparrow was watching them from the hatch, and Tamsin waved him back to work with a jerk of her chin.

Her grandmother had never spoken of Vessar directly. She'd spoken around it, the way you speak around a missing tooth — the marsh parishes, the year the trains ran heavy and came back empty, the aunt who was simply no longer discussed. Tamsin had spent her twenties pulling at that silence thread by thread, and the thread had led her to archives, to bribed clerks, to a burned registry office and a dead end with her own fingerprints on the matchbook. She had sold information to survive that winter. She had never fully forgiven herself, and part of her — the honest part, the part she kept locked below the waterline — knew she might do it again if the price was right and the hunger deep enough.

And now a stranger stood on her gangplank holding the actual book. The paper. The ink. The names in government handwriting, tidy as tide tables.

"If that family is mine," Tamsin said carefully, "I want the page. Whatever it says. That's my price for passage, and we'll negotiate the rest later."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's expensive. Fair would be you handing me the whole book and walking away." She shouldered past Ulyana onto the deck. "Get aboard. Stow your own gear, stay out of my engine room, and don't lie to me about anything, even small things. Small lies are how big ones get comfortable."

Ulyana came up the plank. Behind her, on the levee road, the gray sedan's door opened.

"Tamsin." Ulyana's voice dropped. "Company."

"I see them." Two men now, walking down the levee path with the unhurried gait of people who owned the morning. One carried a document wallet. "Ferro! Cast off the stern line. We're leaving early."

"We're not loaded—"

"We're leaving early anyway."

The men quickened. The one with the wallet called out something official-sounding about a customs inspection, and Tamsin shouted back that inspections were scheduled through the harbor office like civilized business, and threw the bow line off its cleat even though the bow line wasn't theirs to throw — it belonged to the barge rafted alongside, and she'd pay for that later, or apologize, or run faster.

The *Marrow* backed off the pier with a shudder of reversed propwash. The sedan doors opened fully now, three men spilling out, and the one with the wallet broke into an actual run along the quay.

"Sparrow! Get us mid-channel!"

The boy gunned it. The old diesel knocked and caught and knocked again, and the quay slid backward, and the running man stopped at the pier's end with his shoes at the very edge of the water, watching them go, making no attempt to hide that he was memorizing their heading.

Ulyana stood at the rail beside Tamsin, both of them breathing hard, the town shrinking behind them in the rain.

"He'll radio ahead," Ulyana said.

"He'll radio ahead," Tamsin agreed. "Kessel Locks is sixty miles downriver. We make good speed, we're there tomorrow night. The monsoon front hits the coast the night after." She turned and looked at the surveyor — really looked, for the first time since the Salt Barrel, at the exhaustion and the stubbornness braided together in her face. "So we have a margin of about one day, and I already don't like the sound of my port engine."

* * *

The sound got worse by noon.

It started as the knock above twelve hundred RPM, the one Tamsin had lived with for two seasons, planning to fix it during the dry weeks that never came. By midafternoon it had developed a metallic edge, a ringing overtone like a bell struck underwater, and by the time they passed the abandoned coal tipple at Mile 41, Tamsin was lying in the engine room's greasy heart with a stethoscope pressed to the block, listening to her boat die by degrees.

"Bearing," she said, emerging with black hands and a blacker mood. "Main bearing, port side. It's letting the crank wander. I can nurse it at low revs, maybe. Or it seizes, and then we're a floating warehouse waiting for whatever Renner sends."

"How long at low revs?"

"To the locks? Tight. To anywhere past the locks?" She shook her head. "And that's assuming the weather holds, which—" She squinted upstream, where the sky had begun doing the thing sky does before a monsoon: piling itself into towers, bruise-purple at the base, the air gone thick and sweet and wrong. "That's not holding. That's two days out at most. Maybe less. The river's already up a foot since morning."

Ulyana crouched beside her at the engine room hatch, and for a moment neither spoke. The *Marrow* pushed steadily down the swollen current, banks sliding past — cattle standing belly-deep in flooded pasture, a church roof just visible above drowned trees, the whole landscape quietly reverting to the water it had always been.

"My grandfather surveyed these banks," Ulyana said suddenly. "The rail line ran right along here. Mile markers every hundred meters. He measured everything." A short, bitter breath. "I used to think that was the family disease. Measuring. Counting. Keeping track."

"And now?"

"Now I think keeping track might be the only honest thing anyone in my family ever did." She looked at Tamsin. "Your grandmother. From the marsh parishes?"

"Marsh parishes, yes."

"Then when we stop tonight, I'll show you the entries. Both of them. And you'll tell me what you want to know, and I'll answer everything I can." Ulyana paused. "Even the parts that are ugly. My parts especially."

Tamsin studied her. There was something almost offensive about the offer — this stranger volunteering intimacy like it cost nothing — and something, underneath, that Tamsin recognized and distrusted precisely because she recognized it: the look of a person who had decided that truth was a debt and intended to pay it in full, alone if necessary.

"Don't promise ugly truths yet," Tamsin said. "We're not friends. We're two people on a leaking boat with the same enemies. That's an alliance, not a friendship, and alliances break the moment the math changes. Remember that when you're showing me pages."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a contract term." Tamsin hauled herself upright, knees cracking. "Now come learn where the bilge pump lives. If the bearing goes, you're on the hand pump, and I'm telling you now, surveyor — I will work you like hired labor and thank you for nothing."

The first rain hit an hour after sunset, fat warm drops that hissed on the deck plates and then thickened into a steady drumming roar. They anchored in the lee of a drowned orchard, engines ticking, and ate rice and tinned fish while the river climbed visibly up the bank, swallowing the orchard fence post by post.

At midnight, Tamsin woke to a sound she knew too well: the bilge pump cycling twice as often as it should.

She found the leak in the lazarette — a seam weeping where the hull had flexed against something submerged, probably the coal tipple's old pilings, probably hours ago. Not catastrophic. Not yet. But the river was rising, and rising rivers find every weakness, and somewhere ahead of them a man with a fountain pen was arranging the locks and the law and the weather itself against them.

Ulyana appeared at the hatch with a lamp, took one look, and picked up the hand pump without being asked.

"Two days to the front," Tamsin said, watching her pump, watching the water gain and lose and gain again. "One day to the locks. One bearing that hates us."

"Then we're ahead on points," Ulyana said, and pumped.

Tamsin almost laughed. Almost. Outside, the rain came down like the sky meant it, and the *Marrow* groaned southward through the dark, carrying two strangers, one dying engine, and a book of names toward Kessel Locks — where, though neither woman could know it yet, a man with binoculars was already waiting.
