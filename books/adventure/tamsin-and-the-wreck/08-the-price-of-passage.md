## 8. The Price of Passage

The rain came in the night like a debt collector, and by morning the *Marrow's Grace* sat low in the water and Tamsin Adeyemi stood ankle-deep in her own bilge, deciding how much a stranger was worth.

Not the stranger herself — the woman had already proven she could run, could carry a chest heavier than her conscience through a flooded survey camp with three men crashing through the reeds behind her. Tamsin had watched her come down the muddy bank at first light, wild-eyed, ledger clutched to her ribs like an infant, and something in the set of her shoulders had made Tamsin throw the mooring line before she'd made any decision at all. That was the trouble with instincts. They signed contracts your head never read.

No — the question wasn't what Ulyana Crane was worth. The question was what she'd pay.

Tamsin worked the hand pump in slow strokes, watching the gray water spiral out through the scupper and into the river that wanted the boat. The *Grace* was forty years old and honest about her flaws: a weeping seam aft where the caulking had given up two seasons ago, a fuel line that sulked in cold weather, a hull that forgave nothing. Tamsin knew every one of her sins because they were all hers too. She'd bought the boat with money she still didn't like to count, earned in ways she liked even less, and the boat was the only thing she'd ever owned outright, the only thing nobody could take back or rewrite.

"You're pumping wrong," Ulyana said from the wheelhouse doorway.

"I've been pumping this boat for six years."

"Then you've been doing it wrong for six years. Long stroke, pause at the bottom. You're churning, not lifting." She came down the steps, took the handle out of Tamsin's hands without asking, and demonstrated. She was right. Tamsin hated that she was right.

"Engineer," Tamsin said.

"Surveyor." Ulyana kept her eyes on the pump. "Closed lines. Decommissioned rail along the upper river. I measure things that don't exist anymore."

"That's a strange profession for someone who runs from her family."

Ulyana's jaw tightened. "You don't know anything about my family."

"I know you got a lawyer's letter you carried around unopened for five years, because it fell out of your pack last night when you dumped it on my deck, and I know you picked it up and put it back without looking at it, and I know people who do that." Tamsin leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed. "I also know what's in that chest, because I saw the seal before you wrapped it up again like it might bite. Vessar."

The name landed between them the way names like that always did — flat, small, enormous. A country that had lasted eleven years and been erased so thoroughly that maps printed after its dissolution showed only blank steppe and a polite note about administrative reorganization. But the river remembered. The river always remembered; that was the whole reason Tamsin piloted it instead of some clean coastal route. Her grandmother had come down this water in 1943, in the hold of a barge, one of four hundred souls listed in manifests stamped with that same seal, and the river had carried her past checkpoints because the pilot that night had been paid in flour and hadn't asked questions. Tamsin had grown up on that story the way other children grew up on saints' lives.

"The ledger," Tamsin said. "The one with the families. I want to see it."

"No."

"No?"

"It's not mine to show around." Ulyana pumped twice more, then stopped, as if the motion itself had become unbearable. "It belongs to the dead."

"The dead don't read." Tamsin heard her own voice go hard and didn't soften it. "My grandmother's name is in ledgers like yours. Adeyemi. Out of Kolvar station, third week of the deportations, destination left blank because nobody wrote down where those trains actually went. I've spent thirty-one years not knowing if she had brothers on that train, if there's a whole branch of my family that ended in a rail yard I couldn't find on a map. And you're standing on my deck, in my bilge water, telling me no."

Ulyana set the pump handle down with exaggerated care, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it. "You don't understand what's in it."

"Then explain it to me."

For a long moment the only sound was the river slapping the hull and the tick of water finding new places to leak. Then Ulyana went forward, unwrapped the oilcloth from the ledger alone — leaving the manifests bundled — and opened it on the chart table under the lamp.

It was worse than Tamsin had imagined. She'd imagined numbers, columns, the cold arithmetic of atrocity. It was that, but it was also *thorough*. Names, ages, occupations, the value of confiscated property assessed to the nearest mark. Someone had sat at a desk with good light and done careful work. Someone had been proud of this work. Page after page, station after station, and Tamsin ran her finger down the Kolvar entries with her heart going like a hammer until —

"Adeyemi." Her voice cracked on her own name. "Sela Adeyemi. Age twenty-six. Occupation: seamstress." Her grandmother had been twenty-six. Tamsin had a photograph of her at thirty, laughing on the dock at Meridian Point, and here was the state's assessment of her at twenty-six, valued alongside a sewing machine and two goats. "Property: one loom, household goods. Escort: Second Company, garrison detail." She looked up. "Where's the rest? Where did they take them?"

Ulyana turned pages with fingers that weren't quite steady. "The destinations are in a separate section. Cross-referenced by convoy." She found it, held it, and then — Tamsin saw it happen — hesitated.

"You hesitated," Tamsin said quietly. "You looked at the page and you hesitated, and now you're going to tell me it's better I don't know. Say it. Go on. Everyone who's ever held information over my head has told me it's better I don't know, so say it and let me tell you what I think of it."

"It's not—" Ulyana closed her eyes. Opened them. "There's a page missing. The convoy manifest for Kolvar is referenced but the page itself has been cut out. Cut, not lost — the stubs are still in the binding. Somebody removed it deliberately, and everything else in this ledger is intact, which means whoever cut it needed that page gone specifically." She tapped the stub. "Your grandmother's convoy. The record of where they went doesn't exist anymore. Maybe it never reached this ledger. Maybe someone destroyed it decades ago. Either way, showing you this page would be handing you a wound with no medicine in it."

"So you get to decide what I can bear."

"Yes," Ulyana said, and the honesty of it knocked Tamsin back a step. "Right now, yes, because it's in my hands and I'm the one carrying it downstream, and I have spent five years learning exactly what knowing does to a person. I found my grandfather's signature in this book three days ago. Do you understand? Not his name in some archive. His *signature*, authorizing rolling stock for a convoy out of Drenna station. I grew up eating dinner across the table from that hand. And knowing hasn't healed anything. Knowing has just made sure I'll never be able to look at a photograph of him again without seeing ink."

"Then you know exactly why I need to see it anyway."

They stared at each other across the chart table, across the open ledger, across sixty years of other people's bookkeeping, and Tamsin felt the strange vertigo of recognizing someone — not liking them, not yet trusting them, but recognizing them, the way you recognize a face across a crowded port because they're wearing the same expression you wear when you think nobody's watching.

* * *

She made tea because neither of them was going to win the argument standing up, and because her mother had taught her that negotiations go better when both parties are holding something warm. Outside, the rain thickened and thinned in long slow pulses, and the river rose another finger's width against the pilings.

"Here's my price," Tamsin said, setting the mugs down. "I run you downriver. Through the delta, past Kessel Locks, wherever you're going — and you're going somewhere specific, aren't you, because people don't steal chests and run rapids on a whim. I take you there. In exchange, whatever page bears my grandmother's name, whatever it says, I get it. Copy or original, I don't care which. Even if it says she died in a ditch outside a camp nobody named. Even if it says worse."

"And if it says nothing?" Ulyana wrapped both hands around her mug without drinking. "If the cut page means the answer is simply gone?"

"Then I finally get to stop waiting for it. That's worth something too. Waiting is the worst part. I've been waiting since I was nine years old and found my grandmother crying over a map of a country that isn't on maps anymore." Tamsin sat down across from her. "Name your counter-price. You want something. Nobody risks their neck for a stranger for free."

"I want the river," Ulyana said. "Your knowledge of it. Renner's men will be behind us and the monsoon's coming and I don't know these channels — I know rails, and rails don't move. Rivers do. So I'm buying a pilot, not a boat."

"Fine. Anything else?"

A pause. Then, reluctantly, like pulling a splinter: "When we get where we're going. Whatever I decide to do with the chest. I want you to argue with me about it. Properly. Before it happens. Because everyone who ever should have argued with my grandfather apparently stayed quiet, and I'd like to break the tradition."

Tamsin almost smiled. "That's the cheapest price anyone's ever asked me for. Arguing's free."

"Not with me it isn't. Ask anyone."

They shook on it, and Tamsin noticed that Ulyana's handshake was a surveyor's handshake — brief, precise, sealing a measurement rather than a friendship — and thought: *good*. Friendships could sink you. Contracts floated.

But the argument wasn't finished, because arguments like theirs never are, and it resurfaced that evening while they patched the aft seam by lamplight, Tamsin holding the caulking iron and Ulyana mixing oakum, both of them soaked to the elbows.

"You really believe it heals," Ulyana said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I believe silence kills." Tamsin drove the iron home. "My grandmother never talked about Vessar. Never. Fifty years in this country and she wouldn't say the word. And you know what happened? The silence grew teeth. My mother inherited it and passed it down like furniture — this whole haunted parlor where certain questions weren't allowed. I found out about the deportation from a schoolbook. A *schoolbook*, two paragraphs, and I remember thinking, that's it? Four hundred people from one town and that's it? The silence didn't protect anybody. It just meant the only version of the story left standing was the one written by the men with the stamp."

"And the truth? Your grandmother's name in a column next to her sewing machine — does that heal you?"

"It's not supposed to heal *me*." Tamsin sat back on her heels. "That's what you keep getting wrong. You're thinking of truth like medicine, something you swallow and wait to feel better. It's not medicine. It's a debt. Somebody wrote my grandmother down like she was cargo, and somebody has to write her back as a person, and until that happens the debt sits there accruing interest — in me, in my cousins, in every kid who grows up feeling haunted by a country they've never seen. Truth doesn't heal. Truth *settles*. There's a difference."

Ulyana was quiet for a long time, working the oakum into the seam with more force than it needed. "In the camps they talk about testimony," she said finally. "Survivors saying that bearing witness mattered more than justice, sometimes. That being counted was the whole point. I used to think that was sentiment." She wiped her hands. "Now I think maybe you're right and I'm wrong, and I hate it, because if you're right then what I've been doing — keeping it closed, keeping it safe, keeping it *mine* — is just another kind of silence. Just my grandfather's silence with better intentions."

"That's the first honest thing you've said since you climbed aboard."

"Don't get used to it."

Outside, the rain turned suddenly serious — the fat, deliberate drops of a front assembling itself — and Tamsin stepped out onto the deck to look north, toward the low black wall of cloud stacking up over the hills like a fleet at anchor. Too early. The monsoon wasn't due for days yet, but rivers didn't consult calendars, and the barometer on her bulkhead had been falling all afternoon like something diving.

"We leave at first light," she said. "Kessel Locks closes when the flood crest hits, and if we're caught south of it when that wall comes through, we sit anchored in some backwater for three weeks with Renner's friends picking their teeth waiting for us. We run ahead of it."

Ulyana joined her at the rail, the ledger's oilcloth bundle under her arm. For a moment neither spoke, and the river breathed beneath them, patient and vast, carrying everything it had ever been given.

"One condition I didn't mention," Tamsin said. "Whatever's in that chest — when we deliver it, I'm there. I don't hand my grandmother's story off to strangers and walk away. I watch it land."

Ulyana looked at her, and in the lamplight spilling from the wheelhouse Tamsin saw something shift in her face — not softening exactly, more like a lock turning. "Deal," she said. "Crew?"

"Crew," Tamsin said, and went to check the engine, because dawn was seven hours away and the river was rising, and somewhere behind them, she'd have bet the boat, a man with a consulate card was already making calls.

She was right. But she wouldn't learn how right until the radio crackled awake at midnight with a voice reading a warrant — her passenger's name in it, and a reward, and a description of a green-hulled river boat answering to the *Marrow's Grace*.
