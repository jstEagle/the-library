## 9. Monsoon Warning

The radio woke her before the storm did.

It was a cheap set, bolted to the wheelhouse bulkhead of Tamsin's boat — the *Marianne's Daughter*, a name Tamsin said she'd explain someday and then never did — and at four in the morning it crackled into life with the flat, unhurried voice of the river authority reading out the weather bulletin for the lower basin. Ulyana lay in the narrow bunk behind the wheelhouse and listened with her eyes open, translating the numbers into distances without meaning to. It was how her mind worked now. It had been working that way for five years.

*Depression Nine has strengthened overnight. The system is expected to make landfall within thirty-six hours. Rainfall totals of three hundred millimeters or more are forecast for the middle and lower reaches. All traffic is advised to clear the Kessel Locks reach before Thursday noon. After closure, the lower river will be impassable for an estimated three weeks.*

Three weeks.

Ulyana sat up in the dark and did the arithmetic the way she'd once done it on survey crews, chalk on slate, no sentiment allowed. They were anchored forty kilometers above the locks, tied off to a dead bollard on a bank gone soft with the first rains. Forty kilometers of winding channel, maybe six hours at Tamsin's cruising speed if the current cooperated. But the current wasn't going to cooperate. It was already running high and brown and fast, carrying whole trees downstream like matchsticks, and every hour it would run faster.

Thirty-six hours until landfall. Locks closing Thursday noon. And somewhere behind them, Ivo Renner.

She dressed in the dark, pulled on her boots, and went out into the rain.

It wasn't really raining yet — not properly, not monsoon-properly — just a fine grey drizzle that came down out of a sky the color of wet concrete and made the whole world feel like it was holding its breath. The river had swollen over its banks in the night. Where yesterday there had been a strip of mud and scrub between them and the treeline, there was now only moving water, and the trees stood ankle-deep in it, patient as posts.

Tamsin was already awake. Ulyana found her in the engine room, lying on her back under the main engine with a wrench in one hand and a flashlight clenched between her teeth, and for a moment Ulyana just watched her work — the sure economy of her hands, the way she seemed to know what the machinery wanted before it knew itself.

"The radio," Ulyana said.

Tamsin slid out, spat the flashlight into her palm, and sat up. Her hair was flattened with grease and sweat and she didn't bother fixing it. "Heard it."

"Three weeks, after closure."

"I heard it." She set the wrench down and looked up at Ulyana, and in the yellow light of the work lamp her face was very still. "You're doing the math. I can see you doing the math. Stop doing the math."

"Forty kilometers. Six hours, maybe eight in this current—"

"And if we blow an engine, or wrap something around the screw, or the fog comes down on us in the gorge like it does this time of year, it's twelve, and we arrive at the locks with the storm on top of us and the lock-keepers gone home to their families." Tamsin got to her feet, wiping her hands on a rag that had stopped being white sometime in the previous decade. "I've been trapped above Kessel once before. Two years ago, a late flood. Three weeks sitting on a mudbank eating rice and tinned fish while the water ate my paintwork. I don't recommend it."

"So we leave now."

"We leave now." Tamsin said it like a verdict, but she didn't move toward the controls. She stood there a moment longer, arms folded, looking at the chest.

It sat in the corner of the engine room where they'd stowed it two nights ago, wrapped again in its oilcloth, lashed down against the hull with cargo straps. Just a box. Water-stained wood, brass corners gone green, the broken lock hanging loose. The seal of Vessar stamped into the lid, half worn away, like a face seen through frosted glass.

Ulyana caught herself looking at it the way she always looked at it now — the way you look at a door you know opens onto something you can't take back.

"It knows we're talking about it," Tamsin said dryly.

"Don't."

"I'm serious. That thing has opinions. It got my grandmother killed—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "Got my grandmother *taken*. Twenty-six years before I was born, and it's still reaching out of the water to grab people." She shook her head. "Get us some coffee. We cast off in twenty minutes."

* * *

They ran the first twenty kilometers fast.

Tamsin drove the way she did everything — with a competence so total it looked like carelessness, standing at the wheel with one hand resting light on the spokes, reading the water the way Ulyana read track grades. She took the bends wide where the current piled up on the outside, cut them tight where an eddy gave her free speed, threaded the boat through the drowned willows without touching throttle. Ulyana stood beside her with the chart and called out the marks she remembered from her surveys, and it occurred to her, somewhere around the third bend, that this was the first time in five years she had used her knowledge of these lines for anything but measuring their absence.

"You've run this stretch before," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question.

"Twice. On foot, mostly. The old Vessar line comes down along the east bank through here — you can see the embankment when the water's low. My grandfather surveyed it."

"Your grandfather surveyed everything, sounds like."

"He surveyed everything they let him survey." Ulyana kept her eyes on the water. "And a few things they didn't."

Tamsin glanced at her, said nothing, and put the boat around a bend where the current hit them broadside and the whole hull groaned.

The rain started in earnest an hour later.

Not the drizzle — the real thing, the vertical wall of it, the kind of rain that made the river seem to rise even as you watched it, that turned the far bank into a rumor and filled the air with a sound like static, like the whole sky had become a badly tuned radio. Tamsin slowed to half speed because there was no point being fast if you couldn't see the channel, and the *Marianne's Daughter* settled into a steady, laboring grind against the current, and the clock in Ulyana's head ticked louder.

At midday they passed the mouth of the Sorrel, a tributary coming in hard from the west, and Ulyana saw Tamsin look at it and look away.

"What?"

"Ferry crossing used to be there." Tamsin's voice was flat. "Before the war. Before the deportations. My grandmother crossed there twice a year to sell cloth. There's a village on the other side — Sarno. Was." She adjusted the throttle a fraction. "The ledgers list Sarno. Eleven families, October of the last year of the war. Whole village, gone in one train."

Ulyana said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either too small or too large.

"That's why I'm doing this," Tamsin went on, still watching the water. "In case you were wondering. In case you thought it was about the money, or the adventure, or because you have nice maps." A short breath that might have been a laugh. "Eleven families. Somebody wrote them down in a ledger and signed his name at the bottom and filed it, and that was the end of them, officially. And now the official record is in a box on my boat, and I am not giving it back to the people who filed it."

"No," Ulyana said. "We're not."

* * *

The broadcast came at two in the afternoon.

It interrupted the weather bulletin — cut across it, actually, which told Ulyana everything before a single word was spoken, because legitimate notices waited their turn. The voice that came out of the speaker was smooth, educated, faintly amused. It was not Renner's voice, but it had Renner's accent, the accent of the consulate district, of people who had never gotten wet in their lives.

*"Notice to all vessel operators on the lower reach. A warrant has been issued by the regional magistrate's office at Vessar-on-Delta for the detention of one Ulyana Crane, engineer, of no fixed address, on charges of theft of archival property from the estate of the late Emil Crane. The subject is believed to be traveling aboard a river vessel southbound for the Kessel Locks. Operators assisting in her detention will be compensated. Operators harboring her should be advised that such assistance constitutes a criminal offense under the Emergency Navigation Act."*

Static. Then the weather bulletin resumed, blandly, as though nothing had happened.

Ulyana stood in the wheelhouse with the rain hammering the windows and felt something cold settle into her chest — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. She had spent five years building a life with no fixed address precisely so that no document could find her, and here was a document finding her anyway, read aloud to the entire river, naming her a thief of her own grandfather's papers.

"He's good," she said finally. "Fast. The magistrate's office doesn't move that quickly unless someone's paying it to."

"He's thorough." Tamsin hadn't changed course. Her hands stayed easy on the wheel. "My mother used to say the old Vessar families ran the country the way you run an estate — you don't hate the tenants, you just make sure the fences hold. Renner's a fence man." She looked over. "You all right?"

"They said theft from the estate of the late Emil Crane." Ulyana heard her own voice come out level, which surprised her. "That's clever. It makes it a family matter. It makes me the estranged granddaughter stealing from her own dead grandfather instead of—" She stopped.

"Instead of what he actually was," Tamsin finished quietly.

Instead of an accomplice. Instead of a man whose signature sat on a requisition order for eleven families from a village called Sarno. Instead of the reason a woman named Adeyemi had been put on a train that never came back.

"There'll be inspectors at the locks now," Ulyana said. "Real ones, with the warrant in hand. He doesn't need to catch us himself. He just needs the state to do it for him, politely, with paperwork."

"Then we don't get inspected." Tamsin reached forward and tapped the chart, on the stretch of river just below the locks where the channel narrowed between old training walls. "Kessel has two moods. Official mood: you tie up, you show papers, an inspector walks your deck, takes an hour, sometimes three. Unofficial mood: commercial traffic with clean manifests goes straight through, because during pre-storm clearance they're pushing everything through as fast as it floats." She smiled, and it wasn't entirely a nice smile. "So we need a clean manifest."

"With what cargo? We're carrying a stolen chest and a warrant's worth of bad luck."

"With rail spikes." Tamsin tapped the chart again. "There's a consolidation yard two kilometers below us — Harrow Bend. Everything moving ahead of the storm is stacking up there waiting for the locks. Barges, tugs, deck barges with construction freight for the new bridge works. Nobody inspects a consignment of rail spikes twice, especially not spikes consigned to the bridge company, especially not in a storm window when every inspector on the river has forty boats to clear before Thursday noon." She looked up at Ulyana. "Can you forge a shipping manifest?"

Ulyana stared at her. "I'm a railroad engineer."

"I know. Can you *forge* one?"

"I can produce a document that would survive inspection by anyone who doesn't personally know the bridge company's freight clerk." She hesitated. "Which is most inspectors, in a storm window."

"Then that's our ticket." Tamsin turned back to the wheel, and the rain streamed down the glass, and somewhere ahead of them, invisible in the grey, the locks were waiting with whatever Renner had arranged inside them. "Harrow Bend by dark. We raft up alongside a spike barge, borrow a few tons of cover, and go through the locks tomorrow morning as boring as ballast."

"And if it doesn't work?"

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. Through the wheelhouse windows the river ran brown and swollen, carrying its trees, its fences, its drowned fields, everything the rising water took from the land without malice and without return.

"Then we're trapped above the locks for three weeks," she said, "with a warrant on the radio and a man in a dry coat who knows exactly where we are. So." She rolled the wheel a few degrees, and the *Marianne's Daughter* leaned into the current and held. "Let's not let it not work."

* * *

They made Harrow Bend as the light failed.

The consolidation yard was chaos — a mile of moored barges rafted three and four deep, tugs jostling for position, deck lights burning yellow halos in the rain, the air full of diesel smoke and shouted instructions in three languages. Every boat on the upper river had run for the locks ahead of the storm, and now they all sat stacked up here like beads on a string, waiting for morning and the slow arithmetic of the lock chambers.

Tamsin brought the *Marianne's Daughter* in against a long deck barge loaded with crates of bridge iron and palletized sacks, and within ten minutes of tying off she had done what Tamsin did — talked to three skippers, shared a flask of something medicinal, listened to two hours of complaint about lock queues and storm closures, and returned to the wheelhouse with a borrowed bill of lading, a genuine consignment number belonging to a spike shipment delayed upstream, and the cheerful goodwill of a man who now considered her a cousin.

"Consignment 4471-C," she announced, dropping onto the bench. "Sixty tons of rail spikes, Harrow Bend to the Kessel bridge works. Our friend Captain Doss says the bridge company won't miss them because half the shipment's already missing. He says everyone steals from the bridge company; it's practically a municipal service." She pushed a blank manifest form across the table. "Your department, engineer."

Ulyana sat down and looked at the form for a long moment.

Five years ago she would not have done this. Five years ago she had been a person who filed things correctly, who measured twice, who believed — with the whole rigid faith of her upbringing — that documents were promises and signatures were sacred. Her grandfather had taught her that. *A line that isn't documented doesn't exist, Ulyana. Paper is the memory of iron.*

Paper was also the memory of eleven families from Sarno, filed and signed and forgotten.

She picked up the pen and began to write.

Her hand was steady. That was the strange part. She forged the weights from memory of standard spike consignments, forged the freight clerk's initials in a style she invented on the spot — a small, cramped, leftward slant, a man in a hurry, a man who hated storms — and when she finished, the manifest looked so ordinary, so utterly dull, that she almost laughed. Sixty tons of the most boring object in the industrial world. No inspector in a storm window would look twice.

Except one who'd been paid to.

She said so. Tamsin, packing the chest into an empty spike crate — padding it with oilcloth, nailing the lid, stenciling the consignment number on the side in borrowed paint — paused with the hammer raised.

"One inspector," Tamsin said. "Maybe. If Renner's bought him, he'll be looking for a boat, not a crate. Warrants describe people. Freight describes weight." She drove the last nail home and set the crate among sixty others in their hold, indistinguishable, anonymous, buried in ballast. "By tomorrow noon we're through the locks and he's reading a manifest for spikes that were never on our boat."

"And if he opens the crate anyway."

Tamsin looked at her across the lamplit hold, rain drumming on the deck above, the whole yard creaking and groaning around them like a fleet at anchor in a war.

"Then he opens it," she said, "and we find out what we're made of. Get some sleep, Crane. Tomorrow we go through the gate."

Ulyana climbed to her bunk but did not sleep, not really. She lay listening to the rain thicken and the river rise, and in her head the numbers kept ticking over, involuntary, relentless: hours to landfall, kilometers to the locks, minutes per lock chamber, the whole journey reduced to a schedule that could fail at any point and probably would. Thirty-six hours had become twenty-two. Somewhere upstream, a depression with a number instead of a name was walking toward them across the sea, and somewhere downstream a man in a dry coat was making phone calls, and between the two of them, on a boat disguised as ballast, she was carrying her family's crime in a spike crate.

Near dawn the radio murmured again — the river authority, updating its bulletin. *Closure of the Kessel Locks confirmed for Thursday noon. All vessels must be through the chambers or clear of the reach by that time. No exceptions will be granted.*

No exceptions.

Ulyana closed her eyes and, for the first time since the river had given up its chest, wished — briefly, shamefully — that she had never learned to count.
