## 10. Kessel Locks

*Tamsin*

The locks announced themselves before she saw them — a smell of diesel and wet concrete rolling downriver on the wind, then the long gray jaw of the gates opening out of the rain like something the river had failed to swallow.

Tamsin cut the engine to idle and let the current carry them the last kilometer, because an approaching boat that sounded nervous drew attention, and attention was the one cargo she couldn't afford today. Ahead, Kessel Locks sat astride the channel the way it had for eighty years: two concrete towers, a signal mast wearing its red light like a wound, and a customs pontoon jutting into the current where a man in a green slicker stood with a clipboard under his arm and the particular stillness of someone paid to be suspicious.

"Checkpoint," she said, not turning around. "You know what to do?"

Behind her, in the shadow of the wheelhouse, Ulyana Crane said nothing. Tamsin heard her move instead — the soft scrape of the tarpaulin, the knock of knuckles against wood. Checking on the chest again. She'd been checking every eleven minutes since dawn. Tamsin had counted.

"Ulyana."

"I know what to do." The engineer's voice was flat as her survey maps. "I stand in the hold looking bored, and if anyone asks, I'm your cargo handler and I've been on this run six months."

"And if they ask what's in the crates?"

"Rail spikes. Forty cases of rail spikes, consigned to the Marrow Bend reconstruction project, invoice number—" Ulyana recited it without hesitation, and Tamsin felt the small unwilling flicker of respect she'd been fighting since the barge left the loading yard. The woman had memorized the manifest overnight. Had forged the shipping stamp herself with a block of rubber and an ink pad, and the forgery was good — better than good, the kind of good that came from a childhood spent watching paperwork done properly by people who never doubted their right to do it.

"One more thing," Tamsin said. "The inspector will be Vessari. You can tell by the way he stands — heels together, like he's waiting for an anthem. They all do it, the old families' people. So whatever you're carrying in your face right now, put it away. Don't give him a reason to look closer at you than at me."

"My face is fine."

"Your face looks like a woman reading a requisition order," Tamsin said. "I've seen it. It's a face that makes inspectors curious." She let the boat drift another hundred meters, rain ticking off the wheelhouse roof, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quieter. "My grandmother crossed a checkpoint once. Did I tell you that? Not this one — upriver, before the war closed everything. She said the man who waved her through never even looked at her papers. He looked at her hands. Said dirty hands didn't need inspecting." She turned then, finally, and met Ulyana's eyes. "So keep your hands busy. Hold a spike case. Look like labor. Labor is invisible."

Ulyana picked up a spike case without argument, settled it against her hip, and stood in the hold's gray light with rain running off her jaw — and just like that she became furniture. Tamsin almost smiled. Almost.

Then the pontoon loudspeaker crackled: *Vessel inbound, heave to and prepare for inspection,* and there was no more time for anything but the work.

She brought the boat alongside with the kind of touch that took ten years to learn — bow line first, fenders kissing the tire-draped edge of the pontoon, no clang, no scrape, nothing to make a bored man look up twice. The inspector came down the pontoon steps with his clipboard held out of the rain, and yes, there it was: heels together, shoulders squared, a man built from somebody else's discipline.

"Papers," he said.

Tamsin handed them over with both hands and a smile she'd practiced in a hundred ports. "Morning, sir. Cargo's rail spikes, Marrow Bend consignment, we're trying to beat the front."

"Aren't we all." He flipped pages. His eyes were pale and patient and went nowhere quickly. "Adeyemi. That a delta name?"

"Born on the water, sir. Third generation piloting this reach."

"Mm." He turned a page. Turned another. The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat. And then, mildly, without looking up: "Your draft mark says you're sitting heavy, Captain Adeyemi. Manifest says forty cases of spikes. Spikes don't weigh what you're drawing."

The world narrowed to the width of the pontoon planks between them.

Tamsin laughed — a real laugh, or close enough that it made no difference — and slapped the rail. "Sir, you want to hear something funny? Half my ballast is river water. Took on a leak two days back above the fork, pumped half the delta into my bilge getting here. You want to inspect my pump instead of my cargo, I'll show you a machine that's earned its pay." She jerked a thumb at the bilge pump bolted by the stern, still dripping, still running on its slow automatic cycle — because she'd left it running, deliberately, since dawn, so the deck would stay wet and the story would write itself.

The inspector looked at the pump. Looked at the water sheeting off the deck. Looked at Tamsin, whose grandmother had taught her that the best lie is a true thing told slightly sideways.

"Leaking boats," he said, and sighed with the weariness of a man who'd inspected a thousand leaking boats. "Half the river's sinking and the other half's lying about it." He stamped the manifest. "Marrow Bend reconstruction. That's government contract work?"

"Reopening the old Vessar line, sir. First survey crew went through last month."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, submerged, gone. "Vessar line," he repeated. "There's no Vessar line. There never was a line there."

"That's what I heard too, sir." Tamsin kept her voice light, easy, a woman making conversation about weather. "But the invoice has a seal on it older than my grandfather, so I figure somebody knows something I don't."

He handed the papers back. For a moment — one long moment, rain drumming, the red signal light bleeding color into the gray water — he did not let go of the manifest. His pale eyes lifted past her shoulder, into the hold, and found Ulyana standing among the spike cases with her hand resting on one like a woman steadying herself on a bus.

"Your handler," he said. "New?"

"Six months, sir. Best I've had. Doesn't complain, doesn't steal."

"No," the inspector agreed softly. "They usually don't." He released the manifest. "Cast off, Captain. Lock chamber's open. And mind your leak."

Tamsin cast off with steady hands and a heart going like a paddlewheel, and only when the lock gates had swallowed them and the pontoon was a gray smudge in the rain did she allow herself to breathe.

* * *

*Ulyana*

The lock chamber was a cathedral of wet stone, and Ulyana hated it.

She stood in the hold while the gates closed behind them and the water began to fall — twelve meters down to the lower reach, the walls rising around the boat like the inside of a throat — and she thought about how many times her grandfather must have passed through this exact chamber. His surveys would have brought him here. His handwriting was on the ledger; his route ran through these locks; and somewhere in the concrete under the moss and the flood-stain, maybe, his initials sat carved beside the initials of men who had loaded human beings onto trains marked *agricultural transfer.*

"You can stop gripping the crate now," Tamsin said from the wheelhouse door. "We're through."

"We're not through." Ulyana loosened her fingers one at a time. Her palm had printed itself red into the wood. "Renner knew about the checkpoint. He'll have someone past it."

"He watched us load for an hour from the far bank and did nothing. If he had men past the locks, why wait?"

"Because he doesn't need to stop us here." Ulyana heard her own voice go distant, analytical, the voice she used for bridge loadings and failure modes. "Stopping us here is loud. Loud leaves witnesses, witnesses leave records, records are the one thing a man like Renner can't afford. He wants us somewhere quiet. Downstream. Where the river does the work and nobody files a report." She looked up at the falling water marks on the lock wall, measuring without meaning to — the way she measured everything, distances, intervals, the space between lightning and thunder. "He's patient. That's what frightens me. He offered to *buy* the chest. Men who offer first have already priced in taking it second."

Tamsin leaned against the doorframe, arms folded, studying her. "You talk like a survey report."

"It's how I think."

"No." Tamsin shook her head slowly. "It's how you hide. There's a difference, and I've been watching you do both for three days." She pushed off the frame and came down into the hold, boots ringing on the plates, and crouched beside the chest where it lay disguised under forty cases of rail spikes, its brass seal hidden beneath a stenciled shipping label reading MARROW BEND WORKS. "That inspector recognized something. You saw it. The Vessar line — he flinched."

"Everyone old enough flinches at that name."

"Not like that. He flinched like a man who'd been told all his life there was no such line and had decided, privately, not to believe it." Tamsin tapped the chest lid once, lightly, the way you'd tap a wall listening for a hollow place. "Which means there are people inside the machinery who already suspect. Renner isn't protecting a secret. He's protecting a secret that's leaking."

The water level dropped. Through the sluice grates, the lower river appeared in slices — brown, swollen, fast, carrying whole trees like matchsticks. The monsoon front was a bruise-colored wall on the southern horizon now, visible even through rain, and the radio that morning had given it thirty hours to the lower reaches.

"After Marrow Bend," Ulyana said, "the channel forks. North goes through the gorge — Tamsin, I've read the surveys, in flood the gorge is a death sentence—"

"The north fork in flood is a death sentence, yes. The south fork is longer, slower, and passes through three villages where everyone owes me money or I owe them, which amounts to the same thing." Tamsin grinned, and for a second she looked young, unburdened, a girl running cargo through summer water. Then the grin faded. "And somewhere on the south fork, if you're right, a patient man is doing arithmetic. How much fuel we have. How fast we go. Where the quiet places are." She stood. "So we don't take the south fork."

"You just said—"

"I said we don't take it *alone.*" Tamsin climbed toward the wheelhouse, then paused at the ladder, rain streaming off the overhead beam between them. "Get some sleep, engineer. When the gates open, I'm going to need your eyes, and I'd rather they weren't full of ghosts."

Ulyana stayed in the hold after she left. The chamber finished draining; the forward gates groaned apart; the river below reached in and took the boat. She kept her hand on the chest the whole way down, feeling through the wood the faint tremor of the water working against the hull, and told herself it was the cargo she was steadying.

It was the closest thing to a lie she'd told in years.

* * *

*Renner*

From the far bank, through rain-beaded glass, Ivo Renner watched the lock gates open and the little cargo boat slide out into the lower reach, riding low and lying about it.

He lowered the binoculars and permitted himself a small, private smile — the smile he used at funerals and shareholders' meetings, calibrated to suggest grief or satisfaction depending on the lighting.

"She bluffed Ostrer," said the man beside him. Vasik, his driver, his cousin, his hands. "Ostrer's not stupid. He'll file a report tonight."

"Of course he'll file a report. He'll describe a leaking boat carrying rail spikes, and he'll add a paragraph about the Vessar line that he'll regret writing by morning, because my office reads those reports and Ostrer knows it." Renner raised the binoculars again. The boat was a smudge now, merging with the rain, heading south toward the fork. "He won't mention the woman in the hold. He noticed her — I could see him noticing — but he won't write her down. Writing her down means admitting what else might be on that boat, and Ostrer is a man who has decided, very carefully, not to believe in certain things. Isn't that right, Ostrer?" he murmured to the empty river. "You learned it from your father, and he learned it from mine. We are a country of men who decided not to believe."

Thunder rolled down the valley, long and low, the front announcing itself like a guest who intended to stay a month.

"Fuel the launch," Renner said. "Not today. Tomorrow, after the front makes landfall — no one runs the river in the first blow, so no one will wonder about a boat moving in it." He turned up his collar and walked back toward the car, gravel crunching, unhurried. Patience was the family inheritance he valued most. His grandfather had waited out a war, out an inquiry, out three governments; the ledgers had slept sixty years in a flooded depot until a railroad woman with a rope and too much curiosity pulled them out of the water. Sixty years. A few days of monsoon was nothing.

"Mr. Renner." Vasik hesitated at the car door. "If the storm damages the locks — the upper gates are old, the engineers have been warning—"

"Then the river closes, and the boat stays on our side of it, and the river keeps what it keeps." Renner settled into the seat, dry at last, and looked out at the drowning landscape with something close to affection. "But send word to Marrow Bend tonight. Quietly. If the reconstruction office receives an urgent telegram asking about a spike consignment — invoice number whatever she forged — the office will confirm it was never placed. Let the inspector discover his own mistake. Let him sweat. A frightened official checks things, and checked things slow a boat down."

The car pulled away from the locks, wipers slapping, and Renner watched the road unspool and thought, as he often did, about his grandfather's study — the leather chairs, the sealed cabinets, the way the old man had explained the family's position one evening in a voice like a ledger itself: *Everything above the waterline is ours, Ivo. Our task is simply to keep it above the waterline.*

Somewhere downstream, a leaking boat carried sixty years of waterlogged paper toward the light.

Renner checked his watch, noted the interval between the lightning flash and the thunder — four seconds, closing — and chose patience.

By morning, the storm would own the river. And by the time it loosened its grip, he intended to be standing exactly where the boat had to come ashore.
