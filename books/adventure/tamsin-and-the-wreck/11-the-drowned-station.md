## 11. The Drowned Station

The storm found them an hour after dark, which was earlier than the radio had promised and exactly as early as Tamsin had predicted.

"We're not outrunning it," she shouted over the first hard slabs of rain, one hand on the wheel, the other pointing upstream to where the river bent around a shoulder of black hills. "There's an old station on the north bank. Kilometer marker one-sixteen. You'd know it better than me."

"I would," Ulyana said, and the words came out strange, because she had walked that station a hundred times without ever walking it at all — had traced its platform on a survey map, measured its platform edge to the centimeter, noted in her tidy engineer's hand: *structure unsound, recommend demolition.* She had never stood on it. She had never wanted to. It had been one line item among four hundred on a closed line that officially did not exist.

Now it was the only roof on the river.

Tamsin brought the boat in against the current with the kind of economy that made Ulyana aware, again, of how much of piloting was invisible — the small corrections, the reading of water by the sound it made against the hull. The station materialized out of the rain as a suggestion first: a gable, then the ribs of a platform canopy, then the whole sagging body of it, half its lower floor standing in brown water like a man waist-deep who had decided not to struggle.

"Port side's solid," Tamsin said. "The rest is theater."

They tied off to a cast-iron column that must once have held a lamp, and climbed up through the rain onto a platform furred with moss and silt. The water had been higher here once — much higher. Ulyana could see the tide line of old flood years stained across the station wall, three dark bands like the growth rings of a drowned tree. Above the highest band, a painted sign still clung to the brick, its letters bleeding down the face:

**VESZARÁN HALT**

The name of a country that no longer existed, printed in a language the region had spent sixty years politely forgetting.

"You knew this place was here," Tamsin said. It wasn't quite a question. She had a way of making statements into doors and then standing aside to see if you'd walk through them.

"I knew it was on a map," Ulyana said. "There's a difference."

Inside, the station was one long room and a smaller one behind a door hanging by one hinge. The rain drummed on the roof like a crowd that wouldn't disperse. Tamsin found an old iron stove in the corner, still whole, and declared it serviceable in the tone of a woman who had made homes out of less. Ulyana got a fire going with survey stakes and the dry heartwood of a fallen bench, and for a while there was nothing but the small domestic work of survival — wringing out clothes, rationing the bread, setting the chest where the floor was highest and driest, wrapped again in its oilcloth like a body they were keeping.

Ulyana caught herself checking on it the way you check on a sleeping child. She hated that she did it. She did it anyway.

* * *

It was Tamsin who found the carvings.

Ulyana had gone to the back room to see if anything was salvageable, and came back to find Tamsin crouched at the base of the far wall with her lamp held low, rain still dripping from her braid onto the floorboards.

"Come look at this."

The wall there was sheltered from the worst of the damp, and the plaster had fallen away in a wide patch, exposing the brick beneath. Into the soft old mortar, someone had cut letters with a knife or a nail — cut them small and hurried and deep, the way you cut when you're not sure you'll be allowed to finish.

**M.K. 1943 — VESZARÁN — 14 DÍN**

Below it, in a different hand, shakier: **A.S. — the same day.** Below that, another. Another. Ulyana counted without meaning to. Eleven sets of initials, and dates. She knew the dates before she finished counting them, the way you know your own birthday. The fourteenth of Dín. The twenty-second. The third of the next month. The dates of the trains.

The dates of the trains that had never run, on a line that had never been built, through a station that appeared on no public record — and here was a wall full of people who had stood exactly where she was standing, wet and afraid, waiting for a locomotive, and who had cut their names into the mortar because a name in a wall was the last thing a person could own.

"They were here," Tamsin said quietly. "They stood on this platform."

Ulyana's knees decided the question of standing for her. She sat down on the floor beside Tamsin, her back against the cold brick, close enough to read the smallest of the carvings. The fire popped. The rain worked the roof.

"My grandfather surveyed this line," she said.

She heard the sentence leave her and understood that once it was out, there would be no taking it back, and that she had been carrying it for eleven days like water in her hands, and that her hands were tired.

Tamsin didn't say *tell me.* She just turned the lamp a little, so the light fell better on the wall, and waited. It was, Ulyana would think later, the kindest thing anyone had done for her in five years.

* * *

"His name was Emil Crane," she said. "In the ledger — the requisition order — it's signed *E. Crane, Ing.* I used to sign my own reports the same way. *U. Crane, Ing.* I thought it was a family joke. A habit. It was — " She stopped. Started again. "It was a signature on a document ordering forty-one families moved east for labor service. Dated the ninth of Dín, 1943. Five days before the first train."

Tamsin's face didn't change, but something in it went very still, the way the river goes still above a deep hole.

"I grew up on his stories," Ulyana said. "That's the thing you have to understand. He wasn't a monster in a photograph. He was — he made me a whistle out of a piece of brake pipe. He taught me to read contour lines before I could read words. He'd take me out to the yards on Sundays and we'd count the locomotives, and he'd say, *Ulyana, a railway is a promise. Every rail says: someone will come back this way.* " Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She let it. "He measured everything. That's where I got it. He had a notebook of distances — every place he'd ever been, and how far it was from every other place. When he died I inherited it and I thought, this is what a life should look like. Ordered. Measurable. I thought the miles were the honest part of him."

"And they weren't."

"The miles were the *hiding* part of him." Ulyana looked at the wall, at the eleven names. "After the war, Vessar dissolved. The borders moved. Everyone agreed, very quietly, to agree that certain things hadn't happened. The families that ran the railways — his employers — became the families that ran the region. New names on the same stations. And my grandfather went home and raised a daughter and taught a granddaughter to count locomotives, and he never once, not in thirty years, said the word *Vessar* out loud. I found out about the estrangement — my father and him — because my father said he couldn't shake the man's hand anymore. He never said why. I chose not to ask. Do you understand? I *chose* not to ask. I was twenty-three and he was the person I loved most in the world, and I decided that not knowing was a kind of loyalty."

The rain eased for a moment, then came back harder, as if the storm had been listening and had opinions.

"Five years ago my father died. At the funeral, my grandfather's sister — my great-aunt, ninety years old, sharp as a rail spike — took my arm at the graveside and said, *Ask him about Dín.* Just that. Ask him about Dín. And I went to him, and I asked, and he looked at me — " Ulyana pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "He looked at me the way you look at a train you know is going to hit you. And he said, *I measured the line, Ulyana. That's all I did. I measured it.* And I said, *Then who laid it?* And he didn't answer. And I walked out, and I didn't come back. Five years. I told myself I was a woman who preferred distance. I told myself distance was a discipline."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, and it cost Ulyana nothing to hear it, which was its own kind of mercy.

"My grandmother's family was put on a train in the winter of '43," Tamsin said. "From a town upriver. The family story is that they were taken east for labor and that my grandmother, who was nine, came back alone in the spring, walking, and never talked about it. My mother says the family broke into two halves that year — the ones who asked questions and the ones who didn't, and they didn't speak to each other for a generation. I grew up on the quiet side." She touched one of the carvings with one fingertip, barely. "I used to think the quiet side was the strong side."

"And now?"

"Now I think somebody put my grandmother on a train, and somebody measured the line it ran on, and somebody signed the paper, and they all went home afterward and had granddaughters." She looked at Ulyana. "And the granddaughters are sitting in the station. That's what I think now."

Ulyana laughed — a short, broken sound, surprised out of her. "You have a terrible way of comforting people."

"I have a true way of comforting people. It's an acquired taste."

The fire settled. Outside, the river climbed audibly, a slow wet breathing against the platform pilings. Ulyana found she was crying — not dramatically, not the way people cry in stories, but steadily, the way a roof leaks, and Tamsin did not look at her or comment on it, which was the only reason she was able to keep going.

"I keep thinking about the whistle," Ulyana said. "He made me a whistle out of a piece of brake pipe. And I've been trying for eleven days to make the ledger mean something survivable. That he was coerced. That he was small and afraid and everyone was. And maybe all of that is true. But he signed it, Tamsin. He signed it, and then he lived fifty years, and he had every Sunday of fifty years to tell me the truth, and he measured distances instead."

"Maybe that's why he kept the notebook," Tamsin said. "Maybe the miles were his penance. Some men pray by counting."

"Then what does that make me? I inherited the counting. I've been doing his penance for five years and calling it a career."

"No," Tamsin said, with the flat certainty of a pilot contradicting a passenger who is wrong about the river. "You found the chest. He hid it in the water for sixty years and you pulled it out. Whatever you were doing before, you're not doing his penance now. Now you're doing something he didn't have the courage to do." She paused. "Or maybe he did, and the chest was his courage, and he just couldn't be there to carry it himself."

Ulyana looked at her. "You believe that? That he wanted it found?"

"I believe rivers give things back for a reason." Tamsin shrugged. "But I'm a pilot. We're professionally superstitious."

* * *

They took watches through the night, though neither slept much. Around three, Ulyana woke on the bench where she'd lain down and found Tamsin awake by the stove, the lamp turned low, sketching something in her logbook — the channel ahead, Ulyana realized, from memory, every bend and bar of the next forty kilometers, drawn freehand.

"You should sleep," Ulyana said.

"After you." Tamsin didn't look up. "You talked tonight. Talking costs more than it looks."

Ulyana lay back down and stared at the roof beams, where swallows' nests from some earlier decade clung like old lanterns. She felt, strangely, lighter — not absolved, nothing like absolved, but *carried*, as though the confession had been cargo and Tamsin had taken half of it aboard without being asked, the way crew does.

She was drifting toward something like sleep when Tamsin spoke again from the stove.

"Ulyana. The water."

Ulyana sat up. The river had come up the bank faster than any flood she'd surveyed — she knew floods the way she knew everything, in numbers, and this rate was wrong. And there was something else, something her engineer's ear had been worrying at beneath the rain without her noticing: a low, rhythmic groaning from upstream, carried down the valley through the dark.

Not the storm. Storms don't keep time.

"That's metal," Ulyana said, standing. "That's metal under load."

Tamsin was already at the door, looking out into the rain toward the upstream bend. "The Kessel gates," she said. "The locks upstream. If they've opened the floodgates early — or if something's let go —"

The groaning came again, longer this time, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the river's voice changed pitch — the sound of a great deal of water being told, all at once, to come downhill.
