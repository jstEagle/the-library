## 13. High Water

The storm came early. That was the first thing Tamsin understood, standing in the wheelhouse with her hands on the wheel and the sky gone the color of wet slate over the gorge walls. The radio had said two days. The river had said otherwise. Rivers always got their say first.

She'd been running the delta since she was sixteen, and she had learned the monsoon's moods the way other people learned a lover's — the way the air thickened before it, the way the birds went quiet and then frantic, the way the water in the channel turned a strange, glassy brown, like something holding its breath. All of it was here now, hours ahead of schedule, and the gorge ahead of them was already a mile of white noise.

"You're sure about this?" Ulyana stood in the wheelhouse doorway, rain already sheeting off her oilskin, one hand braced on the frame as the boat rolled. Behind her, lashed down under a tarp in the open hold, sat the chest — the chest, the whole reason they were out here playing chicken with a storm instead of tucked into some backwater waiting it out. "The radio said—"

"The radio said two days. The river disagrees." Tamsin checked the throttle, the bilge, the compass. "We go back, we're trapped above Kessel for weeks with Renner's people closing the net. We go through, we're in the lower delta before he can get a boat ahead of us." She glanced back. "You want to wait for him instead?"

Ulyana didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

The gorge took them an hour later, and it took them hard.

Tamsin had run the Narrows in high water exactly twice, and both times she'd been younger and stupider and the river had been kinder. Now the flood had turned the channel into a single churning vein between rock walls, and the water was moving faster than her boat could swim — which meant she wasn't piloting anymore, not really. She was falling with style, steering a falling object, trying to keep the bow pointed downstream and the stern out of the rocks.

"Keep your weight low!" she shouted over the roar. "If we broach, get to the high side!"

Ulyana crouched in the wheelhouse doorway, white-knuckled on the rail, and did not ask stupid questions. Tamsin was beginning to appreciate that about her — the railroad woman's habit of doing what she was told when the situation was genuinely serious, saving her arguments for calmer weather.

The first mile was survivable. The second was not.

A standing wave the height of the wheelhouse rose out of the channel where the river bent against the south wall, and Tamsin had half a second to choose: hit it square and probably flip, or quarter it and probably broach. She chose the wave. The bow climbed, climbed, hung at the top for one endless, weightless heartbeat with the whole gorge spread out below them like a dropped rope — and then the hull slammed down and the world went white.

When the spray cleared, the pump alarm was screaming.

* * *

The pump was a twelve-volt bilge pump, secondhand, bought in a fit of optimism two seasons ago. It had handled rain water and slow leaks without complaint. It was not built for what was coming through the hull now.

"We're shipping water fast," Tamsin yelled, crouched in the engine bay with a flashlight clenched in her teeth. The strainer was clogged with river silt; the pump was sucking air, whining, doing nothing. "I need two minutes to clear it!"

"You have one!" Ulyana was already moving, tearing open the toolbox, finding the spare hose. "Tell me what to do!"

"Unscrew the strainer — no, the *other* — yes — hold that under the water, let it fill, screw it back on. Fast!"

Ulyana's hands, so precise with maps and instruments, shook as she worked. But they worked. The pump coughed, gulped, and caught, and the water in the bilge began, slowly, to go down instead of up.

Tamsin let out a breath she felt in her whole chest, and that was when the chest moved.

* * *

The lashings had held through the wave. They did not hold through the second one, which came without warning off a submerged ledge and threw the boat sideways. Tamsin heard the tarp snap taut, heard the cleat tear half out of the deck with a sound like a tooth being pulled, and then the chest — four hundred pounds of iron-banded oak and everything the dead nation of Vessar had tried to drown — slid across the hold and slammed into the port rail.

The boat heeled. Hard.

Tamsin felt it in her gut before her instruments confirmed it: the list, the rail going under, green water pouring across the deck. If the chest went over the side, the boat would roll with it, or the freed weight would swing them around and put them beam-on to the current, and either way the gorge would finish the argument in seconds.

She didn't think. That was the honest truth of it, the thing she would turn over for years afterward: there was no decision, no brave moment, nothing she could be proud of in the way people mean it. There was just her body moving, out of the wheelhouse, across the tilting deck, hands closing on the chest's iron handle as the rail went under her knees.

The cold hit her like a wall. The current took her boots, her hips, tried to peel her off the deck like a sticker. She got one foot braced against the rail stanchion and held on, and the river pulled, and pulled, and pulled.

"TAMSIN!"

She didn't see Ulyana come. She felt it — arms locking around her chest from behind, a body anchoring hers, boots scraping deck. Ulyana was smaller than her, lighter, and it should not have worked. It worked because Ulyana wrapped the dock line around both of them and cleated it, and because terror does what training cannot.

"Let it GO!" Ulyana screamed in her ear.

"NO!"

"IT'S NOT WORTH—"

"IT'S NOT ABOUT WORTH!" Tamsin heard herself shouting, mouth full of river, hands full of iron. "IT'S ABOUT WHO GETS TO SAY WHAT HAPPENED!"

And then the wave passed, the way waves do, and the boat righted with a groan, and the two of them collapsed on the deck in a tangle of rope and rain, the chest between them, lashed down this time with the dock line and every knot Tamsin's grandmother had ever taught her.

They lay there while the gorge carried them on.

* * *

The river gentled a mile below the Narrows, the way it always did after it had made its point. Tamsin got the pump running steady, got the water down, got the boat pointed down the middle of the channel where the current ran smoothest. Ulyana sat on the chest itself, arms wrapped around her knees, watching the water as if it might apologize.

"You could have died," Ulyana said finally. "For a box of paper."

"You could have died pulling me back." Tamsin kept her eyes on the channel. "We're even."

"We're not even. I did the sensible thing. You did the insane thing."

"I did the *necessary* thing." Tamsin turned then, and found Ulyana looking at her — really looking, the way she looked at a line on a map when she suspected it was lying. "You want to know why I wouldn't let go? My grandmother's name is in that box. Maybe. Probably. And every day that box stays at the bottom of some archive or some lawyer's vault or Renner's fireplace, there's a version of her story that gets told by the people who drowned her. I've spent my whole life on this river knowing that the water keeps what it's given. I'm not giving it back."

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. Rain drummed the wheelhouse roof. Somewhere behind them, the gorge roared on without them, indifferent.

"My grandfather's name is in there too," she said. "And I've spent five years hoping the water would keep that."

"I know."

"I know you know. That's why—" She stopped. Started again. "When I pulled you back. I wasn't thinking about the chest. I want you to understand that. I wasn't weighing it. I just — couldn't let the river have you."

Tamsin looked at her. The rain, the grey light, the exhausted set of the other woman's shoulders — and underneath it, something that hadn't been there five days ago on the dock at Marlow Bend. Something that looked, if Tamsin was honest, like the same thing she was feeling and refusing to name.

"Well," Tamsin said. "Crew does that."

"Crew," Ulyana repeated, testing the word.

"That's what we are now. You bailed my bilge, I saved your chest, you saved my life. That's crew. It's not a thing you apply for. It's a thing you survive into."

Ulyana almost smiled. It was a small thing, worn at the edges, but it was real. "I've never been crew before. I've been cargo. I've been management. Never crew."

"Get used to it." Tamsin turned back to the wheel. The channel ahead ran wide and brown and, for the moment, mercifully calm. "First lesson: crew doesn't let crew sleep. You've got the next watch on the pump. I've got the wheel. And when we make the lower delta—"

She stopped.

Ahead, where the channel bent toward the old ferry crossing, a boat sat anchored midstream. Grey hull, twin outboards, a man standing in the bow with binoculars raised — not searching the water, but waiting, patient as a heron, pointed directly at them.

Tamsin's stomach dropped through the bilge and into the river.

"That's not a fishing boat," Ulyana said quietly.

"No," Tamsin said, and reached for the throttle. "It's not."
