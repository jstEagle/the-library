## 14. The Missing Page

The rain had followed them into the gorge and then, mercifully, given up. By the time Tamsin nosed the *Cormorant* into the lee of a rock shelf below an abandoned signal house, the storm had spent itself to a gray drizzle that hissed on the river like something whispering advice.

Ulyana built a fire in the old stove they'd hauled aboard two seasons ago — Tamsin's boat, Tamsin's stove, though it had been Ulyana who'd resealed the flue pipe with tar and wire — and set the ledger upright in front of it, fanned open like a bird drying its wings.

"You're going to ruin it," Tamsin said from the wheelhouse doorway. She had a mug in each hand and the particular way of standing she adopted when she wanted to disapprove of something without committing to it.

"Water ruins it faster." Ulyana turned a page with the edge of a steel rule, gentle as a surgeon. "Wet paper grows mold. Mold eats ink. I've seen survey archives lost to a single leaking roof." She didn't say: *I've seen my grandfather's handwriting survive sixty years and one flood, and I am not going to be the person who loses it now.* She didn't have to. Tamsin had gotten good at hearing the sentences Ulyana didn't finish.

Tamsin handed her a mug — chicory, burnt, the way the delta drank it — and settled onto the bench across the stove, knees apart, forearms on thighs. A pilot's posture. Ready for weather.

They had been drying documents for six hours when the binding gave.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing Ulyana would remember afterward, how undramatic it was. The spine of the ledger had swollen and split somewhere in the flood, and as she lifted the book to turn its far side toward the heat, the back board simply sighed away from the sewing threads, and a folded rectangle of paper — thinner stock, different watermarks, tucked flat against the boards where no casual eye would ever go — slid out onto the deck plates.

Neither of them moved for a moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the river ran high and brown and patient.

"That's not part of the ledger," Tamsin said quietly.

"No." Ulyana's hands were steady. She was proud of that, later. "The requisition order was loose. This was hidden." She picked it up. It was lighter than the ledger paper, cheaper, the kind of stock a stationmaster might use for a personal letter rather than an official record. It had been folded twice, and the creases were worn soft, as if it had been folded and unfolded many times before being hidden away for good.

She opened it.

* * *

The first page was a waybill. Ulyana knew waybills the way priests know psalms — she could read one upside down at arm's length in bad light. This one was handwritten, which was itself an offense against every regulation she'd ever loved, and it listed freight that was not freight.

*One (1) lot, mixed goods, 14 units, crated. Origin: Kessel Yard. Destination: end of line.*

Fourteen units. Crated. On a date eleven days after the last deportation train listed in the ledger had left Kessel Yard for the camps.

Ulyana read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves. Fourteen people. Crated like machinery, like contraband, because that is exactly what they were — contraband human beings, moved on a rail line that officially did not exist, by someone with access to rolling stock, scheduling authority, and the specific courage or madness required to falsify a manifest under a regime that shot men for less.

The second page was in her grandfather's hand.

She knew it instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall. The firm downstrokes, the engineer's habit of writing numbers twice to guard against ambiguity, the small annotation marks in the margins that she had spent five years studying in his surviving field notebooks — the ones the lawyer's letter had told her were hers now, sitting in a solicitor's office in a city she refused to visit. *R. Oyelaran, Asst. Surveyor, Vessar State Railways,* written in the confident script of a man in his thirties, younger than she was now.

*Line remains unrecorded per agreement. Bridge 4 will not carry loaded weight after spring thaw — advise removal of rails above km 31 immediately following final movement. No further documentation to be kept. What is done is done well and must remain undone on paper.*

Below that, a list. Fourteen names. Not freight designations — names, ages, a child marked with an asterisk and the note *sedated for passage, do not open crates at tunnel.*

Ulyana set the pages down on her knee because her hands had stopped being steady after all.

"What is it?" Tamsin asked. She had come around the stove without Ulyana noticing, and she was looking at the paper upside down, and Ulyana watched her lips move as she read the fourteen names, and saw the exact moment she found the one she was looking for. Her breath went in and stayed there.

"Adeyemi," Tamsin said. "Bintu Adeyemi. Age twenty-six." Her voice came out strange, flattened, like a radio signal from very far away. "That's my grandmother."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I hoped. When I saw the surname in the ledger, I hoped."

"You *hoped?*" Tamsin turned on her, and the grief in her face curdled fast into anger, because that was how Tamsin processed everything — grief first, anger second, tears never if she could help it. "You've been carrying this since the chest came out of the river and you *hoped?*"

"I didn't know what it meant yet. A name in a ledger of the deported could mean—" Ulyana stopped. Made herself say it plainly, because Tamsin deserved plain. "It could have meant your grandmother died in the camps, Tamsin. I wasn't going to hand you that on a wet boat in the rain on a guess."

"And now?"

"Now I'm handing you this instead." Ulyana held up the waybill. "Your grandmother didn't die in the camps. She was smuggled out on a rail line that was never supposed to exist, hidden in a crate, sedated in a tunnel, and delivered to the end of a line that was torn up afterward so no one could ever prove it happened." She heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated it and kept going. "By my grandfather."

* * *

Tamsin sat back down. Slowly, the way you sit when your legs have made an independent decision.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The stove ticked and shifted. Rain resumed outside, soft, indecisive, tapping the cabin roof like a visitor unsure of their welcome.

"My mother used to tell a story," Tamsin said finally. "Her mother told it to her. About the night the soldiers came to the quarter and Bintu ran, and nobody in the family ever knew what happened after. Officially she was deported. There was a record — we found it years ago, a transport list. My grandmother spent forty years telling everyone her sister-in-law died at Torvin camp, and she lit a candle for her every year on the anniversary of the transport." Tamsin laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "She lit candles for a living woman. For herself."

"She couldn't tell anyone," Ulyana said. "If she surfaced, the people who moved her would hang. And the family she left behind—" She gestured at the ledger, still fanning itself dry beside the stove, all those columns of the condemned. "Anyone connected to an escapee would have been punished for it. Collective penalty. It's in the occupation codes; I've read them. Staying dead was the only way to keep the rest of them alive."

"So she stayed dead." Tamsin's jaw worked. "And he buried it. Your grandfather. He tore up the line and burned the records and let her sister-in-law's candle burn for a ghost, and he never—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He never even told *her* family she lived. He just took it with him into the ground."

"He kept this." Ulyana touched the folded pages. "Hidden in the binding of the worst document he ever signed. He carried both. The requisition order that sent them east and the waybill that brought fourteen of them back west, in the same book, for sixty years." She looked at the fire. "I keep trying to make those two facts fit inside one man."

"Do they fit?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. I've been trying since the ledger opened and I don't know." Ulyana pulled the requisition order from her jacket — she had taken to carrying it against her body, which Tamsin had once called morbid and Ulyana had once called responsible, and neither of them had raised the subject again. She laid it on the bench between them, next to the waybill. Same signature. Same hand. Eleven days apart.

"There," she said. "That's the whole inheritance. One signature sends them. One signature saves some of them. And then he spends the rest of his life making sure no one ever learns about the second one — including me, including my father, including, apparently, himself. Because look at the note. *What is done is done well and must remain undone on paper.* He wrote about his own rescue operation like it was a defect in a bridge. Something to be closed out and never spoken of."

"Maybe it was easier that way."

"Maybe it was cowardice."

"Maybe it was both," Tamsin said. "Most things are both. You'd know that better than anyone, Ulyana. You've been running from this family for five years and you kept his maps. You burned through half a career surveying his closed lines. You say you hate him and you've been walking his footsteps since before you knew why."

Ulyana opened her mouth to argue and found nothing there. The stove popped. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered, the storm's rear guard still prowling the high country.

"I measured my life in miles," she said at last, "because miles don't lie to you. A kilometer is a kilometer whether you're proud of it or ashamed. Years are different. Years ask you what you did with them." She looked down at the two signatures lying side by side. "I thought if I could just get far enough down every dead line he ever drew, I'd understand him. Like distance was a kind of answer. And now I have the answer, and it's—" She shook her head. "It's not an answer. It's a man who was monstrous and decent in the same week, possibly the same hour, and I'm supposed to inherit *that*, and there's no unit for it. There's no measurement."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "There isn't." She reached out and took the waybill — took her grandmother's name off Ulyana's knee and into her own hands — and held it the way you hold something that might still be alive. "But you know what I keep coming back to? Fourteen crates. Fourteen people walked out of that quarter's death sentence because one surveyor with a timetable decided to spend his career, his name, maybe his neck on it. Whatever else he was, on that night, on that line, he was the reason I exist." She looked up, eyes bright and hard. "You don't get to hand me only the monster, Ulyana. And I don't get to hand you only the rescuer. We got both. That's the cargo."

* * *

They sat with it until the fire burned low and the drizzle thickened back into honest rain. Ulyana copied the fourteen names into her survey notebook, twice each, guarding against ambiguity, and caught herself doing it in her grandfather's style and did not stop. Some inheritances you choose. Some you simply perform until they fit.

It was near midnight, Tamsin asleep in the wheelhouse chair with the waybill zipped into her jacket over her heart, when Ulyana noticed the third thing.

The binding. She had set the split back board aside hours ago, meaning to re-glue it in the morning, and now, turning it over in the lamplight, she saw what the swelling water had revealed and her tired eyes had missed: a faint rectangular shadow in the paste where a second insert had once lain flush against the boards. Smaller than the waybill. Removed long ago — the paste had dried and cracked around an absence.

Someone had taken something else out of this binding, years before the river ever got hold of it. Someone who knew it was there.

She thought of the lawyer's letter. *Your grandfather's papers include certain items referenced but not present in the estate inventory.* She had assumed clerical error. Clerks made errors. But clerks did not know precisely which item to remove from a hidden compartment in a book sealed in a flooded warehouse sixty kilometers from anywhere.

Which meant Renner — or whoever Renner answered to — had known about the binding. Had known what it contained. Had gotten there first, decades ago, and had been waiting ever since for the rest of it to surface.

Ulyana looked out the rain-streaked window at the black river running high and fast toward the delta, and understood, with the cold clarity of a level bubble finding center, that they were not carrying evidence anymore.

They were carrying the half that someone powerful had missed.

She woke Tamsin at dawn. "Get us moving," she said. "Whatever's downstream, it's already looking for us."
