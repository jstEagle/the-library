## 15. Ambush at the Fork

* * *

**Renner**

Ivo Renner had learned long ago that patience was the most expensive thing a man could own, and therefore the surest sign of wealth.

He stood on the flying bridge of the *Kestrel*, one hand resting on the rail, watching the river fork open ahead of them like a pair of slack jaws. The monsoon's tail still dragged across the sky to the south — a gray curtain of rain that had followed them for two days, sometimes ahead, sometimes behind, like a debt collector who knew where you lived. His hired boat rode low in the water with six men and a mounted engine that drank fuel the way grief drank time.

"There." Vasel pointed. "Port channel."

The pilot boat came out of the rain exactly as Renner had known it would — because he had read the same charts they were reading, and the charts said there was only one sane choice. The western fork ran wide and deep all season, a brown boulevard lined with drowned willows. The eastern fork narrowed into a maze of sandbars and dead channels that the survey maps marked in the old imperial shorthand: *impassable in ordinary water; fatal in flood.*

They would take the west fork. Everyone took the west fork.

"Match their speed," Renner said. "Don't close yet."

Vasel glanced at him. The man had a face like a closed ledger, and Renner trusted him for precisely that reason. "You want them to see us?"

"I want them to *know*," Renner said. "There's a difference. Fear makes people run in straight lines."

He thought about the woman — Crane, Ulyana Crane, granddaughter of Emil Crane, whose signature sat on requisition order forty-one like a scar on the family's skin. He had studied her file the way he studied everything: thoroughly, without sentiment. A surveyor. A counter of miles. Five years of self-imposed exile measured out in decommissioned track. Such people believed the world was legible, that everything could be reduced to gradient and gauge and distance. That was her weakness, and it was also, he admitted privately, a kind of strength he did not possess and had never wanted to possess.

And the pilot. Adeyemi. He'd pulled her record from the harbor authority before dawn three days ago: licensed, competent, twice cited for running unregistered cargo, once cited for running *through* a customs cordon during high water. The citation's language had been almost admiring.

Two women in a leaking riverboat carrying seventy years of buried shame down a flooded river. It should have been comic. Instead it felt like watching a slow leak in the hull of his own life.

His grandfather had taught him that names were assets. That memory was a liability to be managed, written down, depreciated, eventually written off. The interior minister had signed papers in a warm office while trains rolled east, and then he had spent forty years building hospitals and endowing scholarships until the name Vessar Renner meant *philanthropy*, meant *civic duty*, meant the bronze plaque outside the orphanage in Kessel with his grandfather's profile cast in it, looking benevolent and slightly bored.

Renner had kissed that bronze cheek every Sunday of his childhood.

The chest could not surface. Not because of guilt — guilt was for people with time to waste — but because of what unmanaged memory did. It compounded. Like debt. Like floodwater.

"They're slowing," Vasel said.

Ahead, the pilot boat had swung its nose toward the western fork, and Renner allowed himself the smallest exhale. Straight lines. He watched the little vessel commit itself, watched the willows begin to slide past its gunwale, and raised one finger.

"Now. Close the distance. Take the mouth of the fork and hold it."

The *Kestrel*'s engines deepened. Rain began again, soft as static, beading on the brass rail under his hand.

He did not hate them. That was what the mercenaries never understood when they looked at him sideways, waiting for cruelty and finding bookkeeping instead. You did not hate a fire you were putting out. You simply put it out, thoroughly, and then you went home and slept well, because the estate was safe, and the estate was the only thing that outlived anyone.

The fork swallowed the pilot boat. The *Kestrel* turned after it, bow wave folding white against the current, and Ivo Renner checked his watch, because even triumph deserved punctuality.

**Tamsin**

Tamsin felt them before she saw them — a change in the engine note behind them, deeper than any fishing boat, too steady for driftwood traffic. She'd been listening to that river for eleven years the way other people listened to music, and she knew every voice in the choir.

"We've got company," she said. "Big diesel, coming up fast through the west fork mouth."

Ulyana appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, hair plastered flat by rain, the chest's oilcloth wrapped around her shoulders like a shawl she refused to take off. "How fast?"

"Faster than us. Faster than God, probably." Tamsin eased back on the throttle, feeling the hull answer beneath her feet — that soft, wrong flex in the plating aft that had been getting worse since the shoal. "Question is whether they just want to pass or want to pin us."

The answer came thirty seconds later, when the big boat shouldered out of the rain behind them, close enough now that Tamsin could count silhouettes on its flying bridge. One of them stood very still at the rail, hands folded, not gripping anything. She knew that posture. Men who gripped rails were scared. Men who folded their hands were pricing you.

"That's him," Ulyana said quietly. "Renner."

"You've met?"

"He bought me dinner once and threatened me with concern."

Tamsin snorted despite herself, eyes on the water. The west fork ran wide here, maybe two hundred meters bank to bank, deep and honest, the kind of channel you could steer with your elbows. But two hundred meters wasn't enough when the other boat had triple your horsepower and nothing to lose. Ahead, the channel bent left around a drowned willow island, and beyond the bend she could see — in the flashes between rain — the white water of the shallows where the fork braided out into sandbars. A trap with scenery.

They'd run her down in the bend. Push her into the bars, come alongside in the confusion, and take the chest off her deck while she swam. Clean. Professional. The kind of plan drawn up by men who read rivers off paper.

"Tamsin." Ulyana's voice was very level. "What are we doing?"

Tamsin looked at the eastern fork.

It opened off to starboard like an afterthought — narrower, darker, choked with the skeletons of trees the floods had taken years ago. The chart called it the Serev Cut, and the chart hated it. *Impassable in ordinary water,* the old surveyors had inked across it in that confident imperial hand. *Fatal in flood.* She'd grown up on her father's knee hearing about the Serev Cut the way other children heard about monsters: the channel that moved every season, that ate a barge in '61 with nine souls aboard, that no pilot insured.

But her father hadn't just told her the stories. He'd made her learn them. Every seasonal channel in this delta lived in her head the way train lines lived in Ulyana's — mapped in memory, updated by every flood year, cross-checked against the way willows bent and the color of the water and the smell the mud gave off when the river was cutting a new path. And she had run the Serev Cut twice. Once in '09, young and stupid, and once last spring, older and still stupid, both times at medium water, both times alive.

At flood stage, everything she knew about it was wrong. But everything the chart knew about it was wronger.

"The map says that's suicide," Ulyana said, following her gaze.

"The map also says my grandmother's family never existed, so forgive me if I've got trust issues with paper." Tamsin spun the wheel. "Hold onto something. Both hands. If I yell *down*, you get flat on the deck and you don't ask why."

She drove the boat at the cut.

For three heartbeats nothing happened except rain and the roar of the pursuing engine, and then the world narrowed. The willows closed overhead, dripping. The channel walls rose on either side — mud banks four meters high, sculpted by the flood into flowing shapes like melted wax, water pouring off them in a hundred small waterfalls. The current grabbed the hull and shoved, hard, and Tamsin let it shove, riding it the way you'd ride a horse that had decided it knew better than you.

"Depth sounder's lying," she muttered. "It always lies in silt water. Watch the surface instead — see that boil? That's a bar. We go left of the boil. Always left."

Ulyana had braced herself against the chart table, eyes wide, tracking everything. "That's not on any survey."

"No. Surveys come every ten years. Rivers come every day." Behind them, the big boat's engine note changed — hesitation, then commitment, the sound of a man deciding to follow. Tamsin grinned, and it wasn't a nice grin. "He's coming. Good. This part only works if he comes."

The first bend nearly killed them. The channel kinked right around a half-submerged cottonwood trunk, and the current wanted them to go straight, which meant into the trunk. Tamsin threw the wheel over, felt the stern swing wide, heard Ulyana's breath catch, and then they were through, scraping something along the keel that shrieked briefly and let go.

"Log," she said. "We're fine."

"Are we?" Ulyana asked, looking aft.

Tamsin looked. Water was coming in over the transom corner — not much, a thin green tongue of it, sliding across the deck plates toward the drain. More than there'd been an hour ago. The holed plating from the shoal had found friends.

"Pump," she said, and Ulyana was already moving, already knowing where it was, and Tamsin thought, absurdly, in the middle of everything: *crew.* When had that happened?

Behind them the big boat entered the cut.

She heard it before she saw it — the different echo, wider, heavier, a big hull in a small throat. Then it appeared in the rain behind them, sixty meters back and closing, its bow wave slapping the mud banks, its flying bridge scraping willow branches that whipped and snapped like whips. Six men on deck now, one of them hauling on a line, preparing for the boarding they assumed was coming.

Tamsin took them past the second boil, past the drowned fence line that only she knew marked the old ferry crossing, past the leaning chimney of a house the river had eaten decades ago — landmarks strung like beads on a thread no chart had ever held. And then she came to the place she'd been steering toward since the fork mouth, the place her father had named the Wishing Teeth: a field of submerged pilings from some structure older than memory, invisible at flood stage except for one sign. Where the current crossed the buried pilings, the water stood up in a long, low ridge, smooth as glass, like a seam in the river's fabric.

Only visible if you knew to look. Only survivable if you went over it exactly square, at exactly the right speed, letting the hull slap the water between piling tips.

Or — and this was the part she'd been saving — only fatal if you hit it at speed, at an angle, in a heavy boat with a deep draft and a man at the helm who thought rivers were roads.

"Hold on," she said. "This is going to feel wrong in every possible way."

She cut the throttle to idle, swung the boat hard left into an eddy behind the last willow island, and stopped.

The effect was immediate and beautiful. The pursuing boat, committed to the channel at full power, suddenly had nothing in front of it but open water and a target that had vanished. She watched the helmsman — she could see him now, a thick man in a yellow slicker — realize, watch him throw his wheel, watch the big hull begin a graceless, panicked turn in a channel barely wider than its own length.

"Come on," Tamsin whispered. "A little more. A little more angle. There you are."

The big boat slewed. Its bow dug into the current seam. And the Wishing Teeth took it.

The sound carried even through the rain — a long tearing crunch, wood and steel meeting something immovable, the whole forward section lurching upward as if the river had decided to inspect its purchase. The mast went over. Men scattered across the deck like dropped tools. The hull swung broadside, hung there for one impossible moment, and then the flood current took it the way the flood current took everything, sweeping it down onto the pilings again, and again, each impact softer than the last, until it was grinding slowly against the far bank, listing, taking water, done.

Silence, except rain. Except the pump aft, chugging. Except Ulyana's breathing.

Tamsin realized she was laughing — a shaky, unhinged sound she didn't recognize as her own. "Eleven years," she said. "Eleven years everyone told me the Serev Cut was a graveyard. Best investment I ever made, learning a graveyard by heart."

Ulyana wasn't laughing. She was standing at the wheelhouse window, watching the wreck settle against the far bank, and her face had gone strange.

"What?" Tamsin said. "We won. Look at it. We *won*."

"I know." Ulyana turned, and in the gray light her eyes were bright and exhausted and something else underneath. "I'm counting, Tamsin. That's what I do. I count." She held up fingers. "One: we're taking water faster than that pump can push it out. Two: the radio died when we scraped the log — look at it, it's dead, no signal, no mayday, nobody knows where we are. Three: the storm's tail is still north of us and the lower river closes in less than a day. Four—" she paused, and her voice dropped, "—four, we just wrecked a boat full of men who work for a man who has never once, in everything you've told me about him, needed to win twice."

Tamsin's laugh dried up.

Downstream, faint through the rain, she could hear shouting from the wreck — voices, alive, angry, one of them rising above the others in a tone she recognized from harbors and courtrooms and every place where men discussed money. Someone on that boat had a working radio. Someone on that boat was already calling ahead.

"So," Tamsin said slowly, "we're triumphant, sinking, deaf, and hunted."

"That's about the shape of it."

Tamsin looked at the channel ahead — the Serev Cut running on into the rain, narrow and dark and hers, every bead on the thread lit in her memory. Then she looked aft at the thin tongue of green water sliding across the deck plates, patient as arithmetic.

"Then we'd better find somewhere to beach this boat," she said, "before the river decides it wants the chest more than Renner does."

She pushed the throttle forward, and the cut received them, and the rain came down harder, as if the sky had opinions about what happened next.
