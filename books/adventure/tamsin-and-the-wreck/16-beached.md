## 16. Beached

The river gave them up at dawn, spitting the boat onto a shoal of grey silt like something it had chewed and found wanting.

Tamsin felt the grounding before she heard it — a long shudder through the hull, a grinding that started in her boots and climbed into her teeth. She was already moving, cutting the engine, throwing her weight against the wheel to swing the bow off the sandbar, but the *Marianne* had no more fight in her. The propeller bit mud. The current leaned on the stern, swung them broadside, and settled them there with a finality that felt almost gentle.

Then she heard the water coming in.

Not a trickle. A sound like a slow breath drawn through a wound — somewhere below the waterline, forward of the engine bed, the hull had opened along a seam or split a plank outright, and the river was keeping its appointment with them.

"Ulyana." Her voice came out flat, pilot-calm, the voice she used for other people's emergencies. "Bilge pump. Now."

Ulyana appeared from under the tarp where she'd been sleeping, hair plastered to her face, eyes going immediately — Tamsin noted this even through her own fear — to the waterline, reading the boat the way she read rail grades. "How bad?"

"You tell me. You're the one who likes measuring things."

The pump coughed, caught, coughed again. Tamsin dropped into the bilge space with a flashlight clamped between her teeth and found it: a split along the garboard seam, starboard side, maybe forty centimeters of opened wood where the fork-channel ambush had flexed the hull past its limits. The caulking was gone. Through the gap she could see the brown flash of the river itself, moving, alive, patient.

She worked for an hour. Mallet and oakum from the repair kit, wedging fiber into the seam while Ulyana bailed with a bucket because the pump couldn't keep pace alone. The rain came and went in ragged curtains, the tail of the monsoon still thrashing around out there in the hills, looking for something else to ruin. By midmorning they had the inflow down to a seep the pump could manage, and Tamsin sat back on her heels in three inches of grey water and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold iron, exactly where they were.

A shoal, mid-river, exposed by the flood's first retreat. No village within poling distance. Radio dead since the fork — she'd checked four times, hoping against sense. The *Marianne* would float again only if the river rose enough to lift her off, and if it rose that much it would rise over everything else too. They were beached on a bar of silt the size of a tennis court, with a dying boat, a chest full of dead men's secrets, and whatever Renner had left of his hired crew somewhere behind them on the water.

Ulyana climbed aboard, wrung out her sleeves, and looked at the chest.

It sat amidships where they'd lashed it, oilcloth dark with spray, the broken lock hanging open like a slack jaw. Water had gotten at the ledgers despite everything; they'd spent two nights drying pages by the stove, turning them like a mother turning a feverish child. The missing page — the rescues, the smuggled survivors, Ulyana's grandfather's second life — rode inside now, wrapped separately, because Ulyana had taken to sleeping with it under her jacket like a talisman.

"We can't run it," Tamsin said. "Not until the river lifts us off. And when it does—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "When it does, we're a slow, loud target with a hole in her belly. He'll have boats. He has money. We got lucky once at the fork. Luck doesn't repeat."

Ulyana said nothing. She was looking at the chest the way she'd looked at it the first night, back at the survey camp, when it had been a curiosity. It wasn't a curiosity anymore. Tamsin could see the weight of it in the set of her shoulders.

"There's another option," Tamsin said.

"Don't."

"It floats. It doesn't burn worth a damn wet, but there's stove fuel, there's lamp oil—"

"I said don't." Ulyana turned, and her face had gone the color of the silt around them. "You want to talk about this?"

"I want us alive."

"The ledgers are the only copy in the world, Tamsin. Every name. Every train. If it burns, it never happened. That's what he wants. That's what his whole family built — a country that never happened."

"And what good is a ledger to drowned women?" Tamsin heard her own voice crack and hated it. "You want to hand names to the descendants? Fine. Beautiful. But we are sitting on a sandbar with a holed boat and a man with mercenaries upstream of us, and I am telling you as the person whose job is knowing what water does — this river will take that chest, and it will take us, and it will not care which order."

The rain picked at the tarp. Somewhere downstream a fish jumped, a small silver slap of indifference.

Ulyana crouched by the chest. Didn't touch it. "My grandfather signed those requisitions," she said quietly. "He put families on trains. And then he pulled some of them off, in secret, and never told anyone, and let the world believe what the world believed. Do you know what that means? It means the truth isn't one thing. It's a whole cargo hold of things, and you don't get to pick which ones survive. Burning it — that's him deciding again. That's a Crane making history convenient. I won't. I'd rather drown honest."

"That's easy to say from inside your own story." Tamsin stood, water sloshing around her ankles. "You know what my story is? You've never asked."

Ulyana looked up.

"I told you my grandmother was on the lists. I didn't tell you how I found that out." Tamsin's mouth was dry. She made herself go on, because the boat was sinking either way, and a confession weighed less than a lie. "I sold information. Years ago. When I was running lighters out of Port Sable with nothing but debts and a sick mother. There were people — collectors, archivists, call them what you want — who paid for fragments. Old documents, old names, anything from the Vessar years. I found a page in a salvage lot once. A manifest. I copied it and sold the copy and kept the original hidden so I could sell it twice."

Ulyana went very still.

"To whom?" she asked. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — careful.

"I don't know. A broker. The broker had a client. I told myself it didn't matter, that paper is paper, that I needed the money more than history needed the truth." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And then two years later I started hearing about a man from the consulate buying up Vessar material. Quiet purchases. Archival purposes. And I did the arithmetic, Ulyana. I did it late at night, over and over, and every time it came out the same: I might be the reason he knew what existed. I might be the reason he started hunting. My grandmother's name might have been safe in the dark until I carried it into the light and sold it to the wolf."

The silence after that was enormous. The river filled it, muttering against the hull.

"So when I say burn it," Tamsin said, "hear what I'm actually saying. I'm not afraid of Renner. I'm afraid of me. I've been hungry enough to trade other people's dead before, and I survived it, and surviving it taught me I could do it again. Put me desperate enough — a sinking boat, a gunboat behind us — and some part of me starts calculating what a chest like that would fetch. I can feel it doing the arithmetic right now. That's why it should burn. Not because the truth doesn't matter. Because I can't promise you I'm the person to carry it."

Ulyana rose slowly. For a long moment she just looked at her — and Tamsin braced for the recoil, the withdrawal, the polite distance of a woman recalculating her alliances.

Instead Ulyana said: "You could have lied."

"What?"

"You could have let me think you wanted to burn it for safety. Clean reasons. You handed me the ugly version instead." She shook her head slowly. "You've been waiting days to tell me that, haven't you? Since the port bars. Maybe since before."

"Since you asked dangerous questions in a room full of strangers," Tamsin admitted. "I thought: here's another one who'll use me. Then you pulled me out of the gorge instead of saving the chest first." She gestured at the flooding bilge, the ruined radio, all of it. "People show you who they are when things sink."

"So do I." Ulyana knelt, opened the chest, and lifted out the separate packet — the missing page, dry, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth. She held it out. "Take it."

Tamsin didn't move. "What is this?"

"Your grandmother's family. Whatever it says. That was always the price of passage, and I never paid it because I kept hoping you'd forget." Ulyana's voice was steady, but her hands weren't quite. "You just told me the worst thing about yourself on a sinking boat, and then refused to take the one thing you actually want. So take it. Read it. If you're the woman you're afraid of, you'll sell it. If you're the woman I think you are, you'll keep it — and carry the rest of it with me anyway."

Tamsin took the packet. Her fingers didn't want to work right. She unwrapped it there in the sloshing bilge, kneeling in the grey water of a dying boat, and unfolded the page.

The handwriting was cramped, hurried, a clerk writing fast and ashamed of it. Names in columns. Families. Dates. She found the surname halfway down — *Adeyemi* — and beside it, in the margin, a single notation in different ink, older, pressed hard:

*Held at waystation. Line closed. Released south, papers falsified.*

Her grandmother hadn't escaped. Someone had released her. Someone had falsified papers and looked away and buried the evidence, and that someone had been a Crane, and that was why Tamsin was alive to sit in this bilge and be offered the truth by a Crane's granddaughter.

She didn't realize she was crying until the tears hit the paper and she snatched it away, terrified of the water, of all water, of what water took and what it kept.

"Ulyana." Her voice was wrecked. "I can't burn it now. You knew that. You knew that before you handed it to me."

"I hoped."

"You *knew.*"

"I measured," Ulyana said, and there was the ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's what I do."

Tamsin folded the page with hands that shook, wrapped it, and pushed it back across the boards toward the chest — then stopped, and instead tucked it inside her own jacket, against her skin. "This one stays with me. The rest stays with you. That's the deal now. Neither of us carries it alone."

"Agreed."

They worked the rest of the day in a silence that was no longer provisional. Caulking, bailing, rigging a jury brace over the split seam, salvaging what the fork-wreckage had left them. Toward evening the light broke gold through the tearing clouds, and Tamsin climbed to the roof of the cabin to scan the river both ways, and that was when she saw it.

Downstream, perhaps two kilometers, where the channel bent around a drowned forest — smoke. Thin, domestic, deliberate. Cooking smoke. And on the water below it, riding at anchor in the last light, the silhouette of a boat with a cabin too fine for fishermen.

Not behind them anymore. Ahead.

She slid down into the bilge, found Ulyana's shoulder in the dark, and squeezed once.

"They're not chasing us," Tamsin whispered. "They're waiting. And come morning, the river rises — and there's only one channel past that bend."

Above them the first stars came out over the water, indifferent as ever, and the *Marianne* breathed her slow leak beneath their feet, counting down.
