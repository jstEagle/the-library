## 18. What Justice Owes

*Ulyana*

The storm's tail dragged itself north through the night, and by morning the river had begun — only begun — to think about falling. The water still stood in the streets of the village, brown and patient, lapping at the doorsteps of houses on stilts. Children poled washtubs between the buildings like it was a festival. Somewhere a rooster crowed from the roof of a flooded shed, indignant and unbothered.

Ulyana stood in the doorway of the village hall with a tin cup of tea she wasn't drinking, watching the elders arrive.

They came by canoe, one after another, old men and old women in oilskins gone soft as cloth, and they came without being summoned. Word had moved through the village overnight the way word moves in small places — not spoken so much as *known*, passed along some frequency Ulyana couldn't hear. The chest had been carried into the hall the evening before and set on the long table beneath the lamp, and now everyone who had ever been told a story about a train that went into the water country and never came back was coming to look at it.

Tamsin appeared beside her, hair still damp, a bandage on her forearm where the gorge had taken its tax.

"They're coming to read it," Tamsin said. "Not to decide anything. To read it."

"Can you blame them?"

"No." Tamsin turned the cup in her own hands. "I'm just saying you should know what you're watching. This isn't a trial. It's a funeral that got postponed seventy years."

Inside, the hall smelled of woodsmoke and wet rope. The chest sat open under the lamp, the ledgers laid out in careful rows, each page pressed flat between panes of glass borrowed from windows whose frames had rotted away. Elder Sefa — the woman who had met their boat two days ago, who had looked at the Vessar seal and not blinked — stood at the head of the table like a conductor before an orchestra she had waited her whole life to hear.

The reading began at dawn and did not stop.

It was not dramatic. That was what undid Ulyana. She had expected weeping, or fury, or the charged silence of a courtroom. Instead there was only voice after voice taking up the ledger in turn, reading names in the flat, unhurried cadence of people reciting something they already half-knew. *Vessik, Anna. Vessik, Tomas. Vessik, the child Ilya.* And after each name, sometimes, a murmur from the benches: *the miller's family. The ones from upriver. My grandmother's cousin married a Vessik.* The dead were being sorted back into the living world, name by name, matched to faces and farms and family trees that had kept a space open for them for three generations.

Ulyana listened for hours. She did not touch the ledger. It was not hers to touch anymore; she understood that now, had understood it since the moment the elders lifted it from the chest with hands washed clean for the purpose.

But by late afternoon, when the light through the high windows went amber and the reading paused for rice and dried fish, the question arrived anyway. It arrived the way questions do in rooms full of people who have waited decades — not as a whisper but as a weight.

An old man named Doran, his forearms roped with net-scarred muscle, set down his bowl and said, "So. Now that it is read. What happens to it?"

The hall went quiet in a different way.

"To the courts," someone said from the back. "There are lawyers in the capital who would take this. There is a commission for war crimes — it still exists, it meets twice a year."

"The commission that met twice a year while Renner's family built a shipping empire on the freight contracts?" That was a younger woman, sharp-voiced, a schoolteacher by the chalk dust still ghosting her sleeves. "We give them paper and they give us a filing number. In ten years there's a plaque. Plaques don't feed anyone."

"Then the press," Doran said. "Photographs. Every name in every newspaper from here to the coast."

"And Renner buys three of the four papers inside a month, and the fourth gets sued into silence." The schoolteacher again. "You've seen how it works."

"Then what? We keep it under the floorboards and tell our grandchildren?"

"We return it," said a voice near the front, so quiet that everyone had to lean toward it. An ancient woman, nearly blind, her eyes gone the pale blue of river ice. Ulyana realized she had been listening all day without speaking. "Name by name, house by house. Let each family hold the page that holds them. Let the copies be many, so no one hand can gather them all and burn them. Let there be no single chest for any man to steal."

"That's not justice," the schoolteacher said. "That's just scattering."

"It is *planting*," the old woman said. "Justice that sits in one place can be dug up. Justice that is everywhere—" she made a small motion with her hand, like sowing, "—cannot be."

Ulyana felt Tamsin shift beside her, and knew without looking that Tamsin was thinking of her grandmother's name, of the page she had bargained for in a leaking boat, of what it meant to hold your own history in your own two hands instead of receiving it secondhand from an archive, a court, a state that had never once come looking.

Elder Sefa raised her hand and the room stilled. "No decision tonight," she said. "The water is still high and the man from the consulate is still out there. Tomorrow we decide together. Tonight we finish the names."

And they did. They read until the lamp burned low, and Ulyana stayed until the last name, and walked out into the blue dark with the whole ledger living behind her eyelids.

* * *

*Tamsin*

Tamsin didn't sleep. She sat on the hall's veranda with her grandmother's page unfolded on her knee, reading it by moonlight for the hundredth time, and that was how she saw the boats.

Three of them, running dark, no lamps, coming down the flooded main channel with the easy confidence of people who knew the water. Nobody ran the flood channel at night without lamps unless they didn't care who saw them or very much wanted not to be seen. She counted silhouettes. Eight men, maybe nine. One of them stood at the bow of the lead boat with the stillness of a man who had paid to stand there rather than paddle.

She was moving before she finished deciding to move. Down the ladder, across the submerged walkway, up to the room where Ulyana slept on a borrowed cot.

"Ulyana." She shook her shoulder once, hard. "Boats. Three. Dark-running. Coming up the village channel."

Ulyana was awake instantly — five years of survey camps had given her that, the ability to go from sleep to decision with nothing wasted between. "Renner?"

"Who else runs dark on a night like this?"

They woke Sefa, and Sefa woke the village, and the village did what villages that have survived floods and worse have always done: it moved quietly and fast. Lamps went out. Canoes were drawn up under houses. The chest — because of course the chest was still here, because the decision hadn't been made yet, because decisions about justice apparently required a quorum while mercenaries required only fuel — was lowered into a dugout and covered with fishing nets and moved into the reeds behind the drying racks.

Tamsin took the pole position in the lead canoe with Ulyana beside her and watched the three boats slide into the village basin.

Lamps bloomed on the lead boat then, deliberately, a show of openness. And standing in that light, dry-suited, immaculate, holding a lantern as if he were arriving at a garden party instead of a drowned village at midnight, was Ivo Renner.

He called out across the water, and his voice was exactly as courteous as it had been in the port bar a lifetime ago.

"People of the village! Forgive the hour. I've come to make amends." A pause, timed like a man who rehearsed. "I know why my grandfather's name is in your stories. I've come to offer restitution."

Nobody answered. The water slapped against hulls. Somewhere a dog decided against barking.

"I'll say it plainly, then." Renner lifted a case onto the gunwale — a hard case, the kind that carried instruments or money. "For the safe return of certain historical documents misplaced during the flood season, I am prepared to fund this village for a generation. New pilings for every house. A school. A clinic with a real doctor. Dikes that will hold when the next monsoon comes, because it will come." He let the silence work. "Or," he said, softer, and the softness carried further than the promises, "you can keep the papers, and I will be forced to conclude that you are thieves trading in stolen property, and I will involve the authorities, and the authorities are less generous than I am."

* * *

*Ulyana*

Ulyana lay in the reeds with the rain-fresh smell of the nets around her and listened to Renner buy a village, and understood him completely for the first time.

That was the horror of it. Not the mercenaries, not the false warrant, not even the threat. The horror was that his offer was *good*. The school would get built. The doctor would come. The dikes would hold. He would pay, and pay generously, because that was what estate management looked like when the estate included a sin: you bought the debt down, you serviced it annually, you never let it come due all at once in public. He wasn't offering to bury the past. He was offering to *endow* it.

And the village could take the money and keep the copies, and he would litigate them into the ground for twenty years, and the children in the new school would learn a version of events with footnotes, and the old woman with the ice-blue eyes would die knowing the truth had been filed rather than freed.

Ulyana thought about the courts. She thought about the press. She thought about archives with climate control and commissions that met twice a year, and she saw the chest in every one of them: catalogued, sealed, restricted, lost. Evidence became evidence by disappearing into institutions. That was what institutions were *for* — she knew this better than most, daughter of a railway family, granddaughter of a man whose worst act and best act both lived in the same closed file. Anything given to an authority could be repossessed by one. Renner's grandfather had understood that. Renner understood it. The whole machinery of the last century ran on the principle that paper held by power belongs to power.

Which left only the thing the blind old woman had said with her hand moving like sowing.

Deliver it by hand. Name by name. House by house. Make it so numerous and so personal that no institution could gather it back, and no heir could buy it, and no flood could take all of it at once. Justice that couldn't be subpoenaed because it lived in kitchens, in Bibles, in the bottom drawers of fishermen's desks, in the mouths of grandmothers who would read the names aloud whether or not anyone with a law degree approved.

It wasn't clean. It wouldn't convict anyone. Renner would probably die rich. But the drowned would belong to their own people again, and the record would be *everywhere*, and somewhere — Ulyana was sure of this the way she was sure of gauge widths and load tolerances — somewhere a historian fifty years out would find one of those pages in a farmhouse and follow the thread, and the thread would not break, because there would be thousands of threads.

The lantern light swept the reeds. Ulyana pressed herself flat. Through the netting she watched Renner's boat drift closer to the drying racks, close enough that she could see his face, and what she saw there frightened her more than the guns: patience. He wasn't searching. He was *waiting*, the way he'd waited at Kessel Locks, a man content to let time do his work, because time always did, because everything valuable eventually surfaced and could be acquired.

Except, she thought, things that had been given away. Things that had stopped being property at all.

Sefa's voice came across the water then, level and unhurried, an elder answering a child. "The village thanks you for your offer, Mr. Renner. The village will consider it."

"Consider it quickly," Renner said pleasantly. "The water is falling. Boats that wait too long run aground."

"So they do," Sefa said. "Good night to you."

The lamps withdrew. The three boats backed out of the basin and turned downstream toward the wreck of the channel fork where Tamsin had broken their hired pursuer, and Tamsin's hand found Ulyana's arm in the dark and gripped it — not fear, message: *he'll be back at first light with less courtesy.*

They paddled back in through the reeds as the sky went grey. In the hall, the elders were already gathering, woken and grim, and the blind old woman sat at the head of the table with her hand resting on the glass panes over the ledger pages as if she could read them through her fingertips.

"You've decided something," Sefa said, looking at Ulyana. "I can see it in your face. You came in carrying a chest and you're standing there carrying something else."

Ulyana looked around the room — at the schoolteacher, at old Doran, at the families who had spent the day reclaiming their dead — and heard herself speak with a certainty she hadn't planned.

"Don't give it to anyone. Not the courts, not the press, not me. Copy every page tonight. Then tomorrow, when the water drops enough to pole the side channels, we start delivering them — every name to every descendant, hand to hand, house to house. All of us. And when Renner comes back—"

Outside, downriver, a flare went up: white, harsh, hanging over the black water like a accusation. Then a second. Then the sound of engines, more than three boats' worth, coming up the falling river with the storm's last wind behind them.

"—when he comes back," Ulyana said, "we'd better already be moving."
