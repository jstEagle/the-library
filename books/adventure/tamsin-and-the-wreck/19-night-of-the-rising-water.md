## 19. Night of the Rising Water

### ULYANA

The river came for the village at dusk, and it did not knock.

Ulyana heard it before she saw it — a low, grinding roar upstream, like a freight train that had abandoned its rails. The elders had been watching the water all day, reading it the way she read track gauges, and when Mama Sefu stepped out of her doorway and said only, *"Now,"* the whole village moved as one body. Canoes were dragged to high ground. Chickens were stuffed into baskets. Children were counted by name, twice.

By full dark the lower street was gone. Not flooded — *gone*, swallowed whole, so that the river lay where the market had been, flat and black and moving with terrible purpose. Oil lamps floated in clay bowls, set adrift by the old women as offerings, and their small flames rode the current like souls looking for their houses.

Ulyana stood on the raised platform of the village hall with the chest between her feet and felt the boards tremble under her boots. Two days of readings had changed something in her chest cavity; she could no longer pick up the ledger without hearing the names in other voices, the elders' voices, the children's voices reciting ancestors like multiplication tables. It was not evidence anymore. It was a congregation.

That was why, when the first shot cracked across the water, her whole body went cold with a grief that had nothing to do with fear.

Three boats, running dark, engines muffled. They came around the drowned bend of the main street where the flood had erased every landmark except the rooftops, and they came fast.

"Tamsin!" she shouted into the rain.

"Already moving!" Tamsin's voice came from below, from the canoe lashed under the hall's platform, already half in the black water. "You get his eyes on you — I don't care how — and I'm gone."

The plan had been made in an hour of desperate arithmetic: Renner wanted the chest, wanted it badly enough to bring guns into a drowning village, which meant he would follow whatever he believed the chest followed. Tamsin would take it through the submerged streets, where no outsider could read the water. Ulyana would be the decoy — visible, loud, carrying a box-shaped bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

She hefted the decoy. It weighed nothing. That was the whole point.

"Ulyana." Tamsin caught her wrist at the ladder's foot. Rain ran down both their faces and neither wiped it away. "Don't die clever. Die never. That's the assignment."

"I've survived worse than a man with a pistol."

"You've survived *miles*." Tamsin's grip tightened. "This is years. Different animal." Then she dropped into the canoe and was swallowed by the dark, and Ulyana turned to face the river.

The first boat grounded itself against the hall's steps with a splintering crunch. Men poured out of it — four, five, six, rifles wrapped in plastic against the rain. And behind them, stepping carefully, dry under an umbrella held by one of his own men as though courtesy were armor, came Ivo Renner.

He looked up at her on the platform and smiled the smile she had first seen across a consulate bar, patient and polished as a headstone.

"Miss Crane. You continue to make my work difficult."

"You continue to make it necessary," she called back, and lifted the oilcloth bundle over her head. "Come and take it."

She turned and ran — along the platform, down the far ladder, onto the long wooden pier that reached out over the flooded commons where the village kept its boats. Behind her she heard Renner's voice, sharp now, the civility cracking: *"The pier! She'll have a boat at the pier!"*

Boots thundered after her. Good. Every boot on the pier was a boot not following the canoe sliding silent through the drowned streets behind them.

The pier was old cypress, built for a river three meters lower than this one. Ulyana knew that. She had spent two days learning this village the way she learned everything — by measuring it. She also knew what the flood was doing underneath: tearing at the pilings, scouring the mud from their feet, leaving them standing in nothing. A structure is only ever as honest as its foundations, and these foundations were lying.

She ran to the pier's end, spun, held the bundle high.

Renner stopped ten meters away, breathing hard, umbrella abandoned, rain flattening his silver hair. His men fanned out behind him, weapons rising.

"Put it down," he said. "Slide it here, and I give you my word no one else in this village is touched. You have my word, Miss Crane. I keep my word."

"Your word." She laughed, and it came out wilder than she intended. "Your grandfather gave his word too. It's in the ledger. *Requisition approved, transport guaranteed.* Four hundred people, guaranteed."

"That was war."

"This is weather," she said. "It doesn't care whose grandson you are."

And then the river answered her.

There was no dramatic crack, no warning groan. One moment the pier stood; the next, the section behind Renner simply folded, sucked sideways by the current, planks cartwheeling into the black water like cards swept off a table. Two of his men went with it — screaming, then not screaming, then gone downstream. The third scrambled back toward solid ground and did not look back.

Renner dropped to his knees on the last intact span, arms locked around a piling, the flood tearing at his legs, his fine coat, his whole careful inheritance.

Ulyana stood at the pier's end with the empty bundle dripping in her hands, and the decision arrived not as a thought but as a fact, the way distances arrive, the way signatures arrive on a page.

She could walk away. Ten seconds, and the river would settle the account his family had been dodging for sixty years. No trial, no witnesses, no blood on anyone's hands but the water's. The cleanest possible ending. Her grandfather would have understood it — a man who knew how to let things drown.

She threw the empty bundle aside and went down the pier on her belly, planks flexing, and grabbed his wrist.

"Don't—" he choked.

"Shut up and hold on."

The piling beneath him shifted. She got her other hand into his collar, braced her boots against a crossbeam, and pulled — and the river pulled back, and for one long, roaring moment Ulyana Crane hung between a drowning man and everything he represented, and understood that this was the actual ledger, the real requisition, the one being written right now with her signature pending.

She hauled. He came free of the water like a birth, coughing, sobbing, undignified, alive. Together they crawled back along the surviving planks while the rest of the pier surrendered behind them, board by board, until her hands found mud and grass and the hands of villagers pulling them both up the bank.

Renner knelt in the muck, vomiting river water, ruined, rescued.

Ulyana stood over him, shaking, soaked, and found that she was not sorry. That was the strange part. She had expected to feel diminished. Instead she felt measured — weighed against him and found heavier.

"The chest isn't on that pier," she told him, when he could hear. "It hasn't been for an hour. And by morning there will be two hundred copies of it in two hundred houses, and your name will be in every one of them — not as a villain. As a footnote. Footnotes are all cowards ever get to be."

He looked up at her with the rain streaming down his face, and for the first time since the consulate bar, Ivo Renner had nothing courteous left to say.

### TAMSIN

Tamsin had grown up on water that lied.

The delta taught you early: the channel that gleamed safe in daylight would eat you at night; the flooded street was not a street anymore but a current wearing a street's clothes. So she paddled the drowned lanes of the village not by sight but by memory and pressure — the way the paddle blade grabbed, the way the hull drifted a half-degree off true, each tell a sentence in a language only she and the river spoke.

The chest sat between her knees under oilcloth, lashed to the thwart. In the bow, crouched low, sat Dele, the ferryman's son, sixteen years old and furious with fear, because someone had to guide her past the submerged fence lines only the village kids knew, and he had volunteered before his mother could stop him.

"Left," he whispered. "Past the mango tree — the top's showing — go wide left, there's a wall under there."

She went wide left. Something brushed the hull below them, soft as a hand, and slid away.

Behind them, across the black expanse where the commons used to be, muzzle flashes stuttered. Shouts. Then, cutting through everything, a sound Tamsin felt in her sternum rather than heard: the deep structural groan of the pier giving up, followed by screams that ended too quickly.

"They're in the water," Dele breathed. "People are in the water—"

"Paddle," Tamsin said quietly. "Grief later. Paddle now."

She meant it for herself too. Every instinct screamed at her to turn, to haul bodies out, to do *something* — but her job tonight was not rescue. Her job was the one thing in this whole drowning world that could not swim: four hundred names, wrapped in oilcloth, riding between her knees. Her grandmother had been one of those names once, reduced to a line in a manifest, cargo. Tonight the cargo was going to choose its own destination, and Tamsin Adeyemi would paddle it there if her arms fell off at the elbows.

They passed the schoolhouse roof, awash to its ridge. They passed a floating cooking pot turning slowly like a planet. The rain thickened, thinned, thickened again, and somewhere upstream the river kept up its freight-train roar, still hungry, not finished.

At the mouth of the temple lane, Dele suddenly grabbed her arm. "Lights. Back there. Two boats — they're circling."

Tamsin looked. Two lanterns swung low over the water, sweeping the drowned streets in slow arcs. Searching. Renner had kept men back for exactly this — she'd have done the same in his position, and hated herself for knowing it.

"We can't outrun lanterns in open water," she murmured. "So we don't run. We become the water."

She backed the canoe into the lee of the submerged temple wall, laid the paddle flat, and let the current take them — dead drift, no wake, the hull riding among the flotsam, a branch among branches. The lantern light crawled toward them. Fifty meters. Thirty. Tamsin put her hand flat on the oilcloth and thought, absurdly, of her grandmother's hands, flour to the wrists, humming in a kitchen that had been built by a woman who should never have survived to bake anything at all.

*You looked away for me once,* she thought at the long-dead stranger on the closed line. *I'm looking straight ahead for you now.*

The lanterns swept past twenty meters off, paused — a lifetime — and moved on toward the far lanes, chasing a floating tarp that the wind obligingly pushed that way.

Dele let out a breath that shook. Tamsin dug the paddle in, gentle, and they ghosted onward through the sleeping streets, past lit windows where families sat on rooftops holding lamps, past an old man standing waist-deep on his own porch singing something low and steady, a song with names in it.

At the high ground beyond the coconut grove, the elders were waiting with dry blankets and a fire burning inside a drum. Tamsin grounded the canoe, lifted the chest, and carried it up the bank on her own shoulder, refusing every offered hand, until Mama Sefu met her at the top and took the other side, and together — descendant of the drowned and daughter of the delta — they bore it into the hall.

Inside, Ulyana was already there, soaked to the bone, a gash on her forearm being bound by someone's grandmother, alive, *alive*, and the look that passed between them needed no language at all.

"He's alive," Ulyana said. "I couldn't—"

"I know." Tamsin set the chest down at the center of the room, where the lamplight pooled around it like a congregation gathering. "I know exactly what you couldn't. It's the same thing my grandmother's stranger couldn't do the other way." She peeled back the wet oilcloth. "So we finish it properly. Tomorrow, at first light, we read every name. All of them. Out loud. And we make copies until there are more ledgers than lies."

Outside, the rain began at last to slacken, and the river, having taken the pier and nearly taken a minister's grandson, kept rolling past the village in the dark — enormous, indifferent, and for one more night, denied.

But downstream, at the edge of the lamplight, one of Renner's men who had crawled from the water stood dripping in the trees, counting windows, memorizing faces — and turned back through the storm to report that the chest had not been destroyed, had not fled, and was, at this very moment, sitting in a crowded room full of witnesses.

The last night of Vessar's silence had begun.
