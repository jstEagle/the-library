## 21. Inheritance

The page came to Tamsin the way the river gave up everything else that season: slowly, and only after it was ready.

Two days after the reading ended, on a morning so clear the delta looked like something newly made, one of the village elders found her at the boat. Old Sefu — the man who had read her grandmother's name aloud first, his voice cracking on the second syllable as though the name were a bone he'd been carrying in his throat for sixty years — came down the mud path with a folded paper in both hands, holding it the way you hold a sleeping child.

"It's yours," he said. "It was always yours. We just kept it warm."

Tamsin took it without speaking. Her hands, which could tie a bowline in the dark and feel a sandbar shift through thirty feet of brown water, shook like a green deckhand's.

She didn't open it on the bank. She carried it aboard the *Marrow*, down into the cabin where the stove still smelled of the storm, where the hole in the hull had been patched with planks donated by men whose grandfathers had been on the trains, and she sat on the bunk with the paper on her knees for a long time before she let herself look.

* * *

She had known what would be on it since the night of the argument at the shoal, when she'd named her price and Ulyana had stared at her across the guttering lantern with an expression Tamsin hadn't been able to read then and could read now, too well: pity and fear wearing each other's faces.

*What if it says they died?* Ulyana had asked.

*Then I'll know,* Tamsin had said. *Knowing is the whole price of being alive. I've paid worse.*

But knowing and reading were different animals, and the page sat on her knees like a live thing.

The ledger paper had been dried flat between boards, its water-stains turned to pale continents, the ink faded to the color of weak tea but stubbornly legible where it mattered. It was a transport manifest, Vessar Interior Ministry format, the same bureaucratic skeleton as every other page in the chest — date, origin station, destination, number of souls. The number of souls. That was how they'd written it. Not passengers. Not people. Souls, as if even the clerks had known they were doing something that needed a word from the other side of the grave.

Her eyes went down the column of names the way a pilot reads soundings — fast, trained, looking for the rock.

*Adeyemi, R.*

There it was. There her grandmother was, reduced to an initial and a line item, twenty-two years old according to the margin note, listed among forty-one others loaded at a station that officially did not exist onto a train that officially never ran, bound for a camp whose name had been sanded out of three national archives.

Tamsin had grown up on that name. Adeyemi, R. — her grandmother, Ruth, called Mama Ruth by everyone on the river, a woman who sang while she gutted fish and never once, in all Tamsin's childhood, spoke of where she'd come from or why she had arrived alone with nothing, not even a surname she was willing to claim until she married. A woman who had taught Tamsin the channels by drawing them in the dirt with a stick, who had said *the river forgets nothing, so you don't have to remember everything* — and Tamsin had thought it wisdom. Now she understood it was a wound talking.

Her finger stopped below the name. There was a notation in a different hand, cramped and hurried, ink pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*41 loaded. 40 accounted at delivery. Discrepancy noted. Investigation declined.*

Forty-one loaded. Forty delivered.

Someone had looked away. Someone on that closed line, at some dark siding in some hour of some night, had opened a door or turned a shoulder or simply failed to see, and a twenty-two-year-old girl named Ruth Adeyemi had stepped off a death train and into the reeds and lived — lived to marry, to sing, to draw rivers in dirt, to make a granddaughter who would one day run cargo through the delta and recognize a ledger when she saw one because her grandmother had flinched at paper her whole life.

Tamsin read the notation eleven times. Then she did the thing she had not done since she was nine years old, since the day the raiders burned the fish camp and her grandmother pulled her into a pirogue and poled them into the fog while their whole world went up behind them.

She put her face in her hands and wept for her grandmother — not the grandmother she'd lost, but the girl on the manifest, the one who had crouched in the dark under a railcar with her heart going like a pump with a failing gasket, waiting for footsteps, waiting for a flashlight beam, waiting to be either found or forgiven. Waiting for someone to look away.

Somebody had. And Tamsin existed inside that look. Every channel she knew, every knot, every sunrise she'd ever steered into — all of it hung from that single merciful blindness like a boat hangs from its anchor chain.

* * *

Ulyana found her there in the afternoon. Tamsin heard her boots on the deck plates — that surveyor's walk, measured, even, a woman who counted her own steps whether she meant to or not — and she didn't wipe her face. There didn't seem to be any point anymore. They'd pulled each other out of a flood. You don't hide crying from someone who has held your wrist over open water.

Ulyana came down the ladder, saw the page, and sat on the opposite bunk without asking. For a while neither of them said anything. The river talked against the hull. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher went off like a struck match.

"Forty-one loaded," Tamsin said finally. "Forty delivered."

Ulyana nodded slowly. "I know. I did the arithmetic the first night." She hesitated. "I hoped it was your grandmother. I hated myself for hoping it, but I did."

"I know." Tamsin almost smiled. "You're a terrible liar, Crane. It's your best quality."

"The discrepancy — my grandfather signed the requisitions for that stretch of line. He was the surveyor. His signature is on the route orders." Ulyana's voice did the thing it did when she was holding something heavy very carefully, level and slow, like a load gauge. "I spent weeks thinking he was the hand on the door. And then the missing page —"

"And now you think he was the hand that slipped the latch."

"I don't *think* anything. That's what I keep trying to tell myself and failing. The evidence says he helped deport them and then helped smuggle survivors out and then buried both truths so deep that even his own family drowned in the silence." She rubbed her jaw. "I used to believe inheritance was simple. You get the house or the debts. Turns out you get the whole weather system."

Tamsin looked at her — this woman who had come up the river measuring everything in miles, who had once told her, deadpan, over instant coffee in the rain, that she trusted distances because distances never lied to you — and felt something in her chest turn over, slow and enormous, like a barge coming about.

"You know what my grandmother would say?" Tamsin said. "She'd say you're counting wrong again."

Ulyana raised an eyebrow.

"She'd say: the man did a bad thing and then a good thing and then hid them both, and none of those things are yours. You didn't sign anything. You hauled the truth two hundred miles through a monsoon in a leaking tub with a woman you'd known four days." Tamsin tapped the page. "That's your ledger now. Write your own entries."

Ulyana looked away, out the porthole, at the light lying gold on the water. When she spoke again her voice had gone rough. "My grandfather died believing no one would ever know either half of what he did. My father died not knowing any of it. I got handed a locked room and told it was a closet." She shook her head. "I'm glad it's over. I'm also — God help me — I don't know who I am without the survey lines."

"You're the woman who pulled me back over the gunwale," Tamsin said. "Start there."

* * *

They left the village on the third morning, riding the last of the flood swell down toward the delta, the *Marrow* riding light and patched and ugly as a mudlark and handling sweeter than she had any right to. The villagers came down to see them off — Sefu pressing a bundle of smoked fish on them, children running the bank alongside until the current took the boat out past shouting distance. On the stern, lashed under canvas, rode the chest itself, empty now except for the copies the families had taken, going back downstream to be sunk in deep water off the old drowned station, returned to the river that had kept it safe for seventy years. The originals stayed in the village hall, behind glass, beside the list of names carved into new cedar by hands that had learned the letters from their grandparents' voices.

At the delta fork, where the channels braided apart like hair, they idled down.

This was where Tamsin turned north, back to her cargo runs, her clients, her small honest crooked trade. This was where Ulyana caught the coastal packet east, to take the job she'd accepted by radio two nights before — a new line, a real one, surveyed in daylight with public records and published maps, every mile accountable to somebody.

They tied up at the fuel dock at Lastlight Point, and neither of them moved to untie.

"So," Tamsin said.

"So," Ulyana agreed.

The delta spread out around them, wide as forgiveness, smelling of salt and rot and green growing things. A trawler worked the far channel, its wake writing and unwriting itself on the water.

"I've been thinking about what you said," Ulyana said. "About writing my own entries." She reached into her jacket and brought out a folded square of oilcloth, and inside it, the single ledger page — not the manifest with Ruth Adeyemi's name, but the rescue record, the missing page, the one with her grandfather's late-night entries, the smuggled survivors, the falsified counts. The page that had cost him everything and saved forty-some lives and bought Tamsin's whole existence without ever knowing it. "I made a copy for the village archive. This one's yours. You earned it more than anyone."

Tamsin took it carefully. "Crane —"

"Don't argue. I've watched you argue for two hundred miles and I know all your openings." But Ulyana was smiling, and her eyes were wet, and she wasn't hiding it either.

"What will you keep?" Tamsin asked.

"A copy of this. Nothing else. I'm burning the map tonight — the closed-line map, the whole thing. All those stations that never existed." Ulyana looked out at the water. "He drew it to hide people. I carried it to find them. Neither use is mine anymore. Let it go with the smoke."

They stood together a moment longer in the engine noise and the bird noise and the vast indifferent morning.

"You could come north sometime," Tamsin said, aiming for casual and hitting something rawer. "Dry season. I run a regular loop up the Kessel reach. Terrible coffee. Decent company."

Ulyana studied her, and Tamsin watched the surveyor do what the surveyor did — measure the distance between two points, calculate the grade, check the load. Except this time, when she finished, she didn't file the result away in some locked drawer. She just said:

"I'd like that. I'll write first. I'm told I owe somebody a letter five years overdue, and I should practice on easier audiences."

"Easier audiences than me? Nobody's easier than me. I'm famously easygoing."

"You threw a wrench at Renner's boat."

"He was in my channel."

Ulyana laughed — really laughed, head back, the sound startled out of her like a bird off water — and then she stepped forward and hugged Tamsin, quick and fierce, the way you hug someone on a dock when the boat's already moving. "Thank you," she said into Tamsin's shoulder. "For the passage. For the price you paid for it. For all of it."

"Anytime, Crane." Tamsin stepped back, throat tight, grinning like an idiot. "Mind the marks going out. Bar's shifted at Green Two since the flood."

"I'll trust the pilot's notes." Ulyana picked up her bag, slung it, walked three steps down the dock. Stopped. Turned around. "Tamsin. Your grandmother looked at the river her whole life and told you it forgets nothing. I think she was telling you something else. I think she was telling *herself*. And I think — I hope — somewhere she knows it worked."

Then she was gone up the dock toward the packet landing, straight-backed, unhurried, a woman walking into her own years instead of her miles, and Tamsin stood at the rail of the *Marrow* watching until the crowd swallowed her.

* * *

She cast off an hour later, alone, northbound, the patched hull humming under her feet like a healed bone.

In the cabin, pinned above the chart table where she'd see it every time she took the wheel, she fixed the manifest page — *Adeyemi, R.* — and beneath it, in her own handwriting, on a strip of torn chart margin, the notation she intended to spend the rest of her life living up to:

*41 loaded. 41 survived. One of them made me.*

The tide was turning. The channel north lay open, deep and green and full of light, and Tamsin Adeyemi — granddaughter of a girl who stepped off a ghost train into the reeds, inheritor not of guilt or grief but of a single act of mercy, passed hand to hand like a flame cupped against wind — eased the throttle forward and steered for home.
