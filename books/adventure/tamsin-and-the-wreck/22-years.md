## 22. Years

The map burned better than I expected.

I had imagined it would resist — that the heavy stock, the waxed finish my grandfather favored for field work, would fight the flame the way he had fought every honest thing in his life. But it caught on the first match, along the eastern margin where the river delta was drawn in his small, certain hand, and the fire moved across the paper like water finding a channel it had always known was there.

I stood at the iron stove in the survey office at Corren Junction and watched the closed lines go. The Vellarn cut. The old Serevan spur. The line through the drowned station with its carved initials, its dates like teeth. Forty years of his handwriting, every kilometer surveyed in secret, every gradient calculated so that trains could carry people into the dark without anyone asking why the timetable had no return schedule. The ink lifted off the paper first, ghosting upward, and for a moment all those lines hung in the air above the stove as smoke, as if the map were trying to become a railway again before it became nothing.

I let it burn. I did not look away, though my hands wanted to close around it, though some old reflex in me — the engineer's reflex, the archivist's reflex, the granddaughter's reflex — screamed that you do not destroy a document, that paper is truth and truth is sacred. But I had learned, over a year of learning, that this map was not truth. It was the shape truth had been forced to wear. And Tamsin's voice was in my ear the way it always was when I stood at crossroads of any kind: *Some things you keep by letting them go, Crane. Ask the river.*

The last corner curled, glowed orange, and went to ash. I stirred the stove with the poker until there was nothing left but a gray film over the coals, indistinguishable from any other fire's leavings.

What remained on the desk behind me was a single page, flattened under glass now, the way you'd keep a photograph of someone you loved rather than someone you feared. The page of rescues. My grandfather's hand again, but different here — hurried, guilty, alive in a way the survey notes never were. *Fourteen souls, night of the 11th. Brakes reported faulty, investigation pending.* The same lie repeated down the column, eleven times, each one a train that officially never ran, carrying people who officially did not exist, out along the line that officially was never built. He had smuggled them out under cover of his own atrocity. He had buried the evidence not only to protect the guilty — himself among them — but to protect the saved. One act could not be separated from the other. That was the whole terrible arithmetic of him, and I had stopped trying to make it come out even.

Outside the office window, the new line was making progress.

---

They'd hired me in the spring, after the reading, after the story went where stories go when three hundred families decide together that it will be told. There had been inquiries — journalists, historians, a commission from the regional assembly with letterhead thick enough to fold into a boat. I answered what I could answer and referred the rest to the village elders, who handled it with more patience than I have ever possessed. Some of the ledgers went to archives, finally, properly catalogued, copies made in triplicate. The original chest stayed in the village hall at Seren's Landing, on a shelf the carpenter built from salvaged pilings, unlocked. Anyone could open it. That was the point. A thing kept locked is a thing one person controls; a thing left open is a thing everyone guards.

The railway job came through an old classmate of mine named Doret Okafor, who ran engineering for the coastal expansion and who had read about me in the papers and made a judgment call I'm still grateful for. She didn't ask about the scandal. She asked if I could grade a bed through wet clay without the roadbed heaving in the first thaw, and I said yes, and that was the whole interview.

It is a new line. That matters to me more than I can probably explain to anyone who hasn't spent five years walking dead track. It runs from Corren Junction down toward the coast — forty-one kilometers, all of it documented, every survey public record, every easement negotiated in daylight with the landholders' signatures witnessed and filed. When we cut through a hillside, we file the spoil reports. When we build a bridge, the load calculations are published. Any person who wants to know why the line goes where it goes can find out, because there is nothing underneath it. No second ledger. No night timetable. I check sometimes — not because I doubt Doret or the ministry engineers, but because checking has become a kind of prayer for me, the way counting kilometers used to be. I walk the stakes with the drawings in my hand and I confirm that the world matches the paper and the paper matches the world, and both of them are available to anyone who asks.

My grandfather built railways too. I used to think that was our common ground, the thing that bound us across his silence and my exile. Now I understand it was the ground itself that mattered — what you build on top of it, and whether anyone can see the foundations.

---

The mile counter stopped working in the autumn, and I never fixed it.

It was a cheap brass odometer I'd clipped to my pack frame back in my surveying days, the kind that ticks off distance as you walk. Five years it had gone everywhere with me. I measured everything in those days — the walk from the tent to the water, the length of a silence, the distance between myself and my family, which I had once calculated, drunk and miserable in a port bar, as roughly four thousand rail kilometers and unbridgeable. Miles were safe. A mile is the same in every direction; it doesn't care what it measures. You can spend a whole life inside distances and never once be required to say how long you've been gone, or to whom, or why.

Tamsin saw it hanging off my pack the first week aboard her boat and laughed at it. "You count your steps?" she said. "Like a prisoner pacing a cell?"

"It's data," I said.

"It's a rosary," she said, "for a religion with no god in it," and I had no answer, because she was right, and because being seen that accurately by a stranger felt like a lock turning somewhere I hadn't known was locked.

The counter broke crossing the shoal shallows below the fork, the day after the ambush, when everything on that boat was soaked, bent, or praying. I found it later in the bilge water, the little brass wheel seized with grit, frozen at some number I never read. I meant to clean it. I never did. Somewhere between the village and the delta it stopped being a tool and became a relic, and then somewhere between the delta and here it stopped being anything at all, and one morning I realized I had left it in a drawer in my rented room in Corren and had not thought of it in six weeks.

That's how it went, mostly. Not decisions but drifts. Not renunciations but mornings when the old habit simply failed to arrive.

---

I measure in years now. It happened without ceremony, which is how the true things happen, in my experience; the false ones demand ceremony.

Last month Doret asked me, casually, over the site table with its coffee rings and stake maps, how long I planned to stay on the coastal project. The old me would have answered in distance — *until the line reaches the coast, another thirty-odd kilometers* — or deflected entirely, already half-packed. Instead I heard myself say: "Years, I think. However long it takes to build it right."

She looked up. She didn't make anything of it, which I appreciated. But something settled in me when I said it, like a span settling onto its piers. Years. Time you commit to a place and its people. Time that accumulates instead of passing. My grandfather counted tonnage and my father counted money and I counted miles, and all of us were fleeing the same simple question, which is: *how long will you stay, and what will you be answerable for?*

A year ago tonight I was standing in the rain in the village hall at Seren's Landing while Elder Maren read names until her voice gave out and her daughter took the ledger from her hands and read on. Two days, they read. The whole village stayed. Children who couldn't possibly have understood sat still anyway, because the adults were still, and stillness like that teaches faster than words. When they reached the names of the drowned they read them slowly. When they reached the fourteen rescued on the night of the 11th, an old man near the back stood up and said, "That was my mother," and sat down again, and nobody applauded, because it wasn't a performance. It was an accounting. The first honest one that line had ever produced.

I keep the page of rescues in a tube on the shelf above my desk, next to the glass that holds it flat when it's home. I take it out sometimes, usually late, usually after a day of good ordinary work. I am not absolved by it. I want to be clear about that, because I can feel, writing this, the pull toward a tidy ending — the granddaughter finds redemption in the grandfather's secret goodness, the scales balance, the music swells. No. What I feel when I hold that page is closer to vertigo than peace: the knowledge that a man can drive the train into the dark and then drive another one back out, and that both trips are real, and that neither cancels the other, and that I contain the same capacity in proportions I will hopefully never have to test. Inheritance isn't a verdict. It's a set of tools. Some of them you use. Some of them you melt down. All of them you're responsible for knowing you carry.

But there is this, and I allow myself this: on the night of the 11th, eighty years ago, someone chose to look away in the merciful direction. Someone risked everything that mattered to him — his name, his career, his family's standing, all the currency he'd been paid in — to move fourteen strangers along a line that didn't exist. Whatever else he was, he was that, for eleven nights running. And the descendants of those fourteen are alive, and some of them fish the delta waters, and one of them taught me to net mullet last summer with a patience I did not deserve, calling me *sister* by the third day without ever explaining why, because the ledger had already explained it and explanations would have been redundant.

---

The letter came this morning.

I knew the handwriting before I knew the contents — that slanted, impatient script, letters leaning forward like a boat taking weather. Tamsin writes the way she pilots: fast, committed, correcting course mid-sentence. The postmark was three weeks old, delta ports being what they are, and the envelope smelled faintly of diesel and salt, which means she carried it herself to whatever passed for a mail boat and argued with someone about the fare.

*I got the contract,* she wrote. *Two boats now, if you can believe it. The second one leaks, but so did the first one when I got her, and look how that turned out. Renner's trial starts in the spring. The commission subpoenaed me — ME, imagine — so I'll be in the capital in April wearing my one good shirt, telling lawyers about channels they couldn't find with both hands and a chart. Come down for it. Or don't come for it, come after it. The mullet run is May. Your sister-in-netting says you owe her a season.*

Then, lower on the page, in smaller script, the way she says the things that cost her:

*The page hangs framed over my wheel now. Grandmother's name in your grandfather's hand. I look at it every morning before I take the boat out. I used to think inheritance was a weight you got handed whether you asked or not. Now I think it's more like a current — you don't choose it, but you choose what sails in it. You chose well, Crane. So did I, eventually. Mostly because of you.*

*Come south. The river misses you. I checked.*

*I checked* — meaning, I understood, that she had gone looking for me the way I had started going looking for her: not out of need exactly, but out of the habit of a person who has become part of your navigation. We had parted at the delta docks eight months ago with a handshake that turned into an embrace that turned into neither of us letting go for slightly too long, and then she'd stepped aboard and I'd stepped back, and we had both pretended that was a clean ending because pretending is what you do when the true thing is too large to say in a port with strangers watching.

I read the letter twice at the site table while the crew milled around me drinking their morning tea, and then I folded it along its existing creases and put it in my breast pocket, where it rode with me all day up and down the line.

---

At dusk I walked the last kilometer of finished grade alone, as I do most evenings. The rails aren't laid yet — just the ballast, the sleepers stacked like dark bread beside the bed, the stakes marching ahead into the winter light. Forty-one kilometers of documented, daylight, accountable track, and tomorrow we'd add meters to it, and next spring more, and the year after that the first train would come through at a scheduled hour on a published timetable, and anyone at all could stand at any crossing and watch it pass and know exactly what it carried and why.

I stopped at the end of the graded section, where the stakes run out into the unbuilt country, and I did what I always do: I took out the drawings and confirmed the world against the paper. Everything matched. Everything was visible. Nothing underneath.

Then I stood there a while longer, because the light was doing something to the hills that deserved standing still for, and because I have learned — slowly, at the cost of a great deal of mileage — that standing still is not the same as being stuck.

In my pocket, Tamsin's letter pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. *Come south. The river misses you. I checked.* In May the mullet would run. In April there would be a courtroom and one good shirt. Between now and then there was a line to build and a crew to keep and a winter to get through, all of it measurable not in kilometers but in seasons, in years, in the slow accumulation of a life that stays where it stands and answers for what it builds.

I thought about the drawer in my rented room, and the broken brass counter lying in it, ticking nothing, measuring nothing, holding its final number like a held breath. I decided I would leave it there. Not thrown away — I wasn't ready for that, and maybe never would be, and had learned to forgive myself the unfinished things. Just left. A relic of a woman who once believed that distance was a place you could live.

The sun went down behind the western cuts. I turned back toward the lights of the camp, toward the stove and the kettle and the page of rescues waiting on its shelf, and I walked home the way I walk everywhere now — unhurried, uncounted, one year and counting.
