## 1. The Weight of Names

The rain had not yet come, but Lena Halloran could smell it the way she could smell a rockfall before it moved—a pressure in the air, a greenness, a held breath. She stood at the head of her six clients on a ridge above the Marsyangdi valley and let them catch up to her silence, because silence was part of what they'd paid for. *Off-book trek, authentic trails, no crowds.* What they were really paying for was her. Her eye for weather, her memory of every goat path between Besisahar and the passes, and above all her discretion—the unspoken understanding that whatever questions they asked about their guide's past would be answered smoothly, pleasantly, and with nothing true inside them.

"Miss Halloran." The German, Weber, came puffing up the switchbacks with his trekking poles planted like survey stakes. He was sixty-one, retired from something industrial, and had the particular confidence of a man who had never once been lost. "My map shows a trail on the eastern side of the river."

"Your map is twelve years old," Lena said, without turning around. "The landslide took that side out in 'nineteen. The river took the bridge after that. We cross at the lower ford tomorrow."

Weber consulted his map anyway, holding it against the actual mountains as if one might apologize to the other. Lena waited. Behind him, the others straggled upward: the two Australian nurses who talked constantly and listened never; a quiet Japanese accountant named Sato who watched everything; an American couple celebrating something or other, their marriage by the look of the distance they kept between themselves.

She counted heads the way she always did—seven, counting herself—and then counted again, because counting twice was how you stayed alive and once was how you got people killed. Seven. Good.

The ridge dropped into cedar shadow. Below, the valley ran brown-green with early spring melt, terraced fields stitched to slopes so steep they seemed pinned there by faith alone. Somewhere below, a mule train's bells clanked their slow arithmetic. Lena breathed it in—dung smoke, pine resin, wet stone—and felt the familiar tightening in her chest that was not quite fear and not quite peace. This country didn't know her name. That was the arrangement. She'd made it carefully, over years, one forged permit and one avoided checkpoint at a time.

"Is it true you guided Everest?" the younger Australian nurse asked, catching up beside her. Priya—no wait, that was her navigator, back in Pokhara. The nurse was called Deb. Deb with the sunburned nose and the endless questions.

"Annapurna Circuit, mostly," Lena said. "Some high routes east."

"But I read somewhere—" Deb began.

"You read the internet," Lena said, smiling so the edge wouldn't show. "The internet says a lot of things."

It wasn't even a lie, exactly. It was a door closed gently instead of slammed. She'd gotten good at that. Ten years good. Long enough that the smoothness frightened her sometimes—not the lying itself but how little effort it cost now, how the false versions of herself came pre-assembled, ready to wear like rented boots. There was Lena the regional guide, competent, unremarkable, history beginning roughly five years ago. There was Lena the Englishwoman gone slightly native (she let clients assume; correcting passports was more paperwork than it was worth). There was Lena who had "taken some time off" after "a difficult season," which was true in the way that a house fire is a difficult afternoon.

What there was not, anywhere in the catalogue, was Kyrgyzstan. Was the name she'd carried before. Was the rescue that hadn't been one, the report with her name in bold where the blame pooled, the blacklist that followed her across three borders like a dog that knew its owner.

*You're thinking about it again,* she told herself, and put her eyes back on the trail, because the trail deserved attention and self-pity didn't.

* * *

They made camp that night in a meadow the maps had forgotten, a shelf of grass above the river with a spring that ran cold and iron-tasting. Lena worked while the clients ate—Tomas's dal bhat traveled in sealed drums, and Tomas himself had stayed behind in Pokhara this trip, so the cooking fell to her, which meant it fell to boiling water and hoping. She moved through camp checking anchors, checking the food hang, checking the sky, which was doing something complicated in the west. Clouds stacked up like unread mail.

Sato appeared at her elbow with his tea, silent as snowfall.

"The clouds," he said. He spoke rarely, and each word arrived like it had been audited first.

"Building over the Annapurnas," Lena said. "Nothing tonight. Maybe rain tomorrow evening."

He nodded slowly. "In my work, we call this *yohaku*. The empty space. In a painting, the space that is not painted tells you the most." He looked at her with mild, terrible perceptiveness. "You have much empty space, I think."

Lena laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and Sato smiled faintly into his tea and wandered off, satisfied, having said precisely enough.

She stood a long time after the clients turned in, watching the cloud bank. Her tent was the last pitched, as always, positioned between the clients and the drop-off, because that was where the guide slept: in the doorway of the danger. Ten years ago she'd have had a rope team around her, voices on the radio, Eli complaining about the freeze-dried coffee. Now the mountain nights were just hers, vast and unaudited.

Eli. She hadn't let herself think the whole shape of it in months, and here it came anyway, rolling in with the weather. Twelve years since the Tsangpo expedition lost contact. Twelve years since the search was called off at day nineteen because the permits lapsed and the company's lawyers got nervous and everyone agreed, in that soft institutional voice, that there was nothing more to be done. Twelve years since Lena stopped saying her brother's name out loud, because saying it made it a claim, and claims could be denied.

The clouds ate the stars. She zipped into her tent and lay listening to the river, which never stopped talking, which never needed anyone to answer.

* * *

The trouble came on the fourth day, at a place where the trail funneled down to a suspension bridge above white water, and the Nepali Army had decided to remember it existed.

Lena saw the checkpoint from two hundred meters—a sandbagged position, a striped barrier arm, three soldiers in fatigues, one of them already looking up-valley at her group through narrowed eyes. Her body did the math before her mind finished the question: permits (forged, Tomas's cousin's cousin's work, usually fine), route (technically closed above the second village since the flood), names (hers, on a list, possibly two lists).

"Everyone stops here," she called back to the group, cheerful, project-manager bright. "Tea break! Stretch your legs. Nobody crosses yet—we'll go over in pairs so we don't overload the bridge."

She walked down alone, unhurried, hands visible, smile calibrated to the exact frequency of harmless foreign woman. The youngest soldier stepped forward. Sixteen maybe, seventeen, rifle too big for him, trying to look older than his boots.

"Madam. Your papers."

"Papers, of course." She passed over the permit folder, watched his eyes move line by line—he was reading slowly, honestly, which was worse than corruption, because honesty couldn't be steered. "We're a small cultural trek, low impact. My clients are very interested in the villages."

The sergeant came over. Older, mustached, eyes that had checked ten thousand documents and believed perhaps nine of them. He flipped past the permit page and stopped at her guide registration.

"Halloran," he said.

"Yes."

"This is not the same spelling as our list has."

The river roared beneath the planks. Somewhere behind her, Deb laughed at something Weber said, the sound carrying down the slope like a dropped glass.

"Lists are often wrong," Lena said. Her heartbeat had not changed speed. That was the trick nobody taught you—that fear lived in the pulse, and the pulse could be lied to. "Which list is yours?"

The sergeant looked at her for a long moment. Up close she could see the monsoon damp creeping into the sandbags, the rust blooming on the barrier hinge, the general air of a post that existed mostly in theory. He was deciding whether she was worth the paperwork of being right about.

"Where do you sleep tonight?" he asked.

"Below the tree line, at the teahouse in Tal. We'll register there." A lie. Tal's register book had been washed away in the flood and never replaced; she'd confirmed it in March.

He handed the folder back. "The upper trail is closed. Landslide danger."

"We're taking the lower route to the hot springs and turning back. Five days total." Another lie, smooth as river stone.

For one suspended second—one full rotation of the planet, it felt like—his eyes went distant, reaching for something. A training bulletin? A name half-remembered from a briefing years ago? Lena stood inside that second the way she'd once stood inside collapsing seracs, absolutely still, absolutely present, aware that her entire life hung on the mood of a bored man with a rifle.

Then he waved her through. "Go. Before the rain."

She thanked him in Nepali, warm and fluent, gathered the clients, and crossed the bridge in pairs as promised, feeding the plank-bridge her weight gently, watching the water hammer through the gorge below. On the far side she didn't look back at the checkpoint. Looking back was how you told the universe you'd been afraid.

But her hands, when she finally let them, were shaking. Just the fingers. Just for a moment, hidden inside her gloves, walking point with her back to six oblivious strangers who thought they'd hired a guide named Halloran, regional, competent, history beginning five years ago.

That was the thing about invisibility nobody warned you about. It wasn't armor. It was tissue paper. It held until the one day it rained harder, the one soldier who read slower, the one name spelled one letter differently. And underneath it, all these years, she had been walking through the world with nothing between her skin and the fall.

* * *

They descended out of the hills two days later, ahead of the first real rain, and Lena delivered her clients to the jeep road with the practiced warmth of a hostess closing a good party. Tips were generous. Deb hugged her. Sato shook her hand with both of his and said, quietly, "*Yohaku*, Miss Halloran. Take care of your empty spaces." Then they were dust down the road, and she was alone with the gear and the mule man's invoice and the long jeep ride back toward Pokhara, where the lake lay flat and gray under the building monsoon and her crew was waiting.

Her phone buzzed against her hip as the town came into view—Dhaulagiri hiding its head in cloud across the water, the lakeside strip loud with end-of-season music. One message. Unknown number. Local code.

*Ms. Halloran. Dev Kapoor. We should talk. I'm at the Boathouse bar until Friday. It's about a job. And about your brother.*

Lena read it three times while the jeep jolted along the shore road. Around her, Pokhara went on being Pokhara—paragliders stitching the sky, tourists haggling over singing bowls, everything bright and cheap and easy. She typed nothing. Deleted the draft twice. The rain arrived at last, big warm drops exploding on the windshield, and the driver cranked the wipers, and still she sat with the phone glowing in her palm like something radioactive.

Dev Kapoor. After ten years. After Kyrgyzstan, after the testimony he'd given and the one he hadn't, after the career that rose on the same slope hers collapsed down.

And that last sentence, sitting there under everything else like a charge under snow:

*It's about your brother.*

She put the phone face-down on her knee and watched the water sheet off the road, and told herself she would not go. She was already deciding what to wear.
