## 2. Crew of Strays

The bus from the trailhead let Lena off at the bottom of Sarangkot road in a drizzle that smelled of woodsmoke and wet dust, and she walked the last three kilometers into Pokhara because she didn't trust her legs to stop moving if she sat down. Nine days on the back trails with six clients who thought "off the beaten path" was a brand of adventure rather than a legal category had left her with a familiar hum in her bones—the good kind of tired, the kind that meant everything had held together through sheer stubbornness and nothing else.

She came down past the terraced fields where the mist was lifting off the rice in long white ribbons, crossed the iron bridge over the Seti with its river running brown and muscular below, and turned into the lane behind the old bakery where the crew kept rooms above a shuttered tailor shop. The sign on the tailor's door still said OPEN in faded letters, though no one had opened it in years. That was the point. Nothing about them announced itself.

Tomas heard her coming before she reached the stairs. He always did—claimed he could tell her footsteps from anyone's, that she walked like someone apologizing to the ground for using it.

"You look like something the river spat out," he said, standing in the doorway of the kitchen they'd built out of the tailor's back room, arms folded across a chest like a beer barrel, forearms mapped with burn scars from twenty years of camp stoves and one bad grease fire in a lodge at Manang that he refused to discuss.

"I feel like something the river considered," Lena said, dropping her pack against the wall with a groan of buckles. "Six clients. Two of them snored in stereo. One asked me every morning if today was the day we'd see a snow leopard."

"Was it?"

"It's the Annapurna foothills, Tomas. The leopards have better taste."

He grunted, which from Tomas was a laugh, and turned back to the stove where something with garlic and cumin was happening in a dented pot. He cooked the way he did everything else—loudly, with his whole body, as if the food might try to escape. The kitchen smelled like home, which was a word Lena used carefully and rarely, but which this room insisted on anyway: steam-fogged windows, a shelf of mismatched mugs, a calendar from 2019 that nobody had replaced because the photograph—a glacier lake the color of a bruise—was too good to give up.

"Kip's out," Tomas said over his shoulder. "Runner job. The German couple wanted their laundry done in Lakeside and their son wanted—" he waved a spoon vaguely, "—everything. Phones, cigarettes, a girlfriend. Sixteen going on forty."

"He's fifteen."

"He lies about his age the way you lie about your name. Professionally."

Lena took down a mug—the chipped blue one, hers by silent treaty—and poured herself tea from the pot on the back burner, which was always warm, which was always there, which was one of perhaps four reliable facts in her life. Through the window she could see the lake, Phewa, lying flat and pewter-colored under the cloud deck, Tal Barahi temple on its island like a toy someone forgot to put away. Tourists paddled plastic kayaks across its surface. Somewhere out there was a version of her life where she owned an office with a sign and a permit framed on the wall and clients who paid with credit cards instead of cash pressed into her hand in bus stations.

That version had died in Kyrgyzstan, in a whiteout on a ridge she still couldn't say aloud without tasting metal.

She drank her tea and let the kitchen hold her up for a while.

* * *

Priya arrived at dusk, coming up the lane fast, rain jacket slicked dark, a rolled bundle of maps under one arm and the particular set of her jaw that meant she'd spent the day arguing with someone and won, or lost, or both. She was small and quick and moved like water finding its level, and she carried herself with the compressed energy of a person who had once been somebody official and now was not, though she would have peeled her own skin off before admitting what she'd been.

"The checkpoint at Naudanda has new men," she said, shrugging out of her jacket and hanging it with the precision of someone who could not abide disorder. "Army rotation. They stopped me for twenty minutes. Asked why I was carrying topographic sheets."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I'm a geography tutor. That I take students on educational walks." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "The sergeant's daughter is studying for her school exams. I gave him my number and offered to help her with map reading."

Tomas barked a laugh from the stove. "You're wasted on us. You should be running the country."

"The country doesn't want me." Priya said it lightly, the way you'd mention the weather, and Lena caught—as she always caught, because they were alike in this—the flicker beneath it, the thing being carried. Priya had a file somewhere too. Everyone in this house had a file somewhere, real or metaphorical, a paper ghost with their name on it. They never asked each other about the files. That was the founding covenant of the household, unwritten and absolute: you could be anything here except interrogated.

Priya spread the maps on the table, weighting the corners with mugs and a tin of cardamom pods, and Lena leaned over them despite herself. Old habit. Even off-book treks deserved good navigation; especially off-book treks. Priya drew routes the way calligraphers drew script—fine pencil lines, elevation notes in a tiny exact hand, water sources marked with blue dots so small they looked like punctuation.

"There's a new trail cut above Lumle," Priya said, tapping. "Landslide took the old one out in June. And the tea house at Deurali is closed—the family went to India. So if we run the monsoon-shoulder route again, we route through here." Her finger traced a contour line. "And here, we don't exist. No checkpoints, no lodges, no witnesses."

"No witnesses," Lena repeated. It was supposed to sound like strategy. It came out sounding like a eulogy.

Priya looked up at her, and for a second the navigator's mask slipped and Lena saw the woman underneath—tired, watchful, carrying her own sealed box of a past. "It keeps us free," Priya said quietly. "Unwitnessed is another word for unfound. Some days I'll take it."

"Some days," Lena agreed, and neither of them said the rest: and some days it feels like disappearing in installments.

* * *

Kip came home after dark, soaked to the waist, grinning like the rain was a personal gift. He was all elbows and collarbones, fifteen going on sixteen, with a shock of hair he cut himself with kitchen scissors and a voice that cracked when he got excited, which was often. He'd appeared two winters ago outside the teahouse in Ghandruk, offering to carry packs for food, and when Lena told him to go home to his mother he'd said, evenly, that his mother was in Nepalgunj and his father was a rumor, and that he could walk faster than any porter she'd ever hired, and dared her to test him.

She'd tested him. He'd walked her into the ground. Then he'd followed the crew back to Pokhara like a stray dog who had decided the matter was settled, and Tomas had fed him, and that had been that. Three years now. He ran messages, bought supplies, ferried gear across town on a bicycle with no brakes, and absorbed everything about mountain craft the way sand absorbs water—with total commitment and no visible limit.

"We got your flour," he announced, dumping a sack on the counter, then a bag of onions, then, with ceremony, a newspaper folded into quarters. "And the man at the bookstall says there's a big outfit asking around Pokhara. Foreign contract. Big money. He heard it from a taxi driver who heard it from a hotel manager."

"Taxi drivers hear everything and know nothing," Tomas said.

"This one knew a name." Kip unfolded the newspaper with unnecessary drama, found nothing relevant in it, and abandoned it. "Dev Kapoor."

The name landed in the kitchen like a stone dropped into still water.

Lena kept her face still. She was good at that; she'd had practice. But inside, something old shifted its weight, the way ice shifts on a glacier at night—a deep sound, felt more than heard, promising movement to come.

"You know him?" Kip asked, watching her with those quick dark eyes that missed nothing, that idolized her so completely and so transparently that sometimes it was like standing in direct sun.

"I knew him," she said. "A long time ago."

"Was he famous? He's famous now. There are photos of him in the outfitter offices in Kathmandu, shaking hands with—"

"Kip." Not sharp, but final. Kip subsided, hurt flickering across his face, and Lena hated herself a little for it. "Wash the road off. Dinner's nearly ready."

He went, thumping up the stairs two at a time, and Tomas looked at her over the pot with an expression that contained an entire conversation: *I didn't ask. I'm not asking. But I saw that.*

"Eat," Lena said, and sat down at the table among the maps.

* * *

Dinner was dal, rice, a curry of potatoes and whatever greens Tomas had bullied out of the market woman he flirted with by insulting her produce, and flatbread he made by slapping dough between his palms with a violence that somehow produced tenderness. They ate the way they always ate—crowded around the one big table, elbows overlapping, Kip talking with his hands until Priya confiscated the space in front of his plate, Tomas refusing compliments the way other men refused bribes.

This was the part of her life Lena protected most fiercely and acknowledged least. On the trail she was the guide, the authority, the one whose judgment everyone's breathing depended on. Here she was just a woman at a table, passing the bread, listening to Kip describe—in forensic detail—how a tourist's drone had crashed into a buffalo, and how the buffalo had opinions about this.

"So then the drone guy is running in circles," Kip said, mouth full, "and the buffalo is doing this—" he demonstrated with his head, low and swinging, nearly taking out the water jug, "—and the whole village comes out, and the drone is just *gone*, swallowed, and the guy is crying, actually crying, saying it cost eight hundred dollars—"

"Buffalo one," Tomas said. "Technology nil."

"—and Didi, the old lady with the shop? She goes into the field and reaches *into* the dung, right into it, and pulls out the drone. Still blinking. Hands it to him and says, 'In Nepal, even your garbage comes back to you.'"

Priya laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, rare enough that everyone paused to enjoy it—and for a moment the kitchen was so warm and so complete that Lena had to look down at her plate. This was the arithmetic she never said out loud: that she had lost a license, a country, a brother, and a version of herself, and in exchange had somehow been given this. A table. A family assembled entirely from wreckage—hers and theirs—held together not by blood or paperwork but by the daily, unglamorous act of showing up and feeding each other.

Warmth earned, not given. Nobody here trusted easily; trust had been the thing that broke each of them, in one way or another. So they'd rebuilt it in small denominations. Tomas showed love by remembering exactly how much chili each person could survive. Priya showed it by drawing escape routes nobody hoped to need. Kip showed it by bringing the newspaper and the gossip and his whole loud heart, laying them on the table like offerings.

And Lena—Lena showed it by coming home. By walking through that door every time, when every instinct trained into her by the mountains said keep moving, stay light, owe nothing, belong nowhere.

"Your turn," Kip said suddenly, pointing his spoon at her. "Trail story. Something good. You never tell us anything."

"There's nothing to tell. Rain, leeches, a client who cried at the suspension bridge."

"Why did he cry?"

"Fear of heights."

"And you fixed him?"

"I talked him across. One plank at a time. You don't fix fear, Kip. You just refuse to let it make the decisions."

He nodded solemnly, filing it away next to everything else she'd ever said, and she felt the weight of that—the weight of being someone's compass when your own needle was cracked. She thought, not for the first time, that she should send him to school properly, somewhere with walls and certificates, somewhere far from women with blacklists and men with paper ghosts. She thought it, and she knew she wouldn't, because he'd already chosen, and because some stubborn part of her needed the witness. Needed someone to see the competence and believe it meant something, even when the official record said otherwise.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and Kip had gone up to sleep and Priya was bent over her maps rolling them with the care of a surgeon closing a patient, Lena stood at the window with the last of the tea going cold in her hands.

The lake was invisible now, just a blackness beyond the town lights, and beyond it the Annapurna range was a presence rather than a sight—a massing of darker dark, snowfields holding the last light like coals under ash. Somewhere up in that darkness were passes she knew better than her own signature, routes she could walk blindfolded, ground that had been her kingdom once, before Kyrgyzstan took her name away and the Himalaya became a place she haunted instead of inhabited.

"Tomas says there's a big outfit sniffing around," Priya said without looking up from her maps. She said it casually. Too casually. She'd been waiting all evening to say it.

"Kip says."

"Kip repeats. I confirm. The Hotel Glacier View has a guest registered under a company name—Meridian Alpine Expeditions. Kathmandu office, foreign backing. They booked three rooms starting tomorrow." Priya smoothed a curl in the map paper with her thumbnail. "Big money books three rooms, Lena. Big money doesn't come to Pokhara for the paragliding."

Lena watched the reflection of the lamp in the black glass, doubled and faint. *Dev Kapoor.* Twelve years since she'd seen him, since the last time they'd stood on a summit together and he'd said, laughing into the wind, that the two of them would be old and gray and still arguing about rope lengths. Since before Kyrgyzstan. Since before everything.

"If it's him," she said, "he can book his three rooms and drink his coffee and go home."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it's worse. Because strangers with money want things. And the things they want are usually the things other people died keeping quiet about."

Priya was quiet for a moment. Outside, a dog barked twice and gave up. "You've been expecting something," she said. It wasn't quite a question. "All season. You check the road twice as often as you used to. You read the newspapers first now, before the sports section. You're waiting for something."

Lena turned the cold mug in her hands. The chipped blue one. Eli had bought it for her at a stall in Thamel the summer before he disappeared, haggling the vendor down with theatrical outrage while she pretended not to know him. She hadn't told anyone in this house that. Some things stayed in the deepest pocket, sewn shut.

"Everyone's waiting for something, Priya."

"Not you. You wait like it's a person. Like it's going to knock."

Lena almost smiled. Almost. "Go to sleep, navigator. Whatever's coming will still be coming in the morning."

But she stood at the window a long time after Priya's light went out, watching the road below the lane, empty and silvered with rain, and telling herself the tightening in her chest was only tiredness.

Down in the town, three rooms stood ready at the Hotel Glacier View, and somewhere in the dark a bus was climbing the last pass into the valley, carrying whatever was coming toward her at fifty kilometers an hour, headlights sweeping the wet curves, unstoppable as weather.
