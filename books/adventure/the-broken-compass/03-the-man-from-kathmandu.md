## 3. The Man from Kathmandu

Dev Kapoor had rehearsed the speech eleven times on the flight from Kathmandu to Pokhara, and by the twelfth telling he had already abandoned it.

The problem was that every version sounded like a lie, and the trouble with lies was that Lena Halloran could smell them the way a snow leopard smelled blood—something in her went still and quiet and utterly attentive, and then you were already dead. He'd watched it happen to better liars than himself. A French expedition leader in Namche Bazaar who'd promised her a summit bonus that didn't exist. A liaison officer from the tourism ministry who'd told her the weather window was longer than the satellite models said. Both of them had walked away thinking they'd gotten away with it. Both of them had been wrong, and both of them had learned it later, at altitude, when it mattered.

So Dev sat in the corner of a tea house above Phewa Lake with his back to the wall—a habit from old expeditions, though these days the things that came for him wore suits instead of storms—and watched the market below through the open window, and did not drink his tea, because his hands were not steady enough to be seen holding anything.

He found her crew before he found her. That was always the way of it. Lena moved like weather; you felt the pressure change before you saw the front.

Tomas was easy to spot, because Tomas was always the largest thing in any frame he occupied—a slab-shouldered Czech with forearms like mooring rope, arguing with a vegetable seller over the price of onions with the gravity of a man negotiating an arms treaty. He'd put on weight since Dev had last seen him, years ago, across a base camp fire on the south side of something cold. But the hands were the same. The hands that could break down a stove at nineteen thousand feet in a wind that would strip paint off steel.

Priya came out of the alley behind him carrying a crate of kerosene, and Dev's chest did something complicated, because Priya Nair was supposed to be finished. Blacklisted, struck off, done—the way Lena was done, the way he himself would be done if half the boardroom stories about his company's finances ever crawled into daylight. And yet there she was, navigating the market crowd with a folded paper map in her teeth and a look on her face like a woman doing long division she intended to win. She still carried herself like someone who knew where north was without being told. Some people never lost that. Most people never had it.

And then there was the boy.

The kid couldn't have been more than sixteen, all elbows and restless energy, weaving between porters and pilgrims with a canvas satchel slung across his back, delivering messages and small parcels with the seriousness of a diplomat. He stopped to talk to everyone. He touched everything. He laughed at something the onion vendor said, and Tomas cuffed him gently on the ear without breaking off his negotiation, and the boy grinned like the sun had come out just for him.

Dev didn't know the boy's name. That bothered him more than it should have. Ten years ago, Dev Kapoor had known every member of Lena's crews down to their shoe sizes and their mothers' names. Now there was a child running messages for her whom he couldn't place, and the distance between then and now opened beneath him like a crevasse whose far lip he couldn't see.

*You did that,* said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like his own father. *You built that distance yourself, one smart decision at a time.*

He ordered a second tea he wouldn't drink and made himself keep watching.

* * *

The contract was in his breast pocket, in a leather folder worth more than everything the tea house contained. Fourteen pages. Non-disclosure clauses, indemnification schedules, a survey scope written in the bloodless language of mineral prospecting dressed up as "high-altitude hydrological research." His client had insisted on the fiction, and Dev had let him, because the fee was the kind of number that could resurrect a dying company, and Dev's company had been dying for three years now with the slow, dignified persistence of a man walking himself into a whiteout.

Kapoor Expeditions. Once the name had meant something. Once it had been stamped on permits and painted on aircraft fuselages and spoken with respect in the ministries of two countries. Now it meant a rented office above a travel agency in Thamel, two junior guides he couldn't afford to pay properly, and a line of credit extended by a client whose interest rates Dev chose not to calculate too precisely. The Tsangpo survey was the last card in his hand. He knew it. His accountant knew it. He suspected even his ex-wife knew it, though she had stopped taking his calls sometime around the second restructuring.

And so here he was, watching a disgraced guide's ragtag family buy onions in a market in Pokhara, preparing to ask her for the one thing she had sworn never to give.

Because that was the truth under the fourteen pages: this job wasn't really about the source of the Tsangpo. It was about Lena. Her routes, her instincts, her knowledge of ground no permit would ever cover legally. The client had other surveyors. What the client didn't have—what nobody had—was a woman who could read a glacier the way Priya read maps, who knew the headwaters country from a decade of treks that appeared on no manifest anywhere, and who had one additional qualification no one else alive possessed.

She had been there before. With Eli.

Dev closed his eyes briefly and the memory arrived on schedule, punctual as a train: white sky, black rock, the radio call that came in broken and wrong. *Halloran party overdue. Halloran party overdue.* And then the search, and the search after that, and then the finding of nothing, and then the inquiry, and then the moment in a hotel conference room in Kathmandu when a man from the client's company slid a photograph across the table to a twenty-eight-year-old Dev Kapoor and said, quietly, *This is what we recovered. This is what we're prepared to say publicly. Do we understand each other?*

Dev had understood each other. Dev had understood each other very well indeed, and it had made his career.

He opened his eyes. Below, Lena had emerged from the alley behind the butcher's stalls, and the whole market seemed to reorganize itself around her presence the way water reorganizes around a stone.

* * *

She looked older. Everyone looked older; that was the tax. But Lena had paid a different rate than most. There was a new economy to her movements, a stripped-down efficiency, as if she had audited herself over ten years and removed everything that wasn't load-bearing. She wore a faded trekking shirt with the logo of an outfitter that had gone bankrupt, and she carried a coil of rope over one shoulder with the casualness of a farmer carrying a sack of grain, and she was laughing at something Kip—the boy must be Kip, Dev had finally placed the name from a porter's gossip in Baglung—was saying, and the laugh was real, and that was the worst part.

Because Dev remembered what that laugh used to sound like directed at him.

There had been a season, once—Manaslu, the year of the deep snow—when he and Lena had been inseparable, when they'd shared a tent and a rope and a private language of gestures and grunts that needed no words, when clients used to book them as a pair the way you'd book a matched set. *The Kapoors of the ice,* someone had joked once, badly, and Lena had thrown a snowball at him. They had trusted each other with their lives in the technical sense, which is to say completely, which is to say the way you can only trust someone before you've learned what they do when the cost gets high enough.

Then Kyrgyzstan happened, and the blacklist, and Lena fell off the map. And Dev—Dev had climbed. That was the simplest version. While she was falling, he was ascending, and the geometry of it had always struck him as obscene: her disgrace had been the exact shape of his opportunity. Every door that closed for her had opened for him, and he had walked through every one, and he had never once looked back, because looking back at altitude is how people die.

Except that now he was looking back, and the view was making him ill.

Lena stopped at Tomas's stall, said something that made the big man throw up his hands in surrender, took the bag of onions from him, and paid the vendor herself with coins counted out one at a time. Then she stood there a moment, looking out at the lake, and Dev saw her face go somewhere else entirely—not sad, exactly. More like a woman listening to a sound only she could hear. Checking, maybe, for weather that hadn't arrived yet. Or listening for a voice that had been silent for twelve years.

He knew which valley her thoughts went to when her face did that. Everyone who had ever known her well knew. The Tsangpo headwaters. The forbidden ground. The place that had taken Eli Halloran and given back nothing, and taken Lena's brother and her reputation's twin casualties in the same season, and left her standing at the edge of it like a woman at the edge of a grave she was forbidden to enter.

And Dev was about to walk up to her and ask her to go back.

* * *

He left the tea house at four o'clock, when the light went long and gold across the lake and the market began its evening contraction. He crossed the square with the leather folder under his arm and rehearsed the opening line one final time—*Lena. It's been too long*—and discarded it, because it was true and therefore unusable. Truth was a currency he couldn't spend yet. Not until she was committed. Not until the contract was signed and the money was moving and the point of no return lay behind them all.

That was the plan. That had always been the plan. Get her committed first, honest second. It was how business worked. It was how his client worked. It was, if he was being honest with himself in the way he avoided being honest with himself, how Dev Kapoor worked.

He found her at the edge of the ghat, where the stone steps ran down to the water and the boats knocked gently against each other in the failing light. She was alone. Tomas and Priya had drifted toward the food stalls; the boy was nowhere in sight. She stood looking west across the lake at the Annapurna massif, catching the last alpenglow, and Dev stopped six steps above her on the stairs and discovered that his mouth had gone dry.

*Lena. It's been too long.*

She turned before he spoke. Of course she did. She'd probably heard his breathing on the stairs, or registered the displacement of air, or simply felt, through whatever animal sense ten thousand hours in the mountains had installed in her nervous system, that someone was standing behind her with bad intentions and a leather folder.

For a long moment neither of them said anything. The lake lapped. Somewhere behind them a temple bell rang, unhurried, indifferent.

"You look good," Dev said finally, which was a lie, because she looked weathered and hard-used and magnificent, like a ridge line after a storm, and "good" was a word for people who hadn't earned the sight of her.

"Dev." His name in her mouth had no temperature at all. That was worse than anger. Anger meant the account was still open. "Someone told me a man from Kathmandu was asking after me at the trekking office. I assumed it was a debt collector."

"It's a job."

"There's always a job."

"Not like this one." He heard his own voice shift registers, slide into the polished cadence he used with investors and ministry officials, and hated it, and couldn't stop it. "Upstream work. Headwaters survey. Big budget, full support, top-line equipment—"

"No."

"I haven't told you where yet."

"You don't have to." She turned back to the lake. "You brought a folder, Dev. Folders mean lawyers wrote them. Lawyers mean someone upstream wants something they can't ask for directly. And you came to me, which means it's ground I know and shouldn't." She paused. "Which narrows it considerably."

The dread in Dev's stomach, which had been with him since Kathmandu, settled into something colder and more specific. He had planned for anger. He had planned for suspicion, for bargaining, for the long dance of negotiation that would let him reveal the destination only after her signature was dry. He had not planned for her to simply *know*, immediately, effortlessly, the way she read terrain—because of course she did, because reading him was just another kind of route-finding, and he had never once managed to hide a feature from her on a mountain either.

"The fee," he said, because he had nothing else, "is substantial."

"Is it?" She finally looked at him fully, and her eyes were the same gray-green as the lake at dusk, and there was nothing in them he could read, which frightened him more than rage would have. "How substantial does a fee have to be, Dev, before you stop asking what's actually up there?"

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, pleasantly. "It isn't. Fairness is for people who can afford it." She shouldered her rope coil. "Tell your client the answer is no. Tell him the guide he wants doesn't take contracts to valleys she's sworn off. Tell him—" and here her voice did something almost gentle, which was somehow the cruelest register she owned, "—tell him some rivers don't have sources you can buy."

She walked past him up the stairs. She smelled of woodsmoke and rope wax and the particular high-altitude cold that clings to some people permanently, and Dev stood on the ghat steps holding his leather folder against his chest like armor that had already failed, watching her go, and thought with terrible clarity:

*I haven't told her about Eli yet.*

That was the card he was saving. The card his client had given him—the evidence, the exoneration file, the thing that could burn her blacklist to ash. And underneath it, the thing Dev himself had carried for twelve years, the thing that had calcified in him like a stone in a riverbed: the report fragment, the extraction record, the single line in an official document that suggested that somewhere in that valley, against all probability, against all the searches and the inquiries and the funerals held without bodies—

*one member of the Halloran party had been extracted alive.*

He watched Lena's figure shrink along the lakeshore path, swallowed by the crowd and the dusk and the first fat drops of pre-monsoon rain spotting the flagstones, and he understood that the speech he had rehearsed eleven times had never had a chance, because the real speech—the one that mattered—was the one he still couldn't bring himself to say.

Behind him, the temple bell rang again.

Ahead of him, in a guesthouse room he hadn't entered yet, a dossier sat in his pack containing everything she needed to know and everything he most feared her learning—and tomorrow, whether he was ready or not, he was going to have to decide which of those two things it was.
