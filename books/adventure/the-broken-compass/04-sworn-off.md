## 4. Sworn Off

The rain found Pokhara two days before Dev Kapoor did.

Lena felt it coming in her knees first, an old ache that had nothing to do with age and everything to do with altitude and the years she'd spent reading weather in her bones before any forecast could. She'd been re-roping the gear loft behind the teahouse when the first fat drops hit the tin roof like applause from a crowd that wanted something from her, and she'd stood there with a coil of static line in her hands and thought: *early this year.* The monsoon was early. Everything bad always arrived ahead of schedule.

She should have known then. She should have read the signs the way she read cornices and cloud banks—the pressure dropping, the air going thick and electric, the dogs in the lane below barking at nothing. But she'd been tired, and tired people see only what's directly in front of them, so when Priya stuck her head into the kitchen that evening and said, "There's a man asking for you at the table by the window," Lena had assumed debt collector or worse, some bureaucrat with a clipboard and a fine to levy for the off-book trek they'd just come down from.

She had not, in twelve years, once assumed Dev Kapoor.

He'd changed. That was the first thing, the stupid thing, the thing her eyes snagged on while her mind was still catching up to the fact of him. The boy she'd roped up with on Ama Dablam—nineteen years old, all elbows and borrowed gear and a laugh that carried across three switchbacks—had been replaced by a man in a field jacket that probably cost more than Tomas earned in a season. Salt-and-pepper at the temples now. A scar through one eyebrow she didn't recognize, which meant it was newer than their history, which meant there were whole years of him she knew nothing about. He sat with his back to the wall, facing the door, the way climbers sit, the way she sat, and he'd ordered tea he wasn't drinking, and when their eyes met across the smoky little room his face did something complicated—relief and dread fighting for the same ground—and Lena understood, with a lurch, that he had rehearsed this moment and was already losing control of it.

"Lena." Her name in his mouth sounded wrong. Too careful. Like he was handling something with a crack in it.

"Dev." She stayed standing. It was petty and she knew it, but standing meant leaving was easy, and she wanted the option visible between them like a drawn line. "You're a long way from your logo."

That landed. She watched it land—a flicker around his jaw, the ghost of a wince. His company's mark was everywhere in Kathmandu now, stitched onto down suits and printed on airport banners, *Kapoor Expeditions* in clean sans-serif over a stylized summit. He'd built exactly what they'd both talked about building, back when they were broke and hungry and sharing a single sleeping bag between camps because neither owned a second. He'd just built it without her.

"I need to talk to you," he said. "Not here."

"Then you've wasted a trip, because I'm not going anywhere with you."

"Lena—"

"There's a back room," she said, because Priya was watching from the kitchen doorway with an expression that said *handle this inside, not in front of Kip*, and because some stubborn scrap of loyalty to what they'd been made her want to hear him out where no one else could watch him fail. "Five minutes. Then you go."

* * *

The back room smelled of kerosene and dried chilies and the particular sourness of wet wool that never fully dries in monsoon country. There was a table, two stools, a calendar from 2019 featuring a mountain neither of them would ever be paid to climb. Lena leaned against the doorframe rather than sitting, arms crossed, and waited.

Dev set his pack on the table. Not a trekking pack—an expedition duffel, expedition-grade, the kind of bag that announced its own budget. He unzipped it halfway and pulled out a folder, matte black, heavy as a brick, and laid it between them without opening it.

"The Tsangpo," he said. "Headwaters. There's a glacial source in a closed valley on the Tibetan side of the border country—"

"I know where it is."

"—that no commercial survey has ever completed. My client wants it mapped. Properly. Instruments, fixed stations, the full scientific protocol. Six weeks, maybe eight. Full expedition pay, triple your standard rates, all gear provided, porters arranged through my channels. And—" he paused, and she watched him make himself say it, watched the word cost him something even through the polish, "—a completion bonus we can discuss separately."

Lena looked at the folder. Looked at him. "You flew all the way here to offer me a job?"

"I flew here to offer you *this* job. Because there's no one else who knows that river system the way you do, and we both know it. You guided those gorges before you were twenty-five. You read water like—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard, a piton driven into the conversation. "Don't flatter me. You didn't come here because I'm good. You came here because I'm cheap and desperate and I'll take work nobody reputable will touch. That's the pitch, isn't it? That's what you told your client. *I know someone. She's finished, but she's finished in the right places.*"

Dev's jaw worked. For a moment the polished outfitter fell away and she saw the boy underneath, the one who used to get that same look before hard pitches—not fear exactly, but the tightness of a man calculating odds he doesn't like. "It's not like that."

"It's exactly like that." She unfolded her arms. "Here's what I want to know, Dev, and then I want you to pick up your very expensive bag and walk out of my kitchen. Why? Why this valley, why now, why does your client care about a frozen lake no one can monetize? Nobody pays triple rates for hydrology. Somebody wants something up there, and I don't take contracts I can't read."

"You haven't asked the important question yet."

"And what's that?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he'd rehearsed in the market that morning, whatever speech he'd polished on the flight in, abandoned him now. What came out instead was quiet, almost gentle, and it went through her chest like a fall line through rotten snow.

"Why do you think I chose *you*, Lena? Of everyone I could have called?"

The room tilted. Or she did. She put a hand flat on the table to steady herself and hated herself for it, hated the small betrayal of her body, the way grief will find any gap in the armor and pour itself through.

"Eli," she said. Her brother's name. First time she'd said it aloud in months; it came out rusty, half-swallowed. "This is about Eli."

"My client has documentation," Dev said. "From the original search. From the aftermath. Things that were never released. Things about the expedition, about what happened up there, about—" he stopped himself, visibly recalibrating, choosing a smaller truth to buy passage to a larger one. "About whether everyone who went into that valley failed to come out."

"They didn't come out." Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it, because hiding it had never once worked. "Four climbers. Two bodies recovered. Two names on a memorial stone in a cemetery in Kathmandu that I visit every year and stand there like an idiot waiting for the stone to say something back. That's what came out, Dev. That's the whole inventory."

"That's what the report says."

"And you're telling me the report is wrong."

"I'm telling you the report is *incomplete*. I'm telling you my client holds pages that were cut from it, and that completing the survey is the condition attached to seeing them. All of them." He slid the folder toward her, an inch, a gesture that was almost tender. "Read it or don't. But I need an answer by Friday, because the window closes when the monsoon sets in, and after that it's next year, and next year the client goes with another outfit."

Lena stared at the folder the way you stare at a crevasse bridge—knowing that weight is the whole question, knowing you cannot know until you commit.

She didn't touch it.

"No."

Dev blinked. Whatever script he'd run in his head, the ending hadn't included this. "You haven't even—"

"I don't need to open it." She pushed off the table and stood straight, and the rage that had been coiling low in her belly all evening finally stood up with her, shook itself out, filled the little room wall to wall. "You want to know why I won't guide you upriver? You want me to say it out loud? Fine. Here it is, Dev, since apparently twelve years and one funeral weren't enough for you to figure it out on your own."

She pointed at the north wall, past the calendar, past the plaster, at the country beyond it that no map in this room could show.

"That valley took my brother. It took four people and it kept two of them, and the river came out the other side carrying their gear in pieces for a hundred kilometers like it was *showing off*. I swore on his grave I would never set foot in that drainage again. Not for money. Not for a pardon. Not for a file full of paper promises from a client whose name you won't even give me. You hear me? *Never.* There is no version of this where I walk back into that valley, and the fact that you flew here thinking there might be—that you thought you could hand me a folder and I'd trade my brother's ghost for a paycheck—tells me everything about what the last twelve years have done to you, and none of it good."

Dev stood too. Slowly. "Lena. If there's even a chance—"

"*If.*" She spat the word. "You know what 'if' does to a person like me? 'If' is what I lived on for three years after they called off the search. 'If' is what I ate for breakfast every morning while the federation was deciding whether I was a murderer or just incompetent. 'If' is a hook, Dev. It's bait on a line, and I have been caught on that exact hook before, and I still have the hole in my mouth to prove it." She picked up the folder and held it out to him. "So no. Take it. Take it and take your client and take your incomplete reports and get out of my kitchen."

He didn't take it. That was the thing about Dev—even ruined, even cornered, even standing in a room that smelled of kerosene and his own failure, he couldn't quite bring himself to retreat cleanly. He reached for the folder and then didn't close his hand, and for three ugly seconds they stood there holding opposite ends of nothing, and the silence between them was louder than the rain starting up again on the tin roof outside.

"Your name's still on the blacklist because of Kyrgyzstan," he said finally, and Lena flinched like he'd struck her, because it was true and he knew it was true and saying it was the cheapest move available to him and he made it anyway. "One completed survey and the client's leverage opens every door that's been shut in your face. Your license. Your insurance. Your *name*. You'd really walk away from that?"

"My name," Lena said, "is the only thing I've got left that Eli gave me. Our mother put it on my birth certificate and he's the one who taught me to answer to it. I'm not selling it to a stranger to buy it back from another stranger. Get out."

* * *

He left. Not quickly—he was never quick at anything he didn't want to do—but he left, easing past her in the doorway with his duffel and his umbrella and his cologne cutting through the chili smoke, and Lena stood in the back room listening to his footsteps cross the dining area, cross the threshold, dissolve into the drumming rain, and she thought: *that's done.*

Except it wasn't done. Nothing was ever done. That was the lesson of her entire adult life, delivered annually, like a subscription she'd never signed up for.

Because she stood there in the empty room with her heart slamming and her hands shaking, and beneath the rage—which was real, which was white-hot and honest and hers—something else moved. Something quieter and far more dangerous. A single sentence, looping, patient, wearing grooves into her:

*Things that were never released.*

She pressed her palms against her eyes until the dark went orange. Eli had been dead for twelve years, officially. Drowned, frozen, taken by the ice—she'd made herself stop needing a mechanism, made herself accept the flat fact the way you accept weather. But *officially* was a word she'd learned to distrust in the worst year of her life, when official documents had dismantled her career with the dispassion of a machine sorting mail. Officially, she had abandoned clients on a mountainside. She knew precisely how much truth an official report could hold, and it was less than a teacup.

And Dev had hesitated. She'd seen it—seen the moment he decided not to say something, the words rising behind his face like fish under ice and then sinking. Dev lied for a living now; she understood that. But he had never once, in all the years she'd known him, been able to lie about Eli. It was the one subject where his ambition had always gone quiet. Even tonight, even running his client's play, something in him had kept surfacing toward honesty like a drowning man's hand.

Which meant either there was nothing to tell—or there was something, and he was afraid of it, and afraid men with folders full of unreleased pages did not fly across a country on a whim.

Lena lowered her hands. In the doorway, Priya stood with a lantern, having clearly heard more than any of them would ever acknowledge hearing, and behind Priya's shoulder, half-hidden on the stairs, Kip—fourteen years old, all elbows, watching Lena with the naked adoration that was going to get him killed someday.

"He's gone?" Priya asked.

"He's gone."

"You okay?"

"I'm fine." It was a lie and they both let it stand, the way this family let most lies stand, gently, like coats draped over shoulders. "Get some sleep. Early start tomorrow—we've got resupply runs in the morning."

Priya nodded and turned, and Kip lingered a beat too long, the way he always did, looking at Lena like she was a route he was trying to memorize. Then he followed Priya up the stairs, and the teahouse settled into its night sounds—rain, wind, Tomas coughing in the kitchen as he banked the stove—and Lena was alone with the folder's absence and the shape it had left in the air.

She should burn the memory of it. She knew that. She'd sworn off that valley the way amputees swear off doors they can't fit through anymore—not because the door changed, but because walking through it hurt more than living without what was on the other side.

But upstairs, later, lying in her bunk listening to the monsoon arrive in earnest, she caught herself doing the arithmetic she'd forbidden herself. Six weeks. Triple pay. A file with pages that had been cut from an official report. A man who had known Eli, hesitating on the edge of a sentence.

And underneath all of it, faint as a needle swinging in a cracked compass housing, the oldest instrument she owned pointing at a single unbearable word.

*Incomplete.*

In the morning, she told herself. In the morning she'd feel the rage again, clean and simple, and it would carry her.

She lay awake until dawn, and the rage never came back, and that frightened her more than anything Dev Kapoor had said all night.
