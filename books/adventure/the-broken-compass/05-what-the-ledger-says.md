## 5. What the Ledger Says

Priya found the dossier on a Tuesday, which mattered to her because Tuesdays were ledger days, and ledger days were the only days she trusted herself to be honest.

The shop below Lena's flat was shuttered for the off-season, its shutters down and its scale rusting, but Priya kept the books there anyway—kept everyone's books, kept her own above all of them, in a hand so small and even it looked less like writing than like stitching. Every rupee in, every rupee out. Every debt owed to the tea seller, every debt owed by Dev Kapoor's outfitting company to a mule man in Baglung who had never been paid. She tracked it all because numbers didn't lie, and because she had spent enough of her life around people who did.

She had come early, before the light fully committed itself, with tea going cold beside her and the monsoon's first humidity already pressing at the windows like a guest who wouldn't take the hint. The ledger lay open under her hands. And on top of the ledger, where no one had left anything last night, sat a brown envelope.

She knew whose it was before she touched it. The paper stock was too good. The flap had been sealed with an actual wax stamp, which was either arrogance or a joke, and with Dev Kapoor it had always been impossible to tell the difference. He'd been thrown out of this building not twelve hours ago—she'd heard Lena's voice through the floorboards, heard the door, heard the particular silence that followed when Lena Halloran decided someone no longer existed—and yet here was his handwriting on the front, block capitals, patient as a surgeon's:

*FOR THE ONE WHO KEEPS THE BOOKS.*

Priya sat very still for a long moment. Outside, a bicycle bell rang twice and faded. Somewhere downhill a rooster was making its case to no one.

She should burn it. That was the correct answer, the answer she could defend in any court she respected: Lena had said no, Lena had thrown him out, and anything he left behind was his problem now, not theirs. She should carry it to the stove in the back room and feed it to the coals and then go back to her columns, where every number did exactly what she told it to.

Her hand was already opening the flap.

* * *

There were three things inside, and she would remember later that she registered them in order of danger rather than order of size.

First: satellite photographs, glossy, recent, the kind that cost more per sheet than Tomas made in a season. The Tsangpo headwaters rendered from orbit—the gorge cut into the plateau like a scar someone had tried to stitch shut and failed. Rivers braided silver. Glaciers flowing in slow grey tongues. And one cirque near the top of the frame, circled in red grease pencil, holding a lake so dark it looked less like water than like a hole punched through the world. Someone had written coordinates beneath it in the same red. *The eye of the river,* said a note clipped to the corner, in a different hand, typed. Priya's skin prickled. She had never heard the phrase before, and she understood immediately that she was never supposed to have heard it at all.

Second: a report. Official. She recognized the letterhead of the mountaineering federation before she recognized anything else, because she'd seen that letterhead once before, eleven years ago, on a document that had ended her own career—a suspension notice, two paragraphs long, that had taken her four years and most of her savings to appeal down to a mere blacklisting. This one was older. Dated twelve years back. The title ran across the top in bureaucratic type: *INCIDENT SUMMARY — HALLORAN EXPEDITION, UPPER TSANGPO SOURCE SURVEY.*

Eli.

Priya had never met Eli Halloran. He'd been gone three years before Lena scraped into Pokhara with nothing but a duffel bag and a reputation in ruins, and in all the years since, Priya had learned the shape of him the way you learn the shape of a country you've never visited—from other people's maps. From the way Lena went quiet at certain river names. From the compass Lena carried and never used, riding in the top of her pack like a splinter worked too deep to dig out. From the single photograph taped inside Lena's trunk lid: two kids on a scree slope somewhere, squinting into sun, a boy and a girl with the same stubborn jaw, and neither of them smiling because they were too busy being certain of everything.

She read the report standing up, because sitting felt too much like settling in.

It was dry the way official documents about death are dry, each sentence wearing a suit. *Party of six departed base camp on 14 September.* *Weather deterioration.* *Fixed lines compromised.* *Two members unaccounted for following icefall collapse; search suspended 3 October due to conditions.* Names listed. Eli Halloran, expedition leader. A teammate named Osei. Three others marked *recovered, deceased.* And then a sixth name, and this was where Priya's breath went strange in her chest, because the sixth name was not marked deceased or unaccounted for.

It was marked *extracted.*

Anselm Reyes. Status: extracted. Date: 9 October. No location given. No condition given. No follow-up, no debrief note, no hospital record referenced, nothing. Just that one word sitting at the end of the row like a coin left on a dead man's eyes.

*Extracted alive.*

Priya set the report down on the ledger, on top of her neat columns, and discovered her hands were shaking—not with fear, she realized, but with something worse. With appetite. Because she understood, in the way she understood arithmetic, instantly and without wanting to, what this page meant. For twelve years the story had been that everyone up there died, that the mountain closed its mouth and swallowed the whole expedition whole, that Lena's brother was simply gone the way weather is gone. Everyone believed it. Lena believed it. Lena had built her entire exile on top of it, the way you build a house on a foundation you never inspect because inspecting it would mean admitting the ground might shift.

And the ground had shifted. It had shifted twelve years ago, and someone had known, and someone had filed it away in a folder with a wax stamp.

Which raised the third thing in the envelope.

A single sheet, typed, unsigned. One paragraph. Priya read it four times, and each time the same line snagged her, the way a fishhook snags regardless of how gently the current moves:

*Completion of the source survey is a precondition of all considerations currently under review. Subject H's record will be revisited upon delivery of final data. No data, no review. This is not negotiable.*

*Subject H.* Not Lena's name. Her file number, essentially. Her whole ruined career compressed into one letter, one initial, the way the federation had once compressed Priya's own worst day into a reference code she still sometimes dreamed about.

They were buying her. That was what this was. Not offering—buying. The pardon wasn't a gift waiting at the end of the road; it was payment, held in escrow, priced in kilometers of forbidden river. And underneath the purchase price, folded small between the report pages where only someone who read everything twice would find it, a photocopy of a fragment—half a paragraph, torn edge, from some internal memo: *...the Reyes matter remains sensitive. Recommend continued containment. Survivor's testimony inconsistent with field findings and prejudicial to ongoing interests...*

Testimony. Present tense implied. Containment, ongoing.

He was alive. Or he had been, recently enough for someone to still be spending money keeping him quiet.

Priya stood in the shuttered shop with the monsoon pressing at the glass and felt temptation arrive in her body the way altitude arrives—not suddenly, but as a change in pressure, a thinning of the air she breathed. Here was the thing she could hand Lena. Here was proof that the story Lena had swallowed whole for twelve years had a hole in it the size of a living man. Here was hope, documented, laminated almost, with coordinates and letterheads.

And here was the price tag stapled to hope's foot: *no data, no review.* To give Lena this was to send her up that valley. Into the gorge. Past the checkpoints, past the boundary ridge, onto the one piece of ground on earth Lena Halloran had sworn off with a fury Priya had witnessed exactly once and never asked about, because some doors you don't knock on.

Lena would go. That was the terrible part. That was the easy part to predict. Give her this folder and she'd be packed by nightfall, rage and grief and love all pulling the same rope, and she'd drag all of them upriver behind her—Tomas, Kip, me, Priya thought, me, my forged permits, my second blacklist waiting at the end of it like a second grave. And maybe they'd all die in the white water and the icefall, and maybe they wouldn't, and either way Priya would have been the one who opened the envelope.

Unless she burned it.

That was the other road, and it was paved and smooth and it smelled like loyalty. Burn it and Lena stays whole. Stays furious at Dev, stays safe in Pokhara, stays leading illegal treks around the edges of her grief instead of into its center. Burn it and Priya protects her the way you protect someone from a diagnosis—which is to say, you protect them right up until the disease kills them anyway, quietly, untreated, in a house full of people who knew and said nothing.

Priya knew about saying nothing. She had a decade of practice. She had falsified a client's summit log once, in another life, inked a lie into an official record with a steady hand while a hypoxic man slept ten feet away, and she had never told anyone why, and the lie had cost her everything except the man's life, which is to say it had cost her exactly what it was worth. She knew precisely how heavy a secret got when you carried it alone. She also knew that the heaviness was not the worst part. The worst part was the way a secret started making your decisions for you—small ones first, then larger ones—until one day you looked up and realized you hadn't chosen anything in years; the secret had been choosing, and you'd just been signing.

She looked at the ledger under the papers. Her columns marched on, orderly, honest, every entry accounted for. She had built that book the way other people built shrines. And now here was an entry that refused to fit in any column: *one envelope, received, contents priceless, disposition unknown.*

Outside, the light had come up properly. She could hear Kip's laugh somewhere up the street, bright and carrying, the laugh of a boy who had never yet watched a decision ruin anyone. Tomas would be at the market arguing over ginger. Lena would be—Priya paused, listening to her own knowledge of her friend the way a navigator listens to a river through hull plating. Lena would be awake. Lena had been awake all night; Priya had heard the floorboards. Lena was probably sitting in her room right now with the cracked compass in her lap, doing whatever it was she did with her grief on mornings after Dev Kapoor came to town, which was to say losing to it slowly and calling it discipline.

If I give it to her, Priya thought, I'm not giving her a choice. I'm giving her a wound and calling it information.

If I don't, I'm deciding for her. Which is the same crime I've already served time for.

The rain started then, softly, the first real rain of the season, ticking against the shutters like fingers testing a lock. Priya gathered the photographs, the report, the unsigned paragraph, the torn memo fragment, and put them back in the envelope with hands that had gone steady again, because steadiness was a skill and she had paid full price for hers. She did not burn it. She told herself that was because burning evidence was irreversible and she was, professionally, a woman who avoided irreversible entries until the figures were confirmed.

She told herself several things. That was also a skill.

Then she wrapped the envelope in oilcloth, and she did not put it in the shop safe, and she did not leave it on the table where Lena would find it, and she did not drop it in the river, which was ten steps away and would have solved everything the way rivers solve everything. Instead she carried it upstairs through the smell of Tomas's breakfast fire and slid it into the bottom of her own navigation case, beneath the spare maps, beneath the false-bottomed compartment she'd built herself years ago for documents that needed to exist without being found—permits, mostly, and one old federation letter with her own name on it.

Her hands hesitated over the flap a moment longer than the work required.

Downstairs, a chair scraped. Lena's footsteps crossed the kitchen, stopped, and Lena's voice came up through the floor, casual as a drawn blade:

"Priya. You up there? Dev's back at the teahouse. Says he'll wait all week if he has to."

Priya closed the compartment. The click of the latch sounded, in the wet morning hush, exactly as loud as a seal breaking on an envelope.

She straightened, pressed her palm flat against the case, and called down, in a voice she kept level by spending something she'd need later:

"Tell him," she said, "we're listening."
