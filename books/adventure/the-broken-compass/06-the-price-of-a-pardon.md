## 6. The Price of a Pardon

Sleep would not come, so Lena did what she always did when sleep refused her: she got up, dressed in the dark, and let the night have her.

The teahouse was quiet at two in the morning, the kind of quiet that pressed against the ears. Downstairs, Tomas's stove had gone cold, breathing out the last ghost-smell of woodsmoke and lentils. Rain ticked on the tin roof in a rhythm too irregular to be soothing, too gentle to be threatening—the monsoon still weeks away, but already sending scouts ahead. Lena moved through the dark hallway by memory, past Kip's door, where a strip of lamplight leaked under the frame because the boy fell asleep reading and never learned to turn things off. She eased it shut without touching it.

Her own room was small and bare: a cot, a nail for her pack, a window that looked east toward the hills she'd been avoiding for twelve years. She sat on the cot with the dossier on her knees and did not open it for a long time.

It sat there like something dead. Like something waiting.

Dev had left it with Priya—slipped it into her map case like a man planting evidence, which in a way he had—and Priya had brought it to her that evening with both hands, the way you hand someone a live grenade or a newborn, and said only, "You should see this. When you're ready." Then she'd walked away and left Lena alone with it, which was either kindness or cowardice, and Lena hadn't decided which yet.

She'd put it under her cot. She'd told herself she wouldn't read it until morning. She'd told herself a lot of things.

Now she picked it up. The folder was cheap, government-issue gray, soft at the corners from handling. Someone had carried this thing a long way, or kept it close for a long time, or both. On the front, in Dev's neat architect's handwriting: *HALLORAN — READ ALONE.*

"Asshole," she said softly, to no one, and opened it.

* * *

The first pages were hers. Her file, or a copy of it—the official record of Kyrgyzstan, the rescue that had ended her career, rendered in the flat bureaucratic language that made a human catastrophe sound like an accounting error. *Guide L. Halloran elected to continue ascent despite deteriorating conditions. Client M. Voss subsequently perished. Guide's judgment deemed contributory.* She knew every word by heart. She could recite it in her sleep, and some nights she suspected she did. But seeing it typed out, stamped, signed by three men who had never stood on ice in their lives—it landed differently every time. Like hearing your own name spoken at a funeral.

Then came the part she hadn't known. A witness statement, dated eight months after the incident, from a porter named Bakyt Sadykov, who had been fifty meters below her when the weather turned. He wrote that he had urged her to descend. That she had refused. And then—a paragraph the inquiry had apparently never seen—that the client, Voss, had overridden his own guide's final call to turn back, that he had unclipped from the rope himself against her explicit order, and that Halloran had spent forty minutes in whiteout conditions trying to reach him before anyone forced her down.

Forty minutes. She remembered them as hours. She remembered the rope running through her gloved hands like something alive and dying, and the wind taking Voss's voice apart syllable by syllable until there was nothing left to follow, and the moment—she had never told anyone this, not even Priya, not even herself in daylight—when she had understood, with total clarity, that he was gone, and turned back anyway, and hated herself for turning back, and would turn back again tomorrow.

The statement said she'd done everything right. It said the client had done everything wrong. It said the company that employed Voss had buried the porter's testimony, paid off his family, and let her carry the whole weight of it alone.

Lena set the page down on her knee. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, something was moving—not relief, nothing so clean as relief. More like pressure behind a dam, old water finding a crack.

*This is what I get,* she thought. *Twelve years later. A photocopy.*

* * *

She turned the page and stopped breathing.

A report header. An expedition designation she recognized the way you recognize a scar without looking at it. *Joint survey expedition, Tsangpo headwaters region, restricted zone.* The date was eleven years ago. Two years after Kyrgyzstan. One year before Eli stopped answering his radio.

Eli. Elian Halloran, hydrologist, the careful one, the one who read books at dinner and laughed with his whole body, who had followed rivers the way she followed ridgelines, upstream, always upstream, toward beginnings. He'd written to her from Kathmandu, giddy, saying he'd found funding for a source survey in a valley so closed the maps still showed blank paper. *They'll never let me back out once I'm in, probably,* he'd joked. *But think of it, Len. Nobody has ever stood at the eye of it.*

Then the letters stopped. Then the company that funded him stopped existing—or rather, folded itself into another company, which folded into another, names changing like clothes. Then the inquiries closed with findings of *environmental hazard, all personnel presumed lost,* and the valley went back to being blank paper, and Lena went back to leading rich people around mountains they had no business on, lying smoothly about her past, keeping her face very still whenever anyone mentioned the Tsangpo.

The official report on Eli's expedition was four pages long. She read it twice. Aircraft last confirmed at a forward camp. Weather event. Communications lost. Recovery operation suspended after nine days due to terrain and season. All personnel presumed deceased. Signed, stamped, filed.

And then, tucked between page three and page four, a single sheet that did not belong. Not a report page—no letterhead, no stamp. A memo. Internal routing marks. A date six weeks after the recovery was called off.

Lena read it three times, mouthing the words, making sure.

*One (1) individual recovered alive from forward camp area during private salvage operation. Subject stabilized and transported. Disposition handled separately. Recommend no further search activity. Recommend no public disclosure.*

One individual. Recovered alive.

Six weeks after the world had been told everyone was dead.

Lena stood up. She didn't decide to stand; her legs simply did it, and suddenly she was across the small room with her palm flat against the cold window glass, looking out at hills she couldn't see in the dark, hills that lay between her and the valley, hills she had sworn on Eli's grave—on the empty grave, the grave with no body in it, just a stone she'd bought because grief needs furniture—to never enter again.

Someone came out of that valley alive. Someone was pulled out six weeks after the search was abandoned, quietly, privately, by the same company that had hired her brother, and the extraction was buried deeper than the bodies.

Which meant one of two things. Either someone had survived and been made to disappear—which meant Eli might have—*might*, the word was a knife turning—or the company had taken one survivor and left the rest, including Eli, on the mountain, and buried the whole thing to protect whatever they'd found up there that they didn't want found.

Both possibilities were unbearable. She held them both, one in each hand, like stones.

* * *

The rest of the dossier was Dev's work, and she could see his fingerprints all over it: satellite imagery printed in crisp color, annotated in that tidy handwriting. The headwaters drainage. The cirque. A hanging serac marked with a red arrow and a note: *unstable — seasonal calving.* Route options sketched in blue. Checkpoint locations. Permit gaps flagged honestly—he was honest about the illegality of it, at least, honest the way a man is honest when he's already decided you'll say yes.

And on the last page, the terms. The exoneration file—this file, the porter's statement, the buried memo, everything—released to her upon completion of the survey. Full payment. His signature at the bottom, and a line for hers.

He'd built it like a contract because it was one. Her name for her brother's ground. Her absolution for her obedience.

Lena sat back down on the cot and laughed once, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. Because here was the arithmetic, laid out in gray folders and satellite prints: somewhere in that valley, possibly, impossibly, there was proof of what happened to Eli. Maybe even Eli. And the only road to it ran straight through the one place she had promised herself, in the worst hour of her life, that she would never go. And the price of walking it wasn't money. The price was becoming the kind of person who could be bought—with hope, which was worse than cash, because cash you can refuse cleanly.

Hope you can't. Hope gets inside you. Hope does its work before you know you've swallowed it.

She thought about refusing. She rehearsed it, actually, sitting there in the dark—she'd do it in the morning, tell Dev to take his contract and his satellite photos and his tidy handwriting and get out of Pokhara, tell him what she'd told him already, that some valleys keep what they take. It would be easy. It would be right. Tomas would back her. Priya would back her, though she'd look at Lena afterward with those level eyes and know. Kip would follow her anywhere, which was exactly why she couldn't afford to be wrong in front of him.

And then she thought about the memo. *One individual recovered alive.* Six words, and they had undone twelve years of careful grief, the grief she'd finished building like a house, room by room, a stone with his name on it, a version of the story she could live inside: the valley took him, the valley is cruel, the valley is closed, the end. Grief is a house, and she had paid for hers in full, and now a stranger's memo had kicked the door open and the wind was blowing through every room.

Because if one person lived, then the story was wrong. And if the story was wrong, then maybe she had mourned a living man. Maybe she had lit candles for someone breathing. Maybe Eli had spent twelve years—

No. She shut that door hard. Twelve years was too long. Cold was too patient. She knew what mountains did to people; she'd made her peace with the arithmetic of it. Whoever came out of that camp alive had come out soon, and recently, and been hidden. Which meant others might have been alive longer than anyone admitted. Which meant the company had known. Had *chosen*. Had weighed lives against liability and picked liability.

That was the thing that made her hands finally shake—not the hope, but the fury underneath the hope. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in some city she'd never visit, a man had stapled a memo saying *recommend no further search* while her mother was still writing letters to the embassy, while Lena was still calling every number she could find, while the family had spent their last savings on a private charter that flew the gorge twice and saw nothing. They had known. They had watched the searching stop and said nothing, and gone on to become whatever they'd become, and now one of their instruments had walked into her teahouse with a contract and a smile.

She should hate Dev for it. She tried. She reached for the hate the way you reach for a familiar tool and found it dulled, worn thin by ten years of distance and the memory of a rope shared on peaks where they'd trusted each other completely. What she felt instead was something colder and more useful: she wanted to know. And wanting to know was the most dangerous thing a Halloran could feel about that valley.

* * *

Toward dawn the rain stopped. The light came up gray and slow, the way it does before monsoon, the sky low and bruised over the ridge lines. Lena heard the house wake around her—Tomas downstairs, banging the stove awake, muttering at it in Spanish; Kip's door opening, the boy padding to the latrine and back with the exaggerated quiet of the young; Priya up already, because Priya was always up already, doing something precise and invisible that kept them all alive.

Lena sat on the cot with the dossier closed on her knees and realized she had not slept at all, and that it didn't matter, and that she had already decided.

That was the humiliating part. Somewhere around hour three, somewhere between the porter's statement and the memo, between fury and vertigo, the decision had made itself, and everything since had just been her catching up to it. She was going to say yes. She was going to walk into the valley she'd sworn off, lead a crew of strays onto forbidden ground, deal with Dev Kapoor and whatever he was still hiding behind those tidy annotations—all of it for a chance, a sliver, a rumor of truth about her brother. And she despised herself for it, cleanly and completely, the way you despise a reflex.

Because that was the betrayal, she understood now. Not going. Going was just geography. The betrayal was this: that after twelve years of carrying him, the first thing that had made her feel alive since Kyrgyzstan was the possibility of getting him back. That hope, filthy and unwanted, had lit something in her chest that grief had kept banked for a decade, and it felt like warmth, and warmth felt like abandoning him—like saying the mourning had been a waste of time, like saying the empty grave had been a mistake, like saying she'd been waiting all along to be talked out of it.

Eli would have laughed at her. He'd have said, *Len, you're confusing loyalty with punishment again.* He'd have said it gently, infuriatingly, and then quoted something at her from a book she'd never finish.

She looked at the contract line waiting for her signature. Then she slid the dossier under her cot, next to the pack, and splashed water on her face, and went downstairs to the kitchen where Tomas was fighting the fire and losing.

He glanced up, took one look at her face, and stopped mid-swear. "No sleep," he said. Not a question.

"No sleep."

"You read it."

"I read it."

Tomas nodded slowly, feeding the stove a stick like it was a dangerous animal. "So," he said. "When do we leave?"

Down the hall, a door opened. Priya stood in the corridor with the map case already in her hands, and behind her shoulder, half-hidden, Kip—awake, watchful, eyes going from Lena's face to the folder and back, reading the answer before she gave it.

Lena looked at the three of them, her crew, her strays, her family she'd built out of other people's broken years, and felt the full weight of what she was about to spend.

"Get the ledger," she said. "We're negotiating terms."
