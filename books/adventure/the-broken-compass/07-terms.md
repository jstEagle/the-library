## 7. Terms

The teahouse Dev had chosen was the kind of place that existed to be forgotten. It sat at the end of a lane behind the bus station in Pokhara, wedged between a tire shop and a shuttered tailor's, its tin roof held down by stones and its walls papered with faded calendars from years that no longer mattered. The owner, an old Gurung woman named Pemba who owed Dev a favor from a decade he preferred not to examine too closely, kept the back room for men who wanted to talk without being heard. There was a table in it, two benches, a kerosene lamp, and a window that looked out onto a wall of corrugated iron. Nothing about it said *negotiation*. Everything about it said *this is where deals go to be buried*.

Dev arrived first. He always arrived first; it was a habit of control, and control was the only currency he had never managed to devalue. He'd spent forty minutes arranging the room—lamp on his side so the light fell toward whoever sat across from him, kettle filled and heating on the spirit stove because hot water made people slower to anger, the dossier squared on the table like a document of state. Then he'd spent another twenty minutes undoing all of it, moving the lamp to the center, pushing the dossier aside, because Lena Halloran could smell staging the way a snow leopard smelled fear, and the last thing he needed was for her to walk in knowing how much this meeting cost him.

He stood at the window when she came.

She didn't knock. The curtain across the doorway shifted, and there she was—rain-damp from the walk over, hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a bootlace, wearing the same battered shell jacket she'd been wearing when he'd found her in Kyrgyzstan twelve years ago, or one exactly like it, or possibly the same jacket repaired so many times it had become a philosophical question. Behind her came her crew, and Dev catalogued them the way he catalogued everything: Tomas, the cook, huge and slow-moving as a glacier, filling the doorway and making no effort to stop filling it. Priya, sharp-eyed, carrying the map case against her chest like a child. And the boy—Kip, sixteen maybe, trying to look like furniture and failing, because sixteen-year-olds who idolize their bosses have faces like open books with the good pages dog-eared.

"You brought witnesses," Dev said.

"I brought my crew." Lena took the bench opposite him without being offered it, which meant she'd already decided where she would sit before she walked in. "They're part of any deal I make. You want me, you get them."

"That was always the plan."

"Was it." She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the dossier, at the lamp, at the kettle, reading the room the way he'd feared she would. "Funny. Your plan looks a lot like a trap with better lighting."

Tomas lowered himself onto the bench beside her with the finality of a man sitting down to stay through winter. Priya remained standing by the door, map case hugged tight, positioned—Dev noted with something like professional admiration—exactly where she could see both the window and the curtain. The boy stayed half in the corridor, pretending not to listen, listening to everything.

"Tea?" Dev said.

"We're not here for tea."

"The water's hot anyway." He poured four glasses anyway, because the ritual bought him thirty seconds and he needed every second he could get. His hands were steady. He'd made sure of that in the mirror that morning, practicing nothing, rehearsing everything. "Lena. Before we start—the file you read last night. That's real. Every page of it. I don't deal in fakes."

"You deal in worse," Lena said. "You deal in true things arranged to lie."

The words landed where she'd aimed them, which was directly under his sternum. He set down the kettle with exaggerated care. *Don't flinch. She's counting your flinches.*

"Then let's arrange them honestly," he said. "Full disclosure, right now, up front. My company is dying. Kapoor Expeditions has eleven months of solvency left, a warehouse full of gear the market stopped wanting, and a name that used to open doors and now mostly opens invoices. The client approached three outfitters before me. Two said no. One laughed. I said yes because I have nothing left to lose except the thing I've spent fifteen years building, and I'd rather spend it than watch it rot." He spread his hands. "That's my position. That's the whole ugly truth of it. Now you know what desperation smells like on me, and you can price accordingly."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere beyond the corrugated wall, a scooter coughed and died.

Lena finally looked at him, and Dev understood, in the way one understands falling, that the look had been waiting twelve years to happen. There was no hatred in it. Hatred would have been easier. What was in it was worse—an accountant's patience, a grief that had done its arithmetic long ago and kept the ledger open out of habit.

"Here's what I know," she said. "You show up after ten years of silence with a contract to the one valley on earth I've sworn off, holding a file about my brother that you've apparently had long enough to get coffee stains on. You want the Tsangpo headwaters surveyed before monsoon. Your client is a name you won't say. And you're offering—" she nodded at the dossier— "*that*. My exoneration. My name back. Twelve years of ruin, undone, if I walk upstream into the place that ate Eli Halloran." She leaned forward. "So before we talk pay, before we talk gear, before you slide that folder one centimeter closer like it's a bribe and not bait—you're going to tell me why *you* care. Not the client. You. Why does Dev Kapoor need *me*, specifically, when there are guides in Kathmandu right now who'd do this for half the money and none of the history?"

And there it was. The question he'd rehearsed forty answers to, every one of them a door, and behind every door the same small dark room.

"Because you're the best guide on this side of the Himalaya," he said, "and everyone knows it, including the people who blacklisted you."

"Wrong answer," Tomas rumbled. It was the first thing he'd said. "Flattery's the wrong answer."

"It's also the true one," Dev said, "but fine—it's not the whole one." He turned back to Lena. "The whole one is that the client doesn't trust anyone else to survive the approach. The source sits behind terrain that has killed every survey team that's tried it. The client needs someone whose judgment is worth more than her reputation. Which means they need the person whose reputation I helped destroy."

Priya shifted by the door. "Helped destroy," she repeated. Quietly. Like a scalpel finding the edge of something.

Dev didn't look at her. He looked at Lena, because this confession belonged to her alone. "Kyrgyzstan wasn't just bad luck, Lena. The route report—the one that got you blacklisted—I wrote the first draft of it. They asked me, as your former partner, for context. And I gave them context. I told them you'd pushed the schedule past safe margins." His voice stayed level, which cost him more than breaking would have. "I knew the weather window had collapsed on you. I knew you made the call any of us would have made with clients' lives on the line. I let them believe otherwise because their belief was convenient, and because you were the competition, and because I was twenty-nine years old and hungry." He exhaled. "That's why you. Guilt is a terrible reason to hire someone. But it's mine."

The lamp hissed. Kip, in the corridor, had gone very still.

Lena sat unmoving for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was almost gentle, which frightened Dev more than shouting would have. "I knew," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"About the report. About your part in it. Anselm—" she caught herself, a fraction of a second too late, and Dev filed the name away like a coin dropped in a pocket— "someone told me, years ago. A ranger who'd seen the correspondence. So no, Dev, you don't get absolution points for confessing something I've carried since before your company had a logo." She sat back. "What I didn't know was whether you'd ever say it out loud. Congratulations. You're braver than you were at twenty-nine. That's a low bar, but you cleared it."

*She knows.* Something in his chest unclenched and immediately re-clenched around a different shape, because if she'd known for years, then her rage had never been about the betrayal itself—it had been about the silence afterward, the decade of him building his empire on ground he'd stolen from hers. And that meant the negotiation hadn't even started yet. Everything until now had been the soundcheck.

"Terms," Lena said.

"Terms," Dev agreed, grateful for the solid ground of procedure.

Priya came forward at last and sat at the table's end, opening the map case, producing a notebook and a pencil worn to a stub. "I'll write them down," she said. "Both sides. No amendments after we leave this room."

"Standard package first," Dev said, sliding into the cadence of a man reading scripture. "Full expedition rate for Lena as lead guide—double your usual, because it's double the risk. Same for Tomas as cook and base manager, same for Priya as navigator. The boy stays behind."

"The boy," Kip said from the corridor, in a voice cracking halfway between outrage and panic, "has a name."

"The boy stays behind," Dev repeated, "because insurance doesn't cover minors on closed-area permits, and if a sixteen-year-old dies in that gorge, every one of us hangs for it."

"He's seventeen in March," Lena said.

"Then he stays behind until March."

"He comes," Lena said. "Term one. Non-negotiable. He runs messages, tends loads, stays roped within sight of camp—and if you argue, we're done, and your client can explain to his board why the survey died in a teahouse in Pokhara over a teenager."

Dev weighed it. He was good at weighing things; it was most of what he did anymore. The boy was a liability on paper and an asset in fact—Kip would carry loads porters wouldn't, climb things grown men hesitated at, and above all he'd keep Lena human, and a human Lena was a predictable Lena. "He comes," Dev said. "Term accepted. Wages for him at runner's rate, paid to an account he controls, not you."

Kip made a sound in the corridor like a kettle achieving boil and then strangled it into silence.

"Gear," Dev continued. "My warehouse inventory: modern ropes, the new-generation cams, satellite phone, two weather radios, medical kit rated for expedition use, and high-altitude tents that actually shed the kind of rain that's coming. Yours stays as backup. Porters—I've contracted six from the Serti side, experienced, discreet, paid triple because discretion costs."

"Your porters quit the moment the gorge narrows," Lena said. "Everyone's do. Term two: if porters walk, we carry, and the schedule bends to our backs, not your client's calendar. You tell them that now, in writing, so nobody's surprised at nine thousand feet."

"Accepted."

"Route authority." Lena tapped the table once, a single knuckle, a judge's gavel. "Term three. On the mountain, there's one voice. Mine. Your instruments, your GPS, your client's coordinates—they're suggestions. If I say a slope is wrong, it's wrong. If I say we wait, we wait. If I say we turn around—" her eyes came up and held his— "we turn around, and the contract pays out pro-rata, and your file burns with my signature on the cancellation."

"That clause could kill the entire—"

"So could the gorge," Tomas said mildly. "Everything kills the entire something. That's mountains."

Dev looked at the three of them—Lena implacable, Tomas immovable, Priya's pencil already moving—and felt the strange vertigo of watching his own leverage evaporate and realizing he'd never actually had any. The client thought he'd hired a guide. He'd hired a weather system. You didn't negotiate with a monsoon; you built your house where it wouldn't reach, or you got wet.

"Route authority is yours," he said. "In writing. With one reservation reserved for later discussion."

"No reservations."

"One," Dev said, and here it was, the last card, the one he'd been keeping face-down against his heart since Kathmandu. "The completion clause. The exoneration file—the original, certified, with the federation's seal—releases to you only upon completed survey. Source reached, instruments deployed, data recorded. That's the deal. That's the *only* deal. The client was explicit, and frankly, so am I. You don't get your name back for attempting. You get it for finishing."

The lamp flickered. Rain had started somewhere above the tin roof, a soft static, the monsoon sending scouts ahead of the army.

Lena was quiet for so long that Dev counted his own heartbeats—twelve, fifteen, twenty—and then she said, "Fine. Completion clause stands." And then, before relief could finish blooming in his chest, she leaned forward into the lamplight and laid her own card on the table, and it was the ace he'd never seen in her hand in fifteen years of partnership and rivalry.

"But here's my term. Term four. The one that rides above all of yours." Her voice didn't rise at all. "If we find any trace of my brother—anything, a cache, a body, a scrap of equipment, a mark on a rock with his initials on it—the job stops. Immediately. Survey incomplete, contract void, and I don't touch your file again. We go home, and whatever your client wants with that valley, he can send the next fool."

"Lena." Dev kept his voice careful. "If we find remains, the protocol is documentation, recovery if feasible—"

"There is no protocol." For the first time, something moved beneath the surface of her voice, something vast and patient as the lake under the serac he'd seen in the satellite photos. "There is no *protocol*, Dev. There's my brother, who walked into that valley twelve years ago and became a rumor. You're asking me to march past his ghost taking measurements. I'm telling you I won't. Find him, and the mission ends, and I will burn your pardon myself rather than trade one step past his grave for my own name." She sat back. "Those are my terms. All of them, or none."

Dev looked at the clause taking shape under Priya's pencil—*any trace of E. Halloran: immediate cessation, mutual release*—and felt the trap close around his own ankle with a click so quiet only he could hear it. Because he knew what was up there. He had known for years. He knew about the extraction, knew about the survivor, knew that traces of Eli Halloran were not a possibility in that valley but a certainty, and that Lena's term four was a loaded gun lying on the table between them, pointed at the entire expedition, pointed at his last chance, pointed at the truth itself.

All he had to do was object. Hesitate. Ask for clarification, propose language, buy an hour—and she'd smell it, because she smelled everything, and the deal would die in this room with rain on the roof.

Or he could agree too easily, and carry the lie upstream like a stone in his pack, and pray the mountain solved what his cowardice couldn't.

He thought of his warehouse full of unsold gear. Of the letter from the bank. Of the client's voice on the encrypted line, smooth as glacial ice: *Whatever it takes, Kapoor. The valley stays closed.*

"Agreed," Dev said.

Too fast. He heard it land too fast, heard the syllables hit the table like a dropped tool, and saw Priya's pencil pause for half a heartbeat, and saw Lena's eyes narrow—not in suspicion, not yet, but in the way a guide narrows her eyes at a sky that's gone quiet too quickly, at a rope that's come taut without weight, at the specific silence that in the mountains always means one thing:

*Something is about to give.*

"Then we have terms," Lena said slowly.

"We have terms." Dev extended his hand across the table. She took it. Her grip was dry and strong and brief, a climber's grip, testing the hold before trusting it, and he understood that she was already climbing away from him, up the wall of her own suspicion, placing protection against the fall she could feel coming and he couldn't name.

"Tomas cooks tonight," she said, standing. "Everyone eats at the house. Even you, Dev. If we're going to lie to each other for six weeks, we'll do it on full stomachs."

She walked out into the rain without looking back. Tomas followed, ducking through the curtain like a ship passing a buoy. Priya lingered a moment, capping her pencil, and gave Dev a look that contained, precisely calibrated, one part sympathy and nine parts warning, and then she was gone too.

Dev sat alone in the lamplight with four untouched glasses of tea going cold, and listened to the rain thicken over Pokhara, and took out his satellite phone, and held it in his palm for a long time without dialing.

Upstream, under glaciers that had not yet begun to fail, something waited that would make term four either the truest promise he'd ever made or the last lie he'd ever tell.

He still didn't know which.

Neither, he suspected, did the mountain.
