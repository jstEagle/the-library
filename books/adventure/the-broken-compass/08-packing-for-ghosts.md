## 8. Packing for Ghosts

Kip had learned the first rule of the crew before he'd learned anyone's name: never stand still in a room where money is changing hands. So he stood in the doorway of the back room at the Golden Yeti, half in shadow, watching Priya's hands move over the table like a surgeon's—quick, certain, and completely unlike the way her hands moved when anyone was looking.

The table itself was a museum of small crimes. A rubber stamp from a district office that had, according to Priya, "burned down years ago, which is the best kind of office." An ink pad the color of dried blood. A stack of blank permit forms she'd acquired through a cousin of a cousin who worked at a print shop in Butwal and asked no questions because he owed her a favor she had never named. And a bottle of glue, because in Priya's opinion, the difference between an amateur forgery and a professional one was that the amateur forgot that real documents got rained on.

"Pass me the seal," she said, without looking up. She knew it was Kip. She always knew. "Third drawer. Behind the batteries."

Kip fished it out. The seal was brass, heavy for its size, warm from the drawer. It fit into his palm like a secret. "This is the one that says we're a timber survey?"

"This one says we're an *approved* timber survey," Priya corrected, pressing it into the ink, then onto the form with a rolling motion she'd clearly practiced a thousand times. "There's a difference. The approved ones have a registration number. Registration numbers are what soldiers read. Soldiers don't read words. Words take time, and soldiers are paid to be impatient."

"Where'd you learn all this?"

She paused, the seal hovering. For a moment Kip thought she wouldn't answer, and he braced for one of her deflections—the dry jokes, the sudden interest in something across the room. But she just set the seal down and looked at him with an expression he couldn't read, something old and tired living behind her eyes.

"I learned it the way everyone learns things," she said. "By needing to."

Then she smiled, quick and crooked, and the moment closed like water over a stone. "Now hold this form flat. And Kip—if Lena asks, these permits have been legitimate since the day we got them. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Liar," she said approvingly, and went back to work.

* * *

The next morning, the courtyard behind the Golden Yeti looked like a bazaar had exploded into organized piles. Tomas presided over the food stores with the grim majesty of a general reviewing troops, weighing rice into canvas sacks and muttering calculations under his breath. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that had once been black and was now the color of wet sand, and forearms that could knead dough or break a jaw, depending on the day's requirements.

"Kip. Boy. Come here."

Kip came. Tomas handed him a sack of tsampa and pointed at a scale. "Weigh. Write. If the number ends in zero, you did it wrong. Nothing in the mountains weighs a round number."

"That's not true."

"It is absolutely true, and if you argue with me I will make you carry the butter." Tomas's threats were always specific and always about butter, which Kip had learned was the most feared cargo in the entire operation—fifty kilos of it, wrapped in oiled cloth, worth more per kilo than the clients' camera gear and twice as likely to be ruined by carelessness.

Kip weighed and wrote. Around them the courtyard filled: tents going up on the far side so Dev's porters could see the crew's equipment and be appropriately humbled; muleteers arguing with Priya about rates; Dev Kapoor himself standing at the gate with his satellite phone pressed to his ear, speaking in the clipped English of conference calls, his expensive jacket somehow immaculate despite the dust of Pokhara.

Kip watched him while he worked. Dev was not what he'd expected. He'd imagined Lena's old rival as some kind of villain from a story—sleek, cold, all edges. But Dev mostly looked tired, and when he thought no one was watching, he looked at Lena the way people look at a fire they once burned themselves on. Kip had seen that look before, on the faces of men in the teahouses who talked about mountains they'd lost friends to. It wasn't hate. It was something worse.

"You're staring," Tomas said, without looking up from his ledger.

"I'm observing."

"You're staring. Staring is what you do when you want something you can't have. Observing is what you do when you want something you can." Tomas finally looked at him, and his eyes were kind in that brutal way of his. "You want to go on this trek. You've wanted it since Dev walked through the gate. So stop staring and go make yourself useful where it counts. Lena's in the storeroom. She needs someone with small hands and a good memory."

* * *

The storeroom was the coolest place in the Golden Yeti, a stone-floored chamber that smelled of kerosene, dried meat, and years of other people's expeditions. Lena had been in there since dawn. Kip found her sitting cross-legged on the floor, surrounded by gear in a state of organization that looked like chaos but wasn't—Kip had learned to read it. Everything was sorted by weight and by need. The things you might need in an hour went on top. The things that would keep you alive went where your hands could find them in the dark.

"Small hands," Lena said, by way of greeting. "Tomas sent you?"

"Tomas sent me."

"Good. There's a rope bag in the corner, the red one. Inside it there should be six carabiners, three slings, and a prusik cord. Count them and tell me what's missing."

Kip counted. "Five carabiners. One sling."

"Where's the missing carabiner?"

Kip hesitated. He knew where it was. He'd had it for two weeks, ever since he'd found it in the bottom of the gear crate, a worn aluminum oval with a gate that still sprang true. He'd been carrying it in his jacket pocket, turning it over and over, because it was the first piece of climbing gear that had ever felt like it belonged to him and not to the crew.

Lena watched him hesitate, and he watched her watch him, and something passed between them that was not anger and not quite forgiveness but was adjacent to both.

"Pocket," she said. Not a question.

Kip dug it out and set it on the floor between them, ashamed in a way that made his ears burn.

Lena picked it up. She didn't scold him. She turned it over in her fingers, and her thumb found a spot on the spine where the aluminum was worn smooth, and Kip saw something move through her face like a cloud crossing the sun—there and gone, but it changed the light.

"Gate's still true," she said quietly. "Good gate. You keep it."

"What?"

"You keep it. It's yours now. But you check the gate every time you use it, every single time, because the day you stop checking is the day the gate fails. That's the rule. Gear doesn't kill people. Assumptions kill people."

Kip took the carabiner back like it was made of glass. His throat was too tight to speak, so he nodded, and Lena turned back to her packing as if nothing had happened, which was its own kind of gift.

He was almost to the door when he saw it.

It was lying on the canvas next to her pack, half-wrapped in a wool sock: a compass, old-style, brass-cased, the kind with a hinged lid and a mother-of-pearl inlay that had gone yellow with age. Kip had seen a thousand compasses. He'd never seen one like this. The case was dented on one side, a deep crease as if it had been dropped on rock, and when Lena picked it up to stow it, the needle swung—and stuck. It trembled, caught, swung again, and stuck once more, pointing at a spot somewhere between north and nothing.

The needle was cracked. Kip could see it from where he stood, a fine hairline fracture running through the magnetized steel, and the needle moved like a man with a broken leg trying to run.

Lena wrapped it back in the sock with a gentleness that didn't match anything else about her hands. Those hands could haul a man out of a crevasse, could strike a flint in a storm, could throw Tomas out of a card game when he was cheating. But they touched the compass like it might wake up.

"Whose is that?" Kip asked, before he could stop himself.

The storeroom went very quiet. Outside, he could hear the muleteers arguing, and Dev's phone voice, and Tomas's ledger scratching. In here there was only Lena, sitting in a square of dusty light, holding a broken compass in a wool sock.

"My brother's," she said.

Kip knew the shape of the story the way he knew the shape of the mountains—everybody in the crew knew, the way families know things without saying them. Eli. The valley. The expedition that went in and didn't come out. The reason Lena wouldn't take certain jobs, wouldn't cross certain ridgelines, wouldn't say certain names out loud. Kip had been eleven when he'd started running errands for the crew, and even then he'd understood that there was a room in Lena Halloran that no one entered, and the compass was probably the key to it.

"He carried it on every route," Lena said. She wasn't looking at Kip anymore. She was looking at the compass. "He used to say a compass doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. He thought that was the whole secret to everything." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He was seventeen when he figured that out. Smarter than me at seventeen. Smarter than me at twenty-five, probably."

The needle caught again as she turned the case. Stuck between two directions, trembling, unable to commit.

"It's cracked," Kip said. "The needle."

"I know."

"Does it still work?"

Lena looked up at him then, and Kip saw something he had never seen in her face in all his years of watching her from doorways and pack lines and the edges of fires. She looked, for one unguarded second, exactly her age. Tired. Frightened. A woman in a cool dark room holding a broken thing that used to point the way home.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the honest answer, Kip. I don't know if it works. I haven't been able to check, because checking means trusting it, and I can't—" She stopped. She closed her hand around the compass, and when she opened her eyes again the moment was over, packed away, and Lena Halloran the guide was back, the one who could lie to rangers and out-stubborn storms. "It goes in the top lid of my pack. That's all you need to know. Hand me that roll of tape."

Kip handed her the tape. He watched her seal the compass into the lid pocket, watched her press the tape down flat with her thumb, once, twice, like she was taking a pulse.

"Kip."

"Yeah."

"You're the youngest hire on this expedition. Officially, that means you run messages, you help Tomas, you stay within sight of camp. Unofficially—" she looked at him, and her eyes were dry and steady and absolutely serious, "—unofficially, it means you're one of us, and one of us means you do what keeps the family alive, not what keeps you comfortable. There may be a day on this river when those two things aren't the same. I need to know, right now, before we take a single step upstream, that you understand the difference."

Kip thought about it. He was fourteen, and he had spent his whole life being the kid, the runner, the one who carried messages and stayed behind. And here was Lena Halloran, the woman he'd have followed into a landslide, telling him he was one of them, and asking him to prove it.

"I understand," he said. And because it was Lena, and because she had just shown him the broken compass, he added the true thing: "I'm scared."

Something in her face loosened. "Good," she said. "Scared means you're paying attention. Brave and stupid die in the same gullies. Scared and careful come home." She stood, shouldered the pack, and the brass compass rode in the top lid over her heart. "Now go tell Tomas his butter is being loaded wrong. And Kip—"

"Yeah?"

"Don't tell Dev about the compass."

* * *

They left Pokhara two days later, at four in the morning, in the blue-black hour when the town belonged to dogs and bakers and people leaving on expeditions. Kip rode in the back of the supply truck between a crate of kerosene and Tomas, who slept sitting up with his arms crossed, a trick Kip had never managed. Ahead of them the convoy stretched out—Priya's mule train, Dev's hired porters with their brand-new packs, and at the front, in the cab, Lena and Dev, silhouettes against the windshield, not speaking.

The road climbed. The air came through the truck's slats smelling of pine and diesel and the green wet promise of the coming rain. Kip watched the mountains assemble themselves out of the dark, range behind range, the snowfields catching the first light like coals catching flame, and he felt the feeling he always felt and never said out loud: that he was the luckiest boy in the world, and that luck was a debt.

At the first tea stop, Priya appeared at his elbow with a cup of milk tea and a look on her face.

"Dev's porters," she said, low. "Three of them aren't porters. I checked the manifest. They came on at the last minute, names I don't recognize, and they're carrying more ammunition than rice."

Kip's stomach dropped. "Soldiers?"

"Or worse. Private." She sipped her tea, eyes on the convoy, on the new men laughing at something by the mules, on Dev's immaculate back as he talked on his satellite phone at the edge of camp. "Either way, Lena doesn't know yet. And I haven't decided whether to tell her before or after the first checkpoint."

She drained her tea and walked away, leaving Kip standing in the cold with the cup in his hands, watching the convoy wake up around him, and thinking about a cracked needle in a brass case, trembling between two directions, unable to commit.
