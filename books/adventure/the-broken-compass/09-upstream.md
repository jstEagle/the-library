## 9. Upstream

The gorge announced itself before they saw it, the way grief does—a sound beneath other sounds, a pressure in the chest you couldn't quite name. For two days they had climbed through pine and rhododendron, through villages where children ran alongside the mules shouting questions about where the foreigners were going, and then on the third morning the trees simply gave up, the valley walls closed in like hands folding, and there it was: the river.

Lena stood at the lip of the first gorge narrows and felt something old turn over inside her. The Tsangpo's headwaters here were not yet the monster of the maps downstream—this was the river's childhood, all noise and temper, white water braiding over granite shelves, throwing spray twenty feet into air that smelled of wet stone and glacier flour. The walls rose sheer on either side, two thousand feet of gray-green schist streaked with quartz veins that caught the early light like scars. Somewhere above, unseen, the ice fields fed it. Somewhere ahead, past checkpoints and closed valleys and twelve years of silence, it gathered itself toward the source she had sworn never to seek.

She had broken her oath three days ago at a teahouse table, shaking Dev Kapoor's hand because hope was a debt collector and always found you eventually. She still hadn't decided whether to forgive herself for it.

"Beautiful," Dev said beside her, and she flinched because she hadn't heard him come up. He was wearing brand-new synthetic layers in colors that didn't exist in nature, and he had his tablet out already, cradling it against the glare. "Look at the discharge volume. You can see the seasonal pulse from orbit."

"I can see it from here," Lena said. "It's called looking."

He smiled the way he smiled in boardrooms, patient with the rustic. "I meant the data."

"The data doesn't tell you which shelf is rotten." She turned away from him and put two fingers in her mouth and whistled—the sharp descending note her crew knew meant *eyes up, move soon*. Behind them the convoy stirred: Tomas breaking camp with the economy of a man who'd done it ten thousand times, Priya crouched over her battered paper maps with a pencil stub behind her ear, Kip jogging the line of mules with that restless teenage energy that made him useful and exhausting in equal measure. Six porters from the last village, hired at rates Dev's money had doubled without asking anyone whether doubling was wise.

Lena watched Dev watch them, and watched him not understand what he was seeing. That was the thing about polished outfitters, she thought. They could buy gear that cost more than a house and still not know how to read a morning.

* * *

The gorge narrowed by midday until the trail became a ledge chiseled into the cliff face, wide enough for one mule and its shadow and nothing else. Below, the river thundered through a slot no wider than a road; above, the wall went up and up until it was more weather than stone. Lena led, as she always led, moving at the pace of the slowest animal, reading the rock under her boots the way other people read faces.

She stopped at a bend where the ledge dipped through a seep. Water wept across the trail in a sheet maybe ten meters long, feeding a skin of ice at the edges even in this season, and beyond it the ledge pinched against an overhang where the rock had spalled off in plates the size of doors.

"Problem," she called back, and the word traveled down the line the way it was supposed to, each person passing it to the next so that by the time it reached the porters it was already understood as *stop, hold the animals, wait*.

Dev came forward anyway, picking his way fast, too fast, his boots—new, stiff, Vibram-lugged—skating slightly on the wet schist. He arrived breathing hard and held up the tablet like a talisman. "There's a bypass. Look." He turned the screen to her: a satellite image, high resolution, the gorge rendered in false color. A dotted line climbed the south wall. "Old survey track. Switchbacks up to a bench system, rejoins the river six kilometers down. Adds half a day."

"Or," Lena said, "we cross the seep."

"Cross it? It's ice."

"It's ten meters of wet rock with ice on the margins. We rope the mules one at a time, porters hand-line the loads, we're through in forty minutes." She looked at him over the screen. "That bypass is a decade-old line drawn by someone who never walked it. Switchbacks on that wall, in this rock, after monsoon? Half the steps won't exist anymore."

"The imagery is current."

"The imagery is from above. Everything important happens at ground level." She kept her voice flat, professional, the voice she used with clients who wanted to argue about altitude. "You hired me to make route calls. This is me making one."

For a moment something moved behind Dev's eyes—not anger exactly, but the specific frustration of a man whose instruments keep telling him one thing while the world insists on another. He looked at the seep, at the ice, at the drop below where the river chewed white foam against black stone. Then he looked at his crew behind him—two junior guides he'd brought from Kathmandu, both competent, both watching their boss get contradicted by a woman in a patched jacket.

"Forty minutes," he said finally. "If anyone slips, we take the bypass."

"No one will slip," Lena said, and hated herself a little for how much it sounded like a boast, because boasts were what had gotten people killed before, weren't they—or was that the story everyone else told, the version of Kyrgyzstan that fit on a blacklist?

She pushed the thought down where she kept it and went to work.

* * *

Rope work was the one language Lena had never doubted herself in. She built the anchor off a flake of quartz-veined rock that she tested with three hard pulls and her whole weight, rigged a running belay across the seep, and talked the first porter across with her hand on the rope and her eyes on his feet, calling the holds before he needed them—"left foot high, yes, now trust the rope, don't look down, I've got you"—and when he reached the far side and turned around with that stunned grin men got when the mountain let them pass, she felt the small clean warmth that was the only prayer she believed in anymore.

Mule by mule. Load by load. The ice squeaked under hooves. Spray came up cold off the river and beaded in her hair. Kip worked the near anchor, taking up slack, his face set in the fierce concentration of someone memorizing everything for later, and once, when the worst mule—the gray one with the bad attitude—balked halfway and started to sidle toward the edge, Lena was on the rope beside it before she'd decided to move, one arm hooked in its pack strap, her voice low and rhythmic, nonsense syllables, the same sound she'd use on a frightened person, until the animal remembered it trusted someone and stepped through.

Forty-one minutes. She allowed herself nothing resembling triumph.

Dev stood on the far side with his tablet dark against his chest, watching her coil the rope with quick, exact movements. When she passed him he said, quietly, without the boardroom in it: "In Kyrgyzstan, they said your rope work was the best in the range. Before."

The word *before* hung between them like the spray.

"Before is a country I don't live in," Lena said, and walked on.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel fan where a side creek came down out of the dark in a series of waterfalls you could hear but not see. Tomas produced dal bhat from equipment that predated every piece of Dev's kit combined—a dented pressure cooker he'd carried across three ranges—and the smell of garlic and cumin pulled the whole expedition together around the fire the way gravity pulls water downhill.

It was during dinner that the friction stopped being about routes.

"We should talk about the schedule," Dev said, unrolling his own map—laminated, annotated, beautiful—across the gravel between the fires. "The client wants the source survey window locked. If we're at the cirque by day eighteen, we have margin for the descent before the monsoon. That means fourteen kilometers a day average from here."

Lena set down her bowl. Around the fire, the crew went carefully still, the way a household goes still when the parents start.

"Fourteen kilometers a day," she repeated, "in a gorge with no trail, at altitude, with loaded mules."

"With good pacing—"

"You haven't seen the next section." She took the pencil from Priya's ear—Priya surrendered it with the resigned expression of someone who'd seen this coming—and drew on the margin of Dev's beautiful map, right across his dotted optimism. "Here the gorge turns north and the walls close to a hundred meters. There's no ledge. The only passage is the river terrace, and the river terrace floods every afternoon when the sun hits the upper ice fields. We move through that section at dawn or we don't move through it at all. Dawn means we camp short the night before. Your schedule dies there."

"The hydrology data says—"

"Your data," Lena said, "has never been woken at four in the morning by a river rising under its bedroll."

Silence. The fire cracked. Downstream, the Tsangpo muttered to itself in the dark, endless, indifferent, carrying snowmelt toward countries none of them would ever see.

Tomas cleared his throat, which was how he voted. "The woman reads water," he said, in his accented, deliberate way. "Twenty years I cook for her. The water tells her things. I don't ask how."

"The client's timeline isn't arbitrary," Dev said, and there it was—the strain showing through, the failing company, the last card, though he didn't say any of that aloud. "There are windows. Physical windows. If we miss the survey window—"

"Then we miss it." Lena heard her own voice go quiet, which was worse than loud. "But nobody drowns to protect a window. That's the term. That was always the term."

Dev looked at her across the fire, and for a second—one flickering second—she saw something else in his face, something older than the rivalry, older than the contract: the face of the man who had once shared a tent with her at five thousand meters and confessed he was afraid of letting people down. Then it closed over, smooth as lake ice, and he nodded.

"Dawn starts then," he said. "Your call on the gorge section."

"My call everywhere," Lena said. "That's what guiding means. You can afford the permits, Dev. You can't afford the judgment."

Kip, at the edge of the firelight, grinned into his bowl. Priya studied her maps with sudden intense interest. And Dev laughed—short, surprised, real—and the sound did something complicated to the air between them, some negotiation neither of them had authorized.

* * *

She took first watch alone, as she preferred, wrapped in her sleeping bag at the edge of the gravel fan with the river talking to itself below. The stars came out hard and cold between the canyon walls, a strip of universe the width of the sky the gorge allowed. From her pocket she took the compass—Eli's compass, brass case worn to gold at the edges, the needle cracked down the middle so that it trembled between north and something only Eli had ever known—and held it up against the stars.

North, mostly. But wavering. Like her.

Twelve years ago her brother had walked up this same river with a bad map and a good heart, and the river had taken him the way rivers take things: completely, without malice, without returning anything. Everyone said gone. The federation said lost. The file said presumed dead. And now a man with a satellite phone and a dossier full of promises had said *maybe not*, and she had sold her oath for that *maybe*, and here she lay on the river's banks listening to it run toward the place where her brother had ended or hadn't, unable to decide which possibility frightened her more.

Because if he was dead—if the letters waited somewhere in the ice the way she half-believed, half-dreaded—then the last twelve years of exile had been penance for nothing. And if he was alive—

If he was alive, then he had chosen not to come home. And she did not know which version of the truth would break her.

A stone clattered on the far side of the creek. Lena was on her feet before the sound finished, knife out, every nerve lit. Nothing. A hyrax, maybe, or the mountain settling. She stood there in the dark with her heart slamming, looking upstream into the black throat of the gorge, and had the sudden, certain, irrational feeling that the valley was watching them arrive—that all the things the river had swallowed were standing in the dark on either side, counting the intruders, recognizing the bloodline.

Below her feet, faint under the roar, she felt it before she heard it: a deep, subterranean shift, the mountain clearing its throat. The gravel fan shivered. Upstream, invisible in the dark, something enormous let go somewhere high in the ice fields, and the river's note changed—deeper, heavier, a new volume arriving.

By morning there would be a flood pulse coming down this channel. By morning the terrace would run brown.

Lena stood in the starlight with the cracked compass trembling in her hand, and understood, with the calm clarity that always arrived too late to be anything but terrible, that the river had just voted on the schedule too—and that dawn was no longer four hours away.
