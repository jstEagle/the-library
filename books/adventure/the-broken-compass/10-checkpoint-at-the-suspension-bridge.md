## 10. Checkpoint at the Suspension Bridge

Tomas Ilic had cooked for armies, for film crews, for a Polish expedition that once ate yak testicles on a dare above five thousand meters, and in all those years he had learned one truth about fear: it lived in the hands. Not the face—faces could be schooled, smiles bolted on like scaffolding. But the hands always told. So when the soldier stepped into the middle of the trail and raised his palm, Tomas made himself think about his hands, and made them do what they knew how to do: hang loose at his sides, thumbs hooked in his belt, the hands of a man with nowhere to be and nothing to hide.

The checkpoint was not supposed to be here.

That was the first thought, arriving cold and clean as meltwater down his collar. He'd been up this valley twice before, three years apart, hauling flour and fuel oil to a timber camp that had since gone back to the forest, and both times the only authority between the lower bridge and the tree line had been a bored policeman in Soti Khola who waved through anything with wheels or hooves. This was different. This was four men in olive drab under a tarp strung between two boulders, a folding table, a sandbagged position with a machine gun wearing a canvas cover against the dust, and a hand-painted sign staked into the scree that said, in Nepali and then again in English, as if foreigners needed the lesson twice: CHECKPOINT. ALL CARGO SUBJECT TO INSPECTION.

Behind him, the mule train had stopped in the order it always stopped—Priya's lead animal first, then the grain loads, then the fuel drums lashed under tarps that were, if anyone looked closely, lashed by someone who understood weight but not timber. Kip was somewhere near the rear with the spare animals, which was where Lena wanted him, out of sight of uniforms, because a sixteen-year-old with no papers worth the name drew questions the way an open wound draws flies. And Dev Kapoor's satellite cases rode mid-train, wrapped in rice sacks, because Dev had insisted they travel as "survey instruments" and Priya had repacked them without comment into something that looked, from the outside, like nothing at all.

Tomas heard her come up beside him before he saw her. Priya moved quiet for a woman who talked so much; it was one of the contradictions he'd stopped trying to resolve years ago.

"Timber," she said, low, just for him. "We're a timber contract. Upper gorge, salvage cutting, storm-fall cedar. The permits are in the blue duffel, third load."

"I know what we are."

"You know what we are *today*." Her eyes flicked across the sandbags, counting soldiers the way she counted switchbacks—automatically, without appearing to. "Four visible. Probably a fifth on the far side of the gun. There's a track coming off the ridge behind us, you see it? Old logging spur. If this goes wrong, I take the mules up through the landslide scar and we're gone before they finish their tea."

"And if it goes right?"

"Then I make tea for everyone and we learn their sergeant's mother's name." She almost smiled. Almost. "Tomas. Look at me. Whatever happens—you cook. You are the most boring man in Nepal. You are a contractor's cook with bad knees and a permit problem that is somebody else's problem. Can you be boring?"

Tomas looked at her. Forty-one years old, forged papers in a blue duffel, a record she'd never told Lena about until Kathmandu, and hands steady enough to re-lash a cargo while soldiers watched. He thought: *this is why we survive. Not the rope work. This.*

"I have been boring my whole life," he said. "It is my gift."

The sergeant came toward them at the pace of a man who had all day and wanted them to know it. Younger than his rank suggested, maybe thirty, with a neat mustache and boots too clean for the altitude, and he walked past the porters without looking at them—the particular blindness of officialdom toward the people who actually carried things—and stopped in front of Tomas because Tomas was the largest thing on the trail that wasn't a mule.

"Contract," the sergeant said. Not a question. A word he used the way other men used dogs.

Tomas produced the blue duffel's contents from memory: the folder, soft-cornered, water-stained just enough to look honest. Inside, three sheets. One was a genuine timber transit permit, bought legally in Gorkha for a real salvage operation that had ended two months ago; its dates had been altered by a hand steadier than his own. One was a letter of authorization from a contractor named Bahadur Thapa, who existed, who did cut storm-fall cedar, and who would deny everything if asked but whose signature Priya had copied from a public filing with the patience of a monk illuminating scripture. The third was a cargo manifest in Tomas's own blocky handwriting, listing flour, oil, salt, kerosene, rope—and, in a line added that morning in slightly different ink, *cedar stock, rough-cut, 14 pieces*, which explained the long wrapped bundles riding the last two mules that were, in fact, survey tripods and tent poles and Dev's precious aluminum everything.

He held the folder out flat, both hands, the way you offered things to officials here. Deference cost nothing. Deference was cheap armor.

The sergeant read. Or performed reading. His finger moved down the manifest and stopped on the cedar line, and Tomas watched the finger the way he'd once watched a crevasse lip under a client's boot.

"Cedar," the sergeant said. "Upper gorge is closed area for cutting. Since monsoon before last."

"Salvage only, sahib." Tomas let his accent do some of the work—heavy, rural, a man from the Terai plains pretending to know mountains. "Storm-fall. Dead wood standing, government loses it anyway. Contractor has the paper." He tapped the second sheet without taking it. "You can call Gorkha office. Telephone works mornings."

It was a gamble and he knew it. Calling was exactly what the sergeant wouldn't do, because calling meant admitting he might need to, and needing to was weakness in front of subordinates. But offering the call was also what an honest man would do, and dishonest men, in the sergeant's experience, presumably hid behind vagueness. So Tomas gave him specificity instead. Names, offices, morning hours. Specificity was the costume that lies wore to look like truth.

The sergeant grunted and turned to the mules.

This was the part Tomas couldn't control, and he hated it with a physical sensation, a tightening across the chest like a pack strap cinched one hole too far. Because the cargoes were wrong. He'd known it since Pokhara. Timber contracts didn't ride with satellite phones in rice sacks and didn't include a teenage boy and didn't smell, when the wind came off the glacier country upstream, faintly of the survey team's diesel generator. Wrongness had a smell. Soldiers learned it the way cooks learned when milk was about to turn.

The sergeant lifted a tarp corner. Underneath: flour sacks, real ones, half of them real because Tomas had insisted—if they were going to lie, the lie should have a true floor to stand on. The sergeant pressed a thumb into a sack, nodded, moved to the next mule. Fuel drums. He rapped one with a knuckle, listened to it ring full, and Tomas breathed through his nose, slow, the way Lena had taught the new porters to breathe on their first big carry.

Then the sergeant reached the wrapped bundles.

And from the rear of the train, before anyone could stop him, came Kip.

Later, Tomas would replay it a hundred times and still not fully reconstruct how it happened—one moment the boy was a shadow among the spare animals, and the next he was trotting forward with a grin like a cracked plate, holding out a battered thermos in both hands.

"Chai, sahib? We made extra. It's a hot walk, this stretch—no shade till the bridge."

The silence lasted two seconds. Two seconds is a long time at a checkpoint. In two seconds a machine gun can be unslung, a decision can be made, a whole false life can be seen through. Tomas felt Priya go very still beside him—not frozen, he realized, but *aimed*, her weight shifting onto her back foot, her eyes finding the logging spur above them, already running the escape she'd promised.

The sergeant looked at the thermos. Looked at Kip—skinny, sunburnt, harmless as a stray dog. Something in his mustache twitched.

"Boy belongs to you?" he asked Tomas.

"Nephew," said Tomas, and heard how easily it came out, and hated how easily it came out, and said it again firmer: "My sister's son. Carries for the contract. Good worker, bad manners."

"Bad manners," the sergeant repeated, and took the thermos.

He drank. He actually drank, standing there in the dust with the machine gun covered and the river roaring below the trail, and passed the thermos to the soldier at the table, and for a moment the checkpoint became what checkpoints mostly were underneath the rifles: bored men in a hot place, grateful for chai.

It was going to pass. Tomas could feel it passing, the way you feel weather change. The sergeant handed back the folder, waved vaguely at the bundles—"Cedar. Keep it covered, road dust"—and turned to wave the next traveler forward, a herder with two goats who had been waiting in the shade with the patience of stone.

Then the radio on the table crackled.

Everyone's head turned except Priya's, and hers turned slowest, which told Tomas she'd been listening for it since they arrived. The voice on the radio was thin and tinny and speaking a clipped official Nepali, and even Tomas—who spoke enough of six languages to order food, bluff customs, and curse beautifully in four—caught the shape of certain words. *Convoy. Upstream. Foreigners.* And then a name, or part of one, and the sergeant's shoulders changed.

He turned back around.

"The foreigner," he said. "Your employer. Where?"

Tomas's mind went white for half a heartbeat—the old army reflex, the blank page—and then Priya's voice slid into the gap, smooth as ghee on a hot pan.

"Gorkha, sahib. With the contractor. Someone has to stay where the telephone works." She smiled the smile she saved for men who liked being managed. "You know how contractors are. Paper stays with the paper."

The sergeant studied her. Studied her the way you study a ledger line that doesn't quite add. And Tomas watched him decide—watched it happen in the set of the jaw, the small shrug of a man choosing the easier arithmetic. Four soldiers, one bridge, a valley full of closed areas, and a convoy of flour and fuel and a boy's chai. Whatever the radio wanted, it wasn't worth the paperwork of being wrong.

"Go," the sergeant said. "Before the bridge wind comes up. After noon nobody crosses."

They went. Nobody hurried—that was the discipline of it, the hardest part. Mules walk at the speed of mules. Tomas gathered the folder, nodded the nod of a man with nothing in his head, and fell in beside the lead animal, and only when the checkpoint was a smudge of tarp-blue behind the first bend did he allow his hands to shake, just once, and then stop.

* * *

The suspension bridge announced itself by sound before sight—a deep organ-note of wind through wire, rising out of the gorge. Then it appeared: a gray cat's cradle slung three hundred feet above a river the color of ground glass, planks worn to the color of bone, swaying in the up-canyon gusts that the sergeant had warned about. On the far side, the trail climbed into shadow, and above the shadow, visible now for the first time all day, stood the white wall of the high country, and somewhere beyond it, though no map would say so, the valley none of them were supposed to enter.

They crossed in the standard way: cargo first, animals in pairs, porters spaced, no one stopping mid-span no matter what the bridge did. Tomas went near the rear with Kip, because that was where he always went, and because the boy was talking and someone had to listen.

"You called me your nephew."

"Mm."

"That's twice now. Checkpoint and the police post at Arughat. I'm collecting family."

Tomas looked at him. Sixteen, all elbows and appetite, carrying sixty pounds like it was a jacket. In another life the boy would have been someone's son in a kitchen learning to fold dumplings, complaining about it, staying anyway.

"A family is not blood," Tomas said. "A family is who stands at your checkpoint. Today four people lied for you in the same minute without planning it. That is more than my actual sister ever did."

Kip grinned, and the bridge swayed, and the river roared its one note below them, and for a few steps neither of them said anything, because some truths don't need help.

On the far side, Priya was waiting with the mules, already re-checking lashings that didn't need checking, which was how she touched earth after adrenaline. Lena stood above her on the bank, and Tomas watched her look back down the gorge toward the checkpoint for a long moment—not afraid, exactly. Calculating. Recalculating. A woman updating a map that kept redrawing itself.

"They knew a name," Lena said quietly, when Tomas reached her. "On the radio. They were asking about a convoy, and there was a name in it."

"Could be any convoy."

"It could." She turned upstream, toward the white wall, toward the closed ground. "But Dev's client has reach, and reach means someone downstream is describing us to someone upstream. From here on, every stranger is a question." She shouldered her pack. "Move fast today. Camp high, past the landslide scar. I want rock between us and that radio."

They climbed. And Tomas, bringing up the rear as the light went bronze on the ice fields, found himself thinking about the thermos, and the sergeant's twitching mustache, and Priya's weight shifting onto her back foot, ready to run with the whole train rather than leave anyone behind. He had spent thirty years feeding people and calling it a simple trade. It had taken a forged permit and a boy's borrowed nerve to show him what he'd actually been cooking for all along.

Above them, unseen, the first clouds of the early monsoon were stacking up over the Tibetan wall like a debt coming due.
