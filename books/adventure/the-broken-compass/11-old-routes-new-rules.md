## 11. Old Routes, New Rules

Dev Kapoor had learned long ago that the best place to hide a lie was inside a schedule.

He sat on the cold stone bench outside the teahouse at Chhukung, watching the light drain off Ama Dablam's west face and pretending to review the route notes on his tablet. The tablet was a prop. He'd read the same paragraph four times without absorbing a word of it—the gorge narrows above here, porters historically refused past this point—and his mind kept circling back to the ledger he hadn't shown anyone, the one with red ink running down every column like an arterial bleed.

Kapoor Expeditions had eleven days of operating capital left. He knew it to the rupee because he'd counted three times last night in his sleeping bag while Lena's crew snored around him in the stone shelter, Tomas's snore rolling through the room like distant rockfall. Eleven days. Once, the company's name had opened doors from Kathmandu to Zermatt. Now the doors were closing faster than he could hold them, and the survey contract was the last thing in his hands worth anything at all.

He heard her boots before he saw her—Lena walked the way she did everything, deliberately, as if the ground owed her nothing and she intended to return the favor. She came around the corner of the teahouse carrying two chipped enamel mugs of tea and set one down beside him without asking whether he wanted it.

"You've been sitting out here for an hour," she said. "Priya thinks you're plotting."

"Priya always thinks I'm plotting."

"She's usually right." Lena lowered herself onto the other end of the bench, leaving a careful arm's length between them—a distance that was itself a kind of language, one they'd been speaking since Manaslu twelve years ago. She wrapped both hands around her mug. Below them the Imja River ran gray-green with glacier flour, patient as anything that has all the time in the world.

Dev looked at the mountains instead of at her. It was easier to be honest to granite.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said, "and then you're going to wish I hadn't."

"That's how most of our conversations have gone."

He almost smiled. Almost. "The company's finished, Lena. Or nearly. I've been selling pieces of it for eighteen months—vehicles, the Manaslu permits, my share of the Kathmandu warehouse. I let go forty-two staff in March. Good people. People who trusted me with their livelihoods because I stood in front of them at the annual meeting and told them we were positioned for growth." He turned the tablet over in his hands, screen dark now, useless. "I told them that knowing it wasn't true."

Lena said nothing. That was her gift and her weapon both: she could make silence so complete that a man fell into it.

"The client's money is real," Dev went on. "The advance alone would clear the debts twice over. Completion payment clears them five times. And the exoneration file—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I told you the pardon was leverage. What I didn't say is that it's leverage for me too. Not legally. There's no clause. But if I come back from this valley with the survey done, with your name cleared, with the biggest first ascent-grade discovery on the Tsangpo headwaters in fifty years—then Kapoor Expeditions isn't a dying brand. It's the outfit that found the source. Banks forgive a lot when there's glory attached."

"And if you don't come back?"

"Then I'll sell what's left and take a job guiding rich tourists up Kilimanjaro, and I'll hate every minute of it, and I'll deserve it." He finally made himself look at her. Her face in the blue evening light was unreadable, which meant she was reading him very carefully indeed. "That's the confession. My ambition stopped being about summits a long time ago. Now it's just about not drowning. You know something about that."

"Don't compare us," Lena said, but her voice had no edge in it, which frightened him more than anger would have.

* * *

They had met, properly met, on a storm-bound ledge below Camp II on Annapurna's south side, twenty-three years old each and too stupid to be afraid. A Japanese climber named Watanabe had gone snow-blind, and Dev had spent six hours holding him steady against the wind while Lena rigged a lowering system out of two ropes, a sling, and sheer refusal to accept the situation. When they finally got the man down, Dev had vomited from exhaustion and Lena had laughed—not cruelly, but with a wildness that had made him feel, for the first time in his carefully planned life, that someone else understood what the mountains were actually for.

For nine years after that they'd been inseparable. Partners, rivals, occasionally more than either and never quite able to name it. They'd built their outfits within a year of each other—his polished and corporate, hers scrappy and half-legal—and competed for clients with a ferocity that everyone in the industry recognized as its own form of devotion.

Then Kyrgyzstan happened to her, and Eli happened to him, and the decade between then and now had been built out of things neither of them said.

"You want to know why I really took this contract," Dev said. "Not the version I gave you at the teahouse in Pokhara. The real one."

"I assume the real one involves money."

"It involves shame." He set the tablet down on the bench between them like evidence at a hearing. "When Eli's expedition went missing, the federation convened a review board. Standard procedure. They interviewed everyone connected to the team—the outfitter, the sponsors, the liaison officer in Lhasa. Me." The words came slowly now, each one pried loose like a piton from rotten rock. "I'd helped Eli plan the approach. Loaned him satellite time. I knew his route better than anyone outside his own team. And when the board asked me whether I thought the valley was feasible in late season, I said—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "I said I couldn't comment on another operator's judgment. Which was true. And also the single most cowardly sentence I have ever spoken, because what I meant was: whatever happens in that valley, don't attach my name to it."

Lena's mug had gone still halfway to her mouth. Steam rose off the tea between them, thin and white in the cold.

"They cleared me," Dev said. "No findings. No fault. And I built a career on top of that clean bill, and every year it got heavier, not lighter, because I knew exactly what I'd done. Nothing. I did nothing. I watched them bury Eli in bureaucratic language—'exposure to unassessed hazard'—and I signed the condolence letter to you and I never once told you that I'd been in the room where they decided not to look harder."

The river filled the silence. Somewhere above, a chunk of serac calved off with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Lena asked. Her voice was level. Her knuckles, wrapped white around the mug, were not.

"Because we're walking into that valley together in three days, and I can't do it carrying this. Not with you. You'd smell it anyway—you always smelled my lies before I finished telling them." He laughed, short and bitter. "You used to say I lied the way other men breathed. Efficiently. Automatically."

"I remember."

"So. That's the whole of it. Failing company, coward's history, one last card, and I'm playing it in forbidden ground during an early monsoon because I don't have any better options." He spread his hands. "Judge away. You've earned it."

* * *

He expected rage. He had rehearsed for rage—he'd lain awake composing defenses against it, the way he once composed pitches for sponsors. Rage he could handle. Rage was familiar terrain; he and Lena had crossed it many times, and he knew its crevasses.

What he got instead was worse. She was quiet for so long that he checked her face to see if she'd gone somewhere else entirely, and when she spoke, her voice was almost gentle, and gentleness from Lena Halloran had always terrified him more than her fury.

"Do you know what I did the week after the memorial service?" she said. "I drove to your office in Thamel. Sat outside in a taxi for two hours working up the nerve to come in. I was going to ask you to help me organize a private search. Civilian teams weren't allowed in the closure zone, but you had government contacts, sponsor relationships—I thought if anyone could get permission, it was you." She turned the mug in her hands. "I never came in. Because I remembered what you'd become by then. The press conferences. The brand. And I thought, he'll give me a beautiful speech about process and patience and letting the authorities work, and I will stand there listening to it, and I will understand, finally, that the person who held Watanabe on that ledge is gone."

"Lena—"

"I'm not finished." But there was no cruelty in it. "I went home and I drank half a bottle of raksi and I told myself it was easier this way. That you'd have said no. That way I didn't have to find out for certain." She looked at him then, straight on, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "So here's what I need you to understand, Dev. I'm not angry that you were a coward. Cowards are common. I'm angry because I built my whole grief around the assumption that you'd failed me, and I never once tested it, because testing it might have cost me the last person I had left to blame besides myself."

The words landed in Dev's chest like falling rock. He sat with them. There was nothing to do with truth except sit with it.

"And now?" he managed.

"Now I think maybe we were both wrong about each other in useful ways." She drained her tea and stood, brushing frost off the seat of her pants. "Your company is dying. Fine. Mine died eight years ago and I'm still walking. So here's my mercy, and it's going to feel cold, so brace yourself."

She crouched slightly to bring her eyes level with his, the way she did with frightened clients on exposed ground.

"If we find nothing in that valley—if the survey completes and the file comes through and your debts clear and my name gets restored—none of it will fix what you just told me. You'll still have been the man who said 'no comment.' I'll still have been the woman who never knocked on your door. The money and the pardon are just weather, Dev. They pass." She straightened. "But you told me the truth tonight, unprompted, with nothing to gain. That's the first new thing between us in ten years. So I'm going to treat it like it matters. Don't make me regret it."

She turned toward the teahouse door, then paused.

"Tomas is making dal bhat. Eat something. You look like a man who counts numbers in his sleeping bag."

She went inside. Dev remained on the bench, the cold seeping up through stone into his bones, feeling precisely the opposite of absolved—which, he realized, was the point. She hadn't forgiven him. She'd done something far more dangerous: she'd believed him. Believed him enough to keep walking beside him into the closed valley, carrying his confession alongside her grief like two stones in the same pack.

Understanding, he thought, was a much heavier thing than forgiveness. Forgiveness ended a debt. Understanding made you complicit in it forever.

* * *

Inside, the teahouse smelled of woodsmoke and garlic and Tomas's relentless, restorative cooking. Kip was showing Priya a card trick he clearly hadn't mastered; the cards scattered across the table and Priya deadpanned that she'd seen the ace go up his sleeve, and Kip went scarlet to the ears. Lena moved through the room checking gear with that economy of motion Dev had spent two decades trying to copy and never quite managing—her hands reading buckles and seams the way other people read print.

Tomas shoved a bowl into Dev's hands without ceremony. "Eat. You're translucent."

Dev ate. Around him the crew argued amiably about rope lengths and mule feed and whether Kip could carry the satellite phone or would inevitably drop it in a river. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who trusted each other without having audited the trust.

Priya caught his eye across the table. Just for a second. Something in her expression—watchful, weighing—told him she'd noticed the change in him, or in Lena, or in the air between them. She was sharp that way. He'd have to be careful.

Then Lena looked up from the pack she was repairing and said, "Weather report comes in at nineteen hundred. If the monsoon's moving early like the radio keeps hinting, we leave tomorrow at first light instead of in three days. Everyone sleeps tonight."

And Dev felt the old dread settle over him again—but differently now, shared, distributed, like weight on a rope team. Tomorrow they would cross the boundary ridge into the valley that had taken Eli Halloran and swallowed every map that dared to name it. Tomorrow every step would be trespass.

He only hoped that when the valley started giving up what it had kept for twelve years, he would be brave enough to say it out loud.

Above the teahouse, the first clouds of the early monsoon were already stacking up against the peaks, dark and patient, coming sooner than anyone had planned for.
