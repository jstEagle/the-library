## 13. Monsoon Watch

Kip had learned to read weather the way other kids learned to read comics—obsessively, secretly, and with a growing conviction that the pictures were trying to tell him something the adults refused to see.

The radio sat on the shelf between Tomas's spice tins and a jar of yak butter gone amber with age. It was an ugly thing, olive-drab, its antenna mended twice with electrical tape and once, memorably, with a strip of prayer flag cloth that Priya swore improved reception out of sheer stubbornness. Every evening at six, Kip wound the crank for thirty seconds and listened to the Kathmandu forecast in Nepali, then Hindi, then that clipped English voice that always sounded like it was reading someone else's bad news.

Tonight the storm outside was already three days old, which was wrong all by itself. Pre-monsoon storms were supposed to be tantrums—one violent afternoon of thunder, then gone. This one had settled in over the village like a guest who'd read the lease.

Kip cranked the handle. The dial glowed its sickly green. Static, then the English voice:

"—anomalous trough development over the Tibetan plateau. Early monsoon onset now forecast within seventy-two hours, possibly forty-eight. Precipitation totals expected to exceed seasonal norms by—"

The voice dissolved into static. Kip cranked harder. It came back mid-sentence, worse:

"—advising all high-altitude parties to descend below four thousand meters by week's end. River levels on the Tsangpo tributaries projected to rise—"

He turned it off.

For a long moment he just sat there in the smoky kitchen of the elder's house they'd been lent, listening to the snow hiss against the shutters, doing the arithmetic nobody had asked him to do. Seventy-two hours to the source coordinates. Forty-eight hours before the sky came apart. The numbers didn't fit inside each other no matter how he pushed them around, like stones too big for the cairn.

He carried the radio into the main room, where the fire was down to coals and the argument was already waiting for him, the way arguments in this crew always were—half-formed, hovering, needing only a spark or a piece of news to land on.

* * *

They'd taken the village elder's front room because it was the biggest space and because Dev paid for things in ways that made refusal feel rude. Maps covered the low table now, weighted at the corners with teacups and a stone. The satellite photos glowed on Dev's tablet, blue-white ice rendered like something from another planet, though Kip knew it was only two days' walk above them.

Tomas stood at the edge of the maps the way he stood at the edge of any conversation he intended to win: arms folded, jaw set, radiating the heat of a man who cooked for people he loved and took betrayal of their safety personally.

"The boy has news," Kip said, holding up the radio. "You're going to want to sit down."

Nobody sat down.

"Early monsoon," Kip said. "Seventy-two hours. Maybe less. They're telling everyone under four thousand meters to get down." He paused, because he'd rehearsed this part, because he wanted to be the kind of person who delivered hard news cleanly. "River levels on the tributaries are going to blow out."

The fire popped. Outside, the snow kept falling with that soft industrial patience, like it was being manufactured somewhere above them by the tonne.

Dev moved first. He always moved first—that was the thing about Dev that Kip both admired and distrusted, the way you might admire and distrust a river. He leaned over the tablet, thumb-swiping to the elevation profile, and his face did something complicated and then settled into the expression Kip privately called the Boardroom Face: calm, reasonable, already three moves ahead.

"Seventy-two hours is enough," Dev said. "Barely. But enough—if we move tomorrow at first light and don't stop."

"Enough," Tomas repeated. His voice wasn't loud. It never needed to be. "Enough for what? For you to stand next to a lake and take your pictures?" He pointed one flour-scarred finger at the tablet. "That water comes off that ice cliff. You've seen what a serac does when the temperature swings twenty degrees in a day. You've seen what a gorge does when the river doubles. I've cooked for expeditions for nineteen years, and every single one of them had a man exactly like you, and I can tell you what happened to half of them."

"Tomas—" Priya started.

"No. Let me finish, because everyone keeps letting him finish." Tomas turned, slow, taking in the whole room—Priya curled against the wall with her maps, Lena in the corner shadow where she always sat when decisions were coming, Kip by the door with the radio still in his hands like evidence. "Three days ago we found a carabiner with a dead man's initials on it, and instead of turning around, we found a cache. Instead of turning around, we went higher. Every sign this valley has given us has said *go back*, and every day we have found a new reason not to listen. Now the sky itself is saying it. Loudly. In writing. And still we stand here looking at maps."

"It's not your call," Dev said quietly.

"No," Tomas agreed. "It's hers." And he looked at Lena.

So did everyone. That was the arithmetic of it, Kip thought—the way the whole equation narrowed down to one variable sitting in the shadows with her hands wrapped around a tin cup she wasn't drinking from.

Lena hadn't spoken since he'd come in. She'd listened to the forecast with her head tilted, the way she listened to rivers through tent walls at night, and Kip had watched her do it enough times to know that she was hearing something underneath the words. Not the meteorology. The other thing. The thing that had brought her up this valley in the first place despite twelve years of swearing she never would.

"Tomas is right," Lena said finally, and Kip felt Tomas straighten with victory—and then she kept talking, and the victory curdled. "About the weather. About the serac. About all of it. If I were leading a normal trek, we'd turn around tonight and I wouldn't lose a minute of sleep."

"But?" Dev said.

"But I'm not leading a normal trek." She set the cup down. "And we're not here for a normal reason."

"That's not a reason," Tomas said. "That's a feeling wearing a reason's clothes."

"Yes," Lena said. "It is. And I've spent twelve years pretending my feelings about this valley didn't get anyone killed, so believe me, I know exactly how much they weigh." She looked at her hands. When she looked up again, her voice had changed—flatter, more careful, the voice of someone counting. "Here's what I know. Two days to the source if nothing goes wrong. One day of margin if the forecast holds. It won't hold—it never holds—but it might hold long enough. If we turn back now, we turn back forever. There's no second window. There's no next year. The client's permit dies with the season, and after the monsoon that valley will be closed until October, and by October whatever's up there will have been under six meters of new snow, and maybe under the lake itself."

"You don't know that anything is up there," Tomas said. "A journal page. A direction. That's not a person, Lena. That's a ghost giving you directions."

"I know what it is." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the word *it*, and Kip looked down at the floor because some things you weren't supposed to witness even when everyone in the room was witnessing them. "I also know that if I walk out of this valley tomorrow, I will spend the rest of my life wondering whether Eli was two days above me, and whether two days was something I could have done."

The room went very quiet. Even the storm seemed to lean in.

"And if you go," Tomas said, softer now, which was somehow worse, "and the bridge goes out behind you, or the gorge floods, or the cliff comes down—and I take Kip home and Priya home and I tell their families why—what do I tell them? That we stayed for a maybe?"

"You tell them the truth," Lena said. "That we weighed it. That's all any of us can do. Weigh it honestly and then live with the number."

"That's not leadership," Tomas said. "That's gambling with extra steps."

"Sometimes it's the same thing," Lena said. "The difference is whether you've counted everything first."

She stood then, and crossed to the table, and Kip watched her look at the map—not at the source, but at the route, the whole length of it, her eyes moving the way Priya's did over contour lines except faster, rougher, instinct running ahead of calculation. Her finger traced the gorge. Stopped at the slot canyon they'd roped through. Continued.

"Porters are gone. We carry double loads. That costs us half a day right there." Tap. "The crossing at the moraine—if the river's already rising, we cross it at dawn, coldest hour, lowest water. That buys us back two hours." Tap. "Above the moraine we're on glacier, and glacier doesn't care about rain the way rock does. If we can make the ice before the main body of the monsoon arrives, the last stretch is actually safer in snow than in rain." She looked up. "It's tight. It's ugly. But it's not suicide. It's arithmetic."

"Whose arithmetic?" Tomas asked. "Yours or hope's?"

Lena held his gaze. "Mine. Hope doesn't get a vote. That's the whole point of me."

Kip didn't understand that last part yet—he wrote it down anyway, later, in the notebook he kept folded inside his jacket, because he'd learned that the sentences he didn't understand were usually the ones that mattered.

* * *

The vote, when it came, wasn't formal. Nothing in this crew was formal. Priya simply said, "I can get us across the moraine at dawn if we leave camp at three," which was Priya for *yes*, and Dev said, "I'll take the extra rope weight," which was Dev for *I need this more than you know*, and then it was only Tomas left standing against the current, a big man alone on a gravel bar while the river rose around him.

"You're all insane," he announced, to no one and everyone. "Fine. Fine. But I'm cooking a real meal tonight, because if we die up there, we die unfed, and that I will not allow." He pointed the flour-scarred finger at each of them in turn, ending on Kip. "And you. Boy. You're on weather watch. Every two hours you crank that thing, and the moment that forecast gets worse—and it will get worse—you wake me first. Not her." A jerk of the chin toward Lena. "Me. Because I'm the one who'll tell her, and I'm the one who'll say it in a way she can't argue with."

"Why would she argue?"

"Because she's Lena," Tomas said, as if that explained everything, and maybe it did.

* * *

Kip took the middle watch himself, because he'd volunteered and because he wanted it—wanted the responsibility the way he'd wanted, at eleven, to touch the climbing rope Lena trained on, believing somehow that the rope would teach him things she couldn't. The house was dark around him. The others slept in their bags arranged around the dead stove like spokes of a wheel, and the storm had changed sometime after midnight—from snow to that fine, dry, granular fall that meant the air above them was brutally cold, which meant, Priya had explained once, that the jet stream was dipping down, which meant the monsoon's advance guard was closer than the radio said.

At two in the morning he cranked the radio, cupping it under his blanket so the green glow wouldn't leak.

Static. Then, faint, the English voice again, thinner than before, as if it were calling up from deeper underwater:

"—revised. Onset accelerated. All parties above four thousand meters advised—advise immediate descent. Repeat, immediate. Gorge routes should be considered closed effective—"

Static swallowed the rest.

Closed. Effective—when? Today? Tomorrow? He cranked for a full minute and got nothing but the ocean-roar of dead air. The storm outside had stopped making sound altogether, which was somehow more frightening than the wind—a held breath, the whole sky inhaling.

Kip sat in the dark with the radio warm against his chest and did the lonely arithmetic again, the version nobody else could do, the version that belonged to the youngest person in the room. Seventy-two hours had become something smaller while they argued. Maybe fifty. Maybe forty. And the decision had already been made, and it had been made by Lena, and it had been the right decision based on the information she'd had, and the information had changed, and now—

Now what?

Wake Tomas first. That was the order. That was the job. He looked across the dark room at Tomas's shape, the broad shoulders rising and falling, the man who would tell her in a way she couldn't argue with.

But Kip had been watching Lena for three years, and there was something Tomas didn't know, something you could only learn from watching a person the way Kip watched her—from below, from beside, from the hungry angle of someone who needed her to be worth following. He knew that if they turned back now, something in her would die quietly, without ceremony, somewhere on the descent between here and the tree line, and that she would keep guiding afterward, keep smiling at clients, keep being brilliant—but the part of her that had walked into this valley would be gone, and it was the only part of her that was still entirely alive.

And he knew, too—this was the part that made his stomach hurt—that knowing that didn't make him wise. It made him dangerous. Because love was a kind of bad data. Because he wanted her to keep going, and wanting was not the same as knowing, and the whole art of leadership, maybe, was knowing the difference, and he was sixteen, and he didn't.

The radio crackled once more in his hands, a single syllable of the drowned voice, and went silent.

Kip looked at Tomas sleeping. Looked at Lena sleeping, curled small, one hand outside her bag even in the cold, fingers half-closed as if around a rope or a compass or a brother's wrist.

Then he did the hardest thing he had ever done, which was nothing. He put the radio down. He let them sleep. He told himself he'd wake Tomas at dawn, at the proper shift change, that two hours wouldn't matter, that the forecast might firm up, that he wasn't choosing Lena over the truth, he was just—sequencing. Prioritizing. Being practical.

Outside, the silence continued, vast and load-bearing, like a snow bridge over a crevasse, holding, holding, holding.

And far upstream, in the dark, the river was already beginning to rise.
