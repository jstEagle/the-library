## 15. What Dev Didn't Burn

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Dev finally unzipped his tent and stepped out into the cold. He told himself he was checking the gear lines, that a man who owned an outfitter company—even one circling the drain like his—still had obligations to his equipment. The truth was simpler and uglier: he couldn't sleep in the same small space as his own thoughts, and the night at least didn't ask him questions.

Above the camp, the glacier groaned. It was a sound he'd grown up with, or thought he had—the deep tectonic complaint of ice under strain, like a giant shifting in its sleep. On this side of the range, though, it sounded different. Hungrier. As if the mountain were chewing slowly on something it had swallowed years ago and hadn't finished.

He pulled his jacket tight and walked to the edge of camp where the porters' fire still smoldered. Tomas was on watch, wrapped in a blanket, and gave him a nod that contained no warmth whatsoever. Dev nodded back. Twelve days upriver and the cook still looked at him the way a man looks at weather he's decided not to trust.

Beyond Tomas, past the dark hump of the gear tarp, Lena sat alone on a boulder with her knees drawn up, Eli's compass open in her palm. Even from twenty meters, in the starlight, Dev could see her thumb moving over the cracked crystal in slow circles, the way you'd touch a scar to remind yourself it was real.

*You knew,* said the voice in his head. It had been saying it for twelve years, in Kathmandu traffic jams and five-star hotel lobbies and the blue hour of a hundred summits. *You knew what they pulled out of that valley, and you signed the paper anyway.*

Dev turned away from her and walked out along the moraine until the firelight couldn't reach him, and there, with the river roaring somewhere below in the dark like a train that never arrived, he let himself remember.

* * *

Kathmandu, twelve years ago. The monsoon had just broken and the city smelled of wet brick and diesel, and Dev Kapoor was twenty-nine years old and nobody—a climbing bum with a good resume and no money, sharing a flat above a tailor shop with three other has-beens-who-never-were.

The call came on a Tuesday. Not from the expedition's liaison, not from the federation. From a man named Osei Brandt, who represented Meridian Resources' "cultural survey division," which everyone in the industry understood to mean the division that went places governments didn't want photographed. Dev had met Brandt twice before, at dinners where the food was expensive and the questions were careful.

"Mr. Kapoor." Brandt's voice was smooth as river stone. "I'm sorry to reach out under these circumstances. The Halloran expedition is overdue. I understand you were... close with the team."

Close. That was one word for it. Dev had been supposed to be on that expedition. He'd turned it down six weeks before departure because Meridian had offered him a logistics contract—mapping access corridors for a survey in a neighboring drainage—and the money had been three years of guiding fees compressed into one wire transfer. Eli Halloran had laughed when Dev told him. *Take the money, brother. Someone has to know where the doors are.* They'd drunk to it on a rooftop in Thamel, and Eli had said something Dev would spend the next decade trying not to hear: *Just don't ever let them make you the guy who locks the door.*

"I can help with search coordination," Dev said. "I know their route plan. I know their caches."

"We're aware of your familiarity with the route." A pause, the sound of paper being squared on a desk eleven time zones away. "There are complications. Two of the missing party carried equipment belonging to our organization. Satellite units. Survey instruments. We would prefer any recovery be handled discreetly."

"You want me to find your gear before the rescue teams find it."

"I want you to help everyone find everything," Brandt said pleasantly, "in the correct order."

They flew him in on a chartered Twin Otter with two men who introduced themselves as "logistics consultants" and never once looked at the mountains. The search base was chaos—families crying in mess tents, helicopter pilots arguing about fuel, a federation coordinator with three days of beard and eyes like a man reading a book he already knew the ending of. And there was Lena, seventeen days without sleep, standing in the rotor wash with a map board under her arm, running search grids off pure fury because the official effort kept getting redirected to drainages that made no sense.

She'd grabbed his arm hard enough to bruise. "Dev. Thank God. Tell them. Tell them the route logic is wrong—they're searching the wrong branch. Eli would have gone high, he'd have gone for the cirque—" Her eyes were so raw and so certain that looking into them felt like touching a live wire.

And Dev had looked at the two logistics consultants, who were watching him with mild professional patience, and he had said: "Let me look at the grid first, Lena. Let me do this properly."

Because he'd already seen the map on the consultants' clipboard. The search grids. The shaded exclusion zone around the upper valley—the very cirque Lena was pointing to—and a notation in a hand that wasn't cartographic: *Assets recovered from Sector 4. One (1) individual extracted alive, condition critical, air evac 0400. No further assets located.*

One individual extracted alive.

Not Eli. The handwriting and the timeline told him that much—the extraction happened on day nine, and Eli's last satellite ping, which Dev had memorized, came from higher ground on day eleven. Whoever they'd pulled out of Sector 4 alive, it wasn't Eli Halloran. But someone from that expedition had been found, and carried out in secret, and the people doing the finding had decided that the sister screaming at the search coordinator about wrong drainages did not need to know.

Dev stood in the rotor wash holding Lena's arm and made the calculation that would define his life. If he told her, she would go straight to the federation, the press, anyone—and Meridian would shut the entire search down within the hour, out of spite or caution or simple institutional embarrassment, and whatever chance Eli had in those first critical days would vanish behind a wall of lawyers. That was the version he told himself, then and for years after: silence as protection. Silence as strategy. Keep the search alive by keeping the secret.

So he said, "I'll get us into the right drainage. Trust me." And he spent four days quietly steering the search toward Sector 4—not to expose the extraction but to walk its edges, to see if there was anything left, anyone left, while the consultants watched him with the tolerant amusement of men watching a dog fetch.

On the fifth day they found the cache. Eli's, unmistakably—his blocky handwriting on the supply manifest, his spare rope coiled with the care of a man who expected to come back for it. And beside it, half-buried in scree, a second satellite unit with Meridian's asset tag scraped off but legible under raking light.

The consultants took the unit. Dev let them. And that night, in his tent, Brandt called.

"You've been extremely helpful, Mr. Kapoor. More than helpful. There aren't many men who could have kept this operation coherent." A pause. "Meridian values discretion. We reward it. There will be opportunities."

"What happened to the survivor?" Dev asked. It was the only honest question he asked that whole year.

"A matter for our medical division," Brandt said. "Goodnight, Mr. Kapoor."

The opportunities came. First a permit-fixing consultancy, then a partnership stake in a logistics firm, then his own outfitting company with Meridian contracts folded invisibly into its spine. Within four years Dev Kapoor's name was on the cover of expedition brochures. Within seven he was giving talks about ethical mountaineering at conferences in Geneva, and the irony was so exquisite he'd learned to drink through it.

And Lena? Lena got the blacklist. When the official inquiry concluded—concluding nothing, explaining less—it needed somewhere to put its failure, and it chose the loudest, most insistent voice in the search operation, the guide who had publicly accused the coordinators of incompetence. Dev watched it happen from inside the machine that made it happen, and told himself the same story he always told: there was nothing he could have done. One word from him and the whole fragile apparatus of the search would have collapsed.

It was a good story. It had kept him warm for twelve years, the way a fire keeps you warm right up until you notice it's burning your house down.

* * *

What he'd never told anyone—not his business partners, not the two wives who'd left him, not the therapist in Delhi he'd seen for exactly four sessions—was what he'd done with the file.

After the inquiry closed, Brandt sent him a courtesy package. Standard practice, apparently: documentation of the "resolved incident," sanitized, for his records. Dev had intended to burn it. He'd actually stood at the sink in his flat with the envelope open and a matchbox in his hand, because keeping it felt like complicity and complicity felt like a weight you drowned with.

But he'd read it first. That was his mistake, if it was a mistake—he'd always been a man who read things first.

Inside: the extraction report. Photographs, grainy, of a stretcher being loaded onto a helicopter at dawn. A name partially redacted but not enough—Anselm Reyes, assistant geologist, frostbite, hypothermia, condition critical. A medical addendum noting that the patient, upon regaining lucidity during transport, had become "agitated and uncooperative regarding operational matters," and a recommendation that he be "relocated and supported under terms ensuring continued discretion." In plain language: they hadn't saved Anselm Reyes. They'd purchased him. His silence was part of the invoice.

And beneath that, a single page that Dev had read perhaps four hundred times since. A field note, recovered, unsigned, in handwriting he recognized instantly because he'd seen it on rooftop napkins and route plans and the flyleaf of every book Eli Halloran had ever owned:

*If anyone finds this — we were not lost. We were moved. R. says the survey isn't minerals. Get this to L. She'll know what to do with it.*

Get this to L.

Dev had sat on the floor of his flat until the candles burned down, holding a dead man's handwriting, and then he had put the file in a waterproof case and buried it at the bottom of a footlocker under old guidebooks, because burning it suddenly seemed like the one thing he could not do. Burning it would have been mercy—for himself, mostly—but it would also have been the end of the possibility, however remote, however cowardly deferred, that someday he might hand it to Lena Halloran and say *I should have told you twelve years ago, and here is everything, and hate me if you need to but read it first.*

He had carried the file through two marriages, three offices, one bankruptcy scare, and now up a river gorge he'd sworn privately he'd never enter again. It was in the bottom of his document duffel right now, thirty meters away, wrapped in the same plastic he'd wrapped it in twelve years ago. Some nights he imagined it radiating cold, like a piece of the glacier itself.

And the worst of it—the part that made him get up and check gear lines at midnight—was that he had stopped feeling like a man hiding a secret and started feeling like a man who simply was the secret. Cowardice, he'd learned, doesn't feel like fear. It feels like normal. It feels like a career. You wake up one morning and the lie is load-bearing and the house stands and you keep the lights on, and only at 2 a.m., on a moraine above a river that ate his best friend, does the structure creak loud enough to hear.

* * *

A stone clattered. Dev turned. Kip stood at the edge of the firelight in his oversized jacket, holding two mugs of tea, clearly having been sent by Tomas and clearly having decided instead to deliver one of them to Dev himself.

"You looked cold," the kid said.

"I'm fine."

Kip held out the mug anyway. Dev took it. The boy lingered, glancing back toward the boulder where Lena still sat with the compass, then lowered his voice with the gravity of a fifteen-year-old confiding state secrets: "Is it true you were there? After? When Eli Halloran's team went missing?"

The name landed like a dropped rock. "Where did you hear that?"

"Lena talks in her sleep sometimes. Priya says don't ask her about it." Kip shrugged, uncomfortable. "Everyone knows something happened. Nobody says what."

"No," Dev said. "Nobody says what." He drank the tea. It was terrible, over-boiled, full of leaves. "Go back to the fire, Kip."

The boy went. Dev stood alone with the mug warming his hands and looked up-valley, toward the black seam of the gorge where the river came from, toward the cirque he had visited once in a helicopter and once in nightmares.

Tomorrow they'd cross the boundary ridge. Every step after that was trespass, and every step after that brought him closer to the moment he'd been rehearsing and avoiding for twelve years: the moment Lena found out what was in Sector 4 besides bodies. What he'd known. What he'd signed.

He thought about the file in the duffel. He thought about telling her tonight, here, in the dark, before the valley made the choice for him. He even took three steps toward her boulder.

Then he saw her close the compass, carefully, the way you'd close a coffin, and he saw her shoulders set in the starlight—that familiar, iron discipline that had once been his favorite thing about her and had become, over the years, the thing he hid from most—and he turned around and went back to his tent.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow, maybe, at the right moment, when the route allowed, when she was ready—

The glacier groaned overhead, long and low, like something clearing its throat.

In the morning, Priya would find the dossier he'd left in Kathmandu—or rather, he'd learn later, she already had. But that night Dev Kapoor lay awake listening to the river run down from the eye of the world, and the last thought he had before exhausted sleep took him was Eli's handwriting, twelve years old and still waiting:

*Get this to L.*
