## 16. Night Talk

The cold came up out of the ground after midnight, the way it always did at this altitude—patient, thorough, indifferent to what it took. Priya felt it first in her boots, then her fingers, then the small warm pocket of air she'd been keeping alive inside her jacket, and finally in her thoughts, which slowed and thickened like cooling wax.

She was on second watch with Lena. This had become a pattern over the past week, though neither of them had arranged it. Tomas took first watch because he slept badly anyway and claimed the stove needed tending; Kip took last because Lena refused to let him take anything else; and the middle hours fell to whoever couldn't sleep, which lately meant Priya, which lately meant both of them.

They sat on a flat shelf of stone above the tents, backs against a boulder that still held a little of the day's heat, facing down-valley toward the river they could hear but not see—a long, low breathing somewhere below the dark, the Tsangpo talking to itself in its sleep. Above them the sky was doing what Himalayan skies did on clear nights: showing off. More stars than seemed legal. The Milky Way so dense it looked less like light than like weather, some bright storm frozen mid-swirl.

Priya counted satellites instead of stars. It was a habit from her old life, from the years when she'd worked dispatch for an outfitter in Manali and spent whole nights tracking GPS pings across a wall of screens, watching little moving lights that were each one a person trusting someone else's judgment with their life. She'd been good at that job. That was part of what made the fall so clean.

"You're doing the thing," Lena said.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. Your eyes go side to side. You look like you're reading something."

"Maybe I am."

"On what? The sky?" Lena's voice was dry as old rope. "You memorizing constellations for the exam?"

Priya smiled into her collar. Steam curled away from her breath and unraveled. "Satellites," she admitted. "Old habit. I used to track them for work."

"I know. You told me. Dispatch, Manali, three years." Lena shifted, resettling her shoulders against the rock. "You told me all of it except the part where it ended."

The words landed the way Lena's words always did—not thrown, just placed, precisely, like a piton driven home with one clean strike. Priya watched her breath fog and thought about how many nights she'd waited for exactly this question, and how many nights she'd been grateful it never came.

"There's not much to tell," she said, which was the lie she'd prepared twelve years ago and had kept polished ever since, worn smooth as a river stone from handling.

"Mm," said Lena.

Below them, one of the porters coughed in his sleep. A mule stamped and settled. Somewhere in the dark a rock ticked as it cooled, contracting by fractions of millimeters, the mountain quietly rearranging itself around them while everyone pretended to rest.

Lena didn't push. That was the thing about her that people got wrong—the ones who only knew the legend, the blacklisted guide, the woman who'd walked out of Kyrgyzstan with two clients alive and one dead and a reputation in pieces. They thought she was hard in the way ice is hard, brittle and closed. But Priya had watched her for years now, close up, through bad crossings and worse nights, and she knew the truth: Lena wasn't closed. She was *load-bearing*. Everything she carried, she carried so the structure could stand. And she never asked anyone else to hold weight they hadn't offered to hold.

So she sat there and said nothing, and gave Priya the silence like a room with the door open, and waited.

The wind came down the glacier in its evening shift change, cold and faintly mineral-smelling, carrying the sound of the ice field creaking somewhere above camp—that slow groan the glacier made when the temperature dropped, like a ship settling, like something enormous turning over in bed. Kip would be asleep with his boots inside his sleeping bag the way Lena had taught him. Tomas would have banked the stove coals perfectly, because Tomas did everything perfectly except talk about himself. Dev was in his own tent with his satellite phone and his sealed face, and Priya had stopped trying to read him weeks ago, or rather she'd read him too well and didn't like what the reading said.

Twelve years. She'd been carrying this for twelve years, and here was the open door.

"The client's name was Marcus Webb," she said.

Beside her, Lena went very still. Not surprised—Priya suspected nothing about her ever truly surprised Lena—but attentive, the way she went still at a suspect snow slope, listening with her whole body.

"He was fifty-one years old. Software money, first big mountain, hired our outfit for Baruntse. Not even the summit push I was on—I was support, base camp logistics, radio. My job was to sit in a tent and make sure the numbers added up." Priya pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "On summit day he called in from the south col. His voice was wrong. I'd listened to hundreds of calls; you learn the sound of tired versus the sound of trouble. He was at eight thousand meters, alone between fixed ropes, and his voice was the sound of trouble. Hypoxia. He'd taken his oxygen off to save battery on the mask heater, some nonsense like that, and he was confused, and he told me he was going to keep going because he'd paid for the summit."

"And?"

"And his guide was forty minutes below him with another client who'd turned around. There was no one to send. There was no one *near* him. I ran the numbers—if he kept climbing, if he summited, he had maybe a thirty percent chance of coming back down conscious. If he came down unconscious, he died. Alone, at night, above the col." Her voice stayed level. She'd rehearsed the levelness for years; it cost her nothing now, which was itself a kind of grief. "So I told him the weather window was closing. I told him the forecast had changed. I invented a storm front out of thin air and fed it to a hypoxic man over a crackling radio, and I talked him down. Forty minutes of lying. I described clouds he couldn't see. I gave him wind speeds I made up on the spot. I told him his wife had called the office and asked me to make sure he came home."

"Did she?"

"No. I'd never spoken to her. But he believed it, because people believe what they need to believe at eight thousand meters, and he turned around." Priya exhaled slowly. "He cried on the radio. He thanked me. He came into base camp the next morning furious about the lost summit, and I let him be furious, and three days later he shook my hand and said it was the best decision anyone had ever made for him."

The river breathed below them. The stars burned their indifferent holes in the dark.

"So where does the blacklist come in," Lena said quietly. It wasn't quite a question.

"Afterward. The company wanted the summit record. Webb's contract had a clause—he'd paid a premium for a guided summit attempt, and the outfitter's insurance and their marketing and their whole season's bookings were built on summit statistics. And I wrote the report." She heard her own voice flatten further, going factual, going far away. "I wrote that he reached the top. I falsified the GPS log—I knew how, that was my job, I understood those systems better than anyone in the office—and I filed it, and it stood for two years. Two years of that lie sitting in a database, wearing his name."

"What broke it?"

"He did." Priya laughed, short and without any humor in it. "He got honest. Got religion, actually, or something adjacent to it—started giving talks about ego and mountains and the mercy of turning around. And in one of these talks, in Kathmandu, he said: *I didn't summit Baruntse, and I've never regretted it.* Someone checked the record. Found my log. Found the discrepancy." She shrugged, a small motion that didn't begin to cover it. "The federation inquiry wasn't really about safety. It was about fraud. And the ugly truth is that the fraud charge stuck harder than the rescue would have. Nobody cared that I'd saved his life. They cared that I'd touched the data. Because the data is sacred, right? The record is sacred. You can lie to a dying man's face but you cannot lie to the spreadsheet."

Lena was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had a texture Priya hadn't heard from her before—or had heard only once, years ago, drunk on Tomas's terrible raksi, when Lena had said four sentences about Kyrgyzstan and then never again.

"They offered you a deal," Lena said. "Didn't they. Sign the finding, name yourself, cooperate, and they'd suspend you instead of striking you."

"Two-year suspension, eligibility for review." Priya nodded in the dark. "And I took it. Of course I took it. And then the review board looked at the full file and decided a falsifier of records shouldn't hold a dispatch license at all, because dispatch *is* records, and I've been unlicensed since. Which means every trip I navigate for you is technically illegal. Again. Still." She tipped her head back against the rock. "You hired a criminal, Lena. Congratulations."

"I hired the best navigator on this side of the range," Lena said. "Who happens to lie beautifully under pressure, which, if you haven't noticed, is most of what this expedition is."

Priya turned to look at her. In the starlight Lena's face was all planes and shadow, the scar through her left eyebrow a pale thread, her eyes holding the sky the way a cup holds water—completely, without comment.

"Your turn," Priya said.

"My turn what?"

"You know what." Priya pulled her jacket tighter. "I just handed you twelve years of the worst thing I've ever done. Fair trade is fair trade. Kyrgyzstan. What actually happened up there. Not the federation version, not the version the other guides tell over beer. Yours."

The wind moved through the scree below them, ticking stones against stones. Lena didn't answer immediately. Priya watched her jaw work once, twice, the way it did when she was deciding whether to spend something.

"You know the outline," Lena said finally. "Avalanche on the descent. Three clients roped, me on anchor. I made the call to move them across a slope I should have waited out. The slide took Dawa Sherpa—our sirdar, twenty-nine years old—and buried him before we could get to him. We dug for six hours. The inquiry said my route choice was defensible. The blacklist said otherwise. Both things are true, depending on which hour of which night you ask me."

"That's not the part you don't say."

"No," Lena agreed. "That's not the part."

She was quiet again, and Priya let her be quiet, repaying the silence with silence, the way you repay a loan. Below them the camp murmured in its sleep. Above them the stars performed their ancient indifference. And Priya realized, with a strange floating clarity, that this was why she'd told her story tonight—not because Lena had earned it, though she had, but because Priya had finally understood that Lena's silence wasn't refusal. It was the same architecture Priya herself lived inside. Weight, held. Door, open. Waiting for someone strong enough to walk through it without flinching.

"The part I don't say," Lena began, and stopped, and started again. "Dawa had a radio. After the slide, when we were digging—there was a period, maybe ninety seconds, maybe two minutes, where his radio was transmitting. Open channel. And I heard him." Her voice didn't waver. It went somewhere else instead, somewhere very controlled and very far away. "Buried people can't usually transmit. The snow packs the mic. But his pack had shifted, and the button was pressed against something, and I heard him breathing. Just breathing. For about a hundred seconds. And I knew where the signal was coming from, and I dug in the wrong place."

"Priya—" 

"No. Let me finish, because I've never finished it." Lena's hands were flat on the stone, fingers spread, pressing down as if the mountain might lift otherwise. "There were two signals. Two probe strikes that read as possible voids—one upslope, near the debris crown, one downslope near the flow line. Standard triage says flow line, deepest deposition, highest probability. I chose the flow line. The radio was saying upslope. I heard him breathing upslope, and I chose the math, because the math was what I trusted, because the math was what I *was*." She turned her head then, and looked at Priya directly, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "We dug him out of the flow line site after four hours. Nothing. Then someone remembered the transmission, and we dug upslope, and we found him at hour six. He'd had an air pocket. He'd been alive. Maybe not for six hours—but longer than he would have needed to be, if I'd listened."

The cold had gotten into Priya completely now, into the marrow, and she barely felt it. "Lena. You can't know that a void under the crown would have—"

"I know what I heard." Flat. Final. "And here's the part that makes it unforgivable, the part I've never said out loud: I didn't misread the slope. I didn't panic. I did exactly what I was trained to do, perfectly, and it was wrong, and a man died, and the lesson I took from it—the lesson I built my whole damn life on—was *trust the math*, because if I admit the math failed, then everything fails, and there's nothing left to stand on." She laughed, one note, no warmth. "And then I meet you. And you sit there and tell me you lied to a dying man to bring him home, and it *worked*, and the lie was worth more than the truth. Do you understand what that does to me? Do you understand that your whole existence is an argument against mine?"

Priya stared at her. The Milky Way wheeled overhead, vast and slow, and somewhere beneath them the glacier groaned its long tectonic sigh, and Priya felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a name for—some wall meeting another wall and discovering they were the same wall, built from opposite ends.

"Yes," she said. "I think I do."

"Because here's what I can't stop thinking, since Dev showed up with that dossier." Lena's voice dropped lower, roughened. "Eli went into that valley with a team, and something happened, and for twelve years I've assumed the worst version—assumed he made a call like mine, trusted the wrong thing, and the mountain collected. But what if it was the other way? What if someone up there lied to save someone, the way you did, and the lie is the reason there's no body, no record, no answers? What if the truth I've been starving on for twelve years is actually a *rescue* somebody covered up?" She shook her head slowly. "I don't know. I'm telling you things at altitude that I should keep locked. Forget—"

"Don't you dare." Priya's voice came out harder than she intended. "Don't you dare hand me that and then take it back. I've watched you carry this crew for five years. I've watched you take the blame for things that weren't yours, absorb the risk for things that were, hold every piece of weight anyone dropped. And tonight you finally set one piece down in front of me, and if you pick it back up and pretend it never happened, I will navigate us into a crevasse field out of spite. I know exactly where they all are."

For a moment Lena looked at her—and then she laughed, a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and rough and quickly smothered, but real. It transformed her face entirely, the way a crack of sun transforms a gray slope, and Priya thought: *there she is. Whoever she was before Kyrgyzstan. Still in there.*

"Noted," Lena said. "No taking it back."

"Good." Priya hugged her knees. "Because I have one more thing, and then we're even, and then we never speak of either confession again until one of us is dying."

"Deal."

"The reason I told you tonight. It's not just fairness." Priya looked down at the camp, at the dim shape of Dev's tent, at the antenna wire running from it like a single black hair against the snow. "It's Dev. I've been watching him since Kathmandu, and I know liars, Lena—I *am* one, professionally, historically—and he's carrying something specific. Not general guilt. Something with a date on it. Something with Eli's name on it. And when we find whatever's up there—and we will, because this valley has been leaving us breadcrumbs like it wants to be found—he's going to have to choose between the thing he's carrying and the thing he wants. And I want you to be ready for that choice. Because I don't think he's ready for it himself."

Lena followed her gaze down to the tent. The starlight didn't reach it; it was just a darker darkness among dark shapes, breathing its slow synthetic breath.

"You think he knows something," Lena said. Not a question either.

"I think," Priya said carefully, "that a man who checks his satellite phone at three in the morning, alone, in a valley that isn't supposed to exist on any map he's allowed to be in—that man isn't checking the weather."

Above them, unnoticed by either of them, a satellite crossed the constellation of Orion, steady and silent, a small moving light carrying someone's encrypted traffic through the cold—down toward Kathmandu, toward offices with glass walls, toward a client who was awake too, waiting, watching his own little lights move across his own dark screen.

Lena was quiet for a long time. Then she stood, joints cracking, and looked up-valley into the black where the glacier climbed toward the hidden heart of the range.

"Third watch in an hour," she said. "Wake Kip gently. And Priya—" She paused, half-turned, the starlight catching the edge of her jaw. "Thank you. For the story. Both of them."

Then she was climbing down into camp, sure-footed even in the dark, and Priya sat alone under the enormous wheeling sky with her heart beating strangely, lighter and heavier at once, and counted satellites until it was time to wake the boy—and saw, just before she rose, one more of them slip across the stars, heading upriver, toward the eye of the world.
