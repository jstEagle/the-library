## 17. The Gorge Narrows

The river changed its mind about being a river.

That was how Lena thought of it, watching the Tsangpo's headwaters compress between walls of black schist that leaned together overhead like conspirators. For two days the water had been broad and braided, a silver argument across a valley floor of grey gravel. Now it was a single furious rope of white, and the gorge had narrowed until she could have spat from one wall to the other. The sound filled everything—not a sound anymore but a pressure, a physical weight against the eardrums, so that conversation happened in leaned-in shouts and silence happened nowhere at all.

She stood on a shelf of rock barely wider than her boots, testing the air the way she tested snow. The monsoon had been threatening for a week—building its towers over the Tibetan plateau every afternoon, dissolving every evening into a false mercy of stars. Today the towers had stopped dissolving. They had come down the valley like an invading army, and the light had gone the color of old pewter, and the first rain had started an hour ago as a fine cold grit on her face that was already thickening into something with intent.

"Porters won't go past here."

Tomas had come up beside her, and he had to put his mouth almost against her ear to be heard over the river. He nodded down the shelf to where the four porters they'd hired at the last village stood in a huddle under a tarp, their faces wearing the particular closed expression of men who have made a decision and are waiting for it to be acknowledged.

Lena looked at them. She'd known since morning. You could always tell—porters went quiet before they quit, the way animals went quiet before a storm, and these four had been quiet since the landslide scar at the second bend. One of them, the oldest, a leathery man everyone called Dorje though she suspected it wasn't his name, caught her looking and didn't look away. That was the courtesy of it. He wasn't sneaking off in the night. He was giving her the dignity of a conversation.

She picked her way back to them and shouted over the water: "You want to turn back."

Dorje shrugged, a whole philosophy in the gesture. "The gorge gets smaller. The rain gets bigger. The pay does not get bigger." He held up three fingers. "Three days' wages for one day's walking. We are not thieves. But we are not dead either."

She couldn't argue with the arithmetic. She'd run that same sum herself a hundred times on a hundred routes, and the honest answer was that the gorge ahead—the slot section the maps showed as a double line with no contour detail, which meant no surveyor had ever been stupid enough to walk it—was exactly the kind of ground where wages stopped mattering.

"Wait here," she shouted. "Three days. If we're not back down, go home and tell no one which way we went."

Dorje studied her for a long moment, rain running off his chin. Then he reached into his jacket and pressed something into her hand: a small bundle of juniper, tied with red thread. "For the gorge," he said. "It doesn't like being walked through. Burn it at the narrow place, or at least pretend to."

She almost smiled. "And if I don't believe in it?"

"Then believe in the man who does." He nodded toward the huddle. "We all believe in something stupid. It is how we stay alive."

The porters turned back. Lena stood watching them shrink down the trail until the rain took them, and then she turned to her own people—Tomas with his arms crossed, Priya already studying the gorge walls with the flat calculating stare she reserved for insoluble navigation problems, Dev checking a barometer on his wrist that cost more than everything the porters owned, and Kip, seventeen and soaked and lit up like the gorge itself was a gift wrapped just for him.

Five of them. Four if you didn't count the boy, which Lena had been trying not to do for two hundred kilometers and had failed at completely.

* * *

The slot canyon began where the trail gave up.

There was no trail, really—there was a suggestion of one, a smear of polished footprints on the rock where maybe herders had once gone through, decades ago, before the valley closed and the maps went blank. The walls rose sheer on either side, fifty, sixty meters of wet black stone furred with moss that offered no purchase and no honesty. Between them, the river ran fast and green-black, and the only way through was a sequence of ledges, chockstones, and friction smears that Lena read the way Priya read contours: quickly, greedily, cataloguing every hold and every place the rock might lie.

They roped up at the entrance. Five climbers, one rope, fifty-meter spacing—Lena on point, then Kip, then Priya, Dev, and Tomas anchoring with the heavy pack and his bad knee, which he'd been lying about for three days and which everyone had been politely not mentioning for three days.

"Rules," Lena shouted, turning to face them on the first ledge. Rain streamed off her hood. "Nobody moves until the person ahead calls the hold. Nobody free-solos anything, not even two meters, not even to look cool. If anyone goes in the water, we don't jump after them—we run the ledge and set the rope. Am I understood?"

"Understood," they said, in four different tones: Priya crisp, Tomas resigned, Dev distracted, Kip fervent as a churchgoer.

The first hour was almost kind. The ledges came in honest intervals, the chockstones were solid, and Lena found a rhythm—*left foot high, trust the crack, call it back*—and heard the rhythm passed down the rope like a song, each voice repeating her words to the next. That was the secret of roped travel that no client ever understood: it wasn't about the rope. The rope was a symbol. What actually held you together was the calling, the chain of voices, the fact that five separate people became one organism with five sets of eyes and one shared language. She had learned it from her father. She had taught it to Eli. She had failed to use it on the mountain in Kyrgyzstan, one bad afternoon, one shortcut taken out of confidence, and a client named Marcus had paid for her confidence with his life.

She put the thought down the way you put down something hot. There was no room for it here.

The rain thickened. By midday it had stopped being weather and become a condition, a grey vertical fact, drumming on their hoods, feeding the river below until it rose visibly—she could see it climbing the walls, a dark stain advancing a centimeter at a time, patient as arithmetic. The gorge was a funnel. Everything that fell on a hundred square kilometers of plateau was coming down here, past them, toward the lowlands. The question that mattered was not whether they could climb through. The question was how fast the water would make the lower ledges impassable, and whether the exit ramp she remembered from the old survey notes—Eli's notes, though she hadn't told Dev that—was still there.

"Point!" she called, and found the next move: a traverse across a slab polished by a thousand years of flood, a smear of granite with a crack at its top edge that would take a hand, barely, if the hand was small and the crack was generous. She crossed it, called it, watched Kip come across behind her.

He was good. That was the thing that kept frightening her. He moved the way she moved, weight low, feet placed with intention, no wasted motion, and he'd learned it in two years of hauling loads and watching her, absorbing her craft through his skin the way he absorbed everything—eagerly, uncritically, as if she were a textbook that couldn't have errors in it.

She knew better than anyone what was printed in her margins.

* * *

The gorge narrowed again in the afternoon, and the world became stone and water and rain.

The walls came together until they were walking a slot no wider than a cart, the river roaring at their boots, the sky a ragged grey ribbon far overhead. The juniper bundle rode in her chest pocket. She took it out at the narrowest point, more for Dorje than for any god, and wedged it in a crack where the water wouldn't take it. It felt absurd and it felt necessary, and she had learned long ago that those two feelings usually arrived together.

The trouble came where the slot kinked.

A chockstone the size of a truck had fallen centuries ago and jammed between the walls, and the route went up and over it—four meters of vertical wet rock on the upstream face, then a mantle onto its top, then a downclimb on the far side. In dry weather it was a scramble. In this rain it was a lie the rock was telling, every hold running with water, every crack weeping.

Lena climbed it first. She took her time, found the sequence, set a sling around the chockstone's crown and fixed a handline down the far side. Then she called the others up one at a time. Priya came over like water finding its level, smooth and unglamorous. Dev came over slowly, breathing hard, his fancy shell jacket slick and useless, and she saw him look down into the river once and then deliberately never look down again. Tomas came over with the grim economy of a man who had done harder things in worse places and simply didn't want to be doing this one. Kip came last.

Kip came last because Kip had insisted on carrying the extra rope coil and the med kit, because Kip never let anyone take weight from him, because Kip was seventeen and made of the two most dangerous substances on any mountain: youth and loyalty.

He was halfway up the chockstone when the rock moved.

Not the chockstone—a block above it, a slab the size of a door, loosened by who knew how many centuries of freeze and thaw and finished off by this rain, this day, this hour. Lena heard it before she saw it, a sound like a knuckle cracking in the throat of the gorge, and her body understood before her mind did. She was already moving along the chockstone's crown, already reaching down as the slab sheared away and hit the stone where Kip's shoulder had been half a second earlier, and Kip—Kip did the thing that every guide dreads and every guide has done, the thing that is completely wrong and completely human: he flinched away from the falling rock, out into space, off the route.

His feet went off the wet stone. His hands slapped granite and found nothing. For one full second he hung in the air between the chockstone and the river, and the river was right there, green and black and boiling, and Lena's whole life narrowed to a strap.

She caught the pack strap. Not his hand, not his arm—the nylon webbing over his shoulder, the strap of the med kit, and it came tight with a jerk that went through her arm, her shoulder, her spine, and she heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize, some animal syllable that wasn't a word. The strap held. Nylon held. Her grip held. Kip swung against the chockstone's face, boots scrabbling, found a smear of friction, and for three seconds neither of them moved, the two of them wired together by a strip of webbing over the roar of the water, rain hammering them both.

"Feet," she said. Her voice came out low and steady, which surprised her, because nothing inside her was low or steady. "Find the smear. Left foot. Now."

He found it. She walked him up the rock the way she'd walked a hundred frightened clients up a hundred rocks, her voice flat and certain, each instruction small and achievable, and he came over the crown of the chockstone and collapsed against her and she held him by the straps of his pack with both hands and felt him shaking, felt the rain, felt her own heart trying to break out through her ribs.

"You flinched," she said.

"I know." His voice cracked. "I know, I'm sorry—"

"Don't apologize. Flinching is human. That's why we rope up." She pulled back and looked at him, really looked, and what she felt was so large and so simple that it scared her more than the slab had. "Look at me. You're not cargo, Kip. You're crew. Crew checks the rock above before they climb. Crew calls what they see. You saw nothing because you were watching your feet, and your feet are the least interesting thing about you. Understood?"

"Understood." He was crying, or the rain was; at this altitude, in this water, there was no difference, and she decided not to make one.

They got him over. They got everyone over. On the far side, in the first flat place in an hour, Lena called a halt that Dev protested—*we're losing daylight, we're losing the window*—and overruled with a look that ended the conversation. They drank hot sweet tea from Tomas's battered thermos, all five of them pressed under an overhang like swallows under an eave, and nobody talked about the slab, and everybody kept glancing at Kip, and Kip kept looking at Lena with an expression she recognized because she had once worn it herself, aimed at a guide named Dorje's age now, dead now, who had caught her by a pack strap on a mountain in Kyrgyzstan when she was nineteen.

That was the inheritance nobody put in the brochure. You caught someone, and then you were the one they caught.

* * *

They made the exit ramp at last light.

It was there, exactly where Eli's notes had promised—a ramp of broken schist angling up the north wall to a shoulder of grass above the gorge, steep and loose but walkable, and Lena led them up it with the rain finally slackening into a steady grey whisper, and came out onto the shoulder into a world that felt like another planet: soft ground, dwarf juniper, the river now far below and merely loud instead of total.

They camped in the lee of a boulder. No stove noise, no talking—everyone was spent. Lena took first watch, sitting with her back to the stone, and watched the clouds come down the valley in slow grey fleets, and felt Kip move in his sleep beside the fire ring, and understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that she would now burn down anything that threatened this boy. Not because he was a client. Because he was hers. Because love, on a mountain, is not a feeling but a liability you choose, and she had just chosen it, again, knowing exactly what it cost.

Dev stirred in his bag across the fire ring. He looked at her for a long moment, and she had the strange, certain sense that he was going to say something—that there was a sentence loaded in him, something about the valley ahead, something about her brother, something he'd been carrying since Kathmandu.

"Dev."

"Yeah?"

"Get some sleep."

He closed his eyes. But as she turned back to the valley, she heard him say, very quietly, almost lost under the river: "Tomorrow we cross the boundary ridge. After that, there's no map, and no going back the way we came."

Lena looked north, where the gorge bent toward the closed valley, toward ground that had swallowed her brother, toward whatever the client wanted buried there and whatever Eli had left behind. The rain was starting again.

"Good," she said. "I never liked maps."
