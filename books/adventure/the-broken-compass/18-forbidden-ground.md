## 18. Forbidden Ground

The ridge had no name on any map Tomas had ever seen, which was how he knew it mattered.

He'd learned that much in thirty years of kitchens and twenty of high trails: the places that got labeled were the places somebody wanted you to go. The unlabeled ones were where the real business happened—smuggling routes, burial grounds, the small private geographies of people who wanted to be left alone. This ridge was a gray smear between two contour lines, a wrinkle of scree and dwarf juniper that looked like nothing at all from below, and it was the door.

Lena stood at the top of it with her back to them, wind pulling at her hood, and she wasn't moving.

Tomas had cooked for her through four seasons now. He knew her silences the way he knew his own knives—the short one that meant *think*, the long one that meant *grieve*, and this one, new to him, stretched thin as wire across her shoulders: the silence of a woman standing at a line she'd drawn herself, years ago, in blood.

"Wind's picking up," Priya said quietly behind him. Not a complaint. A clock.

"I know what the wind's doing," Lena said, without turning.

Nobody answered. That was the thing about this crew, Tomas thought—they'd all learned the same language, the one made of not-saying. Kip shifted the weight of his pack and said nothing. Dev Kapoor stood apart with his satellite gear cradled like an infant, saying nothing, which for Dev was nearly heroic. The porters, three of them left now since the gorge, huddled against the rock outcrop and smoked and watched the foreign drama with the patient eyes of men who had crossed worse lines than this one for less money.

Lena stepped over the ridge.

That was all. One boot, then the other, gravel ticking downslope. She didn't look back, didn't mark it, didn't say a word—and somehow that made it worse than any ceremony would have. Tomas felt the crossing in his chest anyway, a click like a lock engaging. On one side of that ridge they'd been reckless. On the other side they were criminals.

He followed her because there was nowhere else to follow.

* * *

The valley opened below them like a held breath.

Tomas had seen a lot of country in his life—the Carpathians of his boyhood, gray and gentle; the Alps, groomed like gardens; these Himalayan fringes, savage and half-feral—but he had never seen land that seemed actively unwilling to be seen. The valley ran north under walls of black gneiss streaked with old snowmelt, and the river at its floor was the color of milk and slate, braiding and rebraiding around bars of gray gravel. Mist moved through it in slow ropes. There were no trails. There were no cairns. There were no prayer flags, which was the detail that got under his skin—up here, every pass, every dangerous crossing, every place a human being had ever hesitated, carried someone's flags, someone's fear made visible and flapping. This valley had none. Either no one had ever come here, or everyone who came here had wanted the coming forgotten.

"Coordinates put the source two days up-valley," Dev said, checking his unit. His voice had the bright, forced cheerfulness of a man walking on a frozen lake. "Weather window holds maybe forty hours before the main system arrives."

"You've said." Lena's voice was flat as the gravel bars.

"And I'll say it once more, because—"

"Dev." She turned then, and even from ten meters Tomas could see what her face was doing, the way grief and command were fighting for the same real estate. "You got your valley. Walk."

They walked.

The going was bad in the way that didn't announce itself—no cliffs, no crevasses, just ground that fought you politely, endlessly. Tussocks that hid holes. Gravel that flowed like water when you trusted it. Willow scrub chest-high that snagged packs and stole hats. They strung out into the loose formation of tired people, and Tomas found himself near the rear with Kip, who had gone quiet in the way that worried Tomas more than talk would have.

"Eat," Tomas said, pressing a piece of tsampa brick into the boy's hand without looking at him.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're fourteen and you climbed a ridge. You're hungry. Your stomach is lying to protect your pride, which is very brave of it, but I am bigger than both of you."

Kip took the tsampa. He chewed. After a while he said, "Is it true there are patrols?"

Tomas considered the question while he walked. Lying to children was a habit he despised in others and practiced himself when necessary, and this, he judged, was a necessary moment wearing its Sunday clothes.

"It's true there is a border post two valleys east," he said. "It's true that soldiers do not enjoy walking up valleys with no roads. It's true that if we see anyone, we will be polite, we will be boring, and we will be timber surveyors, which is what our permits say, and by the time they finish laughing at the idea of timber up here, we will be gone." He paused. "And it's true that none of that will matter if you fall into a river hole because you're walking scared instead of walking careful. So walk careful."

Kip nodded and ate his tsampa and, Tomas noted with satisfaction, began watching his feet again.

* * *

Camp went in at midafternoon in a hollow above the river, sheltered by a shelf of rock, close enough to water and far enough from it to sleep without dreaming of floods. Tomas built the kitchen with the automatic tenderness of long practice—windscreen first, always the windscreen, the stove nestled in stone like an egg. While the snow melted he let his hands work and his mind do the thing it did best, which was drift toward Marta.

He wrote letters to her in his head most evenings. Actual ink-and-paper letters he wrote rarely, because paper could be found on you, and paper in your pocket that said *my love, we have crossed into the closed valley* was not the kind of paper a man wanted explaining itself at a checkpoint. But tonight, with the mist sealing the valley mouth behind them like a lid, with trespass already accomplished and unaccomplishable-to-undo, he took out the notebook he kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his cook kit and uncapped his pen.

*Marta, moja droga.*

His handwriting was a cook's handwriting—fast, slanted, built for lists. He wrote slowly anyway.

*By the time you read this, if you ever read it, I will either be rich or famous or dead or arrested, and knowing me it will be some combination that makes a bad joke of all four. We crossed the line today. You remember I told you about the line, the one Lena swore on her brother's grave she would never cross, and you asked me why I stay with a woman who breaks oaths like that, and I told you it was because she doesn't break them, other people push her through them, and there is a difference, though I admit from the outside it looks identical.*

*I want to tell you what the valley looks like because you would love it and that is exactly why I can't, so instead I will tell you about the boy.*

*Kip. Fourteen, all elbows, lies about his age so smoothly I almost believe him. He follows Lena like a duck follows its mother and pretends he doesn't. Today on the ridge he asked me about patrols and I gave him the speech I give myself every morning, which is that fear is a spice and not a meal. He ate his food and walked careful. I think he is going to be fine. I think that is the only thing going to be fine.*

A porter drifted near, drawn by the smell of tea, and Tomas closed the notebook with his thumb marking the page and poured the man a cup without breaking rhythm. Hospitality was armor too. The porter drank, nodded thanks, drifted off. Tomas reopened the book.

*Here is the thing I cannot say out loud on this mountain, so I will say it to you, who are 4,000 kilometers and one probably-unsendable letter away.*

*I am not afraid of the soldiers. I am not afraid of the ice or the river or the monsoon, which is coming early and will be angry about it. I am afraid of the carabiner.*

*You never saw it. The old woman in the hidden village pressed it into Lena's hand—stamped initials, E.H., her brother's—and I watched my friend hold twelve years of grief in the palm of her hand and close her fingers over it like it was hot. And I understood something I have been trying not to understand since Kathmandu: that we did not come up this river for the pardon or the pay or even for Dev's beautiful doomed contract. We came because a dead man left a trail, and Lena Halloran has never in her life left a trail unfollowed.*

*If we find him—if we find anything of him—I don't know what she will be afterward. People are like sauces. You can reduce them only so far before what's left is either essence or ruin, and no cook alive can tell you which until it's done.*

*Keep the garden. Argue with your sister about the fence, it's good for her. Kiss the children of the neighbors, mine are far away and so am I, and both of those facts are my own fault and my own choice, and I would make them again.*

*Tomas*

He read it twice, decided it was too honest to send and too honest to burn, and folded it into the oilcloth with the others—a growing archive of a life conducted in draft form. Someday, he thought. Someday there would be a mailbox at the end of all this, or a grave, and either way the letters would find their way down the mountain. Paper was patient. It was people who weren't.

"Tomas."

Priya, crouching at the edge of the kitchen light, map across her knees. Her face in the stove-glow had the particular stillness she got when the news was structural rather than urgent.

"Come look at this."

* * *

The map was the old survey sheet, the pre-war one, soft as cloth from handling. Priya had it weighted with stones at the corners, and her finger rested on the valley's upper reach, where the contours crowded together into the white riddle of the glacier.

"The boundary markers," she said. "On the official maps, the closure runs along this ridge—we crossed it, fine, done. But look at the old sheet. Look at what the surveyors drew in 1931."

Tomas leaned in. In faded blue ink, a second line wandered down off the ridge and along the valley floor, looping around a side drainage, and beside it, in English so old-fashioned it might have been carved: *BOUNDARY OF PROHIBITED AREA — BY ORDER — NO ENTRY.*

"They put the line in the valley bottom," Tomas said slowly. "Not the ridge."

"Not the ridge." Priya's finger tapped the blue line. "Which means everything we've done since noon hasn't been trespass. Trespass is crossing a line. This—" she swept her hand up the darkening valley, at the mist, the black walls, the river muttering to itself in the dark— "this is something else. We were inside the prohibited area before we ever crossed the ridge. The ridge was theater. Somebody redrew the boundary higher up to keep people from even getting close, and the old line, the real line, is sitting down here under our camp."

Tomas straightened up and looked north, the way they'd come from, the way they were going. The mist had thickened. Somewhere in it, the river kept up its gray argument with the stones.

"So the question," he said, "is who redraws a border to hide where it used to be."

"That," said Priya, "is the question."

Behind them, at the edge of the firelight, Lena stood alone facing up-valley, and in her hand, catching the last of the stove-light, hung a small steel carabiner stamped with two letters, turning slowly, like a compass needle that had finally found its north.
