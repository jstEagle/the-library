## 19. Eli's Cache

The old woman's directions had been a riddle wrapped in a grandmother's voice, and Lena had spent the whole morning turning them over like river stones.

*Where the cliff leans its shoulder against the sky, look for the birch that grew crooked out of shame. Beneath it, the ground keeps a secret the way old women keep secrets—badly, if you know how to ask.*

Kip had laughed when she'd translated it for the crew at breakfast, but no one else had. They were above the village now, past the last terraced field, into country where the trail was less a path than an argument the mountain was having with itself. The gorge had narrowed overnight. The Tsangpo ran loud and green-white below them, compressed between walls of gneiss so steep the light only touched the water for an hour at midday, and then left it to the shadows again.

Lena climbed with her eyes working in sweeps—the trained, involuntary sweep of a guide who had spent fifteen years reading terrain the way Priya read charts. Scree angle. Snowmelt lines. The telltale shimmer of seep water on rock that meant a slope was thinking about letting go. But underneath all of it, underneath the professional attention she wore like armor, another part of her brain was running the old woman's words against the landscape, matching riddle to stone.

*The cliff that leans its shoulder.*

There. Ahead and to the left, where a massive buttress of gray rock shouldered out over the valley, tilted maybe ten degrees past vertical, as if it had grown tired of standing straight over ten million years and simply leaned. Below it, a fan of talus had spilled down toward the river, and threading through the talus, impossibly, grew a stand of birch—white-barked, thin, clinging to the loose slope like people who refuse to leave a house that's sliding into the sea.

One of them grew crooked. Not wind-crooked, not snow-crooked, but bent at the base in a way that spoke of something—a boulder, long since gone—that had pinned it young and then been taken away. It leaned out over the slope like a question mark.

Lena stopped walking.

She didn't say anything at first. She stood there with her hand on her knee, breathing hard from the altitude, and felt the strange double sensation she'd been carrying since the village: hope arriving not as warmth but as vertigo. Like standing at the edge of something high and feeling your body *want* to fall.

"Lena?" Kip had come up beside her, breathless, cheerful, his pack rattling with the cook pots Tomas made him carry as punishment for some infraction Lena hadn't bothered to learn. "What is it?"

"That tree." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. She tried again. "See how it's bent? That's our marker."

Dev came up behind them, and she heard him stop too, heard the change in his breathing. He said nothing. He'd said almost nothing all morning—not since the village, not since the old woman had pressed the carabiner into Lena's palm and closed her fingers around it with hands like dried leaves. Dev watching her now had the quality of a man watching someone defuse a bomb he himself had planted.

"Tomas," Lena called back down the line. "Break here. Thirty minutes."

They gathered in the shade of the leaning cliff, and Lena worked through the ritual of a rest stop—water circulated, feet checked, Kip sent to chase down a mule that had opinions about a patch of grass—while all the time her eyes kept going back to the birch. Twelve years. Twelve years of monsoon and avalanche and freeze-thaw. Whatever was beneath that tree would be rust and rot and mouse nests, if it was anything at all. She knew this. She told herself this, firmly, the way you tell yourself the rope will hold.

And still her hands shook slightly as she crossed the talus slope.

The ground beneath the crooked birch did keep its secret badly. There was a hollow there, subtle—a settling in the scree, a geometry that didn't quite match the slope around it, the kind of thing you'd walk past a thousand times and never see unless you were looking for the way the earth remembered being disturbed. Lena knelt. The stones were cold through her gloves. She began lifting them away, one by one, working from the edges inward, and behind her the camp went quiet in a way she could feel on the back of her neck.

Twelve years ago, her brother had knelt exactly here—or somewhere very near here—and placed stones in this pattern. Had looked up at this same leaning cliff. Had maybe rested his hand against this same crooked birch, which would have been thinner then, younger, before the mountain finished bending it.

"Want me to dig?" Kip had appeared at her shoulder without her hearing him, which was either a good sign about his soft-footedness or a bad sign about her attention.

"I've got it."

"You're shaking."

"I'm cold."

He didn't believe her. She could feel him not believing her. But he sat down on the scree beside her, cross-legged, and started handing her stones as she set them aside, arranging them in neat rows like he was helping her build something instead of unbuild it, and she loved him a little for that, and hated how easy loving people had become when it was far too late to practice on the person who'd taught her how.

Half a meter down, her glove knocked against something that wasn't stone.

It was an ammunition can. Olive drab, army surplus, the kind sold in every bazaar from Kathmandu to Kashgar, its paint gone chalky with ultraviolet and its hinges furred with orange rust. Someone had wrapped the handle in cord—red cord, once, now faded to the pink of old skin—and sealed the lid's seam with wax that had cracked but held. And stenciled on the top, in white paint applied by a hand that had been careful and unhurried, were three letters:

**E.H.C.**

Elias Halloran Cache. Or Elias Halloran, Careful. Or any of a hundred things. Lena stared at the letters and the gorge went silent around her—the river, the wind, the mules, all of it dropping away like sound underwater—and for one suspended moment she was twenty-six years old again, sitting in a police station in Nyalam, listening to a man explain in careful diplomatic Chinese-accented English that the expedition had been lost, that search operations had been suspended, that sometimes the mountain simply keeps what it takes.

*Lena.* Priya's voice, gentle, from somewhere behind her. *Lena, breathe.*

She breathed. She pried at the wax with her knife. It crumbled away in flakes the color of bone, and the lid hinge screamed with rust, and then the can was open, and the smell that rose out of twelve years of sealed dark was the smell of her brother's gear—paraffin, canvas, gun oil, and under it all something sweet and faintly rotten, the ghost of a chocolate bar he'd never eaten.

Inside, packed with the obsessive neatness that had driven their mother crazy and made him a superb expedition man:

A spare stove, disassembled and greased. Two boxes of matches in waxed paper, dry as the day they'd been sealed. A coil of four-mil accessory cord. A medical kit, its morphine syrettes gone amber with age. A foil blanket. A small tin of tea, Chinese green, the label faded to a whisper. And on top of everything, folded into a square and protected inside a heavy-gauge plastic bag sealed with tape, a single page torn from a spiral notebook.

Lena took it out with both hands, the way you take communion.

The paper had yellowed but the ink had survived—pencil, actually, her brother had never trusted ink at altitude, *ink freezes, Lee, pencil just gets shorter*—and the handwriting hit her like a physical blow. She knew that handwriting. She'd known it across kitchen tables and postcards and the margins of shared route plans for twenty years before the mountain took it. The big looping capitals. The way he crossed his t's like he was striking them through, impatient even on paper.

She read it. Then she read it again, because the first time the words wouldn't stick, sliding off her mind like water off waxed canvas.

*Day 9 above the village. If someone's reading this, you found the cache, which means you're either lost or looking for me, and I'm betting on the second, because I hid this well enough that only family would bother.*

*A funny thing happened on the glacier. We weren't supposed to be up there—we were supposed to stay below the moraine line—but Ren spotted color in the ice and we went up to look, and I'm glad we broke the rule, because the source isn't what the maps say it is. The lake is real, but it isn't the beginning. There's a slot above the cirque, maybe 200 meters, that the survey teams have missed or ignored, and the water comes OUT of the wall. Out of solid ice. Like the mountain has an eye, and it's open.*

*I'm calling it the eye of the river. Ren thinks I'm being dramatic. Ren is usually right but this time I think I'm right first.*

*We're going up tomorrow to look closer. If we don't come back down, go higher. Everything important is higher. And Lee—if it's you reading this, don't make the face you're making right now. You know exactly the face I mean.*

*E.*

The page trembled in her hands. Around her, the crew had drifted close—Tomas crouched with his arms on his knees, Priya standing with her jaw tight, Dev closest of all, close enough that Lena could hear that he wasn't breathing evenly. She became aware, distantly, that she was making a sound. Not crying. Something before crying. A low, involuntary hum, like a wire under tension.

"The eye of the river," Priya read aloud, softly, upside down, because of course Priya had already read it; of course she had. "There's a slot above the cirque."

"The survey teams missed it." Lena heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone else. "Or ignored it." She thought of the client's immaculate dossier, the satellite imagery with its careful annotations, the coordinates that pointed at the lake and stopped. Pointed at the lake and *stopped*, like a sentence cut off mid-word. "Or didn't want it found."

Dev said nothing. She registered that silence the way you register a draft from a door you thought was shut—a small wrongness in the air. But she was too full of Eli to chase it. Later, she would remember that silence. Later, it would matter enormously. For now she folded the page along its existing creases, returned it to its plastic sleeve, and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where it lay against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

"He meant to come back for this," Tomas said. It wasn't a question. The big cook was looking at the cache with the expression of a man reading a letter addressed to someone dead. "Stove. Matches. Medical kit. This isn't a grave marker. This is a resupply point. He planned to come down, restock, and go back up."

"And he never came down." Lena's voice was steady. She was surprised by that. "So whatever's up there—" she tipped her chin toward the head of the valley, invisible beyond the gorge's bend, toward the cirque and the hanging ice and the thing her brother had named with a child's clarity and a scientist's instinct, "—it kept him."

*Kept him.* The word choice arrived from somewhere below conscious thought, and she flinched from it even as she said it. Kept. Not killed. Not took. Kept, the way the old woman's stories kept people, the way valleys in every mountain culture on earth had their spirits and their debts and their doors that opened only inward.

Nobody corrected her. That was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

They cached nothing and took everything—Tomas claimed the stove for the expedition museum-that-didn't-exist-yet, and Kip got custody of the matches, which he treated with the gravity of a knighthood—but the can itself stayed, resealed with fresh wax from Priya's repair kit, buried again beneath its stones. Some things belonged to the mountain. Lena couldn't have explained why she felt that so strongly, only that leaving the empty can there felt like answering a letter. *Found your cache, little brother. Still looking.*

They climbed through the afternoon, and the valley changed character around them the way weather changes—gradually, and then all at once. The birch gave way to juniper scrub, the juniper to bare rock and old moraine. The trail, never more than a suggestion, dissolved entirely, and after that they were route-finding on instinct and the old woman's fragments, which had grown stranger as she'd given them, as if she were reciting something learned in childhood rather than describing a path:

*Follow the water until the water forgets which way is down. Sleep where the ibex sleep. When you can hear the glacier grinding its teeth, turn your back on it and climb.*

By late afternoon they could indeed hear the glacier—a deep, arrhythmic groaning from somewhere above the valley's head, the sound of a mass of ice the size of a city adjusting itself in its sleep. Kip kept stopping to listen, his face lit with a wonder that Lena envied and feared in equal measure. Wonder was expensive at altitude. Wonder got people killed. But she said nothing, because saying nothing was also a kind of love, and she was practicing it.

They made camp on a shelf of moraine above the flood line, tucked against a boulder the size of a chapel, and while Tomas conjured dinner from the day's diminishing stores, Lena sat outside with the compass.

Eli's compass. The one she carried everywhere and used never, its needle cracked down the middle so that it wobbled between north and something adjacent to north, useless for navigation, priceless for everything else. She held it flat on her palm and watched the broken needle shiver, and thought about the page against her chest.

*Don't make the face you're making right now.*

She was making the face. She could feel it—the one their mother called her thundercloud, the one that meant Lena Halloran had decided something and heaven help the obstacle. Except that for twelve years the decision had been simple: never go back, never follow him, let the valley keep what it kept. And now the decision was different and infinitely worse, because the page in her jacket said *go higher, everything important is higher*, and the client's contract said the same thing in the language of money and pardons, and those two voices—her brother's and the client's—were pointing at the same ground for reasons that could not possibly be innocent, and she did not yet know which of them she was obeying.

Maybe both. Maybe that was the trap. Maybe the whole architecture of this expedition—Dev's arrival, the exoneration file, the survivor rumor timed like a key turning in a lock—had been built around the fact that Lena Halloran would burn down any principle she owned if she believed it might lead to Eli. Maybe the bait wasn't the pardon. Maybe the pardon was the cover story and the bait was her brother, and she'd swallowed it whole, hook and line and twelve years of grief.

She looked up. Across the fire, Dev was cleaning the satellite phone's contacts with a cloth, meticulous, unhurried, a man performing calm. He felt her looking and glanced over, and for half a second—less than half a second—something moved behind his eyes that she'd seen once before, years ago, on a rope team in Kyrgyzstan, in the moment before he lied to her about the weather window.

Then it was gone, smoothed over, and he smiled his boardroom smile and raised his mug to her across the flames, and the moment passed, filed away in the place where Lena kept things she wasn't ready to think about—a place that was getting crowded lately, crowded as a lifeboat.

Above them, in the dark, the glacier ground its teeth.

Below them, the river ran on toward the eye that her brother had found and the world had chosen to forget. And Lena sat with the broken compass in her hand and understood, with the vertiginous certainty of a woman stepping off a ledge, that tomorrow they would climb into the last stretch of forbidden ground, and that whatever waited at the eye of the river had been waiting twelve years for someone stubborn enough to come asking.

She just no longer knew whether she was coming to find her brother—or to finish what someone else had started.
