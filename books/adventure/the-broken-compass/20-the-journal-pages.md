## 20. The Journal Pages

Dev had learned long ago that the most dangerous thing a man could do was stand still with his own thoughts. So he kept moving—checking lashings that didn't need checking, testing the tension on guy lines already taut, rearranging the survey crates in the porters' loads for the fourth time since dawn. The camp at the mouth of the hidden valley sat in a bowl of grey morning light, mist rising off the river in slow ropes, and everywhere he looked there was work to pretend at.

The journal page had been drying by the stove since last night.

Lena had spread it flat between two sheets of plastic, weighted at the corners with stones, and then she had slept—or lain awake pretending to sleep, which amounted to the same thing in Dev's experience—and now she sat cross-legged beside the cold fire ring with the page held up to the light, reading it for what must have been the hundredth time. Her lips moved slightly. She didn't know she did that. She'd never known. Twelve years and a blacklist and a thousand miles of river between them, and Dev could still catalogue her unconscious habits like entries in an inventory: the lip movement when she read, the way her thumb pressed into her forefinger when she was doing arithmetic in her head, the particular set of her shoulders before she said something she knew would start a fight.

He should have walked away. That was the thought that kept arriving, patient as the mist, every time he let himself stop moving. He should have taken the client's money, hired some other guide, some other crew of strays who didn't ask questions, and come up this river with strangers. Strangers couldn't look at you the way Lena looked at him across a cook fire. Strangers didn't carry twelve years of shared rope-lengths in their hands like a debt.

But he hadn't walked away, because the truth—the ugly, load-bearing truth he'd confessed to no one but himself, somewhere above the ice bridge, somewhere in the dark hours when even ambition slept—was that he needed to see it through. Not the survey. Not the pardon. He needed to see whether Eli Halloran had left anything behind that could absolve Dev Kapoor of the thing he'd done in Kathmandu twelve years ago, the thing that had built his career out of another man's silence.

And now here was the journal page, and here was Lena reading it, and here was Dev standing ten feet away with his heart going like a porter's pulse on a steep grade.

* * *

He crossed to her the way he'd cross a suspect snow slope—weight committed, no sudden moves.

"Anything?"

She didn't look up. "It's a supply inventory. Rice, fuel, rope. Dated—" she squinted at the water-stained numerals, "—twelve years ago, almost to the week. He wrote neat." A ghost of something crossed her face, not quite a smile. "He always wrote neat. I used to tease him about it. His field notes looked like they'd been typeset."

"May I?"

She hesitated—a half-second he felt in his teeth—and then handed it over. The paper was brittle at the folds, foxed brown at the edges, but the ink had survived the years in the cache better than anyone had a right to expect. Eli's handwriting marched down the page in tidy columns, and Dev read it the way you read anything written by the dead: slowly, superstitiously, as though speed might offend.

Rice, twenty kilos. Fuel tablets. Two spare carabiners. And then, below the inventory, in the margin, a line that was not an inventory at all:

*Sampled the east wall again today. R. says the assays are wrong, that they can't be right. But they are.*

Dev's hands went very still.

"East wall," Lena said quietly. She'd been watching him read; he felt her attention land on him like a hand on the back of his neck. "There's no east wall in the survey brief. The client's coordinates are all cirque and outflow. What east wall?"

"The gorge narrows upstream," Dev said. "There's a wall of rock on the east side where the valley turns north. Granite, mostly." His voice came out level, professional, the voice of a man describing terrain rather than thinking. "Eli was a geologist before he was anything else. Sampling rock wouldn't be strange."

"It's not granite he's sampling." She took the page back from him, gently, the way you take something from someone whose grip has gone wrong. "Look at the next line."

Below the margin note, Eli had written a single sentence, underlined twice:

*If the company finds out we found this, none of us go home.*

* * *

They read it together in silence while the mist burned off the river and Kip clattered pans somewhere behind the cook tent and Tomas swore at him in three languages, and the ordinary sounds of a camp waking up went on around the two of them like weather around a lightning strike.

"Company," Lena said at last. "What company? There wasn't any company on Eli's permit. It was a university expedition. Academic. He told me—he told me it was academic." Her thumb was pressing into her forefinger. Dev watched her do the arithmetic she couldn't finish. "Unless."

"Unless," Dev said.

"Unless the university expedition was a cover." She looked up at him, and her eyes were the grey-green of glacial meltwater, and they were not accusing him yet, but they were loading the accusation, chambering it. "Dev. Who funded my brother's last expedition?"

"I don't know."

"You were in Kathmandu when they launched. You helped them pack. You told me once you carried their crates to the bus station."

"I carried crates. I didn't read manifests." Which was true, and also a lie, and both halves of it sat in his chest like ballast. Because he had seen the crates. He had seen the stenciled markings on two of them—markings that meant nothing to a nineteen-year-old assistant guide with stars in his eyes, and meant everything to the man he'd become, the man who now spent his life negotiating with exactly those kinds of markings. He had been too young, too flattered, too eager to be useful to men who bought their own satellite phones, to ask why a university expedition needed crates stenciled with a logistics company's code instead of a university's crest.

He'd asked later. Much later. After the mountain had eaten six men and spat out zero survivors and one empty legend. He'd asked, and the man he asked had smiled at him over lunch in a Kathmandu hotel, and had said, *Mr. Kapoor, sometimes science needs discreet partners,* and had slid a business card across the tablecloth, and Dev—hungry, ambitious, freshly ruined by association with the Halloran disaster, needing exactly what that card represented—had taken it.

That card had become a career. The career had become this contract. And the contract had become a woman sitting in the dirt reading her dead brother's handwriting and asking Dev questions Dev had sworn, on a hotel tablecloth twelve years ago, never to answer honestly.

"It doesn't say what he found," Lena said. She turned the page over, held it to the light again, hunting for watermark ghosts, impressions from pages pressed against it in some earlier binding. "Just that it can't be right, and that if the company finds out, nobody goes home." Her jaw tightened. "Six men went home in boxes. Or didn't go home at all. So either the company found out—"

"—or they were afraid it would," Dev finished.

The words hung between them. Downstream, the river made its low continuous argument with the rocks, the sound that had become the expedition's heartbeat, so constant Dev had stopped hearing it days ago. Now it seemed very loud.

"We should show Priya," Lena said. "She'll want to photograph it, catalog it—"

"No."

The word came out of him faster than thought, faster than strategy, raw as a flinch. Lena stopped. Turned. Looked at him with the full weight of her attention, and Dev understood, in the space of one heartbeat, that he had just made a mistake that could not be unmade—not because of what he'd said, but because of how fast he'd said it.

"Why not?" she asked. Very quiet. Very even. The evenness of a woman standing on a cornice, testing it.

"Because it's fragile." He heard his own voice assemble the excuse syllable by syllable, smooth as poured concrete. "Paper that old, handled by everyone in camp, photographed, passed around—it'll be dust by the time it matters. If we're taking evidence out of this valley, we protect it. One handler. Controlled conditions. You."

A pause. The river argued. Somewhere upslope a raven made its dry wooden comment on everything.

"One handler," Lena repeated.

"You're his sister. It should be you."

Another pause, longer. Then she nodded, slowly, and folded the page back into its plastic sleeve with a care that looked, from the outside, like agreement, and Dev felt the relief hit him like warm water and taste, immediately afterward, like ash.

Because he had just lied to her face. Not by omission this time, not by the soft gray silences he'd been cultivating for twelve years like a private garden, but actively, fluently, with craft. And the worst part—the part that would keep him awake tonight, if the altitude left him any sleep at all—was that the lie had been good. It had been *professional*. He was getting better at this. That was the thing no one warned you about complicity: it didn't grind you down. It honed you.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he did best: being useful in visible ways. He recalculated the approach to the cirque against the barometer's slow fall. He re-briefed the porters, three of whom had gone quiet and watchful since the cache, sensing—porters always sensed—that the shape of the job had changed from a walk to a reckoning. He answered a garbled check-in on the sat phone with the coded all-well phrase the client required, and hated the sound of his own voice saying it, and said it anyway, clearly, twice.

*All well. Progressing to source. Weather holding.*

Weather not holding. All not well. Progressing toward something the client wanted buried badly enough to fund a full survey expedition to retrieve or erase it, and Dev had known—I've known, he corrected himself, I have *always* known, since the hotel, since the card, since the smile—and he had brought the brother's sister up the river anyway, because the file she wanted was real, and the pardon she wanted was real, and his own ruin was real, and ruin makes excellent company for rationalization.

In the late afternoon he found himself at the edge of camp, ostensibly checking the route ahead through binoculars, actually watching Lena.

She had set up apart from the others, on a flat rock above the water, with the journal page and the old woman's carabiner and Eli's compass laid out in front of her like an altar. She wasn't reading anymore. She was just looking at the three objects, her arms wrapped around her knees, and the light was going gold and then copper on the eastern wall of the gorge—the east wall, *sampled the east wall again today*, and Dev raised the binoculars without meaning to, swept them along the ramparts of rock above the valley's turn, and saw nothing but stone and evening shadow and, high up, a seam of discoloration running through the granite like a vein through marble.

Ore bodies left veins like that. So did other things.

He lowered the binoculars. His hands were steady. That was the detail he would remember later, in the whiteout, in the flood, in whatever came after: not trembling, not guilt-sick, just steady, the steadiness of a man who had practiced.

Kip appeared beside him, silent as teenagers are silent only when they want something or know something. "Dinner's on," the boy said. Then, lower, with the terrible directness of the young: "Is she okay? She's been sitting out there for hours."

"She's fine."

"She doesn't look fine. She looks like she did after the checkpoint. Like she's carrying something heavy in her head."

"Go eat, Kip."

The boy lingered a moment—Dev could feel the question assembling itself behind the kid's eyes, the question about what was really in the journal, about what Dev wasn't saying, because Kip missed nothing and understood less than he thought and more than he should—and then went, boots crunching down the scree toward the smell of Tomas's rice.

Dev stayed at the edge of camp until full dark. Below him, the others gathered around the stove, and their voices rose and fell in the old rhythm—Tomas grumbling, Priya arguing geography, Kip laughing too loud, Lena quiet at the center of it, the axis everything turned on. He watched them the way you watch a lit window from outside in the cold. This was the thing he had never managed to build for himself, he realized: not the career, not the contracts, not the name that opened doors in Kathmandu and Dubai. This. A circle of people who saved each other's food during storms and lied to checkpoints for each other and would, he was now fairly certain, turn on him like a single animal if they ever learned what he was carrying.

And they would learn. That was the arithmetic he could no longer avoid. Every day upstream, the journal accumulated meaning; every day, Lena read and reread and hunted the margins; every day, the probability that she would find the rest—because there *was* rest, he was sure of it now, Eli had been too systematic to leave one page pointing at a secret and nothing else—climbed like the mercury falling. The cache had been deliberate. Meant to be found. Meant to be found by *her*, maybe, or by whoever came looking with clean hands. Which meant there were more pages, higher up, toward the eye of the river, exactly where the client's coordinates ended.

Which meant the client knew it too.

Dev stood in the cold and did the final piece of arithmetic, the one he'd been refusing to do since the teahouse in Pokhara: the client had sent him—Dev Kapoor, Eli Halloran's former partner, the one man alive who might recognize Eli's handwriting and understand what it pointed at—to find and control whatever Eli had hidden. Not to survey a lake. To close an account. And Dev had agreed too easily, and Lena had noticed that he'd agreed too easily, and she had set her term—*if there's any trace of Eli, the job stops*—and he had agreed to that too easily as well, and now the traces were arriving one by one like guests at a wedding, and every agreement he'd made was a rope anchored to nothing.

Tomorrow they climbed toward the cirque. Tomorrow, probably, more pages. Tomorrow, certainly, a version of Dev Kapoor who could no longer claim he didn't know.

"Dinner," Lena called across the dark, her voice carrying the way it always did, pitched to reach the far end of a rope team. "Before it freezes. That includes you, Kapoor."

He turned. The firelight touched her face from forty feet away, and she was watching him with an expression he couldn't fully read—something between invitation and audit—and he realized she had chosen this moment, this public moment, to call him in, the way you call a climber across a bad traverse so they commit in front of witnesses.

"Coming," he called back.

He walked down into the light, into the circle, into the warmth he had no right to, and sat where she indicated, and ate Tomas's rice, and laughed once at something Kip said, and all the while, under his ribs, the journal page lay folded in its plastic like a splinter working its way toward the heart: *if the company finds out we found this, none of us go home.*

The company already knew. The company had sent him.

And tomorrow, he thought, setting down his plate, meeting Lena's eyes across the fire and holding them one second longer than honesty allowed—they would climb into the eye of the river, and either Eli's pages would save them all, or bury them exactly where his brother's expedition had been buried, and Dev Kapoor would finally learn which kind of man he had spent twelve years becoming.
