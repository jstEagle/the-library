## 21. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive so much as descend—a decision made by the sky without consultation.

Priya felt it first in her teeth, an old ache from a frostbite year she never talked about, and she had the tents half-staked when the wind came off the serac fields like something released from custody. By full dark they had abandoned the idea of the ridge camp entirely and dug in beneath the overhang of a blue ice wall where the glacier bent around a rib of rock, hollowing out a cave with shovels and a saw Tomas kept for exactly this purpose and hoped never to use.

"Three walls and a prayer," Tomas said, leaning on the shovel, breath smoking.

"Four walls," Priya corrected. "You're forgetting the snow itself."

"The snow is not on our side."

"No," Priya said. "But it's better than the sky."

They carved the chamber big enough for five to sleep shoulder to shoulder, benches along the walls, a vestibule for boots and packs, a stove niche vented through a tunnel Kip cut with the cheerful incompetence of a boy who had grown up below the snowline and still found all this faintly miraculous. Lena tested the roof with her ice axe—two measured taps, listening—and pronounced it sound for a day, maybe two. Nobody asked what happened on day three. In the high mountains you did not ask questions whose answers were already living in your chest like swallowed stones.

By midnight the whiteout was total. Not darkness—something stranger, a luminous nothing, the world erased between one blink and the next. The storm filled the entrance tunnel within the hour; Dev dug them out twice before Lena ordered him to stop wasting calories, and after that the cave sealed itself and they were inside the mountain's lung, breathing its slow cold breath, waiting.

* * *

Ration math was Priya's job, because ration math was arithmetic and arithmetic did not lie, which made it the only honest thing left above six thousand meters.

She sat in the stove's orange circle with the ledger on her knees—the actual ledger, a school exercise book with a rubber band around it that had survived three seasons and two border crossings—and counted. Fuel for nine days at minimum burn. Food, if everyone ate full rations: four days. Food at half rations: seven. Food at half rations with no movement: eight, maybe, if nobody's body rebelled against the arithmetic, which bodies always eventually did.

"Say it," Lena said. She was sitting across the flame, mending a torn pack strap with dental floss, not looking up, because looking up would have meant acknowledging what the numbers were going to be.

"Half rations," Priya said. "Starting tomorrow morning. And we melt snow in shifts so we're drinking hot constantly—dehydration reads as hunger, and hunger reads as panic, and panic eats fuel faster than the stove does."

"And after day eight?"

"After day eight we're eating the emergency bars, and after the emergency bars we're having a philosophical discussion about it."

Tomas laughed from his bench, low and unamused. Dev said nothing, which Priya noted, and filed, and would continue filing for some time.

Kip was asleep against the far wall, curled around his own knees like a comma, seventeen years old and trying to make himself small enough that no one would count him as a mouth. He'd been quiet since the gorge—quieter than usual, and Kip's usual was already a studied silence, the silence of a kid who had learned early that being useful was the same as being safe. Priya watched him breathe and thought about the ledger again, and about how arithmetic did not lie but could be, under sufficient pressure, negotiated with.

* * *

The fever announced itself on the second morning, politely at first.

Kip woke coughing, waved it off, ate his half-ration of oats with the determined slowness of someone pretending appetite. By noon he was flushed and shivering under two sleeping bags while the rest of them sat in their insulated layers sweating over nothing. By evening his eyes had gone glassy and bright, and when Priya pressed the back of her hand to his forehead she felt the heat come off him like a stone pulled from a fire.

"Chest sounds clear," she told Lena quietly, outside the tent-flap of sleeping bags, in the language of guides. "Probably viral. Could be the water. His body's burning through calories it doesn't have, fighting it."

"How bad?"

"If we feed him properly, three days and he's miserable but fine. If we feed him half rations—" Priya looked at the ledger in her mind, the neat columns, the honest numbers. "His body will win the fight and lose the war. He'll come out of this lighter than we can afford for him to be. We've got real terrain after this."

Lena was silent for a long moment. The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, muffled by ten feet of snow, the storm went on with its work.

"We halve everyone else's again," Lena said finally. "Thirds. I'm not—"

"You're not deciding alone," Priya said. "I know. But let me do the math first, before you decide out loud in front of him. He'll hear everything. He always hears everything."

That was true, and they both knew it. Kip heard everything. It was how he'd survived whatever had come before Pokhara, before Tomas's kitchen and Priya's maps and Lena's rope calls; he had listened his way into a family the way other people climbed into boats.

Priya went back to the ledger and did the math she already knew the answer to, and hated it, and then stopped hating it, because hatred was also a calorie.

* * *

Here is what Priya did not say, that night or any night, because saying it would have cost more than food:

She had done this before. Not the snow cave specifically—though there had been a snow cave once, in a range she no longer named—but the arithmetic of devotion. She knew precisely what it cost to give your ration away, because she had done it for a client once, a man hypoxic and dying on a summit ridge, and she had lied to the federation about his summit record afterward to save his family from learning how close it had been, and the lie had ended her career and begun this life, this crew, this ledger. She knew that hunger was not the hard part. The hard part was watching someone eat what you needed and knowing you'd do it again tomorrow, and the day after, and that this—this stupid, unglamorous, repeated surrender—was the only prayer she believed in anymore.

So she did it without ceremony. That was the trick. Ceremony made the giving visible, and visible gifts created debts, and debts among people who loved each other curdled fast at altitude.

At dinner she ladled Kip's portion heavy and her own light, and when he frowned at his bowl she said, "I ate earlier. While you were sleeping," which was a lie so smooth it surprised even her, and she had been lying professionally for eleven years.

Tomas saw. Of course Tomas saw; Tomas missed nothing that happened near food. Their eyes met across the stove for half a second, and he said nothing, and began, wordlessly, leaving the heel of his bread beside her mug each evening, and she said nothing either, and this was the whole of their conversation, and it was enough.

Dev saw too. Dev, who had spent two weeks being the polished outsider with the satellite phone and the contract, watched her scrape the bottom of her bowl and then watch Kip eat, and something moved behind his face—recognition, maybe, or guilt, or the beginning of an understanding that would take him another week and a flood channel to reach. Priya catalogued it and moved on. She had no use for other people's epiphanies during a famine.

* * *

The second night the storm found a new voice.

It had roared for thirty hours, a flat industrial roar, and now it dropped into a lower register, a vibration more felt than heard, and the whole cave hummed with it. Snow sifted down from the ceiling in fine silver threads. The temperature inside fell until the stove's circle of warmth shrank to arm's length and no farther.

Kip's fever peaked near midnight.

He talked, in the way febrile boys talk—not delirious, exactly, but unguarded, the filter burned away. He called for his mother once, in a language Priya didn't recognize and didn't ask about. He told Lena, who was holding his head in her lap and feeding him melted snow by the capful, that he hadn't stolen anything, ever, that the thing in Kathmandu hadn't been stealing, it had been borrowing, there was a difference, and Lena said "I know, kid, I know," in a voice Priya had never heard her use for anyone living.

And then, drifting, eyes closed, he said: "You'd have left me. Before. The old you. The one from Kyrgyzstan."

The cave went very quiet.

"That's the fever talking," Lena said evenly.

"No." Kip's eyes opened, unfocused, shining. "Everyone says you left someone. On the ice. That's why you don't—"

"Sleep," Lena said, gently, and pressed her palm flat over his eyes until they closed, and held it there, and Priya watched her do it and understood that Lena was holding her own eyes shut too.

Later, when Kip was down again, breathing in long slow pulls, Lena sat with her back against the ice and stared at the stove flame and said, to no one, to the cave: "He's wrong. I didn't leave anyone. I just couldn't save them, and the difference matters, and nobody in the lowlands has ever understood the difference."

Priya, from her bench, said: "I understand it."

Lena looked at her. The flame threw their shadows huge against the blue wall, two women-shaped absences in the light.

"Do you," Lena said. Not hostile. Tired. Testing.

"I falsified a summit record once," Priya said. "To keep a man's family from finding out he came down on a rope with his soul half gone. It ended my career. I've never told anyone in this crew except Tomas, and Tomas only knows because he caught me burning the paperwork." She paused. "I'm telling you because you look like a woman doing arithmetic about whether she deserves her crew, and I want you to know the answer is yes, and I have documentation."

Something cracked in Lena's face—not a smile, exactly, but the place where a smile would go if she could afford one. "Documentation."

"I keep a ledger," Priya said. "Arithmetic doesn't lie."

Outside, the storm screamed and settled and screamed again, and the cave held them, and Kip breathed, and that was the night's whole victory, and it was enough.

* * *

Day three. The storm did not end.

Priya had stopped believing it would end. This was a professional discipline: never believe in the ending, only in the next hour. She divided the next hours into tasks—melt snow, check the vent, check the roof, check Kip, recheck the ledger as though the numbers might have improved overnight out of mercy. They had not. Fuel was the new crisis; the cold had deepened and the stove drank white gas like a frightened animal. At current burn they had five days of fuel and four days of food and a boy with a fever, and somewhere beyond the whiteout a monsoon was gathering that the weather radio, buried in Dev's kit, occasionally remembered to mention in bursts of static.

"—early onset, repeat, early onset, depression tracking north-northwest, expect—"

Then nothing. Then Dev turning the dial through the bands, face lit green, jaw tight.

"Expect what?" Tomas demanded.

"Expect everything," Dev said. "Early monsoon. We may have days, not weeks, once this breaks."

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to answer it with.

Priya gave Kip her lunch.

This time she didn't bother with the lie about having eaten earlier; the lies were getting thin, and thin lies are visible from across a snow cave. Instead she simply set her bowl beside his sleeping bag and said, "Eat it slowly. Fever burns protein. You need it more than my pride needs consistency."

Kip looked up at her with eyes that were clearer today, the fever retreating in stages like a tide going out, and his face did something complicated—young and ashamed and fiercely grateful all at once, the face of a boy receiving proof of a theory he'd been testing since Pokhara: that these people were real, that the warmth was earned and therefore load-bearing, that he belonged inside it.

"I'll pay it back," he said. "When we're down. I'll—"

"You'll eat," Priya said. "That's the repayment. Every bite. That's the entire invoice."

He ate. She watched him, and felt the hollow where her lunch should have been open under her sternum, and found, as she always did, that the hollow was survivable—that devotion, it turned out, was mostly endurance wearing a smaller coat, and that she had been training for this her whole adult life without knowing it.

Tomas put the bread heel by her mug. She ate it. Outside, the whiteout pressed its blank face against the mountain, and the mountain held.

* * *

On the third evening, the storm changed its mind.

There was no dramatic break—no parting of clouds, no theatrical sun. Just a thinning, a loosening, the roar dropping half a register, and then, near dusk, a patch of violet sky visible through the entrance tunnel like a hole punched in the world. Cold flooded in. The stars came out hard and close, the way they do after storms, as though the sky had scrubbed itself clean for the occasion.

Lena stood at the tunnel mouth for a long time, reading the air the way she read everything—silently, completely, with her whole body. Then she came back inside and crouched by the ledger.

"How long to move?" she asked Priya.

"Six hours to break camp safely. Less if we leave gear. More if we're smart."

"We'll be smart and fast. Weather window's maybe thirty-six hours before the next system. After that—" She glanced at Dev's radio, silent in its green glow. "After that we're racing the monsoon up a valley that doesn't want us."

Priya nodded and began packing the ledger away, and that was when Kip, fever-bright but upright, wrapped in both sleeping bags like a cocoon, spoke from his bench.

"Lena. When I was—last night. I said something. About Kyrgyzstan."

"Forget it," Lena said.

"I don't want to forget it. I want to say I'm sorry, and also—" He hesitated, and Priya saw him weigh something, the way he weighed everything, carefully, having learned that words were currency and he couldn't afford waste. "Also that Dev's been making satellite calls at night. When he thinks everyone's sleeping. Three nights now. I hear the antenna zip."

The cave went still.

Dev, halfway through lacing a boot, did not stop lacing. But his hands slowed, fractionally, and his face performed the small controlled nothing that polished men perform when a crack appears in the floor.

"Kid's feverish," Dev said lightly. "Imagining things."

"Sure," Kip said. "Maybe."

Priya met Lena's eyes across the stove, and in them she saw the arithmetic already running—not about food now, but about trust, and contracts, and what a man does with a satellite phone at two in the morning while a storm buries the evidence. The window was open. The mountain waited. And somewhere downstream of every honest number in her ledger, Priya thought, the first lie of the next chapter was already being dialed.
