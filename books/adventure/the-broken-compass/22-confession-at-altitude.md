## 22. Confession at Altitude

The storm had them by the throat for the second day, and by the third it had stopped feeling like weather and started feeling like a country they'd been exiled to.

The snow cave sat in the lee of a serac field below the col, a hollow Tomas had carved with his shovel and their numb hands until it was big enough for five bodies and a stove and not one inch more. Outside, the wind moved at speeds that made language useless. Inside, the world had contracted to the blue hiss of the flame, the wet wool smell of five people who hadn't been dry since the gorge, and the slow arithmetic of the food bags, which Priya counted twice a day now, silently, her lips moving over numbers she didn't share.

Lena sat closest to the stove because someone had to tend it, and because the cold found her first these days. It always had. Even in Pokhara, even in April, there was a part of her that lived at altitude, that never came down. She fed the burner its measured sips of fuel and watched the flame lean when Kip shifted against the wall, and she thought about nothing, which was a skill, which was armor, which was failing.

Kip was asleep. That was the only mercy left in the world: Kip's fever had broken sometime in the gray hours, and he slept now with the boneless totality of the young, his head on Priya's rolled sleeping bag, his breath whistling faintly through chapped lips. Priya sat beside him with her back to the ice wall, mending a strap by feel, her headlamp turned low so the beam made a small amber room within the room. Tomas was on watch at the entrance, though there was nothing to watch but snow, and he watched it anyway, the way old soldiers watch borders that no longer exist.

Which left Dev.

Dev sat across the stove from her, knees drawn up, gloved hands hanging between them like something surrendered. He'd been quiet for two days—not his market-polished quiet, not the outfitter's calculated pause before a pitch, but a real silence, heavy, the kind a man carries rather than performs. The storm had stripped things from all of them. From Dev it seemed to have stripped the varnish.

"You should sleep," Lena said.

"I did."

"An hour isn't sleep."

"It is at this altitude." He almost smiled. "You taught me that. Kyrgyzstan, 2009. You told me four hours was a luxury and I called you a barbarian."

She didn't answer. She didn't want to be reminded that there had been a time when they traded jokes across rope length, when his voice on the radio was the sound of safety. Memory was a currency the mountain kept devaluing.

The stove hissed. Somewhere above them the wind found a new register, a long descending note like a blade drawn along the whole spine of the range, and the cave trembled with it, fine crystals sifting down through the ventilation channel to glitter in the lamplight before settling on their shoulders like a benediction neither of them had asked for.

"Lena." His voice was different now. Lower. Unrehearsed. She knew every version of Dev Kapoor's voice—the boardroom baritone, the trailside charm, the flat clipped tones he used on porters who were moving too slowly—and none of them prepared her for this one. This one sounded young. "Can I say something? Not as your client. Not as—whatever we are on paper."

"You're my employer. That's what you are."

"I'm asking you to forget that for five minutes."

"That's a dangerous request at fourteen thousand feet."

"I know." He pulled his glove off, flexed bare fingers toward the flame, and she saw how the cold had gotten into them, the knuckles waxy, the nails bruised violet. A man who'd spent too many years in offices sending other people up mountains. "I keep thinking about Eli."

The name landed in her chest the way a falling rock lands in still water—one impact, then rings spreading outward through everything.

"Don't," she said.

"He'd have loved this storm. He used to say weather like this was the mountain thinking out loud."

"I said don't." But her voice cracked on the second word, and the crack was louder than the wind, and they both heard it.

Dev said nothing. That was the cruelty of him, or maybe the kindness—he waited. He had always known how to wait. In the old days she'd mistaken it for patience; later she'd understood it was strategy; now, watching his bare fingers tremble slightly in the heat, she couldn't tell the difference anymore, and maybe there wasn't one.

Lena fed the stove another sip of fuel. The flame stood up straight, blue-white, casting their shadows huge and wavering against the curved ice ceiling so that they looked like figures painted on the inside of a bell. She thought about the dossier she'd read alone in her bunk in Pokhara, page after page of satellite imagery and official language, the report on the 2013 expedition rendered in the bloodless prose of people who had never stood where her brother had stood. *Expedition member E. Halloran, status: missing, presumed deceased.* And beneath it, in a footnote she'd read forty times: *one (1) team member extracted via helicopter, condition stable.*

Extracted alive. Someone had come off that mountain breathing. Someone had walked past the search parties, past the inquiries, past her, into whatever witness-protection silence the company had bought, and no one had ever told her.

She had never asked Dev directly. There was a reason for that. There were questions you didn't ask because you already knew the shape of the answer, and knowing the shape was survivable in a way that holding it in your hands was not.

But the storm had eaten three days, and the rations were halved, and delirium was a door standing open in the back of her mind, and through it she could see clearly, for once, without any armor at all.

"The pardon," she said.

"What about it?"

"I don't want it."

Dev looked up. In the stove light his face was all planes and shadow, older than his years, younger than his reputation. "That's the entire point of this expedition. That's what you bargained for. What we bargained for."

"I know what I bargained for. I've been bargaining my whole life. My brother's life for my mother's sanity. My reputation for the crew's rent. One thing for another, always, everything has a price tag, and I've gotten very good at reading them." She heard herself talking faster, the words coming loose the way words do when the body's too tired to hold them. "And then we found the cache. And I read his handwriting. Twelve years dead and his handwriting is better than mine, did you notice that? He underlined things. He wrote *the eye of the river* like it was a place you could go, like it was waiting for him. And I sat there in the snow with his journal and I realized—" Her breath caught. She let it catch. "I realized I don't care if I'm ever allowed on a mountain again. Officially. With a badge. I don't care about the blacklist, Dev. I never cared. It was just easier to want the pardon than to want the thing I actually want."

"And what's that?"

She looked at him across the small blue fire, and the question hung there, and she understood that he already knew, and that he was asking anyway, giving her the dignity of saying it aloud, or torturing her with it—she genuinely could not tell, and perhaps neither could he.

"The truth," she said. "About Eli. All of it. Not the report. Not the satellite photos. Not *missing, presumed deceased*, filed by men in Kathmandu who were paid to file it. I want to know what happened on that glacier. I want to know who came down alive and why nobody told me. I want to know why a company that erases valleys from maps would spend twelve years pretending my brother fell into a crevasse and that was that." Her voice dropped. "I want to know what you know."

The wind screamed. The stove hissed. Between those two sounds there was a silence with weight and texture, a silence she could have touched.

Dev's jaw worked. She watched him assemble sentences and dismantle them, watched the machinery of his caution grind against something else, something that had been rusting in him for a decade. His bare hand closed into a fist and opened again.

"Lena—"

"Tomas," she said quietly, "how's the sky?"

From the entrance, without turning: "Still trying to kill us. Same as an hour ago."

"Good. Nobody's going anywhere." She turned back to Dev. "We have time. For once in this whole cursed job, we have time. So use it."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—a short, airless sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing a verdict. "You always did this. You always made honesty look like the easy option."

"It's not?"

"It's the most expensive thing there is. You of all people should know." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he looked ten years older and somehow lighter, as though the aging itself had burned off some weight. "All right. Here's what I can tell you. Here's what I've told myself I could tell you, for years, rehearsing it in hotel rooms, in airports, telling myself *next time I see her, next trip, when the timing's right*—and the timing was never right because there is no right timing for this."

Lena's heart began to move strangely, quick and shallow, the way it did on exposure. "Dev."

"In 2013, after the expedition went silent, I was working logistics for Meridian Alpine. Junior contracts desk. I processed the insurance filings. I saw the manifests." Each sentence came out like a piton driven into bad rock—slow, tested, half-trusting. "I saw the extraction order."

The cave seemed to tilt. Or she tilted. The stove flame leaned and righted itself.

"There was a helicopter," Dev said. "There was a man on it. And the flight log was altered before it reached the inquiry, and I knew—I *knew*, Lena—and I said nothing, because I was twenty-six years old and Meridian was offering me a regional contract and my father had just remortgaged his house to keep me in gear, and I told myself the man was a stranger, that it wasn't my grief to manage, that the truth would come out on its own the way truth does." He shook his head slowly. "It doesn't. Truth doesn't come out on its own. It just gets heavier. Every year it sits on you it weighs more, and eventually you're carrying a boulder and calling it a career."

Lena discovered she was gripping her own knee hard enough to hurt. She made herself let go. "Who," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Who was extracted. Give me a name."

Dev opened his mouth—

And stopped.

She watched it happen. Watched the word rise in him like water in a well shaft, watched him look at her—at the cracked compass lashed to the outside of her pack, at the carabiner with Eli's initials hanging from her chest strap where the old woman's gift rode against her heart—and watched something in him flinch away from the edge of it. Not cowardice exactly. Or yes, exactly that, but the specific cowardice of a man who understands that some truths, once spoken, cannot be unsaid, and that the person across the fire will never look at him the same way again, and that he is not yet ready to pay that price even though he has been paying interest on it for twelve years.

"Reyes," Dev said finally, and it was half a word, a door opened onto darkness and immediately shut. "Anselm Reyes. That's the name in the file. Whether it's true, whether the man they took off that mountain was really Reyes—that I can't tell you. That's beyond what I processed. That's above my pay grade, then and now."

It was a lie or it was a fragment, and she couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat between them like a third person.

"Why won't you finish?" she whispered. "Whatever it costs you, Dev, whatever you think it does to us—to *you*—why won't you just finish the sentence?"

"Because the sentence doesn't end where you think it ends." His voice was barely above the stove's hiss. "Because if I finish it, this expedition becomes something else. Not a survey. Not a rescue. Something with lawyers in it. And right now, tonight, in this hole in the ice, I need us to be five people trying to survive a storm. Tomorrow I might be brave. Tonight I'm just a man who's cold."

Lena stared at him. Delirium tugged at the edges of her vision, the flame doubling, trebling, the shadows on the ice ceiling multiplying into a crowd of watchers. She thought of Eli underlining his words. She thought of the footnote. She thought of Anselm Reyes, a name she'd never heard before tonight, now branded into her.

"You'll tell me," she said. It wasn't a question. It also wasn't a threat, or not only a threat. It was a fact stated in advance, the way you state the summit before climbing it. "At the source, or on the way down, or in a police station in Kathmandu if it comes to that—you will finish that sentence, Dev Kapoor. I'll wait. I'm good at waiting. Ask anyone."

He held her gaze. Something moved behind his eyes—fear, gratitude, shame, maybe all three braided together—and he nodded once, a small motion, a promissory note signed in the smallest possible hand.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay."

Priya set down her mending. "Fuel check," she announced softly, to no one and everyone, the ritual phrase that meant *enough*. She crawled to the stove, checked the canister with a practiced tilt, and met Lena's eyes over the flame. Whatever Priya saw there, she filed it away without comment—she was good at that, at seeing and keeping—but her hand rested briefly on Lena's shoulder as she withdrew, warm through two layers of fleece, and stayed a half-second longer than the ritual required.

Tomas reported the wind dropping a register. Kip murmured in his sleep and turned toward the warmth. The storm went on outside, vast and indifferent, but inside the bell of ice the small blue flame stood straight, and the five of them arranged themselves around it like survivors around a truth too large to speak all at once.

Lena lay back against her pack and did not sleep. Above her, sifted snow glittered and settled. Somewhere south, in an office tower in Kathmandu, a client was presumably watching a cursor blink on a map. Somewhere north, higher, in the white blindness past the col, a lake waited under its serac, and in it or near it or around it, folded into the ice like a letter into an envelope, was the rest of the sentence.

*Anselm Reyes,* she thought. *Who are you, and what did you walk away from?*

Near dawn, the wind changed direction—a clean, sudden shift, the pressure dropping through her healing ear like a door closing. Lena sat up. At the entrance, Tomas turned from his watch, and the light of his headlamp caught his face, and she saw in it something she hadn't seen in three days.

"Storm's breaking," he said. "And there's tracks outside. Fresh ones. Heading up-valley."

Lena was on her feet before he finished the sentence, her heart hammering out a rhythm that had nothing to do with altitude and everything to do with hope, which at fourteen thousand feet is the most dangerous condition of all.
