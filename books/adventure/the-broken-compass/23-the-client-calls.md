## 23. The Client Calls

Kip had learned to measure the camp's moods by its sounds, the way a sailor reads water. There was the good sound: Tomas's knife against the cutting board, steady as a heartbeat, which meant food and therefore peace. There was the tired sound: rope being coiled slowly, over and over, which meant Priya was thinking too hard. And there was the bad sound—the one he heard now, just past dusk on the fifth night above the tree line—which was no sound at all. Dev's tent flap drawn shut. The satellite phone's antenna extending with that soft metallic whisper Kip had come to dread more than rockfall.

He wasn't supposed to be anywhere near it. He knew that. He was supposed to be in the cook tent, minding the stove, keeping the pressure up on the kettle so Tomas wouldn't have to shout across camp for hot water. But Tomas had sent him down to the cache rocks to fetch the second fuel canister, and the cache rocks sat directly behind Dev's tent, close enough that when Kip crouched among them with the canister cold against his chest, he could hear everything.

"Confirming position," Dev said. His voice had changed. Kip had been listening to Dev Kapoor talk for weeks now—smooth, amused, always performing for an audience even when the audience was only his own boots—and this voice was none of those things. This voice was smaller. Younger. "Grid reference as transmitted. We're two days out from the source coordinates, maybe three if the weather—"

A pause. The wind moved through the pass above them with a sound like sand poured over stone.

"No," Dev said. "No, she doesn't know yet."

Kip went very still. Below him, the kettle in the cook tent began its thin rising whistle, and nobody moved to quiet it.

* * *

He should have walked away. That was the thought that would keep him awake later, turning over and over like a stone in a riverbed: *I should have walked away, and I didn't.* But Kip was sixteen years old and had spent most of those sixteen years learning that information was the only currency a person without money or family or a name anyone respected could actually spend. Information kept Priya ahead of the rangers. Information kept Tomas's timber permits one step ahead of the forgery investigators. Information, Kip had understood since Pokhara, was how strays survived.

So he crouched lower behind the cache rocks, the fuel canister pressing a bruise into his sternum, and listened.

"The search is off." Dev's words came flat, stripped of their usual polish, like stones worn smooth by force rather than time. "Understood. I'll inform the team tonight." Another pause, longer. Whatever the client said next made Dev exhale through his nose—a sound Kip had heard him make exactly once before, at the ice bridge, right after the porter's rope came tight. "That wasn't the arrangement. The arrangement included a full sweep of the upper valley. Her brother's expedition—" A silence. Then, quieter, almost inaudible under the wind: "I'm aware of what I signed. I'm asking you to remember what you promised."

The phone crackled. Even from ten meters Kip could hear the tinny, compressed authority of the voice on the other end—not the words, but the cadence. It was the cadence of someone who had never once in their life been interrupted.

Dev said, "Yes." He said it twice. "Yes. Understood. Coordinates only. We proceed at first light."

The antenna retracted. For a long moment there was nothing but the wind and the far-off whistle of the kettle finally dying as someone—Priya, probably—lifted it off the flame. Then the sound of Dev zipping his sleeping bag, fast, like a man shutting a door on something.

Kip stayed behind the rocks until his knees ached. He watched the light inside Dev's tent go from lantern-yellow to nothing. He thought about what he'd heard—or rather, about the shape of it, because the words themselves were slippery things, sliding around in his head refusing to settle into meaning. *The search is off. She doesn't know yet. Coordinates only.*

Lena was sitting outside her tent. He could see her from here, a dark shape against the last grey band of sky, the way she always sat when the others turned in—facing upvalley, toward the source she'd sworn never to approach, holding something small in her hands. He didn't need to see it to know what it was. Eli's compass. She held it every night now, ever since the cache, ever since the journal page with *the eye of the river* written in a dead man's careful hand. She turned it over and over like a woman praying with a cracked rosary.

And somewhere down in Kathmandu, or Singapore, or wherever men like the client lived, someone had just decided that whatever Lena was looking for didn't matter.

Kip stood up with the fuel canister and carried it back to the cook tent, and his legs felt strange, like they belonged to someone taller.

* * *

Tomas knew immediately. That was the thing about Tomas—he missed nothing, and he pretended to miss everything, which was its own kind of kindness. He took the canister without comment, but his eyes tracked Kip's face the way he tracked weather coming over a ridge.

"You look like you've seen the yeti doing taxes," he said, in his accented English, and handed Kip a bowl of dal that steamed like an offering.

"I'm fine."

"Mm." Tomas chopped something. The knife kept its heartbeat rhythm. "In my village, when a man hears something he shouldn't, he gets this face. Like a goat caught in a fence. You have the goat face."

Kip looked at the dal. Looked at the tent wall, beyond which Lena's silhouette still sat motionless against the sky. The whole camp was here—Priya checking her bearings by lamplight, muttering grid corrections; Tomas cooking; Lena watching the dark—and Dev was in his tent with a secret the size of a valley, and tomorrow at first light they would all walk into it together like lambs following a shepherd who'd already sold them.

"If I tell you something," Kip said, "can you promise not to do anything yet?"

Tomas stopped chopping. In all the months Kip had known him, he had never once seen Tomas stop chopping mid-motion. "That depends," Tomas said slowly, "on whether the thing requires doing."

"It might. Tomorrow. Maybe." Kip took a breath. "I heard Dev on the sat phone. The client called. He told Dev to skip the search. For—" his voice dropped, though there was no one near enough to hear, and saying it felt like handling something stolen— "for remains. Eli's expedition. They're supposed to sweep the valley and look for traces, and the client ordered them to forget it. Straight to the source coordinates. And Dev said yes. Without telling her."

The knife resumed. Slower now. Tomas stared at the cutting board as if the answer to something were written between the vegetables.

"He said 'she doesn't know yet,'" Kip whispered. "Like it was a plan. Like it was already decided."

Tomas was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had lost its joke-shape entirely. "You are sure of what you heard."

"I'm sure."

"And you want to wait."

"I want to do it right." Kip heard himself, and was surprised by the sound of it—that firmness, that adultness. "If we say something now, in the dark, angry, it becomes shouting. Shouting becomes tents zipped and nobody talking for three days, and we're two days from the source with the monsoon coming. If I'm wrong, I've broken this crew for nothing. If I'm right—" He swallowed. "If I'm right, Lena needs to hear it fed and rested, not at midnight in the rain."

Tomas set down the knife. He looked at Kip for a long moment, and then he did something he almost never did: he put his hand on the back of Kip's neck, brief and heavy, like setting a seal.

"When did you become the one with sense?" he asked. "Eat your dal. First light comes early, and tomorrow we will need our ears open."

But his eyes, when he turned back to the stove, were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

* * *

Sleep didn't come for Kip, which was fine, because he hadn't really expected it. He lay in the runner's corner of the gear tent listening to the mountain breathe—the deep groan of the glacier settling, the tick of cooling rock, Priya's even breathing two meters away—and rehearsed.

That was the worst part, he decided somewhere past midnight. Not the secret itself, but the rehearsal. He lay there composing sentences the way Priya composed routes: *Lena, I have to tell you something. Lena, I heard Dev on the phone. Lena, I don't know how to say this, so I'm just going to say it.* Every version collapsed under its own weight. Because here was the thing Kip couldn't get past, the thing that made his stomach hurt worse than altitude ever had: whatever he told her, he couldn't un-tell. And once Lena knew the client had ordered them to abandon Eli—had ordered them to walk past her brother's ghost at speed, on purpose, for profit—then every step they took toward the source became a step she was taking under false pretenses. Every rope call, every shared meal, every moment of Dev's easy laughter at the morning fire would be poisoned retroactively. Kip would be the one holding the poison bottle.

He thought about the carabiner stamped with Eli's initials, wrapped in cloth in Lena's pack. He thought about her face at the cache, when she'd found the journal page—the first time Kip had ever seen hope move across that woman's features like weather, raw and unguarded, before she'd shuttered it away. Hope arriving like vertigo, Priya had called it later, quietly, and Kip had understood exactly what she meant.

And now some voice on a satellite phone had weighed that hope against a schedule and found it wanting.

Near dawn, he heard Lena stir. Heard her boots, her zipper, the small ritual sounds of her morning—water, stove, the compass clicking shut. Through the gap in the tent fabric he watched her stand facing east, where the sky was going the color of old bone, and he saw her shoulders set in the particular way they did when she'd made a decision overnight. She thinks today we search, he realized. She thinks today we start looking for traces of the expedition, because that was the deal, because Dev shook on it, because even Dev wouldn't—

"Lena." Dev's voice, from across camp, bright and terrible in its brightness. "Good. You're up. We need to talk route before we break camp."

Kip's heart slammed once, hard, against his ribs.

"Not the route," Lena said. Her voice carried clearly in the thin air, flat as a frozen lake. "First you can tell me why your sat phone was warm at nine o'clock last night, and why you've been circling me all morning like a dog that's chewed a slipper."

A silence fell over the camp so complete that Kip could hear the glacier groaning five hundred meters below.

She knew. Or she suspected enough. Kip pressed his eye to the tent gap and saw them: Dev standing by the dead fire with his hands loose at his sides, wearing the polished mask he wore for clients, except the mask had a hairline fracture running through it now. And Lena, arms crossed, the compass in her jacket pocket making a small hard shape against the fabric, watching him with an expression Kip had never seen on her face before. Not rage. Worse than rage. Readiness.

"Tomas," Lena said, without turning her head. "Priya. Get over here. All of us, now."

Kip crawled out of the gear tent into the grey morning light, and four faces turned toward him—Lena's, Dev's, Tomas's heavy with the weight of a night's knowing, Priya's sharp with instant calculation—and Lena looked at him last, and longest, and said:

"Kip. You're the one who tells me when something's wrong. So tell me."

The whole valley seemed to hold its breath. Somewhere above them, hidden in cloud, the source waited—and below them, twelve years of lies stood in a circle around a sixteen-year-old boy with a secret burning a hole through his chest.

Kip opened his mouth.
