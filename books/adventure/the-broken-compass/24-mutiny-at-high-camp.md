## 24. Mutiny at High Camp

Tomas had learned long ago that a camp told you everything about the people in it. A tidy camp meant fear held on a leash. A sloppy one meant someone had stopped caring whether they lived. Their high camp, pitched on a shelf of grey moraine above the gorge with the wind combing through it like fingers through thinning hair, was neither. It was the camp of people pretending very hard that nothing was wrong.

He saw it in the way the tents were staked—perfectly, professionally, each guyline taut as a lie—and in the way nobody was talking. Priya sat outside her tent with the maps spread across her knees, studying routes she already knew by heart. Dev stood apart near his equipment sled, checking and rechecking a satellite phone that showed no signal bars, as if staring hard enough might conjure them. And Kip—

Kip was standing by the cook fire Tomas was trying to coax to life with damp juniper, and the boy's face was doing something terrible. It was doing the thing faces do when they are carrying a secret too heavy for their shoulders and haven't yet decided whether to set it down or be crushed by it.

Tomas knew that look. He'd worn it himself once, twenty years ago, on a boat off Gdańsk, holding a letter he should have burned. He'd set it down instead of burning it, and it had cost him a marriage. So when Kip drifted over and crouched beside him, close enough that only Tomas could hear, Tomas didn't pretend not to understand what was coming.

"Tomás." The boy used the accented version of his name when he wanted something; the Polish softened it into something almost tender. "I have to tell you something. And I don't know how."

Tomas fed another stick of juniper to the flames. Smoke curled up blue and bitter. "You tell it like pulling a tooth," he said. "Quick, and then it bleeds for a while, but then it heals. You don't tell it like swallowing glass. That kills you slow."

Kip laughed—a small, cracked sound, no humor in it. "It's about the sat phone. Last night. I couldn't sleep, my head was pounding from the altitude, so I went to get water and I heard—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The client called. Dev took it. They ordered him to skip the search. For remains. Eli's—Lena's brother—they want them to go straight to the source coordinates. Straight there, no stopping. And Dev said yes. He said yes without even asking her."

The wind gusted, flattening the flame, and Tomas shielded it with his body out of pure habit. He looked across the camp at Dev, still fondling that silent phone, and felt something cold settle behind his sternum where his heart should have been.

"You're sure," he said. Not a question, really. Kip's face answered it anyway.

"I'm sure. I heard every word." Kip's hands were shaking, so he pressed them flat against his knees. "What do we do? Do we tell Lena? Do we—Tomás, what do we *do*?"

Tomas rose slowly, joints announcing themselves the way they always did above five thousand meters, and looked around the camp again. Priya with her maps. Dev with his dead phone. Lena somewhere up-slope, checking the route through the icefall they'd cross tomorrow, alone with her ghosts the way she always was.

"We tell everyone," Tomas said. "That is what we do. A crew that keeps secrets from each other dies together. I have seen it." He hadn't, actually—but it sounded true, and truth was mostly a matter of good seasoning. "Tonight. After food. Everyone sits, everyone hears it from you, because you heard it first. Then we decide what kind of family we are."

Kip looked up at him, and for a moment the teenager underneath the expedition runner showed through—all of seventeen, far from home, terrified of breaking the only family he'd ever chosen. "And if it breaks us?"

Tomas put a hand on the boy's shoulder. It felt fragile as a bird's. "Then it was already broken," he said. "We just finally see it. Come. Help me with the rice. Angry people burn rice, and I will not preside over a mutiny *and* a ruined dinner."

* * *

Dinner was dal bhat, because dinner was always dal bhat, and Tomas made it the way he made everything: heavy on the garlic, heavier on the salt, because altitude stole flavor the way it stole breath. He watched them eat and understood that he was watching the last meal of something. Families have last meals all the time without knowing it—a Sunday lunch, a birthday, an ordinary Tuesday—and only afterward does anyone realize what the meal was.

When the plates were scraped clean, Kip set down his spoon. His hand wasn't shaking anymore. Whatever Tomas had given him in that crouch by the fire—the job, the permission, the weight of being first—it had stiffened him.

"I need to tell you all something," Kip said.

The camp went quiet in the particular way mountain camps go quiet: the wind suddenly loud, the stove hissing, a tent flap snapping somewhere like a small applause no one wanted.

Kip told it straight, the way Tomas had taught him. Quick, like pulling a tooth. The phone call. The client's voice, tinny and cold through the satellite static. *Abandon the search for remains. Proceed directly to the source coordinates.* And Dev's answer—not hesitation, not consultation, just agreement, smooth as butter on warm bread.

When he finished, nobody moved. The fire popped. Somewhere below, the river ground its teeth against the gorge walls.

Lena set her plate down with exaggerated care, the way she did everything when she was most angry—slowly, precisely, as if violence were a language she refused to speak fluently. She looked at Dev.

"Say it wasn't like that," she said quietly. "Say you were going to tell me."

Dev didn't flinch. Tomas gave him credit for that much. He met her eyes across the fire, and his face was the polished outfitter's face, the Kathmandu face, the one that had closed like a shop shutter the moment things got difficult. "The call came at eleven. You were asleep. There was no decision to make—the contract specifies delivery of survey data from the source coordinates. That was always the endpoint. The search for remains was never a contractual obligation."

"The search for remains was the *point*," Lena said. Her voice stayed level, which frightened Tomas more than shouting would have. He'd seen her shout. Shouting was her armor coming off. This quiet was armor going on, plate by plate. "You stood in a teahouse in Pokhara and shook my hand and agreed—if there's any trace of Eli, the job stops. Any trace. Your word, Dev. In front of God and Priya and a pot of tea."

"And I've honored it," Dev said. "Every cache, every clue—we've followed all of them. But the monsoon is four days out, maybe less. The window is closing. If we divert now to chase—"

"*Chase?*" The word cracked out of her like a rockfall. "My brother is not a diversion. He is not a line item. He is the reason any of us are on this mountain, and if you can't see that, then you've been walking with corpses this whole trip and calling them porters."

"Tents," Priya said suddenly.

Everyone turned. She'd risen from her map, and her voice had the flat calm she got when the numbers were bad. "Storm's building. Look at the cloud over the serac ridge—if that comes down the valley tonight, we'll want these arguments conducted indoors. Or we conduct them fast."

"No," Lena said. "No, we conduct them now. Because tomorrow I lead us onto that glacier, and I need to know who's roped to me and why."

Tomas stood. He'd been waiting for this, dreading it, and now that it was here he found he was strangely calm, the way he'd been calm the one time he'd fought a man with a knife and discovered that terror and clarity feel identical in the body. He wiped his hands on his apron—he still wore the apron, even here, even at five thousand meters, because it was the last honest thing he owned.

"I will say what I came here to say," he announced. "I am the cook. Nobody listens to the cook until the food runs out, so listen now while it is cheap." He pointed a thick finger at Dev. "You. I carried your sled up this mountain. I cooked for you. I defended you to Lena when she called you a suit with a carabiner. And now I learn you take calls in the dark like a thief and sell our route to a man whose voice sounds like a filing cabinet." He turned the finger toward Lena. "And you. You knew this contract smelled like a butcher shop from day one. You signed anyway because of your brother, and I followed because of you. But I did not sign up to be lied to *inside* the lie. That is different. That is worse."

"Tomas—" Dev started.

"I am not finished." He never raised his voice; he simply let it fill the space between the tents, the way steam fills a kitchen. "So here is my threat, and I make it with love, which is the only way threats between family work. If we go to that lake and come back down without answers—without Eli's truth, whatever it costs—I turn around. I walk down this mountain alone if I must, and I will tell every checkpoint, every ranger, every official with a stamp, exactly where we are and what we are doing, and I will do it in three languages. I am old. My knees are shot. Prison has better food than my cooking anyway. You cannot threaten a man who has already eaten his own cooking for sixty years."

A sound escaped Kip—half laugh, half sob—and quickly strangled.

Priya folded her maps with a series of small, decisive snaps. "For what it's worth," she said, "I side with Lena. All the way. But I'll say the thing nobody wants said: we can't split up. Not here. Not above the icefall. Anyone who descends alone dies alone, and probably takes a rescue team with them. So whatever we decide, we decide it as one rope team, or we don't move at all."

"She's right," Dev said. His voice had lost some of its polish; beneath it Tomas could hear the strain, the failing company, the last card being played. "Which is why I invoked the contract. Not to betray anyone—to keep us moving as a unit. The client holds our permits, our extraction flight, our—"

"Your client," Lena said, "holds a leash. And you've been wearing it so long you call it a collar of honor."

Dev's jaw worked. For a moment—one raw, unguarded moment—Tomas saw the young man he must have been, the climbing partner, the friend, before ambition had laminated him layer by layer like a boot left out in the sun. "I'm trying to save something," Dev said quietly. "Maybe not the same thing you're trying to save. But I'm trying."

The wind picked up, driving grit against the tents, and the cloud over the serac ridge had gone the color of a bruise. Priya was watching it with the expression of a woman counting seconds.

Lena stood. She looked at each of them in turn—Tomas, Priya, Kip, and last, longest, Dev—and Tomas watched her do the arithmetic he'd seen her do a hundred times on a hundred dangerous days: weigh lives against objectives, risk against reason, heart against the mountain. Leadership as lonely arithmetic. He wouldn't wish it on a dog.

"Here's how it goes," she said at last. "Source first. We push to the coordinates, we complete the survey, because Dev's right about one thing—that window is closing and splitting up kills people. But the moment the instruments are packed, the moment we turn for home, Dev gives us everything. The client's name. What the company knows about Eli. Why they want this valley erased. Everything, or I burn the survey cards on that shore and he can explain to his client why his last card came back blank."

"That destroys the contract," Dev said.

"The contract died last night at eleven o'clock," Lena said. "This is what's left. Take it or try to enforce it from Kathmandu, and good luck finding a court that delivers subpoenas above the tree line."

Dev looked at her for a long time. The firelight moved on his face, and Tomas thought: *he is deciding whether to become an honest man, and he doesn't know yet that the deciding is the hardest part.*

"Agreed," Dev said finally. "Source first. Then everything."

"Swear it on something that matters," Tomas rumbled. "Not the contract. Contracts are paper. Paper burns."

Dev hesitated—then reached into his jacket and pulled out something Tomas had never seen him without: a battered aluminum carabiner, old-style, gate scarred silver. He set it on the supply crate between them like a man laying a pistol on a church altar.

"My first rack," he said. "I led my first pitch with this. On the same mountain where I last climbed with Eli Halloran." He looked at Lena. "On that. I swear it."

Nobody spoke. The wind moaned through the moraine. Above them, the bruised cloud had begun, very softly, to snow—the first flakes falling fat and silent into the fire, hissing as they died.

Lena picked up the carabiner, weighed it in her palm, and handed it back. "Keep it," she said. "You'll want it where we're going. And Dev—" her voice dropped, and only the fire and the five of them heard it, "—if there's anything else you haven't told me, anything at all, tonight is the cheapest it will ever be to say it."

Dev opened his mouth.

Closed it.

And in that small motion—the swallow, the shuttered eyes, the hand that curled closed around the old carabiner like a man hiding evidence—Tomas saw the answer to a question nobody had asked aloud yet. He glanced at Priya. She'd seen it too. Her eyes said: *later.*

Above the camp, the snow thickened, erasing the trail they'd climbed, erasing the route ahead, erasing everything except the circle of firelight and the five people inside it—who were, as of tonight, either a family again or a fuse, and no one could say which.

Tomas began gathering the dishes. It was what he knew how to do: feed people, clean up after them, keep the small fires going while the large ones burned.

Behind him, he heard Lena say quietly, to no one and to all of them:

"First light. We move for the lake."
