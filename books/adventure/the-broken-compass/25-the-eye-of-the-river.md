## 25. The Eye of the River

The valley opened the way a fist opens—slowly, grudgingly, and all at once.

Lena felt it before she saw it: a change in the air pressure, a hush that pressed against her eardrums like water. For three days they had climbed through the narrowing throat of the gorge, the walls leaning so close overhead that the sky was a rumor, and now the rock simply gave up its grip and let them through. She rounded the last shoulder of moraine with Kip's breath loud behind her and Dev's boots crunching somewhere to her left, and then there was no more wall at all.

The cirque spread out before them like something the earth had been keeping secret out of shame.

It was bigger than any map had promised. The maps—the ones she'd memorized as a girl, the ones Eli used to trace with his finger while their father cooked dinner—showed a glacier terminus and a dotted line where the surveyors had turned back. What the maps did not show was the color. The lake at the cirque's heart was blue-black, a blue so deep it read almost as darkness, as if the ice below were holding its breath. It lay perfectly still in the windless morning, and its stillness was worse than any violence could have been. Water that still, at that altitude, was water that knew what it was waiting for.

Above it hung the serac.

Lena had seen hanging glaciers her whole life. She'd crossed beneath them, camped under them, slept soundly in their shadow because fear you lived with daily became furniture. But this one made her stomach drop with an old, animal recognition. It clung to the headwall like a wave frozen mid-collapse, five hundred feet of blue-green ice leaning over the lake, fractured through with veins of darker blue where the stress was working. Even from here she could hear it—a faint ticking, a groan, the sound of a thing shifting its weight. The mountain clearing its throat.

"God," Kip whispered behind her. He'd gone quiet for the first time in days, which from Kip was the equivalent of a formal prayer.

Tomas set down his load without a word and just stood, hands on hips, looking at it the way he looked at a meal he'd spent two days cooking. Priya pulled off a glove and touched the rock beside her, as if checking that it was real.

Dev said nothing at all. He was already unclipping the satellite phone case from his pack, already scanning the shoreline with the flat professional hunger Lena had come to know on this trip—and to hate, and to understand. His eyes moved across the lake the way a man counts money.

Lena didn't look at any of it. Not really.

She looked past it.

Twelve years ago her brother had stood somewhere on this shore. That was the only fact that mattered, the only instrument she'd carried up the gorge, and now that she was here she found she couldn't stop scanning—not the serac, not the impossible water, but the ground itself. The boulders. The old moraine lines. Any flat place where a man might have pitched a tent, any cairn a dying expedition might have raised. Her eyes moved over the terrain the way a mother's hand moves over a crowd.

*Eli.* The name sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed years ago and never passed. *You were here. You stood where I'm standing.*

"Halloran." Dev's voice, careful. "We should establish base position before the wind comes up. The client wants the first readings by—"

"I heard what the client wants." She didn't turn around. "Give me ten minutes."

She walked the shoreline alone.

The going was slow—frost-shattered rock, old avalanche debris fanned out across the lower slopes like gray aprons. She moved with her hands in her pockets and her hood up, not because she was cold but because she wanted to be small, wanted to approach the place quietly, the way you'd approach a sleeping animal or a grave. Twice she stopped and crouched, touching things: a rusted tin half-buried in scree, too corroded to identify; a line of stones that might have been a cairn or might have been nothing, just the mountain being random. The mountain was very good at being random. It had been practicing for twelve years.

Near the western shore, where a low ridge of bedrock ran down into the water, she found the first real sign.

A tent platform. Faint—just a rectangle of flattened stone where larger rocks had been pried aside—but unmistakable to anyone who'd spent her life reading campsites the way other people read handwriting. Someone had been meticulous here. Someone who doubled back to check his knots, who alphabetized his gear, who wrote letters home in careful block print because his handwriting was terrible. Lena knelt on the platform and put her palm flat against the stone, and the cold of it came up through her glove and into her bones, and she stayed there until her eyes burned.

*He was right here.* Not lost in some abstract white void, not dissolved into the general tragedy of mountains. Here. On this specific stone. Twelve years ago, a twenty-six-year-old man had knelt where she was kneeling and driven tent stakes into these same cracks, and looked up at the same serac, and thought—what? That he'd found something worth finding? That he'd go home?

She didn't cry. She'd done her crying in the dark years ago, rationed it out like the last of the food, and there wasn't any left for arrival. What came instead was stranger: a kind of vertigo, the ground tilting though it held firm. All those years of refusing this valley, of building her life around its absence the way a river builds around a stone—and now she was standing in the middle of the absence and it was just a place. Cold, beautiful, indifferent. The grief hadn't waited here for her. The grief had been traveling with her all along, in her own pack, and she'd simply never opened that pocket until now.

Behind her, faintly, she heard the crew setting up. The zip of Dev's instrument cases. Tomas swearing softly at the stove. Kip's laugh, high and startled, cut short by the wind. Life continuing, which was its own kind of mercy.

When she stood, her knees cracked, and she noticed what she'd missed kneeling: scratched into the bedrock near the waterline, shallow and weathered, almost erased by frost-heave—

Two letters. *E.H.*

Her brother's initials. Beside them, a crude arrow pointing up-valley, toward the snout of the glacier where it met the headwall.

Lena stared at it for a long time. The wind had come up while she knelt, dragging veils of spindrift off the serac, and the ticking sound from the ice had grown more insistent, a clock counting toward something. She took off her glove and traced the letters with one finger. They were shallow. Hurried. A message left by someone who wasn't sure anyone would ever come.

*Pointing higher,* the old woman had said of the cache. *Always higher. Your brother climbed like a man trying to see over God's shoulder.*

"Eli," she said aloud, and her voice cracked apart in the thin air, one syllable for each word. "You absolute idiot."

* * *

By early afternoon the survey was underway, and the cirque had become a kind of theater with the crew as reluctant actors.

Dev had staked out his instrument line along the eastern shore: the laser rangefinder on its tripod, the GPS receiver drinking satellite signals, the sampling kit laid out on a ground cloth with surgical neatness. He worked fast and well, she'd give him that. Whatever else Dev Kapoor had become—polished, compromised, a man wearing ambition like a suit that no longer fit—he had never lost the technical brilliance that had made him the best rope-mate she'd ever had. He shot his bearings, logged his numbers, called elevations to Priya in a voice stripped of everything except competence.

Priya recorded. Tomas melted snow and forced hot sweet tea on everyone at intervals, doctoring Kip's cup with extra sugar the way he did whenever the boy went quiet. And Kip—Kip had appointed himself Lena's shadow, appearing at her elbow whenever she paused too long staring at the lake, saying nothing, just standing there, a skinny seventeen-year-old anchor in a red shell jacket.

"You keep looking at the ice cliff," he said finally, on her fourth circuit of the shore.

"I'm looking at the runout." She nodded at the fan of debris below the serac. "If it goes, everything inside that arc is gone. Us included."

"So why are we still here?"

Because the client wanted his data. Because the pardon was supposed to be waiting at the end of it like a coin at the bottom of a well. Because twelve years of questions were buried somewhere in this cirque under ice and silence. She said none of it.

"Because we leave tomorrow," she said instead. "First light. We get Dev his readings tonight, and in the morning we're downstream before the monsoon finds us."

Kip looked at the serac, then at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic every young climber eventually does—the sum of beauty against danger, wonder against weight. "It's the most incredible thing I've ever seen," he said. "And I want to leave immediately."

"That," Lena said, and meant it more than she'd meant anything in weeks, "is the smartest thing you've said on this entire trip."

But the afternoon wore on, and the mountain began to speak.

It started as a change in the light. The sun slid behind the western arête and the cirque fell into blue shadow, and in that shadow the lake's color deepened until it seemed less like water than like a hole in the world. Then the temperature dropped—not gradually, but in steps, as if something were descending. The wind died completely. And the ticking from the serac became a groaning, a long tectonic complaint that rolled across the water and echoed off the headwall and came back wrong, doubled, layered, as if the ice were talking to itself.

Dev straightened up from his tripod. "That's normal," he said, to no one in particular. "Glaciers settle at night. Thermal contraction."

"Sure," Tomas said, in the tone of a man agreeing with a child about monsters under the bed.

They worked faster after that. Priya shot her final waypoints; Dev packed the spectrometer with hands that moved a fraction quicker than they had an hour ago. Lena kept her eyes moving between the instruments and the ice, running the timeline in her head—how long to break camp, how far to the descent gully, how much margin the serac would give them if it decided tonight was the night. The margins were thin. The margins were always thin; that was the trade you made for places like this.

It was nearly dusk when she found the second thing.

She'd gone to retrieve the reflector prism Dev had placed on the western shore that morning, and on the way back, cutting below the bedrock ridge, her headlamp caught a glint in the scree. She crouched. Worked it free with numb fingers.

A carabiner. Old aluminum, gate frozen, stamped with wear but legible: a manufacturer's mark she recognized from her childhood, from gear-room floors, from her brother's rack. And scratched into the spine, in the same hurried hand as the bedrock:

*E.H. — 3*

Three. Three carabiners. She remembered suddenly, with a lurch, a letter from Eli's last season—he'd numbered his draws, a habit their father taught him, so he'd know if he left one behind. *If you find number seven, Lena, I've gotten careless.*

Number three had been left behind. Deliberately, maybe—or dropped, or placed. An arrow on the bedrock pointing up-valley. A carabiner in the scree. Her brother had been leaving a trail even here, at the end of the world, either for himself or for whoever came after.

"Lena!" Dev's voice, tight, from across the shore. "We're done. Readings are complete. We can—"

The serac spoke.

Not ticked. Not groaned. *Spoke*—a crack like a rifle report that echoed off the headwall and came back twice, and every person in the cirque froze mid-motion, the way animals freeze. A fine veil of spindrift lifted off the ice cliff's face and hung in the dead air, catching the last violet light, beautiful and obscene.

Nobody breathed. Ten seconds. Twenty. The echo died. The spindrift settled onto the lake, and the lake received it without a ripple, and the silence that followed was the loudest thing Lena had ever heard.

"We're leaving," she said. "Now. Tonight. Camp's struck in twenty minutes and we descend to the moraine bench by dark."

Dev was already moving, folding his tripod, and she caught his face in the headlamp glow—pale, sweat-streaked despite the cold, and wearing an expression she'd never seen on him before. Not fear exactly. Something adjacent to guilt. He kept glancing at the sample case, at the data cards zipped inside his jacket, and then at her, and away.

"Dev." She stepped into his light. "What did the client actually send you here to find?"

For a moment—one long, suspended moment, with the serac groaning overhead and the black lake holding its breath between them—she thought he was going to answer. His mouth opened. His eyes went to the ice cliff, then to the ground where she'd found the carabiner, and something moved behind them like a fish under ice.

"The source," he said. "He sent me to find the source."

And above them, in the dark, the serac began—very quietly, very patiently—to tick again.
