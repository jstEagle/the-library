## 26. The Serac Fails

Dev had spent his whole life learning to read rooms—the boardroom in Delhi where his father's silence meant disappointment, the Kathmandu offices of investors whose smiles were invoices, the tents of clients deciding whether he was worth the money he charged. He could read a room the way Priya read contours on a map: instantly, instinctively, with an accuracy that had made him rich and, in the last three years, had begun to make him sick.

The cirque at the head of the river was not a room. It was a throat.

They came up out of the moraine at first light, and Dev stopped when the lake first showed itself between two ribs of rock, because no amount of satellite imagery, no resolution of drone footage, no briefing document prepared by men in offices who would never smell ice at altitude had conveyed what it actually was. The maps called it unnamed. The client's dossier called it Source Coordinate Seven. Lena, standing beside him with her hands jammed in her pockets, said nothing at all, and her silence told him more than any name would have.

It was a lake the color of deep slate, blue-black, holding the sky upside down in its surface so perfectly that for a moment Dev felt vertigo, as if they stood at the edge of a hole drilled through the world. And above it, capping the far shore like a judgment, hung the serac—a wall of glacial ice perhaps sixty meters tall, leaning out over the water at an angle that violated every instinct Dev had ever developed about what ice should do. It was the color of old bone streaked with blue shadow. Meltwater wept from its base in silver threads. Even from this distance, even across the still air of the morning, he could hear it: a ticking, a groaning, a sound like a giant ship working against its moorings.

"Beautiful," Kip whispered behind him, and Dev thought: *yes, and that's exactly the trap of it.*

Lena turned to him. Her face was wind-scoured, her lips cracked, and there was something in her eyes he hadn't seen since Kyrgyzstan—since before, really—something searching and raw. She wasn't looking at the lake. She was looking past it, past the serac, into some country only she could see.

"He camped here," she said. Not a question.

"The cache journal says the eye of the river." Dev kept his voice level, professional, the outfitter's voice, the one that didn't shake. "This is the terminus of the accumulation basin. Everything above feeds down into—" He stopped, because she wasn't listening to geology. She was listening for a ghost.

"We survey," he said instead. "We get the readings, we mark the outflow, we photograph the serac face for stability documentation, and we are off this ice by noon." He heard himself lay out the schedule like a man laying planks over a chasm, each word a board he hoped would hold.

Tomas grunted from the rear, where he'd been redistributing weight among the porters' packs since dawn. "Noon," he repeated, in the tone of a man who had heard promises about weather and time all his life and believed none of them.

* * *

The survey itself went the way surveys go, which is to say slowly and then suddenly.

Priya established the baseline with the total station while Dev took GPS fixes along the eastern shoreline, boots crunching on ice-burnished stone, the tripod legs biting into gravel that rang faintly, musically, underfoot—hollow places beneath, melt-carved channels running under the surface like veins. The instruments were the client's instruments, expensive and precise, and Dev handled them with the tenderness of a man handling the last valuable thing he owned, which was not far from the truth. Every reading he logged was a card in his hand. Every card was supposed to buy back his company, his name, the version of himself he'd sold off piece by piece over twelve years.

He kept catching himself looking at Lena.

She had barely touched the equipment. She'd walked the shoreline instead, slow, methodical, her eyes on the ground, on the scree slopes, on the dark margins where ice met stone—and Dev understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that she was reading the valley the way he read rooms. Looking for traces. For a cairn, a scrap of fabric, a rusted stove leg. For her brother written in the landscape's handwriting.

Twelve years ago, Dev had known things about Eli Halloran's expedition that he had never said aloud. He had sat in a conference room in Kathmandu while men with better tailoring than conscience explained what had been recovered, what had been suppressed, what would be *kindest*—that was the word they'd used, *kindest*—to keep from the family. And Dev, twenty-nine years old and hungry, had nodded. Had signed. Had taken the lead guide position that opened up when Lena's name went onto the blacklist, and had built a career on the foundation of that nod, and had told himself for twelve years that the truth would have changed nothing, helped no one, saved no one.

The lie had calcified so gradually he'd mistaken it for bone.

A crack rolled across the cirque—long, deep, resonant, like a cathedral door closing—and everyone froze. The porters looked up. Kip's head snapped toward the serac. The sound echoed off the surrounding walls, doubled, faded.

"Just settling," Priya said, but her hands on the total station were very still, and her eyes flicked to Lena, and Dev saw the silent conversation pass between them: *how long, how fast, how far to the outlet.*

"Not settling," Lena said quietly. "Warming. The sun's been on that face for four hours." She checked her watch. "We have until the shade line crosses the base. Maybe less."

Dev looked at the serac and forced himself to assess it as an engineer rather than a frightened man. The fracture lines were visible now that he knew to look—vertical seams in the ice wall, dark as pencil strokes, and at the base, where the glacier's flow pressed outward over the bedrock lip, a bulge. The whole cliff was a held breath. Sixty meters of ice waiting for permission to fall.

"Thirty more minutes," he said. "I need the outflow channel profiled. That's the datum the contract specifies."

He heard how it sounded. He heard Tomas hear how it sounded. The big cook turned slowly, and his face held no anger at all, which was worse—only the flat certainty of a man watching someone make the same mistake twice.

"The contract," Tomas said, "is paper. That—" he jerked his chin at the serac— "is sixty meters of bad news."

"Tomas." Dev made his voice gentle, reasonable, the voice of a man who had talked a hundred clients off a hundred ledges. "The channel is on the east side. We're on the west. If anything lets go, the wave propagates across the lake first, loses energy against the far shore, and wraps. We'll have warning. Minutes of it."

He believed it. That was the terrible thing—he genuinely believed it, the way he'd once believed the men in the conference room, the way he'd believed every comfortable calculation that let him keep moving forward without looking down. The numbers were right. The physics were right. What the numbers did not contain was the bulge at the base of the serac, or the way the morning's meltwater threads had thickened to ropes, or the simple, ancient fact that mountains do not read contracts.

Lena was already moving toward the outflow. "I'll profile it myself," she said. "Kip, with me. Everyone else stays above the high-water line. That's not a request."

And Dev, standing among his beautiful instruments with the data accumulating on his screen like money counting itself, felt something twist in his chest—shame, maybe, or its fossil record—and said, "I'll take the near-shore transect. I'll be quick."

* * *

The near-shore transect ran along the western margin, close under the lateral moraine, forty meters from the waterline. Dev worked alone by design; he wanted, needed, a few minutes without witnesses to stand at the edge of the source of the Tsangpo and feel whatever he was going to feel. This was the place. This was the coordinate that had cost him everything he had left to spend—money, reputation, the crew's trust, Lena's contempt—to reach. Somewhere under that black water, snow that fell ten thousand years ago was beginning a journey to the Bay of Bengal. Somewhere upstream of here, in the accumulation basins below the passes, Eli Halloran had died covering another man's retreat, and that other man had lived twelve years in a valley no map admitted to, and Dev had known, and had said nothing, and had come here anyway carrying the knowledge like a stone in his boot.

He knelt at the waterline to set the last marker. The lake surface was absolutely still. His reflection looked back at him, distorted slightly by mineral film, a gray ghost in gray water. *There you are,* he thought. *That's what's left of Dev Kapoor.*

The sound, when it came, did not begin as a crash. It began as a sigh.

A long exhalation from the far shore, deep and vast, the sound of the serac finally letting go of the tension it had held since the last ice age—or since Tuesday; with glaciers you couldn't tell and it didn't matter—and Dev's body understood before his mind did. He was moving before the crack finished propagating, scrambling up the moraine slope, and he had time for exactly one look back.

The serac was falling.

Not all of it. A wedge of it, a slab the size of a cathedral roof, shearing away along those vertical seams and rotating as it dropped, ponderous and almost graceful, blue shadow and white bone turning over in the air. It hit the lake with a sound Dev felt in his sternum rather than heard—a flat, colossal *whump* that slapped the water flat and then threw it up in a white wall. The displacement wave stood up out of the lake like a thing being born, twenty meters of green-gray water crowned with spray, and began to roll across the cirque with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been stopped.

Dev's mind did the arithmetic he should have done an hour ago. Wave height at generation: twenty meters, attenuating across six hundred meters of lake width. Run-up on the western shore: five, maybe eight meters. The high-water line Lena had ordered everyone above: nine meters. The math held. The math was fine.

Except the falling ice hadn't stopped. Behind the first slab, the undermined face was going with it—ton after ton after ton, a cascade now, the whole lower wall unzipping, and each new impact stacked a new wave onto the first, and the waves met and merged and grew, and the arithmetic dissolved.

He heard Lena scream his name. Or Kip's. Someone's. The sound was thin, absurd, lost under the roar.

Then he saw Priya.

She was below the line—of course she was, of course, because the outflow channel profile was *his* task and she'd gone to finish it, quietly, competently, without telling anyone, the way she did everything. She was at the channel mouth, bent over the total station, and the wave was going to arrive at the outflow first, funneled, concentrated, the entire displaced volume of a dying glacier trying to exit through a gap thirty meters wide, and Priya was standing in the throat of it with her back turned.

Dev did not decide anything. Later, replaying it—and he would replay it for the rest of his life, frame by frame, the way he replayed the conference room, the way he replayed every moment of cowardice he'd ever committed—he would search for the decision and fail to find one. There was no weighing, no calculation, none of the careful self-interested accounting that had governed him for twelve years. There was only the sight of her red jacket against the black water and his legs simply going.

He covered the forty meters in a time he would not have believed possible at sea level, let alone at five thousand meters. The roar was enormous now, filling the cirque, bouncing off the walls, the air itself shoving at him like a hand. Priya heard him—turned—her face opening into a question—and Dev hit her at full speed, both arms around the pack harness, and threw them both sideways down into the channel's rocky throat just as the water arrived.

He got her clear. That part he would always keep. He got her clear—shoved her, actually, one final push that sent her sprawling up the channel wall onto the high rock, above the flood line, safe—and the wave took the space where she'd been standing and then took him.

The water did not feel like water. It felt like the mountain changing its mind. It picked him up the way a hand picks up a toy, and the world became noise and pressure and cold beyond thinking, and Dev tumbled in the brown-green violence with no up, no down, his body slamming off stones he never saw. Something struck his shoulder and he heard, inside his own skull, a crack that might have been rock or might have been bone. His lungs were burning. He was drowning in the meltwater of ten thousand winters at the source of the greatest river gorge on earth, and the last coherent thought he managed was not of his company, or his career, or the file in his pack with Lena's exoneration in it, but absurdly, mercifully: *at least she's clear. At least I did one true thing.*

Then his hand found rock.

A fin of bedrock jutted from the channel floor where the outflow narrowed, some ancient spine the glacier had failed to grind down, and Dev's fingers closed on it with a strength he didn't know he had, and the water tried to peel him off and couldn't quite. He wrapped both arms around the fin, cheek pressed to wet stone, and breathed when the surges allowed, coughed, breathed again, and clung.

The flood lasted minutes. It felt like years. The lake emptied its fury through the channel around him and past him, tearing boulders loose, and Dev hung on that rock fin like a man hanging off the edge of his own life—which, he understood dimly, through the roaring, was exactly what he was doing. All the small surrenders, all the comfortable nods, all the years of saying nothing: they had led here, to this stone, to this water, to this single unguarded act that had cost nothing and bought nothing and was, for that reason alone, the most honest thing he had ever done.

When the water dropped enough that he could lift his head, the cirque had changed. The lake was lower, visibly lower, its black surface agitated, steaming with churned sediment. The serac's fallen wedge lay along the far shore like a broken jaw. And on the rocks above the channel, figures were moving—red jacket, orange rope, small dark shapes rappelling fast and ugly, no technique at all, pure urgency.

Lena's voice reached him across the water, ragged, furious, terrified, alive:

"HOLD ON, DEV! *Hold on!*"

He started laughing, or crying, or both, breath tearing out of him in the cold air, arms locked around the stone. Because he had spent twelve years believing that the truth would ruin him, and here he was ruined, and here he was alive, and the words were already rising in his chest like floodwater, unstoppable, everything he knew about Eli Halloran coming up and out—

"I'm here," he shouted back, voice breaking. "Lena—I'm here, and I'll tell you *everything*—"
