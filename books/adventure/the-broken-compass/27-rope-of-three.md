## 27. Rope of Three

The lake was still rising.

Lena felt it before she understood it—a tremor through the soles of her boots, a deep subsonic groan rolling up out of the cirque like the mountain clearing its throat. The serac that had calved was only the first promise. Above the blue-black water, the hanging glacier kept shedding, house-sized blocks shearing off in slow, silent rotation before they struck and sent up walls of spray. Each impact shoved a new pulse across the lake's surface, and each pulse found the breached moraine at the outlet and squeezed through it harder than the last.

And Dev was in the channel.

She'd seen him go—seen him shove Priya bodily toward the high rocks with both hands, an act so far outside everything Lena believed about him that for one full second her mind refused to file it. Then the surge had taken him. Not the wave itself; he'd been above it, scrambling along the lateral moraine when the outflow channel jumped its banks and turned the low ground between him and them into a brown, boiling river forty meters wide. Now he was on a rock fin in the middle of it, a shark's tooth of gneiss maybe three meters long, hugging it while the flood tore past on both sides like something alive and hungry.

"Dev!" Her voice vanished into the roar. She knew it did. She shouted anyway.

He turned his head. Even at this distance she could read his face—the white ring around his lips, the way his arms were wrapped around the fin with his cheek pressed to wet stone. He wasn't waving. He wasn't calling back. He was just holding on, and holding on, and Lena had spent enough years in mountains to know what that posture meant: he had already spent everything he had getting there, and there was nothing left over for hope.

"Tomas!" She spun. "Kip! Rope, now—all of it!"

Tomas was already moving. That was the thing about Tomas that ten years of shared camps had taught her to stop taking for granted: he didn't ask questions first. He was at the gear cache under the overhang where they'd sheltered from the first surge, hauling the coil of 9-mil static line out of the haul bag, Kip beside him flaking it onto a tarp with hands that shook but didn't fumble. Priya stood above them on the rock slab, soaked to the collar, staring down at the channel with an expression Lena couldn't afford to look at twice.

"Is he—" Kip started.

"He's alive." Lena cut it off before the question could finish forming. "That's all we know and all we need."

* * *

The geometry was bad. She made herself look at it plainly, the way she'd once been famous for doing, back when her name meant competence instead of cautionary tale.

The flood channel ran diagonally below them, fed by the surging lake, maybe thirty-five meters across at the widest point and narrowing downstream where the moraine walls pinched together. Dev's rock fin sat closer to their side—twenty-odd meters of open water between them, then the fin, then another stretch of flood beyond it before the far bank rose in a wall of shattered ice-cored debris. The water wasn't uniform. It moved in pulses, matching the rhythm of the calving above: a swell every twenty or thirty seconds as a new block hit the lake, the surface rising half a meter, accelerating, then slackening slightly before the next blow. Between pulses there were perhaps ten seconds of relative mercy.

Ten seconds. Enough to throw a rope, if the rope arrived true. Not nearly enough to pull a grown man through thirty meters of current against the drag of soaked clothing and hypothermic muscles.

So they wouldn't pull him through. They'd anchor him.

"Here's how it goes," Lena said, and heard her own voice come out flat and even, the guide's voice, the armor she'd worn so long it had grown into her skin. "We fix a line to this bollard"—she kicked the horn of granite at her feet, tested it with her whole weight, felt it answer like bedrock—"and run a tension traverse down to the fin. Someone crosses to him, clips him in, gets a second loop around his chest. Then we ease him off the rock and pendulum him across on the tension line. Nobody swims. Nobody fights the water. The water wins every fight; we just don't enter the ring."

"Who crosses?" Tomas asked. He already knew. He was tying the figure-eight anyway.

"I do."

"No," Priya said from above. Her voice cracked on it. "Lena. Your shoulder—you wrenched it on the ice bridge, you've been favoring it for two days—"

"My shoulder works fine." It mostly did. "And I'm the lightest of us who can be trusted with rope ends, and I'm the one who put this team on this river, which means I'm the one who goes and gets him. That's not arithmetic, Priya. That's just the order of things."

Tomas snorted—an ugly, frightened sound disguised as contempt. "The order of things," he muttered, in his accented English, and drove a piton into a crack behind the bollard with two hard strikes, backing up her anchor because he trusted nothing, least of all granite in a flood.

Kip had gone quiet. Lena glanced at him and saw the boy standing very straight beside the coiled rope, jaw set, eyes too bright. Sixteen years old and trying to make his face into something useful. She remembered being sixteen and making the same face at her brother's back.

"Kip. You're on my brake line with Tomas. When I signal, you two lower me smooth—smooth, you hear me? No jerks. If I go in the water, you arrest and hold, no matter what it does to your hands. Gloves stay on."

"Yes," Kip said. His voice came out steady, which surprised them both.

"And if I signal three tugs, you haul like God himself is pulling with you."

"What does three tugs mean?"

"It means I've got him." She looked at the fin, at the small dark shape clinging to it. "It means I've got him, and then it's on us."

* * *

The rope sang as she went out over the channel.

She'd rigged it fast and clean: main line fixed to the bollard and backed piton, herself clipped to a cow's-tail on a sliding carabiner, Tomas and Kip paying out below in a controlled descent while the tension held her above the flood like a kite on a string. The technique was old—one of the first things Eli ever taught her, actually, on some sunlit practice cliff a lifetime ago, *the mountain doesn't care how brave you are, Len, only how neat*—and her body remembered it without consulting her. Weight on the line. Feet touching down on the few exposed stones when the water dipped. Hand over hand along the static rope, the current's spray hitting her face in cold slaps, the roar so total now that it wasn't sound anymore but weather, a climate of noise she moved through.

Halfway out, the next block came off the serac.

She felt it through the rope before she heard it—that same deep drumbeat—and then the pulse arrived, the channel swelling beneath her, brown water climbing to within a meter of her boots, flecked with foam and something pale and grinding that might have been ice. The fin disappeared. All of it. For three endless seconds Dev Kapoor was underwater, somewhere under that churning brown weight, and Lena's hands locked on the rope and every rational thought in her head was replaced by one word repeated like a heartbeat: *no, no, no.*

Then the swell passed, and the fin emerged, streaming, and Dev was still on it. Still wrapped around it. But higher now—he'd climbed during the pulse, she realized, found four more feet of rock when the water gave him no choice, and now he sat near the tip of the fin with nowhere left to go, the next surge would take him, the next surge would simply take him—

"Lena!" Priya's voice, thin over the roar. "Move!"

She moved. She stopped thinking in words entirely and let the training have her: hand, hand, foot, slide, the rope humming with tension, spray freezing on her lashes, until the fin rose ahead of her and the current slackened in its lee and she dropped the last two meters onto wet stone beside him.

The fin was smaller up close. Barely room for two. Dev looked up at her and his face did something complicated—relief and shame and disbelief all fighting for space—and his mouth moved and produced nothing but a coughing rasp.

"Don't talk." She got the sling off her shoulder, a pre-tied loop of webbing, and worked it around his chest under his arms. His clothing was saturated, heavy as chainmail, and his body underneath it was shaking in the deep rhythmic way that was past shivering and into shutdown. "Arms up. There. Good. Clip." Carabiner into the loop, cinched, checked. Second loop, backup, clipped to the main line. Her fingers were clumsy with cold and adrenaline and she made herself slow down for exactly the length of one breath, because speed was how people died, neatness was how they lived. *The mountain doesn't care how brave you are.*

Another drumbeat from the cirque. Thirty seconds, maybe less.

She leaned close to his ear. "When it hits, don't fight the swing. Let the rope hold you. We're pulling you home."

His eyes found hers. And in them, absurdly, in the middle of a flood that wanted them both, she saw something that looked like apology—not fear, apology—as if he were already beginning whatever speech he'd been carrying for twelve years and the water had simply forced the schedule.

She tugged twice. Felt the line come taut above them, Tomas and Kip taking up the tension, and then the pulse arrived and the world went white with spray and the fin rocked under them and Dev went off the rock into the air, swinging, the rope catching him with a jerk that Lena felt in her teeth—and held.

"Haul!" she screamed upward, three sharp tugs on the line, and felt the answer come down the rope as vibration and purpose, the whole crew become one muscle. Dev swung out over the channel, penduluming in a wide arc, spinning slowly, arms trailing, and for a horrible moment he looked less like a man being rescued than like something the river was refusing to give up. Then the arc carried him toward the bank, toward the bollard, toward Tomas's huge gloved hands closing on the rope and walking it backward, and he hit the scree at the channel's edge and stayed there, moving, breathing, alive.

Lena clipped herself to the traverse line and followed, hand over hand, the last twenty meters feeling longer than everything that had come before it.

* * *

They got him up to the overhang and stripped the wet clothes off him with the brisk impersonality of people who had done this before, in cold tents, on bad nights. Sleeping bag. Both stoves going. Hot sugar water pressed on him in increments, because a body this cold would vomit anything richer. He shook for a long time, curled in the bag with steam rising off him, and the crew arranged itself around him without discussion—Priya closest, monitoring, her earlier fury banked down to embers; Tomas at the stoves, muttering to the flames in Portuguese; Kip sitting cross-legged where he could see Dev's face, watching him breathe like it was a job he'd assigned himself.

Lena sat apart, wringing out her gloves, and tried to feel something other than the strange hollow arithmetic of it. A man saved. A shoulder wrenched. A rope frayed in one spot where it had run over rock, discovered by Kip's sharp eyes and retired on the spot. Everything accounted for. Nothing changed.

Because here was the thing she couldn't say out loud: she had watched Dev Kapoor shove Priya clear of a flood, and she still did not understand it. Ten years of rivalry, of polished contracts and careful distances, of a man who had always seemed to Lena like a ladder-climber wearing a climber's face—and then one honest act, thrown away in half a second, costing him nearly everything. People weren't supposed to work like that. Either you were the kind of person who stepped aside or the kind who stepped forward, and she had spent a decade filing Dev firmly in the first drawer.

The bag rustled. Dev's eyes opened, found her.

"Lena." His voice was gravel dragged through water. "I need to tell you—"

"You need to shut up and drink." She handed him the cup.

He drank. Obediently, mechanically, his gaze never leaving her face. Then, with the cup still in his hands: "The survivor. From Eli's expedition."

The camp went still. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"There was a survivor," Dev said. Each word cost him visibly, like stones pulled one at a time from deep water. "Extracted. Twelve years ago. By helicopter, at night, from a company staging camp below the valley. I knew. I've known since—" His voice failed. He swallowed. Steam rose between them. "Since before your hearing. Since before any of it."

Lena did not move. Somewhere behind her, she heard Kip stop breathing.

"The client," Dev went on, staring at the stove flame now, unable to hold her eyes, "has had him this whole time. Or known where he was. And the pardon, the file, all of it—it was bait, Lena. It was all bait, and I let them use me to set the hook, because I needed—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, wrecked. "Eli didn't just vanish. And I'm the reason nobody ever went looking properly. I'm the reason you spent ten years believing a lie."

The roar of the flood filled the silence that followed, patient and enormous, as if the river had been waiting years to hear it too.

Lena set down her glove very carefully, the way you handle something when your hands want to shake and you will not permit it.

"Start from the beginning," she said. "And don't leave anything out this time."
