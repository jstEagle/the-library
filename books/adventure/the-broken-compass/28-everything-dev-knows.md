## 28. Everything Dev Knows

The water had gone out of him in ropes, in long silver threads that steamed on the stones where he knelt. Dev coughed again and the cough turned into something that was almost laughter, which was worse, because there was nothing funny left anywhere in the world. The flood channel roared behind them, the lake already settling back into its blue-black patience as if it had never been disturbed, as if the serac's collapse had been a private matter between the ice and itself.

Lena crouched in front of him. She didn't touch him yet. He could see her deciding whether to—her hands hovering, the way she'd hovered over his name on a rope team twelve years ago and then tied in anyway, and then untied herself from him forever after Kyrgyzstan.

"Talk," she said.

Not *are you all right*. Not *you idiot, you saved Priya*. Just: talk. As if she'd known, the way she always seemed to know things about him before he knew them himself, that the water in his lungs was only half of what needed to come out.

Dev looked past her shoulder. Tomas stood at the edge of the traverse with Kip, both of them soaked to the collar, Kip's face lit with the wild animal joy of a boy who has just helped pull a man out of a river and hasn't yet learned what rivers take. Priya sat against a boulder, shaking, alive because Dev had shoved her—two seconds, maybe three, the length of time it took for the serac's face to let go and the lake to stand up out of its bed. Three seconds. He had spent twelve years buying things with three-second decisions, and this was the first one he hadn't charged anyone for.

"I need—" His voice came out shredded. He swallowed. "I need everyone to hear it. Not just you."

Lena's eyes narrowed. In the thin gold light off the glacier she looked carved, weathered down to essentials, and Dev thought: *this is what I did to her*, or rather, *this is what I failed to stop being done to her*, and the distinction mattered less than it should have. He got his knees under himself. Stood, swaying, water running out of his jacket cuffs onto stone that had never once in geological history been asked to forgive anybody.

"Then say it," Lena said quietly.

He said it.

* * *

"The survivor," Dev began, and stopped, because saying the word aloud broke something loose in his chest that he'd spent a decade mortaring over. "There was a survivor, Lena. Of Eli's expedition. There always was. I've known since 2013."

The wind came down the cirque and moved through the crew like a hand through grass. Tomas went very still. Kip's smile died by degrees, the way light dies on a peak when the sun drops behind a ridge.

"His name is Anselm Reyes. Spanish national, twenty-six years old at the time. He was the expedition's glaciologist—the client's company sent him, same as they sent Eli, same as they sent six others into a valley that had no business being open." Dev heard his own voice as if from a distance, flat and precise, the voice he used for clients, for permits, for the polished lies of logistics. It disgusted him. He kept going. "When the expedition collapsed—when the crevasse field took two of them and the storm took the rest of the retreat—Reyes made it down. Alone. Frostbitten, half-starved, carrying data cards and a dead man's notes."

"And?" Lena said. Her voice was iron. "And then what?"

"And then Meridian Extractives extracted him." Dev said the company's name deliberately, letting it sit in the cold air between all of them like a body. "Not rescued. *Extracted.* There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. A helicopter came in low over the boundary ridge at dawn—no flight plan filed, transponder dark, painted matte grey. They lifted Reyes out and flew him to a facility outside Chengdu, and by the end of the month he'd signed a nondisclosure agreement so comprehensive it might as well have been written in blood, and he was put on a plane to Madrid with money in his account and a clause over his head that would have taken everything from him, including, I believe, his freedom, if he ever spoke."

Lena's hands had closed into fists at her sides. "You knew this. You knew a man walked out of that valley alive while I spent twelve years thinking my brother's whole expedition went into the ice."

"Yes."

"You knew, and you watched me—" Her breath caught. "You came to my tent in Kathmandu after the memorial. You held my hand. You told me some mountains don't give back their dead, and that the not-knowing was its own kind of mercy, and I believed you, Dev, I *built my life* on you believing—"

"I know." His voice cracked clean through. "I know what I did. Let me finish. Please. If I stop now I'll never start again."

She let him finish. That was its own kind of terrible gift.

"Eli didn't die in the crevasse field," Dev said. "That's the official report—that he was lost with the others during the retreat, presumed buried, case closed, compensation paid, signatures collected. It's a lie. Eli died four days later, at the outflow channel of the source lake. The same channel I was just pulled out of." He pointed at it, at the churning grey water threading away between rock fins toward the gorge below. "Reyes couldn't move fast enough. His feet were gone—third-degree frostbite, black to the ankle. Eli stayed with him. Someone had to stay; the company's own protocol said no man left behind, and every other member of that team was already dead or beyond help. Eli built a shelter from the cache tarps, melted snow on a stove until the fuel ran out, and waited for a rescue that Meridian had already decided not to send."

"Why?" Kip asked. His voice was small. "Why wouldn't they send it?"

"Because of what Eli found." Dev turned to face the lake. He couldn't look at Lena while he said the next part; he'd spent twelve years not looking at her while he said it, silently, every single day, and his throat had worn smooth with the practice. "The survey wasn't just lithium. Every headwater in this range carries trace minerals down from the parent rock, and the parent rock above this cirque is wrong—it's rich in a way that gets governments nervous and companies richer. Eli mapped it. Eli *sampled* it. And then Eli did the thing that got him killed, which is that he wrote it down honestly, with coordinates, with photographs, with his own name on every page, and he cached the originals somewhere in this valley instead of transmitting them."

"He was going to be a whistleblower," Priya said softly. Not a question.

"He was going to be a witness," Dev corrected. "There's a difference too. Whistleblowers leak. Witnesses testify. Eli wanted to stand in front of someone—an inquiry, a court, the federation, I don't think he cared which—and say *this valley exists, this is what's under it, and here is who paid me to pretend otherwise.* Meridian couldn't allow a witness. So when the expedition started dying, they let it die. They pulled the weather window. They denied the resupply request that would have reached the team on day nine—they have the denial on file, stamped, initialed. And when one man crawled out anyway, they vacuumed him up before he could breathe a word to anyone, and they've been erasing the valley ever since."

"Erasing it how?" Tomas asked. His gruffness had gone out of his voice entirely; what remained was just a man asking a question he dreaded the answer to.

"Maps redrawn. Survey teams bought. The closure order that keeps this valley forbidden wasn't written to protect climbers from the mountain—it was written to protect Meridian from climbers. Every permit application routed through an office they fund. Every expedition that's come looking for Eli's route has been turned back at the boundary ridge by soldiers whose salaries flow through a subsidiary of a subsidiary. Twelve years, Tomas. They've kept a dead man's truth locked in a drawer with a living man inside it."

Kip made a sound that wasn't quite a word. Lena hadn't moved. She stood facing Dev with the lake at her back, and Dev understood, finally, in a way he had refused to understand across a decade of market stalls and polite Christmas messages and the careful choreography of two people avoiding each other in a small world, that she had carried the weight of not-knowing the way porters carry loads—distributed, balanced, uncomplaining, and crushing—and that everything he was saying now was taking the load off her shoulders and dropping it directly onto her heart.

"The pardon," she said. Her voice barely carried over the wind. "The exoneration file. Kyrgyzstan. Tell me about that."

Dev exhaled. This was the last door, and behind it was the smallest room, and he had kept it locked even from himself.

"There is no independent inquiry pending," he said. "There never was. The federation isn't sitting on evidence that would clear your name; they're sitting on a settlement. Meridian's legal department drafted the language of your rehabilitation themselves—*rehabilitation contingent upon completion of the source survey*—because they needed a guide who could get a team up here without triggering the checkpoints, and a disgraced guide with everything to gain was the perfect instrument. Your blacklist was convenient to them, Lena. It made you pliable. The pardon was bait on a hook, and I baited it. I read the terms. I understood exactly what I was doing, and I flew to Pokhara anyway, and I sat across from you at that teahouse and watched you set your one condition—if there's any trace of Eli, the job stops—and I agreed too easily because agreeing cost me nothing. Because I never intended for you to find a trace. I intended for us to reach the lake, run the instruments, transmit the data, and walk out with your name cleaned and mine saved and the drawer still locked."

Priya had gone white. "The dossier," she said. "The report I found in Kathmandu. The line about the survivor being extracted alive—you'd read it. You'd read it before you ever handed it to us."

"I wrote the summary myself," Dev said. "From Meridian's internal file. I was given access because I was going to be their instrument, and instruments get shown the manual." He laughed, briefly, horribly. "Do you want to know the worst part? The truly unforgivable part? When Eli disappeared, I was offered a choice. Meridian approached me—me, his best friend, his partner on six routes, the man who taught him to place pro on the Serac des Pères—a year after the loss. They wanted a consultant. Someone who knew Eli's habits, his caches, his thinking. Someone who could tell them where he'd have hidden data. And they offered me what they've now offered Lena: a clean slate. My own record had a stain on it by then—nothing like hers, but a stain. All I had to do was sign a confidentiality agreement and forget that my best friend's last four days were documented in a file I was legally permitted to read."

"And you signed," Lena said.

"And I signed." The words came out of him like teeth. "I told myself it was grief. I told myself I couldn't help Eli anymore, but I could help *myself*, and Eli would have wanted—no. No, that's the lie I built the house on. Eli would have been sick. Eli would have burned the offer on my doorstep. I signed because I was thirty-one years old and ambitious and afraid, and the signature took eleven seconds, and I have spent four thousand days paying interest on those eleven seconds, and today the serac came down and nearly killed me and I lay in that water thinking: *good. Good, this is what it costs,* and then your rope caught me, Lena, your rope, the woman I betrayed, and being saved by her was more unbearable than drowning, and I opened my mouth and everything came out."

The wind gusted. Somewhere above them the broken serac groaned, resettling, indifferent. Kip was crying without noise, the way teenagers cry when they're trying not to, and Tomas had put one heavy arm around his shoulders. Priya stared at Dev with an expression he couldn't read and didn't deserve to.

"So the client," Lena said at last, slowly, assembling it piece by piece the way she assembled a route. "The man on the satellite phone. The voice that ordered us past the remains search. That's Meridian."

"That's Voss. Marcus Voss, VP of Special Projects. He's the one who recruited me, twelve years ago and again last spring. He's listening to our position reports right now, probably, if the storm hasn't knocked the relay out. He thinks we're at the lake running his instruments." Dev paused. "We are at the lake. The instruments are in my pack. The data cards are in my pack. Everything he wants is forty centimeters from my hand."

"What are you going to do?" Kip asked.

Dev looked at the boy—at the kid who idolized Lena, who had stowed away-adjacent onto this expedition, who had nearly died in a slot canyon and been caught by a pack strap and a woman's reflexes—and he felt the full weight of what he was about to do land on him like the serac's shadow.

"I'm going to give them to her," he said. "All of it. The data, the file, the confession, in writing, signed, witnessed. I'm going to burn whatever needs burning and keep whatever needs keeping, and then I'm going to spend whatever years I have left making sure Meridian can't erase one more valley, one more guide, one more dead man's honest pages." He turned to Lena. "I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm telling you what's true so you can decide what to do with it. That's all a witness does. Eli understood that. It took me twelve years and a flood to catch up."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The cirque held them the way a cupped hand holds water—all of it draining slowly toward the same low place.

Lena walked to the edge of the outflow channel. She stood where Eli had stood, probably, in the last photograph he ever took, the frame Dev had seen once in the sealed file: blue-black water, hanging ice, a man's gloved thumb in the corner. She stood there for perhaps a minute. Then she came back.

"My brother stayed with a dying man for four days," she said, "so that no one would die alone. And you sold the knowledge of it for a career." She let that hang. She didn't soften it, and Dev didn't ask her to. "But you also shoved Priya clear of a wave that would have killed her, and you didn't hesitate, and hesitation is the thing I've never once seen in you when it matters." She looked at the pack at his feet. "Give me the cards."

He gave her the cards. All of them—Meridian's survey data, the satellite imagery, the internal correspondence he'd photographed in Kathmandu months ago and carried upriver like a tumor. He laid them in her palm the way you surrender a weapon: grip first, barrel last.

"And the file on Anselm Reyes," she said. "Everything. Last known extraction point, the NDA's jurisdiction, the facility records. If he's alive, we find him. If he's dead, we bring proof home. Either way, he comes off Meridian's books and into the light."

"He's alive," Dev said. "I'd stake what's left of my soul on it. The NDA has a sunset clause—ten years. It expired fourteen months ago. Voss either doesn't know or doesn't care, because a frightened man stays quiet longer than a bound one. But a man who's been free for over a year and still hiding? He's not hiding from lawyers anymore, Lena. He's hiding from what he saw. Or he's waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to come ask." Dev's voice dropped. "Eli waited for a rescue that never came. Maybe Reyes has been doing the same thing, all this time, in some hut above the tree line, keeping the faith of the last man who stayed with him. People like that don't die easy. People like that wait."

Lena closed her fist around the cards. She looked at Dev for a long time—not with forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but with something rarer: the assessment of a guide reading a slope, deciding whether it will hold.

"You lied to me for twelve years," she said. "And then you told me all of it in one breath, standing in wet clothes at five thousand meters, with the client's deadline ticking. That's not redemption, Dev. That's just the truth, arriving late." She turned toward the tents. "Get dry. All of you. We break camp at first light, and we go down."

"Down?" Kip blinked. "Not to the—"

"Down." Lena's jaw was set in the old way, the Kyrgyzstan way, the way it set when she'd already decided and the world just hadn't been informed. "Voss expects data by Thursday. Voss is going to get a phone call he doesn't like. And when he doesn't like it, he'll send people, and those people will come up the gorge, and I intend to be a very long way from here when they do—with a witness, a journal, and every card in this deck." She glanced back at Dev, and for one unguarded second something moved behind her eyes that was not warmth and not hatred but the exhausted tenderness of a woman who has finally put down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. "You called the sat phone your last card. You were wrong. You're going to make the call yourself, tonight, and you're going to tell Voss the survey failed."

Dev felt the fear rise in him like groundwater—cold, ancient, patient. Twelve years of cowardice did not drain out of a man in one afternoon. But he looked at the lake, at the channel where Eli had kept a stranger alive with a stove and stubbornness, and he found that underneath the fear there was a floor, and the floor held.

"All right," he said. "Tonight."

"No," Lena said. She almost smiled. It was a terrible, wonderful sight. "Now. Before the fear sets back in. Some doors you don't walk back through, Dev—and some calls you don't make twice."

The sat phone was in the food tent, wrapped in a sock against the cold. Dev dug it out with numb fingers while the crew watched from the rocks, five faces in the failing light, none of them offering to do it for him, all of them refusing to look away. He powered it on. The signal bars climbed, one by one, like a man climbing toward a decision.

Marcus Voss answered on the second ring, three time zones away, his voice smooth as drill casing.

"Kapoor. Tell me you have good news."

Dev looked at the lake. At the channel. At the woman standing in the wind with her brother's compass in her pocket and her brother's killer's money in her fist.

"The survey failed," he said. "The site's gone. The serac collapsed—we lost the instruments, we lost the data, and we're coming down."

The silence on the line lasted four seconds. Dev counted them, and in each one he felt lighter than he had in twelve years.

"We'll discuss this when you're back in Kathmandu," Voss said finally, and the silk in his voice had a blade under it now. "Both of you. Don't contact anyone. Don't file anything. Kapoor—do you understand me?"

"Perfectly," Dev said, and hung up, and handed the phone to Lena, and the wind came down off the serac carrying the smell of snow, and somewhere far below them, in the drowned green dark of the gorge, the river ran on toward the sea with everything it knew.
