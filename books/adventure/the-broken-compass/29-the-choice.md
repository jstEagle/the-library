## 29. The Choice

The sat phone was still warm from Dev's hand when Lena set it on the flat stone between them.

They were sitting in the lee of the moraine wall above the lake, the four of them and Anselm wrapped in every layer they owned, watching the serac across the water with the wary attention of people who had just seen it kill. The calved ice lay in shattered blue rafts along the near shore, already refreezing at their edges, and the lake itself had gone calm again—that impossible, light-swallowing blue-black, as if nothing had happened here, as if the water had no memory at all.

Lena had a memory. She suspected she would have it for the rest of her life: Dev going into the flood channel like a man stepping off a cliff he'd been standing on for twelve years, and the rope coming tight, and Tomas's hands, and Kip's voice cracking on the third pull, and then Dev coughing out half the lake and, before anyone could ask him anything, starting to talk.

He had talked for an hour. He was talking still, in a low exhausted monotone, answering Priya's questions now—the extraction, the company man named Aldous who had come to the base camp hotel in Kathmandu with a folder and a checkbook, the way everyone had agreed so easily because agreeing was easier than grief. Lena listened with only part of her mind. The other part was on the phone.

It rang at 4:17 by her watch, which meant Voss had waited exactly as long as he needed to in order to make the call feel like his idea.

She picked it up before the second ring. "Halloran."

"Ms. Halloran." The silk was back, but thinner now, stretched over something that moved underneath. "I understand there's been some confusion about the terms of our arrangement."

"Not confusion." She watched the serac. A raven rode the wind off its shoulder, tipped, and slid away downvalley. "A revision."

"Mr. Kapoor has told you—"

"Dev has told me everything." She said it evenly, and heard how the word *everything* landed on the other end of the line, the small silence it opened. "The extraction. The survivor. My brother. Twelve years of your company sitting on all of it while you bought up the survey rights to this valley piece by piece."

"Ms. Halloran." A breath, measured, patient, the patience of a man who had made many people feel unreasonable. "What Mr. Kapoor has told you is a story. What I can offer you is a future. Your record expunged. Full reinstatement. Every door that's been closed to you for ten years, opened—and not just opened. I'm prepared to make you the lead guide on our Himalaya program. Your own team. Your own routes. Real insurance, real permits, none of this—" and here she could hear him almost smile, "—this scavenging along the fringes."

Behind her, she felt the others go quiet. Kip had stopped chewing. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"You've read the file," Voss went on. "You know what a blacklist does to a person. You know what it did to your crew—they're running illegal treks on forged permits because of you, Ms. Halloran. Because of one bad day in Kyrgyzstan that wasn't even your fault. I can give all of that back. All I need is the data cards. The survey completes, we file jointly, everyone walks away whole. It's the cleanest exit you will ever be offered."

Lena looked down at her hands. They were cracked and rope-burned and steady.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Of course."

"Eli Halloran. Did he die alone?"

The pause this time was longer than four seconds. Long enough that she could hear the wind on Voss's end too—he was calling from somewhere high, or maybe that was just the sound of a man standing in an empty room.

"That's an unfortunate tragedy," Voss said, "and I understand your feelings are—"

"He stayed," Lena said. "With a fallen teammate. So the man wouldn't die alone. That's what Anselm says. That's what the journal says. And your company knew, and buried it, and then dangled my brother's ghost in front of me like bait on a hook." She turned the phone slightly, so that her voice carried back to the others without carrying to the lake. "So here's my counteroffer, Mr. Voss. You can keep your pardon. Keep your program. Keep your doors. I'll tell you what I want instead, and it costs you nothing, because it's already true: I want the world to know what happened in this valley. All of it. Anselm Reyes is alive, he's sitting next to me, and he's going home. The journal goes to the federation. The story goes wherever journalists will print it. And your survey—" she looked at the instrument case beside Priya, at the orange data cards racked inside it like ammunition, "—your survey ends here."

"Ms. Halloran." The blade was fully out of the silk now. "Consider very carefully what you're doing. Without my protection, that valley is closed territory. You crossed a boundary ridge illegally. There are soldiers downstream who answer to contracts older than your career. If you walk away from this agreement, you are not walking away as a free woman. You are walking away as a trespasser with stolen property and a fugitive in her camp."

"I know," Lena said.

"And your crew. The boy is sixteen. Do they understand what you're choosing for them?"

That one found its mark; she wouldn't pretend otherwise. She looked at Kip—at the fever-flattened face, the chapped lips, the eyes that had watched her all expedition the way she had once watched Eli—and she thought: *they chose before I did.* They had voted at high camp. They had run the rope together into the flood channel. Whatever she decided on this rock, she would not be deciding it for them.

"They're standing right here," she said. "Ask them yourself if you doubt it."

"I don't need to ask children questions." A rustle of paper, distant and obscene in its ordinariness. "Final offer, Ms. Halloran. The cards, delivered to my contact in Tingri within seventy-two hours, and the pardon files tonight. After that, the offer expires, and so does my interest in your welfare."

"Then I guess we're done," Lena said, "because I've already made my choice."

"And what choice is that?"

She looked at the data cards. Then past them, at the lake, at the place where the river began—*the eye of the river*, Eli had written, twelve years ago, in handwriting that slanted downhill like a man hurrying toward something wonderful—and she said:

"The dead don't take payment. But they keep accounts. And I'm settling his."

She hung up.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. The phone sat on the stone, dark, and the wind came off the serac again, colder now, tasting of the snow that was coming.

"Well," said Tomas. He said it the way he said most things, as if the word were a pot he was setting down heavily on a stove. "That's done, then."

"He'll send someone," Priya said. Her voice was flat with arithmetic; she was already calculating days, distances, the soldiers at the bridge, the monsoon line crawling up the forecast bands. "Maybe not soldiers. Maybe worse—lawyers first, men with papers, men who make things legal-shaped. But someone. We have maybe three days of margin, less if the weather holds long enough for helicopters."

"Then we burn them now," Kip said.

Everyone turned. He was sitting with his arms around his knees, fever-bright, and there was nothing of the stowaway kid left in his face—only the hard clean certainty of someone who had learned, far too young, that some debts are paid in kind.

"The cards," he said. "If we carry them, we're carrying the thing he wants. Every checkpoint, every landslide, every day of descent—we're protecting his property with our lives. Burn them and we're just people walking home."

"It's not that simple," Dev said quietly. "Those cards contain elevation models, flow measurements, hazard assessments. Real science. The kind of data that gets used—for flood warning systems, for the villages downstream, for—"

"For his patents," Priya said. "His mineral claims. His exclusion zones. Dev, you read the same journal pages we did. Eli found something in that cirque. Something Voss wants buried badly enough to buy a dozen careers. If we hand over the cards, we hand over the map to whatever it was."

"I know what I read." Dev's jaw worked. "I'm saying there might be a version where the data survives and the company doesn't. Copy it, strip the metadata, release it open-source after we're out—"

"With what time?" Tomas asked. "With whose hands? Look around, Kapoor. One cook with a bad ankle, one navigator, one feverish boy, one ghost, and two guides the entire industry calls poison. Who exactly is doing the careful data forensics?"

Dev didn't answer. He looked at Lena.

And Lena understood, in the strange clear way that exhaustion sometimes grants, that whatever she did in the next five minutes would be the last thing she ever did as the woman she had been for ten years. The blacklisted guide. The woman who ran illegal treks and lied smoothly and kept her grief packed away like gear she couldn't afford the weight of. That woman had spent a decade surviving. This decision belonged to whoever came next.

She knelt and opened the instrument case.

The cards sat in their foam cradle, orange and identical, each one labeled in Priya's neat hand: *CIRQUE SURVEY 1–6, FLOW SERIES, SERAC MONITORING.* Six months of planning. Dev's money. The client's obsession. Everything the modern world believed a mountain could be reduced to—numbers, grids, proprietary formats, ownership.

Beside the case, wrapped in oilcloth against her hip where it had ridden since the cache, lay Eli's journal. Water-stained, swollen, the cover soft as old leather. In it was a mineral sketch Eli had made of the cirque walls—not a survey, nothing proprietary, just a climber's curiosity rendered in pencil: *strange banding in the east face, copper-green streaks, worth a look someday.* Worth a look someday. He had died before someday came, and a company had spent twelve years making sure no one else looked either.

"Lena." Dev crouched beside her. Up close she could see what the flood channel had taken out of him—the gray under the skin, the tremor in his hands that wasn't cold. "Once these are gone, they're gone. No second copy. No leverage. Voss won't need to negotiate with us anymore, because we'll have nothing he wants except silence. You understand that? You're choosing the harder road with no bargaining chips on it."

"I know."

"The pardon was real. The file exists—I've seen it. You could have had your name back. Your routes. Your—" He stopped, and she saw him reach for the word and fail to find it, and finally he said, "Your life. The one Kyrgyzstan took."

Lena smiled, and it cost her something, and she let him see that it cost her.

"Dev," she said, "my name isn't in a file in Kathmandu. It never was. It's in a journal in my lap, written by a man who stayed behind so a stranger wouldn't die alone. That's the only record of me that matters. The rest of it—" she tapped the foam cradle, the orange cards, the whole glittering apparatus of bought absolution, "—the rest of it is just Voss's handwriting on my life. And I'm done reading it."

She took out the cards, one by one, and stacked them on the stone.

Kip produced the lighter—of course he did; the kid had firecrackers and a lighter and a talent for being exactly where fires started—and held it out to her without a word. She shook her head.

"Yours," she said. "You called it first."

His hand shook a little as he thumbed the wheel. The flame caught, steadied, stood up straight in the windless pocket of air between the moraine and the lake. Lena fed the cards to it one at a time, and plastic burns ugly, black smoke and curling stink and the labels blistering white before they brown, and she watched each one go all the way down—*FLOW SERIES,* then *SERAC MONITORING,* then the six surveys of the cirque, the eye of the river rendered into ash—until there was nothing left but a smear of melted polymer fused to the granite, already cooling, already becoming just another mark on a mountain that had collected a million of them and kept none.

The wind shifted and carried the smoke out over the water, and the lake took it, and gave nothing back.

"There," Tomas said softly, in Nepali, the word meaning roughly *it is finished,* and also, in the way cooks mean things, *now we eat.*

* * *

They broke camp in the last hour of light, fast and silent, the practiced choreography of people who had become one body somewhere on the ice without noticing when. Priya struck the tents. Tomas redistributed weight off his ankle and lied about how much it hurt. Kip, forbidden to carry more than fifteen kilos and ignoring it by twenty, coiled rope with feverish precision. Anselm watched everything with the stunned gratitude of a man relearning that other hands existed, and when Lena passed him the oilcloth-wrapped journal to hold while she laced her boots, he held it against his chest like something rescued from a house fire—which, she supposed, it was.

Dev came and stood beside her at the edge of the moraine, looking down at the lake for the last time.

"I had a speech," he said. "About how I'd fix it. About how I'd spend the company's last money getting us all out clean, and then testify, and somehow land on my feet, because I always land on my feet." He laughed, short and without humor. "Twelve years of building a life on top of a locked room, and it turns out the key was falling in a river."

"Is that an apology?"

"It's a weather report." He shouldered his pack. "Storm's coming, Halloran. The real one and the other one. Voss won't stop at lawyers. And I'm the one holding the receipts."

"Then stay close to me," Lena said. "Downstream, everyone stays within rope-length of me. Those are the new terms."

He looked at her then—really looked, the way he hadn't since Kyrgyzstan, since before, since two young climbers on a rope who believed the mountain owed them nothing—and whatever he saw in her face made him nod once, slowly, like a man accepting a verdict.

Below them the lake lay still in the dying light, blue-black, bottomless, keeping what it kept. Above it the broken serac glowed rose and gold, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful, indifferent as arithmetic. And from the outflow channel at its foot, the river gathered itself and went—over the lip, into the gorge, down through the drowned green dark toward checkpoints and bridges and the sea, carrying everything it knew.

Lena settled the journal against her spine, checked Kip's harness with two fingers out of habit, and turned her back on the source of the Tsangpo.

"Rope up," she said. "We're going home the long way."

Far below, the first rain of the monsoon hit the gorge.
