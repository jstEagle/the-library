## 30. Descent Before the Monsoon

The sky opened at dawn, and it did not open gently.

Priya stood under the overhang of the herder's hut and watched the monsoon arrive like a verdict. It came not as rain but as a wall—a gray curtain sweeping up the gorge from the south, swallowing the ridgelines one by one, erasing the world they had spent three weeks fighting through. The first drops hit the tin roof with a sound like applause, then like gunfire, then like nothing at all because no other sound could survive beneath it. The river below them, which had been a green ribbon for days, turned brown in an hour, then white, roaring with a voice that made conversation impossible and thought difficult.

She checked her watch out of habit. Six-forty. Not that it mattered. There would be no sun today, or tomorrow, or perhaps for a week. From here to the border bridges, they would move inside the weather itself, wrapped in it, blinded by it, navigating by memory and nerve and whatever fragments of map Priya could hold in her head when paper dissolved and screens drowned.

"Tell me we're not waiting for it to pass." Dev stood beside her, shouting over the din. He was soaked already, hair plastered flat, and he looked—Priya thought—not like the polished outfitter from Kathmandu anymore. He looked like a man who had been wrung out and hung to dry and found he preferred the shape.

"It's not passing," she shouted back. "This is the season now. We wait, we starve, and the soldiers get bored enough to start patrolling the high trails. We go."

Lena appeared in the doorway behind them, Anselm Reyes leaning on her shoulder. The frostbitten ghost of Eli Halloran's expedition had made eleven kilometers on his own feet yesterday, a miracle measured against what he'd been two weeks ago, but his face was gray and his hands shook, and every step cost him something he couldn't afford to spend twice. They had lashed together a litter of tent poles and pack straps for the worst sections. It waited outside the hut like a patient animal.

"How far to the confluence?" Lena asked.

Priya closed her eyes and let the gorge assemble itself behind her lids—the way she always did now, the maps she'd memorized in Pokhara layered over everything she'd seen since. "Nineteen kilometers if the lower trail holds. Fourteen if we take the landslide scar above the hot springs, but that's exposed for two hours and in this rain it'll be running mud." She opened her eyes. "There's a third option. The herders' path on the true left bank. It's not on any survey. Old Tomas found it once, years ago, hunting routes nobody used. It stays under the tree line, it crosses the river four times on log spans, and it's steep enough that the mules never went there—which means the checkpoints won't be watching it."

"And the logs," Tomas said from inside the hut, where he was repacking the food bags with the grim economy of a man who had already counted every calorie twice, "will be underwater or gone."

"Some of them," Priya admitted. "Maybe most of them."

Tomas grunted, which from him was a full speech. He emerged with the bags distributed among six packs, having decided without discussion that Kip would carry the lightest and Lena would carry none of Anselm's weight on her back because someone had to stay unencumbered enough to think. That was how the crew worked now—decisions arriving pre-made, distributed like load, so that no single failure could sink them all.

Kip came around the corner of the hut at a run, firecrackers and sat phone secured in waterproof bags against his chest, and skidded to a stop in front of Priya with the particular brightness he reserved for her. Of all of them, the boy had decided that Priya was the one who knew things, and he had appointed himself her shadow, her apprentice, her question-asker.

"Which way?" he asked.

"The way that isn't a way," she said. "Stay close to me. When I stop, you stop. When I say jump, ask how far after you land."

He grinned like she'd given him a gift.

They left the hut at seven, into the teeth of it.

* * *

The first hour taught them the grammar of the monsoon.

Rain in the lowlands was weather; rain here was geology. It moved soil, moved stone, moved whole hillsides with a patience that made landslides seem less like disasters than like sentences finally being completed. Priya led them along the true left bank on a path that existed mostly as a rumor—a line of flattened fern, a notch in a root system, the memory of a herder who had walked it before either of their grandfathers was born. Above them the slopes ran with water, dozens of small silver threads braiding into channels, the channels into cataracts, each one carrying the mountain downhill grain by grain.

She navigated by counting. Steps between landmarks, minutes between bends, the rhythm of the river's sound changing as the gorge narrowed and widened. Her GPS unit had died two days ago, drowned in a creek crossing despite its waterproof rating, and she had felt the loss like the loss of a tooth—annoying, then oddly liberating. Now there was only her head, and her head had never lost signal yet.

At the first river crossing, the log span was exactly as Tomas had predicted: gone. What remained were two moss-slicked trunks angled into the flood like the ribs of a drowned animal, and beyond them thirty meters of whitewater that would kill any of them in ninety seconds flat.

Lena studied it for ten seconds, then started giving rope calls, and Priya fell into the old choreography without thinking. This was the thing about the crew that outsiders never understood—that what looked like chaos was actually a language, that every shout and gesture had been worn smooth by years of use until the words themselves almost didn't matter anymore. *Anchor.* *On belay.* *Watch my line.* Kip fed rope. Tomas anchored. Dev, who three weeks ago would have insisted on his own equipment and his own protocols, clipped into Lena's system without a word of argument, and when the crossing went wrong—when Anselm's litter swung wide in the current and dragged him toward the seam where the water folded over itself—it was Dev who went in waist-deep on the tag line, Dev who took the hit of the current across his chest, Dev who held until they hauled all three of them ashore.

Nobody said anything about it. That was the language too.

They crossed four times that day, and each time the river was higher than the last.

* * *

They camped that night in the hollow beneath a fallen rhododendron, a cathedral of wet black wood with room enough for five bodies and a stretcher, and they ate cold rations because Tomas refused to risk a stove under the canopy. Leeches found them within twenty minutes of stopping. They always did. Priya had long ago stopped flinching at the small dark loops of them working through her sock seams, fat and glistening, drinking with a patience she almost admired. You learned to check each other. That was the ritual: sit in a circle, lift the hems, pick them off one another's necks and backs where fingers couldn't reach. Kip had named the biggest one and declared it his pet before Lena made him burn it off with salt.

"You're smiling," Lena said quietly, beside her. Both of them on watch while the others slept, shoulders touching in the dark.

"I'm counting," Priya said. "We're fourteen kilometers from the confluence. The bridge is at eighteen. And I know where every checkpoint is between here and there, because I've been memorizing this valley since I was nineteen years old and lying to men about why I wanted to know."

Lena was quiet a moment. Rain drummed the dead wood above them like a second heartbeat.

"When this is over," Lena said, "what will you do?"

Priya considered it honestly, which was rare for her. "I don't know. For years the answer was 'disappear properly.' New name, new country, some place flat where nobody asks about mountains." She picked a leech off her wrist and flicked it into the dark. "Now I think maybe I want to be somewhere people can find me. It's a strange feeling. I don't trust it yet."

"It gets worse," Lena said. "Trust me."

And Priya laughed—actually laughed, a short rough sound swallowed immediately by the rain—and realized she couldn't remember the last time she'd done it. Somewhere in the last week, without ceremony, the blacklist had stopped being the center of her life. There were things heavier than shame. A man pulled half-drowned from a flood channel. A boy who ran toward danger carrying fireworks. A cook who wrote letters home every night and burned them every morning. She had spent nine years defining herself by the lie she'd told to save a client's life, and the lie had shrunk, somehow, to the size of a pebble she could finally set down.

* * *

The second day, the mountain tried to kill them twice.

The first time was small, almost polite: a debris flow coming down a side drainage as they traversed beneath it, a brown tongue of slurry moving at walking pace, and they simply outran it laterally, jogging the traverse with Anselm carried piggyback between Dev and Tomas while Kip scouted ahead. Nobody even raised their voice. Priya noted afterward that her hands weren't shaking, and understood that fear had become a tool they kept sharpened rather than a state they fell into.

The second time was not polite.

It came at midday, on the stretch where the herders' path clung to a slope of shattered schist above the river. Priya heard it before anyone—a deep subsonic groan, the sound of a hillside clearing its throat—and she was already turning, already shouting *up-slope, UP*, when the world above them began to pour itself downward.

Not a slide. A *flow*—hundreds of tons of saturated rock and timber liquefying, taking the path with it, taking the fern and the roots and the very idea of a trail. They scrambled forty meters up through the trees as the avalanche roared past below them, close enough that the spray of it soaked them through, close enough that Kip, hauling on a root, looked down into the churning brown and saw a whole tree spinning in it like a matchstick.

When it was done, the silence rang.

Priya looked at the gap where the path had been. Fifty meters of nothing. Beyond the nothing, the path resumed, visible on the far side like a promise made by someone else.

"Options," Lena said. Her voice was level. Priya loved her a little for that, and said so to no one.

"We climb," Priya said. "Straight up, through the slide scar, to the bench above. Then contour west until we drop back onto the path past the scar. Three hours. Maybe four in this visibility. The bench is forested, so the rain won't move us, but the ground will be loose and there's no route finding—I have to feel for it."

"Then feel," Lena said.

So she did. She went first, alone, testing each hold, reading the mountain the way she read faces—for the truth underneath the noise. The others followed in her exact footsteps, a human chain handing up packs and passing Anselm hand over hand, and somewhere in the second hour, in the white blindness of the rain, Priya realized she had stopped navigating altogether. Her feet knew. Her hands knew. Nine years of hiding had taught her every kind of terrain this country owned, and the knowledge lived in her body now, deeper than memory, deeper than the maps.

She surfaced onto the bench at 3 p.m., hauled Kip up beside her, and turned to watch the rest of them come up out of the murk one by one—Tomas with Kip's pack on top of his own, Dev bleeding from a scratch on his scalp he hadn't mentioned, Lena last with Anselm's arm over her shoulders, both of them gray-faced and grinning like idiots.

They stood on the bench in the pouring rain, six people who had no right to still be alive, and Kip started laughing first, and then they all did, helplessly, the laughter torn away by the wind as fast as they could make it.

* * *

They dropped back onto the path at dusk and made the confluence by dark, and Priya allowed herself, for the first time in three weeks, to think about the bridge.

The bridge was the door. Everything beyond it was the world again—roads, telephones, journalists, consequences. Everything before it was the valley, and the valley had taken enough from them. But the soldiers at the bridge had been reinforced since Kip's diversion downstream, and Dev's radio intercepts—if you could call Kip's eavesdropping intercepts—suggested patrols now walked the approaches every forty minutes.

Tomorrow night, then. Tomorrow night, in the deepest trough of the storm, when even soldiers huddled under tin roofs and wished themselves elsewhere.

That evening, drying by the smallest fire Tomas dared, Priya took out the last of her maps—one sheet, soft as cloth now from being unfolded a hundred times—and smoothed it on her knees. Lena sat down across from her without asking, the way family does.

"Show me," Lena said.

Priya showed her. The bridge, the approach trails, the timing of the patrols, the fallback route if the first failed, the second fallback if the second failed. Lena listened the way she listened to everything, completely, and when Priya finished, she reached out and put her finger on the bridge itself, on the inked rectangle that meant safety or capture.

"One more thing," Lena said. "If it goes wrong—if they take someone—you take Kip and Anselm and you don't look back. That's not a request."

Priya looked at her for a long moment. Then she moved Lena's finger aside and tapped the bridge again, twice.

"Noted," she said. "Ignored, but noted."

Lena smiled, tired and real, and outside the rain kept falling on the valley that had tried to keep them, and Priya folded her map away and began, in the privacy of her own head, to count the hours until dark.

* * *

The storm broke fully open an hour before midnight, as if the sky had been saving its worst argument for last, and Priya stood at the edge of the treeline above the bridge approach and watched the lights of the checkpoint swing through the rain below—two torches, moving slow, forty-minute circuits, just as she'd promised.

Behind her, the crew waited in the black: Tomas with his ankle bound tight, Dev with the rope coil, Kip vibrating with the effort of stillness, Anselm propped against a trunk, breathing hard, alive, impossibly alive. And Lena, beside her, watching the same lights, wearing the expression Priya had first seen on a glacier in Kyrgyzstan a decade ago—the expression of a woman about to do something the world would call reckless and the mountains would call correct.

"Priya," Lena murmured, eyes on the torches. "How long since the last patrol passed?"

Priya checked her watch, then the lights, then the water sheeting off the bridge cables below, silver-bright in the torch beams.

"Thirty-one minutes," she said. "Nine to make the crossing. Somebody had better pray I'm still the navigator I used to be."

Below them, the torches paused. One of them lifted, slowly, and swept the darkness up the slope—directly toward the treeline where six shadows held their breath.

The beam climbed. Closer. Closer.

Priya's hand found Kip's shoulder and pressed him down into the wet fern.
