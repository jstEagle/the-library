## 31. The Landslide

Tomas had cooked for armies, for fishing boats in the Barents, for a two-star kitchen in Lyon where the chef threw pans at his head, and none of it—not one hour of any of it—had taught him anything as useful as knowing when a mountain was about to lie to you.

He felt it in his teeth first. That was how he'd always described it to Kip, back when the boy still asked questions instead of just watching: *the mountain talks to you through your fillings, chico. Old metal remembers.* It was nonsense, mostly, but nonsense that kept men alive. The air pressure shifting. A hum below hearing. The particular silence of water deciding to go somewhere else.

They were three hours out of the herder's hut, descending the gorge with Anselm Reyes strapped into a litter between two mules, and the monsoon had stopped pretending to be weather and become a fact of the world. Rain came sideways off the walls of the valley, warm as bathwater and endless as grief. Tomas walked rear guard because he always did—someone had to be last, and last was where the slow ones lived—and because from behind he could see all of them at once: Lena breaking trail, Priya reading the slope like a woman decoding a letter she already knew was bad news, Dev carrying more than his share without being asked, and Kip.

Kip, who should not have been there. Kip, who was seventeen and weighed nothing and moved through rain like it was a game invented for his benefit.

Tomas had decided, somewhere around the second day of descent, that if they made it out, he was going to tell the boy the truth about himself—that Tomas had a daughter in Gdańsk who thought he was dead, or close enough, twelve years of postcards that said *still working, still sorry*—and that loving someone meant standing where the rock would hit you first. He'd been composing the speech for days. He was on the third draft when the mountain spoke.

It didn't roar. That was the thing no one believed afterward. It *clicked*—a sound like a lock turning, deep inside the slope above them, and then the whole face of the valley simply changed its mind.

"Off the trail!" Lena's voice went up like a flare. "High ground, NOW—"

Tomas didn't think. Thinking was for people with time. He saw Kip ten meters behind him, frozen in the way young people freeze—not from cowardice but from the brain's honest refusal to accept what the eyes reported—and Tomas covered those ten meters in four strides that would have made his old rugby coach weep, and put his shoulder into the boy's chest, and drove them both toward the scree apron at the base of a granite fin.

The world came apart behind them.

It wasn't an avalanche sound, exactly. It was the sound of a hillside unbuttoning itself—a long wet tearing, then a rush like the river changing course, then everything at once: mud and boulders and whole trees traveling sideways, the trail they'd walked thirty seconds earlier ceasing to exist, the mule train's spare gear—tents, the food barrels, Priya's maps, half the rope, the medical kit, Dev's precious survey instruments—swallowed in a brown cataract that poured down into the gorge and was gone.

The shockwave hit them as wind and grit. Tomas had Kip under him, curled around the boy like a wall, and something struck his left ankle—a glancing blow, a stone the size of a loaf of bread moving at the speed of a falling car—and he heard it before he felt it, a wet crunch that he recognized with professional calm as *not good.*

Then it was over. Rain again. Just rain.

"Tomas." Kip's voice, right against his ear, shaking. "Tomas, you're bleeding—you're—"

"I'm fine."

"You're not—"

"Kip." He lifted his head. The boy's face was an inch away, gray with terror, rain running off his chin. "Look at me. Are you hurt? Anywhere? Answer properly."

"No. No, I don't think—I'm okay."

"Then I'm fine." He said it the way he said grace: firmly, so God wouldn't argue. "Everything else is negotiable."

* * *

They gathered at the granite fin, seven people and two mules and what remained: the litter with Anselm, one tent fly, a cook pot, three days of food if they starved politely, Lena's rope coil, the sat phone, Eli's journal in its oilcloth, and the compass. Tomas inventoried it aloud while Lena and Priya stood looking at the scar on the hillside—the raw wound of it, forty meters wide, running from the ridgeline down into white water.

"The trail's gone," Priya said quietly. "Not blocked. Gone. There's no cutting across that—it'll be sloughing for days."

"Then we don't cross it," Lena said. "We go over. The goat track on the east wall, up through the birch, traverse high, drop back to the river below the confluence."

"That's two days' climb with a stretcher case."

"It's one day less than standing here."

Tomas let them argue and got on with the ankle. He knew what he'd find before he unlaced the boot, and he was right: swelling already rising like bread dough, the joint wrong in a way ankles shouldn't be, pain arriving in slow deliberate waves that he catalogued and set aside. Fracture, probably. Bad sprain at best. He bound it tight with the last of the tape, splinted it with a trekking pole cut to length, and laced the boot back over all of it until the boot became the cast.

"You're limping," Kip said, watching.

"I'm walking," Tomas corrected. "Limping is a state of mind."

Dev crouched beside him, offered a hand up. Tomas took it, which cost him something—he was not a man who took hands—but the arithmetic was simple. "How bad?" Dev asked.

"How bad do you need it to be?"

Dev almost smiled. "I need it to not be bad."

"Then it isn't." He tested weight on it. The pain went up his leg like a struck bell, and he kept his face doing nothing at all. "See? Already better."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, up through birch scrub gone gold-green with rain, and Tomas learned the new grammar of his body: weight on the heel, never the edge; rest-step; the pole as third leg. Kip hovered. That was the worst part—not the ankle, but the hovering, the boy's eyes tracking him across every slope like Tomas might come apart at the seams.

"Stop watching me," he said finally, when Kip had caught him for the fourth time leaning on a trunk longer than a rest required.

"I'm not—"

"You're watching me like I'm dying. I twisted my ankle saving your stubborn life. This is the proudest I've been in twenty years. Let me have it."

Kip's jaw worked. "You could've died."

"Yes."

"You just—" The boy's voice cracked, and he hated it, Tomas could see him hating it. "You didn't even hesitate. You just came."

Tomas looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off the birch leaves. Somewhere below, the river kept up its argument with the gorge.

"Listen to me, chico, because I'll only say this once and then never again, and if you repeat it I'll deny it under oath." He kept his voice level, almost bored. "There is no version of that moment where I do anything else. None. You could search every Tomas in every world and you would not find one who hesitated. So don't make it heavy. It was light. It was the easiest thing I've ever done. Now pick up the pot and walk."

Kip picked up the pot and walked. But halfway down the switchback, without turning around, he said: "My mother left when I was nine. Nobody ever came for me before. Not once."

Tomas said nothing. Some things you answer by being there tomorrow, and the day after, and he intended to be insufferable about it.

* * *

They camped on a shelf above the river, one fly strung between two trees, seven bodies packed beneath it like spoons in a drawer. Tomas cooked the last real meal—rice, the dregs of the dal, a handful of foraged nettle greens that Kip had gathered with suspicious competence—and served it in shifts because there weren't enough bowls. He ate last, standing, favoring the ankle, and watched his strange family eat in the rain-shadow of a single tarp: Lena feeding Anselm broth with the patience of a woman feeding a fire she intended to keep burning all winter; Priya redrawing their route from memory onto the back of a ration wrapper; Dev sitting apart, holding the sat phone like a man holding a live grenade, because the client had called twice since the landslide and twice Dev had let it ring.

"They'll send people," Priya said, not looking up from her map. "When the check-ins stop. The company has contractors in the valley—we passed their supply cache on day six. They'll come upriver after us."

"Hunted, then," Dev said.

"We were always hunted," Lena said. "Now we're just slower."

Tomas scraped the pot clean and said the thing he'd been chewing since noon. "Slower is fine. Slower is survivable. What's not fine is us pretending the boy can run if it comes to running." He nodded at Kip, who stiffened. "And it can't be me carrying him. You all saw why."

Silence under the tarp. Rain on canvas, soft as fingertips.

"So we change the shape of it," Lena said at last. "We stop being a column. We become water—split up, small groups, different lines, same destination. Harder to follow. If one group gets stopped, the others keep moving." She looked around the circle. "Tomas takes Anselm and the mule, the low route, slow. Priya and Dev take the high traverse. Kip comes with me—we move fastest, we scout, we signal from the bridge."

"No," Kip said.

"Kip—"

"You're splitting us up because of me. Because I'm the slow thing now, except now it's Tomas instead, and—" He stopped, breathed. "I'm not a kid you pass hand to hand. I carried my weight the whole way in. Give me the scouting line. Give me something real."

Tomas watched Lena weigh it—the arithmetic she did, the lonely kind, the kind nobody thanked you for. He saw her arrive at the answer he'd already reached, and hated it, and respected it.

"Fine," she said. "But you walk behind me. Always behind me. And if I say drop, you drop before the echo."

"Deal."

* * *

Tomas took the last watch, because his ankle wouldn't let him sleep anyway and because watch was the one job where sitting down was a virtue. He sat with his back against the mule's warm flank, the boy asleep against the other side, and wrote the letter he'd been writing in his head for eleven days.

*Marta,* he wrote, on the back of the ration wrapper, in pencil worn to a stub. *By the time you read this—if you ever read this—I will either be famous or arrested, and knowing your father, probably both. I want you to know that the man you remember, the one who left, turned out to be worth something in the end. Not much. Enough. I stood where the rock was coming down and I did not move, and I would do it again tomorrow, and that is the whole of what I have to give you. Be taller than me. Love someone inconveniently. — T.*

He folded it into the oilcloth with Eli's journal, where it would travel with the truth whether he made it out or not. Then he sat and listened to the mountain.

It was talking again. Low, in the bones, in the fillings. Not tonight, maybe. Maybe not even tomorrow. But the gorge was full now, swollen drunk on monsoon, and somewhere upstream the company's contractors were moving, and the trail behind them was a brown scar that led straight to them like an arrow drawn on a map.

Near dawn, Priya touched his shoulder and pointed downstream, where the mist was lifting off the water.

A light. Far off, low on the far bank, sweeping slowly along the collapsed trail. Searching.

Tomas closed the pencil stub in his fist and woke the others without a word.
