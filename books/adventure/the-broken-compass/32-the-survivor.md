## 32. The Survivor

The hut appeared out of the weather like something remembered rather than seen.

Lena had almost walked past it. In the white murk above the tree line, stone walls the color of the stone they stood on could vanish entirely, and for three hours she had been navigating by the pressure of the wind against her left cheek and the memory of a contour line Priya had traced with a fingernail two nights ago. The slope had been letting go in small ways all day—trickles of scree, a hiss of meltwater underfoot, the mountain clearing its throat. They needed shelter. She had told herself that much, over and over, the way you tell yourself a rope will hold: not because you believe it, but because the alternative is to stop moving, and stopping is how people die at this altitude.

"There," Kip said, and his voice cracked on the word, and Lena followed his mitten to a dark seam in the gray.

A herder's hut. Dry-stone walls, roof of overlapping slabs weighted with more stone, a doorway so low it was less a door than an apology. Smoke-blackened rock around a vent gap in the roofline, which meant it was used, which meant someone might be inside, which meant—after everything, after the soldiers at the last bridge and the landslide that had eaten their tents and half their food and very nearly Kip—another negotiation, another risk.

Lena stood in the blowing snow and felt something in her chest lean toward that dark doorway with a hunger that had nothing to do with warmth.

"Could be empty," Tomas said behind her. He was moving badly, ankle wrapped in a strip of tent fly, leaning on an ice axe he'd shortened for the purpose. He hadn't complained once since the slope let go. That was its own kind of frightening. "Could be full of soldiers."

"Soldiers don't camp above the tree line in the first storms of the monsoon," Priya said. Her lips were the color of the slate underfoot. "Herders do. Yaks do. Yaks are the better bet."

"Yaks I can handle," Tomas said. "It's the herders with rifles I've been praying to avoid."

Lena didn't answer. She was watching the doorway, and she couldn't have said why. Something about it. The way the snow in front of it was trampled—not the wide, aimless churn of animals but a narrow path, beaten by boots, one set of boots, going in and out and in again over years. A path worn by a single solitary life.

"Wait here," she said.

"Lena—" Dev started.

"Wait *here*." She said it without turning, and the tone closed the conversation the way a door closes.

She crossed the twenty meters alone, boots punching through the crust, and the wind tried to take her hood, her breath, her name, all of it. At the doorway she stopped. Snow had drifted against the threshold in a soft fan, and the fan was broken in the middle by that worn path, and she thought: *twelve years*. The number arrived in her uninvited, the way it always did, the way it had arrived in the village below when the old woman had pressed the carabiner into her palm—*E. H.* stamped on the gate, the metal worn smooth by a decade of someone else's hands.

She ducked inside.

The hut's interior was one room, round-walled, dim, lit by the gray glow of the roof vent and the coals of a fire that had been fed recently enough to still breathe. Dried dung cakes stacked like coins along one wall. A sleeping platform of planks and yak hair rope. A copper kettle. A tin trunk with a broken latch. And hanging from a peg by the door, a bundle of dried herbs and, beside it, a jacket—canvas, patched at the elbows with mismatched cloth, mended and mended again, the kind of garment that outlives its owner's plans.

The man himself was sitting against the far wall with his back very straight, the way soldiers sit, or the way people who learned to sit like soldiers and never unlearned it. He was gaunt in the way that altitude and time together make people gaunt, the face drawn down to its architecture—cheekbones, brow, jaw. His beard was streaked with gray. His hands rested open on his knees, palms up, and the fingers of both were wrong—shortened, blunt, the frostbitten stubs of a man who had paid the mountain a toll in installments.

He was looking at her. He had been, she realized, since the moment she'd come through the door, and he hadn't cried out or reached for a weapon, and there was something in his face—not fear, not quite, but the particular stillness of a man who has been waiting so long for a thing that when it comes he doesn't trust it.

"You're not soldiers," he said. His voice was rusty, a hinge unused. He spoke English with an accent she couldn't place—Spanish underneath, and something high-altitude and Himalayan laid over it like a second skin. "I watched you come up the moraine. Four of you, and a boy. Soldiers don't bring a boy."

"No," Lena said. Her heart was doing something strange, hammering and hollow at once. "We're not soldiers."

He nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something. His eyes went past her to the doorway, to the shapes of the others hunched against the wind outside, and then came back to her, and traveled down, and stopped.

She had taken off her pack inside the door. It hung from her shoulder now, and clipped to its webbing, where it had ridden for the entire expedition—through checkpoints and ice bridges and the whiteout and the gorge and the flood and the burning of the survey cards—was the compass. Eli's compass. Brass casing, cracked crystal, the needle frozen at a few degrees off true, pointing at nothing and at everything.

The man made a sound. It was not a word. It came out of him like air leaving a punctured lung, and his hands came up off his knees, those ruined hands, reaching and then not reaching, hovering in the space between them as if the air had turned to glass.

"Where did you get that," he said. Not a question. A man reading his own sentence off a wall.

Lena's mouth was dry. "It belonged to my brother."

The fire ticked in its bed of coals. Outside, the wind found a new register, a long low note across the roof slabs. The man—she knew, she already knew, the way you know a summit before you reach it, from the shape of the ridge—closed his eyes.

"Eli," he said. "Eli Halloran." He said the name the way you say the name of someone you buried. "He carried that compass on a lanyard his sister made him. He told me about her. On the glacier, at night, when the wind was too loud to sleep. He said she was the best guide he'd ever seen and that she'd have hated every decision we made." His eyes opened. They were wet and dark and terribly, unbearably alive. "He was right. I've had twelve years to check."

* * *

She didn't remember calling the others in. Later she would piece it together: her hand on the door, her voice saying words, the crew filing into the dim room one by one and stopping, one by one, the way people stop at the edge of a drop. Tomas filling the doorway with his bad leg and his ice axe and his eyes going soft and hard at once. Priya pressing a hand to her mouth. Kip staring at the man's hands with the frank horror of the young and then, worse, at his face, because Kip had grown up on the story of Eli Halloran the way other kids grew up on saints.

Dev came in last. He stood just inside the threshold with snow melting off his shoulders and dripping, drip, drip, onto the stone, and he did not look at the man at all. He looked at the floor. Lena registered that, filed it, and set it aside, because there was no room in her for it yet. There was no room in her for anything except the man against the wall.

"Sit," he said, and his voice was steadier now, the rust burning off. "Please. Sit. I have tea. I have—there's tsampa, there's a goat I share with a family two valleys over when they come up in summer, they don't know I'm here, they think the hut is a ruin—" He stopped himself. Laughed, a small broken sound. "Listen to me. Twelve years of talking to yaks and I've forgotten how to do this."

They sat. The kettle went on the coals. The ritual of it—hands, water, the bitter smell of brick tea—gave them all somewhere to put their eyes while the room rearranged itself around the impossible fact of him.

"Anselm," he said at last. "Anselm Reyes. I was the expedition medic." He said *was* the way other men say *am*. "You're Lena. He talked about you so much I feel I've known you for twelve years, and I have, I suppose, in a way I never wanted."

"Why here?" Lena asked. Her voice came out flat, guide-voice, the voice she used on exposed ground. She was grateful for it. It was the only part of her that was working. "Why this hut, why—twelve years, Anselm. There were searches. There was an official report. There was—" She stopped, because the official report was in Dev's dossier, and the dossier was in her pack, and the report had said *no survivors recovered*, and the report had been written by the client's company, and she was beginning, with a slow cold vertigo, to understand what that meant.

"Because I ran," Anselm said simply. "Because they came for me and I ran."

"Who came for you?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then at Dev, who still hadn't raised his head. Then back.

"Sit closer," he said. "And give me your hands. Both of you. What I have to tell you, I'd tell you holding on to something, and you're all I have."

* * *

The tea went around. He drank his in both ruined hands, lifting the tin cup with a care that was painful to watch, and while he drank he looked at the compass where it hung on her pack, and Lena understood that he had been looking at it, in one way or another, for twelve years.

"We made the source," he said. "You should know that first. Eli stood on the shore of that lake. He filled a bottle with the water. He said—" Anselm's voice caught, steadied. "He said he was going to bring it back for you. He said, *my sister swore off this valley, so I'll carry it to her.*" He laughed, or wept, it was impossible to say which. "Twelve years, and I never knew where to bring it. The bottle was in my pack when I came down. I lost it in the river crossing. I have grieved that bottle like a child."

Lena's hands were in her lap. She was holding them very still, because if she didn't hold them still she did not know what they would do.

"Tell me the rest," she said. "All of it. From the beginning."

And he did. He talked as the light in the roof vent went from gray to darker gray, as the storm arrived in earnest and the hut shook and held, as Kip fell asleep against Tomas's shoulder and Tomas stayed awake anyway, as Priya fed the fire with the economy of a woman who has learned that fuel is a form of time. He talked, and the story came out of him in the order that wounds come out—sideways, out of order, circling back, the way a man tells a thing he has only ever told the walls of a hut.

The expedition had found the source, and found something else besides. The client's company had known what was in the valley—not the lake, the lake was a trophy, but the ground under the approach, the mineralization, the survey data Eli had been quietly keeping in his journal. And when the expedition began to come apart—when the storm hit and the porters fled and the client's man ordered the evidence collected and the witnesses contained—Eli had stayed behind with Marcus, their fallen sirdar, who had broken his leg in the icefall and could not be moved fast enough. The order had come down to leave him. Eli had refused. Not heroically, Anselm said. Not with speeches. He had simply sat down in the snow next to Marcus and started building a wind wall, and said, in the mild voice of a man discussing dinner, *I'm not leaving him to die alone, so either you help me or you go.*

They had gone. The company's men had gone, and taken the survey, and taken the story, and written the report. Eli had stayed with Marcus through the night and the next night. Anselm had gone back up with the last of the supplies against orders and found the wind wall and found them both, and Marcus was dead and Eli was dying, frostbitten past saving, and Eli had made him a promise: *take the journal, take the pages that matter, and don't let them write the ending.* And then, because he was Eli, because he was her brother, he had spent his last coherent hour making Anselm memorize the route down, quizzing him, patient, merciless, a teacher to the end.

"And the company's men came back before I could get away with the body," Anselm said. "They took Marcus. They took—" His ruined hand opened and closed. "They took everything except what I had inside my jacket. And then they came for me, in Kathmandu, in the spring. A man came to my room and explained to me, very politely, what my testimony would cost me and what my silence would pay. And I took the payment because I was afraid, and then I hated myself, and then I came back here, because the only place I could live with what I'd done was the place where I'd done it."

The fire had burned low. Nobody moved to feed it.

"He didn't die alone," Lena said. It came out of her before she knew it was in her, and it was not a question, and her voice did not sound like her voice. "Marcus. He didn't die alone."

"No," Anselm said. "He died holding Eli's hand. Eli told me so. He made a joke of it. He said, *tell my sister I finally learned to belay.*"

Lena heard herself make a sound. It was small and it was terrible and it was twelve years old, and Tomas's hand came down on her shoulder like a roof beam, and she let it, and she did not cry, not quite, she stood at the edge of crying the way you stand at the edge of a cornice, testing, knowing the whole white world could go at any moment.

The compass hung on her pack, needle cracked, pointing at nothing.

*He finally learned to belay.*

* * *

Later—much later, when Kip was asleep for good and Priya had banked the fire and even the wind had dropped into the exhausted register it finds before dawn—Lena sat across the coals from Anselm and watched him sleep. He slept sitting up, back to the wall, the way men sleep who have spent years listening for boots on stone. His ruined hands lay open on his knees. In the ember light they looked like something carved, something a village might leave on a cairn to say: *the mountain takes, and here is the receipt.*

Dev was awake. She could feel him awake across the dark of the hut, the way you feel an unsecured load on a rope. He had not spoken since Anselm began. All evening he had sat with his elbows on his knees and his head down, and once, when Anselm had described the man who came to the Kathmandu room, Dev had made a sound like a man swallowing a stone.

"Dev," Lena said quietly.

He looked up. His face in the ember light was stripped of everything he usually wore—polish, ambition, the salesman's warmth, even the guilt that had been leaking out of him for weeks. What was left was young. She hadn't seen the young Dev since Kyrgyzstan, since before, since two kids on a rope who believed the mountain would always give them one more chance.

"He's coming down with us," she said. It wasn't a question either. She was done with questions. "Whatever's left of the monsoon, whatever's on the trails, whatever the client sends. He's coming down."

"Yes," Dev said.

"And then he testifies. To the federation, to the journalists, to whoever will listen. Everything. The extraction, the report, the payment, all of it. And the journal goes with him."

"Yes," Dev said again. And then, because he was Dev, because there was apparently no bottom to what this valley could take out of a man, he added, barely above the coals' whisper: "Lena. There's something I have to tell you. About what I knew. About when I knew it."

The fire ticked. Anselm slept on, upright, faithful, a sentinel of his own shame. Outside, above the hut, above the storm, the glacier that held the source of everything went on grinding in the dark, patient as a debt.

Lena looked at her oldest friend across twelve years of silence and said, "I know. And you're going to tell me all of it. Right now."

Dev opened his mouth.

The wind rose against the roof slabs, and under it—faint, far down the slope, carried up the gorge the way sound carries before a storm—came the unmistakable crack and grind of ice letting go, and then, after a silence that lasted three heartbeats, a second sound that was not ice at all.

Voices. Below them. Coming up.
