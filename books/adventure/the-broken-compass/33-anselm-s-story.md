## 33. Anselm's Story

The fire in the herder's hut burned low and blue, the way juniper does at altitude, and Anselm Reyes watched it the way he had watched fires for twelve years—as if it might turn on him.

He sat propped against the stone wall where Lena's crew had arranged him, his frostbitten feet wrapped in Priya's spare down jacket, his hands curled around a tin mug of tea that Tomas kept refilling without being asked. The mug was the heaviest thing he had held in years. That was not a metaphor. He had learned, in the valley, to want almost nothing. Wanting was the first thing the mountain took, and the last thing it gave back.

They were all looking at him. The boy—Kip—with his eyes too big for his face. The cook, Tomas, who had not stopped feeding him since they'd carried him in from the hut above the tree line. Priya, who had taken his pulse twice and said nothing either time. The outfitter, Dev Kapoor, whose face Anselm knew from a decade of nightmares—older now, silvered at the temples, but the same face that had been in the helicopter.

And Lena Halloran, who had Eli's compass in her hand, its cracked needle trembling between north and something else.

Anselm had recognized it before he recognized her. The compass first. The brass case worn smooth on one side where a thumb would go. He had seen that thumb turn that case ten thousand times, on three continents, in weather that would have killed lesser men. *Eli's compass,* he had thought, in the first lucid moment he'd had in days, *which means the world has finally come to collect.*

"Start wherever you want," Lena said now. Her voice was steady. He remembered that steadiness, how it had made the rest of them feel braver than they were. "Start anywhere."

Anselm took a breath. The air came into him thin and cold, and it hurt, and he was grateful for the hurt because it meant he was still a body, still a man, and not yet the ghost he had spent twelve years rehearsing.

"The last time I told this story," he said, "I was telling it to a man with a clipboard, and I lied in the third sentence."

* * *

Lena did not move. She had learned, in the years since Kyrgyzstan, that the body could hold itself still through almost anything if you gave it a job—count the stones in the wall, count the stitches in your sleeve, count the times his voice breaks. She was counting now.

"In the third sentence," Anselm said, "he asked me who gave the order to descend, and I said the storm decided. That's what I told them. The storm decided. And the man with the clipboard wrote it down, because it was the answer they wanted, and then they gave me a blanket and a plane ticket and a document I signed without reading, and I have been reading it every day since in my head, and every day it says the same thing."

"What does it say?" Priya asked softly.

"That I agreed the expedition never reached the upper cirque," Anselm said. "That there was no second survey. That Eli Halloran died in a fall on the approach, alone, and that no one could have helped him."

He looked up at Lena. His eyes were the color of glacier melt, gray-green, opaque, and she thought: *he has been alone so long he has forgotten how to be looked at.*

"None of that is true," he said.

* * *

Anselm had been thirty-one years old the autumn the expedition went in. He had been the geologist—the client's man, though he had not understood then what *the client's man* meant. He had understood it as a job: go up the river, sample the rock, come home. He had understood the secrecy as the ordinary paranoia of a mining concern. He had not understood, could not have understood, that the valley itself was the secret—that the survey was not the point but the trespass, and that men in Kathmandu and men in Singapore and men whose names he never learned had all agreed, over contracts he never saw, that the headwaters would stay empty on every map money could touch.

Eli Halloran had been the one who figured it out first. Of course he had. Eli read landscapes the way Anselm read strata, and what Eli read, in the folded rock above the second gorge, in the old drill casings rusting under a scree slope where no drill had any business being, was that someone had been here before them and meant for no one to come after.

"He told me on the eleventh night," Anselm said. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the wind moved through the valley with the long patience of a thing that had all the time in the world. "We were in the tents. Everyone else was asleep. He showed me the samples I'd taken and he'd taken, side by side, and he said, *Anselm, do you know what's under this ice?* And I said, tell me. And he said—"

Anselm stopped. His hands had begun to shake, and the tea shivered in the mug, and Lena watched him fight it the way you watch a man fight a river, knowing the river wins, knowing the fighting is the whole point.

"He said, *I don't think they sent us to find it. I think they sent us to lose us.*"

* * *

Lena's counting broke. She had counted to four hundred and eleven and then his voice did the thing voices do when they carry a name, and she was no longer in the hut at all but twelve years younger, in a kitchen in Kathmandu, reading a cable that said *CONTACT LOST, WEATHER WINDOW CLOSED, SEARCH SUSPENDED,* and understanding that the world intended to go on anyway.

"Say his name," she said. She hadn't meant to say it out loud. "Say it the way he'd want it said. He hated euphemism. He used to say a mountain is a mountain and a lie is a lie and never confuse the two."

Anselm smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn thin, but it was real. "He said that. He said it the morning we went up. He said, *If I don't come back, don't let them make it weather.*" He set the mug down because his hands could no longer be trusted with it. "So. No weather. Here is what happened."

* * *

The storm was real. Anselm wanted that on the record, even the record of a fire in a stolen hut. The storm was real, and it came down the valley in the night like a train, and it caught them high on the moraine below the cirque with the survey half done and the samples split between two packs.

But before the storm, there had been the radio call.

"The client's coordinator—call him the voice, that's all he ever was, a voice on the sat phone—told us to abandon the second survey and descend. Not because of the storm. The storm hadn't turned yet. He told us because Eli had radioed the preliminary numbers, and the numbers were too good, and a thing too good is a thing that can't be allowed to exist." Anselm's voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man who had given this testimony a thousand times in the dark to an audience of stone. "Eli argued. Eli argued for an hour. And then the voice said something I have never told anyone, because saying it makes it true, and I have spent twelve years trying to make it not true."

The hut was very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"He said: *If the survey completes, the expedition becomes a liability. Liabilities get resolved.*"

"And Eli understood," Lena said.

"Eli understood," Anselm said. "And Eli made a decision that I have been trying to understand ever since. He said, *We finish it. We finish it and we walk it out ourselves, all the data, all of us, and we put it somewhere they can't burn it.* He split the samples three ways. He gave one set to Marcus—Marcus Adeyemi, our rope lead, the strongest man I've ever known. He kept one himself. He gave one to me. And he said, *Whatever happens, one set walks out.*"

"And then the storm," Tomas said. Not a question. A cook's kindness, giving the story a place to put its weight down.

"And then the storm," Anselm said.

* * *

What the storm did, in Anselm's telling, was slow. That was the horror of it. Not the wind's violence, which was considerable, but its patience. It came down and sat on the mountain and did not leave, and day by day it took things. First the radio, drowned in its own condensation. Then the stove. Then Marcus, on the third day, on a slope that had looked solid for eleven days and chose the twelfth, and the fall broke his leg in two places and cracked his hip, and they dragged him into the tents and knew, all four of them, without saying it, what a broken hip meant at that altitude with no radio and the monsoon coming.

"Eli stayed with him," Anselm said.

Lena closed her eyes.

"The rest of us could still walk. Slowly. Eli rigged Marcus into the most sheltered hollow he could find, gave him the stove, gave him the food—gave him most of the food—and said he'd stay until we sent help or the weather broke, whichever came first. Marcus argued. Marcus was the kind of man who argued about everything except the things that mattered, and this mattered, and he argued, and Eli just—stayed. Sat down next to him. Started telling him about you, actually."

Anselm looked at her, and his glacier eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them because he had decided, somewhere in twelve years of hiding, that he was done wiping them.

"He talked about his sister. The guide. He said she was the best rope handler he'd ever seen and that she'd have gotten Marcus down that moraine in half the time. He said—" and here Anselm's voice did break, finally, cleanly, like ice going out from under a man— "he said, *If anyone comes looking, tell her I wasn't afraid. Tell her I was just tired.*"

* * *

Lena opened her eyes. The fire had burned to a red heart, and around it the faces of her crew were turned toward her, and she found that she could not do the thing she had trained herself to do for a decade, which was to be competent instead of present. There was no route here. There was no rope trick, no checkpoint bluff, no smooth lie to hand a client. There was only a man in a hut saying her brother had not been afraid, and the truth arriving in her chest like water into a dry channel, hurting in the way that only true things hurt.

"Go on," she said. It was all she had.

* * *

They walked out, the three of them—Anselm, and the expedition doctor, and the young porter whose name Anselm had never learned to pronounce properly and who had died, two years later, in a landslide on a different river, and whom Anselm still apologized to nightly. They walked out through four days of storm because Eli had marked the route with wands before the whiteout took the upper mountain, and Eli's wands held, because Eli's work held, it had always held, that was the whole of who he was.

They made it to the herder's settlement—the same village, Anselm realized, looking at Priya, the same village that had sheltered this crew—and from there the story stopped being about mountains and started being about men, which was worse.

"The helicopter came on the sixth day," Anselm said. "Not a rescue helicopter. A company helicopter. And the man in it was not a doctor. He was the voice. I heard the voice coming out of his mouth, and I understood that I had never been talking to a radio at all."

Dev had gone very still by the fire. Anselm saw it, and something old and cold in him turned over and said *now,* and he obeyed it.

"Marcus was alive," Anselm said. "Eli had kept him alive for six days with a broken hip at five thousand meters. And the voice told us that the expedition was over, that the survey had failed, that Eli and Marcus had been lost in a fall on the approach—and that if we said otherwise, if one set of samples surfaced, if one word contradicted the report, then the company would resolve us too. He used that word. *Resolved.* He said it the way you'd say *filed.*"

"And you signed," Lena said. There was no accusation in it. That was the thing Anselm had not been prepared for. He had rehearsed her rage for twelve years. He had built his whole exile out of her rage, brick by brick, because her rage would have been easier to carry than her grief.

"I signed," he said. "The doctor signed. And we flew out over the valley, and I looked down, and I knew Eli was still up there with Marcus, keeping him company in the dark, and I let the helicopter carry me away from him." He lifted his ruined hands, palms up, an offering or a surrender. "That is the whole of it. That is what I have been hiding from for twelve years. Not the company. The company I could have fought, maybe, probably, and lost. What I've been hiding from is the moment I signed my name under a lie while a man was dying alone above me because he believed no one should die alone, and I could not even give him the dignity of being remembered dying."

* * *

The fire had gone to coals. Nobody moved to feed it. Kip had his fists pressed against his mouth. Priya had taken Anselm's pulse again without appearing to, her fingers on his wrist, and Anselm let her, because it was the first time in twelve years anyone had checked whether he was still alive as if it mattered.

"Why now?" Lena asked. "Why come out? You could have stayed a ghost. You almost were one."

Anselm was quiet a long time. When he answered, he answered to the fire, because her face was too much like Eli's and not looking at it was the last cowardice he had left, and he was spending it.

"Because I found out the company is back," he said. "Two months ago. A survey team in the lower gorge. New permits, new names, same money. And I understood that my silence hadn't bought the valley peace. It had only bought them time to come back for the rest." He turned and looked at her at last, straight on, the way you look at the thing you've been circling for twelve years. "Your brother died to put that data somewhere they couldn't burn it. I know where it is, Lena. Eli told me where he buried it. I've known for twelve years. And I never went back, because going back meant admitting the rest."

The wind rose outside the hut, and down the valley, far off, thunder said the monsoon had made up its mind.

"Tell me where," Lena said.
