## 34. Witnesses

The herder's hut smelled of juniper smoke and wet wool, and Dev Kapoor sat in the corner farthest from the fire, watching the man who had come back from the dead eat barley porridge with hands that shook too badly to hold the spoon steady.

Anselm Reyes. Twelve years a ghost. Frostbite had taken three fingers on his left hand and most of the color from his face, but he was alive—alive in a way that made everything Dev had built feel like a paper structure in rising water.

Lena sat across the fire from Anselm, close enough to touch him, though she didn't. She hadn't touched him yet. Dev understood why. Some things, once you reached for them, you couldn't unreach for, and she was still deciding whether this was real—whether any of it was real—the journal pages spread out beside her like shed skin, Eli's handwriting marching across them in that slanted, impatient scrawl Dev would have known anywhere, would have known in the dark, would have known if someone read it aloud to him in hell.

He had been listening to Anselm's story for two hours. The storm outside had settled into a steady hiss against the stone roof, monsoon rain finding its rhythm at last, as if the sky had only been waiting for them to arrive before it opened. Inside, the fire popped. Kip slept against the far wall, exhausted from the border run, his face still smudged with soot from the diversion he'd pulled off with nothing but a satellite phone and a string of firecrackers and more nerve than anyone had a right to expect from a seventeen-year-old. Tomas dozed sitting up, ankle propped on a pack, his breathing the deep even rhythm of a man who had done all he could and knew it. Priya was outside on watch, because Priya was always on watch when the world felt unsteady.

And Dev sat in his corner, holding the weight of what he'd said by the flood channel, turning it over, testing whether it could bear anything.

*Everything Dev knows.* He'd given them all of it. The extraction team that had come upriver under cover of a "recovery operation" while Eli's expedition was still missing. The survivor they'd carried out secretly—Anselm, half-dead, delirious, deposited in a Kathmandu clinic under a false name and then, when he began asking questions, quietly paid to disappear. The mineral survey Eli had found in the headwater rocks—rare earth deposits, the kind of thing that made governments rewrite maps and companies rewrite laws. The client wasn't a surveyor or an academic. He was an acquisition agent, and the forbidden valley wasn't forbidden to protect pilgrims or glaciers. It was forbidden because it was for sale, and the sale required that no one be left alive who remembered what was underneath it.

Eli Halloran had died covering Anselm's retreat. Had stayed behind with Marcus Webb, whose leg was broken, so that Webb wouldn't die alone—and neither of them had died alone, which was the only mercy in the whole account. And then the company had spent twelve years making sure the story stayed buried, and Dev had helped them bury it, because they had shown him the extraction report and offered him a choice: carry the secret and build a career on it, or become another name on a list of people who asked too many questions in the Tsangpo gorge.

He had chosen the career. He had told himself it was grief that made him sign, that he was in no state to fight, that there would be time later. There was always time later. That was the arithmetic of cowardice—it always balanced, right up until the moment you tried to spend it.

"You're doing it again."

Dev looked up. Lena was watching him across the fire. Not accusingly. Worse than that—accurately.

"Doing what?"

"That thing where you rehearse being sorry instead of being useful." She said it without heat, the way you'd note the weather. "You've got a face for it. I remember it from base camp, twelve years ago. You wore it for a week after they called off the search."

The words landed where she'd aimed them, which was nowhere soft. But there was something else in her voice now, something that hadn't been there in the gorge when she'd hauled him half-drowned out of the flood channel, something that had maybe arrived during Anselm's story, during the long night of testimony: not forgiveness, exactly. Recognition. Two people who had both made their bargains with silence, standing on opposite sides of the same fire, finally seeing the whole shape of each other's deals.

"I'm not sorry," Dev said. "That's the problem. Sorry is a feeling. Feelings are cheap." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight moved across his face. "I'm *responsible*. There's a difference, and I spent twelve years pretending there wasn't."

Tomas stirred, opened one eye, and closed it again. Even asleep, the cook kept one ear on the room. It was how they'd all survived—half-listening, always.

"What do you want, Dev?" Lena asked. And it was the real question, the one underneath every question since Kathmandu, since the market, since the contract he'd carried upriver like a bribe wrapped in ambition. What did he want. Not the pardon. Not the survey fee. Not the company's forgiveness or hers.

Dev looked at Anselm, who had stopped eating and was watching them both with the patient stillness of a man who had spent twelve years learning that other people's decisions decided his life.

"I want to burn it down," Dev said. "Not the valley. The company. Meridian Resources—that's the parent name; the client is their acquisitions director, a man named Osei Varga, and he answers to a board in Singapore. I want the extraction report published. I want the mineral survey Eli found entered into the public record before they can buy the land and seal it forever. I want Anselm's testimony on tape, witnessed, dated, filed with the mountaineering federation and three newspapers I trust—one of them, at least, will run it." He paused. "And I want my name attached to all of it. As a witness. As the man who knew and stayed quiet."

The hut went very quiet. Outside, the rain thickened.

Lena set down the journal page she'd been holding. "You understand what that costs."

"I've costed it." His voice was dry, almost professional, the voice he used for budgets and route plans, and somehow that made it worse—more honest. "Meridian will destroy me. They hold liens on my outfitting company—Varga structured the contract that way from the start; I signed it without reading the cross-default clauses, which tells you everything about who I was twelve years ago and not much better about who I was four months ago. When this goes public, they'll call the loans inside a week. My permits get revoked—I know which ministry officials they pay, because I've paid some of the same ones. My guides will lose work. My name goes on the blacklist next to yours, except mine will be deserved and everyone will know it." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin and true. "I'll be finished. Professionally, financially, reputationally. Everything I built on top of that grave comes down with it."

"And you're telling us this," Lena said slowly, "because—"

"Because you won't believe me unless I show you the bill." He met her eyes. "You shouldn't believe me, Lena. You have twelve years of evidence that I say things and don't mean them. So don't believe me. Believe the numbers. I've written them down—everything I own, everything I owe, exactly what Meridian can take and when. It's in my pack. Priya can check my math; she's better at this than I am." A breath. "I'm not asking for absolution. I'm asking to be a witness. There's a difference, and I'm done pretending there isn't."

Silence again. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the walls, water found a path through old mortar and dripped, patiently, onto stone.

Lena looked at him for a long time. Dev had known her for fifteen years, had loved her once in the wordless way climbers love the people they rope to, had betrayed her in ways she still didn't fully know, and he had never seen her look at him quite like this. Not warm. Not cold. Weighing. The way she weighed ice bridges and weather windows and the load a rope could take—except this time the rope was him, and she was deciding whether to trust it with weight.

"The pardon," she said. "If we go public, the inquiry reopens, and whatever deal Varga dangled—your exoneration file, my reinstatement—all of it dies. Meridian will make sure the federation buries the whole thing rather than admit what their sponsor was doing in a closed valley. You know that."

"Yes."

"So you're not doing this to clear your name either."

"No." He laughed softly, without humor. "God, no. My name deserves what it gets. This isn't about names anymore. It's about—" he gestured at the journal, at Anselm, at the rain hammering the roof above Eli Halloran's last route— "it's about the fact that Eli died for a piece of ground that men in Singapore intend to own by Thursday. And the only reason they haven't already is that nobody with proof has been willing to stand up and be destroyed. I was the nearest person with proof, twelve years ago, and I chose my career." His voice didn't break. He'd spent the breaking already, somewhere between the flood channel and this fire. "I'm choosing differently now. That's all. It's not noble. It's just late."

Anselm spoke for the first time. His voice was rough, rusted from disuse and altitude and twelve years of talking only to yaks and wind. "They paid me to disappear," he said. "Did you know that? Fourteen thousand dollars and a one-way ticket to Buenos Aires. I took it." He looked at his ruined hand. "I told myself I'd testify later. Later. I hid in this valley because it was the only place on earth I understood, and I told myself hiding was survival." He turned his eyes to Dev, and they were very clear, very tired. "You're the first person in twelve years to choose the other thing while it was still expensive. Don't ask me to forgive you. Ask me to witness you. That I can do."

Dev felt something move through his chest that he refused to call relief, because relief implied the matter was settled, and it wasn't, and might never be. But it was a start, and starts were all anyone ever got.

Priya came in from the watch then, shaking rain from her jacket, and read the room in three seconds flat—she always could—and Dev repeated his proposal for her benefit, laying out the plan with the same dry precision: testimony recorded tonight, copies made three ways, the journal photographed page by page before they surrendered the original to anyone, delivery to the federation and the press in Kathmandu through channels Priya would choose because Priya trusted no one, which was the correct amount.

"They'll come after the evidence," Priya said, when he finished. She wasn't arguing. She was mapping. "Varga knows you're off the grid, but the sat phone pings are traceable, and the moment you file, every favor he owns gets called in. Border checkpoints. Customs. If we're carrying originals when it breaks—"

"We split it," Lena said. It came fast, decisive, the guide's voice, the one that cut through weather and fear alike. "Copies travel separate routes. Anselm travels with Tomas and Kip, low route, slow, invisible. The journal stays on me. Dev—" she paused, and something flickered across her face, the ghost of an old argument, an old trust, an old wound— "Dev files first. From Kathmandu. Before we're even down off the mountain. If they're going to destroy someone, let it be the man who's already handed them the reason."

"Lena." Dev stood. "You don't have to protect me."

"I'm not protecting you." Her eyes were steady, and behind the steadiness was the flood channel, the rope, the moment he'd shoved Priya clear of the surge and gone into the water himself—she'd seen it, they'd all seen it, and it had changed something that no confession alone could have changed. "I'm spending you where you're worth the most. It's what you'd do. It's what I'd do." Almost a smile. "It's what Eli would do."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped in still water. Anselm bowed his head. Tomas, awake now whether he pretended otherwise or not, grunted—an approval, from Tomas, worth more than applause.

Dev nodded. He found that his throat didn't work properly for a moment, and he let the moment pass unspeaking, because some acceptances are too large for speech.

* * *

They worked through the small hours. Priya rigged the recorder and the spare batteries, and Anselm gave his testimony twice—once in Spanish for the record, once in English, slowly, while the rain kept its time outside like a metronome counting down the distance between them and the world. Dev photographed the journal under the firelight, page by page, hands steadier than they had any right to be, and when he reached the last pages—the mineral survey figures, Eli's furious marginal notes, *"they must not buy this mountain"*—he copied those twice more and memorized the key coordinates, because Priya had taught him that paper burns and memory doesn't, not entirely, not if you've carried it long enough.

Near dawn, Lena sat down beside him while the others slept. She didn't look at him. She watched the fire.

"In Kyrgyzstan," she said quietly, "when it all went wrong—they asked me afterward if I'd made the right calls. Every inquiry, every hearing, twelve years of it. And the truth was I didn't know. I still don't know. Some days I think yes, some days no. That's the part they never put in the reports. That the person being judged doesn't know either." She turned then, and looked at him straight. "But I know about you, Dev. I watched you go into that channel after Priya when every instinct said stay on the rock. Nobody was filming. Nobody was going to pay you. You did it because it was the thing, and you did it fast, before the part of you that calculates could catch up." She held his gaze. "That man exists. He was always in there. I believe him. So when you file tomorrow—when you hand them your career on a plate—I'll believe that too. Not because you're forgiven. Because you're choosing the loss, and I finally understand that's the only currency that was ever real."

Dev said nothing for a long moment. Then: "When this is over—if I still have a passport—buy me a beer sometime. Cheap one. I'll have earned cheap."

Lena almost laughed—a raw, surprised sound, the first in days. "Deal. But you're buying the second round. With interest. Twelve years of interest."

"Usury," he said. "Fine. Worth it."

Outside, the eastern sky had begun to gray above the ridge line, and the rain was slackening into mist, and somewhere below them the river ran on toward the sea, carrying meltwater and silt and the dissolved minerals of a mountain that men in distant rooms believed they had already purchased. Dev packed the recordings in waterproof sleeves, three copies, three pockets, and thought about the man he had been in Kathmandu four months ago, rehearsing a sales pitch in a market crowd, and the man he was now, broke in every way that mattered and lighter than he had been in fifteen years.

By full light they would move. Down the gorge, through the checkpoints, into the teeth of whatever Varga sent against them. Dev shouldered his pack and looked once more at the valley wall where Eli Halloran had drawn his last line on the map, and made himself one final promise, small and hard as a carabiner:

Whatever it cost—he would be there, in the room, on the record, named. A witness. Nothing more, nothing less.

He stepped out of the hut into the thin gold morning, and the descent began.
