## 35. Running the Border

The river had turned against them.

Kip had known it would, in the way you know a storm is coming before the clouds arrive—some animal part of you reading the pressure, the smell, the wrongness in the air. For three days the Tsangpo had been rising, brown and furious, chewing at the trail they'd been following down the gorge, and this morning it had swallowed the last of the path entirely, leaving them nothing but the river itself for a road.

Now they stood in the treeline above the last bridge, and Kip's mouth had gone dry as old rope.

The bridge was iron and timber, a hundred meters of rusted span thrown across the gorge in some forgotten decade, and it was the only way across. On the far side, the trail climbed toward the pass and the border road and, eventually, Kathmandu, and doctors, and a bed Anselm Reyes could actually lie down in. On this side, the trail was a memory and a landslide. There was no choice, really. There never had been.

But there were soldiers on the far side.

Four of them, in green fatigues gone gray with rain, sheltering under a tarp rigged between two boulders. One stood at the bridgehead with a rifle slung casual across his chest, smoking. Another sat on a crate, boots off, rubbing his feet. They weren't looking for anything in particular. They were just there, the way soldiers are always just there, at the one place in the world you cannot go around.

"They're not checkpoints," Tomas had said an hour ago, crouched beside Lena in the ferns, his ankle wrapped in a brace of trekking poles and tape, his voice pitched low enough that the river almost ate it. "They're a picket. Somebody called ahead."

"Of course somebody called ahead," Lena had said. "We burned a man's survey data and walked out of a closed valley with his witness. The company has satellites. The company has friends." She'd said it without heat, the way she said everything now, and Kip had understood that this was what she sounded like when she was most afraid—flat, precise, already three moves ahead of the thing she couldn't control.

Anselm lay against a tree trunk ten meters back, propped between Priya's pack and Tomas's, his frostbitten feet bound in clean bandages that were already graying with sweat. Twelve years in the valley had left him a ghost wearing a man's skin—sixty kilograms maybe, cheekbones like tent poles, eyes that had gone quiet in a way Kip found harder to look at than any wound. He'd walked for four days on feet that couldn't feel. He hadn't complained once. He'd just stopped, this morning, halfway through a step, like a machine running out of fuel, and said, "I don't think I can do the bridge."

Not wouldn't. Couldn't. There was a difference, and everyone had heard it.

"We could carry him," Kip said now, and hated how young his voice sounded.

"On what?" Tomas didn't look at him. "My ankle's held together with prayer. Lena's got maybe one more carry in her and it's not a hundred-meter bridge against soldiers. Priya—" He stopped. Priya was already fifty meters downstream, flat on her stomach at the lip of the gorge, watching the water with the expression she got when she was solving a problem nobody else could even see yet.

Kip crouched beside her. Below, the river slammed through a narrows, white and deaf, and the spray came up in cold ghosts. He shouted over it: "What are you looking at?"

"The outflow," she shouted back. "Where the gorge pinches. Look at the far bank—the trail comes down to the bridge from the north, but there's an old herder's path, see it? Switchbacks, above the tarp. It comes down behind those boulders. If we cross, we come off the bridge on their blind side."

"And then what, we just walk past them?"

"No." Priya sat back on her heels. Rain dripped off her hood. She had that look she got when the honest answer was a bad one. "Then somebody makes them look somewhere else."

Everyone turned. Not toward Priya.

Toward Kip.

He felt it land in his chest like a dropped rock. "No," he said. "No way. I'm not—Lena, tell them."

Lena was looking at him, and her face was doing something complicated. He'd spent two years learning to read that face—the set of her jaw when a client was about to do something stupid, the flicker at the corner of her mouth when he'd made her laugh without meaning to, the thousand-yard flatness she wore like armor. What he saw now was none of those. What he saw was a woman doing arithmetic she despised.

"You're the fastest," she said quietly. "And the smallest. And the only one of us they wouldn't shoot."

"That's not a reason—"

"It's the only reason that matters." She crouched down to his level, and for a second she wasn't the guide, wasn't the legend he'd built out of campfire stories and stolen glances. She was just Lena, tired and rain-soaked, and her eyes were wet-bright in a way he'd never seen. "Kip. Listen to me. You don't have to. I want you to hear that from me first, before anything else. You don't *have* to. But if you do—" She stopped. Started again. "If you do, it's firecrackers and the sat phone and a dead run, and you do not stop for anything. Not if they shout. Not if they shoot near you. You run the herder's path downstream to the narrows, you cross the log we scouted, and you come up the far side to the bridge from behind. We'll be watching. Dev will be on the bridge to cover you. You understand? Nothing touches you."

"And if it does?"

"Then I come get you," she said, "and I will burn down the entire border district doing it."

Tomas made a sound that might have been a laugh or a prayer.

Kip looked at the bridge. At the soldiers. At Anselm, who had closed his eyes against the tree trunk, breathing in small careful sips, as if even his lungs had learned to ration themselves. Twelve years, the man had hidden in that valley. Twelve years of fear, and he'd finally said yes to leaving, yes to testifying, yes to walking out on dead feet—and now a bridge and four bored soldiers were the last thing between him and the world.

Kip thought about the firecrackers in the bottom of his pack. He'd bought them in a village three days ago off a kid his own age, four packets of them, thinking they might scare off a bear or liven up a cold camp. He'd been carrying them without knowing why. That was the thing about Lena's crew, he was realizing. You carried things a long time before you learned what they were for.

"Okay," he said.

* * *

They spent an hour on it, because Lena didn't do anything without an hour on it.

Priya drew the layout in the mud with a stick: the bridge, the tarp, the boulders, the herder's path, the log crossing at the narrows two hundred meters downstream. Dev marked the soldiers' sight lines from memory—he'd watched them longer than anyone, cataloguing their rotations the way he once catalogued gear manifests. The smoker at the bridgehead. The boot-rubber on the crate. Two more under the tarp, one asleep, one playing something on a cracked phone, the tinny music just barely audible over the river.

"Four men," Dev said. "Bored, wet, and underpaid. They're not expecting trouble. They're expecting timber smugglers, maybe. Nobody's expecting a sixteen-year-old with fireworks."

"Seventeen," Kip said.

"Congratulations." Dev's mouth twitched. It was the closest thing to warmth he'd offered anyone in days, and Kip took it, because he understood now what it cost Dev Kapoor to give away even that much.

The plan was simple, which was how Kip knew it was Lena's. He would come down the trail from the north, alone, visible, a lone trekker on the wrong side of a closed border zone—the kind of thing that happened, the kind of thing that got a shout and a wave-down and a lecture. When the soldiers converged on him, Priya, Tomas, and Lena would carry Anselm along the fern bank to the bridge's near end, hidden from the tarp by the boulders. Dev would already be on the span, halfway across, playing the lost outfitter with his polished accent and his expensive jacket, arguing his way across in the opposite direction, drawing eyes north. And at the moment the picket's attention broke into two competing confusions, Kip would light the firecrackers in the riverbrush downstream, drop the sat phone—Dev's sat phone, the one with the client's number still in its log—into the mud where the soldiers would find it, and run.

The phone was the part that made Kip's stomach turn over. It was evidence. It was the thing they'd been protecting since the lake, the thing that could tie the client to the closed valley, to the orders, to all of it. And they were going to leave it in the mud for a corporal to pick up, because Priya had spent a night with it and a knife and a soldering wire she'd heated over the stove, and when she'd handed it back she'd said, in her flat navigator's way: "The call log's a gift now. Let them find it. Let them explain why a survey company's number is in a soldier's recovered phone, calling into a closed military zone." It wasn't losing the phone. It was planting it.

"Last chance to say no," Lena said, at the treeline, her hand on the back of his neck the way she never did in front of the others.

"If you say that one more time I'm going," Kip said, and felt her laugh, one short breath, against the rain.

* * *

He walked down the trail with his hands in his pockets and his heart going like a monsoon drum.

The rain had thinned to a drifting mist, which was worse somehow—it made everything quiet, made his own footsteps loud, made the four soldiers resolve into individual human beings as he got closer. The smoker at the bridgehead was younger than Kip had thought from a distance. Nineteen, maybe twenty. He had acne along his jaw and he smoked the way people smoke who don't like smoking but have nothing else to do. The one with the phone had a crack running the length of his screen and he kept tapping it anyway, hoping, maybe, for a signal that the gorge would never give him.

Kip made himself walk like a client. He'd watched a hundred of them—soft-handed, lost, aggrieved at the world for not being paved. He rounded the last bend and let his face do the thing: the relief, the gratitude, the dumb luck of finding civilization.

"Hey!" He waved both arms. "Hey—hello! Is that a bridge? Oh thank god, thank god, I've been lost since—"

The smoker straightened. The rifle came off his shoulder—not aimed, just held, the way you hold a tool. "You. Stop there. Where from?"

"Downriver." Kip pointed vaguely north, the way Priya had drilled him. "The trail washed out, I've been walking on the rocks for two days, I thought I was going to die out there, is there a road? Please tell me there's a road—"

"Passport."

"I—okay, yes, hold on." He dug in his jacket, slow, fumbling, and of course the passport wasn't there, of course, and he watched the soldier's boredom sharpen into something more official. "It's in my bag, my bag got—"

"Where is your bag?"

"Washed away, I told you, the river took—"

"Name."

And that was when Dev came out onto the bridge.

Kip heard him before he saw him—Dev's voice carried the way it always did, polished and carrying, the voice of a man who had talked his way across a hundred borders. "Excuse me! Excuse me, gentlemen, finally, I have been walking since—" and there he was on the span, arms spread, expensive jacket beaded with mist, the picture of a man whose luck had turned. "Is this the crossing to the road? My porter abandoned me, my radio is dead, I have been—"

The smoker turned toward the bridge. The one with the cracked phone stood up, boots still in his hands. From under the tarp, the sleeper stirred and swore.

Two confusions. North and south. Exactly as drawn in the mud.

Kip's hands were shaking as he backed up a step, babbling—*I have papers, I have a permit, my outfitter has copies, do you know Dev Kapoor, he's very well known, maybe you know him*—and the soldier with the cracked phone came toward him, and Dev's voice rose on the bridge, and for three full seconds the entire picket was looking in two directions at once and seeing neither.

Kip ran.

Not away. Downstream, into the riverbrush, dropping low, the wet branches whipping his face, and he got the first firecracker packet out with fingers that wouldn't obey, tore it with his teeth. Behind him he heard the smoker shout something about the boy—heard the word *stop* crack across the gorge—and he didn't stop. Lena's voice was in his skull, flat and certain: *you do not stop for anything.*

He crouched in the brush at the waterline, touched the fuse to the matches he'd kept dry inside his cap, and lit it.

The firecrackers went off like the end of the world.

In the gorge, with the rock walls throwing sound back and forth, four packets of festival fireworks sounded like a firefight—cracking, echoing, doubling, the reports rolling up the canyon in overlapping waves, and Kip threw the whole burning packet out into the open mud below the trail and ran, and behind him he heard the soldiers' world come apart. Shouting. The crack-snap of a rifle bolt. Someone screaming about shots, about ambush, about the *phone*—and Kip dropped Dev's sat phone into the mud at the edge of the brush, right where a fleeing saboteur would have dropped it, right where it would be found, and he ran for the narrows with his lungs full of fire.

The log crossing was worse than it had looked from above. Priya had scouted it, Priya had sworn by it, but Priya wasn't the one standing on a wet pine trunk over water that wanted to kill him, with a rifle shot cracking somewhere behind—not at him, he told himself, not at him, near him—and the log flexing under his feet like something alive. Halfway across his foot skated. He dropped to a crouch, hugged the trunk, felt the spray hit his face like thrown gravel, and made himself breathe. *Lena's rope calls,* he thought, absurdly, *step, breathe, step.* He crossed the rest on hands and knees.

Then the far bank, the fern bank, the herder's path, climbing. His legs were shaking so badly he had to use his arms. Behind and below, the gorge was chaos—shouts, the tarp collapsing, soldiers fanning out downstream toward the firecracker echoes, toward the phone lying in the mud like a confession. Nobody was looking at the bridge.

Nobody was looking at the bridge.

He came up behind the boulders in time to see it happen: Lena and Priya and Tomas moving Anselm across the span, Tomas hopping on his good leg with Anselm's arm over his shoulders and Priya bearing the rest, Lena out front with her body angled back toward the soldiers, a shield made of one thin woman. Dev was off the bridge now, walking backward, hands raised, still talking—Kip could hear fragments, *my God, was that gunfire, gentlemen, we should all get off this bridge*—the performance of his life, and it was working, it was actually working, because the smoker was staring downstream at the mud and the phone and the drifting smoke of the last firecracker, and the man with the cracked phone was shouting into a radio that crackled uselessly, and for eleven seconds—Kip counted them, he counted every one—four soldiers watched everything except the five people crossing the last bridge over the Tsangpo.

Anselm's boots touched the far side. Tomas set him down against the bridgehead stone, gasping. Lena's hand found the back of Kip's neck again, and this time she didn't let go for a long moment, and her fingers were trembling, and he understood that she'd been counting the same eleven seconds he had.

"You dropped the phone?" she said.

"Right where they'll trip over it."

"And the firecrackers?"

"Three packets left." He pulled them out, held them up. His hands were still shaking. "For the next bridge."

Something broke in her face then—not grief, not quite, but the thing underneath grief, the thing that had been holding it up. She pulled him against her shoulder, once, hard, and let go just as fast, and if her eyes were wet she'd have denied it under oath.

"Seventeen," she said. "God help us all."

* * *

They climbed.

The trail off the far side rose through dripping rhododendron, switchbacking away from the river, and none of them looked back except Kip, once, at the top of the first rise. Below, tiny with distance, the soldiers were clustered around the sat phone in the mud. One of them was holding it up to the sky, turning it over, looking for a signal, looking for an answer. Somewhere in that phone was a number that would unravel everything, and it was sitting in the palm of a bored nineteen-year-old with acne and a rifle, and Kip thought: *good. Let it be heavy. Let them all carry it.*

Anselm rode the last hour on Tomas's back, too spent to walk, his bandaged feet dangling, and Tomas never said a word about the weight, just climbed, breathing like a bellows, one bad ankle and all. Priya scouted ahead and called the turns in her flat, certain voice—*step, breathe, step*—and Dev broke trail without being asked, without complaint, using his expensive jacket to wipe spiderwebs out of the way like it was nothing, like it had always been nothing.

They made the pass at dusk. Below them the gorge filled with cloud, and the last bridge vanished into it, and somewhere down in all that gray the monsoon was arriving, right on time, right on schedule, swallowing the valley behind them like a door closing.

They camped in a rock overhang with a fire too small to see. Anselm slept the whole night through for the first time since they'd found him—truly slept, deep and unguarded, the sleep of a man who had crossed a line that could not be uncrossed. Lena sat up last, as always, and Kip sat up with her, because she'd stopped telling him to go to bed, which he had decided was the highest honor she knew how to give.

"You were scared," she said, after a long time. Not a question.

"Terrified."

"Good." She fed a stick to the fire. "Scared and doing it anyway. That's the whole job, Kip. That's the whole thing. Everything else is just rope work."

He looked across the coals at her—at the woman the world had called disgraced, blacklisted, finished, the woman four soldiers and a river and a burned valley hadn't stopped—and he understood, finally, what Tomas had tried to tell him a year ago over a pot of burnt dal: that she wasn't a legend because she never failed. She was a legend because she kept choosing, every single time, the harder right over the easier wrong, and she did it tired, and scared, and without anyone watching.

Except now someone had watched. Except now someone had run the firecrackers and dropped the phone and crossed the log on hands and knees, and come out the other side still shaking, still here.

"Next time," Kip said, "I want the bridge part."

Lena laughed—really laughed, quiet and cracked and real—and the sound went up through the rhododendrons into the dark, and the river ran on below them, carrying everything downstream toward the sea.

They had two days to Kathmandu. Two days, and a witness with dead feet, and a phone full of evidence in a soldier's hands, and a story the world was about to hear whether it wanted to or not.

Kip closed his eyes and, for the first time in his life, was not afraid of what came next.
