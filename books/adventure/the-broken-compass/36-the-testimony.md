## 36. The Testimony

The rain had followed them all the way down the gorge, and it was still falling when Priya stepped out of the taxi on Lazimpat, but in Kathmandu it fell differently—thin and gray and indifferent, a city rain that didn't care whether you lived or died. After three weeks of monsoon that had tried to kill them, it felt almost like mercy.

Anselm Reyes sat beside her in the back seat, wrapped in Tomas's spare down jacket two sizes too big for him, his frostbitten hands curled in his lap like sleeping birds. He had not spoken since the border. He had barely spoken since the hut above the tree line, where they'd found him living on dried cheese and barley tea and twelve years of silence. But when the taxi turned onto the ring road and he saw the Boudhanath stupa rise white through the drizzle, he made a small sound—not a word, not quite—and pressed his forehead against the window glass.

"He's never seen it," Priya said to Dev in the front seat. "Twelve years in that valley, and he's never once seen the stupa."

Dev didn't answer. He hadn't answered much of anything since the confession, since the night by the flood channel when the words had finally come out of him like a splinter working free—*I knew, Lena. I knew about the extraction. I knew and I took the money and I built a career on top of it.* Since then he'd been a man walking slightly behind his own body, present but not inhabiting himself.

Priya understood. She'd spent years inside her own silence; she knew its architecture from the inside. The way it starts as shelter and becomes a wall. The way you stop noticing you built it at all.

She reached over the seat and touched Anselm's sleeve. "Almost there," she said.

He looked at her with eyes the color of glacier melt, pale and startled-looking, eyes that had watched one horizon for twelve years. "Is it loud," he said. It wasn't quite a question. "The city. Is it loud?"

"Loud," she confirmed. "Smelly. Full of people who will want things from you." She hesitated, then added, because he deserved honesty more than comfort: "That's why we're here."

* * *

The journalist's name was Meena Karki, and she worked out of a third-floor walkup above a photocopy shop in Baluwatar, which told Priya everything she needed to know before they'd said a word. Real institutions had lobbies. Real institutions had receptionists who could be bought. Meena Karki had a desk buried under printouts, a kettle crusted with mineral scale, and a look in her eye that Priya recognized the way you recognize weather coming—a look that said she had been waiting years for something exactly like this and had stopped believing it would come.

"You're the Halloran crew," she said, before introductions. Not a question either. "The ones who went up the Tsangpo headwaters in monsoon. Half the outfitters in Thamel have heard rumors. The other half have heard instructions to say nothing."

"Then we came to the right half," Dev said.

Meena looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw there—the polished outfitter stripped down to the guilty man underneath—seemed to satisfy her. She pulled a chair out from under a stack of files and set it facing her desk. "Sit. All of you. And start at the beginning, and don't skip anything, because I promise you the people you're testifying against have skipped nothing. They've had twelve years to get their story straight. You've had a bus ride."

So they started.

Priya watched Anselm as much as she watched Meena take notes. The first hour, he could barely manage sentences. His voice had gone rusty from disuse, and every time Meena asked him to confirm a date or a name, he would close his eyes and go somewhere else—to the valley, to the ice, to whatever room in his memory held the thing she was asking for—and come back with the answer held carefully, like something hot.

But somewhere in the second hour, something shifted. It happened when Meena asked about the extraction—the helicopter, the men in company jackets, the way they'd taken him off the mountain while Eli Halloran stayed below with the fallen teammate whose name Anselm still could not say without his hands shaking.

"They told me it was handled," Anselm said. His voice cracked and steadied. "They told me if I talked, Eli's family would learn what was in the survey. They told me—" He stopped. Looked at his ruined hands. "They told me no one would believe me anyway. A guide with frostbite and no expedition left. Who was I? I was nobody. So I became nobody. I walked back into the valley instead of out of it, because at least the valley only wanted me dead. It didn't want me *complicit*."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Rain ran down the window in long silver threads.

"That," Meena Karki said quietly, writing, "is the sentence that ends them. Not the helicopter. Not the money. That."

* * *

The Mountaineering Federation occupied a concrete building in Naxal that always smelled faintly of floor wax and old ambition. Priya had been inside it twice before in her life: once to receive a certificate she'd been proud of, once to surrender her guiding license after the summit-record affair, standing in front of a disciplinary panel while a client she'd saved from death swore at her from the gallery because his name was no longer on the list of summiteers.

She had sworn she'd never stand in that building again. Now here she was, third time, sitting in a corridor outside Hearing Room Two with Anselm's statement photocopied into triplicate on her lap, waiting for a clerk to call their names.

Lena sat beside her, very still, staring at the notice board across the corridor where decades of expedition permits hung in plastic sleeves. Somewhere on that board, Priya knew, was the original permit for Eli Halloran's lost expedition—a piece of paper authorizing six people to walk into a valley that had swallowed four of them and hidden the truth for twelve years.

"You don't have to do this part," Priya said. "You could wait outside."

"I've waited twelve years," Lena said. "Outside is where I've been the whole time."

The clerk called them in at 2:40 in the afternoon. The panel was three people this time—an older woman with reading glasses on a chain, a middle-aged man who kept glancing at his phone, and a young lawyer-type who had clearly read everything twice. On the table between them lay a folder stamped with the Federation seal, and Priya recognized the shape of it even upside down: the blacklist file. Her own name was in it. Lena's name was in it. Twelve years of quiet ruin, filed and cross-referenced.

The woman with the glasses opened the folder. "Ms. Halloran. Your case has been reopened under Article Nine, following receipt of new testimony." She looked up. "Do you understand what that means?"

"It means someone finally read the Kyrgyzstan report past the summary page," Lena said.

A flicker of something crossed the woman's face—amusement, maybe, or recognition. "It means," she said, "that the incident report your accuser filed contains discrepancies that were flagged at the time and never investigated. It means that the company now known as Meridian Highlatitudes Expeditions paid for the filing of that report. And it means that until this inquiry concludes, your status is suspended rather than revoked—which is bureaucratic language for: we are sorry, and we are frightened, and we are going to move slowly anyway."

"Understood," Lena said.

"Do you have anything to add?"

Priya expected Lena to say no. Expected her to keep it short, keep it armored, the way she did everything. Instead Lena reached into her jacket and set something on the table: the carabiner, the old aluminum one stamped E.H., worn nearly smooth.

"My brother carried this," she said. "He stayed on that mountain so a dying man wouldn't die alone, and then the company that sent him there erased him and blackmailed the survivor into silence, and used my disgrace as cover, because a disgraced guide's complaints sound like sour grapes." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it—it stayed level, conversational, almost gentle. "I don't want an apology from this panel. I want the report unsealed. All of it. Including the parts that name who signed off on the extraction."

The man with the phone had stopped looking at his phone.

The woman with the glasses closed the folder. "The report will be unsealed," she said. "Whether the names survive publication is not within our power. But the report itself—" she tapped the folder once, "—has been hiding in this building for twelve years. Sometimes the most radical thing a bureaucracy can do is stop hiding something."

* * *

It took nine days for the story to break, and when it broke, it broke quietly, which surprised everyone who had braced for thunder.

Meena Karki's piece ran on a Tuesday, fourteen hundred words on page three beneath a photograph of Anselm Reyes's hands—just the hands, scarred and frostbit, folded on a wooden table. No faces. No dramatic headline. Just: *THE VALLEY THAT WASN'T ON THE MAPS: TWELVE YEARS OF SILENCE AT THE TSANGPO SOURCE.* By Wednesday it had been picked up by wire services. By Thursday, Meridian Highlatitudes' stock had dropped eleven percent, and two of their sponsored athletes had publicly returned their contracts, and a former expedition doctor had come forward to corroborate the extraction with medical records he'd kept in a safe deposit box for a decade, waiting for someone else to go first.

That was the thing Priya hadn't anticipated—the way truth moved like water finding cracks. One testimony made the next one possible. Anselm had gone first, shaking, unable to finish sentences, and his going first had given permission to a chain of people who had each been waiting, all these years, for exactly what he'd finally provided: proof that they weren't alone in knowing.

They watched it happen from Tomas's kitchen in Pokhara, the radio on, Kip refreshing a battered laptop whenever the generator allowed. Tomas cooked through all of it—dal bhat, then momos, then a curry he claimed was his mother's recipe and which Priya strongly suspected was improvised—but he kept turning the volume up between courses, listening with the expression of a man hearing distant artillery and trying to judge which side was winning.

"The company issued a statement," Kip announced on day four. "'Meridian categorically denies—'" He scrolled. "Blah, blah, 'committed to the highest standards,' blah. Also they've deleted their Tsangpo program page. Like, the whole page. Gone."

"Digital ghosts," Priya said. "They're good at those."

On day six, the Federation released the unsealed report. Priya read it alone, late, by headlamp during a power cut, and had to stop twice—not because of what it said about Eli, which she'd already pieced together from Anselm's account, but because of what it said about the aftermath. The internal memos. The risk assessments that had flagged the route as unsafe and been overridden. The line item, buried in an appendix, showing a payment authorization signed eleven days after the disappearance, routed through a Kathmandu subsidiary, with no deliverable listed.

Someone had signed that. Someone had sat at a desk, in an office with air conditioning, and signed away four lives and a survivor's freedom, and then gone to lunch.

She found Lena on the roof afterward, sitting against the water tank with her knees drawn up, watching lightning walk along the Annapurna range like slow white veins.

"You read it," Lena said. Not a question. News traveled fast in a house with five people in it.

"Yes."

"And?"

Priya lowered herself onto the wet concrete beside her. Below them Pokhara glittered and steamed, lakeside lights smearing in the rain. "And I keep thinking about how easy it was," she said. "Not the mountain. The mountain was hard. But the lying—all of it, theirs and ours and mine—that was easy. That was the laziest possible thing. The truth took us everything we had, and it took nine days to land, and it isn't even finished."

"No," Lena agreed. "It isn't."

Down in the city, a temple bell rang somewhere, thin and clear through the rain.

"They offered you reinstatement yet?" Priya asked.

"Letter came yesterday." Lena's voice was careful, flat. "Full reinstatement. Three outfitters called. One of them offered to lead with my name."

"What did you tell them?"

"Nothing. Yet." Lena turned the carabiner over in her fingers, once, twice, the way she did when she was thinking—Priya had watched her do it for weeks without understanding until Anselm had explained it: Eli's habit, inherited. "Everyone keeps asking what I want. The pardon, the apology, the job back. Like one of those is the prize at the end." She was quiet a moment. "Eli doesn't come back. That's the arithmetic nobody wants to hear. You can unravel a company and unseal a report and put names in newspapers, and he's still on that mountain, and Anselm still can't sleep through a night, and I'm still his sister, which means I'm still the person who wasn't there."

Priya said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't cheaper than silence.

"But," Lena went on, and here her voice changed—loosened by a degree, like a knot giving way, "Kip asked me something this morning. He asked if we're going to keep guiding. Together. Properly. Legal routes, honest contracts, the whole boring thing." She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "He'd already picked a name. This kid. Didn't ask anyone. Just decided."

"What name?"

"He won't tell me. Says it's bad luck before the paperwork." Lena looked out at the storm walking the ridgelines. "Nine days ago I would have said no. A month ago I couldn't have imagined being asked." She turned the carabiner over one last time and closed her hand around it. "Ask me again in a week, Priya. I think the answer might be different than the one I've been carrying for twelve years."

* * *

On the ninth day, the Federation convened again—this time not a hearing but a press briefing, held in the same wax-smelling hall where Priya had once surrendered her license. The room was full: journalists, federation officials, two government observers, and a scattering of guides from the Kathmandu community, some of whom had spent years repeating the official version of Lena Halloran's disgrace and now stood at the back wearing the expressions of men hoping to be mistaken for furniture.

Anselm spoke. He had insisted on it, though his doctors advised rest, though his hands shook so badly that Dev had to hold the microphone for him.

"My name is Anselm Reyes," he began, and paused, and the pause stretched so long that someone in the front row leaned forward. "For twelve years I have practiced saying that sentence out loud. In the valley, I said it to goats. They were not impressed." A ripple of uneasy laughter, quickly stilled by the gravity of his face. "I am saying it here because silence was the company's best equipment. Cheaper than rope. More reliable than helicopters. They bought our silence, and it held for twelve years, and then five people in a leaky teahouse decided to stop honoring the purchase." He looked directly at the camera, at the lenses, at whoever needed to be looked at. "Eli Halloran died so that I would not die alone. Everything I have said since is just interest on that debt. Collect it. Spend it well."

Afterward, in the corridor, the older woman from the panel approached Priya while Lena was surrounded by journalists and Dev stood apart, taking calls he didn't seem to want.

"Your own file," the woman said. "The summit record. It will be reviewed alongside Ms. Halloran's. I can't promise an outcome."

"I falsified a document to keep a client alive," Priya said. "Whatever they decide, I already know what I did and why. That part's settled. It's been settled for years." She smiled, tired. "But thank you for saying it out loud anyway. Nobody ever says it out loud. That's most of the injury, you know. Not the punishment. The silence around it."

The woman regarded her for a moment. "You know," she said, "in twenty-two years on this panel, I have processed perhaps four hundred cases. Do you know how many people have ever told me the truth in that room?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Three. Counting you."

* * *

Evening came down over Kathmandu in layers of rain and sodium light. The crew gathered at a rooftop restaurant near Thamel—Tomas ordering for everyone before anyone could object, Kip narrating the day's coverage off his phone like a war correspondent, Dev mostly silent, eating little, watching Lena with an expression Priya couldn't fully read and suspected was meant to be unreadable.

Halfway through dinner, Kip set his phone down with unusual care.

"They arrested two of them today," he said. "Meridian. The operations director and the Kathmandu subsidiary manager. 'Questioned and released pending further investigation.'" He looked around the table. "Released. Same day. But it's moving. Meena says the financial trail goes higher—she thinks there's a minister involved, maybe two. She says it'll take months. Maybe years."

"Justice moves at bureaucracy speed," Tomas said, ladling out more curry. "Slow, and mostly sideways. But it moves."

"Imperfectly," Priya said.

"Everything worth anything arrives imperfectly," Tomas said. "Perfect justice is just another kind of fairy tale. This kind—" he gestured with the ladle at the phone, at the city beyond it, at the whole slow grinding machinery of it, "—this kind actually happens. Ugly and late and incomplete. But it happens."

Later, walking back to the guesthouse through wet streets, Priya fell into step beside Lena. The rain had thinned to mist. Somewhere a scooter buzzed past, throwing up silver spray.

"There's a letter," Priya said. "The reinstatement. It's still sitting unanswered, isn't it."

"It's sitting."

"And the offers."

"Sitting."

They walked a few steps in silence. Then Lena stopped, under the awning of a shuttered trekking shop, and looked at Priya with something new in her face—not the armor, not the grief, but the thing underneath both, the thing that had survived them.

"Tomas is right," she said. "About imperfect justice. About ugly and late." She turned the carabiner over one final time and pocketed it. "But here's what I figured out tonight, watching Anselm say his own name. The pardon is theirs to give. It always was. What's mine is what comes next." She started walking again, faster, the way she moved on a trail when the route had finally become clear. "Come on. Kip's been dying to show us the name he picked. He's got it written on the back of a permit application he forged when he was fifteen, which apparently he's been keeping as a good-luck charm, which is a sentence I never thought I'd say out loud."

Priya laughed despite herself and hurried after her, and behind them the rain kept falling on Kathmandu—thin, gray, indifferent, and for the first time in twelve years, falling on a city where the truth was finally, imperfectly, out loud.
