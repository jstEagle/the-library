## 38. The Compass Mended

The jeweler's shop on a Thamel side street was no wider than a coffin and smelled of solder flux and sandalwood incense, the two smells warring politely beneath a single bare bulb. Lena had walked past it for years without seeing it—Thamel was a bazaar of a thousand identical doors, trekking agencies stacked above pashmina stalls stacked above momo joints, all of it shouting—but today she had stopped, because today she had finally decided to open the tin.

She'd carried it from Pokhara wrapped in a wool sock inside her duffel, the way you carry something that is either nothing or everything and you haven't yet let yourself know which. The tin had once held loose tea. Now it held a compass with a cracked bezel and a needle that swung like a drunk trying to find north, and a carabiner stamped *E.H.* in someone's careful engraving pen, twelve years old and still bright at the bottom of the grooves.

The jeweler was an old Newari man named Mr. Shakya, with reading glasses pushed up into thin white hair and hands so steady they seemed to belong to a different, calmer world. He turned the compass over twice under his loupe without asking what it was or why it mattered. That was the first thing Lena liked about him. In Thamel everyone asked questions; this man simply looked.

"The crystal is cracked," he said. "That I can replace. But the needle—" He held it up to the light. The needle trembled against its pivot, listing east, then west, then settling nowhere at all. "Someone dropped it. Or it was near a strong magnet. The magnetization is damaged. I can re-magnetize it, but it will not be the same needle. It will point true, but it will be—changed."

Lena looked at the little brass instrument lying on the green felt of his workbench, small as a pocket watch, heavy as a headstone. Eli had carried it through the Karakoram before he ever heard of the Tsangpo. He'd taught her to take bearings with it when she was nineteen, his big cracked hands cupping hers around the base plate, saying *don't fight the needle, Lena, you don't steer it, you read it.* She had thought about those words more times in the last year than in the previous decade combined.

"Changed how?" she asked.

Mr. Shakya shrugged, a small precise movement. "It will be accurate. It will not be original. Some people care about that." He looked up over his glasses at her, and his eyes were shrewd and kind in equal measure. "Some people need the object to stay broken, because the brokenness is the memory."

Lena laughed—a short, startled sound, the kind that escapes before permission. "You're a philosopher as well as a jeweler."

"I am a repairman," he said. "It is the same thing. Everything people bring me, they bring me because they want to keep using it. A watch that only sits in a drawer is already dead. You want this to live?" He tapped the compass. "Then it must be changed."

She sat with that while he wrote out a ticket in tiny handwriting and quoted her a price that was almost insultingly modest. Outside, Thamel roared on—motorbikes, tour groups, a boy selling roasted corn, the eternal commerce of a city built on other people's adventures—and Lena stood in the doorway holding the empty tin and felt something in her chest unclench by a single degree.

"Yes," she said. "Fix it. Make it live."

* * *

The expedition museum occupied a converted Rana-era house at the edge of the old city, all carved windows and dusty grandeur, its rooms hung with oxygen bottles and torn tents and photographs of men with frost-white faces standing on summits that had killed half their friends. Lena had never been inside. Museums of mountaineering had always struck her as mausoleums with gift shops—the sport's dead arranged in glass cases next to commemorative mugs.

But Dev had called her three days after they'd come down off the mountain, his voice stripped of all its Kathmandu polish, and said: *The federation wants the journal. And there's a curator here who says the right things. I vetted her myself. I'm good at vetting liars now—it turns out to be my one genuine expertise.*

So Lena went, carrying the journal in a waterproof dry bag against her chest like a smuggled organ.

The curator's name was Meera Tamang, and she met Lena in a back room lined with acid-free boxes rather than among the exhibits, which Lena took as a good sign. Meera was perhaps fifty, with silver threading her braids and the unhurried manner of someone who had spent thirty years handling other people's grief and learned not to flinch at any of it.

"You understand what we're offering isn't purchase," Meera said, laying out white cotton gloves on the table like a surgeon preparing. "We don't buy materials. We accept them as donations, under your terms. You set the access conditions. If you want it sealed for ten years, it's sealed for ten years. If you want to withdraw it, there's a process."

"I don't want it sealed," Lena said. "I want it read."

Meera paused, gloves halfway on. "Most families want the opposite."

"Most families didn't spend twelve years being lied to about why their person died." Lena set the dry bag on the table but didn't open it yet. Her hands were steady—that was the strange part. All the way across town on the microbus, wedged between a sack of onions and a French backpacker, she had expected to shake apart the moment she handed it over. Instead she felt like a guide who has finally put down a pack she should have shed at base camp. "Eli Halloran stayed behind on the ice so a dying man wouldn't die alone, and then the company that sent them buried the whole thing and hunted the survivor who could have told the truth. The journal is the only version of events written in his own hand. It belongs somewhere it can't be bought, burned, or 'lost in processing.' Your museum survived the last government and the one before that. That's better provenance than most archives can claim."

Meera studied her for a long moment. "You've thought about custody."

"I've thought about what happens to things when nobody fights for them."

They opened the bag together. The journal came out smelling faintly of snow cave and kerosene, its cover warped from damp that had never fully dried, Eli's blocky capitals still legible on the flyleaf: *E. HALLORAN — PROPERTY OF NO ONE BUT ME.* Lena watched Meera's gloved hands turn the pages with a reverence that made her throat tighten—not handling it like an artifact, exactly, but like a voice. Because that's what it was. Twelve years of silence, and here was Eli talking again, complaining about the dal bhat, sketching the gorge walls, writing *the river doesn't start, it escapes* two weeks before he died.

"There's one page missing," Lena said. "I kept it. The last one. I'm not ready to give that up yet."

"That's allowed," Meera said gently. "Donations aren't surrender. They're trust."

When it was done—forms signed, conditions logged, the journal sliding into its box like a letter finally reaching its address—Meera walked her out through the exhibit hall. And there, in a case near the window, Lena stopped without meaning to.

A rope coil, fused into a single mass by ice and age. A placard: *Recovered from the South Col, 1979. Donated by the family of Tenzing Norbu Lama.*

"His family comes every year," Meera said quietly, following her gaze. "On the anniversary. They stand there for a while and then they go for lunch. The first few years the mother cried. Now they argue about whose turn it is to pay." She smiled a little. "That's what a museum is for, Ms. Halloran. Not mourning. Continuation. The dead get to keep being part of the family argument."

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold. Lena stood on the museum steps and realized she was hungry—actually hungry, body-hungry, in a way she hadn't been since the mountain. Grief, it turned out, had been occupying stomach space for twelve years, and evicting even part of it left room.

She bought roasted corn from the same boy who worked the Thamel corner and ate it walking, and it tasted like being alive, which was embarrassing and true.

* * *

The rehabilitation center sat on a hillside above the valley rim, a low complex of whitewashed buildings surrounded by marigolds, where the army sent soldiers who'd stepped on mines and the mountaineering association sent the occasional shattered climber nobody else would pay for. Anselm Reyes had been there eleven weeks. The company that had extracted him alive and then erased him for twelve years was currently being dismantled in courtrooms and newspaper columns, and its former asset was learning, at forty-one, how to walk.

Lena found him in the physiotherapy garden, gripping parallel bars, sweating through a gray t-shirt, taking steps with the ferocious concentration of a man defusing a bomb. A young therapist counted aloud beside him. Anselm's face was gaunt still, the frostbite scars on his cheeks and fingers shiny pink, but his eyes—when he saw her—went bright and wet at once.

"Halloran," he said. "You're supposed to knock."

"It's a garden."

"It's my office." He let go of the bars—one step, two, three, free-standing, arms out like a tightrope walker—and made it four steps before his knee buckled and the therapist caught him. He swore in Spanish, then English, then something that might have been Quechua. "Four," he announced to the sky. "Last week it was two. Write it down. Somebody write it down."

The therapist grinned and wrote it down.

They sat afterward in plastic chairs under a jacaranda tree, and a nurse brought tea, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they were the kind of people who understood that silence shared was different from silence endured. Below them the valley haze burned off in slow layers, terraces emerging, rooftops, the glint of the ring road.

"Eli used to talk about you," Anselm said at last. His voice had gotten stronger week by week; it still had gravel in it, but the words came whole now. "Back on the approach. Before everything. He said, *my sister reads terrain the way I read water—you look at a slope and you see where it wants to fail.* He was proud of you. Insufferably proud. I used to tell him to shut up about it."

"He never told me that."

"He wouldn't. He saved the soft stuff for strangers." Anselm turned his teacup in his scarred hands. One finger was gone below the second knuckle; the others moved stiffly, retrained. "I've been thinking about what I owe you. Not the testimony—I'd have given that to anyone who asked, eventually, that was just fear wearing off like anesthesia. I mean the rest. You pulled me out of that valley. You could have taken the pardon and the data and left me to the herders. Nobody would have known. Even Dev didn't know where I was until you found me."

"You hid well."

"Twelve years of practice." He looked down at his legs, encased in braces, absurd and miraculous. "You know what I did the day after the extraction? The company man put me in a jeep and drove me to Kathmandu and gave me money and a new name and a speech about national security, and the next morning I woke up in a guesthouse and I couldn't remember how to be a person. So I walked. Just walked north until the maps ran out, and then I kept going. I told myself I was hiding. Really I was waiting. I just didn't know what for." He looked up. "Turns out it was you."

Lena's throat worked. "Anselm—"

"No, let me finish, I rehearsed this." He held up a maimed hand. "Eli stayed with Marcus so Marcus wouldn't die alone. That's what he chose with his last hours. And I spent twelve years deciding that staying silent was the same thing as staying—that keeping the secret was a way of keeping him. It wasn't. It was just fear with a view." He set the cup down. "So here's what I owe you: I'm going to live loudly. Rehab, testimony, whatever comes. I'm going to walk out of here someday and go home to Bolivia and plant something, and every year on the anniversary I'm going to say their names out loud where people can hear. Eli Halloran. Marcus Osei. The others. Loudly. That's the payment. That's the only currency worth anything now."

Lena reached into her jacket pocket and brought out the carabiner. She'd meant to do this differently—she'd composed speeches on the bus, elegant ones—but her hand simply extended and there it was, the old aluminum catching the light, *E.H.* bright in its grooves.

Anselm went very still.

"The old woman in the village gave it to me," Lena said. "He traded it to them for shelter, on the way in. She kept it twelve years on a cord around her neck." Her voice caught and she let it. "I think it should be yours. You're the one who was there. You're the witness. I'm just the sister."

For a long moment Anselm didn't move. Then he took it with both hands, the way you receive something breakable, and closed his fingers around it, and bowed his head, and Lena watched a grown man weep silently in a garden full of marigolds and did not look away, because looking away was how all of this had happened in the first place.

"He'd have hated this," Anselm managed finally, laughing wetly. "All the ceremony. He'd have said, *it's a piece of gear, Lenz, gear gets used.*"

"So use it," Lena said. "Clip it to your walker. Be insufferable."

He laughed then, really laughed, and clipped it to the frame of his walker where it chimed softly against the aluminum with every step, and the therapist, passing, raised an eyebrow and wisely said nothing.

* * *

She stopped at the jeweler's on her way back through Thamel, though the ticket said three days. Mr. Shakya saw her face through the doorway and shook his head, amused, and disappeared into the back, and returned with the compass.

It lay on fresh green felt, and it was beautiful—new crystal clear as glacier ice, brass polished, the crack gone as if it had never been. And the needle. The needle lay steady, certain, pointing unerringly toward the door and the street and the north beyond them, re-magnetized, changed, alive.

"Try it," Mr. Shakya said.

Lena picked it up. It fit her palm the way it had always fit Eli's, warm metal, worn edges. She turned in place, watching the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, always finding its bearing, patient as bedrock. *You don't steer it,* Eli had said. *You read it.*

"How does it feel?" the old man asked. "Different?"

Lena considered. The compass was not the compass it had been. Neither was she. Both of them had been dropped, cracked, remade by steady hands, and both of them still pointed.

"It feels like it works," she said.

"That," said Mr. Shakya, "is the entire art."

She paid him, overpaid him, and he pretended not to notice. Outside, evening was coming down over Thamel, the neon flickering on, the trekking shops closing, the city exhaling into its thousand small rituals. Her phone buzzed. Kip.

*found a shopfront in Lakeside. cheap. Tomas says the kitchen's a disaster but he can fix it. Priya already drew up route plans. are we actually doing this??*

Lena stood under the buzzing sign of the jeweler's shop, the mended compass in her pocket riding light against her hip, and typed back:

*tomorrow. bring the paperwork. and tell Tomas the first pot of dal is on me.*

She started walking. North, without checking. The needle would have agreed.
