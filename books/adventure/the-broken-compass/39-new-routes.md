## 39. New Routes

The sign went up on a Tuesday, because Tomas said Tuesdays were lucky and nobody had the heart to argue with him about it anymore. Kip held the ladder while Priya screwed the board to the lintel of the old teahouse they'd leased on the quiet end of Lakeside, and when it was done he stepped back into the street and read it three times through, like a prayer he was checking for typos.

**HALLORAN & CO. — SMALL GROUPS. HONEST ROUTES.**

That was all. No mountain ranges promised, no summits guaranteed, no photographs of other people's triumphs stolen from stock libraries and pasted onto the wall. Just the name and the promise, painted by hand in white on weathered blue, because Lena had refused every suggestion of something grander.

"Nobody's going to know what we do," Kip had said, back in the planning weeks, when they'd argued around Tomas's table with maps spread over the rice bowls.

"They'll know what we *don't* do," Lena had said. "That's rarer."

Now, one year almost to the day since they'd come down out of the gorge with Anselm Reyes strapped to a makeshift litter and the monsoon chasing them like a debt collector, Kip stood in the doorway of the office that used to be a chicken coop and watched his first client come up the path.

Her name was Marta Osei, thirty-four, a schoolteacher from Accra who had saved for two years for this trip and had chosen them—chosen *them*, out of every outfitter in Nepal—because of an article she'd read. The article was about the testimony. It was mostly about Dev, actually, and the company that had unraveled, and the inquiry that was still grinding through the federation's committees like a glacier doing its slow honest work. But there'd been a paragraph near the end about the guides who'd brought the survivor home, and Marta had cut it out and carried it in her passport folder across two continents.

"You're the one who made the diversion," she said, shaking his hand at the gate. "At the bridge. The article said—"

"The article exaggerated," Kip said, and felt his ears go hot. "It was firecrackers and a phone. Anybody could've done it."

"Anybody could have," Marta agreed pleasantly. "You did."

He didn't have an answer for that, so he took her pack instead and led her inside, where the smell of Tomas's cardamom tea had already colonized every room, and where Priya was waiting with the route briefing spread out on the table—not a glossy brochure but a hand-drawn map, annotated in three colors of ink, with the dangerous sections marked honestly in red and the words *we turn back here if the weather says so* written right on the paper where a client could see them.

Marta looked at the map for a long moment. Then she looked up at Priya and said, "This is the first time anyone's shown me where I might die before taking my money."

"We find it saves time," Priya said, deadpan, and Kip saw the exact moment Marta decided she was in the right hands.

* * *

The trek was five days, Annapurna foothills, nothing forbidden about any meter of it. That was the whole point. Lena had drawn the route herself, and Kip had watched her do it, pen moving slow, and understood without being told that every line was a kind of vow. There were valleys east of here you could not pay this crew to enter. There were names you could not put on their permits. When clients asked—some of them did, the ones who'd followed the story—Lena gave the same answer every time, flat as a stone: "We don't go there." And if they pressed, she'd add, "Ask me why over the second cup of tea, and I'll tell you. But we still won't go."

Kip had been seventeen when he'd first run messages for Lena Halloran's illegal treks, a kid with fast legs and no family worth the name, who had decided somewhere along the way that she was the most competent human being alive. He was eighteen now, and the hero worship had been ground down and rebuilt into something better by everything he'd seen—the snow cave, the flood channel, the way she'd burned the survey cards without hesitating, choosing a dead man's truth over her own freedom. He knew now that competence wasn't armor. He knew it was just love, wearing work clothes.

But some part of him had never stopped wanting to be her. And today, for the first time, he was going to lead.

Not the whole trek—Lena walked sweep, and Priya handled navigation, and Tomas had stayed behind with his ankle propped up and his radio within reach, running base camp and pretending he wasn't anxious. But the trail from the last village to the high pasture was Kip's. Lena had handed it to him at breakfast without ceremony, the way she handed people things that mattered, so they couldn't refuse.

"Your call," she'd said. "My rope calls, though. You use mine."

"I know them," Kip said. "I've known them for years."

"That's why I'm giving you the trail." She'd looked at him then with those gray eyes that seemed to be always measuring weather somewhere behind your face. "Rope calls are how you tell people the truth on a mountain, Kip. Fast, and plain, and loud enough to hear over fear. Everything else is decoration."

* * *

The morning of the third day came up gold and cold, frost smoking off the terraces, and Kip stood at the head of the line with Marta behind him and the two porters, Dawa and Mingma, behind her, and above them the trail climbed through rhododendron toward a ridge where the wind was already talking.

He ran the checks the way he'd been taught. Harness doubled back. Helmet snug. Pack straps settled. He touched each anchor point himself, because Lena touched every client's gear herself and never once made it feel like distrust—it felt like being counted, like mattering enough to check.

"On rope," Kip called, and used the cadence, the particular rhythm of Lena's voice that lived in his own throat now. "*Clip and call.*"

"*Clipped,*" Marta answered, a little uncertain on the echo, and Dawa and Mingma rang it down the line like bells.

They climbed. The trail was good for an hour, stone steps worn smooth by generations of feet, and Kip set a pace that was slower than his legs wanted and exactly right for Marta's breath, and he learned what Lena must have learned long ago—that leading is mostly listening with your eyes. Marta's stride shortened on the steep bits. She drank too little. So he called a water stop before she needed one, framed it as his own thirst, and watched her color come back.

Near the top the trail crossed a slab of rock polished by runoff, maybe fifteen meters, exposed, with a long green drop to the left. Nothing serious. Everything serious, if you were new.

Kip stopped the line at the edge and looked at it the way Lena would look—reading the rock, reading the sky, reading the people behind him. Dry. Wind moderate. Marta steady but wide-eyed.

"Short roped section," he called back. "*Stay tight, move when I say.*" Then, softer, just to Marta, because Lena had taught him that too—that the loudest voice isn't always the kindest one: "I've got you on the rope the whole way. You can't fall far enough to matter. Ready?"

"No," Marta said honestly, and grinned, and stepped out when he said *go*.

Halfway across, her foot skated on wet stone—just a skate, just a slip of centimeters—but Kip felt it come up the rope like a word, and his body answered before his mind did, weight dropping, arm locking, the brake hand doing what ten thousand practice pulls had taught it. Marta caught herself. Stood. Breathed.

"Nice catch," she said shakily.

"Wasn't a catch," Kip said. "Just a rope doing its job. Keep moving."

But his heart was hammering, and he knew—he knew with total certainty—that somewhere behind him Lena had seen the whole thing, and that she hadn't called out, hadn't moved, hadn't taken it from him. He glanced back once at the rest stop and found her sitting on a boulder, drinking tea from her thermos, watching him with an expression he'd never quite seen on her face before. It took him most of the afternoon to name it. It was pride. Not the fierce, armored kind she wore like everything else. The quiet kind, the kind that doesn't need to announce itself because it isn't afraid of being taken away.

* * *

They made the high pasture by late light, and the mountains did what the mountains do at dusk, which is set themselves on fire and dare you to look away. Annapurna South went rose, then copper, then ash. Marta sat on the lodge steps with her tea forgotten, crying a little, not bothering to hide it, and Kip sat beside her and let the silence be enough.

"My students think I'm crazy," she said eventually. "Spending my savings to walk uphill and sleep cold." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "But I wanted to see something true. You know? Something nobody edited."

Kip thought about the article in her passport folder. About the testimony, and the inquiry, and Dev Kapoor, who had lost his company, his name, and nearly his life, and who now worked—this was true, Kip could barely believe it and he'd watched it happen—as a volunteer instructor teaching rescue rope work to village youth groups, driving out from Pokhara in a battered jeep twice a month, asking for nothing. Dev had come by the office last week to drop off a coil of static line he'd gotten cheap, and he'd stood in the doorway looking at the sign for a long time, and then he'd said, "Halloran & Co. You kept my name off it."

"It's not my name either," Lena had said. "It's hers." Meaning the outfit. Meaning the idea. Meaning, maybe, the version of themselves they were trying to become.

Dev had nodded like a man accepting a sentence he'd earned, and driven off, and Lena had watched the jeep until it turned the corner, and Kip had understood that forgiveness between those two was not a door that opened but a road they were both still walking, and that was somehow better.

"There's a lot of edited stuff out there," Kip said to Marta now. "Mountains especially. Everybody wants the summit photo. Nobody wants the turning-back stories." He wrapped his hands around his own cooling mug. "Our rule is the turnaround counts. If you turn around safe, that's a successful trip. We say it before you pay. Some people don't book."

"And the ones who do?"

Kip looked down the valley, where the lights of the last village were coming on one by one, small and stubborn against the dark. "The ones who do are looking for true," he said.

* * *

On the fifth day they came down, and Tomas met them at the gate with food that could raise the dead, and Priya logged the trip in the ledger she kept—every route, every condition, every decision, written down so the next crew could learn from it or argue with it. "Institutional memory," she called it. "So we don't get arrogant." As if arrogance were a disease you could catch from success, which, Kip was beginning to understand, it was.

Lena debriefed Marta at the table, and Kip listened while she did it, and heard her give the client credit for every good decision of the week and take none for herself. When Marta mentioned the catch on the slab—"your young guide has good instincts"—Lena corrected her without looking up from the ledger.

"He has good training. Instincts are just training that got there faster than thinking." Then she closed the book and finally looked at Kip, and said, in front of everyone, in her flat way that made it weigh more than any speech: "He led the whole high section clean. I'd walk on his rope."

Tomas dropped a pot. Priya smiled into her ledger. Kip stood very still and let it land, the way you let a heavy pack settle onto your shoulders before you start walking.

* * *

That night he couldn't sleep, and around eleven he found Lena on the roof of the old teahouse, sitting against the parapet with a mug of tea gone cold, looking north. Not toward Annapurna. North, where the border ridges lay, where the closed valleys lay, where a blue-black lake sat under a hanging serac in a cirque that would never appear on any itinerary they sold. She went there sometimes, he'd noticed. Not with her feet. With her eyes.

He sat down beside her without asking. That was allowed now. Had been allowed for a while.

"Eli led his first trek at nineteen," she said, after a while. Kip went carefully still. She almost never talked about Eli unprompted; the journal was in the museum in Kathmandu now, behind glass, and the carabiner stayed in her pocket, and grief was a country she visited alone. But tonight she talked. "He was terrified. Dropped a whole pot of dal Bhat on a client. Came home convinced he'd failed." A ghost of a smile. "The client wrote us a letter saying it was the best trip of her life. Said our brother made her feel like the mountain belonged to her." She turned the cold mug in her hands. "That's the job, Kip. Not the summits. Making people feel like the world belongs to them."

"Is that what you're doing?" he asked. "Making it belong to people?"

She was quiet a moment. "I'm trying to make it belong to *me* again," she said. "Turns out the way you do that is handing pieces of it away." She glanced at him sidelong. "Piece at a time. Don't let it go to your head."

"Too late," Kip said.

Below them, Lakeside was settling into its evening sounds—dogs, distant music, a boat knocking against a jetty. Above them the stars were doing their ancient indifferent work. Somewhere south, in a rehabilitation center in Kathmandu, Anselm Reyes was learning to walk again, and had sent them a postcard last month with a single line: *Tell the kid with the firecrackers he saved more than me.* Somewhere east, in a committee room, the federation was still grinding. Somewhere in a drawer in this very building, Lena's pardon letter sat unanswered, and three outfitters' offers with it, gathering dust like artifacts from a previous civilization.

"First solo lead next season," Lena said. It wasn't a question.

"If you'll walk on my rope," Kip said.

"I already told you I would." She drained the cold tea and stood, and paused at the roof door, silhouetted against the stair light. "Kip. The compass is yours. When I'm done carrying it."

His throat closed. He managed, "You're not done."

"No," she agreed, and there was something almost like peace in it. "But someday. Things aren't meant to be carried forever. They're meant to be passed." And she went down the stairs, her footsteps fading into the house where Tomas was already asleep and Priya's lamp still burned over the ledger, and Kip stayed on the roof a while longer, eighteen years old, holding a future that fit in two open hands.

Downstairs, the phone began to ring.
