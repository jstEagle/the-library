## 40. Source

The meadow was not on any map she'd ever trusted, and that was precisely why she'd chosen it.

Lena had walked three days to reach it—alone, unburdened by clients or contracts or the particular weight of other people's expectations. Three days up from the last road, past the terraced fields where children had shouted *namaste* at her and she'd shouted it back and meant it, past the rhododendron forests still holding their late blooms like coals that refused to go out, past the yak herders' stone shelters where an old man had given her salt tea without asking her name. Names were currency again now; hers had been restored, stamped, filed in offices in Kathmandu. But up here names didn't matter. Up here there was only the mountain doing what the mountain had always done, indifferent and magnificent, wearing its glaciers like a crown it never asked for.

The meadow sat in a broad saddle below the tree line, a bowl of grass and wild iris and small blue poppies that opened only when the sun touched them directly. A stream cut through its center, bright and quick, tumbling over stones the color of old iron. She knew without checking—which was itself a kind of knowledge she'd spent a decade refusing—that this water would find its way down, joining other water, gathering tributaries like rumors gather detail, until somewhere far below it became the river. The Tsangpo's headwaters. The eye of the river, Eli had written, and she had spent twelve years believing those words were a place you could stand.

She stood in it now. It looked nothing like what she'd imagined.

That was the first thing she understood, sitting down heavily on a flat rock still warm from the morning sun: she had built the source in her mind as something dramatic—a cathedral of ice, a black lake under a serac, a wound in the earth where her brother had gone in and not come out. She had made it a place of punishment. And here instead was grass. Flowers. Water laughing over stones. A marmot whistling somewhere upslope, annoyed at her presence. The world's indifference, which had once felt like cruelty, now felt almost like mercy.

She set down her pack and took out the bundle.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the same cord Kip used for everything, because Kip had insisted on packing it himself the night before she left. He'd been solemn about it in the way only a nineteen-year-old can be solemn—performing gravity he half felt, half invented—but his hands had been careful, and when he'd handed it over he'd said, "You don't have to do anything with them, you know. You could just keep them."

"I know," she'd said.

"You could read them a hundred more times."

"I know, Kip."

"That's all I'm saying." He'd shoved his hands in his pockets and looked at the floor of the gear room, at the neat coils of rope they'd racked after the season's last trip, and then he'd said, quieter, "He'd want you to put them down, though. That's my actual opinion. You don't have to take it."

She'd taken it. She was here because she'd taken it.

She untied the cord now and unwrapped the oilcloth, and the wind immediately tried to take what was inside—the way wind does, curious, thieving—and she held the pages down against her knees and let herself look at them one more time.

They were copies. Priya had made them in Kathmandu, at a shop near the federation building, on a machine that hummed and flashed green, and the originals—the true pages, Eli's handwriting, his terrible spelling, his marginal jokes—were already behind glass in the expedition museum, catalogued and labeled and safe from weather and fire and the appetites of men like the client. What Lena held was paper and toner. What she held was also everything else.

*Day 6 — Anselm says the gorge narrows to nothing above the second moraine. I think he's wrong but I love being wrong in country like this.*

*Day 11 — Found a spring coming straight out of the ice. Tasted it. Tasted like the beginning of the world.*

*Day 19 — If anyone reads this: I'm not lost. I'm exactly where I meant to be. Tell Lena the compass was always hers.*

She had read that last line perhaps four hundred times. She had read it in hotel rooms and teahouses and once, memorably and shamefully, aloud to Dev in a snow cave while both of them were too altitude-drunk to pretend they weren't crying. She had read it looking for instructions, for absolution, for some final sentence that would tell her what to do with the rest of her life. There was no final sentence. There was just a brother who had loved a river and a sister who had loved him, and between those two facts, all the silence in the world.

The wind came again, warmer now, carrying the smell of sun-baked grass and distant rain—the monsoon was weeks away yet, but the air already remembered it. Down-valley, the stream went on talking to itself, patient, certain, going where it went.

Lena stood up.

She didn't have a speech. She'd thought, on the walk in, that she might say something—that grief deserved ceremony, that a woman who had finally stopped running owed the dead at least a few formal words. But standing there with the pages loose in her hands, she found that everything she might say had already been said, over fires and across watches and into sat phones, said to Dev and Tomas and Priya and Kip, said to Anselm Reyes in a herder's hut while he learned to hold a cup with hands that would never fully work again. The testimony had been given. The truth had been dragged into the light, kicking, and it had done what truth does—it had cost everyone something, and it had freed no one completely, and it had been worth it anyway.

So she said nothing. She simply lifted the first handful of pages and opened her fingers.

The wind took them.

They rose raggedly, catching and dipping, white against the green—one page cartwheeling high enough to flash in the sun before the gust carried it out over the slope, another snagging briefly in the iris stems before a second breath pulled it free. She fed them out slowly, a few at a time, watching each one go. *Day 6.* Gone. *Day 11.* Gone. The pages scattered downslope like a flock of strange birds, settling into the grass, sliding toward the stream, and where they touched the water they darkened, softened, began to come apart into pulp and ink and nothing, joining the current.

She watched one page—the one with the line about the compass—land on the surface of the stream and turn twice, deliberate as a leaf deciding, and then slide away over the stones and out of sight around the bend.

"Tell Lena the compass was always hers," she said aloud, to no one, to him, to the water. "I know. I finally know."

And then—there was no decision in it, no permission asked or granted—it arrived. The weeping came up from somewhere below thought, below the ribs, from the place where she had packed her brother twelve years ago along with the guilt and the blacklist and the careful competent armor of a woman who could lead anyone anywhere except out of her own valley. It came up and it came out and she let it, kneeling in the grass with her fists full of the last pages, sobbing in great ugly unhurried waves that had nothing to do with dignity and everything to do with time. Twelve years of it. The Kyrgyzstan rescue too, which she had never properly mourned because she'd been too busy surviving its aftermath—the client's family, the inquiry, the name she couldn't say in certain rooms. The crew, whom she had loved fiercely and told so rarely. Dev, who had chosen cowardice and then, at the end, in the flood channel with the water tearing at him, had chosen honestly, and paid for it with everything. Anselm, walking again, slowly, on legs that had forgotten how, who had hugged her outside the museum and said, "He talked about you the whole time. Did you know that? Every single day. You were the north he steered by."

She cried until there was nothing structural left in her, until she was emptied down to the bedrock, and then she cried a little more, because it turned out there is always a little more. The marmot had given up complaining. The sun moved. The stream ran.

When it was over she was shaking and light, the way she felt after a long descent—wrecked and weightless at once. She wiped her face on her sleeve, an act so mundane it made her laugh, one wet broken laugh that startled a chough off a boulder. She gathered the last few pages that hadn't caught the wind and walked them down to the stream herself and gave them to the water individually, like introducing old friends.

Then she sat back on the warm rock for a long time and did the hardest thing she had done in twelve years, harder than the ice bridge, harder than the gorge, harder than burning the survey cards while the client's voice crackled with fury through the satellite phone:

She did nothing at all.

She sat in the meadow and let the mountain be the mountain. She watched the light move across the cirque walls to the north—the forbidden country, the closed valley, still closed, still forbidden, and fine, let it be; some places should keep their own counsel. She watched a pair of lammergeiers ride the thermals with their terrible elegant patience. She ate an apple and tasted it fully, which felt like a skill she was relearning. And underneath her, beneath the grass and the stone and the whole slow body of the mountain, she could feel it—not hear, not see, just feel—the water going. Finding its way down through channels older than any nation's borders, older than any company's greed, older than grief itself. Going where it went. Carrying what it carried. Not holding anything, not even for a moment, and arriving anyway.

* * *

She came down over two days, moving easily, sleeping well in her bivy under stars so thick they seemed less like lights than like weather. On the second afternoon she passed through the village where the old woman lived—the one who had pressed the carabiner into her hand a year ago, stamped *E.H.*, worn smooth as sea glass. The woman was on her doorstep shelling peas, and she looked up at Lena with eyes that missed nothing, and she said, "You went up."

"I went up."

"And?"

Lena crouched down so they were level. "I left something there," she said. "I brought something back." She touched the pocket where the carabiner rode, where it always rode now, next to the compass with its new jeweled needle swinging true inside the case Eli had carried across half the world.

The old woman nodded as if this were a complete and satisfactory report, which it was, and gave her a handful of peas to eat on the trail, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it was enough.

In Pokhara, the outfit's small office smelled of Tomas's cooking, because Tomas had decided that a guiding company without a proper kitchen was a contradiction in terms, and no one had ever won an argument with Tomas about food. Priya was at the desk with route plans spread out everywhere, arguing cheerfully with a map the way she argued with maps, which was to say winning. Dev's postcard was pinned to the corkboard between the permit notices—a grainy shot of some Patagonian spire, and beneath it, in his infuriatingly neat handwriting: *Company dissolved. Testimony delivered. Never been poorer. Never slept better. — D.* Kip was out front showing a nervous first-time client how to pack a rucksack, using Lena's rope calls, using her cadence, saying *on belay* and *climb on* with a steadiness that hadn't existed in him a year ago, and when he saw her come up the steps he grinned the grin that had made her decide, somewhere in the gorge with his pack strap in her fist, that whatever happened to her name, this kid was going to be fine.

"Good trip?" he called.

"Yeah," Lena said, and found that it was simply, plainly, entirely true. "Good trip."

She hung her pack on its peg. She washed her hands. Outside, the lake lay flat and gold in the evening light, and beyond it the mountains stood in their ranks, keeping their counsel, letting the rivers run.

Downstream, the water went on—past checkpoints and borders, past villages on maps and villages off them, past everything that had ever tried to own it or dam it or bury what it carried. It did not hurry. It did not stop. It knew the way to the sea, and it went, and it would go long after every name on every file in Kathmandu had faded to blank paper, carrying its small cold cargo of beginnings down through the world.

Lena stood in the doorway a moment longer, listening to it, though the river was days away and would be days away forever.

Then she rolled up her sleeves and went in to help with dinner.
