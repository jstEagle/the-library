## 1. The Weight of Names

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Bad news arrives whenever it likes — it has no manners and no calendar. But good news, real good news, is supposed to come on a Friday, when you've got the whole weekend to sit with it, turn it over, convince yourself it's true.

Instead: Tuesday, rain coming down sideways on the tin roof of my rented room above the gear shop in Calder Junction, and a courier standing at my door holding an envelope heavy enough to be full of coins or contrition. He made me sign for it. That should have been my first clue. Nobody sends a signature-required envelope unless they want proof later that you knew exactly what you were agreeing to.

I signed. My name looked strange on the line, thinner than I remembered it, like a word that had been left out in the weather.

August Yarrow. Once that name had meant something in the Serrano Range. Guides used to say it the way sailors say a lighthouse's name — with respect, with the assumption that the light would be there when you needed it. Eleven years I'd run clients up and down those drainages without losing so much as a fingernail. Then came the Kestrel expedition, and the inquest, and the finding — *operator error, judgment compromised* — and now the name meant something else entirely. It meant the phone that didn't ring. It meant the guide services that suddenly had no openings, the insurance underwriters who'd never heard of me, the old partners who returned my calls with the warmth of men talking to a debt collector.

Three years of that. You can get very good at being nobody. It's a skill, like knots. You practice until your hands stop shaking when you count what's left in the account.

I sat on the bed with the envelope on my knee for a long time before I opened it. Outside, the rain worked at the gutters. Somewhere down the street the gear shop owner — Marta, who still let me have coffee on credit, which was its own small mercy and its own small humiliation — was dragging the shutter halfway closed against the weather.

The letter inside was on paper thick as skin. The Halvard Consortium. A logo like a stylized mountain range folded into an H. And beneath it, a single sentence in type so confident it barely needed to exist:

*We would like to discuss a matter of some urgency regarding the Serrano Range.*

There was a card enclosed, stiff and cream-colored, with an address in the city and a time: Thursday, ten o'clock. No name. No explanation. Just the implicit promise that whatever was urgent enough to spend this kind of money on stationery was urgent enough to pay well.

I want to tell you I threw it in the drawer and thought about it like a careful man. What I actually did was look at the ledger taped inside my closet door — because I kept one, because pretending I wasn't keeping score had stopped working somewhere around month fourteen — and read the number at the bottom. Then I looked at the card again.

The number at the bottom of the ledger was not large. It was, however, the kind of number that arrives with teeth. The loan against my father's place outside of town, which I'd refinanced twice to keep the business alive through the inquest year. The back payments to the guide certification board, without which my credentials stayed frozen in amber. The medical bills from my mother's last winter, which I'd been paying off in installments so small they were less payment than gesture. And underneath all of it, the quiet daily arithmetic of a man eating rice and beans in a rented room while his reputation rotted somewhere out in the weather.

Thursday was two days away. I spent both of them doing what I do best, which is nothing glamorous: I researched. I pulled every scrap I could find on the Halvard Consortium from the library terminal and the battered laptop I kept alive with spite and spare parts. Mining interests, mostly. Holdings scattered across three continents like a man who doesn't sleep well in any one country. Recent activity concentrated in the Serrano Range — a mineral concession, newly permitted, big enough that the financial pages had written about it in the tone people usually reserve for weather systems and wars.

And then the other thing. The thing that made me sit back from the screen.

A government geological survey team had gone into the Serrano Range six weeks ago to do baseline work on the concession land. Standard procedure. Independent verification before the permits went final. Five people, led by a Dr. Elena Vasquez, whose name I recognized distantly from conference proceedings I'd read back when reading conference proceedings was part of my job.

They hadn't come out.

No bodies. No distress call after day nine, when their scheduled check-in simply didn't happen. Search flights had gone up, found the basecamp intact, photographed it, and come home. The official language was *missing, presumed caught in the early-season storm system*, which is the language officials use when they'd prefer everyone stop asking questions. The search had been suspended after eleven days. Weather, they said. Cost, they didn't say.

Six weeks. In six weeks, five trained people don't just evaporate off a mountain. Mountains are indifferent, but they're not tidy. They leave things.

I closed the laptop and lay on the bed and listened to the rain and told myself the truth, which is a discipline I recommend even when it tastes like copper: I did not care why the survey team vanished. Not yet. Not in the way a decent person would care. What I felt was closer to hunger. Somebody had lost people on my mountain, and somebody else was willing to pay a fortune to make the loss go away, and I was three years deep into a life where the only thing anyone wanted from me was the minimum payment.

Wary hope. That's the phrase I'd use, if anyone asked, and nobody ever asks. Hope with its hands up, backing toward the door, checking the exits. Hope that knows exactly how it got burned last time and has already started counting the ways this time will rhyme.

* * *

The city was four hours by bus, and I spent them watching the Serranos recede in the window — white shoulders, gray flanks, the southern pass sitting in its permanent scowl of cloud. People sleep on buses. I never have. Mountains are the only thing I've ever been able to look at without my mind wandering, which probably tells you everything about why my life looks the way it does.

The Halvard Consortium occupied the top four floors of a glass tower that seemed embarrassed by the mountains visible past its shoulder, as if the view were something it had acquired rather than earned. The lobby smelled like money trying to smell like nothing. A receptionist with a headset took my name — said it carefully, *Yarrow*, like she'd been briefed on how to pronounce it, which meant someone had briefed her on me — and put me in a conference room with a table long enough to land aircraft on.

Two people waited. The first was a lawyer; you can always tell, the way you can tell rain is coming by the smell. Gray suit, gray manner, a folder squared to the table's edge with a precision that suggested he ironed his socks. He introduced himself as Counselor Brandt, which is not a name, it's a job description wearing a name as a coat.

The second was Merrick Voss. I didn't know his name yet, but I knew his species immediately: the liaison, the fixer, the man companies build when they need a human face on a decision that has no face of its own. Mid-forties, expensively casual — the jacket cost more than my truck had, if I'd still owned a truck — with the particular tan of a man who golfs rather than climbs. His handshake was calibrated to the millimeter: firm enough to seem honest, brief enough to avoid commitment.

"August Yarrow." He said my full name slowly, tasting it. "You know, we considered about forty guides for this. Your name kept surfacing."

"Surfacing," I said. "Like a wreck."

Something flickered behind his eyes — amusement, maybe, or the assessment of a man deciding whether I was going to be difficult. "Honest. Good. We'll need honest." He gestured me into a chair and didn't sit himself, which was choreography, not accident. Standing men set terms; seated men accept them.

Brandt opened the folder and slid a photograph across the table. Basecamp, shot from the air. Six tents, pitched in textbook formation on the bench above the confluence. Gear racked. A radio antenna standing straight. Snow over everything, but not much — the storm had come late to that elevation, or the camp had been abandoned before it arrived.

"Dr. Elena Vasquez's team," Voss said. "Five people. They went in on the twenty-eighth of September for a six-week baseline survey. On day thirty-one, they missed a scheduled contact. By day forty, the government search had stood down." He paused, letting the dates settle. "The Consortium holds the mineral concession on that land. The permits require completed independent survey verification. Without it — or without the team's field data — our position becomes complicated."

"Complicated," I repeated. "That's a word lawyers use when the simple word costs money."

Brandt's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Voss smiled like I'd passed a test he hadn't announced.

"The Consortium wants the team found," he said. "Alive, ideally. Their instruments recovered regardless. Their field data secured." He laid a second sheet beside the photograph — figures, and my breath did something embarrassing in my chest before I could school it. The retainer alone cleared my mother's medical balance. The completion fee would take the whole ledger, every line of it, and burn it clean. Three years of shame, priced. Apparently shame had a market rate after all.

"And in exchange?" I asked, because there is always an exchange, and men like Voss respect you more for asking.

Voss glanced at Brandt, and Brandt slid a contract across the table. I read contracts the way I read terrain — looking for the place where the ground drops away beneath the obvious route. Most of it was standard: indemnification, insurance riders, extraction protocols. Page nine was where the cliff was.

*All materials recovered during the course of operations — including but not limited to instruments, documents, data storage media, and personal effects of the survey team — remain the exclusive property of the Halvard Consortium and shall be surrendered unexamined. The contractor agrees to make no independent record, reproduction, or disclosure of such materials, and further agrees that the contents thereof shall not be discussed with any party, including members of the recovery team, except as directed by the Consortium.*

Silence clause. I'd seen them before, in the oil years, in the road-building years, back when I still took contracts from men whose money smelled like something. They were never about protecting secrets. They were about making sure that whatever you found on the mountain belonged to the mountain's new owners — including the truth, if the truth happened to be lying around up there unattended.

My old instincts woke up and started pacing. Three years ago I'd have walked out. Two years ago I'd have walked out slower. Today I sat there with page nine under my thumb and did the arithmetic again, the whole ugly column of it: the loan, the board fees, the installments, the room above Marta's shop with its tin roof singing in the rain.

"You want me to find these people," I said, "and hand you everything they were carrying without looking at it."

"We want the situation resolved discreetly," Voss said. "Discretion protects everyone. Including the team, if any of them are alive. Publicity is dangerous to missing persons — it turns rescue into spectacle." He was good. I'll give him that. He'd found the one argument that a guide can't easily refuse, wrapped the leash in a blanket, and called it a kindness.

I thought about Vasquez. Five weeks on a mountain in October, and the search called off after eleven days. I thought about basecamp with its tents pitched neat as a parade ground and no mention anywhere in the reporting of tracks leading out. I thought about my name, August Yarrow, and what it would mean to say it out loud again in rooms where it used to open doors — and what it would cost to say it in rooms like this one, where it would be spoken for me, filed away, attached to a silence clause like a tag on a corpse's toe.

Hope and shame sat down together at the table across from me and refused to leave until I chose between them, which is the trick, of course. There was no choosing. There was only the order you let them win in.

"I'll need full autonomy in the field," I said. "Route decisions, turnaround times, party composition. If I say a slope is done, it's done, and no radio call from you changes that."

"Agreed," Voss said instantly, too instantly, and I noted it the way you note a bridge with fresh paint over rusted cable.

"And I pick my own crew. Local. People who know the drainage."

"Within reason." Another note. Another painted bridge.

I turned to page nine one more time. Read the clause through twice. Somewhere in the tower below us, the city went on making its noises, indifferent as weather. Somewhere north, under snow, five people were either dead or waiting, and either way they deserved better than a search conducted by a man who'd agreed not to see what he found.

But deserving better requires someone able to give it, and I looked at my own reflection ghosted in the glass wall — thinner than the man in the old expedition photos, grayer, with the specific posture of a person who has learned to expect the floor to open — and I understood that I was not currently in a position to be anyone's better. I was in a position to be hired.

I signed.

Brandt collected the pages with liturgical care. Voss shook my hand again, that same millimeter handshake, and held it a half-second longer this time. "The mountain's yours, Mr. Yarrow. Bring our people home."

*Our* people. I heard it and filed it away with the rest — the too-quick agreements, the folder squared to the table edge, the way neither of them had once asked whether I thought the team might still be alive, only whether I could find them. Men who wanted survivors asked different questions. Men who wanted something else asked about instruments and data and discretion.

The bus back left at four. I spent the ride watching the Serranos grow in the window, shoulder by shoulder, the southern pass emerging from its cloud like a door opening onto a room I already knew I shouldn't enter. Wary hope rode next to me the whole way, hands in its pockets, saying nothing, and shame rode in the luggage rack overhead where I'd put it, shifting occasionally, reminding me it weighed more than it looked.

In Calder Junction, Marta had left the light on over the shop door and a thermos hanging on my knob, because that's what belonging looks like when it's real — small, unsolicited, expecting nothing. I stood in the cold holding the thermos and thought about the contract in my pack, page nine folded toward the light like a pressed flower.

Then I went upstairs and started pulling gear, because in eleven days' worth of planning and three years' worth of hunger, I had forgotten to ask the question that matters most, the one I'd been trained to ask first and had somehow skipped past in a conference room with a view:

Nobody had asked me to bring them back.

Not once, in forty minutes of talk about instruments and data and discretion, had Merrick Voss said the words *bring them home alive* — and a man who forgets to hope out loud is a man who already knows the answer.

The rain stopped sometime in the night. When I stepped out at dawn to check the sky, the Serranos stood clear and white and silent under a hard blue sky, and every compass needle in my kit pointed north, steady and true, toward whatever was waiting up there with my name on it.
