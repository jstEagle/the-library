## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The Serrano Range doesn't announce itself. It just accumulates — foothills, then shoulders, then the white wall of the high country rising out of the drainage like something the earth had been holding in its throat for ten thousand years. I'd been staring at it from the helicopter for forty minutes, and it still hadn't said a word to me. Mountains that quiet are either empty or occupied, and I've learned you don't get to know which until they decide.

"Basecamp's in the cirque below the eastern spur," Desh Okonkwo said over the headset. He was riding shotgun, which meant he got the view and I got the rotor wash. "Government team marked it on their last check-in. Coordinates were clean."

"You've been up here before?"

"Twice. Guiding a seismic crew, years back." He turned half around in his seat to look at me. Desh was sixty-one if he was a day, built like a doorframe, with a gray beard he kept trimmed short enough that it couldn't be grabbed in a fight. I'd worked with men like him my whole career. They don't talk much, and when they do, every word has already been weighed. "Never this far up the drainage, though. And never in October."

"October's fine," I said.

"October is a coin flip," he said. "You know that."

I did know that. The Serrano passes in October were a slot machine — three days of bluebird, then a katabatic storm rolling off the icefield that could pin you down for a week. The consortium had looked at the calendar, looked at the missing survey team, and decided the odds were acceptable. That told me something about how much they valued the people they'd hired me to find. Or maybe about how much they valued whatever the team had been carrying. Hard to say which worried me more.

Behind us in the cargo bay sat the rest of the crew: Petra Ilic, twenty-six, junior guide, first contract since her certification; two consortium-hired packers named Brandt and Okafor who hadn't said ten words between them; and enough rope, hardware, and freeze-dried food to outfit a small war. Petra caught me looking and gave me a nod — quick, controlled, the kind of nod you give a boss you haven't figured out yet. She had a face that seemed permanently on the verge of asking a question and a jaw that seemed permanently determined not to ask it. I liked her already, in the way you like a dog that hasn't decided whether to bite you.

The pilot dropped us on a gravel bench above the river at 8,400 feet, and the sound of the rotors died into a silence so complete I could hear the glacier ticking somewhere up-valley, settling into itself like an old man into a chair.

I stood there a moment and let the mountain look at me. Old habit. You give it a minute. You let it take your measure before you start taking yours.

Then I shouldered my pack and walked toward basecamp, and within thirty steps I knew something was wrong.

* * *

Here's what a dead camp looks like. I've seen enough of them — some from accidents, one from something worse I don't talk about at parties. A camp that's been abandoned looks *abandoned*. Guylines sag or snap. Tent flies collapse under snow load or flap themselves to rags. Pots sit out with rainwater in them. Gear gets left behind because carrying it became impossible, and gear that gets left behind gets scattered, because weather is a vandal and time is its accomplice. There's a texture to it. A smell of surrender.

The government team's basecamp had no texture at all.

Three tents stood pitched on the gravel bench, guyed taut, flies tensioned like they'd been set that morning. The cook tarp was rigged over a fire ring stacked with unburned wood. Food caches hung from a bear pole — proper bear hang, counterbalanced, twelve feet up and four feet out, textbook — with the bags sealed and labeled in neat block capitals: DAY 9, DAY 10, DAY 11. Two stoves sat on a flat rock beside a fuel canister, both dry, both capped.

And tracks. That was the thing. There were no tracks leading anywhere.

The ground around the camp was the fine gray glacial till that holds a print like wet cement. My own boots were writing a record behind me with every step. But the camp itself sat inside a perfect circle of untouched ground, as if six people had descended from the sky, lived there for eleven days, and ascended back into it.

"August." Petra had drifted to the edge of the camp, crouched, not touching anything. Good instinct. First person on scene touches nothing until the scene tells you why. "There's boot prints here. Under the fly of the middle tent."

I came over. Sure enough, pressed into the till beneath the tent's vestibule — sheltered from wind, from melt, from everything — were the crisp outlines of mountaineering boots. Size ten-ish. Several sets, overlapping. The ground under a tent fly stays pristine for months. These prints were sharp-edged. Fresh-looking.

"They walked out," Petra said slowly, "but the wind erased everything except—"

"No," I said. "Wind doesn't work selectively."

She looked up at me.

"Wind erases by exposure," I said. "It would have softened these too. Same grain, same exposure window." I straightened up and turned a slow circle, reading the basin the way you read a page. Gravel bench. River fifty meters below, loud enough to cover conversation but not loud enough to cover a shout. Eastern spur walling off the weather from the north. It was, I had to admit, a well-chosen camp. Textbook again. Everything about this place was textbook, and that was exactly what was wrong with it. Real camps aren't textbooks. Real camps have the fire ring moved twice because the smoke kept blowing into someone's tent, and a latrine trench dug badly the first time and redug properly, and arguments written in the placement of things. This camp had been set by someone following a manual, or set by someone who wanted it to look that way.

"Desh," I called. "Radios."

He was already in the equipment tent. He came out holding two handhelds, checked the battery indicators, and thumbed one on. Static, then a clean channel scan. He handed it to me without comment, which from Desh was a paragraph.

Functional. Fully charged. Left behind by a team that had walked out — or been taken — into the highest, coldest week of the season, without radios, in a range where nobody goes.

"Brandt, Okafor — perimeter," I said. "Two hundred meters, all directions. Look for sign. Don't wander onto the moraine." They went without a word, and I watched them go with the mild, professional suspicion I extend to all silent men employed by silent organizations.

Petra had moved to the food cache. She untied the counterbalance knot — clean hands, no gloves, careful — and lowered one bag. Opened it. Held up a vacuum-sealed pouch.

"This is a day-nine ration," she said. "Sealed. And—" she turned it over, checked the date stamp, frowned. "This lot was packed eight weeks ago. For this expedition. So they cached forward rations here before they ever went up."

"Standard practice," Desh said. "Cache heavy, climb light."

"It's *good* practice," Petra agreed. "That's what bothers me. Everything here is good practice. Who camps like this?"

"People who expect inspectors," Desh said.

Nobody laughed. It wasn't a joke.

I walked the camp again, slower. Cook tarp. Fire ring — and here, finally, something human: ash in the ring, real ash, and half-burned ends of spruce dragged down from the treeline, so they *had* burned wood, had cooked, had lived here. But the ash was old. Rain-crusted. Whatever fires had burned here had burned days ago, maybe longer, while the tents stayed pitched and tight and the gear stayed squared away. Somebody had kept this camp *maintained* after whoever lived in it stopped living in it. Or somebody had built a stage set and forgotten that fire leaves a timestamp.

"August." Petra's voice had gone quiet in a different way. Not discovery-quiet. Wrongness-quiet. She was standing at the center of the camp, holding her compass flat on her palm, frowning at it with her head cocked slightly, like a bird listening for worms.

"What've you got?"

"I'm getting a bearing on the eastern spur. Checkpoint for our search grid." She didn't look up. "Except the spur is there." She pointed. "And the needle says it's there." She pointed somewhere else entirely — south-southeast, into blank air above the river valley.

I pulled my own compass. Laid it flat. Let the needle settle.

It settled wrong. Fifteen degrees, maybe seventeen, off true — and not consistently off, either. When I rotated my body, the needle swung lazy, lagging, like it was swimming through something thicker than air. I'd felt that lag once before, years ago, on a ridge in the Talkeetnas with a client whose pack frame was steel. Localized interference. Except none of us were wearing steel, and the interference wasn't coming from us.

Desh checked his. Brandt and Okafor, drifting back from the perimeter, checked theirs at my gesture. Five compasses. Five wrong needles, each deviating a little differently, all of them sluggish, all of them lying.

"Magnetite?" Brandt offered. "Iron-rich intrusion in the spur. Happens."

"Happens," I agreed. "In one spot. This is the whole basin. And magnetite doesn't make a needle *lazy*, it makes it grab. This is something else."

Okafor said nothing, but his eyes went up-valley, toward the glacier and the high country beyond it, and stayed there a beat too long. I filed that.

Petra was still crouched, turning her compass slowly, watching the needle drag. "My instructor used to say a compass never lies," she murmured. "It just answers whatever question the ground asks it." She looked up at me. "So what question is this ground answering?"

I didn't have an answer. What I had was a full inventory of wrongness stacking up in my head like snow loading a slope: a camp too perfect, tracks that existed only where they'd been protected, radios left behind charged and functional, rations cached for a return nobody seemed to have made, and now five compasses agreeing on nothing except that the truth of this basin pointed somewhere other than where we were standing.

Six people had walked out of this camp, or been carried, or had never really been here at all. And whatever had happened to them, the mountain had tidied up afterward — or someone had.

"Pack it up," I said finally. "We make the lower moraine before dark. Tomorrow we climb the eastern spur and grid the drainage." I paused. "Nobody touches the camp again. Photograph everything. If the consortium wants their instruments recovered, I want a record of exactly what condition we found them in."

Petra shot me a look — quick, sharp, the first question finally escaping her jaw. "You think we're going to need to prove something."

"I think," I said, kneeling to cinch my pack, not looking at her, "that a camp this tidy isn't a camp. It's a message. And messages are written by somebody, for somebody." I stood. "Our job is figuring out who was supposed to read it."

We moved out single file along the bench, the river roaring below us, and behind us the tents stood in the failing light, pitched and perfect and patient, waiting for owners who had walked into the white corridor above and left no tracks leading anywhere at all.

It was Petra, twenty minutes later, as the light went copper on the spur, who said it quietly, almost to herself, almost to me:

"The compasses weren't broken, August. They were pointing at something."

I didn't answer. But all night, in the thin yellow circle of the stove light, I found myself looking north-northeast — the direction every needle had strained toward, however lazily, however wrongly — and wondering what was up there in the high country that could bend iron, erase footprints, and keep a camp warm for ghosts.
