## 3. The Silence Clause

The helicopter came in at 6:40 in the morning, which told me everything I needed to know about Merrick Voss before I ever met him. Men who arrive at dawn want you to know they've been awake longer than you have. It's a power move dressed as efficiency, like a handshake that squeezes a half-second too long.

I was on my third cup of camp coffee — the kind that tastes like it was brewed through an old boot and is proud of it — when the rotors flattened the spruce tops downvalley. The sound rolled up the drainage the way weather does, and every head in camp turned toward it. Six days we'd been at the abandoned basecamp, cataloguing gear without ghosts, and in six days nothing had come up that valley except cloud and one very indignant marmot. Now this.

Petra Ilic appeared at my shoulder with the particular silence she'd perfected, the one that meant she had something to say but wanted me to ask first. She's twenty-six and already understands that the most valuable thing a junior guide can offer a senior one is the illusion of having figured things out alone.

"Company," she said.

"Observant."

"Early."

"Also observant." I set down the cup. "Coffee's yours if I'm not back in an hour."

She looked at me the way people look at a dog they suspect knows something about the missing mail. Then she took the cup.

* * *

The helicopter set down in the meadow below camp with more precision than the terrain deserved, kicking up a spiral of grit and glacier dust that hung in the cold air like a curtain between us and whatever was stepping out of it. One man. Charcoal jacket, no logo, which in my experience means expensive. He ducked under the rotor wash with the ease of someone who'd done it a hundred times and never once thought about what the blades could do to a skull.

"Merrick Voss," he said, extending a hand before he'd finished closing the distance. His grip was dry and calibrated. "Halvard Consortium. You're Yarrow."

"Last time I checked."

"I'll be quick. I know you're mid-operation." He glanced past me toward the tents, the cache, the neat rows of survey equipment we'd photographed and tagged. "Impressive setup. Very thorough."

"We're thorough because the situation is strange. Tents pitched, food cached, radios charged, and not one footprint leaving camp. Either your survey team grew wings or somebody wants us to think they did."

"Or," Voss said mildly, "they moved out over rock."

"Over six kilometers of talus without scuffing a single lichen? I've seen goats leave more evidence."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not annoyance exactly. Recalculation. He filed my tone away somewhere and adjusted his approach by a degree, the way a good negotiator does, the way a good liar does, and from where I stood those were often the same training.

"Can we talk privately?"

"You can talk wherever you like. My crew hears everything anyway. Sound carries up here."

A small smile. "So I've read. Your file is quite something, Yarrow. Kestrel Traverse, two years ago. You got nine clients off that mountain in conditions that killed two guides with better reputations than yours."

"And still ended up unemployable. There's a lesson in there about how the world works, but I haven't found it yet."

"The lesson," Voss said, "is that outcomes matter less than narratives. Which is why I'm here." He nodded toward the helicopter. "Walk with me."

We walked. The meadow was stiff with morning frost, each grass blade sheathed in its own little glass sleeve, crunching underfoot like we were wading through broken crockery. Behind us the Serrano Range stood up against the sky, white and indifferent, holding whatever it held.

Voss talked while we walked, which I later understood was deliberate. People agree to things more easily when their bodies are moving forward. Some part of the brain mistakes momentum for consent.

"The survey team was contracted through a government mineral assessment office," he said. "Their instruments are proprietary-calibrated. Expensive. Sensitive. The Consortium has a significant financial interest in recovering them intact."

"Instruments," I said. "Not people?"

"People first, obviously." He said it smoothly enough that a less suspicious man might have missed the half-beat delay. I didn't miss it. I'd spent fifteen years reading clients' hesitations for a living; hesitation is where the truth keeps its coat check ticket. "But the instruments matter. Core samples, spectral logs, assay data. If any of that is compromised — exposed, leaked, damaged — there are legal consequences. For everyone involved."

"Involved in what?"

He stopped walking. We were maybe two hundred meters from the helicopter now, far enough that the pilot was just a silhouette behind glass, close enough that I could see the fine stitching on Voss's collar. He reached into his jacket and produced a tablet, then a stylus, then — with the small ceremony of a man who has done this many times and enjoys the ritual — a document.

"The original contract you signed covered search and recovery," he said. "It's being amended. Standard rider. The Consortium requires confidentiality regarding anything recovered. Field notes, data files, sample materials. All of it becomes Consortium property upon recovery, and all personnel are bound to non-disclosure. Indefinitely."

I looked at the screen. Legalese stacked in dense gray paragraphs, the kind of text designed to be scrolled past rather than read. Clause seven was highlighted in soft blue, as if the highlighting itself were a courtesy.

"'Personnel shall not disclose, publish, or otherwise communicate the contents, condition, or existence of recovered survey materials,'" I read aloud, "'to any party, including government entities, without express written authorization.'" I looked up. "Including government entities. Your survey team worked for a government entity."

"The survey team is missing, Mr. Yarrow. Their employer's interest in this operation is currently theoretical."

"That's a hell of a sentence."

"It's an accurate one." He held out the stylus. "Sign, and the amended fee clears within the hour. Triple the original rate. Enough, I'm told, to settle your outstanding obligations in full."

There it was. He hadn't needed to say it — the number was in the file, he'd admitted as much — but saying it out loud made it real, made it a hand around the back of my neck, gentle as a shepherd. My debt. Forty-one months of compounding interest on equipment I'd financed during the last year anyone would stake money on me, plus the settlement I'd paid a client's family out of shame rather than obligation, plus the ordinary humiliating arithmetic of a man whose income had dropped ninety percent in two years. A number I knew better than my own blood pressure.

Triple the rate. Debt gone. Name still blacklisted, sure, but a man with no debt can survive a bad reputation. A man with debt gets eaten by it slowly, in monthly installments.

And all it cost was a signature promising I wouldn't tell anyone what I found.

Here's the thing I want to be honest about, since honesty became a theme of this whole disaster later: I read clause seven twice. I read the indemnification section once. I noticed that "recovered survey materials" was defined broadly enough to include a burned scrap of paper, and I noticed that the penalty for breach was framed in terms I couldn't parse without a lawyer, which meant it was large. I noticed all of it, and I understood what it meant, and I signed anyway.

The stylus scratched against the glass. Small sound. Enormous sound. Somewhere in the back of my skull a voice that sounded uncomfortably like my old mentor, Ruth Calloway, said, *You always could smell a bad route, August. The question was never whether you could see the danger. It was whether you'd turn around.*

I handed the tablet back.

"Pleasure doing business," Voss said.

"Don't flatter yourself. I'd have signed for half."

"No, you wouldn't have. That's why I offered triple." He tucked the tablet away and studied me for a moment with something almost like respect, the way a card player studies an opponent who folded exactly on schedule. "One more thing. Operational guidance. When you locate Dr. Vasquez's team — and I'm confident you will — recover the sealed sample case first. It's a hard-shell case, roughly sixty centimeters, Halvard markings on the lid. Priority above the injured, if it comes to triage."

"Above people."

"Above *priorities*, Mr. Yarrow. Let's not be dramatic. The case weighs eleven kilos. It will slow no one." He turned toward the helicopter, then paused, and for just a moment the polished liaison surface thinned and I saw something underneath it — not conscience, I don't think Voss has one, but urgency. Real urgency, the kind that leaks. "Eleven days. That's when the concession permits expire. Whatever happens on this mountain needs to happen before then."

"What happens after eleven days?"

"After eleven days," Voss said, climbing into the cabin, "none of this matters to anyone who matters." The door slid shut. The rotors spun up, flattening the frost-flat grass in expanding rings, and the helicopter lifted off into the clean morning light and shrank to a speck heading south toward the world of boardrooms and quarterly filings.

I stood in the meadow with the rotor wash settling around me like snow that wasn't snow, and I tried to feel like a pragmatist. That's the trick I'd been running on myself for two years — dress the compromise in sensible clothes, call it realism, call it paying dues, call it what any grown man in a broken industry would do. Pragmatism is self-contempt wearing a good jacket. I knew that even then. I signed anyway.

* * *

Back in camp, Petra had the coffee waiting and Desh Okonkwo had the questions. Desh is fifty-three, built like the terrain he's spent his life on, and he has the veteran's gift of looking unhurried even when he's worried, which is how I knew he was worried.

"Liaison," he said. Not a question.

"Consortium lawyer-adjacent. New rider on the contract. Confidentiality clause."

Desh chewed on that along with his breakfast oats. "Confidential about what? We haven't found anything except gear that behaves like it's been abandoned by ghosts."

"Their instruments. Sample case. Anything we recover belongs to them, and we don't discuss it. With anyone. Including the government office that employed the survey team."

Petra looked up sharply. "That doesn't make sense."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't."

"It makes perfect sense," Desh said grimly, "if the instruments contain something the company doesn't want the government to see. Question is what." He scraped his cup clean. "You signed it."

It wasn't a question either, but it landed like one. I met his eyes. Desh has that quality — he doesn't judge you out loud, he just holds the space open for you to judge yourself, which is worse.

"I signed it," I said. "Debt's cleared. All of it."

"Congratulations," he said, without a gram of congratulation in it.

"Desh—"

"I've got no room to talk, August. I've carried loads I shouldn't have for paychecks I needed. Everybody on this mountain has." He stood, shouldered his pack, and looked up at the white corridor of the drainage where it narrowed into the high country. "Just do me one favor. When we find whatever it is they don't want discussed — take ten seconds before you decide what kind of secret it is. Ten seconds is free. Everything else costs."

He walked off toward the cache to run the morning inventory. Petra lingered, turning her compass over in her fingers — the altered one, the one that pointed wrong, the anomaly she'd flagged on day two that I'd logged and quietly failed to think about hard enough.

"August," she said. "The compasses."

"What about them?"

"They don't just point wrong. They point wrong *consistently*. Same deviation, same bearing, every unit we've tested. That's not damage. Damage is random." She held it up. Magnetic needle steady on a bearing that had nothing to do with north. "Somebody calibrated these to pull east-northeast. Deliberately. Before the survey ever started."

I took the compass from her. The needle sat there, obedient to some instruction I hadn't given it, pointing toward the deep interior of the range — toward country our route maps showed as blank, unmapped, unvisited.

Toward whatever the survey team had really been sent to find. Or sent to avoid. Or sent to die avoiding.

I thought about clause seven, and the stylus scratching glass, and eleven days, and forty-one months of debt dissolving like frost in sun. I thought about Ruth Calloway's voice in the back of my skull, and how the correct answer to a bad route is always the same, and how rarely anyone takes it.

"Pack for four days above treeline," I said. "Weather window closes tomorrow night. We move up the drainage at first light."

Petra pocketed her compass and went to break camp, and I stood a moment longer watching that needle hold its wrong bearing, steady as a vow, pointing at the thing I'd already sold my silence to.
