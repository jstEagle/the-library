## 4. Into the White Corridor

The Serrano drainage starts as a polite little valley and ends as a corridor of granite with opinions. By the second morning it had stopped being a place and started being a throat — walls rising on either side, the river running gray-green and fast down the middle of it, the sky narrowed to a strip of dirty wool above. We walked up that throat in single file, six of us, carrying enough rope and hardware to outfit a small war.

I'd done this stretch eleven times before, back when my name still opened doors. The drainage hadn't changed. I had. There's a particular feeling you get walking terrain you know well with people who are deciding whether to trust you — every route-finding call becomes a referendum, every pause at a fork a small election. I tried not to campaign too hard.

The party was me; Petra Ilic, who carried her pack like she was apologizing to it; Desh Okonkwo, who did not apologize to anything; two Consortium-hired porters named Brandt and Cole, whose job description seemed to be "present"; and Dr. Amara Chen, the glaciologist Voss had insisted on adding at the last minute, ostensibly to "assess ice conditions." She'd spent the first day mostly silent, taking photographs of rock faces instead of glaciers, which told me something about how much assessing she planned to do.

The weather was the real subject of conversation, when there was any. It had been deteriorating since dawn — not dramatically, nothing you could point at, just the light going flatter and the wind picking up a mineral smell off the icefields above us. Old guides call it "the white corridor" for a reason. When the storm comes down this valley, it doesn't arrive so much as condense around you until the world is the size of your hood.

"River's up," Desh said, an hour into the morning.

He said it the way other men say good morning. We stood at the confluence where the main channel took the meltwater from the eastern cirque, and he was right: yesterday it had run shin-deep over the gravel bar; today it was thigh-deep and moving with that muscular, businesslike speed that means it has somewhere important to be and doesn't care what's standing in the way. Somewhere above us, a warm rain on the icefields was doing its work.

"We cross here or we don't cross," I said. "The alternative is the east bank trail, which adds nine hours and puts us under the serac zone by dark."

"And the survey team crossed here?" Desh asked.

"Their track log says they did."

Desh looked at the water, then at me, and I got the sense he was filing something away. He was fifty-one, built like a doorframe, twenty-six years of climbing on four continents, and he had the specific calm of a man who has already survived everything the mountain can think of and is now mainly curious. Voss's dossier called him "reliable." In my experience, men described as reliable are usually described that way by people who've never needed them to be anything else.

"Petra," I said. "You're first light."

She blinked. "Me?"

"You're lightest and you're fresh. I'll throw you the line, you cross to the far gravel bar, we set a tension traverse, everyone else crosses clipped in. Standard."

"It looks fast," she said. Not complaining. Measuring.

"It is fast. That's why you're on a rope." I coiled the throw line. "Knees bent, feet wide, face upstream. If it pushes you, let it push you downstream at an angle — never fight straight across. And if it takes you anyway, float on your back, feet first, and I'll swing you in."

She nodded, and to her credit she didn't do the thing most people do, which is narrate their own fear out loud until it fills the whole river. She just watched the water for a long moment, reading it, the way you'd read a page you were going to be tested on.

I threw the line. It landed short of the bar, dragged, caught on a boulder. Second throw was clean. Petra unclipped from the group rope, clipped into the throw line with a carabiner at her waist — old school, but right for this — and stepped in.

The river hit her at mid-thigh and shoved. I saw her weight shift wrong for half a second, the instinct to lean against the current, and then she corrected — turned slightly, gave the water her hip instead of her chest, and let it slide past her while she crabbed sideways foot by foot. Halfway across, the depth found the seam where two channels braided together and jumped to her hips, and the whole line came taut, humming, and for three full seconds she was less a person crossing a river than a kite on a string.

Then her boot found the bar and she hauled herself out, gasping, soaked to the sternum, and turned around and gave us a thumbs-up with a hand that was shaking like a tuning fork.

"Nice correction at the seam," I called across.

"I hated it," she called back.

"That's how you know you did it right."

We set the tension traverse and ferried the others across. Brandt went through the deep seam and came up coughing and furious at the water personally. Chen crossed dry-ish, having apparently negotiated some private arrangement with physics. Desh crossed last, unclipped halfway through because, he said, the rope was slowing him down, which was either true or a message, and I honestly couldn't tell which, and I was beginning to suspect Desh preferred it that way.

On the far bank, wringing out socks, Petra sat down next to me. Up close her hands were still trembling and her lips had gone the color of the river.

"Thank you for not making it a big deal," she said.

"Crossing rivers is not a big deal. People make it a big deal and then they die."

"No, I mean—" She gestured vaguely at the group, at the day, at the whole arrangement. "Voss told me I was hired because I was cheap. He said it like it was a joke, except it wasn't. So thank you for treating me like a guide instead of ballast."

I didn't have a good answer for that, so I gave her the true one. "You read the water before you stepped in. Most people look at the river. You read it. That's not cheap. That's rare."

She looked at me like I'd handed her something fragile, and I understood suddenly that nobody had told her anything like that in a long time, maybe ever, and I felt the familiar old ache of knowing exactly what that was like, from the inside, from these last two years of doors closing.

"Drink something," I said, because tenderness makes me itchy. "You're no use to me hypothermic."

* * *

By early afternoon the light had gone to pewter and the wind had found its voice — a low, steady moan coming down-valley behind us, which meant the weather was coming from the icecap, which meant it would keep coming. I called a halt under an overhang and studied the track log on the battered tablet Voss had given us, the one containing the survey team's GPS breadcrumbs.

That was when Desh crouched next to me and asked the question.

"This route," he said. "Walk me through it."

"You've seen the log."

"I've seen the log. That's what bothers me." He tapped the screen, tracing the breadcrumb trail with one thick finger. The line wandered up the east side of the drainage, cut west across the river — our crossing — climbed the talus slopes below Mount Serrano proper, then doubled back southeast along a bench above the icefall, then north again into the high cirque. "Look at it. It's a drunkard's walk. Why would a geological survey route go up the granitic wall and then traverse back across the metasedimentary belt? If you're mapping ore bodies, you sample the contact zones. The contact zones are all on the west flank. This route touches them once, briefly, like it's avoiding them."

I looked at the trail. He was right, and I had noticed it yesterday, and I had decided not to say anything, because saying things like that out loud tends to open doors you can't close. But Desh wasn't a man you could leave things unsaid near. He collected them.

"Maybe they were following a drainage for access," I said.

"For two days? With a geologist leading?" He shook his head slowly. "August, I've supported surveys in the Wrangells, the Coast Range, the Atacama. Survey teams move like they're reading a book. They stop constantly. They zigzag toward whatever they're sampling. This team moved like they were trying to get somewhere specific without looking at anything on the way."

"They were trying to get somewhere specific," I said. "They vanished. Someone sent us to find them. Maybe the route doesn't have to make sense if the point was the destination."

"Which was?"

I zoomed the map out. The breadcrumbs terminated in the high cirque below the eastern ridge — the same cirque, more or less, where the search grid Voss had drawn for us conveniently began. "There," I said. "And before you ask why a survey team would camp in a cirque with no exposed geology worth a damn, I'll save you the trouble. I don't know."

Desh grunted. It was a thoughtful grunt, a grunt with a future.

"Your liaison," he said. "Voss. He drew the search grid himself?"

"He provided it."

"Before or after the team disappeared?"

"After. Obviously."

"Mm." Desh stood, knees cracking like green wood. "You know what I've learned in twenty-six years? When a rich man tells you exactly where to look, the interesting thing is usually what he's telling you not to see." He clapped me on the shoulder, nearly putting me into the scree. "But I'm retired from suspicion. Mostly. Ask me again at altitude."

He wandered off to check Brandt's pack straps, and I sat there with the tablet glowing in the flat light, feeling the shape of something I couldn't name yet. The silence clause sat in my contract like a stone in a boot. Find the team. Recover the instruments. Tell no one what the instruments contain. I'd signed it because the money cleared my debt, and I'd told myself that was pragmatism, and I was getting very practiced at telling myself things.

Chen appeared at my elbow. She'd been photographing the east wall again, I realized — the granitic wall, the one the survey route hugged for two days without sampling.

"Beautiful batholith," she said, showing me a shot. Then, quieter, without changing her tone at all: "You should know that whatever's in those instruments, Mr. Yarrow, I'm paid to observe, not to forget. There's a difference between those jobs. Some people in this valley haven't figured that out yet."

And she walked away, leaving me holding a photograph of blank granite that somehow felt like a warning label.

* * *

We made the bench camp by four, tucked into a hollow below the terminal moraine, and I put the tents up with the wind quartering behind us because tomorrow it would be in our teeth. The storm was close now. You could taste it — that metallic edge in the air, the pressure sitting on your eardrums like a thumb. Barometer had dropped fourteen millibars since noon. In the Serranos, that's not weather. That's a countdown.

We ate in the big tent, six people and the smell of wet wool and freeze-dried chili, and for a while nobody talked, and it was the good kind of nobody-talking, the kind that happens when a group stops being a list of names on a contract and starts being a unit. Brandt told a story about a mule in Peru with strong political opinions. Even Chen laughed. Petra laughed hardest, that slightly-too-loud laugh of someone who hasn't been included in a room like this often enough to take it for granted, and I caught Desh watching her with an expression I recognized, because I suspected I was wearing the same one.

It's a strange alchemy, a rope team. Twelve hours ago these were strangers with a payroll relationship. Now Brandt was saving Petra the last of the cheese, and Desh was re-pitching her guyline without being asked, and when the wind gusted and flattened the tent wall against our backs, everybody reached for the pole at the same time without discussing it. Camaraderie isn't a feeling. It's a habit that forms faster than you expect and breaks slower than you'd hope. I'd lost mine two years ago, along with everything else, and I could feel it knitting back over me like new skin, and I didn't trust it, and I wanted it anyway.

Later, in the dark, Petra crawled over to my spot by the door.

"Can't sleep," she said. "The wind sounds like the river."

"It does that. It's the valley. Same shape, different medium."

"August." She hesitated. "The compasses. Did you check them tonight?"

I had, actually, out of a habit I couldn't explain. All four of ours agreed with each other and disagreed with the sun by roughly seven degrees west. I'd assumed local deviation — the Serrano batholith is iron-rich in places, throws magnetic readings all over the drainage.

"They're consistent," I said. "Off by seven degrees, all of them, all night. It's the rock."

"Ours agree with each other," she said. "But the survey team's compasses didn't. Remember at basecamp? I checked the ones left in the gear cache. Every one of theirs was off by a different amount. Seven degrees, twelve degrees, fifteen. Like somebody adjusted each one individually."

I lay there in the dark listening to the wind come down the corridor, and I thought about a team of six people walking two days through impossible terrain, navigating on instruments that had each been tampered with separately, and what kind of journey you could only make if nobody on it knew quite where they were.

"Why would someone do that?" Petra whispered.

"Keep them lost," I said. "Or keep them foundable. Depends who's turning the screws."

She was quiet a moment. "Which do you think it was?"

Above us, the wind rose to a register I didn't like, and somewhere high on the icefield something boomed — an avalanche, distant, the mountain clearing its throat.

"I think," I said, "that tomorrow we're going to find out."

Nobody slept much after that. And at 0300, when I crawled out to check the anchors, I found the breadcrumb trail on the tablet doing something it hadn't done before: the last recorded point of the survey team, the one in the high cirque, had acquired company. A second marker, faint, intermittent, blinking in and out like a heartbeat.

A radio. Still transmitting. Still alive.

From somewhere ahead of us, in the white.
