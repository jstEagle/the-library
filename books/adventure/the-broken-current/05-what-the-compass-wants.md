## 5. What the Compass Wants

My grandmother used to say that a compass doesn't point north. It points home. She said it the way she said most things — while doing something else, hands busy, eyes elsewhere — and I spent thirty years of my life not thinking about it until the morning I stood at the edge of the Serrano drainage and watched my compass needle drift eleven degrees off everything the sun told me.

Eleven degrees doesn't sound like much. Eleven degrees is the difference between a safe line down a couloir and a slide for the ages. Eleven degrees is the difference between the trailhead and the morgue, if you're tired and it's snowing and you trust the little needle more than your own eyes. Every guide learns this. Most of us learn it secondhand, from a story that ends badly for someone with a name and a family.

I'd been carrying the anomaly since basecamp, folded up small in the back of my head like a receipt I didn't want to total. All six compasses in the survey team's abandoned kit pointed wrong — not broken-wrong, not stuck-needle-wrong, but *consistent*-wrong, all of them off by the same amount, as if someone had paid a visit to the earth's magnetic personality and negotiated new terms. Broken compasses disagree with each other. These agreed. That was the part that kept me up in my bag at night, listening to the wind work the tent fly like a man trying a lock.

And mine — the one I'd pulled from the survey team's spares box on day one, half as a souvenir, half because my own had a hairline bubble in the capsule — mine pointed wrong too, and it pointed wrong *toward something*.

I hadn't told August yet. I'm not proud of the delay. There's a version of me that wants to claim I was being methodical, gathering data, applying the scientific method. That version is a liar. The truth is I liked being the one who knew something. I was the junior guide on a crew of legends, the person whose job description was essentially *carry things and don't die*, and for once I had a thread that nobody else had even seen. It was petty and it was human and I fed it for two days before it started feeding on me.

* * *

Day four up the drainage. The weather had that held-breath quality that experienced mountain people learn to distrust more than outright violence — a high gray lid, air too still, the kind of sky that's not threatening you, just thinking about it. We were camped at 3,400 meters on a bench above the river, and the survey route — the one plotted on the maps the Consortium had given us — bent west into a wall of broken terrain that made no sense for anyone, let alone a government geology team carrying core samples.

Desh had said as much at breakfast, around a mouthful of oats. "Nobody samples a cliff face. You sample where the rock tells you to, and rock tells you in drainages, not on vertical ground. This route's a fiction." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, a big man with a big man's economy of motion and a habit of treating catastrophe as a scheduling problem. But his eyes had gone to August when he said *fiction*, and August had gone very still, the way people do when a thought they've been avoiding knocks politely at the door.

August didn't say anything. August had been doing a lot of not-saying since the helicopter came and went with Voss and his silence clause. I watched them recheck the route map for the fourth time that morning and decided it was time.

"So," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, which is one of the two or three useful things my mother gave me. "The compasses."

August looked up. "What about them?"

I laid mine on the cook pot lid between us, next to the map. "Watch." I rotated the map, aligned the compass housing to the map's north, and let the needle settle. It settled eleven degrees east of where it should have. Then I picked up the compass and turned ninety degrees, and the needle swung with it, obedient, and stopped eleven degrees east of *that*. "It's not broken. It's biased. All of the survey team's are, by the same amount. I checked them against the sun lines three mornings running."

Desh leaned in. Okonkwo, the veteran, the man whose name on a route description made climbers go quiet. He picked up the compass like it was a wounded bird. "Consistent deviation," he murmured. "That's not damage. That's *calibration*."

"Compasses can be biased?" I asked, and immediately hated the question, because Desh was already nodding with the weary patience of a man who'd forgotten what it was like not to know things.

"You can remagnetize a needle. Shift it. It's a two-minute job with a strong magnet and a steady hand." He turned the compass over, checked the baseplate, the bezel. "Somebody on that survey team did this deliberately. Question is why."

"To get lost?" I offered.

"No." August had gone quiet in the way I was learning meant the machinery behind their face was running hot. "To get *found*. Or to make sure anyone following them went somewhere specific." August's finger came down on the map, on the drainage west of our position, the one the official route ignored. "Eleven degrees east. If you navigate by these compasses from the basecamp, trusting them over the terrain, where do you end up?"

Desh did the arithmetic with his finger, tracing a line. The line bent away from the official route, away from the cliff faces, down and across the river and up into a side drainage that the map rendered as blank white — unglaciated, low angle, unremarkable. The kind of place nobody goes because there's nothing there to want.

"There," Desh said.

* * *

August made the call the way August made every call — fast, flat, and with a half-second delay that I eventually understood was them arguing with themselves and losing. "We take a look. Half day, light packs, back before the weather decides anything." A pause. "Petra, you're on point. Your compass, your deviation. I want you calling the line."

Which is how I ended up walking into blank white country with five people behind me and a needle in my hand pointing at something nobody had authorized.

I want to be honest about what that felt like, because the honest version is embarrassing. It felt *good*. It felt like being handed the keys to a vehicle I'd only ever ridden in. For two years I'd been the junior guide, the one who flaked ropes and melted snow and got told to double-check things I'd already triple-checked, and now here was August Yarrow — *the* August Yarrow, the name that got said in guide houses in the tone people reserve for storms and exes — walking in my footsteps and navigating off my needle. The thrill of it went through me like the first sip of something too strong. I remember thinking, absurdly, *my grandmother would have liked this*, and then thinking, less absurdly, that my grandmother had also said pride comes before a fall, and she'd had a saying for literally every human failing, which in retrospect should have told me something about her.

The side drainage narrowed. The river gave way to scree, the scree to a bench of old moraine, and the country got quieter in that specific mountain way where the quiet isn't absence of sound but presence of attention. Twice I stopped and made Desh confirm my sun line against the needle. Twice the needle insisted on its eleven degrees like a man refusing to correct his testimony.

"There." Desh said it first, but I'd seen it half a second before — a shape on the moraine that wasn't a shape the glacier would have left. Angular. Stacked. Deliberate.

A cairn. But not a route-marking cairn. Route cairns are small, hasty, built by tired people for tired people. This one was waist-high and *built* — stones fitted, not piled, the work of someone with time and intention. And tied to the top stone, snapping in the wind like a small flag of surrender or defiance, was a strip of survey tape. Orange. Faded to the color of something that had been shouting for a long time and was now just hoarse.

Nobody said anything. We didn't need to. Five people walked up that moraine in the particular silence of people who have all privately decided the same thing and don't want to jinx it by agreeing out loud.

August reached the cairn first. I watched their hands — steady, I'll give them that, steadier than mine would have been — work the top stones loose. Under the caprock, in a cavity lined with a folded groundsheet, was a bundle.

It was the size of a loaf of bread. Wrapped in the groundsheet, tied with cord, and — this is the detail that got me, that still gets me — *sealed with survey tape*. The same orange. Whoever left it had used the tools of their trade to wrap it up like evidence, because that's what it was. Evidence is just a word for the truth when the truth is in trouble.

August looked at me. "You found the line. You do it."

I'd like to report that my hands were steady. They weren't. I untied the cord with fingers that felt like they belonged to someone standing slightly to the left of my body, and I unwrapped the groundsheet, and inside was a field journal.

Half of it was burned.

Not singed, not water-damaged — *burned*, the front third of the notebook charred to a black lace that crumbled when I breathed on it, the remaining pages smoke-stained and warped, as if someone had tried to destroy it in a hurry and then, for reasons I would spend the rest of this expedition understanding, stopped. Or been stopped. Or run out of time, or nerve, or lighter fluid. The cover was gone. What remained started at page thirty-something, and the first legible line was written in a hand so cramped and slanted it looked like it was trying to escape the page.

I read it out loud. I don't know why out loud. I think because if it was only in my head it could still be a mistake.

*"Core samples 14 through 22 do not match the assay reports filed with the concession application. I have re-run the density and fluorescence checks three times. The ore body as described in the Halvard submission does not exist. What exists is low-grade disseminated sulfide, commercially worthless. Someone has substituted the sample series. The substitution is not sloppy. It is professional."*

The wind came up the drainage and worried the tape on the cairn and I stood there holding a dead man's — or a living man's, we didn't know yet — handwriting, and the thrill I'd been drinking for four days turned in my stomach to something with a much higher proof.

Desh took the journal gently from my hands and read the next pages. His lips moved. August didn't take it at all. August stood looking west, up the drainage, at nothing, at everything, at the blank space on the map where a compass had been paid to point.

"There's more," Desh said. His voice had changed — flattened, the way it did when he was handling something heavy. "She names the sample numbers. She names the lab. She names—" he stopped.

"Say it," August said.

"She names the Consortium liaison. Voss. Says Voss was on site in week two, 'overseeing quality control,' which she notes is not a phrase government surveys use." Desh looked up. "August. This isn't a lost team. This is a team that found out what the mountain is actually worth, and someone wanted them gone before they could say so."

* * *

Here is the thing about dread that nobody warns you: it doesn't feel like fear. Fear is fast and clean, an animal thing, adrenaline and heart rate and *run*. Dread is slow. Dread is the thrill of discovery with the serial numbers filed off, dread is the exact same chemical cocktail — the widened eyes, the quickened pulse, the sense of *finally, something is happening* — wearing a different coat. My hands were shaking and my mouth was dry and some traitorous basement-level part of me was still whispering *you found it, you, Petra Ilic, junior guide, carry-things-and-don't-die, YOU found it* — and I hated that part of myself with a completeness that surprised me.

Because here's what else I understood, standing at that cairn with the burned journal in the wind: whatever had happened to the survey team had happened *because of this book*. Someone had tried to burn it. Someone had failed, or relented, or run out of matches, and then had carried the half-ruined thing up a blank drainage and buried it under stones with a tape flag, which is not what you do with evidence you want destroyed. That's what you do with evidence you want *found* — but only by the right people, only by people who can read eleven degrees of magnetic deviation like a signature.

The survey team hadn't gotten lost. They'd left a trail. And the fact that they'd needed a trail — the fact that they couldn't just walk out and tell someone — told me everything the journal's missing pages didn't.

"Pack it," August said finally. Their voice was level in the way that costs something. "Waterproof bag. It doesn't leave your pack, Petra. Nobody's radio hears about this. Not yet."

"Not yet," Desh repeated. Not a question. A weight placed on a scale.

August met his eyes, and then mine, and what was in their face wasn't the calm of a guide making a route call. It was the calm of a person standing at a fork and knowing that both paths went through the same fire. "Not yet," August said again, and turned back down the moraine, toward camp, toward the radio we were all now thinking about differently.

I wrapped the journal in its groundsheet, tucked it against the dry bag in the center of my pack, where it sat like a coal that hadn't finished burning. On the way down I glanced back once at the cairn, and the orange tape was snapping in the wind, hoarse and persistent, saying the thing it had been saying to nobody for who knows how long.

That night the weather finally stopped thinking about it and started. And just as we got the tents guyed out against the first real gusts, the radio crackled with a voice I was beginning to associate with the taste of copper — Merrick Voss, cheerful as a salesman, asking how the search was going.

I watched August pick up the handset. I watched the half-second delay. And I held my pack against my knees in the dark like something that could still be saved.

"August," Voss said, warm as a heating vent. "You're not going to believe the timing. I've got news about the permits."
