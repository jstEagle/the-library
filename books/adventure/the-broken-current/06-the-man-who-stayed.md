## 6. The Man Who Stayed

The compass lied to us for six hours before it told the truth.

Petra's altered needle had dragged us off the survey route and up a shelf of wind-scoured granite that no sane geologist would have chosen, which was exactly how I knew we were in the right place. Sane people don't hide cairns under overhangs. Desh grumbled about the detour the whole way — "We're following a broken toy up a dead-end drainage, Yarrow, this is how expeditions end" — but he climbed anyway, because that's what veterans do: they complain with their feet still moving.

The cairn itself was nothing. A meter of stacked stone, half collapsed, marked with a tail of survey tape gone gray and brittle. But behind it, cut into the lee of an outcrop, was a snow cave.

I want to say I knew immediately someone was alive inside. I didn't. What I knew was that the entrance had been maintained — the walls carved smooth, the drift packed and re-packed, a flat slab set as a door. Abandoned camps look abandoned. Lived-in caves look *defended*. There's a difference you learn fast when you've spent enough winters reading other people's survival decisions, most of them bad ones.

"Desh," I said quietly. "Left side. Petra, stay on the tape."

He moved without question. I knocked on the slab with my ice tool, three slow raps, the way you'd knock on a stranger's door rather than kick it down.

Nothing. Then, from inside, a voice like gravel dragged over gravel:

"If you're Consortium, I'm not coming out."

* * *

Dr. Elena Vasquez weighed maybe forty kilos and looked like she'd been carved by the same wind that had been working the Serrano Range for ten thousand years. She sat propped against her pack at the back of the cave with a climbing axe across her lap — not for climbing, I noted. For swinging. Her left leg was splinted with a tent pole and survey flagging, a piece of improvisation so ugly it circled back around to elegant. Beside her, wrapped in a ground sheet and strapped to her wrist by a carabiner and a length of cord, sat a metal sample case the size of a small suitcase.

She hadn't let go of it once since we'd come in. Not to shake hands. Not to take the water Petra offered. Not even to pull her sleeping bag tighter when Desh got the stove going and the cave finally warmed above the temperature of regret.

"You're the search party," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm August Yarrow," I said. "Halvard contract."

Something moved behind her eyes at the word *Halvard*. It wasn't gratitude.

"Then you can report back that you found me alive, and you can leave now."

"We're eleven hours from basecamp and the weather window closes tonight," I said. "Nobody's leaving. Drink your water."

For a long moment I thought she'd refuse on principle. Then her hands — shaking, blue at the nails — took the bottle, and pride lost another round to biology. I turned away to give her the dignity of losing privately and studied the cave instead.

It told a story, the way all camps do, if you read them honestly. One occupant, not seven. One sleeping platform, one set of cook gear, one careful pile of ration wrappers — and here was the detail that put ice in my spine — the wrappers were counted. Stacked in bundles of five, squared off like someone keeping books. Rationing wasn't discipline at this level. Rationing at this level was arithmetic performed by someone who expected to be here a very long time.

Seven people's worth of food, one person eating it.

"Doktor Vasquez." Desh crouched near the stove, keeping his voice gentle the way he did around injured things and junior climbers. "Your team. The rest of the survey crew. Where are they?"

The axe shifted a centimeter on her lap.

"They're accounted for."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer you're getting."

Petra, at the cave mouth, watching the weather build in the west — she'd appointed herself our barometer somewhere around day two and hadn't been wrong yet — glanced back at me. I gave her the smallest shake of the head I could manage. Not yet. You don't interrogate a starving woman while she's drinking your water. You wait until she's warm, until her guard drops with her blood sugar, until the story comes out the way stories always come out of people who've been alone too long: sideways, in pieces, whether they mean to tell it or not.

So we waited. We melted snow. We checked her splint, which was infected at the edges and needed real attention, and she submitted to that with the grim cooperation of someone who understood that a dead guide helps no one. And through all of it, the case stayed strapped to her wrist like a bad marriage.

* * *

She started talking around dusk, when the light through the entrance went the color of old tin and there was nothing left to do but sit with each other.

Not about the team. About the mountain. She talked about the geology — the fold structures in the east ridge, the glacial erratics that shouldn't have been where they were, the way the Serrano drainage bent wrong, like a river remembering an older course. She talked the way lonely experts talk, pouring out months of stored observation at whoever would hold still, and I let her, because every word was also reconnaissance. Every detail she volunteered was a detail she'd decided was safe to give.

"The consortium sent you because of the permits," she said eventually. "Eleven days. That's why Voss is pushing."

"You know Merrick Voss?"

"I know his type. He came to basecamp twice before the season. Very charming. Very interested in our *timeline*." She said the word like it tasted of copper. "He never once asked about the data. Did you find that strange, Mr. Yarrow? A mining company funding a mineral survey, and the liaison never asks what the rocks say?"

I thought about the silence clause sitting in my pack, initialed twice, and said nothing, and watched her register the nothing, and saw her file it away with everything else.

"Where is your team?" I asked again, softer this time.

She looked at the stove. At the ration wrappers, counted and bundled. At her own hands.

"Ahead of us," she said. "Down the mountain."

"When?"

"Before the storm."

"Alone? Injured leader, storm inbound, and you sent six people down the east ridge *alone*?"

"I sent them down the east ridge because staying with me was going to kill them." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a hairline fracture in something that had been holding for weeks. "My leg was broken on the second week. Bad landing, scree slope, my fault, my mistake. Every day I slowed them down was a day less food, a day deeper into the window. So I made the call any of you would have made. I stayed. They went."

Desh and I exchanged a look over her head. It was a plausible story. It was even, mostly, a true-shaped story. But I'd spent twenty years listening to people explain their decisions on mountains, and the true ones are usually simpler than this, and the false ones usually arrive pre-defended.

"And the two who didn't make the rendezvous," Desh said quietly. "The two the Consortium says are unaccounted for."

The axe handle creaked under her grip.

"Accounted for," she repeated, "is a flexible word."

* * *

Petra took first watch, which meant nobody slept, because Petra has never in her life taken a quiet watch; she narrates the weather like a sportscaster. Around midnight she hissed down into the cave: "August. Lights. Down-valley. Moving."

I crawled up beside her. Two points of light in the black drainage below, too steady for headlamps on the move, too slow for a rescue party. Watching lights. Or being watched by them.

"Voss?" Desh murmured.

"Voss is in a helicopter in a hangar somewhere warm," I said. "Those are boots."

Behind us, in the dark of the cave, Dr. Elena Vasquez pulled the sample case against her chest and held it the way you hold the only thing left that's true.

"Mr. Yarrow," she said, and her voice had changed — the exhaustion burned off it, replaced by something harder. "You signed their paper. I can see it in how you ask questions. So I'm going to tell you one thing, and you can decide what kind of person you're going to be with it."

I turned around.

"There is no ore body," she said. "There never was. The core samples in this case are worthless rock from a hole the Consortium already knew was dry, and the assay reports your employers are so desperate to recover say otherwise, and eleven days from now, if those reports reach a filing office instead of a shredder, Halvard loses four hundred million dollars and several people go to prison. That's what I've been guarding. That's why I'm still on this mountain. Not because of my leg." She almost smiled. "Because of my signature. My name is on the original data. They need me dead or discredited, and either way, they need this case."

Outside, the lights in the valley blinked once, twice — deliberate, rhythmic. Signal, not lantern.

"And your team?" I asked. "The two unaccounted for?"

Her silence lasted long enough for the stove to tick as it cooled.

"Ask your employers," she said finally, "what happened to the two surveyors who carried copies of the data off this mountain before I did."

The lights below went dark, all at once, like a held breath released.

And I sat there in the cold with a silence clause in my pack and a dying scientist's truth in my ears, understanding — with the particular clarity that only arrives far too late to be useful — that I hadn't been hired to find a missing team.

I'd been hired to finish making them missing.
