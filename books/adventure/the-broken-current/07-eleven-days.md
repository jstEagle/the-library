## 7. Eleven Days

The radio woke me at what my watch claimed was 4:40 in the morning, which was a lie, because time inside a snow cave is a rumor you tell yourself to stay sane.

I had been dreaming about the core samples again — the way they'd looked laid out on the folding table at basecamp, gray and unremarkable as gravel from a parking lot, while Marcus Delgado's voice shook with something between grief and fury. *There's nothing here, Elena. There was never anything here.* In the dream the samples were glowing, which they had not done in life, and I understood even in the dream that this was my mind's cheap sentimentality, dressing up worthless rock so it would feel like it deserved what happened next.

Then the radio hissed, and I stopped dreaming.

It wasn't our frequency. It was the Consortium's — channel four, the one I'd told my team never to touch, back when touching it meant paperwork and now apparently meant worse. The voice that came through was smooth and unhurried and had the particular warmth of a man reading from a script he'd rehearsed in front of a mirror.

"Dr. Vasquez. This is Merrick Voss, Halvard Consortium liaison. I know you can hear me. Your rescue party has a radio, and radios are honest that way."

I lay still on my side, watching my breath drift toward the cave ceiling. August Yarrow was asleep six feet away, or pretending to be, curled against the wall with their pack for a pillow. I could hear the others breathing too — the young guide, Petra, whose compass had led us to my cairn like a bloodhound; the big climber, Okonkwo, who snored with the regularity of a metronome and therefore could not have been awake.

"I'll keep this brief," Voss said, "since I imagine your battery situation is dire. The concession permits expire in eleven days. Eleven days after that, every claim staked in the Serrano drainage reverts to open bidding, and everything your government team did up there becomes an academic curiosity. So here is the arrangement. You bring out the sample case intact. All of it. And the Consortium remembers its friends."

He paused, the way men do when they think silence is a form of pressure rather than a form of contempt.

"If the case doesn't come down," he continued, "the permits lapse, the claims dissolve, and the Consortium's position becomes... defensive. We would be obliged to protect our investment. Legally, of course. But thoroughly."

I should mention, for the record, that I am a geologist. I have spent twenty-two years listening to rock tell me the truth about itself, because rock is incapable of lying — it can only be misquoted. Men like Voss misquote rock for a living. And somewhere in the last three weeks, half-starved in a hole in a mountain, guarding a box of worthless stone like it was the ark of the covenant, I had stopped being afraid of men like him.

Which is not the same as being safe from them.

* * *

August was not asleep. I knew this because when I finally sat up, their eyes were already open, catching the faint gray of the entrance light, and they said nothing, which was its own kind of confession.

"You heard," I said.

"Hard not to." August sat up slowly, the way people move when their body has learned to ration movement. They were thinner than the man in the Consortium's file photo — I'd seen the file, back when I still thought knowing my rescuers' résumés mattered. Disgraced guide. Debt. A reputation with a hole burned through the middle of it. The kind of person the Consortium hires precisely because they can't afford principles. "Eleven days."

"He's bluffing about the permits. Partially." I pulled my sleeping bag around my shoulders like a shawl. My hands still didn't work right in the cold; the frostbite had taken some feeling from two fingers, and I resented them daily. "Permits don't just evaporate. There's renewal processes, appeals. But he doesn't need the permits to be real. He needs *you* to believe they're real, because you're the one carrying the case."

"And if I don't believe it?"

"Then he escalates. That's what 'thoroughly' means. He has contractors on this mountain, Yarrow. I've seen their tracks. Someone took Tomás's radio off his body before we found him."

August looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the wind worked at the snow wall across the cave mouth with the patience of a sculptor who had all eternity and no client.

"You've known since the beginning," August said. Not a question. "Since we found you. What's in the case."

"Yes."

"And you let us haul it up a ridge in a storm without telling us why anyone would want it badly enough to kill for it."

I had prepared answers for this. I had prepared them in the snow cave, alone, over eleven days of eating melted snow and half a energy bar per day, talking to myself the way solitary people do, building arguments like cairns. Every one of those arguments collapsed now, in front of this tired, broke, compromised guide, because none of them survived contact with the simple fact that two of my people were dead and I had known why.

So I told the truth instead. It felt foreign in my mouth, like a language I'd studied but never spoken.

"The Serrano concession is a fraud," I said. "Not a geological disappointment — a fraud. The Consortium's exploration subsidiary submitted core assays eighteen months ago showing a copper-molybdenum porphyry body of commercial grade. Those numbers drove the stock from four dollars to thirty-one. My survey was commissioned by the Ministry to independently verify the deposit before extraction permitting. Standard due diligence. Except the Ministry official who signed my contract answers to a deputy who sits on Halvard's advisory board, and my route plan was pre-approved by the Consortium's own geologists, and every site they sent me to had been salted."

"Salted."

"Planted. Doctored cores, spiked soils, ore fragments hauled in from a property four hundred kilometers south and buried at the sample depths. It's an old trick. It works because nobody expects it at this scale, and because verifying a salted site costs more than most governments will spend." I flexed my damaged fingers. "But I've been doing this long enough to smell it. The alteration halos were wrong. The mineral zoning made no structural sense. Desh noticed it too, actually — asked why the survey route ignored the actual geology. Your Consortium wrote my route, Yarrow. They walked me past the real rocks to the fake ones."

August was quiet. Then: "And the case."

"The case is the proof. Unsalted samples I collected myself, off-route, from sites I chose by reading the mountain instead of the itinerary. Forty-one cores. They show what the mountain actually contains, which is low-grade disseminated sulfide — technically ore, economically garbage. Nothing there worth the cost of the road, let alone the mine. Which means every dollar of that stock rise was built on rock that doesn't exist." I heard my own voice flatten, go clinical, the way it does when the alternative is shaking. "It's not a mining play. It's a pump-and-dump wearing a hard hat. When the permits expire and the project quietly dies, the executives will have sold months ago, and the only evidence of the crime will be sitting under four hundred meters of ice."

"Except it's not," August said. "Because you're sitting here with it."

"Except I'm sitting here with it."

* * *

Here is what I have not told anyone, and what I will put down now because a confession needs a witness and the mountain is the only one available:

I did not come up here planning to die. But I came up here understanding that dying was on the menu.

When I confirmed the salting — day nine of the survey, kneeling in a pit I'd dug off-schedule, holding a "porphyry core" that had no business existing in that host rock — I called my team together and told them everything. Marcus wanted to radio the Ministry immediately. Tomás wanted to finish the survey properly, document everything, build a case that couldn't be dismissed. Sofia cried, then got angry, then got practical, which is Sofia entire. And I made the decision that has been sitting on my chest ever since: we split the evidence. Half the cores went into the sealed case I would carry. The journal, the duplicate logs, the photographic record went with the team, split among five packs, headed down the east ridge toward the valley relay while I stayed high with the decoy — because I reasoned, correctly as it turned out, that whoever came for us would come for the leader and the case, and I would rather be the one they found.

I told myself it was tactics. It was also arithmetic: five people running are harder to catch than six, and I was the slowest among us by then, my ankle wrapped and useless since the scree field. I sent my people down a mountain into a building storm to save a box of rocks, and I told myself the rocks were worth more than their safety because the rocks were the truth and the truth protects everyone.

Two of them are dead. Maybe. I don't know. That not-knowing is the tax on the decision, collected daily, compounding.

What I know is this: the truth does not protect anyone. It just survives them. It sits in a sealed case while Marcus falls into a crevasse, and it keeps being true. I used to believe that was enough — that truth outliving its witnesses was a kind of victory, that the data would speak. But data doesn't speak. Data gets introduced into evidence by lawyers working for whichever side can afford the introduction, and the Consortium can afford anything.

That is why I guarded the case with a broken flare gun and eleven days of stubbornness. Not because I believed the truth would win. Because I wanted one thing on this mountain that Voss could not buy, and I was willing to be the price of it.

* * *

"You understand what he's offering you," I said to August, later, when the gray light had strengthened enough to make the cave legible. "Not me. You. Bring the case down, hand it over, and the Consortium pays your debt, clears your name, maybe even un-blacklists you. You're the perfect courier — you need this redemption more than you need the truth, and everyone in that helicopter knows it. That's why they hired you. That's why they hired someone *breakable*."

August didn't flinch, which I respected. Most people flinch.

"You think I don't know that?" they said. Their voice was dry, almost amused, the humor of a person standing on a ledge looking down. "Voss read my file aloud to me in his office. Like a bedtime story. *August Yarrow, lost the Ferro Ridge expedition, two clients hospitalized, license suspended, debts totaling—*" they waved a hand. "He didn't hire a guide. He hired a leash."

"So why are you still here? You could have handed me over already. Radioed him my location. Collected whatever finder's fee comes with a silenced scientist."

"Because Petra found your cairn using a compass that doesn't point north," August said, "and because Desh was right about the route, and because I've spent three years learning exactly what it feels like when powerful people decide what you're worth. I know the shape of that deal from the inside." They paused. "Also because you look like hell and I don't leave injured people on mountains. Habit."

"Habit," I repeated.

"It's the only clean motive I've got left. I'm keeping it."

I almost laughed. It hurt, laughing at altitude on half rations, so I converted it into a cough, which hurt differently.

"Yarrow. Listen to me, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to stop being reasonable." I leaned forward, and my ankle screamed, and I let it. "If you bring that case down that mountain, three things happen. One: the evidence disappears, and with it the only leverage anyone has over these people. Two: the stock recovers, because Voss will spin the survey team's deaths as a tragic accident during heroic verification work — martyrs for the deposit. Three: in about fourteen months, a road goes into this valley, a tailings pond goes into that drainage—" I pointed at the cave mouth, at the white corridor below us, the river we'd crossed, the ground Petra had nearly lost her boots in, "—and everything downstream of it gets poisoned on schedule, and every permit and study that allowed it will trace back to a verification survey signed by *me*. My name. On the fraud. Posthumously or otherwise."

"That's not certain—"

"Nothing is certain. That's the whole con. Certainty is expensive and doubt is free, and they manufacture doubt by the ton." I held up my ruined fingers, one by one. "Marcus. Tomás. Sofia, who is nineteen years old and wanted to be a glaciologist. I am the only person alive who can testify to what's in this case, and I am telling you, as a scientist and as the woman who got those people killed: the case does not go down this mountain intact. Not to Voss. Not to anyone. If it comes down, it comes down *open*, or it doesn't come down at all."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere above us, cornices were forming on the east ridge, loading themselves like accusations.

August looked at the sample case where it sat wedged behind me, aluminum scarred, straps frayed, containing nothing but forty-one pieces of boring rock and the entire net worth of several very wealthy liars.

"Eleven days," August said finally. "He gave us eleven days."

"He gave us a countdown," I corrected. "Countdowns aren't deadlines. They're levers. He wants you calculating, Yarrow. He wants you doing arithmetic with other people's lives until the numbers come out his way. That's how he operates. That's how they all operate."

"And what do you want?" August asked. "Honestly. If I could snap my fingers — what happens?"

I considered it. Nobody had asked me that in weeks. Everyone wanted to know what I *knew*, what I'd *seen*, what was *in the case*. Nobody wanted to know what I wanted, as though a whistleblower were a filing cabinet with legs.

"I want my team down the mountain," I said. "All of them, living or dead, named and buried properly. I want Sofia to get to be a glaciologist. I want the case opened in front of cameras, not handed over in the dark. And I want—" my voice caught, and I was furious at it, and pushed through, "—I want to stop being the woman who did the math. That's all. That's the whole list."

August nodded once, slowly, and I watched them arrive at something — not agreement, not yet, but the place where agreement might be built. Then they reached for the radio, checked the battery, and began dialing through frequencies with the focused calm of a person preparing to lie to a dangerous man.

"What are you doing?"

"Answering him," August said. "He's expecting a report. And I'm going to tell him exactly what he wants to hear — that we have the case, that the doctor is cooperative, that we'll move south toward the pass as soon as weather allows." They glanced back at me, and there was something in their face I recognized from mirrors: the expression of someone about to spend the last coin of their integrity and hoping it buys more than it costs. "You said he wants me calculating. Fine. Let's give him calculations."

"And the truth?"

"The truth," August said, "stays sealed until we're past the last checkpoint. After that—" they shrugged, and the shrug contained an entire unwritten contract, "—we'll see what the mountain says."

Outside, far to the southeast, I heard it before I identified it: a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Then another. Then a rolling, continuous rumble, and Petra's voice from outside the cave, sharp with alarm:

"Avalanche! The east face — it's coming down!"

The east ridge. The route my team had taken. The direction of the last checkpoint, the relay, the truth.

The mountain, it seemed, had opinions of its own.
