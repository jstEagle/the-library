## 8. The Missing Five

I have carried a lot of things down mountains for people who couldn't carry them themselves: packs, bodies, blame. So I knew the weight of what Vasquez was holding before she said a word. It was in the way she sat with her hands flat on her knees, the way she looked at the fire like it owed her an apology.

We were in the lee of a granite shoulder, four tents and a cook tarp and a storm chewing at the edges of everything. August had given Vasquez the spot nearest the stove, which I took for strategy and not kindness. August doesn't do kindness that doesn't pay rent.

"Say it again," August said. "Slowly."

Vasquez took a breath that seemed to come from somewhere below her boots. "I sent them ahead. Five of them. Down the east ridge, the morning before the storm front came in. I stayed with the samples and the junior tech to finish the last core log. The plan was to meet at the lower bivouac in two days."

"And?"

"And the storm came in nine hours early. I've been trying to raise them on the relay ever since." She paused. "Three made it down. I found them at the bivouac when I finally came off the ridge myself. Two didn't."

The stove hissed. Wind dragged its nails across the tarp. Nobody said anything for a while, which is a thing mountain people are good at — silence as a professional courtesy.

"Names," August said.

"Okafor and Lindqvist. Marcus and Sanna."

"And the three who made it?"

"Safe. Downvalley. They walked out on the east side before the pass closed."

August nodded slowly, the way a person nods when they're doing arithmetic they don't want to share yet. I'd seen that nod before, on other mountains, in other mouths, and I knew what it meant: someone was about to be weighed.

* * *

Here's what you should know about me. Desh Okonkwo, forty-one years old, twenty-two seasons on rock and ice, and in all that time I have never once been able to do the thing that good guides are supposed to do, which is to let the mountain keep what it takes. I've gone back. Every time. For a client's dropped camera once, in a crevasse field that would have killed me nine ways, and I went back for the goddamn camera. People call it loyalty. My therapist — the one season I could afford a therapist — called it something else, but I didn't like her tone.

So when Vasquez said *two unaccounted for*, something in my chest stood up and put its coat on.

"East ridge," I said. "How far back along it?"

"Eight, maybe nine kilometers from here," August said, not looking at me. "Past the shoulder."

"I wasn't asking you."

"I know." August looked up then, and there it was, the arithmetic, already done, sitting behind those gray eyes like a verdict. "You were asking the mountain."

"Then let the mountain answer."

"Desh."

"Eight kilometers in, eight out, if the weather holds," I said. "We've got the window. Voss said the front stalls tomorrow afternoon. We go light, two of us, we're back by—"

"By when?" August's voice was quiet, and quiet was worse than loud. "Back by when? Walk me through it. Weather holds until tomorrow afternoon — maybe. Then the front reorganizes and comes down the east side, which is exactly where you'd be. You'd be racing it home with whatever you found. And what would you find, Desh? Two people, nine days missing, in a storm that buried the ridge in a meter of snow. What's the survival math on that?"

"I've done worse math and gotten lucky."

"Luck isn't a plan."

"No," I said. "It's what you have left when you won't make a plan."

That landed. I saw it land. August's jaw did the thing it does, the small tightening, the tell of a person swallowing something that tastes like the truth. Around the stove, Petra had gone very still, the way she does when adults are doing something she thinks she should be taking notes on. Vasquez watched August with an expression I couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to.

"Say the number," I said.

"What?"

"You've done it. I can see it. Say the survival number."

August was silent long enough that the wind filled the space. "If they sheltered well, if they had the gear, if they found a cave — maybe. Maybe one in four. If they were caught in the open, zero. And the probability that they sheltered well, with gear, nine days ago, on a ridge that got hit first and hardest—" August shook their head. "You want me to say it. Fine. Under ten percent. Combined. And the cost of looking is that we spend our weather window, all of it, and then eleven people die on this mountain instead of two."

"Ten," I said. "Not eleven. Don't count yourself in the ledger, it's insulting to both of us."

"Desh—"

"Because that's what this is, right? The ledger. I've watched you do it since basecamp. Every decision runs through the column, risk against return, and it's clean, it's beautiful, it's exactly what the Consortium pays for." I stood up. The tarp was low and I hit my head, which felt appropriate. "But here's the thing about the ledger. It's always right, until the day it's about you. And then you pray to God somebody on the other side of it is a sentimental bastard like me."

* * *

August didn't answer right away, and I want to be fair here, because history — my history, anyway — tends to make August Yarrow the villain in every scene where they say no. But I watched their face in the stove light, and it wasn't a villain's face. It was the face of a person doing long division in their head and hating every digit.

"Petra," August said. "Weather."

Petra blinked, then pulled the barometer out of her jacket like she'd been waiting to be called on. "Steady drop all day. Wind's backing to the northeast, which is wrong for this time of year. My call, the front stalls tomorrow morning, maybe midday, then it reorganizes and comes back harder. If we're moving, we move at first light and we're somewhere sheltered by noon."

"And the ridge?"

Petra hesitated. She looked at me, and I gave her nothing, because it wasn't fair to make her carry my argument. "The ridge gets the reorganized front first. Full force. By tomorrow night it'll be unsurvivable up there."

"Thank you," August said. "That's the honest answer." And then, to me: "There it is. Not my arithmetic. The sky's."

"The sky doesn't care whether they're alive," I said. "That's supposed to be our job. The caring part."

"And if caring kills us?"

"Then it kills us doing the only thing that makes us different from the people who hired us."

That one hung in the air like a dropped tool. Because we all knew what the Consortium was. We all knew what the silence clause was. And August, blacklisted, broke, one contract from oblivion, had signed it anyway — I'd watched them sign it — and I think we all understood, without saying it, that this argument wasn't really about Okafor and Lindqvist. It was about whether August Yarrow was still the kind of person who went back for the camera.

Vasquez spoke for the first time in minutes. "They had two days of food. They had a tent between them. Marcus Okafor has—" her voice caught, and she steadied it with visible effort, the way you'd self-arrest on a slope. "Had. Has eleven years in the Alaska Range. Sanna is the best routefinder I've ever worked with. If anyone could survive nine days up there, it's those two. But they'd need to be found by someone who knows the east ridge, and there are exactly two people on this mountain who do." She looked at me. "One of them is me."

"Vasquez," August said, a warning in it.

"You can't climb the way you are. You know that."

"I know that."

"Then it's him." She turned to me. "I'll draw you every camp, every cache, every cave I know on that ridge. I'll give you the route they'd have taken. But you go tomorrow at first light or you don't go at all, because after noon you're not a rescue, you're a second emergency."

* * *

August walked out into the wind after that, and I gave it ten minutes before I followed, because some conversations you have to have where nobody else can hear the answer.

They were standing at the edge of the shelter, back to the storm, hood up, and when I came alongside they didn't look over.

"If I say yes," August said, "I'm sending you to die for a one-in-ten chance. You understand that's what yes means. It's not noble. It's a wager."

"I understand what yes means."

"And if I say no, you'll go anyway. You'll wait until I'm asleep and you'll take Petra's spare rack and you'll go, and you'll probably die, and I'll have to live with having let you."

"That's the most honest thing you've said since basecamp."

August almost smiled. Almost. "Here's my problem, Desh. Everyone keeps asking me to be the person who says yes. Vasquez needs it. You need it. And the worst part — the genuinely pathetic part — is that I want to be that person. I've spent three years being the person the industry says no to, and I would very much like to say yes to something." They turned and looked at me, and the wind was pulling at their hood and their eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and absolutely clear. "So I'll say yes. But it's my yes, with my conditions. You go at first light. You take the radio. You turn back at 1400 hours whether you've found them or not — not at the turnaround you decide in the moment, the one we agree on now, while we're both still smart. And if the front comes early, you come down, and you don't argue with it, because I will not trade you for them. That's the ledger. That's my arithmetic. Hate it if you want."

I stood there in the wind and found, to my surprise, that I wasn't angry. I was something closer to grateful, which was a new and uncomfortable sensation, like a boot that fits after years of the wrong size.

"One condition back," I said. "You tell Voss. Tonight. On the radio. You tell him we're diverting and he can put it in whatever column he likes."

August's jaw did the thing again. "He'll threaten to pull the extraction."

"He'll threaten. He won't do it. Dead guides are bad for stock prices, and that man thinks in nothing but."

"You've met his kind before."

"I've been his kind's client," I said. "It's worse."

August looked back at the ridge — you could just see it from there, a black saw-blade against a bruised sky, holding somewhere along its length either two living people or two memories — and I watched them take a breath and let the arithmetic go, just for a second, just long enough to say what they said next.

"Then we go at first light," August said. "Both of us. You and me."

I hadn't expected that. "You're not fit for the ridge."

"No. But I'm fit for the shoulder, and the shoulder's got line of sight to the upper ridge. I can run relay and glass the slopes while you move. Two sets of eyes, one radio, and if you find them, you'll want a second body for the carry." They shrugged, that economical shrug of theirs. "Besides, if you're going to defy my arithmetic, the least I can do is stand where I can watch you do it."

* * *

We told the others around the stove, and it went the way those things go: Petra already packing before August finished the sentence, Vasquez unrolling a map with hands that shook until they touched the contour lines and then didn't. She talked for an hour — camps, caches, a cave behind a serac at 4,300 meters that she'd used in a whiteout two seasons back, the exact line Okafor would have taken because she'd have taken it herself. She drew until her pencil went blunt and then kept drawing with the wood.

It was near midnight when the radio crackled. Voss, from wherever Voss lived when he wasn't materializing out of storms, wanting a position report. August picked up the handset, and I watched them take the breath, and I heard them say it — plain, no varnish: *diverting to the east ridge at first light, two missing surveyors, will resume the extraction route on return.*

The silence on the other end lasted long enough that I started counting seconds.

Then Voss, smooth as glaze: "Understood. But understand me, Yarrow — the window is the window. If the diversion costs us the permit, the helicopter leaves with or without you, and I will not be spending Consortium money on a recovery operation for sentimentalists. You have until the front returns. Not an hour after."

"Copy," August said, and put the handset down with a care that was somehow more violent than slamming it.

Petra was grinning in the dark like the storm was a present. Vasquez had her eyes closed, lips moving — a prayer, or a route plan, or both. And I sat there with my boots on, already dressed for morning, feeling the old familiar thing in my chest that my therapist had a name for and I still prefer to call loyalty.

At 0500 the wind backed around to the northeast, right on schedule, like the mountain had been listening.

At 0530, as we were breaking camp in the gray, Petra came back from the perimeter at a jog, and there was something in her face that stopped all of us mid-motion.

"There's a light," she said. "Up on the ridge. Moving. It flashed three times, then three again." She was breathing hard, and her eyes were bright, and her voice, when it came, was almost a whisper. "It's a signal. Somebody up there is signaling."
