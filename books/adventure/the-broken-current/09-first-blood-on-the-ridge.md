## 9. First Blood on the Ridge

I learned to read snow the way other kids learn to read faces — by necessity, and too young, and with consequences for getting it wrong. My father ran a mountain rescue station above Kranjska Gora, and before I was ten I could tell you what a slope had done last night just by the sound it made under my boots. So when Desh stopped at the lip of the crevasse field on the third morning out from Vasquez's cave and said, quietly, "Petra. Come look at this," my stomach knew before my head did.

The field spread below us like a frozen sea that had tried to swallow itself. Blue ice in shattered plates, crevasses running diagonal to our route, some of them wide enough that you could drop a truck into one and hear nothing hit bottom. The storm had passed two days ago and left everything scoured clean, wind-polished, honest. That was the problem. Snow that honest doesn't lie, and what it was telling us was written in a line of disturbed surface running down toward a collapse zone maybe four hundred meters in.

"Tracks," I said.

"Old tracks," Desh said. "Wind's been at them. But they're there."

August crouched beside us, and for a long moment none of us said anything, because we were all reading the same sentence. A party of at least three had come through here — not up from the valley, but *down* from the ridge, moving fast, roped carelessly or not at all based on the spacing — and then the line of their passage bent sharply toward the crevasse field instead of around it, which no competent mountaineer does, which meant either incompetence or desperation, and then the tracks converged on the collapse zone and simply stopped.

"Vasquez said her team went down the east ridge," August said. "Ahead of the storm."

"Five days ago," Desh said.

"Two unaccounted for."

Nobody moved. Above us the sky was doing that high-altitude thing where it looks like a lie — hard blue, harmless-looking, while the wind at the col behind us moaned like something with an opinion. I remember thinking, with the strange clarity fear sometimes hands you: *this is the part where it stops being a search.* We'd been telling ourselves a story since basecamp — missing persons, paperwork problem, a job with a paycheck attached. The snow had just torn that page out.

"We go down," August said. "Roped. Single file. Nobody steps anywhere I haven't stepped."

* * *

Probing a crevasse field is slow, ugly work. You drive your probe pole into the snow every half meter, feeling for the hollow note, and you do it hundreds of times, and mostly the mountain answers *nothing, nothing, nothing,* until the once that it answers *yes.* My arms were burning by the second hour. Vasquez, wrapped in Desh's spare parka and moving on a taped ankle and sheer fury, insisted on coming down with us, and when August started to object she said, "If it's one of mine down there, you don't get to decide whether I see him," in a voice that ended the conversation.

It was me who got the hollow note.

It came up through the probe like a struck bell — that wrongness, that absence where the world should be solid — and I called it, and August was beside me in four strides, and we cleared the snow bridge's edge with shovels and looked down into blue dark.

He was thirty meters down, wedged where the crevasse narrowed, and even from above I could see he hadn't survived the fall. He lay at an angle no living body chooses, one arm flung up toward the light as if the last thing he'd done was reach for it. His red shell jacket made him look almost festive against the ice, which is the kind of detail your mind supplies because the alternative details are unbearable. There was a name stenciled on the chest pocket. *OKAFOR.*

"That's Renny," Vasquez said behind me. Her voice didn't crack so much as flatten, like a signal going out of range. "Renaldo Okafor. He was twenty-six. He wanted to do glaciology. He kept saying the Serrano cores would make his career." A pause. "He was afraid of crevasses. He told me that his first week. He said he'd face anything except falling into the dark."

We rigged the haul system in silence. It took forty minutes to get him to the surface, and I won't describe it beyond saying that the mountain gives back its dead reluctantly, and cold, and heavier than physics should allow. Desh did most of the hauling. August worked the edge anchor with a stillness I recognized later as the specific discipline of a person keeping themselves together by giving their hands a job.

When Okafor was laid out on the surface, we did what you do. Vasquez knelt and said words in Spanish I didn't fully catch, her hand on his jacket, and Desh took off his hat, and I stood there holding the rope coil and thinking about a twenty-six-year-old who was afraid of exactly this and had walked into it anyway, five days ago, in a storm, carrying whatever he'd been carrying.

And then I saw the pack.

Or rather, I saw where the pack wasn't. A surveyor descending a ridge carries a pack — instruments, food, shelter, radio. Okafor's harness still had gear slung off it: an ice tool, a sling of carabiners, a water bottle frozen solid. But his back was bare. And the snow around him —

I crouched. "August."

"What?"

"Look at the snow around him. Look at what's *not* there."

August came over and went very still, the way they do, and then dropped to one knee and put their face close to the surface like a jeweler with a stone.

"Disturbance," August said. "Around the body. Post-mortem."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning somebody got here after he fell." August's gloved hand traced the air above the snow without touching it, sketching the story written there. "These are boot prints — different sole pattern than ours, different than his. Someone came down into the collapse zone, or along it, found him — or already knew where he was — dug through the debris here, see the churn? Took the pack. Took—" They stopped.

"Took the radio," I said, because I'd seen it too now: the small rectangular compression mark in the snow beside where the pack had been dug out, the shape of a radio case pressed into the surface, and no radio in it.

Desh had come up on our flank. "His team had radios," he said slowly. "Three radios between five people, standard government issue. If Okafor had one—"

"Then whoever took it has it now," August said. "And has had it for days."

The wind picked up right then, funneled along the ridge, and it carried that sound it makes across open ice — a long exhale, like the mountain deciding something — and I felt the hair stand up under my balaclava. Because here is the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing: a radio works both ways. Whoever held Okafor's radio could listen to any frequency the survey team used. Could have been listening since the storm. Could have heard Vasquez's check-in schedules, heard *us* calling on the same band, heard Voss's transmissions from the helicopter.

Someone wasn't lost on this mountain. Someone was *operating* on it.

* * *

We made camp that afternoon in a wind scoop below the ridge crest, and August did something I'd never seen a lead guide do: they posted watches in pairs and then took first watch alone, sitting at the camp's edge with their back to a boulder, facing downslope, facing the direction the strange tracks had gone.

I couldn't sleep anyway, so I made tea and brought a cup over and sat down next to them without asking. August glanced at me, decided against protest, and took the cup.

"You saw it too," I said. "Before I pointed it out. At the crevasse lip."

"I saw tracks that shouldn't exist."

"The sole pattern. Did you recognize it?"

A pause long enough that I thought they wouldn't answer. Then: "Military surplus. Vibram-type lug, square heel block. Common enough." Another pause. "Not common on government survey teams."

"Not common on Consortium contractors either?"

August turned and looked at me fully then, and in the flat grey light their face was hard to read, but not impossible. There was something in it I'd been learning to recognize over these days on the mountain — a war being fought behind the eyes between the person who'd signed the silence clause and the person who'd signed on to bring everyone home alive.

"You're sharp, Ilic," they said. "That's going to be worth something before this is over, and it's also going to be a problem, because sharp people see things, and seeing things on this mountain is about to become dangerous."

"I've been in danger before."

"Different kind." August sipped the tea. "Out here the danger's usually indifferent. Rockfall doesn't hate you. Weather doesn't want anything from you. This—" they nodded downslope, toward the vanished tracks, toward the stolen radio, toward the whole invisible architecture of intent we'd stumbled into the edge of — "this is a person. A person with a plan, a head start, and a radio that might let them hear everything we say on any channel we use."

"So we stop using the radio."

"We can't. Voss expects position reports. Silence reads as trouble, and trouble makes him do unpredictable things." August's jaw tightened. "From tomorrow we speak in code on the air. Locations rounded, conditions understated, nothing about the sample case, nothing about the journal, nothing about Okafor. As far as anyone listening knows, we're a tired crew walking a tired grid."

"And what do we actually know?"

August was quiet for a moment. Below us the glacier groaned — a deep tectonic complaint, the ice settling, the mountain rearranging itself in its sleep.

"We know someone reached Okafor before we did, within hours of the storm clearing, moving fast, and took only communications equipment. Not the instruments — a surveyor's pack would carry a GPS unit, a camera, possibly data cards. Those were left or taken selectively; I'll know more when we log what's missing against the manifest Vasquez described. We know they moved on downslope, east, along the route the second missing surveyor would have taken." They stopped.

"And?" I said, though I'd gotten there myself, and the getting-there was cold in my chest.

"And if they took Okafor's radio to control what he might broadcast," August said, "then they're looking for the second man for the same reason. Or they've already found him."

* * *

We buried Okafor the next morning, as much as you can bury anyone in ice. Desh cut a shelf in a drift above the crevasse field, and we laid him in it with his ice tool — Vasquez insisted on that, said he'd want it, said he'd earned it — and stacked the blocks back over him, and Vasquez read something from her field notebook, not a prayer exactly, more like a report delivered to the only authority she still answered to. She read his name, his age, the date, and then she said, "He told the truth in every sample he logged," and closed the book, and that was the eulogy, and it was more honest than most eulogies I've heard spoken over open graves with priests in attendance.

Afterward, Vasquez pulled me aside while August and Desh broke camp. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"You found the disturbance," she said. "You understood it fastest."

"Desh and August—"

"They're good. You're fast." She gripped my arm through her sleeve, and her hand was lighter than it should have been, five days of starvation in that grip. "Petra. Listen to me. Whatever you think is happening on this mountain, assume it's worse, and assume it's organized. My team didn't get scattered by weather. We were followed out of the high camp. I sent them down ahead because I thought distance would protect them, and I was wrong, and Renaldo is dead, and I will carry that, but I will not carry it pointlessly." Her voice dropped. "The case isn't rock samples. Well — it is rock samples. That's the whole horror of it. Worthless rock, logged as ore, sold to investors as the find of the decade. And somewhere on this ridge is a man named Tomas Reyes who has the duplicate core log, and if he's alive, everything depends on him staying alive long enough to matter."

"And if the people following him have the radio—"

"Then they know he's alive," Vasquez said, "and they know roughly where, and they are not trying to rescue him."

She released my arm and walked back toward the packs, small and straight-backed against the enormity of the ridge, and I stood there a moment letting the fear finish arriving. It came all the way in, cold and complete, and then — this is the part I didn't expect, the part I'd only ever read about in other people's accounts — it changed state. Fear, given nowhere to go, nowhere to run, nowhere to hide, does something strange. It compresses. It turns dense. It stops being a thing that happens to you and becomes a thing you hold.

I checked my compass out of habit. It swung lazily, wrong as ever, pointing at whatever altered magnetic nonsense the Consortium's cheap housings hid. I'd stopped trusting it days ago. I realized, standing over Okafor's ice grave with a stolen radio somewhere downslope and a hunted man somewhere ahead, that I'd transferred the trust somewhere else — to the snow, to the tracks, to my own reading of them, to the small sharp instrument of paying attention. The mountain had taken away every reliable bearing we had. Fine. We'd navigate by evidence.

"Petra!" August's voice, from the packs. "Front of the line. You're on track-finding today. Anything moves on this ridge, I want you to see it before it sees us."

I shouldered my pack and took the point, and I'll admit what I felt, because it surprised me: not courage exactly, and not calm. Something leaner than either. The resolve of someone who has finally stopped hoping the situation is what it appears to be, and started planning for what it actually is.

We climbed east along the ridge through the morning, single file, watching the snow. Around noon, at a saddle where the wind had stripped the slope to old firm nevé, I found the next message the mountain had been keeping for us.

Boot prints again — the square-heeled military soles, two sets this time — moving fast along the contour, heading east.

And beside them, faint, half-erased by wind, smaller prints. A lighter man, limping — one footprint deeper than the other, a drag mark between strides — heading the same direction, slightly ahead.

Tomas Reyes was still moving. He was injured, alone, and running.

And twenty meters past where his trail crossed the saddle, half-buried in a drift, glinting dully in the sun, lay a portable relay antenna, freshly planted, its cable snaking down behind a rock — aimed not at the valley, but at us.

Someone had built a listening post directly on our route. Which meant someone knew exactly where we were going.

Which meant, I understood with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that from this point on, the mountain wasn't the thing hunting us.
