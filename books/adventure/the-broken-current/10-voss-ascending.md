## 10. Voss Ascending

The radio crackled at 0600 with a voice I'd been dreading for three days.

"Yarrow. Voss. We're coming up to you. Two hours out."

No question about whether that was convenient. No request for coordinates — he already had them, which was the first thing wrong with the morning. The second thing was the weather: low cloud, spitting snow, visibility maybe four hundred meters. Nobody flies in that unless they're not flying so much as walking, and nobody walks into the Serrano drainage in January for fun.

I sat on my heels outside the cook tent with the handset in my mitt and watched my breath do its little ghost impression, and I thought about all the ways a man can answer a message like that. "Copy" was the professional one. "Negative, stay off my mountain" was the honest one. What I actually said was, "Copy. We'll put water on."

Because that's what I am now, apparently. A man who puts water on for people he doesn't trust, and calls it pragmatism instead of cowardice. There's a whole taxonomy of these things. I could write a field guide.

* * *

They came out of the white at 0815 like figures developing on film. Three of them roped loosely together, moving well — too well, actually, for men who'd supposedly hiked twelve kilometers of broken moraine before breakfast. Voss led. Behind him were two others in matching black shells, new gear, tags still bright, packs riding high and light the way packs ride when they're carrying nothing but confidence.

Petra materialized beside me the way she does, without sound, like the mountain had grown her overnight. "That's them?"

"That's them."

"They look like they're going to a funeral."

"They look like they're going to *conduct* one," Desh said from behind us. He'd come out of his tent with his ice axe already in hand, which told me everything about where his head was. Desh Okonkwo has climbed things that would make a chamois dizzy, and he doesn't take his axe to breakfast unless something smells wrong.

Vasquez stayed inside. She'd heard the radio too. She looked at me once, level and unreadable as a core sample, and said, "Your call how you play it," and went back to guarding that aluminum case like a bear guards a carcass.

So there we were. My expedition, such as it was: me, Petra, Desh, a half-starved scientist with a busted ankle and a box of rocks worth nothing and everything, and now three visitors from the money side of the world, stomping into camp with their breath steaming and their smiles pre-assembled.

"August Yarrow." Voss stuck out his hand. His glove was leather, city-weight, and cold to the touch even through mine. "Glad you're alive. When you went quiet on the radio yesterday I'll admit I worried."

"We didn't go quiet," I said. "We went climbing. Radio's got a shadow behind the east ridge. You know that. You've read the survey maps."

Something crossed his face — quick, controlled, gone — like a fish under ice. "Of course. Well." He turned a slow circle, taking in the camp: three tents, the cook tarp, the cache, Vasquez's cave entrance roped off with wands. "Remarkable work. Remarkable. And Dr. Vasquez? Truly alive?"

"Injured. Dehydrated. Alive."

"And the case?" His eyes did the thing where they pretend not to look at the thing they're looking at. Every negotiator I've ever met has that trick, and every one of them thinks they invented it.

"Intact," I said. "Sealed. Cold."

"Good. Good." He rubbed his hands together like a man warming up for a speech. "Then let me introduce my colleagues. Kessler. Brandt. They're logistics contractors — specialists in high-altitude load movement. I brought them because frankly, August, getting that case down this mountain with your crew's condition is going to be a serious undertaking, and Halvard doesn't leave its people under-resourced."

Kessler nodded. Brandt nodded. Both of them had the flat, patient stillness of men who'd been paid to stand somewhere and be ready. I've hired porters for twenty years; I know what labor looks like, and it doesn't look like that. Labor fidgets. Labor checks its boots. These two stood like furniture that could kill you.

"Helicopter couldn't make it in this cloud?" I asked.

"No," Voss said smoothly. "Weather window's closing. We came up the drainage on foot."

"From where?"

"The lower camp. Your basecamp."

"That's thirty-two kilometers," Petra said. She hadn't raised her voice. She never raises her voice; she just states distances the way other people state grievances. "Over the moraine field. In this visibility. Starting when?"

Voss smiled at her the way you smile at a child who's asked an inconveniently good question. "Early."

"We left tracks coming up," she said. "There are no tracks below the second bench. I checked this morning. Snowfall stopped at 2200 last night. If you walked thirty-two kilometers since then, you averaged better than four kilometers an hour over ground that took our team nine hours going downhill with fresh legs."

The silence lasted maybe two seconds. It felt like a glacier calving in slow motion.

"I don't know what to tell you," Voss said, and here was the interesting part: he didn't get angry, didn't fluster, didn't scramble. He simply let the lie sit there between us, unrepaired, like a man who knows the bridge is out but figures you'll cross anyway because the alternative is swimming. "Perhaps the wind took them. Mountains are funny that way."

"Mountains are funny," I agreed. "But they're not funny enough to erase six sets of boot prints and leave yours."

Desh made a small sound that might have been a laugh or might have been him clearing his sinuses. With Desh it's often both.

* * *

We fed them, because I'm a professional and also because I wanted them sitting down, drinking tea, expending calories on conversation instead of anything else. Kessler and Brandt ate like machines — efficient, silent, eyes moving. Voss ate like a man performing appetite, and talked the whole time, which is how I learned the most useful thing of the morning: a man telling a story he rehearsed keeps to the script, and a man improvising keeps checking the exits.

"So walk me through it," I said, pouring him a third cup. "You got my position off the sat check-in yesterday. Then what?"

"Then I mobilized. Chartered a flight to the valley strip, waited out the worst of the cloud, flew as far as the lower camp when the ceiling lifted briefly around dawn—"

"You landed?"

"Touched down long enough to drop us. Then the pilot pushed back ahead of the weather."

"To where?"

"Valley strip."

"Flew through the same cloud you couldn't fly through an hour earlier."

"He knows the terrain," Voss said. "Local man."

I nodded along, warm and agreeable, and inside my head I was laying his story out on a table next to the facts like a man comparing a forged signature against a real one. The valley strip is ninety minutes' flight from the nearest town with a charter service. To have a bird over the lower camp by dawn, he'd have mobilized before my sat check-in — which means he knew where we'd be before we reported it. Which means either he had another source on this mountain, or he'd never lost track of us at all.

And there was a third possibility, the one I liked least: that someone else had been reporting our position. Someone with a radio. A stolen radio, broadcasting from the east ridge, that Vasquez's missing surveyor had been using to shout the fraud at anyone with ears. Maybe Voss wasn't tracking us. Maybe he was tracking *him*, and we were just the obstacle standing between Voss and the only witness who mattered.

"You look tired, August," Voss said kindly.

"I'm always tired. It's the altitude."

"It's more than that." He set down his cup and gave me the full weight of his attention, and I'll grant him this: the man is good. Sincere is his best register. "I want to say something plainly, because I think you're a plain man. This has been a hard operation. Communications failed, weather turned, people got hurt. That's not on you. Halvard understands mountains. What Halvard needs now is simple: the doctor and the instruments, delivered safe, within the permit window. Do that, and I give you my word — no inquiry into the delays, full payment plus hazard bonus, and a letter that will end the blacklisting business permanently. Sterling-level references. Whatever you want your career to look like after this, it can look like that."

He said *Sterling* like a name meant to mean something to me, and it did — R.M. Sterling, the guide who wrote the books everyone in the trade grew up on, whose endorsement used to be worth more than a certification. Dead ten years now, but his name still opens doors, or used to. Voss had done his homework. That was almost flattering.

"And if the instruments show something awkward?" I asked. "You remember the silence clause. Recover the instruments, tell no one what they contain. But nobody ever told me what they contain."

"They contain rock samples," Voss said. "Rocks are rarely awkward."

"Dr. Vasquez seems to disagree."

"Dr. Vasquez," he said, gently, the way you'd correct a child's grip on a knife, "has been alone on a mountain for nineteen days. Her team scattered, her equipment compromised, her judgment — through no fault of her own — unreliable. You've seen her condition. Would you stake a company's future on the testimony of a starving woman with a head injury?"

"She staked her life on it."

"Yes," Voss said, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind his face that wasn't kindness at all. "People do that. It's why we insure them."

* * *

After breakfast I walked the perimeter, which is guide-speak for needing to be alone, and found Desh already out there doing the same thing, which is how I knew he needed it worse.

"Well?" he said.

"Well what?"

"Don't 'well what' me, Yarrow. You've been reading that man since he stepped out of the snow. Give me the read."

I crouched by the cache and pretended to check the lashings. "His story doesn't hold. No tracks, impossible timeline, and he knew our position before we transmitted it. Either he's got a second source or he's been following the east-ridge broadcasts."

"The missing surveyor."

"Maybe. Or maybe he's got eyes closer than that." I said it quietly, but not so quietly that it wouldn't travel. Camps are small. Words have legs.

Desh grunted. He squatted next to me, forearms on knees, watching the two contractors across camp as they checked — of all things — each other's harnesses, with the wordless economy of men who'd worked together in places where checking matters. "Those two aren't porters," he said.

"No."

"I've seen that type. Bogotá. Kinshasa. The kind of contractor who shows up when a mining company needs a problem carried somewhere specific." He paused. "Or made to disappear somewhere specific."

"You're speculating."

"I'm a climber. Speculating about conditions is my job." He stood, rolled his shoulders. "Eleven days on the permits, Voss says. Nine now, probably. Storm building in the south — you saw the pressure trend. So here's my question, boss, and I want the honest version, not the contract version. When we go down this mountain, who walks in front?"

It was a good question. It was the question. Because a party moves at the speed of its leader, and a leader decides which line to take, and a line can be honest or it can be convenient, and I had spent the last three years of my life taking convenient lines and calling it survival.

"Honest version," I said. "I don't know yet."

Desh looked at me for a long moment. He's got one of those faces that makes disappointment feel like a physical event, weather moving through. "When you figure it out," he said, "figure it out fast. Because that man brought muscle to a science project, and muscle doesn't hike thirty-two kilometers for tea."

* * *

At midday the wind swung south and started hauling, and the barometer I keep in my breast pocket began its slow dive, and I stood at the edge of camp watching the cloud stack up over the southern pass like a wall being built brick by brick, and I did arithmetic I didn't like.

The southern route was dying. Not today, maybe not tomorrow, but soon — katabatic flow off the icefield, the kind of storm that pins parties for a week and kills the careless ones faster. If we were going out, it would be east, over the ridge, toward the missing surveyor and his stolen radio and whatever else was waiting up there. Toward the person, possibly, who'd been feeding Voss our coordinates.

Behind me, voices. Voss and Vasquez, standing by the cook tarp in the flat gray light. I caught fragments on the wind — his smooth, hers cracked but steady — and then one sentence of hers, clear as a bell across the ice:

"You can carry the case out yourself, Mr. Voss. It weighs eleven kilograms. The contents weigh considerably more."

And Voss laughed, easy and warm, and clapped her on the shoulder, and said something I couldn't hear, and the two contractors shifted their weight in the snow, very slightly, like dogs changing stance.

I looked at the southern wall of cloud. I looked at the east ridge, sharp and blue-white against the sky. And I understood, finally and completely, that the mask wasn't slipping anymore.

It was off. It had been off since the moment he walked into camp with no footprints behind him, and the only question left — the one I'd have to answer before the storm answered it for me — was whether I'd been wearing one too, all this time, and whether I was ready to take mine off where everyone could see.

The wind picked up. Somewhere above us, on the east ridge, a radio was still broadcasting.

I went to find Vasquez.
