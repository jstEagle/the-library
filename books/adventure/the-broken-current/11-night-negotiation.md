## 11. Night Negotiation

The storm gave us a gift of six hours of stillness, which in the Serrano Range is roughly the equivalent of a stranger handing you an envelope of cash and saying *don't ask questions*. Nobody trusts it. Everybody takes it anyway.

We camped in the lee of a granite shoulder at 3,400 meters, tents pitched on wind-scoured rock because snow here came and went like a bad reputation. Voss and his two contractors had their own site fifty meters downwind — close enough to watch us, far enough that we couldn't hear them, which I suspected was the entire point of the arrangement. The contractors were named Brandt and Cole, or claimed to be, and they moved like men who'd been paid to be forgettable. They ate their rations facing outward.

I ate mine facing Vasquez.

She sat against the tent wall with her injured ankle propped on a pack, the sealed sample case between her knees like a sleeping animal she didn't trust not to bite. She'd eaten half her ration and stopped, the way people do when hunger has become a habit rather than a request. Nine days she'd been up here before we found her. Nine days alone with worthless rock and the truth about it, guarding both.

"Dr. Vasquez," I said. "Got a minute?"

"I've had nine days of minutes." Her voice was dry as the ration crackers. "Spend yours carefully."

I crouched beside her, keeping my voice under the wind's floor. Desh was on first watch, twenty meters out at the ridge line, a dark shape against a darker sky. Petra was already asleep, or performing sleep convincingly enough that it counted. I waited until I was sure, then wasn't sure, then decided certainty was a luxury this mountain had never stocked.

"I want to talk about how we get down."

"We walk down. It's not complicated. It's just terrible."

"The southern pass is still our route if the weather holds. Voss says—"

"Voss says a lot of things." She shifted, and the case shifted with her, her hands never leaving it. "You signed something for him. I can tell. You have the posture of a man carrying paper he wishes he could burn."

"That obvious?"

"You look like me," she said. "That's how."

I let that sit for a moment. Out past the ridge line, the sky over the eastern peaks had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong — deep violet shading into something almost green. Weather was stacking up behind the divide. We had maybe a day, day and a half, before it came over the top of us, and after that every option on this mountain would close except the ones that killed you.

"Here's my offer," I said. "You cooperate. You come down with us, no games, no detours, case stays sealed until we're off the ice. When we hit the extraction point, you get your say. Whatever happens after that — the data, the journal, whatever you're carrying in that head of yours — gets decided by you, then, with lawyers if you want them, with journalists if you want those instead. But you get down alive first. That's the deal. Cooperate now, decide later."

Vasquez looked at me for a long time. In the last flat light before full dark, her face had the quality of terrain — everything carved by weather, nothing wasted.

"Decide later," she repeated. "That's what you're offering. The *right* to decide later."

"I'm offering you a way off this mountain that doesn't end with Voss taking that case off your corpse."

"And what are you offering yourself?"

"Nothing. That's the beauty of it."

"Liar," she said, without heat. "You're offering yourself the story you'll tell afterward. *I got her down alive.* It's a good story. It might even be true. But it isn't nothing, Mr. Yarrow. Nothing doesn't need to be negotiated at midnight, ten meters from the man paying you."

I should have walked away then. Instead I did what I always do, which was stay and make it worse.

"Fine. Here's the honest version. I'm broke. I'm blacklisted. My name is worth less than the radio batteries we're burning through. Halvard clears my debt and maybe — *maybe* — buys back enough of my reputation to work again. If that case comes down unopened and unremarked, I eat next winter. If it comes down opened, I don't. So yes, I want you to cooperate, and yes, I want it for reasons that would embarrass us both if we said them out loud in daylight." I heard my own voice go flat and professional, the guide's voice, the one I used to hide behind on bad routes. "But the part about you getting down alive is real. I don't leave people on mountains. Ask anyone who'll still talk to me."

"They left *you*," she said. "On a mountain. That's why you're here instead of somewhere better."

The wind picked up briefly, worried the tent fabric, and died again. Somewhere below us, Brandt or Cole coughed once, deliberately, the way men do to announce they're awake.

"Doesn't matter why I'm here," I said.

"It matters entirely why you're here. It's the only thing about you that matters." She pulled her jacket tighter. "But fine. Your deal. I'll take most of it."

"*Most* of it."

"I cooperate. I come down. No detours." She turned so the case was fully between us, her hands flat on its lid like a woman taking an oath on a book. "But the seal stays intact until we're past the last checkpoint. Not the extraction point. The checkpoint — the relay station below the pass, where there's a working transmitter and more than one person present. Because here's what your midnight honesty hasn't accounted for, Mr. Yarrow: the moment we reach that helicopter, the only witnesses to anything are whoever Voss decides to bring home. Dead women tell no tales, but neither do living ones who get talked out of them at 12,000 feet. I've read enough accident reports to know how survivors' stories get written. Usually by the company."

"The helicopter comes whether you cooperate or not. Voss needs survivors. He told me—"

"He told you what he needed you to believe. He needs *you*, Yarrow. You're the licensed guide. If anyone dies on this descent, he needs a professional signature on the paperwork saying it was the mountain's fault. You're not his employee. You're his alibi with crampons."

I wanted to laugh. It came out as something smaller and uglier.

"And the checkpoint changes that how?"

"At the checkpoint there's a transmitter, a logbook, and a second party — the fisheries crew rotates through every ten days; they were due yesterday. Witnesses who aren't on Halvard's payroll. Once I've stood in front of them and logged our position officially, the case can open or stay shut, and either way the truth has a body outside this mountain." She shrugged. "It costs you nothing. It costs Voss nothing, unless Voss intended something at the extraction point that he'd prefer unwitnessed. And if that's the case—" her eyes came up to mine, level and tired and absolutely certain, "—then you needed to know it before the helicopter, not after."

I sat with that. The cold came up through my knees and settled into my chest where it found plenty of room.

"You think he'd leave people on this mountain."

"I think he left *me* on this mountain. For nine days. With a broken ankle and half a stove. I think he hoped the weather would do his editing for him." She said it without drama, the way you'd report a core sample result. Data, not grievance. "I think the man who flew up here with two contractors and a story about *helping carry the case* has already decided how many people this operation can afford to bring home. I'm asking you to make sure the number includes everyone."

"And if I refuse? If I say extraction point, take it or leave it?"

"Then I'll take it," she said simply. "Because the alternative is staying here, and I've done my time on this mountain. But you'll spend the whole descent knowing you made a promise you didn't believe in, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what happened at the helicopter. I've watched men carry that. It weighs more than the case."

* * *

I walked the perimeter after that, because walking is what I do when sitting means thinking and thinking means feeling. The temperature was falling through the floor. My breath crystallized and hung around my head like a small personal storm system, which felt accurate.

Desh fell into step beside me at the ridge line without being asked. He did that. Twenty years of high-altitude work had burned the small talk out of him entirely; he simply appeared where the weight needed sharing.

"Well?" he said.

"Well what?"

"You were talking to her for forty minutes. I count things. It's a habit." He handed me a thermos cap of something that smelled like it had been invented out of desperation and improved by practice. "So. What's the deal?"

"There's no deal."

"August." Just my name, delivered with the patience of a man who'd watched me lie to myself on three continents. "I was on watch. Sound carries up here. Wind was wrong for it, everything carries."

I drank the terrible coffee and looked east, where the bruised sky had begun to spit the first dry grains of snow, harbingers, scouts sent ahead of the main army.

"She wants the checkpoint instead of the extraction point," I said finally. "Case stays sealed till we're past the relay station. She thinks Voss is planning something at the helicopter."

"Do you think that?"

"I think Voss flew onto a mountain in a storm window with two muscle-bound ghosts and a story that doesn't survive daylight. I think he knew where we were camped before we radioed it. I think—" I stopped. Saying it aloud made it load-bearing. "I think I signed a silence clause with a man who may be planning a very quiet expedition home."

Desh nodded slowly, the way he nodded at cornices — acknowledging the danger without pretending surprise.

"So what did you offer her?"

"A way down. Cooperate now, decide later."

"And she countered."

"Checkpoint."

"And you took it."

"I took it."

"Good," Desh said.

I looked at him. "Good? Desh, I just cut a private side deal with the client's principal problem, fifty meters from the client's hired muscle, while on the client's payroll. There's a word for that and it isn't *good*."

"The word is *survival*," he said. "And the other word is *late*. You should've done this four days ago." He took the thermos cap back and drank. "For what it's worth — and I know exactly what it's worth, which is nothing — I think she's right about the helicopter. I've carried cases for companies before. Companies don't send two contractors to help carry eleven kilos of rock. You can hire a porter for that. You send contractors when you're worried about what happens if the cargo develops opinions."

"Then why am I the only one who feels sick about it?"

"Because you're the one who signed the clause." He said it gently, which was somehow worse. "Petra didn't sign anything. Vasquez signed nothing. I signed nothing. You're the only man on this mountain who sold something he hadn't finished paying for, and now you're trying to buy it back in installments, at night, in secret, hoping nobody notices the transaction." He capped the thermos. "Everybody noticed, August. Petra noticed. I noticed. I'd bet the contractors noticed, because they notice everything, it's their job. The only person still pretending is you."

The snow thickened around us, fat flakes falling straight down in the dead air, the kind of fall that meant the storm above was deciding, gathering its argument.

"What would you do?" I asked. It was a weak question and I hated myself for asking it, but the altitude strips you down to whatever's honest.

Desh considered it seriously, because he considers everything seriously.

"I'd stop making deals in the dark," he said. "Deals made at midnight are how you end up owning things you can't see. Make it in daylight tomorrow. Tell the group. All of it — the clause, the counteroffer, all of it. Not because transparency is pretty. Because if Voss is planning what she thinks he's planning, the five of us are only going to survive it as *five*. Secrets are how he wins. He's counting on each of us holding our own little piece of the dark." He looked at me, and in the dark his face was mostly voice. "You split us apart tonight, one bargain at a time, and by the pass there won't be a team. There'll just be five separate people with separate deals, and a man with a helicopter deciding which deals get honored."

He clapped my shoulder once, the way you'd test an anchor, and went back to his watch.

I stood there a long time, letting the snow land on me. Below, the contractors' fire had gone out — or been put out; men like that don't waste light. Voss's tent was a black rectangle against the snowfield, patient as a closed door.

In ours, Vasquez slept with her hand on the case. Petra slept with her back to the wall and her boots laced, which told me she'd heard everything too, and had decided, like Desh, that the information was safer inside her than aired. Two people on this mountain had eavesdropped on my conscience tonight, and neither had said a word, and somehow that was the loudest thing that had happened all day.

Tomorrow, I thought. Daylight. Tell them all of it.

Then the radio crackled at my hip, and Merrick Voss's voice came through the static, pleasant and wide awake, a man who had also been counting.

"Yarrow. You up? Good. Change of plans. Storm's moving faster than the models said — we move on the south pass at first light, all of us, together. And August?" A pause, filled with the sound of a man smiling into a microphone. "Bring the doctor. Bring the case. We wouldn't want anyone getting separated from their baggage."

The frequency clicked dead. Above the eastern peaks, the sky began, quietly, to come apart.
