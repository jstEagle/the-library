## 12. The Pass Closes

I've been in weather that wanted to kill me before. I've stood on ridgelines while the sky came apart like wet paper, and I've told myself the same lie every time: *this is just wind, this is just snow, you've seen worse.* The mountain always let me keep the lie. That's the bargain high places make with you — they scare you, and then they don't, and you walk away believing you won.

The storm that came down off the Serrano icefield on our ninth morning did not make that bargain. It made a different one, and it named its terms in the first ten minutes.

We heard it before we saw it — a sound like a freight train running on rails that didn't exist, somewhere above the col to the south. Petra was boiling water when it started, and she froze with the stove lighter still clicking, that little spark-wheel going *tick tick tick* into the silence, because everyone in camp knew what we were hearing and nobody wanted to be the one who said it.

Katabatic. Gravity wind. The glacier exhaling cold air down its own throat at sixty, seventy kilometers an hour, and it would not stop for hours, maybe days. It would fill the southern pass like water filling a bathtub, and anything in that pass — man, tent, prayer — would be a leaf in the drain.

"Okay," August said, zipping out of the cook tent into the white roar. "Okay. Nobody moves. Nobody does anything heroic."

I stayed where I was and thought about arithmetic.

That's my job on these trips, mostly. August reads terrain the way other people read faces; Petra reads instruments and, apparently, compasses that lie; Vasquez reads rock. Me, I count. Calories, rope lengths, hours of daylight, meters of elevation against the fuel in the stove. Somebody has to know what the numbers say when everybody else is busy hoping. Hope is a fine emotion but a terrible unit of measurement.

And the numbers had been getting ugly for two days.

* * *

Here's the ledger as I had it written in my head, in the neat columns I keep behind my eyes:

Eleven days until the permits expired, per Voss's radio call. We'd burned through most of them getting up here, finding Vasquez, finding the dead man in the crevasse, finding the second surveyor's trail go cold toward the east ridge. Fuel: enough for six more days if we cooked lean. Food: seven, if nobody got greedy. Weather: the forecast window Voss had promised us was closing like a door in another room, and now here was the sound of the latch.

And the southern pass — the extraction route, the one Voss's helicopter was staged beyond, the one every plan we'd made assumed — was currently being sealed by a wall of moving air cold enough to crack steel.

Six people. One injured man already carried once. A sealed sample case that half of us were ready to throw in a crevasse and the other half would die before letting go of. And a liaison with two contractors whose story about how he'd found us still didn't hold together no matter how many times he told it.

I ran the numbers again, hoping they'd improved overnight. They hadn't. Numbers are loyal that way.

"Desh." August crouched into the cook tent, snow crusted across their shoulders like they'd been wearing it as a coat. Their face had that look I'd learned to dread over fifteen years of working together — the calm one, the decision-already-made one. "You've been doing the math."

"I've been doing the math."

"And?"

"And the pass is closed. Not 'might close.' Closed. That wind will run twelve hours minimum, probably thirty-six, and after it stops we'll have a day of avalanche hazard before anything down there is walkable." I held up fingers. "Call it three days lost if we wait for south. We have food for seven. Fuel for six. And the helicopter isn't going to sit on that side of the range burning charter money while we sit on this side burning ours."

August nodded slowly, the way they do when you've confirmed something they already knew and they're deciding whether to tell you the rest.

"There's another way," they said.

"The east ridge."

"The east ridge."

Of course there was. There's always another way, and it's always worse, and it's always the one you take anyway. That's mountaineering. That's life, if you squint.

* * *

Vasquez took the news better than I expected, which is to say she didn't argue at all. She sat in the corner of the cook tent with her splinted wrist cradled against her chest and said only, "Marco went east."

The second missing surveyor. Marco Reyes, according to the journal pages we'd recovered — the one who'd walked out ahead of the storm and never come back, the one whose radio somebody else was apparently using to talk to the world.

"You think he's alive," Petra said. It wasn't quite a question. She'd been quiet since the crevasse, since the dead man with his radio taken, since all of us started understanding that we weren't alone on this mountain and never had been.

"I think he was smart enough to dig in," Vasquez said. "He was the best of us in bad conditions. And I think whoever has his radio knows exactly where he is." Her voice didn't shake. I noticed that. I also noticed her good hand pressed flat against the sample case, like a woman keeping a hand on a sleeping animal she didn't entirely trust.

Petra looked at me. I looked at the numbers.

East ridge meant elevation gain we couldn't afford, exposure we couldn't protect, and a descent on the far side through ground none of us had seen except on a map with optimistic contour lines. East ridge meant walking toward whatever had taken a grown man's radio off his body. East ridge meant — and this was the part I kept circling back to, the way your tongue finds a broken tooth — east ridge meant leaving the southern pass behind, and the southern pass was where Voss wanted to go, and any plan that put distance between us and Merrick Voss was a plan I liked better than I could professionally justify.

"We should search for him," I said. "Before anyone asks me whether we can afford to."

Nobody asked. That was the thing about this crew by then — we'd stopped pretending the questions were open.

* * *

Voss disagreed. Of course Voss disagreed.

He came out of his tent with the contractors flanking him — Brandt and the quiet one, Sorel, both of them built like equipment rather than men — and he stood in the wind with his hood back like the storm was a personal insult he intended to file a complaint about.

"The pass opens in two days," he said. "Maybe less. Katabatics burn out. We hold position, we conserve fuel, we walk out south on schedule."

"On schedule," August repeated. There was a particular flatness to their voice when they used that word around Voss. I'd been cataloguing it for days. "Merrick. Your schedule died nine days ago when the survey team stopped answering radios."

"My schedule is the only reason there's a helicopter within two hundred kilometers of this rock pile." He wasn't shouting. That was the worst part. He had the reasonable man's voice, the boardroom voice, the voice of someone explaining why the layoffs are actually a kindness. "Every hour we spend on that ridge is an hour the aircraft burns fuel waiting. Every hour past the permit expiry is an hour the whole operation becomes trespass. You want to explain to a magistrate why you were on closed concession land?"

"I want to explain to a widow why we didn't look for her husband," August said.

Something moved behind Voss's face — quick, gone fast, but I caught it. Not guilt. Calculation. The flicker of a man re-pricing an asset.

"Reyes is dead," Voss said. "A week in this cold without shelter? He's dead, and the ridge is a graveyard, and I'm not spending my margin digging graves." He turned to the group, spreading his hands, the salesman's gesture, offering us all a fair deal. "South. When the wind drops. Anyone who wants to come east can explain their choices to the same magistrate."

And there it was. The fork in the road, served up with a smile.

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that moment, because honesty is the whole point of testimony, or so the inquiry letter keeps reminding me. What happened was this: part of me wanted to go south. Part of me — the tired part, the fifty-one-year-old part, the part with a bad knee and a daughter who'd stopped asking when I was coming home — looked at that sealed pass and thought, *two days of sitting still, then a helicopter, then hot food and a bed and never thinking about the Serrano Range again.* The storm had done me a favor. It had given me an excuse. All I had to do was stand still and let the mountain make my choice for me.

Then I looked at Vasquez's hand on the sample case. Then at Petra, who was watching August with the expression of someone waiting to find out what kind of person she'd signed on with. Then at the east ridge itself, visible in the tearing cloud for maybe four seconds at a stretch, white and knife-edged and full of nothing good.

And I thought about Marco Reyes, who might be alive, and about the fact that Voss had just told us, plainly, in front of witnesses, that he considered a living human being to be *margin.*

You learn things about people in storms. The storm doesn't create the truth; it just strips away everything else.

* * *

August called it at dusk, when the wind dropped from murderous to merely hostile and we could hear each other shout from three meters instead of one.

They didn't give a speech. August Yarrow doesn't give speeches; they give decisions, wrapped in just enough explanation to keep the peace. They stood us all in a line outside the tents, snow blowing sideways, and laid it out.

"South is closed for three days minimum. We have food for seven and fuel for six, and the aircraft won't wait past the permit date regardless of what Voss tells dispatch. So south doesn't get us out. It gets us stuck, hungry, and negotiating from weakness." A pause. Snow ticking off their jacket. "East ridge gets us over the top tomorrow, down the far side in two, and puts us within relay range of the extraction frequency by day five. It also gives us a chance — one chance — to find Marco Reyes alive."

"And if he's dead?" Sorel said. First words I'd heard from the quiet contractor in two days. His voice was surprisingly gentle, which somehow made it worse.

"Then we bring him home," August said, "and we've lost nothing but altitude we had to lose anyway."

"And the case?" Voss asked.

"The case goes east with us. You're welcome to come, Merrick. You're welcome to go south alone. Those are the options."

For a long moment Voss just stood there, and I watched him do the arithmetic I do — except his columns were different. His columns had things like *leverage* and *witnesses* and *how much of this story survives the descent.* I watched him arrive at a number he didn't like.

"You're making a mistake," he said finally. "All of you. I'm telling you that as the person who signs the checks."

"Noted," August said. "Desh. Rope teams. We move at first light."

* * *

I didn't sleep. None of us really slept — the wind saw to that, hammering the fly like a fist on a door, and under the hammering, the other sound, the one I'll hear for the rest of my life whenever a tent shudders: the deep, patient groan of the icefield settling, like something enormous turning over in bed.

Around two in the morning, Petra crawled into my vestibule without asking, which she'd earned the right to do somewhere around the river crossing, and sat with her back against my pack.

"I keep thinking about the crevasse," she said. "About him falling and nobody hearing it. A whole week ago. Maybe two. He was down there the whole time we were walking around up here reading compasses."

"He didn't suffer long, likely. Cold takes people gently, once it commits."

"That's supposed to help?"

"No," I admitted. "Nothing helps. But I'm told the alternative to saying it is carrying it silently, and silent carrying is how guides end up strange."

She almost laughed. In the dark, in that wind, it was the bravest sound I heard all day.

"Desh. If it comes to it — if we're on the ridge and it's between the case and a person—"

"Don't ask me hypotheticals at two a.m., kid. I only do arithmetic in daylight."

But she didn't leave, and eventually she slept sitting up against my pack, and I sat awake doing the one calculation I'd been avoiding, the one that didn't fit in any column:

Two contractors. One liaison with a satellite phone and no scruples. Five of us, one of them injured, one of them twenty-four years old, one of them a scientist with a broken wrist guarding a box of worthless rock like it was a child. And somewhere ahead of us on the east ridge, a stolen radio, broadcasting who-knew-what to who-knew-whom, and a man named Marco Reyes who might be breathing.

The necessary choice wasn't east versus south. I'd understood that much by midnight. The necessary choice was coming later, on the ridge, in the weather, and it was going to require me to pick, in front of everyone, what kind of man I was — and then live in the country that choice built.

At first light, the wind dropped like a curtain.

August stood at the edge of camp facing the east ridge, and the clouds tore open just enough to show us the route: a white spine climbing into gray, beautiful and terrible, the only road home.

"Rope up," August said. "We're going over."

Behind us, I heard Voss say something low to Brandt that the wind took most of. What reached me was just the last word, carried backward down the drainage like a promise:

"—alone, then."
