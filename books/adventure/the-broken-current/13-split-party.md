## 13. Split Party

The morning the storm broke, I learned that a compass is just a needle with opinions.

We'd been camped in the lee of a moraine for eighteen hours while the katabatic wind screamed down off the icefield like it had a personal grudge against our tents. Nobody slept much. Desh's ribs were strapped with tape and stubbornness, Vasquez had eaten half a ration bar and declared herself "functional," which from her was practically a sonnet, and August hadn't slept at all — I could tell because their headlamp was still on at 0300, and because when I crawled out to pee at 0315 they were sitting on a rock in the dark, staring south toward a pass that no longer existed.

By dawn the wind had dropped to a mutter. The southern pass, the one we'd been aiming for since the snow cave, was gone — not closed exactly, but erased, filled with fresh slab and spindrift to a depth that made my professional opinion unprintable. August stood at the edge of camp with the map weighted down by two rocks and looked at it the way you look at a bill you can't pay.

"East ridge," Vasquez said. She said it quietly, but she'd been saying it in her head all night; you could see the words wearing grooves in her.

"The east ridge has a man on it," Voss said. He'd appeared out of the storm yesterday with his two contractors — Reyes and the big quiet one named only as Brandt — carrying rope and confidence in equal measure. "And whatever took his radio."

"The east ridge also has a route," August said. "The south has a cliff of rotten snow where a route used to be."

I was boiling water when the decision actually got made, which is how most decisions get made on a mountain — by someone standing apart from everyone else, doing math nobody else wants to do. I watched August trace the ridge line with one finger, then look up at Voss, then look away. There was a whole conversation in that looking-away. I'd been around long enough to read it: *you go your way, I'll go mine, and we'll both pretend this was always the plan.*

* * *

The split happened at 0740, announced like a weather briefing.

"Two parties," August said, crouched over the map so we all had to crouch too, breath fogging the plastic. "Voss takes Reyes and the low route — the glacier margin, down-valley, then west along the lateral moraine to the extraction point. Slower but sheltered. The rest of us take the east ridge. We rendezvous at the crossing, day after tomorrow, noon."

"Why?" Voss asked.

It was a fair question and August answered it like a professional, which is to say smoothly and without any of the truth in it. "If the ridge goes bad, someone needs to be at the extraction point with the radio. If the low route goes bad, we need to be able to come down to you. Redundancy." A pause. "Also, you wanted to push south. The low route lets you do that without taking Vasquez's injury into terrain she can't handle."

Voss looked at August for a long moment. I watched him decide to believe it, or rather to pretend to believe it, which on this mountain passed for the same thing. "Fine," he said. "But the case stays with me."

"The case stays with whoever can actually carry it out," August said. "That's us."

For a second I thought he was going to argue. His jaw did something complicated. Then Brandt shifted his weight behind him — just a shift, boots grinding on scree, nothing you could call a threat unless you'd spent your life reading bodies — and Voss smiled instead. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Day after tomorrow," he said. "Noon. Don't be late, Yarrow. The helicopter doesn't wait, and neither does the permit clock."

They went south along the moraine, three figures shrinking into the gray, and I remember thinking: *that's either the smartest thing anyone's done all week or the worst.* I genuinely could not tell which. That's the thing about command from the outside — it looks like clarity. From the inside, I'd learn, it looks like choosing which way to be wrong.

* * *

The first hour of the east ridge was the best hour we'd had in days, and I want to record that honestly, because what came after doesn't deserve to own all of it.

The sky had scrubbed itself blue-black and hard. The ridge ran northeast in clean steps of granite and old snow, wind-scoured, almost friendly. Desh moved well for a man with cracked ribs — slow, deliberate, breathing in that controlled way people breathe when every inhale costs them something and they've decided not to mention it. Vasquez climbed like a scientist: testing everything twice, trusting nothing, making surprisingly good time anyway. And August —

August climbed like someone who had finally stopped performing. I don't know how else to put it. For the whole trip up until then there'd been a version of August Yarrow on display, the disgraced guide doing penance in public, careful and ironic and slightly armored. Somewhere between the snow cave and the split, that person had been set down like an overloaded pack. What was left moved lighter.

I roped up behind them and let myself feel, briefly, the thing I'd felt on my very first alpine start at nineteen: that a mountain after a storm is the closest thing the world has to a clean slate.

Then we topped the first step of the ridge and saw the tracks.

* * *

They were a day old, maybe less. Boot prints in the wind-firmed snow, heading east ahead of us, and beside them a second set — larger, different tread, moving with the deliberate spacing of someone carrying weight. Two people. One of them had been dragging something for part of the way; there was a furrow between the prints for fifty meters, then it stopped, as if whatever had been dragged had been picked up, or dropped, or become unnecessary.

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind made its small comments.

"That's the surveyor," Vasquez said finally. Her voice had gone flat, the way voices go when feeling would be too expensive. "Tomas. He'd have come this way. Down-ridge toward the valley settlements." She pointed at the second track. "That's not his."

"No," August said.

"So who—"

"We'll find out," August said, and started walking, and that was the whole briefing.

I fell in behind them and did my own arithmetic, because somebody on this rope had to think about anything other than the obvious. Two sets of tracks meant the missing surveyor might be alive, which was the good news, and I held onto it the way you hold onto a good hold on bad rock — knowing it might not take your weight. The bad news came in several sizes. Size one: someone had reached Tomas before us, and had taken his radio, and radios on this mountain had recently become the most valuable objects in the world. Size two: the second set of prints was fresh enough that we weren't following a story. We were following someone who was still in it.

Size three I didn't write down, but I carried it anyway. It went like this: August had split the party within hours of learning that hostile people were on the mountain, and had sent the weakest group — Voss, one contractor, the low route — away from us, and had kept the strongest group, and the scientist, and the evidence, together. You could read that as strategy. You could also read it as a man deciding whose survival he was going to be responsible for, and quietly, without ceremony, subtracting everyone else from the list.

I looked at the back of August's hood and wondered which it was. I'm still wondering, some days.

* * *

We camped that night in a notch below the second step, four bodies wedged into space for two, and the loneliness of the thing arrived properly for the first time.

Here's what nobody tells you about being the junior person on a rope in a crisis: you are included in everything and consulted about nothing. August and Vasquez talked in low voices past midnight — I heard fragments, *checkpoint*, *relay*, *the journalist*, words that meant the fraud was still moving through August's hands like something hot — and Desh lay awake breathing in his careful metered way, and I lay awake listening to all of it and belonging to none of it.

I thought about calling out. Just saying it into the dark: *hey, I know you're awake, all three of you, and I know what you're deciding, and I'd like to be a person in this conversation instead of cargo.* I even had the sentence built. But there's a rule I learned young, watching guides operate: the moment you ask to be reassured, you become a load. And I did not want to be a load. So I lay there with my unsaid sentence and my cold feet and understood, finally, why August sat alone on rocks at three in the morning.

Command isn't a rank. It's a solitude you volunteer for, and once you've volunteered, everyone treats it as a service you're rendering, and no one — no one — asks what it's costing. I wasn't in command. I was twenty-six years old and the newest body on the rope. But lying in that notch, listening to other people make decisions that would decide whether I lived, I got a preview of it, and the preview was enough to last me.

Around two, August stirred. I kept my breathing even, the way you do, and felt more than saw them sit up, check the anchor, check the sky, check us — each of us, in turn, a long pause apiece. Auditing us. Counting us. Deciding things about us that we wouldn't be told.

Then they lay back down, and the mountain breathed, and I slept after all, which felt like a betrayal of something I couldn't name.

* * *

Morning came up gold and viciously clear, the kind of light that makes everything visible and nothing easier. We gained the second step by nine and the third by eleven, and at the top of the third step the ridge bent east around a shoulder of black rock, and there, four hundred meters below us in a shallow col, was a scrap of orange.

A tent. A one-person emergency bivvy, pitched badly, half-collapsed under rime. And next to it, small in the distance, a figure sitting upright against a rock, facing up-ridge. Facing us. As if he'd been waiting. As if he'd heard us coming hours ago and simply run out of the strength to wave.

"Tomas," Vasquez whispered, and her hand found my sleeve and gripped it hard enough to hurt, and I let it hurt, because it was the first honest thing anyone had touched in days.

August knelt, pulled out the binoculars, and was still for a long, long time.

"There's a radio," they said at last. "Next to him. It's not ours." A pause. "And there are fresh tracks going down the far side of the col. One set. Heading toward the crossing."

Toward the crossing. Where Voss would be in thirty-one hours. Where all of us were supposed to be, noon, day after tomorrow, with a case full of worthless rock and a truth worth millions.

August lowered the binoculars slowly. When they turned around, their face had done the thing faces do when a plan meets reality and reality wins — composed, but composed the way a fracture is set.

"Petra," they said. "You're with me. We go down fast. Desh, Vasquez — descend to us at your own pace and keep the rope tight." They looked east, down the far side of the col, at the single line of tracks marching toward everything we were trying to protect.

"Someone's about to get to the crossing before we do," August said. "And I need to know who, before Voss does."

We clipped into the rope and dropped into the col, into the wind, into whatever came next.
