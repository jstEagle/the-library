## 14. Signal Fire

The tracks were wrong, and I want that on the record.

Not wrong the way a liar's story is wrong — too neat, too smooth — but wrong the way a body is wrong when it's been walking on something it shouldn't be walking on. The stride was short. The left footprint dragged a little at the toe. Whoever made them was hurt, or exhausted, or both, and they'd been moving through terrain that would kill a healthy man in an hour without stopping to rest.

"Whoever this is," Petra said beside me, reading my face instead of the snow, "they're not one of Voss's contractors."

"No."

"Contractors walk like they're being paid by the step." She crouched, touched the edge of a print where the wind had already begun to sand it down. "This person is being paid in staying alive."

I didn't say anything. There was a version of me — the version who'd signed the silence clause with a pen he hadn't even flinched while holding — who would have said *then we turn around, we let the mountain keep its secret, we deliver the case and collect the check.* That version had been dying slowly since basecamp, and somewhere between the crevasse and the col, I think it finished.

We followed the tracks east.

* * *

The ridge narrowed until there wasn't a ridge anymore, just a spine of wind-carved ice with air on both sides and the kind of exposure that makes your hindbrain start writing letters of resignation. We moved roped, thirty feet apart, axes planted like punctuation. Below us to the north, the drainage we'd climbed for three days lay folded in blue shadow. To the south, the pass we couldn't take sat under its lid of storm cloud, sullen and closed.

Desh caught up to us at the second col, Vasquez breathing hard behind him on the rope. He looked at the tracks, then at me.

"You're not turning back," he said. It wasn't a question. Desh didn't ask questions he'd already decided the answers to; he just wanted you to say it out loud so the saying of it became yours.

"The trail leads somewhere," I said.

"Trails always do. That's their whole con."

"This one leads to a radio."

He raised an eyebrow — the good one, the one that still worked fully after the crevasse fall hadn't gotten him, only slowed him. "You've developed a belief system."

"I've developed a hypothesis." I nodded at the drag mark in the snow. "Somebody took Okafor's radio off his body. Somebody's been carrying it up this ridge, alone, injured, for days. You don't steal a radio to call for help and then climb *higher*."

Vasquez had gone very still behind Desh. When I glanced back at her, her face had the look of a woman watching a door she'd sealed years ago begin, very quietly, to open.

"You know who it is," I said.

She didn't answer. But her hand went to the strap of the sample case — the reflexive gesture, the one she made maybe forty times a day, the one I'd stopped counting — and that was answer enough.

* * *

We found the smoke at midafternoon.

Not real smoke — the wind wouldn't have permitted it — but the ghost of it: a thread of gray haze rising out of a fracture in the rock fifty meters ahead, thin as a hair, bending flat almost immediately. A signal fire built by someone who understood that on a ridge like this, you don't build a fire to get warm. You build it to be seen.

Petra saw it first. Of course she did. She'd been seeing things first since the compasses pointed wrong, and I'd stopped being surprised and started being grateful, which is how trust works when it happens fast and under pressure — you don't decide it, you just notice it's already there.

"Fire means he wants company," she said.

"Or bait."

"For what? He's got a stolen radio and a broken body." She looked at me. "August. Who walks uphill for three days on a busted leg to light a fire nobody's looking for?"

"Somebody broadcasting," I said, and the word landed in my own chest like a dropped tool.

Because that was the thing I'd been circling since the tracks, the hypothesis I hadn't said aloud, the shape forming under the snow of everything Voss had told us and everything that hadn't added up. Voss had appeared on this mountain twice now — once at basecamp with his helicopter and his silence clause, once at our camp with his two contractors and his story about how he'd "tracked our route from the air." Both times, he'd known exactly where to look. Both times, his story about *how* he knew had shifted slightly, the way stories do when they're load-bearing and the load keeps changing.

A helicopter doesn't find a man on a ridge by luck. A helicopter finds a man on a ridge because the man has been shouting into the sky for days.

* * *

The shelter was a fracture cave — a split in the ridge rock, maybe eight feet deep, roofed with a slab of stone that had wedged itself there in some ancient collapse and decided to stay. Someone had walled the mouth halfway with cut blocks of snow, leaving a gap for the fire's draft. Inside, in the orange light, a man sat propped against the wall with his right leg stretched out at an angle legs don't volunteer for, wrapped in a survey-team bivouac sack, holding a radio in both hands the way you hold a thing you've been guarding with your life.

Which, it turned out, was exactly what he was doing.

He saw us coming — of course he did, we'd been visible against the skyline for twenty minutes — and he didn't reach for anything. He just watched us approach with eyes that had gone past fear into some country beyond it, and when I ducked through the gap into the cave, he looked me up and down and said:

"You're not Consortium."

It wasn't a question either. Everyone on this mountain had stopped asking questions and started issuing verdicts.

"August Yarrow," I said. "Mountain guide. Currently employed by the people you clearly don't like, which puts us in an awkward position I'd rather resolve before introductions go any further."

The man laughed. It cost him — I watched the laugh travel down to the leg and come back as pain — but he laughed anyway, and there was something in the sound that undid a knot I'd been carrying since basecamp. Tents pitched. Food cached. Radios functional. No tracks leading anywhere. For six days I'd been walking through a mystery shaped like an absence, and here, in a hole in a rock at fourteen thousand feet, was the absence, alive, amused, and holding the missing piece.

"Tomas Reyes," he said. "Junior geologist, Serrano concession survey. And you're standing in the reason your employers are losing sleep."

* * *

We got him fed first. There are protocols even in the middle of revelations — melt snow, heat broth, check the leg, splint what's splintable — and following them bought all of us ten minutes to breathe before the truth arrived. His leg was a bad break, badly set, healing crooked in the cold. He'd walked — crawled, mostly — four kilometers upslope on it. When Desh heard that number he turned away and stood at the cave mouth for a long time, looking at the ridge, saying nothing, doing whatever veterans do with admiration that arrives attached to horror.

Then Tomas reached over, picked up the radio, and set it in my lap like a man handing over a child.

"Channel 6," he said. "Every night, nine o'clock, for eleven days. Open band. Plain language." He smiled, and there was frostbite scarring at the corner of his mouth that pulled the smile sideways. "I told anyone listening exactly what Elena found in those core samples. Exactly. Assay values, depths, coordinates. I figured if I was going to die on this rock, the least I could do was make sure the lie died with me. Loudly."

I looked down at the radio. An old field unit, government issue, the antenna replaced with a improvised whip of wire and tent pole. Scratched into the casing, in handwriting I recognized from the half-burned journal — patient, precise, a scientist's hand even in desperation — was a schedule. Nine p.m. Every night.

"That's how Voss found the mountain," I said.

"That's how Voss found the mountain," Tomas agreed. "First transmission, I got a reply inside an hour. Friendly voice. Said he was a journalist, said he'd been sitting on tips about the Halvard play for months, said keep broadcasting, help was coming." His laugh again, thinner this time. "Second night, the voice changed. Started asking questions journalists don't ask. Wanted to know where the physical samples were. Wanted to know if the case was intact. Wanted to know *exactly* where we'd cached it." He looked at Vasquez. "That's when I understood nobody was coming to print the story. They were coming to bury it. So I stopped answering direct questions and started transmitting the data itself, raw, every night, whether anyone was listening or not. Can't interrogate a frequency."

Vasquez had lowered herself onto a rock across from him, and the two of them looked at each other for a long moment — the leader and the junior, the woman who'd sent her team down the east ridge into a storm and the man who'd survived it alone for eleven days on a broken leg and a stolen radio and pure, weaponized spite.

"You sent us ahead," Tomas said to her. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, the way you'd place a core sample.

"I sent you ahead because the storm was coming and someone had to carry the truth out the other way," Vasquez said. "Redundancy. You scatter the evidence so no single disaster can erase it. It's the first rule of—"

"—of honest science. Yeah. I remember the lecture." He rubbed his face with a hand that shook from cold or exhaustion or eleven days of talking to the sky. "Okafor went into the crevasse two hours after we split. I couldn't get to him. I got his radio out because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because it was the only thing on this mountain that could still tell the truth, and I wasn't going to leave it in a hole."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the wind worked at the snow wall they'd built, patient as a creditor.

* * *

Here is the thing about vindication: nobody warns you it feels like this.

I had spent six days suspecting the math. The route that made no geological sense. The compasses pointing wrong. The silence clause. The case Voss wanted recovered more than he wanted the men who'd carried it. And every suspicion had been correct, every single one, and sitting there in the firelight holding a scratched-up radio, I felt absolutely nothing that resembled satisfaction. What I felt was arithmetic. Eleven days of broadcasts. Eleven nights some voice had answered, or tried to, or circled the signal like a shark testing a hull. Eleven days during which the fraud had been public knowledge to anyone with a receiver and the curiosity to listen — and the permits were still valid, the stock was still trading, the machine was still running, because a signal on an open band in the Serranos reaches exactly as far as the world's indifference allows.

Tomas had shouted the truth into the sky for eleven days, and the sky had sent him Merrick Voss.

"So the data's out there," Petra said slowly. She'd been quiet through all of it, sitting near the fire, feeding it slivers of fuel, listening the way she listened — completely. "Anyone could have recorded it."

"Anyone with a radio tuned to channel six at nine p.m. in one of the emptiest mountain ranges on the continent," Desh said. "So: no one."

"Not no one." Petra looked up. "Voss found him. Which means Voss's people monitor the band. Which means whoever Voss reports to monitors the band. Which means—" She stopped, and I watched her arrive at it, watched her face do the thing faces do.

"Which means the fraud may already be documented somewhere above Voss's head," Vasquez finished quietly. "In a file. In a recording. Sitting in some office in the city, waiting for someone to decide it's useful."

"Or waiting to be deleted," Desh said.

"Either way," I said, "it means the truth isn't only in this case anymore." I put my hand on the sample case between us, the sealed aluminum box that had cost Okafor his life and Desh nearly his and was going to cost all of us something before this was over. "And that changes what it's worth."

"To whom?" Vasquez asked.

"To everyone," I said. "Including us."

* * *

We stayed an hour. Long enough to splint the leg properly, to get calories into Tomas, to argue — in the low, fast shorthand of people who've learned that arguments on mountains must be brief or fatal — about what came next. He couldn't walk. Not really. Four kilometers of ridge descent on a crooked-healing break would finish what the storm started. Which meant carrying him, which meant slowing to a speed the weather would not forgive and Voss's contractors might not need.

"We leave at first light," I said finally. "Desh and I rig a litter. Petra takes point. Vasquez stays close to Tomas." I looked around the fire at each of them. "And we get off this ridge before the next front comes down, because the next front is going to be worse than the last one, and none of us are going to be alive to care about ore assays if it catches us at altitude."

"And the case?" Vasquez asked.

I looked at the radio in my lap. Then at the scratch-etched schedule on its casing. Nine p.m. Every night. Eleven days of a dead man's discipline, broadcasting into the void, and the void had answered with helicopters and contractors and a silence clause with my signature on it.

"It's nine-forty," I said.

They all looked at me.

"He hasn't missed a night in eleven days," I said. "We're not going to let tonight be the first." I held the radio out to Tomas. "Tell them what you always tell them. And then tell them something new — tell them the guide switched sides."

Tomas took the radio. His hands had stopped shaking. He keyed the transmit switch, and outside, above the cave, above the ridge, above the whole white indifferent architecture of the Serrano Range, the antenna began to speak.

And forty minutes later, from far to the south, faint but unmistakable across the open band, a voice answered — a voice I knew, smooth and unhurried and terribly patient, a voice that had shaken my hand at basecamp and meant none of it.

*"Yarrow,"* said Merrick Voss. *"We need to talk."*
