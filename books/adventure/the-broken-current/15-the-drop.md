## 15. The Drop

I've hung off enough cliffs in my life that the fear should have worn a groove of familiarity by now, something I could settle into like an old boot. It hasn't. Every time is the first time. That's what I tell the young ones when they ask — Desh, you've done this a hundred times, how do you not get scared? And I tell them: you get scared every time, or you stop paying attention, and the day you stop paying attention is the day the mountain files its paperwork on you.

So I was paying attention.

The east ridge had been grinding us down for two days. Wind out of the northeast with teeth in it, the kind that finds the gap between your collar and your neck and stays there like a debt collector. Vasquez was moving better than she had any right to, considering she'd spent weeks in a snow cave eating her own reserves, but she moved like a woman who'd made peace with whatever came next, and that's either courage or damage, and usually it's both. Petra was ahead of me on the rope, small and quick against the gray rock, and August brought up the rear with the sample case lashed to their pack like a coffin they'd agreed to deliver.

The case itself had been bothering me since the snow cave. Not because of what was in it — I didn't know what was in it, none of us did except Vasquez, and she wasn't talking — but because of what it *cost*. Everything we'd done since finding her, every decision, every argument, every mile of bad ground, all of it bent around that aluminum box like water around a stone. Voss wanted it. The Consortium wanted it. Eleven days of permits, a helicopter, two contractors with military posture and civilian smiles. You don't spend that kind of money chasing rocks unless the rocks are worth more than money, or worth less than anyone will admit.

I kept that thought folded up in my chest pocket where I keep the thoughts I'm not ready to read.

The rappel station sat at the top of a drop August called "the Step" — forty meters of near-vertical granite with a ledge at the bottom no wider than a kitchen table, then another pitch below that before the ridge relented into scree. Standard ground, technically. But the wind was doing twenty-five gusting forty, and the ledge was glazed with verglas where spindrift had melted and refrozen, and there were exactly two ways down: our ropes, or gravity's.

August checked the anchor twice, then a third time, which told me everything about how they felt about it.

"Petra first," August said. "Then Vasquez. Then me with the case. Desh, you're last — I want your eyes on everyone's descent."

"I love being the babysitter," I said.

"You're good at it."

"It's because I'm old," I said. "Old people are invisible. We just stand around judging."

Petra laughed — a short, tight sound, the laugh of someone who hadn't slept properly in three days — and racked her device onto the rope. She went over the edge smooth and controlled, feeding line through her brake hand, boots walking backward down the rock. Halfway down she stopped, and her voice came up thin through the wind.

"There's someone on the ledge."

Everything after that happened fast, the way bad things do, as if the world has been waiting politely for its cue.

* * *

He came up from below. That was the part my mind kept snagging on afterward — he came *up*, over the lip of the ledge like something surfacing, a big man in a gray shell jacket with his hood back and his face bare and calm, a face built for explaining things to people who had no choice but to listen. One of Voss's contractors. The one they called Reyes, though I'd have bet my last dollar it wasn't his name.

He had a hold of Petra's rope.

Not cutting it — cutting would be murder, and men like Reyes prefer deniability — but hauling on it from below, weighting it, pulling it out of line so that instead of hanging plumb against the rock, Petra swung out over the void like a bell struck sideways. Her brake hand locked. Her boots skated on the verglas looking for purchase that wasn't there.

"Desh!" August shouted, already moving toward the edge.

I was closer.

Here's the thing nobody puts in the brochures about mountain rescue: it's mostly arithmetic done at speed with incomplete information. Petra weighed maybe sixty kilos geared up. The pendulum was carrying her toward the left side of the ledge, where the rock overhung and there was nothing to land on but air. Reyes stood between her swing arc and the anchor, big as a door. If I went for Reyes, Petra hit the wall or missed the ledge entirely. If I went for the rope —

If I went for the rope, I could catch her. Maybe. If I anchored myself first, which took seconds I might not have, or if I trusted my own hands and boots and thirty years of catching falling things, which took nothing but faith in a body that had started sending me complaints about its working conditions somewhere around fifty-two.

I stepped off the stance and let the rope run.

Not a rappel. A controlled fall, boot edges biting, glove smoking on the line, closing the distance between me and her swing arc before physics finished its sentence. The world narrowed to rope and rock and the small bright figure of Petra Ilic arcing through gray air, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that still embarrasses me: *this is going to cost.*

She hit me at the bottom of her swing.

Or rather, I caught her — arms around her harness, my back slamming the rock, my right boot finding a seam and holding and then, with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard, not holding. Something in the leg went. Not broke — I know broke; broke has a specific signature, a white flare and a wrongness of geometry. This was worse in its way, quieter: a deep tearing give, like a rope core failing strand by strand under load, the sheath still holding but the strength gone out of it.

We swung together into the wall. My shoulder took the rock. Petra screamed — not fear, fury, the scream of a person being handled — and then we were both on the ledge, tangled, alive, and Reyes was standing over us with the wind pulling at his jacket and his hands empty and open, the way a man holds his hands when he wants witnesses to note that he's holding nothing.

"That was unnecessary," he said.

* * *

Pain is interesting company. It doesn't argue. It just states facts, over and over, in a flat voice, until you agree with it. My leg stated its facts while I got Petra untangled from me and pushed her behind my body, which was, I'll admit, more instinct than strategy — she was faster than me on one leg, probably faster than me on two.

August came down the rope like judgment arriving late, and even from the ground I could see them reading the scene the way I'd read it: Reyes, the ledge, the angle of Petra's rope, the arithmetic of what he'd been about to do and stopped just short of doing.

"He was pulling her off the wall," I said. "Your man here was winching her into the void like a fish."

"Adjusting a fouled line," Reyes said.

"On a glaze ledge. In a forty-knot gust." August crouched next to Petra, checked her hands, her harness, her eyes. Then they looked at the sample case, still lashed to their own pack, and I watched them arrive at the same conclusion I'd reached ten minutes earlier: this was never about stopping us. This was about the case. Petra was leverage or distraction or both, and we'd walked the whole length of the ridge straight into the geometry of it.

"The rest of your party is coming up the pitch below," Reyes said pleasantly. "Second contractor. So let's not make this complicated. The case comes with me. Everyone walks away. Well." He glanced at my leg without apology. "Mostly walks."

"And if we say no?" August asked.

Reyes smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on people with less altitude than us.

"Then it gets complicated," he said, "at altitude, which history suggests goes badly for everyone."

And that's when Vasquez did the thing that I will think about for the rest of my life, however long the rest of it turns out to be.

She unclipped the case from August's pack herself.

* * *

"No," August said.

"You want to know what's in it?" Vasquez said. She held the case out over the ledge. Forty meters of air below her, then scree, then the long gray fall of the mountainside. The wind tried to take her hood and she let it. "Eleven days you've carried it. He's killed for it — or nearly. Your whole contract bends around this box." Her voice was steady in the way that costs something. "So look."

She set it down on the ledge between us, knelt, and worked the latches. They were iced; she fought them with bare fingers, skin sticking to metal, and didn't seem to notice. Reyes shifted his weight — one step, half a step — and August moved between him and her, and for a moment the ledge held four people and a box and a silence so complete I could hear the wind thinking.

The latches gave.

Inside, packed in foam cut with government-neat precision, were core samples. Dozens of them. Gray-green cylinders of rock in labeled sleeves, each one tagged with a hole number and depth, each one exactly what a mineral survey is supposed to produce, the physical gospel of the Serrano concession.

Vasquez picked one up. Snapped it in half across her knee like kindling.

It broke along a seam of plain, worthless, barren country rock. No sulfides. No ore shimmer. Nothing but stone, the same stone I'd been walking over for two days, the same stone that makes up ninety-nine percent of every mountain range on earth.

"Fifteen holes," she said. "Every one of them dry. I logged them myself. And then the lab reports came back showing grades that would make a mining engineer weep, and I asked to see the assay certificates, and three days later my radio 'failed' and my team was ordered down the east ridge ahead of a storm that hadn't arrived yet." She dropped the broken half. It clicked on the rock and rolled to the ledge's edge and stopped, teetering. "There's no mine here. There never was. There's a stock price, and this—" she nudged the case with her boot— "is the prop."

Reyes hadn't moved. That was the thing I noticed, the thing that mattered: he hadn't moved to stop her. Because he already knew. Of course he knew. Men like him don't get sent up mountains to guard secrets from the help; they get sent to manage the help. The case was never the treasure. The case was the *evidence*, and evidence is only dangerous when it reaches people who can use it.

Which meant the plan had just changed, and we were all of us standing inside the new one without having been consulted.

"Put it back together," Reyes said quietly. "We're taking it down. All of you. The helicopter's at the col."

"And if the case takes a fall on the way?" August asked. "Accidents happen. You said so yourself — complicated, at altitude."

Something flickered behind Reyes's calm then, the first honest thing on his face all day. Not fear exactly. Calculation discovering its limits.

Above us, at the anchor, a rope end whipped in the wind. The second contractor, coming up the pitch. And below us, somewhere past the scree, the weather was turning — I could feel it in the pressure the way you feel a storm in an old fracture, that deep ache before the sky commits.

My leg had stopped stating its facts and moved on to demands. I put my weight on it anyway, slow, testing, and it held the way a bad rope holds — technically, temporarily, with a story attached.

Petra crouched beside me. Her hands were shaking now, delayed shock arriving on schedule, and her voice came out low, meant only for me.

"You caught me."

"Part of the service," I said.

"It cost you."

I looked at my leg, then at the broken core sample lying at the lip of the ledge, half of the truth of it rolled right to the edge where one gust would take it down the mountain to wherever truths go when nobody catches them.

"Everything does," I said. "Question is whether it bought anything."

Below us, the second contractor's helmet rose over the lip of the pitch — and behind him, far down the ridge, the first flakes of the storm came slanting in out of the white.
