## 16. What Loyalty Costs

Desh weighed more than a man should. That was my first thought, and it was a coward's thought, because what I actually meant was: *Desh weighs more than I can carry, and here we are anyway.*

He was conscious by then — mostly. The fall had opened his left thigh from hip to knee in a way that made Petra go quiet, and Petra does not go quiet. We'd splinted what could be splinted, bound what could be bound, and stuffed the rest of our first aid into wounds that drank it like sand drinks water. Vasquez had done the field surgery with the calm of a woman who'd once stitched her own hand on a glacier at four thousand meters, narrating each step to Petra in a low teaching voice, as if the lesson were the point and not the bleeding.

"Pressure's holding," Vasquez said, for the third time in an hour. She said it the way you say *the rope is holding* over a crevasse — not as information but as prayer with a stethoscope.

"Good," I said, and adjusted Desh's arm across my shoulders, and took another step.

The extraction point sat nine kilometers downvalley, a flat gravel bar beside the glacial river where a helicopter could theoretically land if the weather cooperated and the pilot felt brave. Nine kilometers. With a two-hundred-pound man on a litter between four people, one of them half-starved after three weeks alone on a mountain, one of them a junior guide who hadn't slept in thirty hours, and me, whose body had been filing complaints since the second day and had recently escalated to formal grievance.

We moved at maybe a kilometer an hour. Do the arithmetic. I did, constantly, the way you tongue a broken tooth.

* * *

The radio crackled at noon.

I had it clipped to my pack strap, Voss's frequency dialed in because not knowing what he was saying had become worse than hearing it. For six hours he'd been silent, which I'd told myself was good news. Silence meant he wasn't watching us. Silence meant the contractor — Sable, the one who'd put Desh on the ledge — had come back down off the ridge empty-handed or hadn't reported in.

Silence, it turned out, meant he'd been waiting until we were committed. Committed to the valley floor, committed to the river corridor, committed to a route with exactly one exit and no branches. You learn to recognize that kind of patience. It's the patience of a man who has stopped negotiating and started scheduling.

"Yarrow." His voice came through clean, almost warm, which was somehow worse than static. "Give me a position."

I let the silence stretch. Beside me, Petra's head turned a few degrees — she'd heard, of course; the thing broadcast like a town crier — but she kept walking, steadying the litter's front rail.

"Yarrow. I know you can hear me. Your radio's been squelching on my channel since the ridge."

"Reading you," I said finally. "Weak and scratchy. Say again?"

"Don't." A pause, and when he came back the warmth had been stripped out like insulation from a wall. "Here's where we are. The helo is fueled and on station at the south strip. Pilot's mine. Weather window closes tomorrow, eighteen hundred. After that, nothing flies for seventy-two hours minimum, and your friend on the litter doesn't have seventy-two hours. You know it. I know it."

Desh stirred against my shoulder. He'd drifted in and out all morning, surfacing with little jokes that fooled nobody. Now his eyes were open, fixed on the middle distance, listening.

"So bring me the case," Voss said. "Bring the data cards. Bring Vasquez's journal, whatever pages survived the fire. You do that, and everyone walks onto that helicopter, including Okonkwo. You don't, and I make a call, and the pilot makes a different call, and you spend three days on that gravel bar watching him bleed out while the storm decides how this ends. That's not a threat, Yarrow. That's a weather report."

"You're a real poet, Merrick."

"I'm a man with a helicopter and you're a man with a problem. Position?"

I gave him a position. It was true, roughly, and there was no point lying about it — he'd tracked us this far, and a lie would only buy me the particular pleasure of being caught. Then I keyed off and walked, and the weight on my shoulders got heavier without gaining a gram.

* * *

We halted at fourteen hundred in the lee of a moraine ridge, and I called a council of war that was really just four exhausted people sitting in the dirt passing a thermos lid of water.

Vasquez spoke first. Of course she did. Three weeks alone in a snow cave will strip a person of every social grace except conviction.

"He's bluffing about the timeline," she said. "Partially. The window is real — I've watched these systems build for twenty years. But he won't leave us. Not with witnesses scattered across a mountain who've seen his contractor try to kill people. Dead guides generate inquiries. Inquiries generate subpoenas. Voss survives on the absence of paper."

"And if he decides dead guides are cheaper than subpoenas?" Petra asked. She wasn't being dramatic. She was asking a logistics question, which is the most frightening way to ask it.

"Sable already tried once," Vasquez said. "On the ledge. That wasn't improvisation."

"No," I said. "It wasn't."

Everyone looked at me. I looked at the river, running gray and muscular below us, hauling its freight of ground-up mountain toward the sea without any opinion about any of us.

"Here's the shape of it," I said, because someone had to say it and I was apparently still the person they'd decided that was. "If I give him everything — case, data, journal — he flies us out, and the fraud dies quietly in some consortium archive, and the next survey crew up here maps a worthless ore body with honest instruments and dishonest numbers, and somewhere downstream a pension fund eats the loss. If I refuse, we gamble four lives on a bluff I'm maybe sixty percent sure is a bluff, and I'm gambling with money that isn't mine."

"It's not a bluff," Vasquez said flatly.

"Sixty percent," I repeated. "And the forty percent has a helicopter in it."

Petra turned the thermos lid in her fingers. "There's a third option," she said carefully. "You give him something. Part of it. Buy the flight with the case — the rock itself, which is worthless — and hold back the data cards and the journal."

"And he discovers the cards are missing before we're wheels-up," Vasquez said, "and then we're on a helicopter with a man who has no further reason to keep us alive."

"He needs us alive," Petra said. "You said it yourself. Witnesses."

"Witnesses are only expensive until they're not."

I listened to them argue and felt something in my chest I recognized with real disgust: relief. Because they were arguing about the *data*, about principle, about truth and fraud and the public interest, and none of them had said the thing that was actually sitting in the middle of our circle wearing my face.

The thing was this: I could end Desh's suffering with a signature. My signature. The same hand that had signed Voss's silence clause in a heated tent eleven days ago, telling myself it was pragmatism, telling myself everybody signs things, telling myself the clause probably didn't even mean anything. One more signature now — *yes, Merrick, everything's coming* — and the helicopter comes, and Desh lives, and Petra lives, and Vasquez lives, and the only thing that dies is the truth, which has never once bled out on a mountainside that I'm aware of.

That's the corrupting arithmetic of other people's survival. Nobody warns you about it in guide school. When it's your own life on the line, you can be brave, because bravery is cheap when you're the only creditor. But put a dying man on a litter and a sealed case in the equation, and suddenly courage costs exactly what Desh weighs, and you find out what your principles weigh too, and it is never enough.

"Desh," I said. "You've been quiet."

His eyes found me. They were clear, which was worse than foggy. Foggy men don't understand their options.

"I heard most of it," he said. His voice was gravel and distance. "For the record — and stop looking at me like that, Yarrow, I'm lucid, I'm just tired — for the record: don't you dare trade it for me."

"Desh—"

"I've spent thirty years on mountains," he said. "I've buried friends who died for dumber reasons than a stock scam. If you hand that man the evidence so I can limp through another decade of bad knees and ex-wives, I will haunt you specifically and personally, and I am very annoying as a ghost. Ask anyone."

"That's touching," Vasquez said dryly, "but he's also wrong. Nobody dies for my data. That was never the deal I offered any of you."

"The deal," I said, "was that I get everyone home alive. That was the whole contract. Everything else was decoration." I stood up. My knees filed their complaint. "So here's what happens. We keep moving. Nobody decides anything until we see the gravel bar and whatever's sitting on it. And Merrick gets his answer at eighteen hundred tomorrow, not one minute sooner, because every hour we hold is an hour he has to wonder."

"And what do you tell him in the meantime?" Petra asked.

I keyed the radio. "Merrick. Yarrow. Case and data are intact and moving toward you. I'll confirm delivery at the LZ. Don't call this number again unless the helo's rotors are turning."

A long pause. Then, softly, almost kindly: "Good man, Yarrow. Knew you'd get there eventually."

I turned the radio off and did not look at anyone, because I had just told a lie to save time, and the terrible part — the part I'll carry longer than the cold or the fear — is how easy it was. How natural. Like a compass needle finding north.

Except my north was pointing somewhere wrong, and I knew it, and I kept walking anyway.

* * *

We made five kilometers by dusk. Five. Four to go, and Desh's pulse thready under Vasquez's fingers, and the sky in the west going the bruised color that means the weather report Voss gave me was the one honest thing he'd said all week.

We camped in a hollow above the river, no fire, hot water bottles improvised from canteens packed around Desh's leg. Petra took first watch. I lay next to the litter and stared at the underside of the tarp and listened to Desh breathe — shallow, fast, the respiration of a body spending reserves it didn't have.

Around midnight he surfaced enough to talk, and I pretended to be asleep, and then didn't, because pretending had gotten heavy too.

"August." Just my name. First time he'd used it.

"Yeah."

"When you were blacklisted — after Kessler's party — was it true? What they said?"

I considered lying to a man with a hole in his thigh, and found I couldn't. "Some of it. I made a call in whiteout conditions that got two clients frostbitten. The part they left out was that the client overrode my call and I let him, because he was paying, and because I wanted the season's bonus. So yes. True enough. I chose the money over the mountain's opinion, and the mountain billed me later."

"And now?"

"Now I'm choosing again," I said, "and I notice it's the same choice with better lighting."

He was quiet a while. The river ran. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the scree with a sound like slow applause.

"You know what I figured out," Desh said, "lying on this thing all day with nothing to do but think? Loyalty isn't a thing you owe. Everybody talks like it's a debt — loyalty to the client, loyalty to the outfit, loyalty to the guy who signs your checks. But it's not a debt. It's a direction. You pick what you're loyal *to*, and then you walk that bearing, and everything else is just terrain."

"And if the bearing runs through a helicopter I can't afford?"

"Then you walk it," he said, "and you let the people who love the truth carry it the last stretch. That's what she's for." He nodded, barely, toward Vasquez, asleep against her pack with the sample case strapped to her chest like a child. "You don't have to be the one who delivers it, August. You just have to be the one who doesn't destroy it."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't either a promise or a confession.

But I lay there in the dark doing the arithmetic one more time, and for the first time in eleven days the numbers came out different. Not braver — I want to be clear about that, because the story people will tell later shouldn't have me growing a spine at midnight. No. What changed was simpler and colder: I finally understood that Voss had never held the power in this transaction. He had a helicopter. I had a choice. And a man with a choice, standing on a gravel bar with nothing left to lose but other people's contempt, is the most dangerous animal on this mountain.

I looked at Vasquez, asleep with the truth strapped to her heart. I looked at the radio, dark and patient on my pack strap.

Tomorrow at the LZ, Merrick Voss was going to get exactly what I'd promised him.

I just hadn't promised him which parts were true.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and hard, wind out of the south, the storm's outriders already combing the ridgelines. Four kilometers to the gravel bar. Desh's fever had climbed in the night; Vasquez dosed him with the last of the antibiotics and didn't meet anyone's eyes while she did it, which told me what the dose meant.

We broke camp in silence. I shouldered my end of the litter. Petra took hers. And we started down the last stretch of valley, toward the river, toward the flat gray stone where salvation was supposedly parked with its rotors turning — four people walking a bearing I hadn't yet admitted I'd chosen, carrying a dead man's worth of rock and a live woman's worth of proof, and behind us, faint on the wind, the first flakes of the storm that would decide whether any of my arithmetic mattered at all.

At the bend above the gravel bar, I raised the binoculars.

The helicopter was already there.

And so was Voss — standing alone on the stones, hands in his pockets, looking up-valley directly at us, as if he'd known exactly when we'd crest the rise.

Waiting. Smiling.

Holding something in his hand that caught the light.

It glinted orange. Survey tape.
