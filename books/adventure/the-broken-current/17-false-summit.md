## 17. False Summit

I have spent thirty years learning to read rock, and I can tell you this: the most dangerous thing on a mountain is not the ice or the wind or the crevasse waiting under a bridge of snow like a bad joke with no punchline. The most dangerous thing is a person who has decided what they're willing to lose.

I recognized it in August Yarrow the way you recognize a fault line — not because anything's moving yet, but because you can see where everything *will* move, given pressure and time.

It was the morning after Desh caught Petra at the rappel station, after the case cracked open on the ledge and spilled its worthless gray heart across the ice for all of us to see. Desh lay in the tent with his leg splinted and his color wrong, refusing morphine on the grounds that he wanted his head clear, which was either admirable or insane, and knowing Desh it was both. Petra hadn't spoken since the fall except to check his pulse every twenty minutes like she was winding a clock. And August sat outside in the wind with the radio, listening to Merrick Voss explain, in the patient tone of a man describing terms to a slow child, exactly how we were all going to die if August didn't cooperate.

I stayed inside the tent. Not out of cowardice. Out of arithmetic. There were things I needed to do that required my hands steady, and listening to Voss's voice did not keep them steady.

"—the helicopter lifts from the lower glacier at fourteen hundred tomorrow," Voss was saying, tinny through the speaker. "It will be there for ninety minutes. If the case and the data are aboard, everyone flies out. If they are not—" A pause, artfully constructed. You could hear him smiling through it. "Then I'm afraid the weather window closes, Ms. Yarrow. For all of you."

"You've made that point," August said. Flat. Tired. I'd been listening to that voice for days now, and I had catalogued its registers the way I catalogued strata: the command voice, the doubt voice, the voice August used when lying to herself. This was none of those. This was something new. This was a voice with nothing left in it but weight.

"Then we understand each other."

"We understand each other," August repeated.

And I heard it — God help me, I heard it — the small surrender in the repetition. The sound of a person signing something they shouldn't. I knew that sound intimately. I had heard it in boardrooms in Denver when the core logs came back thin and some vice president of exploration suggested, gently, that perhaps the assay lab had made an error, and wouldn't it be prudent to re-run the numbers, and by the way here's a bonus structure that might make re-running them very attractive indeed. Six people signed. Five of them went home rich. One of them sent her team down an east ridge into a storm and stayed behind with a box of rocks.

You can guess which one I was.

* * *

August came into the tent without announcing it, which was unlike her. August announced things. Announced rope lengths, weather windows, rest stops, the exact number of minutes remaining before a decision became someone else's problem. Command as a form of courtesy. But she ducked through the flap silent as weather, crouched beside me where I sat against the pack wall, and put something in my hands before I could speak.

The journal pages. Burned at the edges, soft as old leaves. And beneath them, folded twice, the real core log — my own handwriting staring back at me from three months ago, the assays I'd recorded before anyone suggested they might be more useful *reinterpreted*.

"I kept them separate from the case," August said quietly. "Since the ledge."

Petra was outside with Desh, close enough to hear raised voices, far enough — I checked — not to hear this. The stove ticked. Somewhere above us the wind worked at the ridge like a patient sculptor who hated the mountain and had all the time in the world.

"You're giving these to me," I said. It wasn't a question, but I made it one anyway, because I needed to hear the answer in a human voice rather than in the weight of paper.

"I'm agreeing to Voss's terms," August said. "Case, data, handoff at the glacier. That's happening. He'll get his package." She looked at the pages in my hands. "He'll get the package. What he doesn't get is the only copy of anything that matters. Those were never in the case. They were never in my pack. As of right now they're in yours, and I don't know where they are, and neither does anyone else."

"Yarrow." I turned the pages over. My own field notes, the ones I'd burned copies of in the snow cave the night I understood nobody was coming up for me — burned them so that if Voss's contractors found me first, they'd find ash and a dead woman and no story. Except I'd kept the originals, because a scientist keeps originals, because burning the truth entirely felt like letting them win twice. "If you hand off the case and the log copies and walk onto that helicopter, you're clean. You're the loyal contractor who delivered. You get your name back. You get the industry back. That was the whole — that's what you told me you wanted, that night at the cave. Redemption."

Something moved across August's face. Not pain exactly. More like the moment a climber realizes the route they've been on for hours doesn't connect to the summit — that all that effort was real, and the map was wrong.

"I did want it," August said. "For about eleven days I wanted it more than air. And then I watched Desh catch Petra off a ledge for a box of gravel, and I did the math I should have done in Halvard's office." She almost smiled. It didn't take. "There's no version of this where I hand Voss the truth and he lets it stay true. He'll bury it under six feet of legal paper and I'll spend the rest of my career being the guide who found Dr. Vasquez and asked no questions. That's not redemption. That's just a longer leash."

"So don't hand it to him."

"I am going to hand it to him. All of it. Exactly as agreed." Her eyes came up and held mine, and I saw the thing I'd been circling for days finally land. "Because if I don't, he strands us. And if I try to run with it, he hunts us, and Desh can't run, and I won't trade Desh's leg or Petra's life for a headline. So I pay him. I pay him with the fake truth, the worthless copy, the performance of loyalty." A breath. "And you carry the real thing down the mountain in your pocket, and you don't tell me where it is, and if it goes wrong — if he searches us, if it goes wrong at the glacier — then it was never mine. I never saw it. You acted alone, a delirious half-starved scientist who stole documents from her own survey, and I, tragically, failed to supervise you."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's a *workable* plan. Terrible plans are the only kind that survive contact with mountains."

I laughed. I shouldn't have — Desh was hurt, the storm was building, and somewhere below us a man with two contractors and a radio was planning our futures like line items. But I laughed, because it was the first honest sentence anyone had spoken to me in four months, and honesty does that to you when you've been rationing it.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why not transmit it yourself from the relay, like—"

"Like the second surveyor? Because the relay's compromised, because Voss monitors frequencies, and because anything I carry, I can be searched for." She stood, brushing snow from her knees, businesslike again, the window closing. "You're the one person on this mountain Voss has already written off. He thinks you're cargo. Damaged, half-dead cargo. Nobody frisks the cargo." At the tent flap she paused, and for a second the command voice dropped away and there was just a tired person in a cold tent. "Elena. I might not make it to the helicopter. You should know that's a real possibility. I've done the arithmetic on the handoff about forty ways and every version has a branch where it goes bad, and in most of those branches I'm the one standing closest to Voss when it does."

"And you're still doing it."

"I'm still doing it." She said it simply, the way you'd confirm a bearing. "So those pages aren't a gift. They're a contingency. If I come down that glacier tomorrow, you give them back and we argue about press strategy like civilized people. If I don't—" She shrugged. "Then you were always going to tell the story anyway. I'm just making sure you can."

She went out. The flap fell. I sat with the burned pages in my lap and the stove ticking and thought about faith.

I lost my faith in institutions a long time ago — that's easy, that's practically a career requirement in geology, you watch enough beautiful hypotheses die against ugly data and you stop believing in tidy stories. But I'd kept a private, unexamined faith in something smaller: that people were mostly legible. That you could assay a person the way you assay ore, run enough tests, and know what was actually in them. I had August Yarrow assayed as a mercenary with a conscience, a woman who'd signed the silence clause and hated herself in exact proportion to how much she needed the money. Predictable. Contained.

And here she was handing me the one thing that could destroy her, asking nothing, guaranteeing nothing, walking out into the wind to go lie to a dangerous man on behalf of people who would never know what it cost.

Faith extended without guarantee. That's the only kind that counts, I suppose. The guaranteed kind isn't faith; it's accounting.

* * *

We broke camp at first light, and the mountain gave us its opinion of our plans immediately: spindrift smoking off the ridgelines, the sky the color of dirty pewter, that particular pressure-drop smell in the air that every mountaineer learns to dread and every mountaineer ignores at least once.

Desh insisted on walking. Of course he did. We rigged a haul line for him and he used it like a suggestion, planting his good leg and swearing with tremendous creativity and precision, and Petra hovered at his flank pretending to manage the rope while actually managing her terror of him falling, and between the two of them they produced a system that worked, which is the definition of marriage or expedition travel, I forget which.

August led. August always led, but today there was something different in the set of her shoulders — the difference, I thought, between a person climbing away from something and a person climbing toward it. Same motion. Completely different animal.

At the last high col before the glacier dropped away toward the extraction point, we stopped, and I got my first look at the false summit.

Every range has one. A shoulder of rock that reads, from below, as the top — that promises relief, horizon, an end to effort. And every climber learns it eventually: you crest the false summit and discover the real one is higher, farther, separated from you by a stretch of ground harder than anything you've crossed to get there. The cruelest trick terrain plays is honesty. It never lies about where things are. It just lets your hope do the cartography.

Voss's voice crackled in on schedule, checking our position, confirming the helicopter, confirming the terms. August answered in the flat surrendered voice, coordinates and estimates, a loyal contractor delivering the goods. I stood ten feet away with the real core log pressed flat against my ribs inside my parka, over my heart like a bad metaphor, and listened to the two of them conduct their transaction across nine thousand feet of killing cold, each believing they were buying the other.

When the radio went dead, August looked back down the line at us — at Desh gray-faced and grinning anyway, at Petra with her jaw set, at me — and held my eyes for exactly one second longer than coordination required.

*Carry it,* that look said. *Whatever happens next, carry it.*

Below us the glacier spilled white and blue toward the lower valley, and somewhere down there a river ran under the ice, the broken current that fed everything, and I remember thinking — with the calm of a person who has finally put down something heavy — that whatever happened at the handoff, the truth was no longer on the mountain.

It was walking down it, in the pocket of a woman everyone had counted out.

August turned and led us onto the glacier, and the wind rose to meet us like it had been waiting.
