## 18. The Broken Current

There's a particular kind of quiet that comes before a decision you can't take back. Not peace — I want to be clear about that. Peace is what you feel when the choice is already made and your body has caught up to it. This was the other thing: the held breath of the mountain, the glacier-fed river running gray-green with rock flour, and me standing at its edge doing arithmetic with other people's lives.

The river came down from the Serrano icefield in three braided channels, maybe forty meters of moving water total, cold enough to stop a heart in ninety seconds and strong enough to make that a formality. On our side: me, Vasquez, Petra, Desh on a litter made from a tent pole and two sleeping pads, and the sample case, which Desh had cracked open on the ledge two days ago to reveal exactly what Vasquez promised — rock with all the economic value of gravel from a mall parking lot. On the far side: the valley, the relay mast, the extraction meadow, and the helicopter that Voss had promised would come only if the case came to him intact.

Voss was forty minutes behind us with his remaining contractor, a slab of a man named Krieg who communicated mostly through the angle of his shoulders. Voss's voice had been on the radio all morning, smooth as a sales floor.

*August. I know you can hear me. The case crosses with you, or nobody crosses. I have the only extraction. Think about your people.*

He'd been saying some version of that for two days, and the terrible thing — the thing I'd had to sit with through two nights of storm-battered camp — was that he was right. He did have the extraction. He'd bought the bush pilot, he'd filed the flight plan, and he'd made sure that every other option out of the Serrano ended in a body recovery operation. That's what money does when it gets cornered. It doesn't roar. It just quietly buys all the doors.

Except Voss had one problem, and the problem was me.

* * *

Here's what I knew, and when I'd known it.

Back at the abandoned basecamp, Petra had noticed the compasses all pointed wrong — fifteen degrees off true, every one of them, including the brand-new spares in the survey team's own kit. We'd spent a chapter of this disaster assuming it was some local magnetic anomaly, some buried iron seam, the mountain lying to us the way the mountain lies to everyone eventually.

It wasn't the mountain. It was Vasquez.

She'd confessed it on the east ridge, in the wind, in the flat voice of a woman who had decided honesty was the last tool left in her kit. The survey team had magnetized the needles themselves. Deliberately. Before the storm, before everything went wrong, Vasquez's team had altered their own instruments — a small, elegant act of sabotage, a physical record that any competent navigator would eventually catch. Proof that the survey was theater. That the "promising anomalies" Voss's consortium had paid the government to confirm were drawn onto the map by hand.

The compasses didn't point north. They pointed at the lie.

And Voss, who had come up this mountain with a falsified route plan and two contractors and a helicopter on retainer, had been navigating by those same instruments since he landed. I'd watched him do it. I'd watched him take a bearing off his compass at the last camp, and I'd watched the needle sit fifteen degrees fat of true, and I'd said nothing, because at that point I still thought I was going to hand him the case and buy my people's way off this rock with my silence.

That was before Desh. Before the ledge. Before I'd spent a night listening to a man who'd carried Petra eighty meters of vertical ice with a dislocated shoulder describe, in a voice slurred with morphine from our last shared kit, why he'd done it. He hadn't said anything noble. He'd said, *She was falling, August. There wasn't a decision.* And I'd lain there in the dark realizing that Desh had just described the only kind of loyalty worth having — the kind that doesn't check the contract first.

I'd spent eleven years checking the contract first. The silence clause. The debt. The blacklist. Every compromise dressed up as pragmatism, every betrayal wearing the costume of necessity. And here was the thing I understood now, standing at the gray water with the cold coming up off the ice in sheets:

Voss was going to get everyone across that river, and then he was going to make sure the truth never crossed with them. Not because he was evil in the way villains are evil in books — because he was afraid in the way powerful men are afraid, which is to say completely and without any practice at it. The case was worthless rock. The case had always been worthless rock. The only thing of value on this entire mountain was the data Vasquez carried in a waterproof sleeve against her ribs, and the only thing standing between that data and a shredder was me.

So. The river.

* * *

I'd spent the morning doing what I used to be paid for, back when my name meant something on a permit application: reading terrain.

The braided channels shifted every season, and this one had shifted recently. The main channel, the deep one, ran hard against the far bank. But the near channel — the one any crossing party would hit first — was different. The glacier had calved a section of ice shelf into the valley sometime in the last month, and the flood had rafted plates of it against the near gravel bar, then frozen them in overnight and thawed them loose again. From a distance it looked like a bridge. Gray ice over gray water, textured like the surrounding scree, a tempting ramp leading out toward the middle channel.

It was a lie made of water. Those plates were a meter thick at best, sitting on moving current, undercut at every seam. A hundred kilos on the wrong plate and the whole raft would go like a dropped chandelier. I'd seen it take a horse in Patagonia. The horse was a rumor by the time it surfaced.

Nobody would cross there on purpose. But if your compass was lying to you — if you took your bearing off a needle that pointed fifteen degrees east of truth, and you trusted it the way Voss trusted everything he'd paid for — you wouldn't come down to the river at the safe gravel bar at all. You'd come down fifty meters upstream, right where the ice raft fanned out like a welcome mat.

I'd tested it the night before, alone, in the dark, with Petra on watch pretending not to watch me. I'd taken a bearing on the far-side cairn using one of the altered compasses, walked it down to the water, and marked where it put me. Then I'd walked the true line. Fifty-two meters apart. Fifty-two meters between a safe crossing and a death trap that looked, from above, like a shortcut.

The mountain had been lying to us since basecamp. I'd just decided it was finally time to let it lie to Voss.

* * *

"You're quiet," Vasquez said. She crouched beside Desh's litter, adjusting the pad under his shoulder with hands that shook from cold and hunger and eleven days of conviction. She'd lost twenty pounds guarding a box of worthless rocks, and she'd do it again, and that was the most frightening and admirable thing about her.

"I'm thinking."

"Voss is forty minutes out. Maybe less. Krieg walks like he's being paid by the step."

"He is."

She looked at me. Elena Vasquez had a face built for interrogation rooms — flat, patient, impossible to read unless she wanted you to. She read mine easily enough. "You've decided something."

"I've decided the river's high."

"August."

"Here's what happens." I crouched, drew in the gravel with a stick, because some part of my brain still believed plans needed a map even when the map was dirt. "We cross at the true bar. Single file, rope on, the route I flagged this morning with the orange tape — you saw the tape, don't ask me why it's there, it's there. We get Desh across, we get up the moraine, we're at the relay by fourteen hundred. You transmit everything you've got to whoever's listening on the valley repeater, and then it doesn't matter what Voss does, because the story's out of the bottle."

"And Voss?"

"Voss takes a bearing off his compass." I tapped the dirt. "And his compass takes him onto the ice."

The silence lasted three seconds, which on that riverbank felt geological.

"You're going to kill him," Petra said. She'd come up quiet, the way she did, and I still hadn't learned to hear her coming. She was looking at the ice raft with the expression of someone doing the same math I'd done and arriving at the same ugly sum.

"No," I said. "The ice is going to put him in the water, and the water is going to give him a lesson he may or may not survive, and Krieg — Krieg is big enough and mean enough to probably pull him out, because Krieg's contract doesn't pay out if Voss drowns. I'm not killing anyone. I'm just... not correcting their navigation."

"That's a very fine distinction," Vasquez said.

"I've had eleven days to get fine."

"It's not a distinction at all," Petra said, and there was no judgment in it, which was somehow worse. "You're choosing where he walks. You know what you're doing."

"I know exactly what I'm doing." I heard my own voice come out flat and cold and recognized it as the voice I used on clients when the weather turned — the voice that said *I have this under control* whether or not it was true. Except this time it was true. That was the strange part. Eleven days of improvisation, of lies of omission, of signing things I shouldn't have signed, and this — this ugly, calculated, unforgivable thing — was the first decision I'd made on this mountain that wasn't bought by someone else's money.

"You could warn him," Vasquez said quietly. "Flag the safe route for everyone. Let him cross it. Take the case across, hand it over, and hope the data gets out some other way."

"Hope isn't a route out of the Serrano."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She looked at the water for a long moment. "I spent six weeks watching that company falsify a survey that governments will use to lease land, and every day I told myself I'd fix it through channels, through the report, through the proper process. The proper process is what put me in a snow cave eating my own boot laces." She stood. "Do it."

"You don't get a vote," I said. "None of you do. That's the point of command — it means the ugly decisions are yours alone, and so are the consequences."

"Then I'm exercising my right to say I'd have made the same one," Vasquez said. "Move. He's closing."

* * *

We crossed the way I'd flagged it, and it was ugly and it was perfect.

The true bar was honest water — knee-deep in the near channel, waist-deep and fast in the middle, and I put the strongest bodies upstream of the weakest and we leaned into the current like a picket fence. Petra took Desh's litter across with me, the two of us walking it high, water slamming our thighs, and I heard Desh — morphine-fogged, shoulder wrecked, half frozen — start laughing somewhere in the middle channel, a raw whooping laugh, and Petra yelling at him to shut up and hold on, and I remember thinking: *this. This is what I sold. This is what the silence clause was priced against.* Not the money. The moment when people hold a line together in cold water and laugh about it after.

We came up the far bank and didn't stop. Up the moraine, Desh on a rope-haul between Petra and me, Vasquez carrying the case — the worthless case, the rock that had started a fraud and a manhunt and a body count — because Voss needed to believe we still had it, needed to chase it, needed the compass to be right about something.

We reached the moraine crest at 13:40. Below us, the river valley opened like a gray wound, and upstream, two figures came down the slope toward the water at a pace that meant business.

I raised the radio. "Voss."

His voice came back immediately, and I could hear the smile in it, the salesman's warmth laid over something with teeth. "August. Good. You're across. Smart. Now bring the case back to the bank and we'll finish this like professionals."

"Come to the river," I said. "I'll leave the case at the crossing point. You can carry it to the meadow yourself. I'm done being your pack animal."

A pause. He was recalculating. I could hear it — the tiny silence of a man deciding whether the offer was a trick. But he was tired too, and cold, and he'd been chasing us for two days on a mountain that hated him, and the offer was exactly what he wanted to hear: the case, unguarded, his.

"Krieg and I will collect it," he said. "And August — this is the right call. When this is over, there's a place for people who make the right call."

"I know," I said, and hung up, and the taste of it was like glacier water — clean, freezing, and full of ground-up rock.

* * *

From the moraine crest, we watched.

Voss came down to the water first, moving well for a man who'd spent two days above four thousand meters — that's the thing about the powerful, they're often genuinely competent, it's how they got powerful. He stopped at the edge of the ice raft and pulled out his compass, and I watched him do it, watched him hold the little brass-cased lie in his palm and take his bearing on the far bank.

The needle pointed fifteen degrees east of true.

He adjusted. He stepped out onto the ice.

"He's going to see the tape," Petra whispered. The orange flagging on the safe route was visible, downstream, fifty meters away. "He'll see the tape and know."

"He'll see the tape and think we crossed there," I said. "Why would we flag the route we *didn't* take?"

Voss crossed the first plate. Then the second. Krieg followed, huge and careful, and for one long moment I let myself believe the mountain might just let them pass, that the trap would spring on nothing, that I'd built all this dread over a raft of ice that would hold.

Then Voss, hurrying now, eager, took the third plate at a diagonal — the way a lying compass would tell you to — and the plate moved.

It didn't break. That was the horrible elegance of it. It *shifted*, a meter, grinding, and the seam opened, and the river came up through it the way rivers do — not dramatically, not with a roar, just with the patient, total authority of something that has all day. Voss went down to his waist. The case — the worthless case, which I'd left, per my radio promise, on a rock at the water's edge on the *near* side, exactly where his compass told him it would be — was behind him, untouched. He grabbed for the plate edge, and the plate tipped, and the current took his legs, and then Krieg did exactly what I'd predicted, because Krieg's contract paid for Voss alive: he dropped flat, got a fist in Voss's collar, and held on while the gray water pulled like a team of horses.

The sample case sat on its rock. The river took Voss instead.

He came out twenty meters downstream, coughing, dragged by Krieg onto the far gravel, soaked through in air that was already below freezing. And as we turned to climb — as Vasquez shouldered the radio and Petra took Desh's litter and I took one last look back — I saw Voss on his knees in the gravel, looking up at the moraine, looking directly at me across the whole gray width of the valley.

He knew. There was no question of it. He'd done the same math I had, and the needle had told him the answer: *Yarrow knew. Yarrow sent me onto the ice.*

The radio crackled in my hand. His voice, stripped of every ounce of salesman, down to the bare rebar underneath:

"You're finished. You hear me? There is no version of this where you work again. I will spend the rest of my life making sure—"

I turned the radio off. First time in eleven days the silence was mine.

We climbed. Behind us, the river ran on, carrying nothing but rock flour and the last of my old life, and ahead — I could see it through the haze — the relay mast stood against the sky, and Vasquez was already unzipping the waterproof sleeve against her ribs, and the wind up there was right for transmission.

The data went out at 14:06. Who received it — and what came down the valley to meet us — was the next thing the mountain had in store.
