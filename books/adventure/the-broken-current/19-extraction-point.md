## 19. Extraction Point

I have never been so tired in my life, and I once spent forty hours awake with a client who insisted he could hear his dead mother in the moraine and needed me to corroborate.

We came down off the east ridge at first light, or what passes for first light inside a weather system that seems to regard the sun as a personal insult. Desh was on a litter we'd lashed together from trekking poles and the last of the survey team's rope, unconscious more often than not, his breathing doing this wet, hitching thing that I made myself listen to every ninety seconds because if I was going to carry the man out, I owed him the courtesy of noticing whether he was still in there. Vasquez walked on her own, which was either a miracle or a testament to what a person can do when they've spent eleven days deciding that dying was not on the schedule. August broke trail. The sample case was gone — the current had it, along with whatever dignity Merrick Voss had left — and August carried instead the burned journal in a dry bag against their chest, like a heart transplant they hadn't decided whether to accept.

The extraction point was a bench of gravel and glacial silt where the Consortium's helicopter had landed Voss a lifetime ago — four days, actually, but time on this mountain has stopped pretending to be linear. We arrived at nine in the morning and I sat down on a rock and immediately understood why people in old stories lie down in the snow.

"Petra." August's voice, from above me. "Not yet."

"I'm just resting my eyes."

"You're resting your whole body on a rock in the wind."

"That's efficiency."

August didn't laugh, but something in their shoulders came down half an inch, and on this mountain, that's a laugh.

* * *

The radio had been dead since the river.

Not broken — dead, which is different, which is the kind of dead that means someone on the other end is choosing not to speak. We'd tried every fifteen minutes on the way down. August had tried with the flat, professional cadence of someone reporting a routine extraction, and then, when that got no answer, with the flat professional cadence of someone reporting a routine extraction slightly louder. Nothing. The frequency just sat there breathing static at us like a patient who's decided to stop cooperating.

"He won't leave us," Vasquez said. She was crouched by Desh, tucking the sleeping bag tighter around him with hands that shook from cold and something else. "Voss. He can't. Not now."

"He can," August said. "He absolutely can. He's got a helicopter and a company that would prefer this mountain to keep its mouth shut."

"No." Vasquez looked up, and I saw it again — that quality she had, the thing that had kept her alive in a snow cave guarding a box of worthless rock, the thing that made you want to argue with her and made you afraid you'd lose. "He needs survivors, August. Think about it. If we die out here — all of us, the whole search party plus the survey leader plus his own contractors — that's not a quiet story anymore. That's an inquest. That's names in newspapers. He needs us walking onto that helicopter under our own power so he can control what we say when we land."

"He can't control what we say."

"He can try," Vasquez said. "That's the whole job description."

I thought about the contractor on the ledge, the one who'd come at us with a piton hammer and a face like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. He was somewhere behind us now — Voss had called him off the radio before the river, or the river had settled that account in some other way; August hadn't said, and I hadn't asked, because there is a category of question you save for below the snowline. The other contractor had gone south with Voss, into the pass that closed, and I found I didn't much care what had happened to him either. The mountain was doing its own audit.

At eleven, the static changed.

Not a voice at first. Just a rhythm in it, a pulse, like something turning over in its sleep. August had the handset up before I'd finished registering it, and I watched their face do something complicated — relief and contempt arriving at the same moment and sharing a chair.

"Yarrow." Voss's voice, thin through the speaker, stripped of whatever silk he'd had left at the camp. He sounded like a man who had been swimming. "Confirm your position."

"Extraction point," August said. "As agreed. Four souls. One stretcher case."

A pause. I could hear wind on his end, and under the wind, water — a lot of water, moving fast. I glanced at August. August's face said *don't*.

"The case," Voss said.

"The case is gone."

Another pause, longer. When Voss spoke again, the thinness was gone and what was underneath it was worse — a flat, careful calm, the voice of a man reading from a script he'd written on the flight over. "Yarrow, listen to me. That helicopter is the only way off this mountain. The pass is closed. The east ridge will close behind you by tonight — I've seen the satellite feed. If I lift off without you, you are a footnote. Nobody finds you until spring, and by spring nobody's looking."

"That's a hell of a negotiation posture," August said. "You're supposed to start with what I get."

"What you get is your life and your crew's lives and the chance to keep your mouth shut, which — given your history — I'd have thought you'd be grateful for. You're good at silence, Yarrow. It's the one thing you've never failed at."

I watched August take that. I watched it land, and I watched them hold it the way you hold a load on a steep pitch — not fighting it, just keeping it from moving you. And I thought: *this is what they did to you for years. People saying that exact thing in that exact voice.* And I thought: *he's not wrong, and that's why it works.*

"The journal," August said. "The core log. The transmission records. Those are gone too. They're out of my hands. You're not negotiating for them because there's nothing to negotiate."

"You're lying."

"I'm wet," August said. "Everything else is true."

* * *

Vasquez pulled me aside while August talked Voss in circles — circles being generous; it was more like two people pushing the same rock in opposite directions. She walked me thirty meters down the gravel bar, out of handset range, which told me everything.

"You transmitted," I said.

It wasn't a question. I'd known since the ridge. I'd seen her at the relay station with the stolen radio's guts spread out on a flat rock like surgery, and I'd seen her face after, and there are only so many faces a person can make.

"Last night, before the storm front. The ridge relay still had power — the survey team built it to spec, it would have survived a nuclear event." She said it without pride, which somehow made it worse. "The full dataset. Core logs, the falsified assay reports, the original samples' spectrometry, the internal correspondence Voss was stupid enough to send over an open channel in March. Everything. It went to Marisol Ferreira at the *Sentinel*. She's been sitting on the Consortium story for a year waiting for someone inside to give her the documents."

"And she'll run it."

"She'll run it the moment I confirm we're off the mountain. I gave her a window. Forty-eight hours, then she runs it whether we're down or not." Vasquez looked back up the valley, toward the river, toward the place where the current had taken the case. "I wasn't going to let that rock come back."

"You know what this does to August."

"I know what it does to all of us." She turned to me, and for the first time since the snow cave, Dr. Elena Vasquez looked tired — not mountain-tired, but the other kind, the kind you can't fix with sleep. "Petra. I've watched August Yarrow work for eleven days. I've watched them make every hard call correctly and hate every single one. Whatever happens when we land — they'll land with their crew alive. That's not nothing. That's the only thing that was ever actually theirs to save."

"And the truth?"

"The truth was never ours to hold," she said. "It was only ours to carry. There's a difference. I think they know it. I think that's what scares them."

* * *

The helicopter came at 2:40 in the afternoon.

We heard it before we saw it — that flat, arrogant chop bouncing off the valley walls — and then it came around the shoulder of the ridge with Voss's livery on the tail and the whole sky seemed to lean toward it. I got Desh ready. Vasquez packed the medical kit. August stood out on the open gravel with the wind tearing at them, and I swear to God they did not wave, did not signal, just stood there like a man waiting for a bus he intended to complain about.

It touched down in the usual wall of rotor wash, and the side door opened, and Merrick Voss climbed out into the wind.

I had imagined him, in the days since the camp, in various states of disarray. I had not imagined him accurately. He was soaked to the skin, his expensive jacket plastered to him, one boot dark with silt, and there was a scrape along his jaw that had been bandaged by someone with more urgency than skill. The river, I thought. The river got him too. The mountain had taken his contractors, his case, his plan, and his dry socks, and it had sent him back to us looking like exactly what he was: a man whose money had run out of reach.

He came toward us hunched against the wash, shouting things we couldn't hear, and August stood there and let him arrive.

"Where is it?" Voss shouted, right up close, and even over the rotors I could hear the crack in it. "Where is the data, Yarrow?"

"Downstream," August shouted back. "With your case. With your credibility. With your March emails. It's all in the same place, Merrick — it's all in the river. You should feel at home."

Something moved through Voss's face — I watched it happen, frame by frame, the way you watch a cornice start to go. Confusion first, because he was a man who had never once considered that information could exist in more than one copy. Then the arithmetic. Then, arriving last, the thing under everything: fear. Not of the mountain. Of a phone call.

"You transmitted," he said. Quieter now. The rotors were idling and the words just carried, plain, in the open air, in front of God and the pilot and me.

"Ask your IT department," August said. "I never touched a radio that mattered."

And that was the moment I understood what August had done — not just on the river, not just with the compasses and the ice, but here, standing in the wash, letting Voss arrive at the truth on his own so that August's hands stayed technically, immaculately clean. It was the most dishonest honest thing I'd ever seen, and it was masterful, and I felt a fierce, complicated pride in them that I didn't have room to examine yet.

Voss looked at the four of us — the stretcher, the doctor, the junior guide, the disgraced guide — and I watched him do the last of his math. He needed us. Every one of us alive and walking and, ideally, quiet. We were the difference between a story and a scandal, and he knew it, and we knew he knew it, and for one long moment the five of us stood there in the rotor wash in a perfect, mutually understood silence.

"Get them aboard," he said to the pilot.

* * *

Desh woke up on the helicopter.

Not all the way — his eyes opened to slits, and when I leaned over him he found my hand and gripped it with a strength that surprised me, given everything, given the ledge, given the way he'd caught Petra — caught *me* — out of the air like I weighed nothing, like it was the easiest thing he'd ever done, and then spent two days paying for it.

"Hey," he rasped.

"Hey yourself."

"You're crying."

"I'm not crying. It's condensation. Altitude thing. Very technical."

His mouth did the ghost of his grin. "The case," he said. "Did we—"

"The rock's in the river," I said. "The truth's in the cloud. We're in a helicopter. Batting a thousand."

He made a sound that was either a laugh or a cough, and his eyes found August across the cabin, and something passed between them that I wasn't part of and didn't need to be. Then he slept again, really slept, and I sat back against the fuselage and let the vibration shake my teeth and looked out the window at the Serrano Range falling away beneath us.

It should have felt like winning.

That's the thing nobody tells you — that the moment you've been climbing toward can arrive and feel like this: like a held breath finally let out, except what comes out isn't relief, it's just air, and you're still you, and Desh's blood is on the stretcher straps, and a man is dead in a crevasse we couldn't stop to mourn properly, and somewhere below us the river is carrying a box of worthless rock toward the sea as if it matters, as if the river knows, and the mountains don't care about any of it. We had won. I could see it objectively, the way you can see a summit from the air — small, clean, complete. It just didn't feel like anything. It felt like being too tired to feel.

Vasquez was watching the peaks recede with an expression I couldn't read. August was watching Voss, who was watching his satellite phone.

And then Voss's phone buzzed, and he looked at it, and the color left his face the way light leaves a valley.

He looked up. He looked straight at Vasquez. And Vasquez looked back at him with the calm of a woman who had mailed a letter days ago and was only now enjoying the sight of it being delivered.

"What did you do?" Voss said.

"Forty-eight-hour window," Vasquez said. "It's been forty-eight hours."

The helicopter banked toward the valley, toward the airstrip, toward the world, and I pressed my forehead to the cold window and watched the Serranos go, and I thought about what was waiting down there — cameras, questions, the machinery of consequence grinding itself up to speed — and I thought about August's face when the truth stopped being a choice and became a fact.

We were still twenty minutes out when Voss started making calls.

Nobody answered.
