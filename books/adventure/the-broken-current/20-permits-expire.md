## 20. Permits Expire

The valley forgave the mountain before it forgave us.

That's what I kept thinking, three days after the helicopter set us down on the gravel strip at Cordero Flats, watching the news cycle do its work. The Serrano Range had spent two weeks being a place where people vanished and compasses lied and men like Merrick Voss sent contractors up frozen ridgelines to retrieve inconvenient rocks. Now it was a backdrop. A graphic. *MYSTERY IN THE HIGH SERRANOS: FRAUD AT 14,000 FEET.* The mountain didn't care. The mountain never cares. It was the valley that had opinions.

I watched most of it from the window of a motel room in Cordero that smelled of pine cleaner and old cigarettes, a room I paid for with the last functioning credit card I owned, because the Halvard Consortium's payment — the one that was supposed to clear my debts, the one I'd signed a silence clause for — had been frozen along with everything else the Consortium touched. Federal asset freeze. I read about my own paycheck in a wire story written by someone who clearly thought "escrow" was a spicy word.

Petra had the room next door. Desh was two floors down in an actual bed, which the doctors at the regional medical center in Andes Sur had fought for and won after four days of arguing about whether his spine could tolerate transport. Four cracked vertebrae, they said. Not five. He'd joked from the gurney that he'd been robbed. Petra hadn't laughed. Neither had I, really, but I'd appreciated the attempt. That was Desh all over — pricing out his own catastrophe and finding it a bargain.

Vasquez was gone by then. Transferred to a hospital in the capital under government escort, which is a phrase that sounds protective until you realize it can also mean *contained*. She'd transmitted the dataset from the ridge relay before we ever reached the extraction point, and whatever she'd sent had landed like a core sample dropped from orbit. The story broke forty hours later. It broke everywhere at once.

* * *

Here is how a fraud dies in public:

First there's the data itself — Vasquez's full survey package, the real core logs, the geochemical assays showing the "Cordillera Playa" ore body running at trace concentrations that wouldn't justify mining the tailings pile of a schoolyard. Beside it, leaked in the same dump, the falsified versions. The ones the Consortium had filed. The ones that had moved stock prices for eighteen months while Halvard Minerals climbed thirty percent on a deposit that existed mostly in PowerPoint.

Then there's the money trail, which journalists found faster than we'd found Vasquez's snow cave, because money leaves tracks even when people don't. Shell consultancies. A permitting consultant in Zurich who'd been paid, per the filings, "for geological advisory services," and who turned out to be the brother-in-law of a Halvard board member. A pattern of insider sales timed — this was the part the financial press savored — *with uncanny precision* ahead of every negative internal memo.

And then, last, there are the faces. Because a fraud isn't real to people until it has a face, and the face it got was Merrick Voss, photographed at the Cordero airstrip in a borrowed parka, soaked to the waist, being walked across the tarmac between two federal police officers with his hands cuffed behind him and his chin up like he was reviewing troops.

I wasn't there. Petra was. She came back to the motel and sat on the end of my bed without asking, which by then was allowed, and told me about it in her flat, precise way, the way she reported compass deviations and ice conditions.

"He asked for you," she said.

"What?"

"At the airstrip. When they were loading him into the car. He looked past the officers, like he was checking the tree line, and he said, 'Tell Yarrow the current always wins.' Then they put him in the car."

I lay there looking at the ceiling water stain, which had the approximate shape of the southern pass.

"That's not a threat," I said finally. "That's a man losing."

"It sounded like a threat."

"It's designed to sound like a threat. That's cheaper than being one." I rolled onto my side. "He needs me implicated, Petra. If I'm just a guide who got played, then he's a fraud who got caught. If I'm a co-conspirator, then everyone's dirty and his lawyers have somewhere to stand."

"Are you?"

"A what?"

"A co-conspirator." She asked it the way she'd once asked whether the compasses pointing wrong meant magnetic anomaly or sabotage — no drama, just instrumentation. "You signed the clause. You carried the case. You agreed to his terms over the radio at False Summit. I heard you."

"I know you heard me."

"So?"

"So I don't know yet." I sat up. "That's the honest answer. Ask me again after the inquiry."

She nodded slowly, and then she did something I didn't expect, which was reach over and turn off the lamp, plunging the room into blue television light, because I'd left the news on mute and neither of us had mentioned it.

"You'll tell the truth," she said. "It'll cost you everything you've been trying to buy back. But you'll tell it."

"You sound very sure."

"I watched you reroute a man onto rotten ice to save four lives and lose a fortune. People who do that don't stop doing it when the ice melts." She stood, stretched, paused at the door. "Also, you snore. If they take your license over this, at least I won't have to share tents with you anymore."

The door clicked shut behind her. On the muted screen, a drone shot of the Serrano Range scrolled past under a banner reading *BILLIONS IN LOSSES*, and I thought: wrong number. Not billions. Just everything some people had, which is how those numbers always get reported — as if the only losses that count are the ones with commas.

* * *

Desh called on the fourth morning, from his hospital bed, voice rough but level.

"They're scattering," he said. "Like flies. You watching?"

"I'm watching."

"Calloway resigned yesterday — the CEO. Statement cited 'personal reasons.' Personal reasons, August. His personal reason is a helicopter and a passport." A dry sound down the line that might have been a laugh. "Two board members gone quiet. The general counsel took 'indefinite leave.' You know what indefinite leave means in a company under criminal investigation? It means the lifeboats launched at night."

"And Voss?"

"Voss is the fall guy. They built him for it — contractor, no board seat, deniable chain of command. He'll trade testimony for leniency, give them Calloway, and serve four years in a minimum-security facility with a tennis court." Desh paused. "Which means everyone above him walks. That's usually how it goes. You know that better than anyone."

I did know it. I'd lived the small version of it already — the Kestrel Ridge accident, three years back, where the client's family got a settlement, the insurance company got a discount, and I got a blacklisting that no one ever formally announced because formal announcements create records and records create liability. The industry didn't fire me. It just stopped calling. I'd spent two years checking my phone like a man waiting for a pardon that was never in the mail.

"August," Desh said, and I could hear him shifting against pillows, careful of the vertebrae. "Whatever notice comes — and it will come — remember something. You did the arithmetic right. Every time. Even when the arithmetic was ugly."

"The arithmetic got a man killed on that ridge."

"No. The storm killed him. The fraud put him on the ridge. You just refused to lie about which was which." A nurse's voice murmured in the background; he covered the phone, then came back. "They're discharging me Friday. Petra's driving me to my sister's in Valparaíso. Come see me before then."

"I will."

"And August — when the inquiry letter comes, read it twice before you answer anything. Lawyers write those things hoping you'll skim."

* * *

The letter came on the sixth day, which surprised me only because part of me had assumed the machinery would move slower, or not at all. Institutions are like glaciers in that way — either immovable or suddenly calving, nothing in between.

It arrived by courier, not email, which told me they considered it serious. Heavy cream envelope, embossed return address: *Ministerio de Minería y Energía — Dirección de Fiscalización*. Office of Inspection and Enforcement. Inside, two pages in Spanish so formally worded it took me three readings to be sure I understood it, and then a third page in English, certified translation, as if they'd anticipated exactly how long I'd stare at the Spanish.

*Formal Notice of Inquiry.*

Not charges. Not a subpoena. An inquiry — which is the bureaucratic purgatory between suspicion and consequence, a word designed to sound neutral while meaning *we know*. The text informed me that, pursuant to the collapse of Concession SR-114, an investigation had been opened into "all parties holding contracts, permits, or professional certifications connected to the Cordillera Playa survey operation," and that I, as holder of Mountain Guide Certification MG-4471 and signatory of a confidentiality agreement with Halvard Consortium subsidiaries, was requested — *requested*, with the gentle menace of all such requests — to appear before the Commission on the fourteenth day of the month, at nine o'clock, to "provide testimony regarding the circumstances of the expedition, the disposition of survey materials, and any knowledge pertaining to the falsification of geological data."

*Any knowledge pertaining to the falsification of geological data.*

There it was. The whole trap and the whole door, both drawn in the same ink. Because I had knowledge. All of it. And the question the letter didn't ask but intended was: *when did you know, and what did you do about it, and why does your signed silence clause exist in a federal file with your name on it?*

I read it twice, like Desh said. Then a third time, standing at the motel window while rain came down on the parking lot and a delivery truck idled, and I understood the shape of it. They weren't asking me to testify about Voss. Voss was caught; Voss was cheap; Voss was already talking. They were asking me to testify about myself. About the contract I signed for money. About the eleven days I'd spent carrying sealed evidence while negotiating with the man who wanted it buried. About the moment at False Summit when I'd said *yes, fine, we'll bring you the case* over an open channel, knowing I was lying, and let them believe it.

An inquiry doesn't want heroes. Heroes complicate the record. What an inquiry wants is a clean sequence: crime, discovery, punishment. And I was a loose thread hanging off the clean sequence — a guide who'd signed the silence clause, taken the money, and then broken it. Guilty of the crime's logistics and innocent of its purpose, which is the worst possible position, because it means both sides need something from you and neither side trusts you.

I sat on the bed with the letter in my lap for a long time. Outside, the rain kept working the parking lot, and somewhere down the highway a truck geared down through the pass approach, engine braking, the sound the mountain makes when it lets you go.

Petra knocked once and came in, holding two coffees from the gas station, and saw the envelope before I could turn it over.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it."

"Are you going to open—" She saw it was open. She handed me a coffee and sat down on the radiator cover, which was the only other place to sit, and read the letter upside down, which she could do, because Petra could do everything.

"Fourteenth," she said. "Nine o'clock. That's eight days."

"Seven and a half."

"You're going to tell all of it."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because saying it out loud made it load-bearing.

"All of it. The clause. The money. The deal at False Summit. The ice at the river — including the part where I used a sabotaged compass to steer a man toward a crevasse field, because that's what happened, and if I round it off into heroism I'm just doing what Halvard did to their core samples." I looked at her. "Clean samples or nothing. That's the whole lesson of this job, isn't it? Of every job."

Petra was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the window.

"They'll take your certification," she said. "Maybe permanently. You know that."

"I know that."

"After everything you did to get them home—"

"Petra." I almost smiled. "Getting them home was the easy part. Getting them home was just walking downhill with heavy packs. This is the part where I find out what any of it was worth."

She looked at me for a long second, and then she set down her coffee, crossed the small room, and hugged me — quick and fierce, the way she did everything, arms around my shoulders like she was belaying me onto something.

"For the record," she said into my collar, "I'd follow that compass anywhere. The fixed one. When you build it."

"When I build it," I said, and didn't ask what she meant, because I was afraid the answer was a future I had no right to plan for yet.

* * *

I went to see Desh on Thursday, his last day in Andes Sur. He was propped up in the discharge wheelchair looking like a king in exile, duffel packed at his feet, and when I told him about the letter he listened all the way through without interrupting, which was its own kind of respect.

"So," he said at last. "The commission. Nine o'clock, the fourteenth. You walk in, you swear to tell the truth, and you burn the bridge."

"Burn the bridge."

"Your certification. Your standing. Every operator who ever might have hired you — they'll all hear you testified against your own contract. Doesn't matter that you were right. Guides who talk aren't guides anymore. You know the culture."

"I wrote the culture," I said. "Three years ago I was on the other end of it, being disappeared by it."

"Then you know exactly what it costs." He wheeled himself closer, and his voice dropped into the register he used on the mountain when the weather was turning and he needed me to actually hear him. "August. Listen. There's a version of this where you say just enough. Name Voss, confirm the fraud, keep your own signature out of the frame. Lawyers do it daily. The commission would probably accept it — you found the survivors, you saved lives, case closed, certification intact. In two years you're leading trips again. Maybe not the big consortium jobs, but work. Real work."

"And?"

"And I watched you carry a dying man's weight down an east ridge in a whiteout." He held my eyes. "I'm not going to tell you which version to pick. I'm going to tell you that I already know which one you'll pick, and that I've already decided what I'll say about you afterward, and that neither of those things changes."

"What will you say?"

"That August Yarrow is the only guide I've worked with whose instruments I'd trust more than their reputation." He grinned, crooked, tired. "Which, granted, is a low bar in this industry right now. Half the certified geologists in the country just got exposed as fiction writers."

I laughed — really laughed, first time since the river — and it hurt something in my chest that had been braced for days, some muscle I hadn't known I was clenching since the extraction point.

Outside the hospital, the clouds had torn open over the valley and the Serrano Range stood up behind the town, white and indifferent, the southern pass visible as a pale notch. Somewhere up there was the snow cave, the cairn, the crevasse that held Tomás Reyes — the surveyor we'd pulled from the ice, the one whose name the news reports kept misspelling. Somewhere up there was the rappel station where Desh had caught Petra falling and paid for it in vertebrae. The mountain kept all of it, the way mountains do, without comment, without judgment, holding everything at altitude until the meltwater brings it down.

I stood in the parking lot a long time, looking up.

Eight days. Then the commission, the oath, the whole truth with my own name woven through it like a flaw through crystal. I expected nothing from it. No redemption, no reinstatement, no phone calls returning. I'd spent three years learning what the industry does to people who tell the truth at the wrong volume, and I had no illusions that this truth would be different just because it was bigger.

But standing there in the wet parking lot with the range rising over the town, I felt something I hadn't felt since before Kestrel Ridge. Not hope, exactly. Something colder and more durable than hope. The feeling of having stopped waiting to be let back in.

Because here's what nobody tells you about disgrace: the worst part isn't the fall. It's the waiting afterward — years spent standing outside the door of your own profession, hat in hand, rehearsing apologies for sins you're not even sure you committed, hoping someone inside will glance at the list and find you acceptable. I'd done that. Three years of it. And the door had never opened, and now I finally understood that it was never going to, not because of what I'd done on the mountain, but because doors don't open for people who stand outside them. Doors open from the inside, for insiders, and I had stopped being an insider the moment I chose four lives over one career, and then chosen the truth over both.

So no — I expected nothing from the inquiry. Let it come. Let them take the certification, the standing, the name in the registry. I had a journal with burned edges in a plastic bag, a junior guide who'd learned to read bad compasses, a climber with four cracked vertebrae who'd already decided what he'd say about me, and a doctor in a capital hospital who'd trusted me with the only copy of the truth when trusting me was the stupidest thing she could have done.

That was the ledger now. It didn't balance in any currency the Consortium had ever issued.

The rain started again, soft on the gravel. I turned up my collar and walked back toward the motel, and behind me the pass held its pale notch in the skyline like a door left ajar — not an invitation, just a fact, the way the mountain had always meant it.

Eight days.

I went inside and started writing down everything I remembered, beginning with the day I signed the clause, and I did not stop until dark.
