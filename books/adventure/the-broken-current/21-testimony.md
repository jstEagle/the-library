## 21. Testimony

The hearing room in Valdez had the particular charm of a place designed by people who hated both light and conversation. Fluorescent panels humming overhead, a long table scarred by decades of nervous fingers, a window that looked out onto a parking lot instead of the mountains that had started all of this. I'd spent eleven days on the Serrano Range wishing for a window. Now they gave me one and it faced asphalt.

I sat at the witness table with a glass of water I didn't touch and a copy of my own signed contract in front of me, three-hole-punched like it was going into a binder of other people's sins. Exhibit C-4. The silence clause was paragraph nine. I could have recited it from memory. *The contractor agrees to make no public or private disclosure of any data, observations, or findings recovered during the course of engagement.* I had read it twice before signing, which was two more times than I'd read most contracts in those broke, hungry months, and I had signed anyway because the money cleared my debt and because I told myself everyone signs things they don't mean.

That was the version I'd rehearsed. The truth was uglier: I hadn't just signed it, I'd been relieved to sign it, because a clause like that tells you exactly what kind of people you're working for, and knowing what kind of people you're working for lets you stop pretending you have choices.

"Mr. Yarrow." The chair of the inquiry board was a woman named Okafor with reading glasses on a chain and the patience of someone who had heard every possible version of every possible lie. "You understand this is not a criminal proceeding. We are determining facts for the record."

"I understand," I said.

"Then let's begin with the contract itself. Did you read the silence clause before signing?"

Here was the fork in the road. I'd known it was coming since the subpoena arrived, and I'd spent two weeks — two weeks of bad sleep and worse coffee — telling myself there was an honorable way down the mountain that didn't require walking off a cliff. I could describe Voss's threats. I could describe the falsified core samples, the worthless rock in the case, the stock fraud Elena Vasquez had documented page by burned page. All true. All damning to Halvard. And none of it required me to say the sentence that would end my career in front of six people taking notes.

Because here's the arithmetic nobody teaches you about testimony: the Consortium's lawyers were already finished. Voss was arrested at the airstrip with a wet radio in his pocket and a paper trail you could follow from orbit. My testimony against Halvard was decorative. What the board actually wanted — what would actually matter — was whether the guide who found the survey team had followed the rules, and if he hadn't, why.

"Mr. Yarrow?"

"I read it," I said. "And I signed it. And then I spent most of the expedition trying to figure out how to break it without getting anyone killed, and when I finally figured out how, I did. But I want to be clear about the order of operations, because I think the order matters." I turned to face the board directly. "I didn't sign that clause under duress. Nobody held a pen to my hand. I signed it because I needed the money and because I'd already decided, somewhere in the back of my head, that I was the kind of person who took jobs like that. So when you write up your findings, don't put me down as a victim of Halvard Consortium. Put me down as a willing employee who got lucky enough to find his conscience before the permits expired."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone says more than was asked. Okafor looked at me over her glasses for a long moment.

"You understand that disclosure of this nature will likely result in the revocation of your guiding certification pending review. Possibly permanently."

"I've done the math," I said. "It's the only math I'm good at anymore."

* * *

They kept me for six hours. Six hours of questions about compasses and cairns and the katabatic storm, about the faulty instruments Petra had flagged on day one, about why I'd rerouted Voss onto unstable ice at the river crossing — and I told them all of it, including the part where I'd used a broken tool as a weapon, including the part where the current took the sample case downstream and I stood on the bank and felt nothing but clean cold relief. I described Desh catching Petra's fall on the ledge. I described Vasquez transmitting the dataset from the ridge relay while frostbitten and half-starved. I described the second surveyor, alive, hoarse from days of broadcasting fraud into the empty air, and the first one, dead in the crevasse, whose name was Tomas Reyes and whom I made the board say back to me twice so it went into the record properly.

At one point one of the junior board members — a young man who looked like he still ironed his shirts — asked me whether, given the outcome, I considered my decisions justified.

"That's not a question guides get to answer," I said. "We get outcomes, not verdicts. Everyone came home except Reyes, and Reyes died before I ever met him. Whether my decisions were justified depends entirely on whether you believe I'd have made them differently if the ore body had turned out to be real. And honestly? I don't know. That's the whole testimony, if you want the short version. I don't know what I'd have done if the rock had been worth something. You should probably factor that in."

He wrote that down. I genuinely hope it made it into the report.

* * *

Desh was out of the hospital by the time the hearings wrapped — six weeks after the ledge, his left arm pinned and plated, his shoulder a lattice of hardware that set off airport security from thirty feet. He'd taken to standing in doorways with his good arm crossed over the sling, looking like a man perpetually deciding whether the room was worth entering. Mostly it wasn't. But he came to my hearing's last day, sat in the back row with his hat on his knee, and walked out with me afterward into a gray drizzle that couldn't decide whether to be rain or snow.

"They're going to pull your ticket," he said. Not a question.

"Probably."

"And you did it anyway. On the record. With your own lawyer sitting next to you making sounds like a dying kettle."

"He made good sounds. Expensive ones."

Desh laughed, then winced, then laughed again, which was how he did everything now. We stood under the courthouse awning watching the traffic go by, and for a while neither of us said anything, because we were the kind of friends who'd earned the right to shut up together.

"You know what I keep thinking about," he said finally. "The ridge. When you said no to the detour. I hated you for about four hours. Then we found Reyes, and I hated the mountain instead, which is healthier." He shifted the sling. "But I want you to know I understood it even while I hated it. That's the job. You do the arithmetic and you live inside it."

"That's the most generous thing anyone's said to me in years."

"Don't get used to it. I'm mostly bitter." He looked over at me. "What are you going to do? Genuinely. Because I've watched you check your phone eleven times today waiting for the board's decision, and you pretend you haven't."

"I'm going to wait for the decision," I said. "And then I'm going to stop being a guide, apparently, and become whatever comes after a guide. There isn't a word for it yet. Unemployed, probably."

"There's always nonprofit work," Desh said, with the elaborate casualness of a man planting a seed he'd been carrying around for weeks. "Conservation surveys. Low pay, good people, nobody asks you to sign silence clauses." He shrugged with one shoulder, the other being full of titanium. "Just saying. Some of us know people."

"Some of us do," I said, and let it sit there between us, warm, unexamined, like a coal that would still be burning in the morning.

* * *

Petra found me two days later at the diner where I'd been eating breakfast long enough that the waitress had stopped asking what I wanted. She slid into the booth across from me without ceremony, ordered coffee she wouldn't drink, and put a small object on the table between us.

My compass. The one from the Serrano trip — the one that had pointed wrong, that had led us to the cairn, that I'd used to steer Voss onto the rotten ice. It had been evidence for months. Apparently evidence gets released eventually, once the lawyers are done picking through it.

"It points true now," Petra said. "I checked. Whatever was wrong with it, the lab fixed it or it fixed itself. I thought you should have it back."

I picked it up. It was lighter than I remembered, the housing scuffed, the needle settling almost immediately, steady, certain, pointing north with the boring reliability of a thing that has no secrets.

"Why?" I asked. "Why bring it to me?"

"Because you'll need bearings," she said, "wherever you land. And because—" She stopped, restarted, which she almost never did; Petra spoke in finished sentences the way other people breathed. "Because I've been thinking about what happens next. For me. And I keep coming back to the fact that the best fieldwork I've ever done, I did with you, on a mountain that was trying to kill us, carrying a lie we ended up telling the truth about. And I know your certification is probably gone. And I know you might not want to guide again after everything. But someday — not now, maybe not for a year — if you ever put a team together again, I'd like my name on the list. First, if there's a list. I'm saying I'd work with you again. Deliberately. Knowing everything."

I looked at her across the table, this junior guide who'd caught the wrong compass readings when the veterans missed them, who'd climbed east into a storm because someone had to, who was sitting in a diner offering me a future I hadn't dared to sketch for myself.

"You know I'm radioactive, right?" I said. "Professionally speaking. Anyone who works with me inherits the stink."

"I've smelled worse," Petra said. "I worked a season on a fishing boat."

I laughed — really laughed, the first time since the hearing — and turned the compass over in my hand, and felt something shift in my chest that I didn't have a word for yet. Grief, maybe, for the career I'd just torched with my own testimony. Release, too, tangled up in the same knot so tightly I couldn't tell which strand was which. Maybe they'd always been the same thing. Maybe every honest ending is both.

"Someday," I said. "When there's a list, you're on it. First line."

She nodded once, satisfied, and finally drank her coffee, and outside the window the drizzle had given up and turned to proper snow, fat flakes coming down over the parking lot, and somewhere north of town the Serrano Range stood white and indifferent under clouds, keeping its secrets, having given up the only one that mattered.

My phone buzzed on the table. Unknown number. I almost didn't answer.

I answered.

"August Yarrow?" A woman's voice, crisp, unfamiliar. "This is Marion Aldous, Aldous Wilderness Trust. We've been following the inquiry. I have a proposition for you — and I think you're going to want to hear it."
