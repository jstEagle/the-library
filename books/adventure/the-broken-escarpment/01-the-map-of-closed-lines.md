## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The last stake came out of the ground at dusk, and Ivo Dravenmoor stood over it a long moment before she pulled it free.

Seven years she had been doing this—walking dead lines with a transit and a notebook, measuring the slow decay of railways no train would ever ride again. This one was the Merrow Branch, forty miles of rusted track stitched along the shoulder of the Carrow Mountains, abandoned before the war, forgotten by everyone but the men who'd built it and the woman who now catalogued its ruin. The rails had gone the color of dried blood. Birch saplings grew up between the ties, thin and white as candle stubs. In another twenty years the mountain would take it all back, and no one would know there had ever been a line here at all.

She understood the impulse. She had made an art of it herself.

Ivo worked the stake loose with her boot heel and her gloved hands, rocking it until the frozen earth gave it up with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw. She wiped the mud from the stamped number on its face—*MB-114*—and set it in her satchel with the others. Eleven stakes. The number sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, as it always did, though these eleven were only iron and meant nothing. Numbers were like that. They waited years to mean something.

Above her, the escarpment caught the last of the light. It ran for sixty miles along the northern sky, a wall of gray limestone seamed with shadow, and somewhere behind it lay the Serran River gorge—the country she had condemned with ink and arithmetic seven years ago, the country she had sworn never to look at again. From here it was only a shape, a bruise on the horizon. She could almost pretend that was all it was.

Almost.

Her camp was a lean-to of canvas and cut boughs against the ruined wall of a water tower, and beside it her crew's fire had burned down to a red eye. There was no crew, not really—two local men hired to carry instruments and sent home that morning with their pay, because the last day of a survey she always did alone. That was the rule. The last day belonged to the burning.

She knelt at the fire and took out the notebook.

It was a good survey. That was the bitter joke of her life: she was very good at this. Every grade, every curve, every culvert and rotted trestle of the Merrow Branch rendered in her small, exact hand, with margins full of the observations that separated a surveyor from a mere measurer—the way frost heaved the ballast on the north-facing cuts, the way the old drainage had been designed by someone who'd loved the line and understood water. Pages and pages of it. Enough to rebuild the branch, if anyone wanted it rebuilt. No one would. The consortium had struck it from their ledgers a decade ago, and the consortium did not strike things twice.

She fed the notebook to the fire a few pages at a time, the way she always did, so it would catch cleanly and leave nothing half-burned to be pried out of the ash. The paper went up green and then black, curling like leaves. Her figures bloomed orange at the edges and vanished. *Grade 2.1 percent. Trestle 9 unsound. Recommend abandonment.* Words she had written once before, on another line, in another life, words that had come true in ways no arithmetic could account for.

This was the ritual, and she did not let herself think of it as penance, because penance implied a debt you intended to pay, and Ivo Dravenmoor had stopped intending anything except motion. It was simpler than that. A survey existed to be used—to become a rail line, or a road, or a verdict—and hers never would. So she unmade them. She left no trace. She moved through the world the way water moves through limestone, taking the shape of every passage and keeping none, arriving nowhere, depositing nothing. Seven years of it. Fourteen surveys, fourteen notebooks, fourteen small fires in fourteen cold camps.

She was tired in a way sleep did not touch.

It was not the tiredness of the body, though her body had plenty to answer for—knees that ached by October, hands scarred and rope-burned, an old fracture in her left collarbone that sang when the weather turned. It was something under that. The tiredness of a woman who had spent seven years being no one in particular, everywhere at once, and who had begun to suspect that the person she'd erased had not died after all but was simply waiting, patient as the mountain, for her to stop walking long enough to catch her.

The last page went into the fire. She watched it burn and stirred the ash with a stick until it was gray all through, indistinguishable from any other camp's leavings. Then she banked the fire for the night and sat down on her bedroll with her back against the tower wall, and looked at the escarpment going dark, and allowed herself, as she allowed herself most nights, exactly one minute of thinking about Tam.

Her brother had liked the high country best. He'd said the air up there tasted like coins. He'd been twenty-six, with a laugh that carried across a whole valley and a habit of whistling while he worked that had driven their first crew chief half mad. He had been standing on the Kestrel Bridge span at eleven minutes past six on the morning of the fourteenth of March, seven years ago, holding a cup of coffee he never drank, when the span let go.

The report said fatigue failure. Undetectable. An act of the mountain, of time, of God. Ivo had written the inspection summary herself, three weeks before, certifying the bridge sound for heavy freight. She had signed it. She had known—had *suspected*, the word she used, the word she hid behind, though some nights even the word collapsed—that the center span's lower chord was compromised. She had suspected, and she had needed the contract, and she had written *sound*, and eleven men had stepped onto her sentence and fallen through it.

Tam had been the eleventh name on the list. Alphabetical. She had checked, afterward, whether he'd have been first if they'd listed them differently. He would not. Some part of her had needed to know that, needed the small mercy of his name falling where it fell, and she had hated herself for needing it, and she had run anyway.

The minute ended. She closed it like a door.

She was just lying back when she heard the mule bell.

There should have been no mule bell. The two carriers had gone down-valley at noon, and the Merrow Branch was forty miles of nothing, and nobody climbed to a dead line at night without reason. Ivo came up off the bedroll with her hand already inside her coat, on the grip of the short-barreled pistol she carried the way other women carried a locket, and stood listening.

The bell came again, closer, along the old service track from the pass. Then a lantern swam into view between the birches, swinging with a walk, unhurried—a man's walk, a man who had walked a long way and expected to be received. Behind the lantern the mule materialized, gray and patient, and behind the mule a figure in a long oilskin coat with the collar up against the wind.

"You're a hard woman to find, Dravenmoor," the man said. "Fourteen surveys in seven years. I had to buy a season of telegraph logs from three different companies to build the pattern." He lowered the lantern, and the light climbed his face, and Ivo felt the ground tilt under her feet in a way it had not tilted since the morning the bridge fell.

She knew him. Everyone in the trade knew Casimir Voss—knew the neat beard, the gold-rimmed spectacles, the reputation for never losing a contract and never forgetting a face. His rival's face, some had called hers, in the years when she'd still been someone whose face could be known.

"Surveyors don't advertise," she said. Her voice came out level. Seven years of practice.

"No," Voss agreed. "They don't. They burn their notebooks, too—I saw the ash before I saw your fire. Very thorough. Very consistent." He smiled, and it was not a friendly smile; it was the smile of a man laying cards face-up, one by one. "Consistency is how I found you. People think hiding is about variation. It isn't. It's about habit, and you, Miss Dravenmoor, are the most habitual fugitive I have ever hunted."

"I'm not hiding."

"Of course not." He dismounted, tied the mule to the water tower's rusted ladder, and reached into his coat—not for a weapon; she tracked the motion anyway—with the unhurried confidence of a man who already knew how this would end. What he brought out was a telegram, folded once, and a letter that had traveled far enough to go soft at the corners.

"Message came for you care of the Merrow Junction office," he said. "You collect your mail under a false name, which is sensible, but the office keeps duplicates, and duplicates can be bought." He held out the telegram. "But the offer is real, and it won't wait, and we both know you're going to take it."

Ivo did not move. The fire ticked. Somewhere up on the escarpment, wind moved through stone with a sound like a long exhalation, like the country itself breathing, waiting.

"Read it," Voss said gently. "Then we'll talk about the bridge."

The word went through her like a stake driven home. She took the telegram because her hand decided before she did, and unfolded it, and read it by the light of the fire she had built to erase herself:

*DRAVENMOOR. CONSORTIUM REQUIRES SURVEYOR OF RECORD FOR SERRAN RIVER EXPEDITION STOP TERMS GENEROUS STOP DEPARTURE WITHIN THE MONTH STOP REFUSAL WILL BE NOTED STOP*

*Noted*, she thought. As in recorded. As in filed. As in kept.

Voss watched her read, and above them both the escarpment stood black against the stars, sixty miles of condemned stone, and Ivo Dravenmoor understood, with the calm of a woman watching her own footbridge finally crack, that the running was over.

"Within the month," she said.

"Within the month," Voss agreed, and unwound his scarf, and settled himself by her fire as though he owned it—as though he owned the ash of everything she'd ever burned.
