## 2. The Man Who Kept Receipts

The telegram had said *Harrow's Rest, Room 9, midnight*, and Ivo Dravenmoor had spent the whole of that day deciding not to go.

She was good at deciding things. It was, in fact, the only skill she had never doubted. Seven years of deciding—deciding to move on before a town learned her face, deciding to take the next dead branch line instead of the last one, deciding which memories to burn with the survey notes and which to keep in the locked box behind her ribs. Deciding was easy. It was the space between decisions that hurt, and so she kept herself in motion, a woman made entirely of departures.

But Harrow's Rest was a rail-yard hotel at the edge of a town called Cinder Flats, and she was already there. That was the trouble with telegrams: they found you where you already were. She told herself she could walk out tonight, take the morning freight south, be three hundred miles gone by the time whoever waited in Room 9 understood he'd been stood up. She had done it before. She had done it eleven times, once for every man the Greywater Bridge had taken down with it into the gorge, her brother among them, though she never let herself count them in order because counting in order meant naming them, and naming them meant—

*Stop.*

She went anyway. Of course she went. Curiosity was the one appetite she'd never managed to starve.

* * *

Harrow's Rest smelled of coal smoke, lamp oil, and the particular sourness of hotels that existed only because trains stopped near them. The stairwell light was a single bulb behind smoked glass, and her boots rang on iron treads worn smooth by decades of men going up to sleep badly. Room 9 was at the end of the corridor, under a flickering gas sconce, and the door stood open an inch, as if the man inside wanted her to know he wasn't afraid of what might come through it.

Ivo pushed it open with two fingers.

Casimir Voss sat in the room's single chair with his coat still on, hat on his knee, reading a newspaper he clearly had no interest in. He was thinner than she remembered, greyer at the temples, but he had the same quality that had made him insufferable when they'd trained together under old Surveyor Halden—the quality of a man who was never surprised, because he had already imagined everything that could happen and filed it away. His kit lay beside him: leather cases, brass instruments wrapped in oilcloth, and a document case that he set, deliberately, on the bed between them like a man laying down a card.

"Dravenmoor." He didn't stand. "You look terrible."

"You look rich." She stayed by the door, one hand on the frame. "Consortium money agrees with you."

"It agrees with everyone eventually." He folded the newspaper with the precision of a man folding a map along creases he himself had drawn. "Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"You always did." Something moved behind his eyes—not quite warmth, not quite contempt. Something older than either. "Seven years, Ivo. You've gotten careful. New initials on your field books. Different handwriting every third contract. You burned your notes in a brazier outside Marrow Junction two weeks ago—I know because the night watchman sold me the ashes' location, and I paid a boy to sift them. Do you know what survives burning? The wire bindings. Little spirals of copper with your fingerprints in the corrosion." He smiled without any pleasure in it. "You leave no trace except the traces you can't see. It's almost admirable."

Her mouth had gone dry. She kept her voice level, because level was all she had left. "If you've come to sell me something, Voss, name the price."

"Oh, I'm not selling." He opened the document case. "I'm collecting."

He drew out a bundle of letters tied with string, and even before he set them on the bed she knew what they were. She knew by the weight of them, by the way they sat there—innocent paper, yellowed at the edges—and by the way her own body reacted, a cold flooding up from her knees as if the floor had turned to river water.

"That's not possible," she said. "Those were sealed. They were in the company archive at Greywater. The office burned."

"The office burned. The records annex didn't." Voss untied the string with maddening patience. "Your preliminary report on the Greywater crossing, dated the fourteenth of March. Your final report, dated the second of April. And this—" he held up a single sheet, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty, "—this is the letter from Aldous Renwick, consortium engineer, instructing you to revise your load figures. Dated the ninth of April. Eleven days before the bridge came down."

The room tilted. Not literally—the walls stayed where walls stay—but something in her did tilt, some internal horizon she had spent seven years leveling by hand, stone by stone, and now it slid.

"I never wrote those revisions," she heard herself say. "Renwick dictated the figures. I copied them. I signed it because if I hadn't—"

"If you hadn't, you'd have been dismissed, blacklisted, and the bridge would have come down anyway, built to Renwick's numbers by men who didn't know better." Voss finished it for her, flatly, the way one reads back a survey line. "Yes. I've read the letters enough times to recite them. I know exactly how much guilt belongs to you and exactly how much belongs to them, and I'll tell you something you may find interesting, Dravenmoor: the proportions are not what you think."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't tell me what I am. You weren't there."

"No," he agreed quietly. "I wasn't there. Teodor was."

Her brother's name in Voss's mouth hit her like a winch handle swung into the sternum. She gripped the doorframe. The gaslight buzzed. Somewhere below, a locomotive sighed against its coupling, restless, wanting to move, and she envied it with her whole body.

"He was my friend too," Voss said. "That's the part you never accounted for, running. That other people loved him. That we also lost him. That some of us spent seven years not running."

"What do you want?" It came out as a whisper. She made herself say it again, louder. "What do you want from me?"

Voss rose at last. He crossed to the window and looked out over the rail yard, where floodlamps turned the fog into a pale, churning sea and freight cars sat in their sidings like sleeping animals. When he spoke, he spoke to the glass.

"The Serran River rises above the escarpment. There are works up there—old consortium engineering stations, abandoned since the disaster, sealed and forgotten. The consortium has hired me to mount an expedition. Officially: to survey a rail route through the gorge your report condemned as unbuildable. Unofficially—" he turned, and his eyes were the coldest thing in the cold room, "—unofficially, I intend to reach those works and take what's in them. Every record. Every letter. Everything Renwick and his masters wrote about why a sound bridge needed to fail."

The words landed strangely, like stones thrown into deep water—she heard them but couldn't yet feel where they sank. "Why the escarpment? Why now?"

"Because the valley is worth more empty than occupied. Because eleven deaths were cheaper than fair purchase. Because men like Renwick write arithmetic instead of epitaphs." He came back toward her, and she saw now that beneath the coldness he was shaking, very slightly, a tremor in the hands of a man who had carried something heavy for a long way. "And because I need someone who knows that gorge. Someone who surveyed it. Someone whose name is on the condemnation report—which means, Ivo, that your signature is the first thing anyone will look for when the truth comes out. You're not invisible anymore. You're the most visible woman in this province, and you don't even know it yet."

"I could refuse."

"You could." He picked up the contract—a single page, dense with clauses—and held it out. "But here is what refusal looks like. Tomorrow morning these letters go to the provincial registrar, with your name attached as the falsifying surveyor. You lose your license. You lose every alias you've built, because aliases collapse the moment the true name becomes famous. You spend the rest of your life as the woman who killed the Greywater eleven—including, in every account ever written, her own brother. Or—" he shook the page gently, "—you lead my expedition up the Serran within the month, and when it's done, when I have what I need, these letters burn. All of them. Yours included. You walk away clean, and no one living ever knows."

It was a trap built perfectly, the way Voss built everything, each clause bearing the load it was designed to bear. She could see the whole structure of it at a glance, and she hated him for the elegance, and she hated herself more for the relief that moved through her underneath the dread—because he was offering her the one thing she had run seven years to find: a version of the story where she was innocent.

And some tired, buried part of her whispered that she didn't believe in that story anymore.

"And if the expedition kills us?" she asked. "That gorge took a bridge. It'll take boats. It'll take people."

"Then we'll be dead," Voss said simply, "and the letters go to the registrar with my will. I keep receipts, Dravenmoor. Even past the grave. Especially past the grave."

Outside, the midnight freight began to move—a long groan of couplings taking up slack, then wheels, then the slow thunder of it pulling out through the fog toward somewhere else. Ivo watched the window light shudder with its passing and felt the old hunger rise in her, the animal longing to be on that train, to be nowhere, to be no one.

But Voss had just explained to her, with receipts, that *no one* was a place she could no longer afford to live.

She took the contract. The paper was warm from his hand, or cold from hers; she couldn't tell anymore. She read it standing, twice, while he watched her with the patience of a man who had already calculated the answer to four decimal places.

"One month," she said finally. "I choose the crew. Every hand on this expedition answers to me on the river, not to you. And Voss—if I find out you've lied to me about what's up there, I will put you off the boat myself, contracts be damned."

Something flickered across his face then, quick and unreadable—the first expression all night he hadn't chosen in advance. "Agreed," he said.

She signed. Her handwriting, for the first time in seven years, was her own.

At the door she paused, one hand on the iron frame, and asked the question she'd been carrying since the letters came out of the case. "Teo's stake. From the Kestrel Bend tunnel. Was it in the annex too?"

Voss's silence lasted three seconds too long.

"No," he said.

She walked out into the fog and the coal-smell and the crying of couplings, down the iron stairs of Harrow's Rest, and began, right there in the rail yard at one in the morning, to make a list of the damned—the disgraced, the indebted, the unemployable—who might agree to follow her up a river that killed bridges. It would have to be a crew of people with nothing left to lose.

She knew exactly where to find them. She had been one of them for seven years.
