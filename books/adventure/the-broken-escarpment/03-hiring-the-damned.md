## 3. Hiring the Damned

The hiring hall in Corven's Landing smelled of wet wool, lamp oil, and the particular despair of men who had been told no too many times. Ivo stood at the trestle table with a ledger open before her and let them come to her one by one, reading faces the way she read contour lines—looking for the places where the ground fell away.

She needed eleven. She had budgeted for nine and a contingency she did not name aloud. Voss had given her a purse with consortium stamps on the seals, heavy enough to make the drawer of her coat sag, and a list of requirements so precise it might have been written by a machine: strong backs, river experience, no questions about destination beyond what the contract stated, and above all, discretion. *Discretion*, in consortium language, meant shame. Everyone they hired would have a reason to keep their mouth shut, and everyone they hired would know that was why they'd been hired, and everyone would pretend otherwise. It was, Ivo thought, the most honest dishonesty she'd ever been asked to conduct.

The first morning produced ditch-diggers and drunks. The second produced a man who set his hands on her table like he was laying claim to it.

"Maret Sorrel," he said. "You want someone to move rock."

He was fifty, maybe older, built like something the river had failed to finish eroding. His forearms were a lattice of old powder burns, pale scars over darker skin, the map of a career written on him in a language everyone could read. Ivo read it. So did every other man in the hall, which was why they gave him space at the edges of the room.

"Your license was pulled after the Halloway cut," she said.

"My license was pulled because the Halloway cut collapsed, and the collapse was blamed on my charges when it belonged to the shale." He said it without heat, the way a man repeats a thing he has said so many times the words have worn smooth. "I told them the strata wouldn't hold a vertical face. They wanted vertical anyway. Eleven months later, half the mountain came down on their schedule instead of mine."

"Eleven men died at Halloway?"

"Fourteen." His eyes were the color of river water over slate. "You'll want to check my references. My references are all dead or lying."

Ivo looked at him a long moment. There was a version of this conversation where she asked the careful questions, weighed liability, moved him along. But she had spent seven years learning exactly how it felt to be the person a report got wrong, and she knew the sound of a man telling the truth nobody wanted.

"I need someone who can bring down a tunnel face in the Kestrel Bend narrows without bringing down the narrows," she said. "Can you do that?"

Something shifted in his face—not hope, nothing so generous, but the recognition of work worth doing. "Show me the stone and I'll tell you what it will forgive."

* * *

The pilot found her, which should have warned her.

She'd posted no notice for river men; the Serran above the falls wasn't a route you advertised. But on the third evening, as she sat in the back room of the Anchorline counting charges and rope lengths by lamplight, a woman filled the doorway—tall, weathered to the color of old rope, with a scar that split one eyebrow and gave her permanently skeptical expression an exclamation point.

"You're Dravenmoor." Not a question. "Sable Ferren. I ran freight up the Serran for nine years before your people closed it."

"I don't have people."

"You worked for the consortium. Close enough." She dropped into the chair across from Ivo uninvited, and Ivo noted the way she did it—the economy of a body that had learned not to waste motion on boats in current. "They condemned the upper Serran the same season they condemned the escarpment line. Same engineers. Same reports. Funny thing about those reports—I ran that water nine years, and none of the hazards in them matched any hazard I ever saw."

Ivo kept her face still. "What do you want, Ferren?"

"Passage money. And the chance to run water I've been told doesn't exist anymore." She leaned forward. "I heard you're going upstream. All the way up. I don't care where or why. I care that somebody's finally paying to find out what's actually up there instead of what some desk-man wrote about it."

"And if the desk-men were right?"

"Then I've been wrong for nine years and I'll drown knowing." She smiled, and the scar jumped. "I'm cheap, too. That's the grudge tax. I charge less because I want it more."

There was a lesson in that, Ivo thought later, watching Ferren walk out into the rain with the particular stride of a woman who trusted her own balance more than any footing offered her. The consortium had made its enemies efficient. Every person it discarded came back sharper at the edges.

* * *

Ansel Quill arrived last, and wrong.

The others came to the table with their damage held out like credentials. Ansel came in from the rain shaking off his coat like a man arriving at a dinner party, carrying a leather medical case worn soft at the corners, and asked if the chair by the stove was taken before sitting in it.

"I saw the posting," he said. "Expedition doctor, remote service, duration unknown. That's either very good pay or very bad intentions."

"Both," Ivo said.

He laughed—a real laugh, quick and unguarded, and then seemed surprised at himself for it. He was younger than she'd expected, thirty perhaps, with ink stains on his fingers that had nothing to do with medicine and a way of looking at her that lasted a half-second too long to be professional. "Dr. Ansel Quill. Late of the Meridian Charity Hospital, formerly of several other places that stopped being able to afford me."

"Why did they stop?"

"Because I treated the people who couldn't pay and billed the people who could at rates the administrators found insulting to the latter." He opened his case as he spoke, showing her the contents unasked: sutures, splints, a surgeon's kit kept with a precision that contradicted everything else about him. "I can pull teeth, set bones, deliver babies, and remove arrows. I assume the expedition offers opportunities in most of those categories."

"Fever, probably. Drowned lung. Crush injuries."

"All specialties of mine." He closed the case and looked at her directly, and there it was again—that too-long look, but this time she understood it differently. He was reading her the way she read stone. "And you? What's your injury, Miss Dravenmoor? Everyone on these expeditions has one. Mine's pride. The blaster's is grief wearing anger's coat. Yours is—" he paused, and she braced for it, whatever perceptive cruelty was coming— "you haven't slept in a week. I can see it in your hands. You're holding the table edge to stay upright."

Ivo let go of the table edge. "You're hired. Stay out of my business."

"Those are usually the same instruction," he said mildly, and shook her hand, and his grip was warm and steady and entirely without agenda, which frightened her more than Voss's letters ever had.

* * *

Voss arrived on the fifth day to inspect her progress, and the friction began within the hour.

"The route goes through Kestrel Bend," he said, standing over her charts in the Anchorline's back room while rain hammered the shutters. "The old tunnel. Straight through."

"No." Ivo tapped the map. "The tunnel's been sealed seven years. Half of it's down—we don't know how much. We portage around the bend, take the ridge line, come at the upper gorge from the east."

"That adds nine days."

"It adds nine days of living crew."

Voss's finger came down on the tunnel entrance marked on her chart, pressing hard enough to whiten the nail. "The contract specifies the survey corridor. The corridor runs through Kestrel Bend. I didn't hire you to improvise geography, Dravenmoor."

"You hired me because I'm the only person alive who knows what's under that mountain." She didn't raise her voice; she never raised her voice, and somehow that made the room quieter than shouting would have. "I signed the report that condemned that line, Voss. Whatever's in that tunnel, I put it there. Which means I'm the only one who gets to decide how we meet it."

It was the closest she had come to saying it aloud in seven years, and she watched it land on him—not shock, because he'd known all along, but something colder: satisfaction at hearing his leverage confirmed. He straightened slowly.

"We go through the tunnel," he said. "But we go through it your way. Your methods, your sequence. If it costs us days, that's your debt to explain."

"Days are cheap," Ivo said. "I've been paying in worse currency for years."

He left without another word, and the rain filled the silence behind him.

* * *

They departed on a gray morning with the river running high and brown with snowmelt, eleven souls and forty days of supplies crammed onto two flatboats and Ferren's patched-up sternwheeler, the *Lark*. Maret Sorrel stood at the bow with his arms crossed, watching the water like a man greeting an old adversary. Ansel moved among the crew checking hands and feet for the marks of cold, and Ivo caught herself watching him do it, and made herself stop.

Ferren brought the *Lark* alongside the landing stage and looked up at Ivo on the deck. "Nine years I've argued with a piece of paper," she said. "Today I get to argue with the river instead. Better odds."

Below, the current took the boats, and the gorge walls rose on either side, and Ivo stood at the stern watching Corven's Landing shrink to a smudge and then to nothing. Behind her lay seven years of burned notes and false names and permanent motion. Ahead lay the sealed dark of Kestrel Bend, and whatever Voss actually wanted from the source of the Serran, and a doctor who had looked at her hands and seen what she refused to.

Ansel appeared beside her at the rail, offering a tin cup of coffee without comment. She took it. Their shoulders almost touched.

"First night on the water," he said. "Try to sleep. If you can't, I'll be awake anyway. Doctors always are."

The river carried them upstream, into the country her own signature had killed, and Ivo drank the coffee, and did not sleep, and for the first time in seven years, did not entirely mind the company of her insomnia.
