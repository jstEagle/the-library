## 4. Upstream

The Serran gorge announced itself long before we entered it. For two days we had heard it as a sound beneath the sound of the river—a low, grinding patience, stone being argued against stone—and on the third morning the walls rose up on either side of us like the pages of a book closing. Grey limestone streaked with rust and old water, so tall that the morning light reached the channel only in a thin gold seam, and the flatboats slid into that seam one after another, four of them, riding low with rope, blasting powder, timber, and eleven souls who had each, in some manner or other, been asked to leave somewhere.

I had signed on as expedition doctor, which is to say I had signed on to be needed. It is a particular kind of hunger, and I recognized it in the others: the pilot, Maret, who steered the lead boat with a grudge against the consortium so old and polished it had become an heirloom; the blaster, Oskar Fenk, disgraced out of the Kessel works, whose hands never stopped moving, checking, rechecking; and the surveyors, quiet men who watched Ivo Dravenmoor the way sailors watch a barometer.

And Ivo. I watched her too, but not the way they did.

* * *

From the first morning aboard, I understood that Dravenmoor slept the way a miser spends—rarely, and never all at once. She took the middle watch on the lead boat, then the dawn watch, then found reasons to remain awake through the ones between. She sat at the bow with the current charts spread across her knees, though she had drawn them herself and could have recited them backward. When I brought the coffee pot down the line of boats at first light, hers was the cup still full from the night before, gone cold and bitter as ditchwater.

"You should sleep," I told her on the second morning.

"I sleep."

"You don't. I've been watching."

"Then you've been wasting your watch, Doctor." She did not look up from the charts. Her voice was not unkind; it was walled, the way a fort is walled—built to be lived behind, not to be admired.

I let it go, because a man learns early in that trade when a door is locked and when it is merely heavy. But I kept watching, because watching was half my work and the other half was pretending I wasn't.

She denied herself everything else with the same discipline. She ate last and least, taking the heel of the bread and the thin part of the stew, waving off the cook's protests with a flick of two fingers. When the rain came through the gorge on the second day, sideways, cold as a debt collector, and Maret rigged the oilcloth over the stern where the crew huddled, Ivo stayed in it. She stood at the bow with water running off her jaw and her collar, reading the water, reading the rock, reading—though I did not yet know it—something older than either. She rationed herself the way a besieged garrison rations grain, as though comfort were a currency she had stopped being allowed to spend.

I had patched enough men to know the difference between austerity and penance. This was penance. I did not yet know for what. I only knew that whatever it was, it weighed enough that she carried it standing up.

* * *

The winch accident happened on the fourth day, at the narrows below the old Kestrel Bend works, where the channel pinched and the current ran green and fast over drowned timber.

We were warping the second boat up along the cliff-side ring bolts—old iron eyes the rail company had set a generation ago, rusted to lace but sound enough if you trusted them gently. Ivo ran the winch herself. She would not delegate it; I had noticed she never delegated anything that could fail. Fenk fed the line, Maret called the water, and I sat amidships with my kit open, because that stretch of rock had a look I did not like, the look of a place that collects debts.

The line parted at the third bolt. Not snapped—*parted*, the way old wire does, all at once and without argument, and the winch handle came around with the whole fury of the recoil behind it. I saw it coming and I saw her see it, saw her choose in the half-second between—not to jump clear, but to throw her forearm across the handle and hold it, because Fenk was in the handle's arc and she was not.

The crack of it went through the boat like a pistol shot. She went down on one knee, and the boat swung, and Maret was shouting, and in all of it Ivo made no sound at all. That was the thing I would remember longest: she made no sound.

I had her arm across a supply crate before the boat finished swinging. The forearm was wrong below the elbow—not broken, I thought, not broken, but the skin had split along the ridge where the handle had struck, a long ugly mouth of it, welling dark.

"Hold still."

"I'm holding still."

"You're holding still the way a held breath holds still." I cleaned it, and she watched me work with that flat, level attention she gave to everything, as if pain were data. Under my hands her arm was rigid, and beneath the rigidity, fine as a wire under cloth, a tremor.

"You could have moved," I said. "You had time."

"Fenk didn't."

"You didn't know that when you reached for the handle."

She said nothing. I stitched—four of them, small and neat, and I will confess I took a pride in my stitches and was annoyed at how much I wanted them to be beautiful, and for whom. She watched every one go in.

"It'll scar," I told her, winding the bandage. "You'll wear it the rest of your life."

"Most things leave scars. At least this one's honest about it."

I tied off the bandage and did not let go of her wrist right away. I meant to let go. There is a professional moment where you finish and you withdraw, and I missed it—deliberately, I'll say that now, though I told myself otherwise for weeks. I held her wrist a moment longer than medicine required, my thumb over her pulse, feeling it run fast and steady as the current under the boats.

"Thank you, Doctor," she said, and her voice had gone careful, the way ground goes careful over a hollow.

"Ansel," I said. "You've bled on me. I think we're past *Doctor*."

Something moved through her face then, quick as a fish under ice—something startled, almost alarmed—and she withdrew her arm and stood and was at the winch again before I could say another word, checking the replacement line bolt by bolt, as though the accident had been a personal failure and the whole river was watching for her to repeat it.

* * *

That evening we made camp on a gravel shelf below the old works, and the gorge did what the gorge does at dusk: it turned the river to hammered pewter and filled the hollows with a blue so deep it seemed less like light than like water standing on end. Fenk built a fire small enough to hide from the sky. Maret drank from a bottle he pretended was medicine. The surveyors slept the sleep of men who trusted their leader absolutely, which troubled me more than any of it.

Ivo sat apart, as she always did, at the edge of the firelight, her bandaged arm cradled against her, and I brought her the coffee I had kept back, hot, from the bottom of the pot.

"I didn't ask for it."

"No," I said, and gave it to her anyway.

She held the cup in both hands and looked into it. For a while the only sounds were the river and the fire and Maret's bottle, and I stood there like a fool with nothing left to offer, about to retreat, when she said, without looking up:

"Why do you do that?"

"Do what?"

"Give things without wanting something back. Nobody does that. Nobody *can* do that. There's always a price; people just don't put it on the bill."

I crouched down to her level, at the edge of her firelight, careful not to step inside it uninvited. "Maybe I just like the giving," I said.

"Nobody likes the giving."

"I do. It's the only part of the trade that ever made sense to me. Everything else—fees, favors, debts, the whole ledger of it—I never learned the arithmetic. I only ever learned how to hold a wrist, or close a wound, or sit with a man while he's frightened. There's no bill for that. There never was."

She looked at me then, straight on, for the first time since the winch. The firelight was in her eyes and behind her eyes was something I could not name yet—something cornered, but not by me. By the kindness itself, I think. Kindness had come at her like weather through a broken window, and she had no wall for it, because all her walls faced outward, toward threats, toward Voss and the consortium and the river, and here was a thing that did not come like a threat at all, and it frightened her more than all of it put together.

"You should be careful, Ansel," she said quietly.

"Of what?"

"Of me."

"I patch hands for a living," I said. "I know exactly how dangerous you are. You held a winch handle to save a man who'd have done it for you, and you didn't make a sound. That's the whole of what I know, and none of it scares me."

She almost smiled. It was the almost that undid me—there was a whole face behind that almost, a face I had not seen and she had not shown anyone in seven years, if the hollows under her eyes told the truth, and I suspected they did.

"You should sleep," I said again, and this time she didn't answer with a deflection. She looked at the fire, and the firelight moved on her face, and she said, "After this watch," which was what she always said, and I rose to go.

But as I turned, Maret came up from the waterline, quick and low, with his bottle forgotten in his hand, and said the thing that ended the evening and started the next trouble:

"Dravenmoor. There's a light up ahead, past the bend. Where the tunnel's supposed to be sealed. Somebody's burning something in there—or somebody's *home*."

Ivo stood. She stood the way a door stands when the wind takes it, all at once, and I saw her hand go to her bandaged arm without knowing it did, and I saw her look upriver into the dark where the light was, and every wall she had ever built went up at once, higher than the gorge itself.

"Nobody sleeps," she said. "Get the boats tied down."

And we stood there in the blue dark, eleven souls on a gravel shelf, watching a fire we could not explain burn inside a tunnel her own report had condemned and sealed, while the Serran ran past us, cold and indifferent, carrying something down from the bend that caught the firelight and flashed—small, bright, and metal.
