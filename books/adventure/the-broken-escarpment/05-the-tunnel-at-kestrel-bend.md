## 5. The Tunnel at Kestrel Bend

The mountain had been breathing all morning.

Ivo felt it through the soles of her boots before she heard it—a slow exhalation of cold air from the tunnel mouth, then a long, grudging inhalation, as though the escarpment were deciding whether to let them in. The men called it draft. She knew better. Seven years ago she had stood on the lip of this same bore with a theodolite and a clipboard and a lie in her throat, and the mountain had breathed on her then too, patient as a creditor.

"Kestrel Bend," Rennick said beside her, spitting into the scree. The pilot had come up from the boats for no reason he'd admit to; he stood with his arms crossed like a man attending a funeral he disapproved of. "You're sure it's this one."

"I'm sure it's the one on the map."

"That's not what I asked."

She didn't answer. Ahead of them the tunnel mouth showed itself through a curtain of alder and rockfall—a black arch maybe twenty feet high, half-swallowed by the slope, its masonry collar furred with moss gone silver in the shade. Someone had sealed it after. Ivo could see the seam where fresh stone met old: a plug of mortared rubble laid across the bore like a tombstone laid flat. Her company's work. Her company's shame, poured by men who'd been told only that the tunnel was unsafe, which was true, and that nothing inside was worth saving, which was also true, if you didn't count eleven names.

Voss came up the trail at his usual pace, unhurried, notebook already open. He wore the same gray coat he wore everywhere, and somehow it was never dirty.

"Bore's two hundred forty yards to the daylight shaft," he said, reading from memory or from some paper Ivo hadn't seen. "Collapsed at roughly one-forty, per your own report." He glanced up, and there was the small cruelty in his eyes that never quite became a smile. "Per your own report, Miss Dravenmoor. You wrote it very precisely. It's almost the only thing you ever wrote precisely."

"The charge pattern's set," she said. "We blow the seal at noon, vent for an hour, then go in on the left wall where the arch is soundest. Tam can walk us through the bad stretch."

Tam Oduya was already at the face, checking his primer lines with the tenderness other men spent on children. The disgraced blaster. He'd been drummed out of the Serran Copper works for a misfire that killed no one but cost him everything anyway, and he handled explosives now the way penitents handle candles—carefully, and with his head bowed. He looked up at Ivo and nodded once. Ready when you are.

She checked her watch. Ten minutes to noon. Somewhere below, the river ran green and loud through the gorge, and above, a hawk turned in the blue slot of sky between the walls. Everything ordinary. Everything waiting.

"You've been quiet since Kestrel camp," Ansel said, coming up behind her. The doctor carried his kit even up here, out of habit or superstition, and his sleeves were rolled to the elbow, forearms brown from the river days. He stopped at her shoulder and studied the sealed mouth of the tunnel without asking what it was. He was good at that—studying things without demanding they explain themselves. It was the quality she distrusted most about him. "You slept maybe two hours last night. I heard you walking the bank."

"Checking the moorings."

"At three in the morning."

"Boats drift."

He let it go, the way he always did, and she hated how much room he gave her, because the room made her want to fill it with truth, and truth was the one cargo she could not afford to carry. She checked her watch again. Noon.

"Fire in the hole," Tam called, soft as a prayer.

* * *

The blast was less a sound than a change in the world's pressure. Ivo felt it in her teeth, in the wet of her eyes, in the old fracture line behind her sternum that never quite healed. Dust boiled out of the tunnel mouth in a gray lung, rolled over them, and settled. When it thinned, the seal was gone—blown inward, swallowed by whatever dark the company had filled the bore with—and the mountain's breath came out cold and mineral and endless.

They vented an hour. Then Voss said, "Lamps," and they went in.

Inside, the air was ten degrees colder and tasted of iron and standing water. Their lamps made small trembling circles on the masonry, and beyond those circles the darkness had texture—Ivo could feel it against her skin, dense as wool. The left wall held, as her report had promised it would, because she had written that part honestly. That was the joke she'd carried seven years: the report was mostly honest. Every measurement true, every load calculation correct, every warning accurate—except the one paragraph, four sentences long, where she had written that the Kestrel span's piers were sound when she knew they were not, because a consortium director had stood behind her chair while she drafted it, and her brother's wages, and her own, had been standing behind her brother.

Four sentences. Eleven men. One bridge down into the Serran on a market morning, with a freight train folded into it like paper.

Her lamp found the collapse at one hundred forty yards exactly, because of course it was exact; she had measured the rubble field herself from the far side, before. They worked through it for three hours, Tam calling holds, Rennick hauling debris with a fury that had nothing to do with debris, Ansel moving among them with water and splints and that terrible gentleness. And Ivo walked the left wall with her hand trailing the stone, the way you'd trail your hand along a banister in a house you grew up in, and told herself she wasn't looking for anything.

At the two-hundred-yard mark, her fingers stopped.

A stake. Iron-topped, cedar-shafted, driven into a drilled socket in the living rock—the kind her company used for permanent bench marks, the kind that outlasted rails and bridges and men. Moss had climbed its lower third. But it stood. Upright. Plumb. After seven years of flood and frost and the mountain's slow shrug, it stood plumb, because her brother had set it plumb, because Teodor Dravenmoor had never in his life set anything crooked.

Ivo put her palm flat against it. The iron cap was cold enough to burn.

*Teo.* His name arrived not as thought but as weight, a full tonne of it set down on her chest all at once, so that she had to put her shoulder to the wall and breathe in short, careful sips. This was his section. She remembered now—remembered him laughing about it on the last leave, saying the Bend survey was the best assignment on the line because the light came down the ventilation shaft at midday like something holy. *You'll see it when you sign off my figures,* he'd said. *Best light in the mountains.* She had signed off his figures. She had signed off everything. Four sentences, and the light that came down the shaft at midday fell on rubble instead.

Her vision went grainy at the edges. Grief, she had learned, was not sadness; sadness was weather. Grief was structural. It was the mountain itself pressing in, and you lived inside it or you did not.

"Dravenmoor." Voss's voice, back down the bore, bouncing off the masonry. "Status on the far face?"

She straightened. She worked the stake loose with both hands—slowly, so the sound wouldn't carry—rocking it in its socket until the mountain gave it up with a grudging grind. It was four feet of cedar and iron and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything. She wrapped it in the oilcloth from her pack, lashed it flat along the outside of the pack frame beneath the bedroll, where a length of spare timber would be unremarkable to any eye but hers.

"Face is clean," she called back. "We can daylight by tomorrow evening if the shaft chamber holds."

Voss emerged around the rubble nose, lamp raised, and swept the wall where the stake had been. His light paused—one heartbeat, two—on the empty drilled socket, and Ivo kept her breathing level and her face set to the professional blankness she'd worn so long it had grown into the bone.

"Something was set here," Voss said.

"Bench mark. Standard practice." She heard her own voice come out dry and bored, and marveled distantly at the craftsmanship of it. "Company pulled the recoverable ones when they sealed the line. They missed that one."

"Mm." He wrote something in the notebook. He always wrote something. "Your brother worked this section, didn't he. I've read the crew rosters."

The pressure came back, a hand closing on her sternum. "Everyone worked every section. We were short-handed."

"Of course we were." He clicked the pen shut. "Get your rest, Miss Dravenmoor. Tomorrow we reach the shaft chamber, and I want you sharp. What's above us is the reason we came."

He walked back toward the bore mouth, lamp swinging, and Ivo stood alone in the black with her hand pressed flat over the oilcloth bundle riding against her spine, feeling the iron cap's cold through canvas and wool as if it hadn't warmed at all.

Ansel found her there ten minutes later. He didn't ask why she was standing motionless in the dark with her eyes open. He just handed her the canteen and stood close enough that his shoulder touched hers, and stayed.

Above them, faint through two hundred feet of rock, came a sound none of them could name yet—a deep, rhythmic knocking, like someone tapping a pipe. Like a signal. Like a question being asked of the mountain, patiently, over and over.

Rennick's voice floated up from the bore mouth, tight: "Anybody else hear that?"
