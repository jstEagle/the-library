## 6. What the River Remembers

The Serran spoke to Ansel before he ever saw it.

He heard it through the tent canvas at three in the morning, a low grinding under its voice, like a man talking with stones in his mouth. He had treated enough bodies to know the difference between a river that was moving and a river that was *carrying* something. This one was carrying. The whole gorge knew it. Even the mules stood hipshot and uneasy in their pickets, ears swiveled toward the black water.

He lay awake and listened, and thought about Ivo Dravenmoor's hands.

She had come to his tent the night before for the dressing change on her palm—the winch had taken the skin off it in one long ribbon, and she had let him work without a word, watching the mountains instead of him as though he were merely weather. Her hand had been steady. Her pulse, where his fingers rested at her wrist, had been going like a bird trapped in a wall. She never flinched at pain. She flinched at kindness. He had learned to offer it sideways, obliquely, the way you fed a half-wild animal—leave the thing down and step back and pretend not to watch while she took it.

Now he dressed by candle stub and went out into the rain to check the boats.

* * *

The flatboats lay tied in the eddy below camp, riding high and light, and that was the first wrong thing. A loaded boat should sit deep. Ansel put his lantern over the gunwale of the nearest and saw the cargo shifted forward, lashed badly, crates riding bow-heavy—and saw, wedged under the thwart, the coil of spare line that should have been strung between the boats as a safety tether, lying slack and unused.

Voss came up behind him out of the rain, silent as always, his oilskin beaded silver in the lantern light.

"Something troubles you, Doctor?"

"The boats are trimmed wrong." Ansel kept his voice level. "And there's no cross-line run. If we take water in the narrows tomorrow, these two will part company and we'll lose whatever they're carrying."

"We won't take water in the narrows."

"You've read the river?"

"I've read the *survey*." Voss smiled the way a ledger smiles. "Dravenmoor's own charts. The stage gauge at this season runs eighteen inches below flood line. There is no risk."

Ansel looked at the river. It was not eighteen inches below anything. It was a brown muscle flexing in the dark, hauling whole trees along its spine, and every pilot's instinct he'd absorbed secondhand from Halloran—the riverman with the grudge and the good eyes—said the same thing: the Serran was gathering itself somewhere upstream, in some hanging valley or breached moraine, and when it let go it would not consult anyone's charts.

"Your survey is seven years old," Ansel said.

"Rivers don't change."

"Rivers are the only things that do nothing else."

Voss laughed softly, adjusted his collar against the rain, and walked back toward his tent. Behind him, in Ivo's shelter, a candle burned. Ansel could see her shadow through the canvas, bent over her maps, still working at an hour when sleep was the only sane occupation. She worked the way other people prayed.

He went to bed and did not sleep, and the river talked all night with its mouth full of stones.

* * *

It let go at noon.

There was no drama to the warning—one moment the Serran ran brown and swollen, the next it rose, visibly, the way water rises in a lock, except no lock on earth filled this fast. Halloran saw it first and began to scream, and his scream did what nothing else could have: it moved men faster than thought. The crew was mid-portage around the fish ladder rapids, half the cargo hauled up onto the shelf rock, the boats still in the eddy with four men aboard lashing down what remained.

Ansel would remember the next ninety seconds for the rest of his life in fragments, the way he remembered bad nights in the cholera camps—in pieces that refused to make a whole.

Halloran running the bank, waving his hat, bellowing *high ground, get them out*. The four men in the boats looking upstream at a wall of water that had been a bend of river a heartbeat ago. And Brannock—the disgraced blaster, the quiet man with powder-burned forearms and a laugh like a door hinge—dropping his load of crate-handles, sprinting down the shingle, and cutting the boats loose.

Not untying them. Cutting them. One stroke of his belt knife through the painter of the lead boat, then wading chest-deep to slash the second, because knots freeze under panic and knives don't. Because if those boats went downstream pinned and wrapped against the rocks, the expedition died right there, a hundred miles from anywhere, and Brannock understood that arithmetic better than any of them.

The flood took the shingle where he stood.

Ansel saw it plainly: the wave lifting Brannock like a leaf, the man twisting once in the air—not reaching for help, Ansel noted later, but reaching for the second boat's trailing line, getting one fist around it, holding it clear of the rocks so it wouldn't foul—as the water folded over him and closed like a book.

They found the boats two miles down, beached and battered but alive, Halloran having ridden the lead boat through the worst of it with the tiller bar bent double in his hands. They found Brannock at dusk, in a willow brake, laid out neat as Sunday by the retreating water. His fist was still closed. When Ansel pried it open, gently, there was a length of frayed hemp rope inside, torn clean off the boat's ring.

He had held on to the end. He had made sure the boat got away first.

* * *

They buried him above the flood line, in ground Ansel had to pickaxe open because the mountain up here was more stone than soil. The crew stood in the drizzle while he said the few words there were to say. Voss checked his watch twice. Ivo stood at the foot of the grave with her hat in her hands and her face doing absolutely nothing at all, which was how Ansel knew she was somewhere very far away and very deep down, in a place with eleven graves in it already.

Afterward, he found her at the riverbank, washing the grave dirt from her hands in water still brown with the flood.

"You knew," he said.

She didn't turn. "Knew what."

"That the river was rising. You grew up on rail crews; your father read water before he read letters—I've heard Halloran tell it. You saw those boats trimmed wrong. You saw no cross-line. You said nothing."

"Ivo Dravenmoor doesn't countermand orders," she said, wringing out her sleeve. "I'm under contract. Voss holds the command."

"He holds the contract. You hold the expertise, and every man on this crew knows it. You could have stopped the loading with a word." He stepped closer, close enough to see the rain beading in her lashes, the exhaustion carved under her eyes like erosion. "Brannock is dead because someone decided a schedule mattered more than a tether line. Was that you, or him?"

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind her face, some vast pressure against a door. He had seen that look before, in men coming out of fevers, on the edge of saying the true thing.

"It was the gauge reading," she said. "Eighteen inches below flood. Voss showed me the chart himself. Any surveyor would have trusted it."

And there it was. Not the lie itself—he couldn't have proven it was a lie, not then—but the *shape* of one, the too-smooth surface, the way her voice went flat and official, reciting facts like a witness coached for testimony. He had spent ten years listening to people tell him things in field hospitals and quarantine tents, and he knew the sound of a truth being carried carefully, and he knew the sound of a lie being carried carefully, and they were different sounds. Hers was the second.

Because he had seen her own field notes. Two nights ago, when she'd slept—or hadn't slept—at last and he'd covered her against the cold, her journal had lain open on the crate beside her, and he had not meant to read it, but the page had been facing him, and surveyors write numbers the way priests write scripture, plain and impossible to mistake. Her own hand. *Stage gauge unreliable—readings predate upstream slide at Redwater. Recommend re-survey before narrows.* Dated six days ago.

She had known. She had written it down. And she had said nothing, because saying it would have meant confronting Voss, and confronting Voss meant opening whatever war was running underneath this expedition like groundwater, and Ivo Dravenmoor would bury a man rather than open that door.

Or rather—she would let a man be buried.

"Any surveyor would have trusted it," Ansel repeated quietly.

"Yes."

"You wrote a note recommending a re-survey. I saw it in your journal."

Her hands went still in the water. The river pulled at her sleeves, patient, insistent, the way it had pulled at Brannock. When she finally turned to look at him, her eyes were the color of the flooded Serran, and just as opaque, and just as full of things moving underneath.

"You read my journal."

"I read one page I couldn't avoid. I'm sorry for it. I'm not sorry for asking the question it raised." He held her gaze. "You can lie to Voss. You can lie to the tribunal you seem to think is waiting for you somewhere downriver. But don't lie to me while I'm standing in the water that killed a man today. I've earned better than that, and so has he."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, the river was already forgetting Brannock, folding him into its long brown memory with the trees and the silt and everything else it carried and never gave back.

"Ivo," Ansel said, softer. "What is it Voss actually wants up this river? Because it isn't a rail route. Nobody burns coal and money like this for a rail route."

Something in her posture changed—a settling, a decision being made or postponed, he couldn't tell which. She lifted her chin, and for the first time since he'd known her, she looked at him without armor and without deflection, and what was in her face frightened him more than the flood had.

"Ask me again at the source," she said. "When there's no river left between us and the answer. Ask me there, Ansel, and I swear to you I'll tell you everything—the report, the bridge, all of it. Everything I've been carrying since Kestrel Bend."

"And until then?"

"Until then," she said, turning back to the water, "help me carry the ones who didn't make it."

Above them, on the ridge, Casimir Voss stood alone in the rain, studying the gorge ahead with his field glasses raised—and lower, briefly, toward the narrow slot of the old sealed tunnel at Kestrel Bend, as if measuring it for something.
