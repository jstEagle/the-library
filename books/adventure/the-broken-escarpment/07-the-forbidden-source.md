## 7. The Forbidden Source

The river ended the way all rivers end—not with a wall but with a whisper.

Ivo stood in the shallows where the Serran broke apart into a hundred braided threads, each one thin as wire, spilling out of a cirque of black stone that held the sky like a cupped hand. Above the last of the water, wedged into a fold of the mountain, stood the ruins of the old pumping works: a timber headframe gone silver with weather, a tumbledown of sheds and flumes, and behind it all, cut into the rock itself, the dark mouth of an adit that no map she'd ever seen had bothered to name.

Three weeks upstream. Three weeks of flatboats hauled over gravel bars, of tunnels that breathed cold air into her face like the exhalations of something buried alive, of Voss walking at the head of the column with his ledger strapped to his back like a pilgrim's burden. She had told herself every night that they were mapping a rail route, that the contract was real, that the numbers in her notebook meant what numbers were supposed to mean.

She had stopped believing it somewhere around Kestrel Bend. But belief was a luxury; motion was survival. So she kept walking.

"Grades steepen past here," Pell said beside her, squinting up at the cirque. The pilot's grudge had softened into something almost companionable over the miles, though he still spoke to her the way a man speaks to weather—useful, untrusted. "You're not putting a line up that face. Nobody's putting a line up that face."

"No," Ivo said. "Nobody is."

Voss was already climbing toward the headframe, boots finding the old stair treads without hesitation, as if he'd walked them before. As if he'd read about them so many times they'd worn grooves in his mind. Ivo watched him go and felt the familiar cold open under her sternum—the cold she'd carried for seven years, the one that told her *run*.

She didn't run. She followed him up.

* * *

The pumping works smelled of creosote and rust and, beneath it, the mineral breath of deep stone. Snowmelt dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a clock. Voss had set a lantern on a survey table that someone had abandoned mid-task forty years ago—dividers still open, a pencil worn to a stub, a mug with a fossilized ring of tea at its bottom. He moved through the ruin like a man returning to his own house after a long war.

"You knew," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. Her voice came out flat, scraped clean by three weeks of river. "This was never a route survey. There's nothing to build here and you've known that since before you put my name on the contract."

Voss didn't turn. He crouched at a hatch in the floor—an iron door set flush with the planking, painted over so many times it had become part of the wood—and worked a folding key from inside his coat.

"I told you the consortium wanted a route through the escarpment," he said. "I never told you they believed one existed." The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "They wanted you, Dravenmoor. Specifically you. The woman who condemned the Kestrel Bridge. Because if anyone could certify, with a straight face and a licensed signature, that this valley could never be served by rail—it was you."

The cold under her sternum spread outward, down her arms, into her hands. "That's not—"

"Open the hatch."

"I said that's not how it happened."

"I know exactly how it happened." Now he looked up at her, and there was nothing triumphant in his face, which was somehow worse than triumph would have been. There was only the terrible patience of a man who had spent years assembling a case the way other men assemble cathedrals—one stone at a time, in the dark, alone. "I know better than you do, I think. Open the hatch, Ivo. Then we'll both know everything."

She should have turned around. She should have walked down through the silvered timbers, gathered the crew, pointed them downstream, and vanished the way she'd always vanished—burning notes, changing towns, leaving no trace but boot prints in mud that rain would forgive.

Instead she knelt and lifted the hatch.

* * *

Stairs went down into the mountain, cut rough, dry as bone. The lantern light swung shadows across walls scored with old tool marks, and the air grew warmer, which was wrong, which meant the rock around them was holding heat the way a body holds heat, and Ivo thought absurdly: *the mountain is alive down here.* At the bottom, the passage opened into a chamber that had been a company vault once—shelving of angle-iron, document cases stacked in military rows, and everywhere the fine grey silt of decades settling over paper that someone had thought important enough to bury like seed grain.

Voss began pulling cases without ceremony, setting them on the floor, opening them. His hands were steady. Hers were not.

"What am I looking at?" she asked.

"The Serran Consolidated's private engineering archive. Everything the consortium didn't burn in the spring of—" he checked a date on a folder cover, though Ivo suspected he knew it by heart— "of the year your bridge fell." He handed her a folio. Its cardboard cover was soft with age, its corners rounded by handling. "Start there."

She started there.

The first pages were routine: load calculations, steel certifications, correspondence about cement deliveries. Then a memorandum, typed, initialed by two men whose names she recognized from consortium letterheads she'd signed her life away to—and the memorandum was short, four paragraphs, and she read the third paragraph three times because her eyes kept sliding off it the way a boot slides off ice:

*...it is the assessment of this office that the Kestrel crossing, as specified, will not sustain design loads beyond the seventh year of service. This is acceptable. A failure within the operational window will serve to condemn the valley corridor, depress land valuation ahead of acquisition, and foreclose competing access. Recommend inspection regime be assigned to personnel of demonstrated... flexibility...*

*Personnel of demonstrated flexibility.*

Her own name was on the next page. Not as author—as *assignment*. A staffing note. A line item. *Dravenmoor, I.—assigned final inspection, Kestrel span. Discretion advised.*

The chamber did not spin, though she braced herself against the shelving for it. That was the vertigo of it—the world refusing to move, refusing to crack open, while the ground truth of her entire adult life quietly inverted beneath her feet. Seven years. Seven years of burning notes and sleeping in rail-yard hotels with her back to the wall and waking at every whistle in the night certain the dead had come to collect. She had built her whole exile on a single foundation: *I did this. My hand wrote the lie. My brother died because of me.*

And now a stranger's memorandum, older than her crime, told her the lie had been commissioned. That she had been chosen the way a tool is chosen. That the bridge had been murdered and she had been hired as the alibi.

"They tested me," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, coming from someone standing in a vault under a mountain. "Before the bridge. They must have. Small falsifications. They watched to see if I'd—"

"They ran eleven crews through that valley before you," Voss said. "Ten refused to sign what needed signing. You were the eleventh." He said it without cruelty, which was its own kind of cruelty. "You weren't the fall, Dravenmoor. You were the instrument. And instruments get tuned."

"My brother." The words came out of her like something torn. "Tomas was on that span because I certified it. He trusted my—"

"Yes." Voss let the silence stand. He was good at that; she remembered it from the old days, from the rival surveys when they'd been young and hungry and honest in ways she could no longer reconstruct. "And that is precisely why the consortium will hang for it. Not because the memorandum proves sabotage—memoranda can be disavowed, signatories can die conveniently. But because you can testify. The instrument, testifying. They never planned for that. No one plans for the knife to pick itself up."

Above them, faint through the rock, came the sound of voices—Pell shouting something about the boats, Ansel's lower register answering. The expedition, going on with its evening, unaware that the reason for its suffering had just changed shape entirely.

Ivo set the folio down on the survey shelf with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might detonate. Her hands would not stop trembling, so she pressed them flat against the iron.

"Why show me this now?" she asked. "Why not at the hotel? Why drag us up the whole goddamned river?"

"Because I needed to know what you'd do when you learned the truth." Voss closed the case he'd been reading and looked at her across the lantern light. "Whether you'd take it and run—disappear with the proof, sell it, use it to buy yourself a new name in some country with no extradition. Or whether you'd finally stand still." He tapped the memorandum once, lightly, with one finger. "There's a second copy of everything, Ivo. In a bank, under a name you don't know. If anything happens to me between here and the tribunal, it surfaces anyway. So don't get any ideas."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. I would have." He almost smiled. "But understand what happens next. This evidence is worth a fortune to the consortium—enough to buy their survival. Enough to buy yours, ten times over. They'll offer you everything to help them bury it. And I'm offering you the alternative: testify, lose your license, lose your name, and watch them fall." He shouldered his pack, the ledger riding against his spine. "We talk terms tomorrow. Tonight, think about which of those women you want to be—the one who runs, or the one who stays."

He climbed the stairs and left her alone in the warm dark with the dead men's paperwork.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the mouth of the adit with her knees drawn up, watching the stars wheel over the cirque, and the folio lay in her lap like a sleeping animal she was afraid to wake.

Ansel found her there sometime past midnight—he always found her, that was the trouble with him—and lowered himself onto the cold stone beside her without asking questions, which was his way of asking the largest question of all.

"You look like a woman who's seen a ghost," he said.

"Worse." She stared at the black seam where the mountain met the sky. "I've seen a mirror."

His shoulder settled against hers, warm through two layers of wool, patient as the dripping snowmelt. Below them the river whispered out of the earth, beginning its long journey down the valley it had been lied about, and Ivo understood, with a clarity that felt less like relief than like freefall, that whatever she decided tomorrow morning in the lamplight of the pumping works, there would be no more running after it.

Either way, the motion stopped here.
