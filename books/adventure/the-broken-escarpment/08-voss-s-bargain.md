## 8. Voss's Bargain

The works above the source of the Serran were a city built for one purpose and then abandoned mid-sentence. Ivo walked its spine that evening—the raised timber walkway that had once carried ore carts down from the high benches—and felt the mountain breathing around her. Snowmelt ticked somewhere in the dark. The stamp mill's skeleton stood against the last light, its iron bones furred with frost, and the wind coming off the glacier above made a low, continuous note through the empty sluices, like a choir that had forgotten its words.

Voss was waiting for her at the surveyor's hut, the only structure still whole enough to hold a lamp. She had known he would be. He had spent three days cataloguing the records they'd found in the company vault—crates of them, water-stained but legible, wrapped in waxed paper by clerks who had presumably been told the documents would never be needed again. All afternoon she had heard him through the wall: the scrape of a chair, the dry whisper of pages, once a laugh so brief and joyless it might have been a cough.

She pushed the door open. He didn't look up.

"Sit down, Dravenmoor."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He squared a stack of papers against the table's edge with the precision of a man racking rifle cartridges. The lamplight caught the scar along his jaw, the one he'd carried since Kestrel Bend, back when they were both young and the mountain hadn't yet taught them what it cost. "Do you know what you're looking at? Really?"

"I know what you told me it was."

"What I *told* you." He finally raised his eyes. There was something almost gentle in them, and that frightened her more than cruelty would have. "I told you we were mapping a rail route. That was true. I told you the consortium wanted the escarpment surveyed. Also true. But I didn't tell you why a company that abandoned this valley eleven years ago would spend a fortune sending armed men up a river nobody wants." He tapped the stack. "Because they didn't abandon it. They *emptied* it. Look."

She looked. She had been looking at these papers for two days, but she had been looking at them the way you look at a wound you already know is yours—refusing the specifics. Now Voss spread them out for her like a surgeon laying out instruments, and she made herself see.

The bridge. *Her* bridge, the Greywater span, the one her report had condemned in all but name, the one that went down on a Tuesday morning in March with eleven men on it. There were requisitions here, dated two years before the collapse. Orders for inspection deferrals. A memo, initialed by three directors, authorizing the "managed retirement" of the span pending "revaluation of corridor assets." And beneath all of it, the thing that made her stomach turn over slowly, like a boat in heavy water: an appraisal of the valley itself. Timber, ore, water rights. Assessed at a fraction of its worth. Assessed, the appraiser's note said, *contingent upon removal of the rail corridor from service.*

They had killed the line to buy the valley. They had killed the line, and the line had killed eleven men, and she had signed the paper that made it legal.

"Ivo." Voss's voice was quiet. "You didn't falsify that report in a vacuum. You were tired, you were rushed, you were told the inspection schedule was already approved. They built the machine. You were the last gear in it. That's not absolution. But it's context, and context is worth money."

"Money." She heard her own voice go flat. "You dragged eleven people up a river that killed one of them to get paid."

"I dragged them up a river to get *leverage*." He stood, and for a moment he was just a tired man in a wet coat, older than she remembered him being. "Here's the bargain, and I'll only make it once. These records go to a tribunal in Meridian if I take them down the mountain myself. The consortium falls. Good. But tribunals subpoena witnesses, and witnesses get examined, and the first witness they'll examine is the surveyor who signed the Greywater inspection. Your name is in here, Ivo. Not as a victim. As an instrument."

He slid a single sheet across the table. Her own handwriting, seven years younger, seven years less careful. The inspection record for the Greywater span, the one she had rewritten in a hotel room with her brother's death three days cold, changing *condemned* to *deferred*, changing a date, changing everything.

"I can make this page disappear," Voss said. "I've had seven years to learn how. You help me sell it back to them—quietly, privately, the whole file, and they'll pay anything to keep it out of a courtroom. You get your cut. You get your name out of the record. You get to keep walking."

"And if I don't help you?"

"Then I bury it. All of it. The valley stays stolen, the dead stay unpaid, and you stay exactly what you are—a woman running down dead branch lines burning her notes." He smiled without warmth. "Or there's a third option. You take it to the tribunal yourself. You walk into that courtroom and hand them your own confession along with their crimes. And they take your license, and your name, and probably your freedom, and the valley gets its money and you get to be honest for the first time in your life. For about a decade."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the glacier wind rose and fell in the sluices.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"Since the day I found your name in these files. Which is why I came looking for you, Ivo. Not for the route. For this." He gathered the papers back into their stack, unhurried. "Think tonight. Give me your answer at first light. And I'd think about who you owe, if I were you. The dead don't pay debts. The living do."

She left the hut without answering. The cold outside was a physical thing, a hand pressed against her chest, and she walked the timber spine of the dead works with her collar up and her thoughts in pieces. Sell it. Bury it. Confess it. Three doors, and every one of them opened onto a version of herself she could not bear to be.

She was still walking when she heard the gravel shift behind her, and knew the weight of those footsteps before she turned around.

Ansel came up the walkway with his coat open and no lamp, as if the dark were a country he'd been born in. His hands were tucked in his sleeves. There was frost in his hair. He stopped three paces away, close enough that she could see the question already in his face, and she understood that he had been at the hut's window, or against its wall, or somewhere in the dark where men like Voss forgot to look.

"How much did you hear?"

"Enough." His voice was steady, but his eyes weren't. "The report, Ivo. Voss said you rewrote it. He said there's a page with your handwriting on it. I want to hear it from you. Not him. You."

The wind came down off the glacier and filled the silence, and she stood in it and understood that this was the moment everything had been walking toward—not the tribunal, not Voss's bargain, but this: a man who had stitched her hand at the winch and asked her nothing, now asking her the one thing she had spent seven years making sure no one would ever ask.

"If I tell you," she said, "you'll look at me differently."

"I've been looking at you for two months." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I've seen you not sleep. I've seen you give away your own rations and log yourself on the worst watches. I've seen you stand at the edge of every drop we crossed like you were measuring it for a coffin. Whatever it is, I already know it's heavy. Tell me anyway."

So she told him.

She told it plainly, because anything else would have been another kind of lying. The Greywater span, March, eleven years ago. The inspection she'd been ordered to rush. The hairline fracture in the third pier she had found and documented and then—because the company's engineer had laughed at her, because the schedule was approved, because her brother was on the crew and she wanted to believe the men who said the span would hold—she had rewritten. *Deferred* instead of *condemned*. A date moved. A signature that was hers.

Three days later the span went down at half past seven in the morning, and her brother Tomas went down with it, and ten men with him, and she had been running survey lines on dead railways ever since, burning her notes in camp stoves, leaving no trace, as if a person could outrun a page with her own handwriting on it.

When she finished, Ansel was silent for a long time. The sluices sang. Somewhere below, the river moved in the dark, patient as everything else in this valley.

"You didn't kill them," he said at last.

"The report did."

"The directors did. The men who wrote *managed retirement* in a boardroom while men crossed the span every morning." He stepped closer. "But you want it to be you. I know. It's easier to be the monster than to be the gear."

"Don't." Her voice came out ragged. "Don't make it smaller. I need it to be the size it is."

"Then let it be that size," he said, "and do the right thing at that size." He took her hand—the scarred one, the one he'd stitched—and held it between both of his, and his palms were cold and did not let go. "Voss will buy your silence or sell it. The consortium will do worse. But there's one person alive who can put that page in front of a tribunal with the truth attached to it, and it isn't Casimir Voss. It's you. Not because you owe them. Because you owe Tomas."

She closed her eyes. She had imagined this moment a hundred ways—confession as ruin, confession as relief—and never once as this: a man holding her hand in the freezing dark, asking nothing, offering less, just *stay here while it's true*.

"If I testify," she said, "I lose everything. My license. My name. Maybe my freedom. Seven years of running, and I end up exactly where I started, except everyone will know."

"No," Ansel said. "You end up somewhere you've never been. Standing still."

She looked at him then, really looked, and felt the last of the running go out of her like a tide.

* * *

She gave Voss his answer at first light, standing on the timber spine with the glacier burning pink behind her and the whole dead works listening.

"I'm taking it to the tribunal," she said. "Myself. My confession attached. And if you sell that page or bury it, I'll swear out a statement saying you found these records and suppressed them, and the tribunal can decide what that makes you."

Voss regarded her for a long moment, the wind pulling at his coat, and then he did something she had never seen him do in twenty years of rivalry. He laughed—really laughed, short and startled, like a man who has just lost a hand of cards to a better player.

"You'd burn your own life down just to spite me."

"No," Ivo said. "To spite *them*. You taught me the difference."

He looked at her a moment longer, and something in his face shifted—the ledger closing, the bargain dying—and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and careful, the way it did when he was about to say something true.

"You should know, then. Before you decide what you're carrying down that mountain." He glanced back at the hut, at the crates of records inside. "The night the flood hit, I sent a man ahead to Meridian. He's already there. And he's not carrying copies, Ivo. He's carrying word to the consortium's directors that we found the vault—and that their appraiser's memo is sitting in a surveyor's hut above the Serran, and who's holding it."

The cold went through her like the river through the sluices.

"They'll come for it," Voss said. "And they'll come for us. We've got maybe two days to get down the Kestrel Bend tunnel with everything they want buried." He shouldered his pack, and for the first time since Meridian, Casimir Voss looked afraid.
