## 9. The Descent

*Ivo*

The records weighed less than a dead man's boots.

That was the thought that came to her as she wrapped the oilcloth bundle a third time and lashed it with cord—the whole architecture of the consortium's crime, eleven years of forged inspections and bought engineers and a bridge built to fail, all of it fitting into a package she could carry in one hand. Tam Verrone's ledgers. The correspondence between Halloway Mining and the survey office that had signed off on the Kestrel span. Her own name, appearing twice in the margins, once as a witness, once—God help her—as a signature.

She had not slept. Above the source of the Serran the air was thin and mineral-cold, and the abandoned works crouched around them like the ribs of something that had died standing. Through the broken roof of the assay office she could see stars, hard and indifferent, and below them the black seam of the gorge where their boats waited, three days' descent from anywhere.

Voss appeared in the doorway without sound. He had a way of arriving like weather—not sudden, but inevitable, so that you felt you had always known he was coming.

"You're up," he said.

"Someone has to be."

He crossed the room and set his lantern on the assay table, and its light climbed the wall and found the great survey map still pinned there, yellowed, curling at one corner. The escarpment line ran through it like a scar. She had drawn part of that line herself, seven years ago, before she understood what it was for.

"We leave tonight," Voss said. "You and me. Down the old haul road on the east bank. There's a tram station at Graywater; we can be on the mainline by Thursday."

Ivo did not look up from her knot. "And the crew?"

"The crew stays provisioned until the relief party comes through. A week, maybe ten days." He said it the way he said everything, flat and reasonable, a man reading terms aloud. "The river's dropping. It's the only window we'll get, and you know it."

"And if the river doesn't drop?"

"Then they walk out. They're walkers, most of them. That's why you hired them." He paused, and when she still didn't answer, his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse. "Ivo. You've read what's in those papers. If we take this to the tribunal ourselves, we control it. We choose what's seen. If we wait—if we go down the river with six witnesses and a pilot who hates us both—every copy of this gets argued over, delayed, lost. The consortium has lawyers older than the escarpment. But two people, walking in off the mountain with originals—" He let it hang.

She finished the knot. Pulled it tight. Tested it.

"You want to sell it," she said. "Or bury it. You told me as much last night, and Ansel heard every word, so there's no version of this where the crew trusts either of us now. Which means your window closed about an hour ago."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes—not anger, calculation, the fast gray arithmetic of a man re-pricing a market. "Ansel," he said. "Of course. The doctor." He picked up his lantern. "Think about the east bank, Ivo. Think about who you were before you became whatever it is you're pretending to be out here. That woman would have already been halfway down the haul road."

"That woman is the reason eleven men are dead," Ivo said. "So no. I don't think I'll consult her."

* * *

*Ansel*

Ansel heard the argument end and counted to sixty before moving, because he had learned on this river that haste was how people got killed, and because his hands, which never shook over a wound, were shaking now.

He found Maren Oduya first, at the boats, mending a gunwale by lamplight with the patience of a woman who had spent twenty years being underestimated by men in better coats. The pilot looked up, read his face in one pass, and put down her knife.

"He's going for the east bank," Ansel said. "Tonight. With the evidence. And he means to strand us."

"I know." Maren went back to the gunwale, drew the stitch tight. "Been watching him case the haul road since supper. Question is what the doctor wants done about it."

"Not done. Prevented." Ansel crouched beside her, keeping his voice under the sound of the water. "If he takes the tram at Graywater, the papers are gone, and everyone on this crew becomes a loose end. Loose ends get cut, Maren. You've worked for companies. You know."

"I know." She tested the stitch with her thumb. "There's a place below Kestrel Bend where the haul road crosses the tunnel portal. Single track, single bridge, timber trestle older than sin. If a man were standing on that trestle with an axe—" She glanced up. "I'm not saying anything. I'm asking what kind of doctor you are."

Ansel thought of Ivo's hand under his bandages, the winch rope burn she hadn't flinched from, hadn't even mentioned until he'd made her. Thought of the lie he'd caught her telling on the flood day—*the risk was mine alone*—and how she'd looked at him afterward like a person watching a door close on the last lit room in a burning house.

"The kind who doesn't want anyone else to die on this river," he said. "But the kind who'll stand on that trestle if it comes to it."

Maren smiled without warmth. "Good answer. Bring the axe."

* * *

*Ivo*

She was halfway down the switchbacks above Kestrel Bend when she understood that Voss had never intended to ask her twice.

It was the light that told her—a lantern bobbing far below, moving too fast along the east-bank haul road, and behind it, fainter, another. She stood on the cut bench with the oilcloth bundle against her chest and did the arithmetic she should have done hours ago: Voss had gone ahead. Voss had taken the second copy of the records—the one she'd believed locked in the strongbox—and left her the original, or left her nothing, depending on how the night went. Either way, she was meant to follow or be forgotten.

She followed. Of course she followed. Not for Voss—for the papers, for the eleven names, for the stake of her brother's that still stood upright in the dark of the tunnel like a small stubborn spine refusing to lie down.

The gorge narrowed. The river spoke to itself in the dark, low and continuous, a voice that had been rehearsing the same sentence for ten thousand years. She could hear the falls at the Bend before she saw them, a white noise rising through her boot soles, and then the tunnel mouth opened on her left, black and breathing cool air, and beyond it the trestle bridge crossed the cut on legs of greasy timber.

Two figures stood on it.

One held a lantern. One held an axe.

"Maren," Ivo called, and her voice cracked strangely in the cold. "Maren Oduya, put that down."

"Ask him first," Maren said, not turning. "Ask him what's in the strongbox."

Voss stood at the far end of the trestle, and in the lantern light his face was perfectly composed, a man interrupted mid-transaction. "Doctor," he said pleasantly, "you're a long way from your kit."

"It's in my kit," Ansel said. "Everything. I brought it, in case someone needed patching." He shifted his grip on the axe—not threateningly, Ivo noticed, just firmly, the way a man holds a tool he intends to use only if the world insists. "Give her the papers, Voss."

"They're my papers. I paid for this expedition. Every ration, every stick of dynamite, every day of that woman's"—a flick of the lantern toward Ivo—"considerable expertise. The evidence belongs to whoever can use it, and I can use it. She can't. She's a confessed forger with a target on her back. In six months nobody will print a word she says."

"That's true," Ivo said.

All three of them went still. The falls filled the silence.

"That's true," she said again, stepping onto the trestle, feeling it give slightly, living wood under dead weight. "Which is exactly why it can't be yours, Casimir. Because the moment this story has a hero, it stops being a story about eleven dead men. You'd make it clean. You'd make it *useful*. And useful things get traded away—that's the whole lesson of the valley." She held out her hand. "The papers. All of them. Both copies."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we stand here until the sun comes up and the crew comes down the cut behind me, and you explain to six witnesses why you tried to leave them stranded above hostile territory with half rations." She took another step. The trestle groaned. Below her, through gaps in the planking, the white water showed like teeth. "You're a careful man, Casimir. You keep receipts. So receipt this: I am done running, and I am done bargaining, and the only thing I have left to spend is the truth, and I intend to spend all of it."

For a moment—one long breath of falling water—she believed he might actually do it. Hand them over. Some part of him, the part that had tracked her across seven years and three territories, had always wanted the truth more than the money; that was the tragedy of Casimir Voss, that he'd mistaken obsession for principle for so long he couldn't tell them apart anymore.

Then he moved for the lantern instead—to throw it, she realized, to fire the trestle—and Maren swung the axe, not at him but at the near trestle leg, one blow, two, and the old timber split with a sound like a rifle shot, and the bridge lurched.

What happened next happened in pieces. Voss stumbling, catching himself, the lantern going down and rolling, flame licking across grease-black planks. Ansel dropping the axe and lunging for the lantern. Ivo reaching for Voss's coat collar and finding it, holding it, shouting at him to jump for the cut bench—and Voss wrenching free, wrenching toward the far side, toward the haul road, toward Graywater, always toward the exit—

—and the plank beneath him giving way like a held breath finally released.

He didn't scream. That was the thing she would carry. He went down through the smoke and the sparks with his hand outstretched, not toward her, toward the ledger bag strapped across his chest, protecting the papers even as he fell, and then the white water took him the way it took everything, without malice and without memory.

* * *

*Ansel*

They got the fire out with river water and burned hands. Then they sat on the cut bench in the smell of wet ash while Maren went back up the trail for the others, and Ivo knelt at the edge of the trestle with the rescued ledger bag open across her knees, checking every document by lantern light, page by page, like a nurse counting instruments after surgery.

"He saved them," Ansel said quietly. "At the end. He reached for the bag."

"I know." Her voice was scraped raw. "I don't know what to do with that."

"You do the same thing you're doing now. You keep them intact. All of it—even the parts that hang you."

She looked up at him then, and the lantern light caught the exhaustion and the grief and underneath both something new, something that had stopped running. "When we get down the river," she said, "when we reach the railhead—I'm going to the tribunal. First train. I'm going to stand up in front of God and the consortium's lawyers and I'm going to read my own report into the record, the false one, and then these." She pressed her palm flat against the oilcloth. "Every exit I ever had is behind me, Ansel. This is the only door left, and it goes straight through the middle of me."

Ansel sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and said the only true thing left to say:

"Then I'll be sitting behind you when you do."

Below them, the Serran ran on toward the sea, carrying nothing now but starlight—and somewhere downstream, past the falls and the Bend and the drowned works, the railhead lamps of Graywater were already burning, waiting to see what came down out of the mountains.
