## 10. The Rebuilt Line

The tribunal chamber had once been a freight depot, and it still smelled like one—cold iron, creosote, the ghost of coal dust ground so deep into the floorboards that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift it. Ivo sat in the witness chair with her hands folded in her lap and noticed that she was not shaking.

She had expected to shake. She had rehearsed the shaking on the long train ride north, in the third-class carriage with the window rattling in its frame, imagining the moment her name was called and the whole machinery of the law turned its attention on her like a floodlamp. She had imagined her voice failing, her hands betraying her, the old reflex of flight rising up through her chest like water into a cracked hull. Seven years of running had taught her exactly how fear moved through a body, and she knew all its roads.

But when the clerk read out *Ivo Dravenmoor, licensed surveyor, of the former Serran Valley Consolidated*, she only felt quiet. A vast, level quiet, like the surface of the river at dawn before anyone has put a boat on it.

"State your name for the record," said the presiding magistrate, an old woman with spectacles that caught the gray light from the high windows.

"Ivo Dravenmoor." Her voice carried further than she intended. The chamber was full—families from the valley sitting in rows on benches brought in for the purpose, men with their hats on their knees, women holding children who were too young to remember the bridge but had grown up inside its absence. "Ivo Dravenmoor. And I am here to confess."

She told it plainly. That was the discipline she had set for herself: no ornament, no self-flagellation dressed up as eloquence, none of the novelist's tricks she'd once envied in other people's testimony. She described the autumn seven years ago when the consortium's engineers had delivered their pressure and their deadlines, when the inspection schedule had been compressed to something no honest survey could survive. She described the flaw in the third pier—the hairline fracture in the poured stone that a proper report would have condemned, that she had measured twice and then, under threat of dismissal, measured away. She described signing her name to a page that said the bridge was sound.

She did not describe what it had been like to hear the news. She did not think the record needed it, and she did not think she could have gotten through it. Eleven names. She kept them in order in her head as she spoke, an inventory she had never once let herself misplace: Halvard Dravenmoor first, always first, and then the ten others, each with his trade and his home village, because a list without faces was just another kind of falsification.

When she finished, the magistrate asked if she understood that her testimony, given freely, would likely end her career, her license, and possibly her liberty.

"Yes," Ivo said. "That is why I'm here."

* * *

They took her license the following morning. It was a small ceremony, almost clerical—a man at a desk in a government annex, a stamp, a ledger line. He asked her to sign beside the entry revoking her certification, and she did, and watched her own name become a matter of public record: entered, dated, archived. For seven years she had burned notes, paid cash, used half-names at rail-yard hotels, slept with her boots by the door. Now her name belonged to everyone. There was a strange relief in it, like setting down a pack she had forgotten she was carrying until her shoulders unlocked.

The verdicts came down over the following weeks, one after another, like stones dropped into deep water. The consortium's directors were indicted; two fled and were taken at the coast; the chief engineer who had delivered the deadlines turned crown's evidence and wept on the stand, which satisfied no one. The valley's families received compensation—not enough, never enough, but real, drawn from seized assets, administered by a board with three valley residents among its five seats. Ivo read about it in newspapers she bought at kiosks under her own unhidden name, and each time she saw the figures printed she thought of Halvard's hands, blunt and careful, tapping a survey stake into frozen ground.

There was a letter, once, from a widow in Lowmark whose husband had been a riveter on the bridge crew. Ivo read it standing in the street outside her lodging house. The woman did not forgive her. She wrote that she did not forgive her, and that she was writing anyway because someone ought to know that the money had come, and that her daughter had used part of it for schooling, and that this was not the same thing as being made whole. *You will live with it,* the letter ended. *I am glad. I am glad you will live with it.* Ivo stood in the cold until her fingers went numb, and then she wrote back: *I will. You have my word. I will live with it every day, and I will not ask you to make it lighter.*

She never heard again, and she didn't expect to. Some accounts stay open. She had stopped wanting them closed.

* * *

Spring came late to the escarpment that year, the way it always did, the snow holding on in the shaded cuts until well past the month when the lowlands had gone green. Ivo took the work train up from the junction because Ansel was riding out with the medical corps attached to the construction gangs, and because—for the first time in seven years—she was going somewhere without running from anything or toward anything, just going.

She had no position on the project. She had offered her testimony, her records, her knowledge of every cut and grade along the old alignment, and the tribunal had taken the first two and declined the third; a confessed falsifier does not survey bridges, whatever else may be true of her. So she rode in the second-class carriage like any other visitor, with a paper ticket in her pocket bearing her full legal name, and looked out the window as the line climbed into country she knew better than her own hands.

The gorge opened around them the way it always had—the river running quick and green below, the walls stepping up in ledges of gray limestone—but it was changed too. Where Kestrel Bend had been a wound, a collapsed tunnel mouth choked with rubble and dead timber, there was now a fresh cut, cleanly blasted, timbered with new pine, a work train's lights strung along its ceiling like a procession of small moons. Men waved at the passing carriage. Someone had planted a flag on the spoil heap, absurd and bright.

At the terminus camp, Ansel met her on the platform, coat collar up against the wind, looking exactly as he had on the flatboat a year ago except for a new scar splitting one eyebrow and a steadiness around the eyes that hadn't been there before. He didn't say anything sentimental. He just took her bag in one hand and her hand in the other, and they walked up through the camp together while gaffers called greetings to him and studied her with open, unhurried curiosity—and she found she didn't mind being seen. That was the strangest thing. Being seen had become survivable.

"They've finished the first span," Ansel said. "Set the last rivet Tuesday. They're walking it tomorrow for the formal opening, but the engineer owes me a favor and says we can go out tonight, if you want."

"Do you want?"

"I want to see your face when you get to the middle," he said.

* * *

They went at dusk, when the work crews had gone down to their tents and the gorge had filled with that blue hour light that made the limestone glow like something held up behind a lamp. The new span crossed the river where the old bridge had fallen—or rather, where its wreckage had been cleared away, because nothing of the old structure remained; the consortium's engineers had carted off even the broken piers, as if demolition could be made to look like geology. But Ivo knew the place. Her feet knew it. When she stepped onto the span's near approach she felt the exact spot where the third pier had stood, the one she had measured and unmeasured, and she stopped there and put her palm flat against the new steel.

It was warm from the day. It hummed faintly, some subsonic conversation between the wind and the cables, and beneath it she could feel the river's deeper pulse coming up through the foundations, patient, indifferent, older than any of them.

"You don't have to do this alone," Ansel said quietly. "Whatever this is."

"It isn't alone," she said. "That's the point. I built it alone. This—" she tapped the steel, "—they built with eleven hundred men and a year of drawings and every lesson written in blood. Nobody hid anything. Every weld gets inspected twice, by different hands. I checked."

Ansel smiled. "Of course you checked."

"I asked to see the inspection reports." She laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first laugh she'd had in this country in seven years. "The site engineer thought I was mad. I told him I was the reason double-inspection exists."

They walked on, out over the water. Halfway across, the span curved slightly—it was a cantilever design, elegant, nothing like the old stone-and-iron bridge—and from the center the whole gorge lay open in both directions: downstream toward Kestrel Bend and the lowlands, upstream toward the source and the abandoned works where Voss had found his records, where everything had turned. Voss himself was alive, convalescing in a prison hospital after the fall, awaiting his own trial; he had sent her one letter, a single page, containing neither apology nor accusation, only the sentence *You were right to keep them intact*, which she had read a dozen times and still did not know how to answer. Perhaps she never would. Some debts between rivals stayed open too.

Below the span, the river ran on. Above, the first stars were coming out in the east, over the escarpment's rim.

"Here," Ansel said. "This is the middle."

She looked down. Far below, the water slid green and silver over the shallows, and on the far bank she could see, small in the dusk, the white posts the compensation board had erected—eleven markers along the valley road, one for each man, each facing the bridge. Her brother's stood closest to the water. From up here it was only a pale grain of wood, but she knew it was there, the way she knew all of them were there, and she knew she would come back, year after year, as long as her legs allowed, to walk this span and count them.

"I keep waiting," she said, "to feel like running."

"And?"

"And it's not there." She tested the truth of it as she said it, probing the old wound the way she'd once probed stone for fracture lines. "Seven years I thought the motion was keeping me alive. Turns out it was just keeping me moving." She turned to him. The wind came up the gorge and pulled at her hair and she let it. "I don't know how to be stationary, Ansel. I don't know what I am if I stop."

He took her hand, the scarred one, the one the winch had nearly taken, and pressed it between both of his, warm against the evening cold.

"You're the woman who walked back," he said. "That's plenty. We'll figure out the rest."

They stood together at the center of the span while the dark came up out of the valley and the lamps along the cables flickered on one by one, a string of small lights leading across the escarpment toward the country beyond—and for the first time since she had signed her name to a false page, Ivo Dravenmoor stood still, entirely still, on solid and honest ground, and did not want to be anywhere else.
