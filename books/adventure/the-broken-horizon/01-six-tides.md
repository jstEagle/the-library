## 1. Six Tides

The storm came up out of the west like something owed.

Ivo Pemberton saw it first as a bruise on the horizon, a smudge of green-black that didn't belong to any honest sky, and he did what he always did with weather and with debts: he measured it. Anemometer up, laptop balanced on the passenger seat of the chase truck, rain already ticking against the windshield in fat, impatient drops. The flats ran flat and silver ahead of him for eleven miles, cut by a single farm road that turned to gumbo mud every time the sky opened, and the supercell was walking down that corridor like it had an appointment.

Wind shear at two hundred meters: forty knots, gusting fifty-five. Rotation tightening. He did the math without thinking about it, the way other men counted their birthdays — not because it told him anything new but because the numbers were steadier than years. He was thirty-nine by the calendar. By miles logged chasing weather across four states and one drowned coastline, he was somewhere past a million, and a million was a number that never asked him what he'd done with his life.

"Come on," he murmured to the storm. "Show me your line."

The mesocyclone hung off the truck's left fender like a great gray lung breathing itself. Beneath it the wall cloud lowered, ragged, and a tail of condensation began to spin — slow at first, then with intent. Ivo pulled off the farm road onto the shoulder, killed the engine, and stepped out into air that tasted like iron and cut hay. The wind hit him sideways, shoved him a half-step toward the ditch, and he leaned into it the way you lean into a debt collector's handshake: braced, unsmiling, taking its measure.

Funnel. Thin as wire at first, then thickening, dropping toward the wheat with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been told no. Ivo filmed it, called coordinates into the spotter network, and felt the old clean joy rise in him — the only feeling left that wasn't about money. For six minutes the tornado walked the fields, took apart an empty barn, and set the pieces down gently forty yards away like a child losing interest in a toy. Then it roped out, thinned, and died, and the storm moved on eastward trailing rain, and the sky over the flats went the color of pewter and forgiveness.

Ivo stood in the wet grass until his hands stopped shaking, which took longer than it used to. Then he checked the fuel gauge, checked the balance in his account — four hundred and twelve dollars, minus whatever the satellite feed cost this month — and drove back toward the river town of Harrow Bend, where the water was high, the season was turning, and three men who called themselves optimists were waiting to buy the last thing he owned.

* * *

The Anchorline sat where the road ended and the river began, a low brick building with a porch sagging under neon and a dock out back where the working boats tied up. Inside it smelled of fryer grease, river mud, and the particular staleness of money that has been sitting too long in someone else's pocket. Ivo parked the chase truck between a grain hauler and a skiff with no name, wiped the storm off his face with a bar towel, and went in.

He saw them before they saw him, or let him believe so. Three men at the corner table beneath the mounted paddlefish, drinking nothing stronger than coffee, dressed like men who had been told what wealthy people wear and had purchased it secondhand. Vess Corrigan sat with his back to the wall, hands folded, smiling the way weather smiles just before it turns. To his left, a heavy man with a ledger-thin mouth — Bram Kettle, though Ivo wouldn't learn the name until later — and to his right a younger one doing something quiet with a phone.

"Ivo Pemberton." Vess said it like a toast. "Storm chaser. Salvage diver. Man of many trades and, if our figures are right, few profits."

"Our figures are right," Ivo said. He didn't sit. Sitting was agreeing.

"Then you know why we're here." Vess gestured at the chair across from him anyway, patient as rising water. "Sit. Have a coffee. It's terrible, but it's free, and free is a word you don't hear often these days."

Ivo sat. The waitress came; he waved her off. On the table between them lay a folder, gray, unmarked, squared to the table edge with a precision that made his skin crawl. Everything about these men was squared. Their cuffs, their papers, their sentences.

"You hold paper on my boat," Ivo said. "The *Kestrel*. Thirty-one foot, aluminum hull, twin screws. Worth maybe eighteen thousand on a good day."

"The *Kestrel* is worth eleven thousand on a good day," Vess said pleasantly, "and today is not a good day. You owe us sixty-two thousand, four hundred dollars, plus interest accruing at a rate I'd be embarrassed to say aloud in front of the fish." He patted the paddlefish above him without looking at it. "You've paid late twice. You've paid short once. You've paid excuses the other nine times."

"I've been working."

"You've been chasing weather," Vess said. "Which is beautiful, I'm sure. I watched the footage from this afternoon, actually. Lovely funnel. Very photogenic. But tornadoes don't pay interest, Mr. Pemberton. They don't pay anything. They're a lot like dreams that way."

Bram Kettle stirred his coffee without drinking it. The younger man hadn't looked up from his phone once, and somehow that was worse than being looked at.

"So here's what the optimists have for you," Vess went on, and the word *optimists* landed on the table between them like a coin — worn smooth, passed hand to hand, meaning whatever the holder wanted it to mean. "An opportunity. A chance to convert debt into equity, so to speak. A salvage job."

Ivo kept his face still. "I do salvage."

"We know. That's rather the point. You're good at finding things underwater, and there's something underwater — or nearly — that needs finding." Vess opened the folder a precise inch, then closed it again, a man savoring his own reveal. "The *Meridian Star*. Expedition vessel, hundred and ten feet, converted trawler. Went upriver five weeks ago on a private commission and went silent three weeks ago, somewhere past the drowned villages, past the old channel markers, in water nobody charts because the charts are twenty years out of date and the river has rearranged itself since."

"Boats go silent all the time," Ivo said. "Radios fail. Batteries die. Crews get drunk or lost or both."

"Not this boat." For the first time Vess's smile flickered, adjusted, recalibrated — a compass needle finding true north after a knock. "This boat was carrying cargo worth more than everything in this room, including the building. Scientific equipment. Prototype. And the client who financed the expedition wants it back, and the client pays for results, and results require a man who knows rivers and wrecks and doesn't ask expensive questions."

"What kind of equipment?"

"The kind whose description costs extra." Vess slid the folder across the table, finally, and Ivo saw that inside were photographs: the *Meridian Star* at her moorings, white hull, blue boot stripe, a crane on her aft deck shrouded in canvas; crew manifests with names redacted in black bars like censor's tape; and a single page of coordinates, the last known position, marked with a question mark in pencil by some clerk's uncertain hand.

"Six tides," Vess said. "That's the window. The seasonal flood comes early this year — the river's already up two feet since Tuesday — and when tide six passes, the wreck will be buried or scattered or gone somewhere even you can't follow. Six tides to find her, confirm the cargo, and signal us. Do that, and your debt disappears. Every dollar. Clean slate. You walk out of this bar owing nothing to anyone, and isn't that," and here the smile came back full, warm as lamplight, false as a painted horizon, "what every man wants?"

Ivo looked at the photographs. The *Meridian Star*'s rail gleamed. Somewhere upriver, past the drowned villages, she was waiting — or her bones were — and the river between here and there was rising, and the men across the table were smiling, and none of those facts fit together in any way that favored him.

"And if I say no?"

Vess spread his hands, regretful, almost tender. "Then we take the *Kestrel*, we take the truck, and we take the next eleven years of your earnings, and you keep chasing storms in a rented car until your knees give out. Which, frankly, might suit you. Some men prefer their ruin to come slowly. It gives them time to get used to it."

The younger man finally looked up from his phone. His eyes were flat and brown and empty as a drained pool, and he looked at Ivo the way a surveyor looks at ground: estimating, measuring, deciding where the foundations would go.

"Six tides," Ivo said.

"Six tides," Vess agreed. "We'll outfit you. Fuel, gear, a boat — yours won't make that water, don't argue, we've seen her lines. Captain Reyes runs supply runs upriver this time of year; she owes us nothing and likes it that way, but she'll carry a paying passenger, and you'll pay. Consider it an advance against your success." He tapped the folder. "Sign, and the clock starts at tomorrow's low tide."

Outside, the rain had started again, soft and steady, the storm's far edge dragging across the rooftops of Harrow Bend. Through the window Ivo could see the river running high and brown and fast, carrying branches and secrets down toward the sea, and beyond it the last gray light of the broken sky.

He thought about the funnel, the six clean minutes of it, the only honest thing he'd touched all day. He thought about a million miles and sixty-two thousand dollars and a name he hadn't spoken in twenty years, sleeping at the bottom of him like a wreck of his own.

"Pen," he said.

Vess produced one from his breast pocket, already uncapped, already offered, as if he'd been holding it out for hours. As if the whole evening had been built around this single gesture, the way a storm builds around a column of rotating air.

Ivo signed. The pen scratched. Somewhere upriver, in the darkening water, something waited that had already cost eight men their silence, and the first of the six tides began to turn.

"There," Vess said, folding the contract with a sound like a wing folding. "Don't you feel lighter already?"

Ivo did not answer. He walked out into the rain, and behind him he heard the optimists laughing softly among themselves, the way men laugh when the weather is about to turn and they are certain they've brought umbrellas enough for everyone.
