## 2. The Optimists

The bar had a name once — the Gull and Anchor, or maybe the Anchor and Gull; nobody alive could say which came first — but now it was just the place at the end of Pier Nine where the light stayed amber no matter what the sky was doing outside. Ivo Pemberton pushed through its door with salt drying white on his jacket and a headache shaped like a supercell still rotating behind his eyes.

He'd measured the storm in miles on the drive back. Forty-one miles of crosswind on the causeway, nine miles of hail that sounded like someone feeding gravel into a drum, and the last six miles under a sky so green it looked bruised. He'd done the arithmetic automatically, the way other men checked their watches: wind shear at two hundred feet, forward speed of the cell, distance to the nearest hard shelter. He did not think about his age. He thought about his age only in the sense that he made a point of not thinking about it, which was itself a kind of thinking, and he knew it.

The optimists were already there.

He saw them the way you see weather coming — not the storm itself but the color of it, the pressure drop, the way the regulars at the far end of the bar had gone quiet and found urgent business in their glasses. Three of them, seated at the corner table beneath the mounted marlin that some previous owner had shot and some subsequent owner had failed to dust. They sat like men who owned the table, the bar, the pier, and possibly the tide itself.

Ivo knew two of them by reputation. Bram Kettle he'd met before, briefly, in a context involving a broken hand that hadn't been his. Kettle was built like cargo — dense, squared off, indifferent to being stacked — and he sat with his hands flat on the table as if the table might otherwise leave. The third man was a stranger, narrow and gray, wearing a suit that had been expensive in a decade Ivo didn't remember fondly.

The one in the middle was Vess Corrigan.

She was smaller than her reputation. That was the first thing. The second thing was the smile. It wasn't warm and it wasn't cold; it was anticipatory. It was the smile of weather about to turn — the sky going an odd, pretty color ten minutes before the sirens start. People looked at that smile and felt lucky. Ivo looked at it and started calculating exits.

"Pemberton." She said his name like she was confirming a delivery. "Sit down. You look like a man who's been chasing something."

"Only the usual," Ivo said. "Weather."

"And how did the weather treat you?"

"It missed me. They usually do, eventually."

Kettle grunted. The gray stranger poured three fingers of something brown into a fourth glass and slid it to the empty chair across from Corrigan, and Ivo understood that the chair was his, had always been his, had perhaps been reserved since before he walked in. He sat anyway. That was the trouble with him and chairs, and with debts, and with doors: he always went through them even when he knew better.

"You're wondering why we asked you here," Corrigan said.

"I'm wondering why you asked me *here*," Ivo said, nodding at the room. "You people own buildings with carpet."

"We do. But this is a working conversation, and you're a working man. We wanted you comfortable." She turned her glass a quarter-turn on the table, precise as a dial. "How much do you owe, Ivo? Total. Across everyone."

The question landed like a depth charge. Not because it was rude — politeness had drowned somewhere upriver years ago — but because she asked it the way you ask a man his weight when you're calculating how much chain you need to hold him.

"I don't discuss figures," Ivo said.

"I'll discuss them for you, then. Fourteen thousand to Marlowe and Sons. Six to the fuel syndicate, though they've stopped calling it fuel and started calling it a lease. Two thousand three hundred to the harbormaster's discretionary fund, which is a beautiful phrase. And eleven thousand to us." She smiled again, that turning-weather smile. "Thirty-three thousand, give or take interest, which never gives. You make it back in pieces. Chasing storms for camera crews. Salvage runs. A little ferrying, when your boat's running, which is most weeks."

"Your point?"

"My point is that you are a man who measures everything," Corrigan said, "and what you've measured is that you will die owing more than you owed the year before. Am I close?"

Ivo drank. The whiskey was good, which annoyed him. Cheap whiskey would have been easier to refuse.

"What's the job?" he said.

Corrigan glanced at the gray man, who produced a folder from inside his coat and laid it on the table without opening it, the way a dealer lays down a card face-down.

"The Meridian Star," she said.

The name went through the bar like a cold front. At the far end, a fisherman who'd been pretending to study his glass actually stood up, paid, and left. Ivo kept his face still because keeping his face still was the one skill he'd mastered that couldn't be repossessed.

"Expedition vessel," he said. "Research outfit. Went quiet upriver — what, five tides back?"

"Six," Corrigan said. "Silent since. No distress call, no debris, nothing at the last known mooring. Her owners are insured through instruments we hold. Her cargo manifest includes items of considerable value." She paused, letting the silence do a share of the talking. "Including a piece of equipment. A memory-recording device. Prototype. Worth more than this pier, this town, and every boat tied to it. Possibly worth more than all three combined twice over."

"Memory recording," Ivo repeated. "You mean like a camera."

"I mean," Corrigan said, and here her voice dropped half a register, the way it does when the wind shifts, "like a machine that records what a person remembers. All of it. Every mile of it. Playable back. Transferable." She watched him say the word transferable land. "The expedition was taking it upriver for field trials. Then they stopped answering."

"So hire a salvage fleet. You clearly have them. I've seen their boats."

"We have fleets," Kettle said. His voice was gravel settling. "Fleets are loud. Fleets get noticed. There are already two outfits running the river looking for that hull, and if one of them finds it first, the device ends up in a buyer's hands in the southern ports and our insurance position becomes a legal problem instead of a profit. What we need is one man, one fast boat, and no flag."

"And you," Corrigan said, "are famously unflagged."

Ivo turned his glass. Outside the salt-fogged window, the harbor lights were coming on one by one, each with a small stutter, like the town remembering itself.

"Why me?" he said. "Honestly. You've got enforcers. You've got Kettle. You've got money, which buys pilots."

"Honesty. Good. I prefer it, and I'll return it." Corrigan leaned forward, and up close he could see that her eyes were pale gray, the exact color of a sky deciding. "Because you know the upper river. Because you chase storms for money, which means you go toward things other people flee from, and right now everything about the Meridian Star is a thing to flee from. And because—" she tapped the folder once, lightly, "—you need thirty-three thousand reasons to say yes, and I am prepared to offer you thirty-five."

The number hung there. Thirty-five thousand. Debt cleared, plus two. Two thousand of open water between him and the bottom. He tried to feel relief and found instead a familiar sensation, the one from just before a storm makes landfall — the pressure dropping, the air going strange and sweet, the animal part of the brain whispering *run* while the rest of you stands in the yard watching the wall cloud come down out of a green sky.

"And the catch," Ivo said. "There's always a catch. Nobody pays thirty-five thousand for a boat ride."

"The catch is the terms." Corrigan slid the folder across the table and opened it herself, turning it to face him. Inside was a contract, single page, dense type, and at the top, where a sane document would say *days*, it said something else.

Ivo read it twice.

"*Six tides*," he said.

"Tides," Corrigan agreed. "Not days. The river rises early this season — you may have noticed the marks on the pilings. After the sixth tide, the wreck line floods past recovery, and whatever's down there stays down there until it rots or someone richer than us dredges it a decade from now. So: six tides. You find the Meridian Star, you secure the device, you signal us, and we handle the rest. In exchange, your ledger with us closes. All of it. Eleven thousand, gone. And we extend credit against your completion bonus, so you can settle Marlowe and the fuel syndicate immediately — we'll advance you the funds tonight."

Tonight. That was the hook set deep and clean. Not after the job — *tonight*. Money in his account before he'd signed anything binding him to anything, so that by the time he read the fine print properly, the fine print would be a formality, because the alternative would be giving the money back, and no man gives back the money that saved him.

He read the fine print anyway. Clause four: failure to locate within six tides constituted default, and default reverted all advances at double rate. Clause seven: any salvage recovered became optimist property entire. Clause nine — clause nine was short, and short clauses in contracts are like calm patches in rivers, the deepest water lying underneath.

Clause nine said: *The contractor affirms his identity as stated herein.*

His name. Printed there. **IVO PEMBERTON**.

He stared at it longer than the clause deserved, and Corrigan watched him stare, and he understood that she had put it there deliberately, that clause nine was a test he couldn't see the shape of yet.

"That's my name," he said.

"It is," Corrigan said pleasantly. "Is it?"

For a moment — half a heartbeat, less — the bar went away. He was twenty-four again, standing in the orange light of a burning boathouse on the far coast, watching twenty years of somebody else's life curl up in the heat. He could smell the pitch smoke. He could hear the sound a name makes when it burns — which is no sound at all, which is the whole problem, names go quietly, they don't scream, they just stop existing and take everything attached to them along. There had been a different name then. There had been a different man, younger, worse in some ways and better in others, a man who'd struck a match he told himself was an accident for two decades running. That man had walked away from the fire and kept walking, coast to coast, river to river, changing harbors the way other men changed shirts, until the name he wore now fit well enough that some mornings he almost believed it had always been his.

Almost. Mornings like this one, in bars like this one, across tables from women like this one, the seams showed.

"It's mine," Ivo said. "It's the only one I've got."

"Then sign," said Vess Corrigan, and smiled like weather about to turn.

The pen was already waiting. Of course it was. The gray man had probably been carrying it since noon, the way you carry an umbrella when the forecast says rain and you've arranged the rain yourself.

Ivo looked at the signature line. It sat there at the bottom of the page like a horizon line — the place where the sea of what he owed met the sky of what he was willing to become to stop owing it. He thought about his boat, the *Lark*, tied up at Pier Seven with a bilge pump that ran more often than the engine. He thought about the fuel syndicate's last letter, which had stopped being polite in March. He thought about the fact that he was forty-six years old and owned nothing in this world that couldn't be towed away, and that the only thing he'd ever truly buried was buried so deep even he needed a map to find it.

Six tides. Twelve days, near enough. One river. One wreck. One machine that recorded what a person remembered — every mile of it, transferable.

He signed. The pen moved smoothly, better than pens had any right to move in dockside bars, and his name came out in his handwriting, which was the strangest part — that the old lie still wrote itself so easily, fluent as water finding its level.

Kettle collected the contract, checked the signature against something on his phone, and nodded once, a door closing. The gray man shook Ivo's hand with a grip like a notarized seal. Money moved somewhere in the world's invisible machinery; Ivo felt it the way you feel a tide turn, not seeing it, only knowing that everything floating just shifted direction.

"A pleasure doing business," Corrigan said, rising. She buttoned her coat — an ordinary gesture, and somehow the most frightening thing she'd done all evening. "You'll want to review the Star's last radio log before you sail. Our office will send it over tonight. Study it closely, Mr. Pemberton. The river keeps what the river keeps, but radio waves—" she paused at the edge of the lamplight, and for just a moment the smile flickered, recalibrated, became something almost honest, "—radio waves keep things too. Even the ones that shouldn't exist."

She left. Kettle left. The gray man folded himself out the door behind them like a letter being returned to sender, and the bar exhaled, and the regulars resumed their conversations at a volume slightly lower than before, as if the room itself had signed something.

Ivo sat alone with the untouched whiskey the gray man had poured him and did the math one more time, because math was the only prayer he knew. Thirty-five thousand against thirty-three owed. Two thousand clear. Six tides to earn them.

Two thousand dollars of freedom, purchased by signing his name — the false one, the good one, the one that fit — onto a document that measured his remaining life in tides rather than days.

Outside, the harbor clock began striking nine, and somewhere upriver, in the dark past the last channel marker, the Meridian Star waited with its cargo of other people's memories, and the first of the six tides was already turning.

* * *

The log arrived at midnight, delivered to the *Lark* by a boy on a bicycle who wouldn't meet his eye and pedaled off before Ivo could tip him.

Ivo spread the printout on his chart table under the single swinging lamp, next to the coffee he'd forget to drink, and began to read the final transmissions of the Meridian Star — and by the third page, he forgot entirely about the money, the debt, the contract, and the name he'd signed.

Because the coordinates in the last transmission, the ones read out in a calm, steady voice at 0300 on the final night —

those coordinates didn't exist.
