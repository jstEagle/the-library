## 3. Dead Air

The optimists' office smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet — ozone and old carpet and the faint chemical sweetness of a dehumidifier working too hard against a coastal damp it would never win. Ivo sat in a chair bolted to the floor across from Vess Corrigan's desk and tried not to look at the contract still lying between them, its ink drying into something he'd already agreed to.

Vess had left him alone for exactly four minutes. He knew because he counted everything now: miles, minutes, dollars owed, dollars worse than owed. Counting was how he kept the other numbers out — the ones with names attached, the ones that burned.

The terminal on the corner of her desk was old, government surplus, the kind of machine that hummed like it was thinking about something unpleasant. A sticky note in someone else's handwriting said *MERIDIAN STAR — FINAL LOGS — DO NOT DISTRIBUTE*, which meant Ivo was absolutely supposed to distribute them, just quietly, just to himself, just until he found what they were paying him to find.

He pulled the chair over. The screen threw blue light up into his face.

Twenty-two hours of audio. The Meridian Star had gone silent at 0347 on the morning of the ninth, eleven days ago. Salvage protocol said listen from the end backward; grief protocol, if there was such a thing, said the same. Ivo scrubbed the timeline to the last transmission and pressed play.

Static first. Then a voice, clipped by interference, reading numbers.

*"—mark, mark. Position forty-one degrees, seven minutes north. Longitude—"*

Ivo stopped it. Rewound. Played it again.

Forty-one seven north. He knew that water. He'd chased hail cells across it two seasons back, when the flats still had a road running through them instead of a memory of one. Forty-one seven north put the Star somewhere she had no business being — sixty miles east of her filed course, out past the drowned shelf where the river splayed into a hundred dying channels and the charts went gray with the notation *unsurveyed*.

He pulled up the expedition's last known position from the insurance filing. Compared. The discrepancy wasn't error. Error drifted. This was a hard cut, a deliberate turn, a captain steering his own vessel off every map that mattered and then announcing the coordinates out loud, on an open channel, as if he wanted them remembered.

As if he wanted them *found*.

Ivo sat back. The dehumidifier clicked and sighed.

He played the rest of the final transmission. The voice read the longitude — also wrong, also deliberate — then said, *"Meridian Star, standing by,"* and the channel held open for nine seconds of river-hiss before someone, or something, ended the recording mid-breath.

No distress call. No mayday. Captains didn't read false coordinates in calm voices unless they were either lost beyond saving or leaving a message. And Aldous Wren, according to everything Ivo had read in the past two days, was not a man who got lost. Twenty-six years on the water. Surveyed three channels the government had given up on. The kind of skipper whose charts other skippers copied without shame.

So: a message. Left for whoever came looking.

Which was, apparently, Ivo Pemberton, who had never in his life been the person anyone left messages for.

* * *

He worked through the night in the room above the dockside bar, the one the optimists were paying for, which meant they could hear through it if they wanted to and probably did. The walls were thin enough that he could smell the beer downstairs, could hear Ondine Reyes's supply boat knocking against its pilings whenever a wake came through — a sound like a slow heartbeat, patient and indifferent.

He built a timeline on the back of a tide chart. Transmissions logged over the Star's final six days, played in order, annotated in pencil so small the letters looked like insect tracks.

Day one of the log: normal. Weather reports, cargo checks, Wren's voice dry and economical, a man who treated radio time like money. Day two: normal, except the crew list read aloud during a safety drill included a name Ivo couldn't find anywhere in the manifest — a technician, female voice answering to the name *March*, listed nowhere in the papers Vess's people had given him. He flagged it. Filed it under *later*. There would be a lot of *later* on this job; he could feel them stacking up like storm cells on a horizon.

Day three: the coordinates started changing. Not drifting — jumping. Wren reported positions that leapt miles between hourly checks, impossible distances for a vessel making headway against current, unless the Star was moving in ways vessels don't move. Or unless the positions weren't real. Or unless something about the way Wren was reporting had come loose from the way Wren was living.

Day four: silence for nineteen hours, then a single transmission at 0211, thirty-one seconds long. No voice. Just a sound Ivo played six times before he let himself name it.

Water. Moving water, close-miked, the way it sounds against a hull when you're adrift in a channel too narrow for your draft. And underneath it, almost lost, a rhythmic knock — hull against something solid, patient, metronomic. Like a boat nudging a dock no chart showed.

Day five: Wren again, and this was where Ivo's pencil stopped moving.

The captain's voice was the same voice. Same dry economy, same flat coastal vowels. But he was reading coordinates again, and these ones didn't just fall outside the surveyed channels. They fell outside the *river*. Ivo checked them three times against three charts, including the old military survey from before the floods, the one that showed the land as it used to be when it was land. The coordinates put the Meridian Star forty miles inland, on a hillside that had been a hillside since the retreat of the ice.

A ship does not sail uphill. A ship does not report position from a place that has been dry for ten thousand years.

Unless the man reading the numbers wasn't reading numbers at all. Unless the numbers were code, or damage, or a message wearing the costume of nonsense.

Ivo rubbed his eyes and listened to day five again, and this time he heard what he'd missed: beneath Wren's voice, faint as breath on glass, a second rhythm. Not the water-knock from day four. Something faster. Almost like speech played at the wrong speed, like a voice recorded and slowed down until words became weather.

He slowed the audio. Stretched it. The software on the surplus terminal was garbage but it could do that much, and when he dragged the hidden layer down to half speed and then quarter, the weather resolved into something with consonants.

He couldn't make out the words. But he could make out the *shape* of them — the cadence of a woman speaking calmly, evenly, the cadence of someone reading instructions. Or reciting something learned by heart.

The lullaby came on day six.

* * *

It was 0347 when it happened, the timestamp matching the moment the Star went dark, and Ivo understood afterward that he had been listening to the exact minute of whatever happened for the rest of his life, the way you remember the exact second a tornado drops out of a wall cloud and stops being a possibility.

Wren's voice first. Steady. Reading the false coordinates one more time, the inland ones, the impossible ones, slower now, like a man dictating his will. Then: *"If anyone hears this, the device is not what we were told. Do not recover it. Do not—"*

The transmission didn't cut. It *changed*. Mid-word, clean as a blade, Wren's voice stopped and another began, and the channel stayed open, and for thirty seconds the radio carried a lullaby.

Not hummed. Sung. A woman's voice, low and unhurried, singing a melody Ivo did not know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a street from a dream — the tune arriving already familiar, already carrying some weight of meaning he couldn't source. It was simple. Four phrases, descending, the kind of melody designed to be sung over a cradle, over a fever, over fear. The singer's voice was calm in a way that made his skin crawl, because nothing about that night on that river should have permitted calm, and yet she sang like the world was exactly the size of a small dark room and perfectly safe.

Then the recording ended. Not faded. Ended, like a door.

Ivo sat in the blue light with his heart going too fast and did the thing he always did with fear, which was convert it to arithmetic. Crew list: eleven souls, nine men, two women. He had their names. He had their emergency contacts, their next of kin, their service histories — the optimists were thorough about people they intended to collect against. He went down the list twice, then a third time, checking each name against the voice in his memory the way a man pats his pockets for keys he knows are gone.

None of them fit. The two women aboard were fifty-eight and twenty-three, respectively, and neither had any recorded history in music, in performance, in anything that would explain a voice trained like that. And more than training — there was something in the singing that wasn't skill. It was intimacy. That song belonged to that voice the way a scar belongs to a body. You don't sing like that for an audience. You sing like that for someone who needed you to.

And the melody itself. He hunted it for an hour through every database the terminal could reach — folk archives, maritime song collections, the digitized hymnals of three drowned counties. Nothing. The lullaby existed nowhere except in those thirty seconds, which meant it hadn't come from anywhere. Which meant it had come from someone. From a kitchen, maybe. From a mother. From a private country of one family's nights, carried up a river on a salvage ship and broadcast on an open channel to no one.

To him. To whoever came after.

Ivo looked at the tide chart nailed above the desk, the one he'd marked with six Xs counting down to the deadline in his contract. Five tides left after tonight. Somewhere upriver, in channels that didn't exist on paper, a dead man's ship was sitting where no ship could sit, singing coordinates from a hilltop, and the only human sound left in the whole record was a lullaby sung by a woman who wasn't on the crew list, in a voice that sounded like it was remembering something the rest of the world had forgotten.

He thought about the fire again. He hadn't let himself think about it in years, and here it came anyway, uninvited, the way it always arrived — not as images but as heat, as the smell of pitch and burning paint, as a name rising in his throat that he swallowed back down like a stone. Twenty years, and the name still fit his mouth perfectly. That was the cruelty of buried things. They kept.

He wondered, suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, whether the woman singing had buried something too. Whether that was what the song was — a message not of location but of method. *This is how you survive losing yourself. Hum something true until the rest grows back.*

"Get a grip," he said aloud, and his voice in the rented room sounded like a stranger's.

Downstairs, the bar had gone quiet. Through the floorboards he heard the last customers leave, heard chairs going up onto tables, heard the particular silence of a river town settling into the hour when the water does its real business unseen. The supply boat knocked against its pilings. Knocked. Knocked.

Hull against something solid. Patient. Metronomic.

Ivo sat very still.

It was nothing. It was a boat at a dock; boats knocked. He knew that. He wrote it down anyway, on the tide chart, in pencil, under the six Xs: *day 4 — knocking. day 6 — song. both = messages? both = her?*

Her. He'd already started calling the unknown voice *her*, he realized. Already assigned her a self, a history, a reason. That was dangerous. On the water you learned early that the mind fills fog with shapes, and most of the shapes are wrong, and believing the wrong shape is how people die in water shallow enough to stand in.

But the coordinates weren't fog. The coordinates were arithmetic, and arithmetic didn't lie. Aldous Wren had spent his final hours broadcasting a position forty miles inland and a warning about a device, and then a woman not on his crew had sung a song that existed nowhere on earth, and then the river had closed over all of it like a mouth.

Five tides. Two days of fuel. One rusting supply boat captained by a woman the harbormaster described, when Ivo asked, with a shrug that contained multitudes: "Reyes knows the river. Whether the river knows her back is her business."

At dawn Ivo folded the tide chart into his jacket, pocketed the audio drive, and went down to find Ondine Reyes before the optimists woke up and remembered to give him orders. Whatever was waiting upriver, it had left a message in the only language that survives drowning: a song, and a set of numbers that pointed at the impossible.

He intended to answer it.

The harbor fog was coming in off the flats as he walked, thick and white and full of shapes his mind insisted on filling — and for half a second, in the gray, he saw the fire again, and heard the name, and kept walking anyway, measuring the distance to the boat in steps because miles were for people with futures.
