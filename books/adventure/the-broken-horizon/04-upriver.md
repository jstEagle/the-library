## 4. Upriver

The boat was called the *Magpie's Regret*, which told Ivo everything he needed to know about her owner before he ever met her.

She sat low in the brown water at the end of a rotting pier forty miles past the last paved road, a supply vessel that had once hauled diesel drums and now hauled whatever paid. Her hull wore rust the way old men wear scars — not decoration, just history that hadn't bothered to heal clean. Paint flaked off her superstructure in long curls. A crane arm hung over her stern at an angle that suggested either neglect or contempt.

Ivo stood on the pier with his duffel over one shoulder and his sonar rig in a case at his feet and did the arithmetic he always did: draft, freeboard, engine noise, escape routes. You could take the man out of the storm but the storm kept its books on you.

"You're the one who wants upriver."

The voice came from below, from the shadow of the wheelhouse door, and then the woman who owned it came up the ladder like she'd been poured upward — broad-shouldered, gray-streaked hair cut short as a hedge against sentiment, hands that looked like they'd been carved from dock pilings. She stopped three feet away and looked at him the way a river looks at a bridge: measuring what he'd cost her when the flood came.

"Ivo Pemberton."

"I know." She said it without malice, just inventory. "You're the one who chases weather. There was footage, years back. You standing in a field while the sky came apart behind you."

"Bad judgment makes good film."

"It makes dead men, mostly." She spat over the rail, unhurried, precise. "Ondine Reyes. This is my boat. You want passage to the Wren Line, that's four days of river nobody charts anymore because the charting costs more than the cargo. You want me to say yes, you'll tell me why a salvage man is going after the *Meridian Star* alone, with no crew, no dive team, and no gun I can see."

"No gun," Ivo agreed. "Guns attract guns."

"And you're planning to be invisible?"

"I'm planning to be fast."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. A concession to the possibility of one. "Fast," she repeated. "In that rig?" She nodded at the sonar case. "That's survey equipment. That's *slow* equipment. You're going to stop and look at things."

"When the river tells me to."

She studied him for a long moment, and Ivo had the uncomfortable sensation of being read by something older than any instrument he owned. Then she jerked her chin toward the gangway.

"Eight hundred for the run. Half now. You eat what we eat, you work when there's work, and if I tell you to jump, you ask how deep. Deal?"

He shook on it. Her grip closed around his hand like a lock engaging.

"One more thing." She didn't let go. "There's traffic ahead of you. Two boats came through yesterday flying salvage colors out of Corvane Yard. Paid in cash, asked about the Star's last known heading, didn't tip the pilot worth a damn. If they're two tides ahead of you, Mr. Pemberton, you're not racing the river."

"I know what I'm racing."

"Then get aboard before I change my mind, because I liked my life better five minutes ago."

* * *

The *Magpie's Regret* carried a crew of exactly one besides Ondine: a boy of maybe sixteen called Tuck who served as deckhand, cook, and entire engineering department, and who looked at Ivo's storm-chasing rig with the naked hunger of someone who had never seen a horizon wider than the riverbanks.

"You really drive *into* them?" Tuck asked, hauling the sonar case onto the aft deck. "Tornadoes? On purpose?"

"On purpose."

"Why?"

Ivo considered several answers — the money, the footage, the physics — and discarded all of them because none were true. "Because it's the only thing loud enough," he said finally, "that I can't hear myself think."

Tuck nodded slowly, as though this were the most reasonable sentence he'd ever heard, and went forward to cast off.

They got underway at slack water, the diesels coughing twice before they caught, and the *Regret* swung her blunt bow into the current and began the long climb into country that maps had given up on. Ivo stood at the stern rail and watched the pier shrink, then the town, then the smoke of the town, until there was nothing behind him but water carrying everything away — the optimists' contract folded in his jacket like a knife against his ribs, Vess Corrigan's smile, the six tides ticking down somewhere in the dark arithmetic of the moon.

Ahead, the river widened and slowed and turned the color of old brass, and the drowned country began.

* * *

He found Ondine on the flying bridge that evening, one hand on the wheel, a tin cup of coffee in the other, steering by a combination of instinct and what appeared to be outright conversation with the water. He'd watched her do it all afternoon: reading boils and eddy lines, easing the boat around snags that no instrument flagged, taking channels that his charts — good charts, expensive charts — insisted were shoaled solid.

"You're off the marked channel," he said.

"The marked channel silted in three years ago." She didn't look at him. "The river made a new one. Charts are letters the river wrote to somebody else."

"That's poetic."

"That's practical. Poetry is when it doesn't matter." She sipped her coffee. "Your turn. Why alone? Salvage men travel in packs. Packs mean shares, shares mean witnesses, witnesses mean somebody can't steal the prize. You've got no pack. So either you don't trust anyone, or nobody will have you, or whatever's on that boat is worth so much you'd rather die than split it."

"Could be all three."

"Could be." The wheel turned a lazy degree under her hand. "But here's what I think. I think you're not doing this for the money at all. Money men are cheerful. You've got the face of a man paying off something that isn't denominated in dollars."

Ivo said nothing. The river said plenty. Somewhere off the port bow, a heron lifted out of a drowned cottonwood, unhurried, ancient, indifferent.

"Two tides," Ondine said. "That's what you've got before Kettle's people reach the wreck line, if they haven't already. Bram Kettle. You know him?"

The name landed in Ivo's chest like a dropped anchor. "Corrigan's enforcer. Used to be."

"Used to be until he wasn't. Men who leave the optimists leave for one of two reasons — they got rich enough, or they saw the books." She glanced at him sideways. "Which are you?"

"I'm the third kind. I owe them."

"Ah." She said it the way a doctor says *ah* over an X-ray. "So you're not chasing the Star. You're being chased into it. And if you come back empty-handed—"

"They'll take the only thing I have left."

"Which is?"

"My name," Ivo said, and heard how it sounded, and wished he'd said anything else.

Ondine turned back to the river. For a long moment the only sound was the diesels and the water worrying the hull. "My grandfather ran this stretch during the last big flood," she said at last. "Carried people out of the drowned villages when the government said the villages weren't there anymore. Officially, the map said dry land. Unofficially, three hundred souls were sitting on rooftops waiting for a boat that officially couldn't exist." She shrugged. "I keep the *Regret* running because somebody has to carry what the charts won't admit to. People. Secrets. Sometimes both in the same hull."

"Is that a speech about whether you'll help me?"

"It's a speech about rates." The corner of her mouth again, that almost-smile. "Helping costs extra."

* * *

On the second day they passed the first of the drowned towns.

It came out of the morning mist without warning — a church steeple rising alone out of flat silver water, then rooflines, then the black lattice of a water tower, the whole settlement sitting a fathom under the surface like a body under a sheet. The river had taken it decades ago and never given it back, and the water above it lay strangely still, holding the shapes of streets and fences and front yards in a way that made Ivo's skin crawl. It didn't look flooded. It looked *remembered*.

"Tallow Bend," Ondine said from the wheel. "Four hundred people, once. They knew the levee was bad. They stayed anyway. People always stay." She eased the throttle down as they crossed over the sunken main street, and Ivo could have sworn the wake went quiet, as if the boat itself were lowering its voice. "Locals say the river keeps what it takes. Not the houses. The *having been there*. You drift over a place like this and you feel it looking back."

Ivo stood at the rail and looked down into the green murk where a schoolhouse might have been, and thought about the *Meridian Star*, silent now for eleven days somewhere upstream, carrying a machine that recorded what a person was. He thought about the thirty seconds of lullaby on the radio log, that impossible melody threaded through dead air, and the coordinates that existed nowhere on any chart — coordinates that had sounded, when he played them back a dozen times in his bunk, less like a position than like an address in someone's memory.

A machine that could hold a self whole. He wondered what the river would charge for that kind of cargo.

"You've gone somewhere," Ondine observed.

"Just doing sums."

"Sum this instead." She pointed forward with her chin. Smoke, thin and blue, rose off a bend two miles ahead. Too much smoke for a campfire, too steady for a wreck burning. "That's a fuel cache. Salvage fleet keeps them along the route. Which means Kettle's lead boat isn't two tides ahead anymore."

Ivo felt the arithmetic shift under him like ballast sliding. "How far?"

"Half a tide. Maybe less. They'll have divers in the water by tomorrow's dark." She brought the cup to her lips and drank, watching the smoke with eyes that had measured ten thousand miles of bad water. "So here's your real choice, Mr. Pemberton. I can push us through the night runs — the Narrows, the Sawtooth, all the water the fleet won't risk in the dark — and put you at the wreck line by dawn, ahead of them, tired and lucky. Or we run sensible hours and arrive second, and you spend the rest of this contract learning what Bram Kettle does to people who arrive second."

"What's the catch with the night runs?"

"Catch?" She smiled fully this time, and it was worse than Vess Corrigan's, because it was honest. "The catch is that nobody has ever run the Sawtooth at night and lived to invoice for it. The catch is that I'd be betting my boat and my boy on your reasons being good ones. So before I burn that fuel, you're going to tell me what's actually on the *Meridian Star* that's worth dying for — and this time you're going to tell me true."

The smoke rose ahead of them, patient as a flag. Behind them, the drowned town slid away under its sheet of remembering water. Six tides had become four and a half, and the river ran on toward the wreck line like a held breath.

Ivo set his hands on the rail, felt the engines change note beneath him as Ondine waited, and understood that whatever he said next would decide not just the race, but what kind of man arrived at the end of it.

"It's a machine," he said. "And it doesn't record memories. It *replaces* them."

For the first time since the pier, Captain Ondine Reyes took her eyes completely off the river — and pushed the throttle forward.
