## 5. What the River Keeps

Ondine Reyes had been reading papers for thirty years, and she had never once read the words on them.

Papers told you what a man wanted you to believe. Faces told you what he was afraid of. So when Ivo Pemberton came up her gangplank at Greywater Landing with a leather folder held out like an offering, she took it, weighed it in one hand, and looked past it at him instead.

The documents were good. She'd give him that. The salvage license bore the correct watermark — she tilted it against the morning sun and watched the river-otter shimmer exactly where it should. The insurance rider was stamped by an office that had burned down two years ago but whose seals still circulated among people who knew which drawers to buy them from. The name on the manifest was *Ivo Pemberton*, which was either his real name or the one he'd decided to wear this season, and honestly it made no difference to her which.

What mattered was the way he held his shoulders while she read. A man with clean papers reads over your shoulder, impatient, already halfway upriver. A man with forged ones stands very still and watches your face like a man watching weather come off the water.

Pemberton watched her face.

She closed the folder and handed it back. "Passage is four hundred to the Wreck Line, eight hundred if we go past it. Food's included. Whiskey's extra. You sleep on deck or in the hold with the cargo, your choice."

"That's it? No questions?"

"Questions cost extra too." She turned for the wheelhouse. "We cast off on the turn of the tide. Don't be late. The river doesn't wait and neither do I."

He wasn't late. That was the first thing she learned about him that the papers hadn't said: Ivo Pemberton was a man who understood deadlines the way other men understood scripture. He came aboard forty minutes early with a single duffel, a case of instruments she didn't ask about, and eyes that kept going upstream the way a man's eyes go toward a fire he'd left burning behind him.

Ondine had ferried smugglers, surveyors, debt-collectors, widows, one archbishop, and twice a man she was fairly sure was a ghost. She knew running when she saw it. Most men ran downstream — toward ports, toward crowds, toward the salt water where names dissolved. This one ran upstream. Toward whatever had gone silent out there in the drowned country. Toward the thing he'd been hired to find and clearly hoped he wouldn't.

*Upstream-shaped trouble,* her grandmother would have called it. The worst kind. Downstream trouble chased you; upstream trouble waited, patient as silt, and grew heavier the closer you got.

She cast off on the turn of the tide and pointed the *Mariana's Debt* north.

* * *

The river above Greywater wasn't a river so much as a memory of one.

Sixty years of seasonal floods had pushed the channel wider and shallower, until the water spread across the lowlands like ink across wet paper, drowning fields, roads, whole parishes. What had once been villages now existed as rooflines breaking the surface at low water — terracotta tiles furred with moss, chimney stacks leaning like drunkards, the occasional church spire standing proud of the floodplain with its bell long since looted and its clock hands frozen at whatever hour the water had come.

Ondine ran the channel from memory. Charts were useless here; the river redrew itself every spring, moved sandbars like furniture, opened new cuts through old orchards and sealed them again by autumn. The hydrographic office printed maps of this stretch every three years and every three years they were lies by the time the ink dried. But Ondine had been running this water since she was nine years old, standing beside her mother at the wheel of a boat smaller than her current skiff, learning to read the surface the way priests read scripture — the color of the water telling depth, the texture of the ripples telling bottom, the smell of the wind telling what weather was coming down from the high country to argue with the tide.

"Your charts are wrong," Pemberton said, an hour in, comparing his instruments against the banks sliding past.

"Yes."

"The channel markers say—"

"The markers were placed by a government boat in April. It is now September. In April that marker sat over twelve feet of water. Right now there's a sandbar under it you could picnic on." She eased the wheel two degrees starboard, feeling rather than seeing the bar's edge. "The river keeps what it wants and moves the rest. Markers are just suggestions."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "How deep is your draft?"

"Four feet loaded. We're drawing three-two today." She glanced at him. "Why?"

"Because my sonar says the channel should bend left here, and your boat is bending right."

"My sonar," she said, "is a fisherman named Tullio who sits on the bow and throws rocks at anything that looks wrong. And the channel bends right because it bent right yesterday, and the day before, and because the left side has a snag field from the orchard that flooded in '09 that will open your hull like a letter." She let the wheel breathe through her palms. "You can trust your machines, Mr. Pemberton. Machines are honest. But they're honest about a world that stopped existing the moment they took their reading. Out here, yesterday is the only map worth having."

Something shifted in his face — not offense, which she'd expected, but interest. He put the sonar away. "Yesterday, then. What does yesterday say about the Meridian Star?"

And there it was. Not even a full tide out of port and he was asking the question the papers pretended he wasn't being paid to ask.

"Yesterday," Ondine said, "the Star went quiet eleven days ago, somewhere above the Wreck Line, and everything that floats on this river has an opinion about why." She nodded forward, at the bow, where Tullio sat hunched in his oilskins with a line of river tobacco going, pretending not to listen and failing completely. "Tullio says pirates. The fuel dock at Corben's Reach says engine trouble. The Sisters at the mission say God took the vessel for reasons of His own, which is their answer for everything including bad harvests and the price of rope."

"And you?"

"I don't guess about the upper river. The upper river doesn't reward guessing." She found the channel mark she'd been waiting for — a dead cottonwood leaning off the east bank at precisely the angle her mother had taught her to look for — and brought the *Mariana's Debt* around it in a long, easy arc. "But I'll tell you what I know instead of guessing. Eleven days ago the Star passed Corben's Reach heading up, riding heavy, lower in the water than a survey vessel should be. Riding like she was carrying something dense. And the man who signed her fuel chit paid in new notes and didn't haggle, which on this river means either a fool or a man in a hurry, and Captain Aldous Wren was never a fool."

"You knew Wren?"

"Everyone on this river knew Wren. He ran surveys before the floods made surveys interesting again. Honest man. Boring, even." She smiled without much humor. "Boring men make the best captains. It's the interesting ones you have to watch."

"And which am I?"

She looked at him properly then, for the first time since the gangplank. Standing at her shoulder in the wheelhouse, salt-and-river worn, with the kind of tired that lives in a man so long it stops looking like tired and starts looking like architecture. Interesting, certainly. Running, definitely. But there was something else underneath, something she'd only seen in maybe five passengers in thirty years: a man doing arithmetic he hated, and doing it anyway, because the alternative was worse.

"Boring enough to pay upfront," she said. "That's all I need."

* * *

They made the drowned parish of Saint Aubin-le-Marais by midafternoon, and Ondine slowed the boat, because she always slowed there, though she'd have cut out her own tongue before explaining why.

Saint Aubin had gone under in the great flood twenty-two years back — not swept away, not ruined, just quietly taken, the water rising over a week until the village gave up arguing and accepted its new life as a lake bed. The people had resettled on the ridge two miles east. They still held the feast day of their patron on the shore across from their old church, and once a year they rowed out and lowered flowers over the drowned square.

From the wheelhouse, at the right angle of light, you could see down into it. The water up here ran tea-dark with tannin, but in the slant of late afternoon it went briefly transparent, and the village lay below like a thing asleep: streets, walls, doorways, a well with its bucket still on its chain. Windows that had been dark for two decades caught the light and threw it back amber, so that the whole drowned town seemed lit from within, as if the people had simply stepped out a moment ago and meant to come back.

Pemberton stood at the rail and stared down into it, and Ondine watched him stare, and understood something more about him than any paper could have carried.

"It pools," he said quietly.

"What does?"

"The light. The — I don't know. Everything." He gestured at the water. "It's like the memories settle here. Like silt."

Ondine's hands went still on the wheel.

Her grandmother had said almost those exact words, once, standing at this same stretch of channel: *the river keeps what people can't carry.* Ondine had been young enough then to laugh. She wasn't anymore. Thirty years of ferrying the drowned country's survivors and their children and their children's children had taught her that grief on this river behaved like sediment — it didn't wash away, it settled, layer on layer, in the channels and the slack water and the flooded orchards, and every boat that passed stirred a little of it up whether it wanted to or not.

"A lot of leaving happened here," she said. "Water doesn't erase. People think it does. Water just files things away where nobody checks." She throttled up gently, and the amber windows slid astern. "My family lost our house in that same flood. My mother used to bring us out here every year on the anniversary and point at a patch of water and say, *the kitchen was there.* Just like that. Like pointing at a grave. Then she'd turn the boat around and go home and cook dinner, and that was that. You don't get over water. You just learn its schedule."

Pemberton didn't answer. When she glanced over, he was looking at the drowned village going away behind them with an expression she recognized from the mirror, from bad years, from the anniversary runs she no longer made: the look of a man seeing his own flood in someone else's water.

Whatever he was carrying, it was upstream-shaped. And it was heavy.

"Tullio makes coffee at four," she said, gruffer than she needed to be. "It's terrible. Drink it anyway."

* * *

The warning came at dusk, and it came the way warnings did on the upper river: sideways.

A supply skiff flagged them down at the mouth of the Verre Cut — a lean, fast boat with a boy at the tiller who couldn't have been more than sixteen and had the river-hardened eyes of a man of fifty. He came alongside shouting before Ondine had even slowed.

"Mariana's Debt! Cap'n Reyes! Message from Corben's Reach!"

She brought them together with the ease of long practice, hulls kissing, and took the oilskin-wrapped note the boy held up. Inside, in the blocky handwriting of the harbormaster's clerk:

*REYES — SALVAGE FLEET SIGHTED ABOVE WRECK LINE. THREE VESSELS, ARMED, WORKING FLAG OF CONVENIENCE. LEAD BOAR NAMED KETTLE. THEY ASKED AFTER ANYONE FOLLOWING THE STAR'S TRACK. THOUGHT YOU'D WANT TO KNOW. R.*

Kettle. Bram Kettle. Ondine felt the name land in her chest like an anchor dropping. She'd known Kettle back when he wore the optimists' colors openly instead of a working flag — back when he'd come to her dock with contracts and smiles and offers to buy her route outright, and she'd told him, politely, that the *Mariana's Debt* carried people, not cargo, and that he could take his money and drown it. He hadn't liked that. Men like Kettle never forgot a refusal; they just filed it under debts outstanding.

Three vessels. Armed. Asking after anyone on the Star's track.

Which meant her.

She looked up. Pemberton had come to the rail and was reading her face the way she'd read his at the gangplank — carefully, professionally, like a man checking weather.

"Well?" he said.

"Your competition is ahead of us. Three boats, guns, and a leader whose manners I can describe from personal experience." She folded the note away. "They're asking who's following the Star's track. Which means they know someone might be. Which means whoever hired you isn't the only one who heard the Star go quiet."

"I figured as much." He said it flatly, without surprise, and that told her something too: Ivo Pemberton had known the race was hostile when he signed on. Had probably priced it in. "Can we make up the distance?"

"At night, yes. They'll be anchored by dark — Kettle's men don't run black water, too many snags, and Kettle himself doesn't gamble with hulls he owns." She looked north, where the last light was draining off the floodplain and the drowned country was dissolving into shapes and suggestion. "I can run black water. I was raised on it. But I'll want to know why I'm risking my boat and Tullio's neck to close on armed men."

Pemberton was quiet for a moment. Below them, Tullio had stopped smoking and was watching the both of them with the frank interest of a man who intended to repeat every word of this conversation at every dock between here and the sea.

"Because there's someone alive on that wreck," Pemberton said finally. "Or I believe there might be. And because the thing they're salvaging shouldn't end up in Kettle's hands. Or mine, honestly. But especially not his."

It was the *or mine, honestly* that did it. Ondine had spent thirty years listening to men lie about their motives, and the rarest sound in the world was a man naming his own corruption out loud, unprompted, like a debt he was choosing to acknowledge. Her grandmother had a word for such men. She'd also had a word for fools, and the two words sounded nearly identical.

"Night run, then," Ondine said. "You'll take the bow watch. Tullio will teach you the rock signals — two clinks for clear water, three for snag, continuous ringing for stop, and if you hear the bell he keeps up there, you hit the deck, because the bell means I've already seen it and you haven't."

"And after the Wreck Line?"

"After the Wreck Line," she said, turning the wheel as the Verre Cut swallowed the last of the light, "we're in country the charts won't admit exists. So we'll do what everyone on this river does." She found the first star through the broken cloud, fixed it over the funnel of her smokestack, and held it there. "We'll go by what the river remembers."

Ahead, far up the black channel, so faint it might have been imagination, a light blinked twice and went dark — the kind of disciplined, deliberate blink that belonged to no fisherman and no missionary.

Kettle's picket, marking the door.

Ondine eased the throttle forward, and the *Mariana's Debt* slipped into the dark, running quiet, running fast, carrying her debt-ridden passenger and his upstream-shaped trouble straight toward the thing the river was keeping.
