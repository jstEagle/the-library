## 6. The Wreck Line

The sonar screen glowed green in the dark of the wheelhouse like something alive, and Ivo Pemberton had learned long ago that the things that looked alive on a sonar were usually the ones that wanted to kill you.

"Again," he said.

Ondine Reyes ran the sweep a third time without complaint, though her jaw was set in the particular way it set when she thought he was wasting her diesel. The *Magpie's Rest* — her word for the boat, never his — crawled upriver at six knots, dragging the towfish through water the color of steeped tea. On the display, the riverbed rendered itself in ghostly relief: silt bars, drowned pilings, the long shadow of a sunken barge from some war nobody aboard remembered.

And there, at the edge of the third channel bend past the drowned village of Callow's Ferry, the debris field.

"There," Ondine said, before he could point. She had good eyes. Better than good — she read that screen the way other people read faces, catching the small lies in the returns. "You see it too."

"I see it."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear if we're lying to ourselves the same way."

Ivo leaned closer. The debris spread across the bottom in a pattern nearly two hundred meters long, but it wasn't scattered. Wrecks scattered. That was the first law of disaster — entropy applied to steel and timber the same as anything else. A ship that broke apart did so chaotically, pieces flung by current and collision into a fan of wreckage that thinned as it went.

This wasn't a fan. This was a line.

Objects lay along a rough axis, spaced with an almost ceremonial regularity: a cargo crate here, a hull section there, a life ring mount, a section of railing, all of it arranged as if someone had walked the riverbed laying out place settings for a dinner party at the bottom of the world.

"That's not where things fall," Ivo said quietly. "That's where things are put."

Ondine killed the sonar and let the wheelhouse go dark except for the running lights bleeding orange through the windows. Outside, the river moved with its low endless mutter, and somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"The Meridian Star didn't sink," she said.

"No. It grounded." He straightened up, working it through, because saying it out loud made it real and he needed it real before he could decide what to do about it. "Deliberately. They brought her in shallow, maybe on that shelf east of the channel marker, and they offloaded. In order. Somebody stood on the deck and handed things down in sequence."

"In sequence to what? There's nothing down there but mud and catfish."

"To somewhere." Ivo rubbed his eyes. Three days upriver now, two tides burned, and the optimists' contract sat folded in his breast pocket like a splinter working its way toward his heart. "A wreck is an ending. This is a *transition*. Somebody wanted the Star to stop being a ship and start being something else."

"A warehouse."

"Or a door."

Ondine gave him a look — the flat, measuring look she'd been giving him since Callow's Ferry, when she'd glanced at his forged salvage papers exactly once and then handed them back without a word. She knew. He was almost sure she knew. And she said nothing, which was either mercy or strategy, and with Ondine Reyes it was probably both.

"You're enjoying this," she said. "Don't think I can't tell. You've got that look men get when the mystery finally pays them some attention."

"I don't enjoy anything. Enjoyment costs money."

"Everything costs money. That's why I run this boat sober." She turned back to the wheel. "We'll anchor above the field tonight. Current's wrong for diving until slack water after midnight. You want to go down there and play at being a crab, be my guest. But I'm telling you now, Pemberton—" she pointed one brown finger at him, nail chipped, hand steady as a compass needle, "—whatever your creditors think is down there, somebody else thinks it too. We're not alone on this river anymore."

He'd seen it himself that morning. Smoke on the eastern bank, thin and deliberate, the kind of smoke you make when you want to be found. And once, at dawn, a shape on the water behind them: low, dark, riding high and fast, gone before he could get the glasses on it. Not a fisherman. Fishermen didn't run at dawn without lights.

*Two tides ahead,* the harbormaster's boy had told him back at the last fuel dock. *Big boats, big money, asking the same questions you're asking.*

The race had teeth now. Ivo could feel them at the back of his neck.

* * *

They anchored in the lee of a bluff gone soft with creeper vine, and Ivo went over the side at slack water with a dive light and a knife and the specific dread of a man who has stopped believing in coincidences.

The water was warm near the surface and cold below, a thermocline like a held breath. Down he went, into the green-black, his light carving a cone through water thick with suspended silt. Catfish drifted past like rumors. A snag of cypress reached up from thirty feet down, branches stripped white, fingers pointing at the sky it would never touch again.

The Meridian Star lay on her starboard side against the old shipping shelf, half-buried already, the river claiming her by inches. Ninety feet of expedition vessel, research fittings along her spine, her name still legible under a skin of algae. Ivo swam her length slowly, and the longer he looked, the colder he felt, and it wasn't the thermocline.

She hadn't struck anything. No impact scarring on the bow. No breach below the waterline except the ones the river itself had made, slow and patient. Her grounding was surgical — she sat on the shelf at an angle chosen to keep her deck accessible even at low water, her port rail just kissing the surface at the lowest ebb.

A ramp of planking led from her deck down into the shallows. The planks were new. Untreated pine, silvering fast in the water, but new.

Somebody had built a road.

Ivo followed it. It ran from the Star's deck across fifty meters of flooded shelf to the base of the bluff, where it ended at a seam in the rock — a cave mouth, wide enough for cargo, dry inside by the look of the tide lines on the stone. Around the entrance, bolted into the rock, were steel eyes for winch points. Industrial hardware. Recent.

He hung there in the cold water, breathing hard through his regulator, and understood what he was looking at. The Meridian Star wasn't a wreck. She was a delivery truck, parked at the loading dock and abandoned. Everything worth taking had already been carried up that plank road and into the hill.

Including, very possibly, a memory-recording device worth more than every debt Ivo owed and then some.

His light caught something else, near the ramp's midpoint. A body.

For one lurching second his heart tried to climb out through his throat — and then the light steadied and he saw it was a mannequin. An expedition suit, full environmental rig, stuffed and posed, seated upright on the planking with its hands resting on its knees like a patient man waiting for a bus. The faceplate was fogged from the inside. Someone had written on it in grease pencil, in careful block letters:

**WE TOOK WHAT WAS OURS. LEAVE THE REST FOR THE RIVER.**

Below the words, smaller: **TWO TIDES.**

Ivo stared at the dead glass face for a long time. Then he checked his air, marked the location on his slate, and went up.

* * *

He didn't tell Ondine about the suit. Not because he didn't trust her — he was no longer sure what he felt toward her qualified as either trust or distrust; it was something rawer and more useful than either — but because the message changed the arithmetic, and he wanted to do that arithmetic alone.

*Two tides.* The writer had known someone would come. Had known how the searchers would arrive, by water, following the debris line like breadcrumbs. Which meant the writer had known about the rival fleet too — or *was* the rival fleet, or worse, was neither, was the expedition itself, alive and tidy and three moves ahead.

He sat on the *Magpie's Rest*'s foredeck after midnight with a cup of coffee gone bitter and cold, doing the math. Six tides total. Two spent. If the message meant two more, then the window on whatever was in that cave closed at tide four, and the optimists' deadline closed at tide six, and between those two numbers lived every version of his future, most of them bad.

"You found something," Ondine said from the wheelhouse doorway. Not a question.

"A note."

"From who?"

"That's the interesting part. Either from whoever emptied the ship, or from whoever wants me to think the ship was emptied." He turned the cup in his hands. "There's a cave in the bluff. Winch points, plank ramp, the whole arrangement. The Star was grounded on purpose to unload her. Whatever the optimists sent me to find may already be sitting in a hole in a hill, forty meters from open water."

"And the message?"

"Says leave the rest for the river. Says two tides."

Ondine came and leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that he could smell the engine oil on her and, beneath it, river water and tobacco. She looked upstream, where the black water slid past the anchor light and the bluff stood against the stars like a sleeping animal.

"My grandmother ran cargo on this stretch," she said. "Before the flood took Callow's Ferry. She used to say the river keeps two ledgers — one for what goes down, one for what comes back up. And the second ledger's always shorter." She glanced at him. "Men who write notes on dead men's suits want to be found, Pemberton. Or they want to be feared. Usually both. Question is which one serves them better right now."

"You think it's a trap."

"I think everything on this river is a trap. The question is whether it's *your* trap or *mine*." She pushed off the rail. "Get some sleep. At first light I'm moving us downstream a mile and anchoring in the willows. If somebody's watching that cave, let them watch an empty anchorage."

"Why downstream?"

"Because this morning's shadow came from upstream," Ondine said, "and I'd rather know what's behind me than guess what's ahead." She paused at the wheelhouse door. "Also because my son lives two bends down, and if this turns into the kind of trouble I think it's turning into, I want him on my boat before it does. I carry people, Pemberton. Remember that about me."

She went inside. Ivo stayed on the foredeck until the coffee was undrinkable even by his standards, listening to the river run its endless inventory of everything it had ever taken.

* * *

The shadow came back an hour before dawn.

Ivo heard it first — engines, but wrong for the river. Too smooth, too quiet, the muffled throb of a boat built to be unheard. He was on his feet before his mind finished the sentence, crossing the deck, killing the anchor light, waking Ondine with two knocks on the wheelhouse glass the way she'd taught him. She appeared already dressed, already awake — he suspected she slept the way cats did, in shallow installments — and had the binoculars up before he said a word.

"There," he breathed. "Off the starboard quarter. In the willow line."

The shape resolved out of the dark by degrees: a skadow, low-slung, matte gray, the kind of shallow-draft interceptor that customs services used and smugglers stole. It sat motionless in the willow shadow forty meters off their beam, and standing at its bow was a figure holding something long and horizontal.

"Kettle's people," Ivo said. He knew the name now; the fuel dock boy had given it up along with everything else — Bram Kettle, ex-optimist enforcer, currently running the salvage fleet that ate optimist contracts like this one for breakfast. "It has to be."

"It's one boat," Ondine murmured. "One boat doesn't board. One boat *announces*."

The announcement came as a sound like the world tearing a sheet.

The shot cracked across the water and the tracer round went over the *Magpie's Rest* in a streak of orange, close enough that Ivo felt the heat of it pass through the space where his head had been a half-second earlier, and smacked into the bluff behind them with a spark and a slap of echo that went rolling away upriver like rude applause.

Nobody spoke. The river kept muttering. Somewhere a night bird started up again, indifferent.

Then a voice carried across the water, amplified by a cheap loudhailer, flat and unhurried:

"*Morning, Magpie. This is Kettle's water now. Turn south at the next bend or the next one won't be a warning. You've got one tide.*"

The skadow backed out of the willows, swung in a silent arc, and ran — fast, low, gone upstream into the dark like a rumor returning to its source.

Ivo stood at the rail with his pulse hammering in his ears and the smell of cordite ghosting across the water, and found, to his own disgust, that he was smiling. Not because it was funny. Because fear, for him, had always been the purest form of clarity, and for the first time since the dockside bar, he could see the whole board.

Kettle knew where they were. Kettle knew where the cave was — or believed he did. Kettle had given them one tide, which meant Kettle couldn't move openly yet either, which meant there were rules even out here, thin ones, fraying ones, but rules. And rules were levers. His creditors had taught him that much, if nothing else.

"They'll come back with more boats," Ondine said. She hadn't flinched during the shot; she stood now with her arms crossed, studying the empty willow line like a woman memorizing a face. "That was a courtesy call. Next time it's a boarding party, and Bram Kettle doesn't do courtesies twice."

"Then we've got one tide to be somewhere he isn't."

"Where?"

Ivo looked upstream, toward the bluff, toward the cave mouth hidden behind its curtain of floodwater, toward the plank road and the dead glass face and the grease-pencil promise of two tides. Toward whatever waited in the dark that both fleets were willing to kill over.

"Downstream," he said. "For now. We do what he told us to do."

Ondine turned and looked at him fully, and her eyes in the pre-dawn gray were narrow and bright and entirely unsurprised. "You're going to obey the man who shot at us."

"I'm going to make him think I did." Ivo picked the folded contract out of his pocket and held it up between two fingers, feeling the paper's cheap weight, the weight of everything Vess Corrigan and her smiling optimists had hung around his neck. "Kettle watches the front door. So we go in through the river. There's a second approach to that cave — I saw it on the sonar, a flooded cut behind the bluff, deep enough for the towfish, maybe deep enough for a diver with a rebreather. Nobody guards a door they don't know exists."

"And if the note-writer knows it exists?"

"Then," Ivo said, "we'll finally meet the author."

Ondine held his gaze a moment longer, weighing him, the way she weighed everything — current, cargo, men. Then she nodded once and stepped into the wheelhouse, and the diesel caught on the first try, and the *Magpie's Rest* began to slide downstream through the last of the dark, quiet as intent.

Ivo stayed at the stern rail and watched the bluff recede, and beneath the fear and the arithmetic, one image kept surfacing, unbidden: the mannequin on the plank road, hands on knees, waiting patiently in the drowned light.

*We took what was ours.*

Which meant, somewhere in that hill, there was a list of what belonged to whom. And Ivo Pemberton — debtor, liar, storm-chaser, man with a buried name and a burning debt — was suddenly, desperately curious whose name was written at the top of it.

Behind them, faint on the southern horizon, the sky flickered once, twice, like a signal lamp.

Weather was coming. The kind that made rivers rise and liars honest.

One tide left.
