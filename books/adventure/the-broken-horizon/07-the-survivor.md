## 7. The Survivor

The hold was breathing.

That was the thought that came to Ivo as Ondine cut the dive lights across the flooded cargo deck of the Meridian Star — not *flooding*, not *swollen with river water*, but *breathing*, the surface rising and falling in long, slow swells that had nothing to do with the current. Silt hung in the beams like breath in cold air. Forty feet above, the hull groaned against the sandbar that had cradled it for three tides now, and the sound traveled down through the water and arrived in Ivo's chest like something knocking politely.

"Pressure's wrong in here," Ondine said. Her voice came through the comm flat and close, the way it always did, as if she were reporting weather. "Air pocket. Big one. That's a sealed compartment behind the bulkhead, and if it's still holding after three weeks, someone's been keeping it that way."

"Or something," Ivo said, and immediately hated himself for it. He was a man who dealt in wind speeds and barometric drops, in numbers you could bet your life on because they didn't care about your feelings. Ghost stories were for people who'd never stood under a supercell and felt the sky decide, calmly, to kill them. "Air pocket means survivors. Survivors mean salvage rights. Keep it professional."

"You keep it professional," Ondine said. "I'll keep us alive. Those are different jobs."

They swam the length of the hold, fins kicking up slow ghosts of silt. The Meridian Star had been a salvage vessel herself — that was the irony nobody had paid for yet — and her cargo deck was built like a cathedral's basement, ribs of steel arcing overhead, crates lashed in rows that had been disturbed. Not scattered. *Disturbed.* Moved with intention. Stacked into a barricade near the aft bulkhead, and beside the barricade, a workbench cobbled from a hatch cover and two cargo straps, and on the workbench, a hand-cranked bilge pump wired to the ship's failing emergency bus, its handle worn bright where hands had worked it, and worked it, and worked it.

Someone had been pumping for three weeks.

"Look at the water line on that bulkhead," Ondine said. "It's been maintained. Someone's fought this compartment inch by inch."

Ivo's light found the hatch. It was dogged shut from the inside — every wheel spun down, every latch thrown — and stenciled across it in flaking navy paint: *DRY STORES 2*. Someone had turned a storeroom into a lifeboat. Someone had turned a tomb into an argument.

He put his gloved hand on the hatch wheel and felt, through forty feet of river and three weeks of silence, a vibration. Faint. Rhythmic. On the other side of the steel, something was tapping.

Ondine heard it too. He saw her head tilt in the beam of her light, saw her eyes narrow above her regulator. The tapping came again — three quick, three slow, three quick. Over and over. Patient as tide.

"That's not distress," Ondine said. "Distress is random. That's a pattern. That's someone who's been tapping long enough to get *good* at it."

Ivo spun the wheel. It turned with the smoothness of recent use, and the hatch swung inward, and the air that came out was warm and close and smelled of diesel, tinned peaches, and — impossibly, absurdly, in the drowned belly of a dead ship — *lavender*.

His light swept the compartment and stopped.

A woman sat on an upturned crate in the middle of the room, hands folded in her lap, as though she had been waiting for them the way you wait for a train you already know is late. She was thin — three weeks of ship's biscuit thin, maybe less — with dark hair cut short and ragged, hacked with a knife by someone who hadn't cared how it looked. She wore a salvage tech's jumpsuit with the name patch torn off, leaving only a pale rectangle of fabric and a ghost of adhesive. Her ankles were wrapped in strips of blanket. Her eyes were clear. Not the glassy, salt-crazed stare of someone three weeks alone in a steel box — clear, level, *assessing*, the eyes of someone reading a gauge.

She looked at Ivo first. Then Ondine. Then, deliberately, at the hatch behind them, as if confirming the exit. Then back to Ivo, and she smiled.

"Captain," she said. Her voice was hoarse from disuse but steady, pitched to carry, the voice of someone used to being obeyed. "You're late. I radioed the coast authority eleven days ago. I assume they sent a private contractor instead, which tells me the insurance company has stopped pretending this is a rescue." She unfolded her hands. "Aldous Wren. Master of the Meridian Star. You'll want my log, my manifest, and my statement, in that order. And you'll want them before the next tide, because this ship is going to shift off the bar, and I'd rather not still be aboard when she does."

The compartment went very quiet. Even the river seemed to lean in.

Ivo had spent the last two tides imagining what they'd find in the wreck. Bodies, mostly. He'd made himself picture them — the bloated, the broken, the ones the river kept — because he'd learned long ago that the things you refuse to picture are the things that find you at three in the morning. He had pictured a mass grave with a salvage bonus attached. He had pictured nothing at all, which was worse.

He had never, in any version, pictured this.

"You're—" He stopped. Started again. "Captain Wren is a man."

The woman tilted her head, and the smile didn't move. It was the smile that did it, Ivo thought later. Not the words. The words were strange; the smile was *wrong*. It was a man's smile worn like a mask a size too small — confident, dry, faintly amused at the world's expense — and it sat on her face the way a borrowed coat sits on the wrong shoulders.

"Is he," she said. "That's a curious thing to lead with."

"There's a crew manifest," Ivo said. "Wren's photo is on it. Sixty-one years old. Gray beard. Six foot four. I've read the file four times. You're—" He gestured, uselessly, at all of her. "You're not him."

"And yet," she said, "I have his memories. All of them. His daughter in Corvane, who writes twice a year and signs her letters *Dad*. His first command, the *Kestrel*, lost off the shallows in the year of the double flood. His knee, which aches before rain — it's aching now, incidentally, and you should know there's a squall line coming down the valley, because I can feel it in a knee that isn't mine." She raised one hand and flexed it, watching her own fingers with the mild interest of someone checking a stranger's watch. "I remember signing for this ship. I remember every crew member's name, their evaluations, which ones I trusted and which I merely tolerated. I remember the night the experiment failed. I remember it better than I remember my own name, which — " and here the smile finally flickered, a hairline crack across the mask, " — is a problem I've been solving for twenty-two days."

Ondine had drifted to the edge of the compartment, and Ivo could feel her not-saying-anything the way you feel a storm cell building on the horizon: a pressure, a weight, a decision being made somewhere out of sight.

"What's your name?" Ondine asked.

The woman looked at her for a long moment. And Ivo watched something happen — watched the captain's composure hold, hold, and then, at the edges, thin. Like ice over moving water.

"I don't know," she said. The voice was still Wren's voice, the cadence still Wren's cadence, but underneath it, faintly, something else surfaced — something younger, rawer. "That's the truth of it. I've gone through every drawer in this head of mine. Wren's whole life is in there, shelved and indexed, every year of it. And behind it there's a door that's been painted shut. I know there's a *me* behind that door. I can feel her the way you feel weather coming. But when I open my mouth, he's what comes out." She looked down at her hands — small hands, technician's hands, a callus on the right forefinger that Wren's hands would never have earned. "I've been calling myself captain because the alternative is calling myself nothing, and nothing doesn't pump a bilge."

Ivo's mouth was dry inside his mask. He thought of the radio log — the voice reading coordinates that didn't exist, the thirty seconds of lullaby — and felt the two facts reach toward each other in the dark of his mind like hands in deep water.

"The log," he said. "The final transmission. Was that you?"

Something moved behind her eyes. "Which part?"

"The coordinates. The ones that don't exist."

"I read them," she said simply. "Wren read them. They were the coordinates of the experiment site — or the coordinates the device *believed* were the experiment site. It doesn't record the world, Mr. — " she paused, waited; he didn't offer a name, and the smile flickered again, this time with something that might have been respect — "Mr. Contractor. It records what people *remember* of the world. And when it failed, it didn't stop recording. It started *writing*."

The tapping, Ivo realized. The pattern she'd been tapping on the hull. Three quick, three slow, three quick. He'd assumed it was a code, a distress signal, a message for any rescuer. But she'd been alone. There had been no one to send it to.

"What's the pattern?" he asked. "The tapping. What does it mean?"

She looked at him, and for the first time the captain's armor came all the way off, and what was underneath was so tired and so young that Ivo had to grip the hatch frame.

"It doesn't mean anything," she said. "It's a rhythm. I tap it when the memories get loud. I've been doing it since before — before the door painted shut. My hands knew it before I did." Her voice dropped. "It's the only thing I have that isn't his."

Above them, the hull of the Meridian Star groaned — a long, deep complaint, the sound of a ship settling deeper onto the bar as the river dropped with the ebbing tide. Dust and rust sifted down through the air pocket like brown snow.

"We're leaving," Ondine said. It wasn't a suggestion. She'd unclipped from the hold's interior line and was watching the water level at the hatch lip with a professional's flat attention. "This compartment's air is going stale, her hull is going to roll when the next tide turns, and I don't ferry corpses. Can she dive?"

"She survived three weeks in a steel box," Ivo said. "She can dive."

"Can *she*?" Ondine said, and looked at the woman — at the stranger wearing a dead man's memories like a coat — and the question was not about swimming.

The woman stood. It was a captain's motion, economical, unhurried, and she gathered a waterproof satchel from beside the crate — Wren's log, Ivo guessed, and the manifest, and whatever else she'd guarded for twenty-two days in the dark. She paused at the hatch, one hand on the frame, and looked back once at the compartment: the barricade, the bilge pump with its bright-worn handle, the small stubborn architecture of survival she'd built alone in the drowned dark.

"Twenty-two days," she said, to the room or to herself. "Aldous Wren would have made a joke here." The smile came back, and this time Ivo could see the seams in it, the stitching, the effort. "I'm still learning where he ends."

They went up through the black water in a line — Ondine first, the woman second, Ivo last, his light sweeping the flooded hold one final time. At the edge of the beam, on the bulkhead beside the hatch, he saw what he'd missed on the way in: rows of tally marks scratched into the paint, twenty-two of them, and beside the marks, scratched deeper, in a hand that had pressed hard enough to gouge steel, a single word.

Not *help*.

*Remember.*

* * *

The surface hit them like a different world — gray river, gray sky, the *Reyes* rusting at anchor a hundred yards downstream, the squall line the woman had predicted already stacking up black over the western bluffs exactly where she'd said it would be. Ondine's deckhand, a silent boy called Ferro, hauled them aboard and got the woman wrapped in a blanket and a mug of something hot into her hands, and she held the mug with both hands and stared at the steam as if it were evidence.

Ivo crouched in front of her, dripping, his heart doing something he refused to name.

"I need to ask you some questions," he said. "For the salvage claim. Standard verification."

"Ask."

"Your name."

"I told you. I don't—"

"Your *name*," Ivo said. "Not the one you remember. The one on your contract. The one on the crew list. Wren's memory would know his own crew. Every name, every evaluation — you said so. So look at me the way you looked at that hatch, and tell me who the technician in the torn jumpsuit is."

She went still. The steam rose. Somewhere behind her eyes, behind the painted door, something turned over.

"Sela," she said at last. Slowly, like a word in a language she'd almost forgotten. "The manifest says Sela March. Memory systems technician, second class." She looked at her own hands. "It doesn't feel like mine. But it doesn't feel like a lie, either. It feels like — a coat that fits, if I don't look down at it too long."

"Sela March," Ivo repeated, and took out his slate, and began, with hands that were not entirely steady, to write the report that would either save his life or end it: *Survivor located. Identity uncertain. Condition stable.* Three true sentences, he thought, in a job that had never once rewarded him for telling the truth.

Above them, the first thunder came down the valley. The woman — Sela, *Sela* — looked west at the storm, and Ivo saw her flex her right hand, once, unconsciously, fingers tapping three-quick, three-slow, three-quick against the mug.

And then she said, in Aldous Wren's dry, commanding, utterly wrong voice:

"You should know, before we go any further — the device is still aboard. Intact. And I'm the only one alive who knows exactly where Wren hid it."
