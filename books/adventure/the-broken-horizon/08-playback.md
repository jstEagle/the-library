## 8. Playback

The first thing she remembers is a lighthouse.

It stands on a gray headland above a gray sea, and its beam turns with the patience of something that has never once been in a hurry. She knows the smell of the lamp room — hot brass and whale oil, though the oil was replaced by electric filament decades before she was born, and she knows this because she read it in the keeper's log, which she also remembers, every entry, in a handwriting that is not hers because the hand that wrote it was not hers either.

She remembers being Aldous Wren.

That is the shape her mind makes when she reaches for herself: *Aldous Wren, fifty-one years old, captain of the expedition vessel Meridian Star, widower, man who takes his coffee black because his wife used to put honey in it and he could not bear the sweetness after she died.* She remembers his wedding. She remembers his wife's name — Marta — and the exact pitch of her laugh, and the way the hospital corridor smelled the night the laugh stopped. She remembers forty years of tides and harbors and the particular loneliness of command.

She does not remember her mother's face.

She knows she had one. There is a hole in her where a woman should be standing, and around the hole the edges are clean, surgical, as if someone took a scalpel to the film of her life and cut out every frame in which that woman appeared. She can remember her own hands holding things — soldering irons, calibration probes, a chipped mug with a cartoon octopus on it — but when she tries to follow those hands backward to the person who owned them, the memory slides off like water off waxed canvas.

Her name was—

The thought arrives and then dissolves, and she is left holding the dissolution itself, which is somehow worse than nothing. Grief needs an object. This grief has none. It is mourning for a house she cannot find on any map, for furniture she cannot describe, for a voice she cannot hear even in imagination — because imagination, she is learning, is just memory wearing a mask, and all her masks belong to a dead man.

* * *

The fragments come at odd hours. That is what she has decided to call them — hours — though down here in the flooded hold of the Meridian Star, where the water rises and falls with the river's breathing, time is more suggestion than law.

The first fragment is always the same. It plays behind her eyes like a film run through salt water, colors bleeding at the edges:

*A laboratory that used to be a cargo bay. Banks of equipment humming along both walls, their indicator lights reflected in the black water sloshing at her ankles. The device sits in the center of the room inside its open pressure case, and it is beautiful the way a storm is beautiful — you can see the power in it, coiled and patient.*

*Wren is there. Captain Wren, standing by the console with his arms folded, watching her work with an expression she now recognizes from the inside: the careful neutrality of a man who has already decided and is waiting for everyone else to catch up.*

*"Run it again," he says.*

*"Captain, the calibration drifts every time we push past— "*

*"Run it again, technician."*

*And she does, because she is good at her job, because she has always been good at her job, because being good at your job is the one thing no flood or failure can take from you. Her fingers move across the interface. The device's hum climbs a half-step, the way it always does, and somewhere in the banks of equipment a relay clicks over, and the readout begins to scroll.*

*She is watching the numbers when the first wrongness arrives. It feels like a word she almost remembers. It feels like turning around quickly in an empty room.*

*"Captain," she says, and her own voice sounds far away, "something's reading me."*

*"That's not possible. The intake array is pointed at the archive core, not at—" He stops. He looks at the readout. She watches him look at it, and she watches the careful neutrality crack, just slightly, at one corner, like ice before it goes.*

*"Shut it down," he says.*

*She reaches for the cutoff. And the world folds.*

That is where the fragment always ends. Not fades — ends, clean as a guillotine, because whoever edited her did not believe in fade-outs.

* * *

There are other fragments. Smaller ones. Shrapnel.

She remembers rain on the Star's deck the night they salvaged the device from the drowned station, and she remembers being cold, and she remembers Wren saying, *Careful with that, it's worth more than every soul on this boat,* and she remembers thinking — her thought, her actual thought, she is nearly certain — *that's exactly why I don't trust it.*

She remembers a corridor. Running? No — walking fast, with a data slate clutched to her chest, and the sense of having stolen something, though whether she stole it or rescued it she can no longer say. The slate was warm from her body heat. On its screen, a file tree. Somewhere in that tree was a name.

Hers. Presumably hers. The name at the top of her own personnel record, the one she must have read a thousand times, typed into a thousand forms, heard spoken by a thousand voices —

Gone. The corridor remains. The warmth of the slate against her sternum remains. The name is a white rectangle where text should be, and she has learned not to press on it, because pressing on it produces a sound in her head like a wire snapping under tension, and afterward she shakes for an hour.

She remembers laughing once. She cannot remember why. It is the cruelest fragment of all — joy preserved perfectly while its cause is amputated. Some part of her keeps replaying it like a woman returning again and again to a burned house, lifting charred beams, looking for anything recognizable underneath.

* * *

On the third day — she thinks it is the third day; Ondine Reyes counts days aloud the way other people count money — the tall one with the storm-chaser's eyes comes down into the hold again.

Pemberton. Ivo Pemberton. She knows his name the way she knows everything now, by observation rather than inheritance. He moves like a man who measures distances professionally. He crouches at the edge of the water with his boots dry and looks at her the way people look at things they have decided are fragile, and she hates it, and she is grateful for it, and she does not know which of those feelings belongs to her.

"You slept," he says.

"I reviewed." She hears Wren's voice come out of her mouth — the low harbor-baritone of it, unhurried, accustomed to being obeyed — and sees him flinch, very slightly, and wishes she had any other voice to offer him. "The archive playback. What's left of it."

"Anything?"

"There's a gap." She holds up her hands, watches the firelight from his lantern move across knuckles that are too broad, too scarred, too much a captain's hands and not enough whatever-they-used-to-be. "Between the last calibration run and the flooding of the hold, there's a gap. Eleven hours, give or take. Someone sealed it."

"Sealed it how?"

"The way you'd seal a hull breach. Deliberately. Competently." She lets her hands fall. "I know how the sealing works, Mr. Pemberton, because I know how the machine works. I know its architecture the way Wren knew his engines. Which means either I learned it in eleven years as his technician—" the title surfaces whole, intact, absurdly durable, *technician*, the one plank of her old self the flood left her to cling to, "—or I learned it in the last three weeks, from the inside."

He is quiet for a moment. Water drips somewhere in the dark, keeping its own slow time.

"Which do you think?"

"I think," she says, "that Aldous Wren would never seal a gap in his own log. He was a fanatic about records. Every decision countersigned, every deviation documented. A man like that doesn't erase eleven hours." She meets the storm-chaser's eyes. "A man like that gets erased."

* * *

The second playback comes to her in the deepest part of the tide, when the hold is darkest and the water is highest and the others have gone up to sleep in the superstructure. She does not choose to play it. That is the thing she cannot make them understand — Ondine with her hard practical mercy, Pemberton with his careful questions. The device is still live. Its case is breached but its core is warm, and she carries its residue in her wetware the way a drowning man carries river water in his lungs. Sometimes it plays *her.*

This time it gives her the last hour.

*The laboratory again, but wrong — the indicator lights strobing, the water at her knees gone from black to a strange arterial brown where the emergency dyes have bled into the flood. Alarms. Not the ship's alarms; the device's alarm, a sound like a struck bell heard through cotton.*

*She is on the floor. She does not remember falling. Her hands are pressed flat against the deck plating and she is watching them as if they belong to a stranger, because they do, because they are beginning to, because the readout across the room says INTAKE: ACTIVE and SOURCE: LOCAL and she understands, with the terrible clarity of the final minute of anything, what local means.*

*"Sela."*

*Her name. Someone says her name. Wren is crouched over her, gripping her shoulders, and his face is the face of a man watching a fire take a building he built. "Sela, look at me. Fight it. You pull yourself out the way you came in, you hear me? You're the best engineer I ever— "*

*"Captain." Her voice is already changing. She can hear it thinning, rising, rearranging itself toward some other register. "Captain, I can't find the edges of me."*

*"Sela—"*

*"I can't find the edges."* And here the fragment does something no recording should do: it shows her what she felt, raw, unmediated, the terror of a woman whose interior walls are dissolving while she stands inside them. *"There's so much of him, Captain. He's so much bigger than me. Forty years. He kept everything. Who keeps everything?"*

*"A hoarder," Wren says, and his voice breaks on it. "A coward. Sela, listen—"*

*"He loved her so much."* The tears come, and they are real tears, her last real tears, shed by a self that has perhaps four minutes left. *"Marta. He loved her so much and it's coming in like a tide and I can't — I can't hold my own mother's face and his at the same time, there isn't room, there isn't—"*

*"Then let go of mine!"* Wren shouts it, and it is the most human thing she will ever remember him doing, this controlled man screaming at the machine and at himself. *"Let go of me! Keep yours! Keep yours, Sela, keep—"*

*But the tide does not negotiate. The tide comes in.*

*The last thing the technician experiences — the true last thing, the final unedited frame of the woman who was — is a choice. Small, stubborn, made in the wreckage of everything else. She cannot keep the face. But she can keep the melody. Somewhere, in some corner the intake array hasn't reached yet, she stuffs a lullaby the way a child hides a stone in her pocket before being dragged away from a beach.*

*She does not know if it survives.*

*The fragment ends.*

* * *

When she surfaces from it, she is crying.

She registers this with a strange double vision — the woman weeping, and the observer noting the weeping, and the horrible knowledge that the observer's detachment is borrowed too, lifted wholesale from a dead man's habit of command. Wren wept twice in forty years, she knows. She knows this the way she knows his coffee order. His discipline is in her now, load-bearing, holding up the architecture of a person who does not exist.

But the tears are hers. She decides they are hers. She has almost nothing left to decide about, so she decides this, fiercely, the way you grip a rail in a gale.

The lullaby. She searches for it immediately, hands pressed to her temples as if she might feel it physically, a stone in a pocket, a small dense weight of *before.* She hums scraps into the dark — three notes here, two there — and each scrap tastes almost right and then collapses into Wren's memories, into some shanty Marta sang, into a hymn from a funeral, into nothing.

Not there. Or there, but buried under forty years of a stranger's hoarded life, deeper than she can dig.

She sits in the rising water until the shaking stops, and then she does what technicians do. She inventories.

What she has: the device's architecture, complete. Wren's memories, complete, a library she did not ask for and cannot return. Her own skills — engineering, calibration, the grammar of machines — apparently too useful to delete, or too entangled with Wren's own expertise to separate cleanly. One name, surfacing now in the quiet like a fish rising to inspect the surface: *Sela.* Just Sela. Whether it is a first name or a surname or a fragment of a longer one, she cannot tell. It fits her mouth. That is all she knows.

What she has lost: everything the name used to open.

And what she suspects — this is new, this is the thing the playback has shown her, glinting at the bottom of the eleven sealed hours like metal under silt — is that the gap was not sealed by the machine.

The machine takes. It does not tidy. The gap was sealed by a *person*, after, with access to the archive core, working methodically in the flooded dark. Sealing the hours in which the technician became herself. Sealing the evidence of what Wren ordered — or what Wren allowed — or what Wren, in the last minutes, tried desperately to undo.

Someone aboard the Meridian Star edited her. Someone who understood the device well enough to hide what happened.

Which means someone aboard the Meridian Star may still understand how to bring her back.

* * *

She finds Pemberton at first light, up in the wheelhouse, drinking coffee he has clearly sweetened out of some private superstition. Ondine is at the helm, reading the river. Neither speaks. Sela stands in the doorway with the water dripping off her and says:

"When we reach the wreck line — the real one, not the debris field — there's a compartment below the aft ballast tank. Wren's safe room. He kept his true logs there, off the ship's network. Everything the official record leaves out."

Pemberton lowers his cup. "How do you know that?"

She almost smiles. It comes out crooked, grief-shaped, borrowed and not-borrowed all at once.

"Because Aldous Wren told me everything," she says. "And one of the things he told me, in the last hour he was still only himself, was that if the device ever turned on its own crew — he'd hidden the undo key where no optimist, no rival, and no machine would ever think to look." She steps into the light. "I'm going to find my name, Mr. Pemberton. And then I'm going to find out who sealed those eleven hours. Because whoever they were — they're the reason I'm the only one left."

Ondine's hands went still on the wheel. Ahead of them, around the next bend, the river widened into a brown plain of drowned treetops — and anchored among them, riding high and wrong, sat a salvage skiff flying no flag at all.
