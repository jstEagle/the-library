## 9. Tide Two

The radio woke him before the light did.

Ivo had been sleeping in the wheelhouse again, folded into the captain's chair like a man trying to become cargo, and the crackle of the set came through his dreams as rain — which was fitting, because in the dream it had also been rain, and a fire, and a name shouted across a parking lot that he still refused to hear clearly even asleep. He surfaced with his heart going hard and his hand already on the transmit key out of twenty years of habit.

*Pemberton.* Vess Corrigan's voice came through the static the way cold air comes under a door — thin, patient, finding every gap. *Status report. Tide two is closing.*

He looked at the river. Gray-green, swollen early, carrying whole trees past the bow like it was clearing its throat. Behind them, somewhere south, the rival fleet's smoke sat on the horizon in three neat columns. Ahead of them, nothing but the drowned country and the coordinates Sela-as-Wren had given them at dawn, delivered in a man's calm cadence that made the hair on Ivo's arms stand up no matter how many times he heard it.

They were eleven hours from the wreck line. Maybe less, if Ondine pushed the *Cormorant's Grace* harder than any sane pilot would.

*Pemberton.*

"I'm here." His voice came out level. That surprised him. "We're making good time."

*Define good time.*

"Define proof," he said, and then hated himself for the cleverness of it, because cleverness was exactly what they'd hired.

There was a pause on the line long enough for him to hear the room behind Vess — ice in a glass, a chair creaking, someone laughing far away in an office where drowning rivers were a line item. Then: *You know what proof looks like. Hull number. Salvage tag. A photograph of the device's pressure case, serial visible. Or a photograph of Wren's body, if it comes to that. We're flexible people, Ivo. Flexibility is why we lent you money in the first place.*

The glass clinked again. The laugh again. Somewhere in that office, his debt was being discussed between sips.

"We haven't reached the site," Ivo said. "Debris field starts at the bend below Kestrel Shoals. Sonar's picking up scatter consistent with—"

*Ivo.* Sweet as syrup poured over a hook. *Don't sonar me. You found her. I can hear it in your voice. You've had that tone since the second day, when you started lying about fuel consumption.*

His stomach dropped through the hull and into the river.

Because she was right. He'd been lying since the second day — small lies, procedural lies, the kind that felt like navigation rather than deception. Fuel numbers shaved down so nobody could calculate his true position. Speeds inflated. A weather report invented out of whole cloth because the real one would have told them he was running ahead of schedule, and a man ahead of schedule was a man who didn't need more rope. Each lie had arrived fully formed, smooth as river stone, and he hadn't even had to think about them. They'd simply been there, waiting, the way a fire waits in old wiring.

*Here's what happens now,* Vess said. *You send us proof by tide four, or Bram Kettle stops being your problem and becomes your replacement. He's already offered. He works cheaper. He works meaner. The only thing you have that he doesn't is a head start and my sentimental attachment to men who pay their debts.* A pause. *Don't confuse that with affection.*

"And if the device is damaged?"

*Then bring us the damage. Broken things appraise too. It's the empty-handed men we can't use.*

The channel clicked off before he could answer, which was its own kind of message. Conversations with the optimists never ended; they just stopped being recorded.

Ivo sat in the gray half-light with the handset warm in his fist and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the survivor. Proof by tide four meant photographs of Sela March — alive, wrong, wearing Captain Aldous Wren's memories like a stolen coat — transmitted to a company that had used the word *body* the way other people used *inventory*. Or photographs of the device, which meant telling them exactly where it was, which meant handing them the one thing in the world that could rewrite a person wholesale, the thing Wren had apparently built to order, the thing whose buyers were already named in ledgers Ivo had only glimpsed.

Or it meant lying well enough, long enough, to get them all upriver and gone before the optimists understood they'd been played.

He keyed the mic one more time, though the channel was dead, and said quietly to the empty water: "I know what I am."

The river didn't argue. That was the trouble with rivers. They'd seen everything.

* * *

Ondine Reyes found him an hour later at the rail, drinking coffee that had gone bitter as medicine, watching the drowned village of Harrow's Bend slide past on the port side. Rooftops broke the surface in rows, patient as teeth. A church spire leaned at an angle that suggested it had given up arguing years ago.

"You talked to Corrigan," she said. Not a question. She had a way of stating things that dared you to make them interesting.

"Company check-in. Routine."

"Mm." She leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that he could see the salt cracks in her knuckles and the old scar splitting one eyebrow. Her eyes stayed on the rooftops. "Routine. So tell me what you told her."

"The truth. Mostly. Debris field, timeline, weather."

"Mostly." She turned and looked at him then, and Ivo Pemberton, who had stared down a tornado at four hundred yards and lied to federal investigators with a straight face afterward, felt his ears go hot. "You told her you hadn't found the Star."

"That's not a lie. We haven't raised anything yet."

"You told her you hadn't *located* it." Ondine's voice stayed flat, unhurried, the voice of a woman reading depth soundings. "But last night you stood at this exact rail with the March woman while she gave you bearings off Wren's own memory, and you wrote them down, and you asked me how fast we could make the bend below Kestrel Shoals. I don't forget conversations, Ivo. On this river, forgetting gets people killed."

The current pulled a floating branch past the hull, slow and ceremonial, like something being presented for judgment.

"So either you lied to her," Ondine said, "or you're lying to me. And I've decided which one I can live with. But I want to hear you say it, because a man who lies smoothly without noticing is a man I need to count my silverware around."

"I lied to her." It came out easier than the confession should have. That was the part that frightened him — not the lie itself but the *finish* on it, the lacquer of it. Twenty years ago, standing in a burning boatyard with a name he couldn't say anymore, he'd been a terrible liar. His voice had cracked. His hands had shaken. The fire had taken the evidence and left him only the shame, and shame had kept him honest the way a scar keeps you careful.

Somewhere along ten thousand miles of chasing weather, the shaking had stopped. He couldn't remember when. That was the horror of it — corruption didn't arrive like a storm front you could see coming. It arrived like mileage. You just looked down one day and the odometer had turned over and you couldn't find the moment it happened.

"She wants proof by tide four," he said. "Photos. Hull number, salvage tags, the pressure case. Or—" He stopped.

"Or," Ondine prompted.

"Or a body."

She was quiet a long moment. Below them, the engine turned over with the patience of something that had outlived better engines. "And what did you tell her?"

"Nothing. Channel died."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "What did you *decide*, Ivo? Because I watched your face while you talked. You weren't deciding whether to lie. You were deciding how much."

He had no answer that wasn't another lie, so he gave her silence, and she took it, and somehow that was worse.

"Here's what I know about men like you," Ondine said finally, pushing off the rail. "You think debt is a hole you fall into. It's not. Debt is a current. It doesn't want your money — money's just the receipt. It wants your steering. Every payment you make with something that isn't money, you drift a little further from wherever you were originally headed." She tapped the deck once with her boot. "I carry people on this boat. That's the whole business. People, not cargo. The day I start ferrying cargo that talks back, I'll have stopped being a captain and started being a truck. Don't make me a truck, Ivo."

She walked aft toward the wheelhouse, and he watched her go, and the terrible thing — the thing he turned over and over like a stone with something alive under it — was that some cold practical chamber of his mind had already begun drafting the next lie. Something about equipment failure. Something about the debris field being scattered wider than hoped. A delay, plausible, professional, bought with nothing but words.

Convenient. That was the word for it. Corruption beginning not as greed or fear but as convenience — the small daily discount you took on yourself because the full price was inconvenient.

* * *

Sela March found him at dusk.

She came up the companionway with Wren's stride — that measured, command-deck walk that belonged on a taller body — and stopped at the rail beside him holding two cups of tea she'd made herself, and the domesticity of it was so strange against the wrongness of her posture that Ivo almost laughed.

"Wren used to do this," she said, handing him a cup. "Stand at the rail at end of watch. He said a captain who won't look at the water alone will lie to it in company." She sipped. "I keep remembering things like that. I don't know if they're mine."

"They're his."

"Yes. But I'm the one who has to live in them." She looked out at the darkening river. "Ondine says you lied to your creditors today."

Word traveled on this boat like damp through wood. "She says a lot of things."

"She said you were good at it." Sela turned those calm, borrowed eyes on him — Wren's patience installed in a stranger's face, and beneath it, if he looked hard, a flicker of something younger and more frightened, like a lamp behind frosted glass. "I wanted to ask you about that. Lying. Because there's a version of me that's very good at it now. Wren lied to his crew for six weeks before the experiment. I have all of it in my head — every reassuring briefing, every falsified consent form, every night he smiled at technicians who trusted him. I can feel how easy it was for him. How *pleasant*." Her hands tightened on the cup. "And sometimes, when I'm tired, that ease feels like mine. Like it's available. Like I could reach for it."

"What are you asking me, Sela?"

"I'm asking if it ever stops feeling easy." Quietly. "Or if you just stop noticing."

The river ran black past the hull. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered over country none of them could see, and the barometer on the bulkhead — Ondine's barometer, older than the boat — had begun its slow fall toward storm.

"It doesn't stop feeling easy," Ivo said. "That's the trap. You notice, or you don't. Those are the only two options, and the second one's a lot more comfortable, and comfort is how it wins."

Sela nodded slowly, as if filing it somewhere. Then, in a smaller voice, with something of her own surfacing through Wren's composure like a swimmer breaking water: "Tide four. That's when they want their proof?"

"Tide four."

"And what are you going to give them?"

He looked at her — at the woman who was also a dead man, at the living evidence of what the device in its pressure case could do, at the person the optimists had priced and the rival fleet was racing to claim. He thought about the lie he'd already drafted against tomorrow's radio call, sitting ready in his throat like a loaded tool.

"I'm going to buy us time," he said.

It wasn't quite a lie. It wasn't quite the truth. It was, he realized with a chill, exactly the kind of sentence a man says on his way to becoming something else.

From the wheelhouse, Ondine's voice carried across the deck, flat and certain as a depth sounding: "Storm cell building northwest. Barometer's falling fast. Whatever we're doing at the bend below Kestrel Shoals — we do it before dawn, or we don't do it at all."

Below them, in the flooded dark ahead, the Meridian Star waited on the bottom with its secret intact, and two tides of grace left on the clock.
