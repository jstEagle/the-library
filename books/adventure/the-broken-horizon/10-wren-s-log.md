## 10. Wren's Log

Sela woke knowing where the device was.

That was the first thing she understood about her new condition: knowledge arrived the way weather did, without her asking for it, without any door she could point to. She opened her eyes in the dim of the Meridian Star's flooded lower deck — Ivo had rigged a tarp over the broken hatch, and the water below sloshed green and patient against the bulkheads — and she simply *knew*. Wren had hidden the pressure case in a compartment behind the galley's coolant manifold, sealed with a magnetic key he kept sewn into the lining of his coat. She could feel the weight of that coat on shoulders she didn't have. She could feel the key's small cold disc against her ribs.

"I know where it is," she said.

Ivo looked up from the sonar unit he'd been nursing all morning, its screen painting the drowned corridors in ghostly amber. He had the look of a man who hadn't slept so much as paused between worries. "Where."

"Behind the galley. There's a false panel — Wren installed it himself, the second night out, before anyone else was aboard. He didn't trust the crew." She paused, because the next part came unbidden, arriving in Wren's own voice inside her skull: *I don't trust anyone with something this valuable. That's not paranoia. That's bookkeeping.* "He said — he thought that trusting people with treasure was just paranoia with better manners."

Ondine, who had been coiling line by the hatchway, stopped coiling. "You're quoting him."

"I'm *full* of him." Sela heard her own voice go flat and strange. "That's the problem. I know the galley's deck plate is loose because he pried it up drunk on the third night to check the bilge pumps. I know he cheated at cards with the first mate and always let her win the last hand because he liked the way it made her generous. I know his mother's maiden name. I know the combination to his personal strongbox and the name of the woman he wrote letters to and never sent." She pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, as if she could hold something in. "I know everything he knew. And I know it the way you know your own hands."

Ivo set the sonar unit down very carefully, the way a man sets down a thing that might go off. "Then let's go get it."

* * *

The galley of the Meridian Star had gone soft with drowning. Silt furred every surface in a grey nap, and the smell was the river's own — cold iron and old vegetation and something faintly, terribly organic, like the ship was digesting itself. Sela waded in first. Behind her, Ivo held a work lamp whose beam turned the water into moving amber glass, and Ondine brought up the rear with a pry bar and the expression of a woman cataloguing exits.

"Manifold's there." Sela pointed. "Behind it. The panel swings left, not out — it's hinged on the pipe collar. Wren had it made by a fabricator in Corvane's Landing who owed him money. The man did beautiful work and hated him for it."

She reached through the knee-deep water and found the pipe collar with her fingers. Cold. Furred. And beneath her touch, a seam so fine her fingertips wanted to call it imagination. She pushed left. The panel swung on a whisper of greased hinge — greased, after all this time, because Wren had specified marine-grade everything, because Wren had never met a contingency he didn't budget for.

Inside: darkness, and the smell of rubber and clean metal, and a shape.

Ivo brought the lamp around. The pressure case sat in its cradle like an egg in a nest built by something very careful. Matte grey composite, ribbed, the size of a sea chest, stenciled along one flank in Wren's own block capitals: **MS-1 — DO NOT OPEN BELOW DECK 2**. Below that, smaller, almost tender: **HANDLE LIKE A HEART**.

Nobody laughed. The water lapped. Somewhere above them, the ship ticked and settled, talking to itself.

"It's intact," Ivo said, and Sela heard it in his voice — that hunger, quick and quickly buried, the hunger of a man whose whole life had been measured in debts. He reached for it.

"Wait." Sela heard herself say it in a voice that wasn't entirely hers — or was it more hers than she'd admitted? She lifted the case out herself, both arms under it, water sheeting off its flanks. It weighed what a heart weighs, if a heart were the size of everything you'd ever been. "There's a sequence. Wren had a protocol for moving it. You don't carry it level, you carry it angled — fifteen degrees, vents down. If the case ever flooded, the archive medium would—" she stopped, because the knowledge was arriving even as she spoke, Wren's training sessions replaying behind her eyes, Wren's hands demonstrating on a dummy case in a dry warehouse in a city she'd never seen, "—the medium would slough. Everything in it would turn to soup. He made the whole crew practice. Twice a week."

"You remember his training sessions," Ondine said. It wasn't a question. It was an autopsy.

"I remember *being* trained." Sela angled the case, vents down, and felt the absurd tenderness of it, the way you hold something asleep. "I remember his hands showing me how. I can feel them. That's the part nobody warns you about — it's not like reading a book. It's like remembering a life you lived. It has the *texture* of having happened to me."

They moved it out through the flooded corridor, Sela in front with the case against her chest, Ivo behind with the lamp, Ondine last, watching the water behind them the way she watched weather. It took eleven minutes. Sela counted them in Wren's habit of counting everything, and hated that she counted, and counted anyway.

* * *

They laid the case on the supply boat's aft deck, under the tarp, lashed against the rail. The river ran wide and brown-gold in the late light. Ondine put two miles of water between them and the wreck before she let anyone speak.

Ivo crouched beside the case and put his hand flat on its flank, the way you'd check a horse, or a fever. "It's worth more than everything I've ever lost," he said quietly. Not to them. To the case, maybe. To himself.

"Careful," Sela said.

"Careful of what?"

"Of how that sounds. Of how it *feels*." She sat down across from him, cross-legged, her borrowed body aching in places she was learning to call her own. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it."

Ivo waited. Ondine, at the wheel, didn't pretend not to listen.

"I love this machine," Sela said. "I want you to understand that. Not the way a technician loves her work — I knew that love, and it's gone, it went with everything else. I love it the way Wren loved it. It's in me like a river's in a channel. He built it. He *made* it, Ivo, with his own hands, over nine years, and to him it wasn't a product, it wasn't a weapon, it was — " she searched the borrowed memory and found the word already waiting, "— it was the first honest mirror anyone ever built. Every recording device before it captured the surface. This one captures the *depth*. What you actually were, underneath the story you told yourself. Wren used to say most people died as strangers to themselves, and that this was the cruelest waste in the world, and that he could end it."

She watched Ivo's face while she said it. She watched the hunger and the fear fight for the same ground, and she watched hunger win the first exchange.

"That sounds like a good thing," Ivo said.

"It is a good thing. That's what makes it terrible." Sela leaned forward. "Do you know what happened to me? Not the version you've guessed. The real one. Wren ran the first full-depth overwrite on a human subject and it *worked*. That's the horror. It didn't burn me out, it didn't leave me a husk. It left me *functional*. Better than functional — I have a captain's competence, a captain's nerve, a captain's twenty years of river sense, all of it clean, all of it mine now. If you needed a leader, you could do far worse than me. The machine doesn't destroy. It *replaces*. And the replacement is seamless. I only know I'm Sela March because the logs say so. Given a week, I'd have stopped checking the logs."

The water said nothing. The engine said nothing.

"So when I tell you I love this machine," she finished, "understand what you're hearing. You're hearing the voice of the thing it makes. And the thing it makes is *grateful*."

Ivo had gone very still. Sela had learned — from Wren, from twenty years of reading crews — to read stillness the way Ondine read water. His stillness said: *I have debts. His stillness said: I have a past I'd pay anything to not carry. His stillness said: this machine could make a man who never did the thing I did.*

"You're thinking about it," she said gently. "What it could take out of you."

"I'm thinking about what it's worth," Ivo said, and it was smooth, and it was a lie, and she let him have it because Wren had taught her that you catch more with patience than with nets.

* * *

Night came down on the river, and with it a wind that smelled of rain somewhere far upstream. Ondine anchored in the lee of a drowned church — only its tower stood above the waterline, a stone finger accusing the sky — and they ate cold rice and tinned fish by a single lamp, and nobody talked about the case lashed under the tarp, and everybody was talking about it.

It was Sela who broke first. She couldn't help it. Wren had never been good at silence either.

"There's a log," she said. "His personal log. It's in the case — a separate partition, sealed. He recorded himself, every night of the expedition. The machine was his own first subject before it was ever mine." She wrapped her hands around a tin cup of Ondine's terrible coffee. "I know what's in it. That's the worst part. I know it the way I know my own name, except I don't know my own name, not really, not anymore — it's a word in a file. But Wren's log, I *have*. He recorded the night he decided to run the overwrite on me. I can tell you his reasoning word for word. He sat in his cabin and he said, *'The subject is willing. The subject understands the risk. And if it works on her, it works on anyone, and if it works on anyone, then everything I've suffered was tuition.'*" Her voice cracked on the last word, and she wasn't sure whose crack it was. "He called it tuition. Nine years and one drowned crew and one erased woman, and he called it *tuition*."

Ivo stared at her across the lamp. "You said the crew drowned."

"The river drowned the crew." Sela looked into her coffee. "But Wren's log will say why we were anchored where the flood caught us. Why he ordered the Star into the shallows and then, at the last hour, ordered her back out to mid-channel. There was a disagreement. There was a man named Okonkwo who was the first mate and who had read the manifest and understood what the device was really for, and who told Wren he'd rather scuttle the ship than let it reach the buyers. And Wren—" Sela stopped. Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange double vision, Sela's fear moving through Wren's hands. "Wren recorded the whole argument. It's in the log. The last thirty seconds of it, the part where Okonkwo says, *'You're not selling a machine, Aldous, you're selling everyone in it'* — I can hear it. I can hear it right now, in his voice, in this galley, and I was there when it happened and I was not there when it happened, both, at once, forever."

The lamp hissed. The river ran.

"Open it," Ivo said.

Ondine set down her cup with a click. "Ivo."

"Open the case. Play the log. I want to hear it." His eyes had the light they'd had over the sonar screen, over the wreck line, over every horizon he'd ever chased. "If we're carrying this thing through five tides of hell, I want to know exactly what we're carrying."

"And if the log tells you it's exactly what she says it is?" Ondine's voice was quiet, which Sela had learned was how Ondine said things that mattered. "What then?"

Ivo didn't answer. He looked at the tarp. Under it, the case waited with the patience of a thing that had already outlasted one crew, one storm, one river, and one woman's entire self.

Sela stood. Her heart — Sela's heart, Wren's calm — was beating very fast and very evenly at the same time.

"The magnetic key is sewn into the lining of his coat," she said. "The coat went down with the ship. But Wren kept a duplicate in the chart room safe, and I know the combination, and the safe is on the deck that's still above water." She looked at Ivo, and then at Ondine, and heard herself say the thing she'd been circling all night, the thing that terrified her most because it came with no resistance at all, gliding out of her on Wren's rails:

"I can have it open in an hour. And Ivo — when you hear his voice, when you hear what he built and what he was willing to pay for it — try to remember that the machine loves you back. That's its whole trick. It's not a mirror. It's a *door*, and on the other side of it is a version of you that never has to be you again."

She turned toward the bow, toward the dark shape of the wreck's superstructure still visible against the last grey light, toward the chart room safe and the combination she carried like a splinter.

Behind her, she heard Ivo say, very softly, to Ondine or to himself or to the river:

"An hour."
