## 11. The Rival Fleet

Ondine Reyes had been reading water for forty years, and the water that morning was reading back wrong.

She stood at the wheel of the *Mariana's Grace* with her coffee going cold in one hand and her eyes on the channel ahead, where the river bent east around a drowned orchard. The surface there had a texture she didn't like — a long, flat slickness, like something heavy moving underneath and pressing the chop down. Not a sandbar. Sandbars made their own weather. This was the silence of something deliberate.

"Your friends are back," she said.

Ivo Pemberton came up from below with his jacket half on. He'd taken to sleeping in it, which told her everything about how well he trusted the river these days. He followed her gaze to the bend and went still in the particular way of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"How many?"

"Three skiffs, maybe four. They've been holding station behind us since before dawn, far enough back to pretend they're not following." She sipped the coffee anyway. Cold coffee was still coffee, and out here you learned not to waste anything, including bad mornings. "They're not pretending anymore."

The first skiff came around the orchard at full throttle.

It was a salvage runner, low-slung and armored at the bow, painted the gray of wet concrete. Behind it came two more, fanned wide to cut off both channels around the orchard's drowned fence line. On the lead boat's deck stood men with the posture of people who had done this before — legs braced, weapons low but visible, faces arranged into expressions of professional regret.

"That's Kettle," Ivo said quietly.

"You know him."

"I know *of* him." Ivo's jaw did something tight. "Bram Kettle. He used to collect for the optimists. Before your time, probably. They retired him when he broke a debtor's arm in front of witnesses who mattered. Retirement didn't take."

Ondine set down the cup. She didn't reach for the rifle racked beside the wheel — not yet. Reaching for it early was how you turned a negotiation into a funeral. Instead she cut the engine to idle and let the *Grace* drift, showing them a boat that wasn't running.

"Below," she said to Ivo. "Take Sela. Keep her quiet."

"She won't like—"

"*Nobody* likes it. Go."

He went. Good. The man could argue, but he knew when arguing cost more than obedience, which put him ahead of most passengers she'd carried.

The lead skiff swung alongside with a wash that rocked the *Grace* against her own fenders. Grappling hooks came over the rail — three of them, thrown by hands that had thrown them ten thousand times — and then Bram Kettle stepped aboard as if he owned the boarding rights to every hull on this river.

He was bigger than Ondine remembered from rumor, which was itself a kind of reputation: a man whose size kept getting exaggerated because the truth wasn't frightening enough. Gray stubble, a jaw like a mooring cleat, and eyes that did no work at all — flat, patient, the eyes of someone who had learned that watching was cheaper than talking.

"Captain Reyes." His voice was gravel poured slow. "Ondine Reyes. The river rat with the clean record."

"My record's none of your business."

"It is when you're carrying my cargo." He walked the deck without asking permission, boots ringing on the plates, and Ondine let him, counting the men behind him — six aboard, plus the two skiffs holding off with more. Seven guns she could see. Probably nine she couldn't. "Meridian Star expedition property. A device. And a survivor, if the rumors upstream are worth what I paid for them."

"I carry people, not cargo."

"So I've heard." Kettle stopped at the rail and looked back toward the stern, toward the cabin where Ivo had gone. "You've got a storm chaser aboard. Pemberton. Works for the optimists, same as I used to. Which means whatever's in your hold belongs to the same people who used to sign my checks." He turned those flat eyes on her. "Here's how today goes. You hand over the woman and the machine, I pay you a finder's fee that would buy you a new boat and a dock to keep it at, and everybody stays dry. Or we do this the other way, and I want you to understand — I don't enjoy the other way. It's just cheaper."

Ondine leaned against the wheel housing and folded her arms.

"You know what I notice about you, Kettle? You said *the other way* twice. Men who enjoy violence say it once, loud. Men who are afraid of it say it twice, quiet." She nodded at the grappling lines taut between the boats. "You're afraid of something. And it isn't me."

Something flickered behind the flat eyes — not anger. Recalculation.

"The river's rising," she went on. "You feel it? Early flood this year. Every hour, this channel gets meaner, and every hour you spend boarding honest boats is an hour your skiffs have less room to run. So here's *my* offer. You cut loose, you go tell whoever sent you that the trail went cold, and you live to be retired a second time."

Kettle smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all week — a smile with no weather behind it, just geometry.

"Captain, I've drowned better arguments than yours in worse rivers than this."

And that was when the sky ended.

There was no build to it. No gathering dark, no wind arriving politely in advance. One moment the morning lay flat and white above the tree line; the next, the western horizon simply *broke*, a wall of green-black cloud standing on the earth like something erected rather than born. The pressure dropped so fast Ondine's ears popped and the coffee in her abandoned cup shivered into rings.

Flash storm. The kind the old pilots called *river-killers* — born off the high country, dropping a season's rain in twenty minutes, turning a navigable channel into a chute of white water before a man could finish a prayer.

One of Kettle's men shouted something about the sky. Another already had a hand on the grapple lines.

Kettle didn't move for three full seconds. Ondine watched him weigh it — cargo against crew, greed against survival — and watched the arithmetic come out the only way it could. The river always got its vote, and the river had voted.

"Cut loose," he said.

The hooks came free. The skiffs peeled away west, running for the slack water behind the orchard levee, and Kettle stood at the rail a moment longer, looking at Ondine with those flat eyes.

"This isn't over, Captain."

"No," she agreed. "But it's postponed, and postponement's the best deal anyone ever gets from a flash storm."

He dropped back onto his skiff, and they were gone into the growing dark.

* * *

The world went black at noon.

Ondine ran the *Grace* upriver through rain so dense it stopped being weather and became terrain — a second river falling vertically through the first one. Lightning walked the ridgelines in long white strides, and thunder came not as sound but as pressure, thumping her chest like a fist on a door.

Ahead, the channel she'd have chosen on any normal day was already gone. She could see it drowning — the safe line past Marker Nine disappearing under brown water moving fast enough to carry whole trees. The river had jumped its banks and was now flowing through places that had been fields, roads, a churchyard, memory.

"Pemberton!" she roared over her shoulder. "Get up here! I need eyes!"

He came scrambling, Sela behind him — the woman moved strangely, surely, like someone operating a body from a manual. Ivo grabbed the rail beside her, hair plastered flat, and stared at the chaos ahead.

"That's not the channel," he said.

"The channel's dead. We're going through the orchard."

"Through the—" He looked at her. Really looked, the way you look at a person to check whether they've gone mad or whether you're the one who has. "Those trees are under eight feet of water. There are fences under there. Wells. A tractor, maybe."

"There's also current, and current doesn't hide things — it *tells* you about them." She pointed with her chin at the flooded field. "See the lines? Where the water breaks smooth, something's shallow. Where it boils, something's deep. Where it runs in ropes, that's the old drainage ditch, and the ditch runs straight to the high ground above the gorge. Trees I can't help you with. Pray."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we find out together, quickly."

She brought the bow around and drove into the flood.

It was like entering a fight. The *Grace* slammed sideways within the first ten seconds, a submerged fence post kissing the hull with a sound like a struck bell, and Ondine corrected without thinking, hands and wheel and engine speaking a language older than words. Rain hammered the wheelhouse roof like applause from a hostile crowd. The drowned orchard rose around them — black trunks streaming, branches combing the surface, apples torn loose and rocketing downstream past the windows like small fleeing planets.

"Port side!" Ivo shouted. "Rope in the water—"

"I see it." A fence line, strung between two trees, sagging just under the surface — a net waiting to strip her propeller. She gunned it, took the *Grace* over the top of the sagging wire at the exact moment the wire dipped in a swell, felt it scrape keel with a sound like fingernails, and then they were through, and the ditch opened ahead exactly as she'd promised: a rope of smoother current threading the chaos, running true.

Behind them, through a tear in the rain, she saw one of Kettle's skiffs try the main channel instead.

She watched the river eat it.

No drama to it. The skiff hit the flooded gorge head where the old channel dropped away, stood on its nose for one impossible instant, and then the brown water folded over it and it was gone — no wreckage, no shouting, nothing but a boil of foam and a single life vest surfacing like a pale flower. Two men. She'd count them later, in the way river people counted the dead: not with guilt, but with ledger precision. The river's books balanced themselves; she only recorded the entries.

"Don't watch," she said, and didn't know if she meant Ivo or herself.

They ran the ditch for six minutes that felt like six years. The water narrowed, accelerated, screamed around bends that existed only in her memory of charts drawn by dead surveyors. Twice the hull struck things she never saw. Once the engine coughed — weed in the intake — and died for eleven seconds that aged her a decade, until Ivo, without being asked, wrenched open the intake hatch and tore the clog free with bare hands while the flood tried to take his arm as payment.

Then the ditch spat them out.

High water became higher ground. The *Grace* surged into the slack pool above the gorge, and Ondine killed the throttle, and the sudden silence rang louder than the storm had. Rain still fell, but gently now, apologetically, as if the sky had spent its temper.

They drifted. Steam rose off the deck. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered like a man grumbling after losing an argument.

Ivo slumped against the rail, breathing hard, knuckles bleeding where the intake hatch had bitten him. Sela stood in the cabin doorway, perfectly dry somehow, watching Ondine with Wren's calm appraisal.

"That maneuver," Sela said, in the captain's measured voice, "was not in any navigation manual I have memorized."

"No," Ondine agreed, wiping rain from her face. "Manuals are written by survivors. I learned from the other kind."

* * *

They tied up that night in a cliff cave the old pilots had used for generations — a deep limestone mouth halfway up the gorge wall, big enough for the *Grace* to nose inside, with a stone shelf above the flood line where a fire wouldn't be seen from the water. Ondine rigged the anchor lines to iron rings driven into the rock by hands a century dead, and cooked rice and dried fish on a single burner, and let the adrenaline drain out of the boat in long silences.

It was Ivo who finally spoke.

"Why?" He sat across the fire, bandaging his hand badly. "Back there. You could've handed us over. Saved yourself the risk. Kettle's fee would've bought that new boat you joke about."

Ondine fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"You want the honest answer or the flattering one?"

"Honest. Flattering answers are how the optimists talk."

That surprised a short laugh out of her. "All right. Honest answer: handing over a living woman to Bram Kettle is the kind of thing that follows a person. Not the law — the law's a rumor out here. I mean the river keeps accounts. Every pilot knows it. You put something wrong in the water, and the water remembers, and one day, years from now, in some channel you thought you knew, it hands the bill back to you with interest." She looked at him across the flames. "I've seen it happen to good pilots. Bad ones too. The river doesn't care about your record. Only your weight."

"And the flattering answer?"

"The flattering answer is that I like you, Pemberton, and I like the strange woman, and I'm too old to start disliking myself for money." She shrugged. "But honest answers travel further."

Ivo was quiet a while. The fire cracked. Water dripped somewhere deeper in the cave, keeping time like a patient clock.

"When I was running the intake line today," he said finally, "with my arm in the water. Eleven seconds. Engine dead, current pulling." He flexed the bandaged hand. "You didn't hesitate. Didn't even look back to see if I'd make it. You just held the boat steady and trusted me to do my part."

"Did you doubt I would?"

"No. That's what I can't stop thinking about. Twenty years I've measured everything in miles — distance from trouble, distance from debts, distance from the fire I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "People don't trust me, Reyes. They hire me, they chase me, they threaten me. Nobody just *holds the boat steady*. I don't know what to do with it."

Ondine studied him across the fire — the storm chaser with his miles, the debtor with his buried name, the man who calculated wind speed instead of his age. Running from something upstream-shaped, she'd thought the day she read his forged papers and said nothing. She'd been right. But she was beginning to suspect the shape of the thing upstream wasn't a place.

It was a version of himself he'd left burning.

"Then learn," she said. "Trust is just a boat. Somebody holds it steady, somebody does their part. That's the whole religion." She stood, joints cracking, and nodded toward the stern where Sela sat wrapped in a blanket, humming — softly, absently, a melody Ondine hadn't heard before. Something small and old. A lullaby, maybe, though it didn't sound like it belonged to the woman singing it. "Get some sleep, Pemberton. Tide turns at four, and after today, I want daylight under us before Kettle finds his nerve again."

Ivo lay down. But Ondine stayed up, feeding the fire, listening to the drip of the cave and the mutter of the gorge below.

Because she'd noticed what Ivo hadn't. When the melody drifted back to them across the firelight — that small, lost tune — Ivo's face had done something no storm chaser's face should do.

He'd mouthed along.
