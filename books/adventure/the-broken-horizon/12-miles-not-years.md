## 12. Miles Not Years

The storm found them an hour after dark and drove them off the river like a hand shoving at their shoulders.

Ondine read the lightning the way other people read street signs — a flicker behind the western ridge meant *move now*, a second flicker meant *faster* — and she swung the boat into a slot in the cliff face that Ivo would have sworn didn't exist until the bow was already inside it. The cave swallowed them whole. Behind them the river went white and roaring; ahead of them was stone, dry stone, and the smell of old campfires from travelers who'd had the same idea across a hundred years.

"Nobody argues with weather," Ondine said, killing the engine. "You just get out of its way and let it be wrong about you."

She rigged a tarp over the cockpit, lit one lantern low enough that its light didn't reach the water, and then did what she always did when there was nothing to do: she slept, folded against the gunwale with her cap over her eyes, breathing slow as tide.

Ivo sat on the cargo hatch with his back against the cold rock and listened to the storm try to get in.

Sela March sat across from him, knees drawn up, hands loose around a tin cup of tea gone cold. In the lantern light her face kept doing something he couldn't stop watching — it wasn't hers. The tilt of her head when she listened, the way her thumb moved along the cup's rim in small absent circles: those were gestures he'd seen on the radio logs, on the expedition photographs Vess's people had given him. Captain Aldous Wren, dead three tides ago by every measure that mattered, sitting in a woman's body and wearing her calm like a borrowed coat.

She caught him looking.

"You're doing the arithmetic again," she said. Wren's voice out of Sela's mouth — lower than it should have been, more certain. "I can tell. You do this thing with your jaw."

"I don't do arithmetic."

"You do. You count things. Miles, knots, dollars, tides." She said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Wren used to say a man who counts everything is counting away from something."

The thunder rolled down the canyon and filled the cave to the ceiling. Ivo waited for it to pass because anything he said under it wouldn't have been true enough.

"I'm not Wren," she added quietly. "I know that. But some of him is still in here, and right now he's very patient, and I think you should use that while you have it."

"Use it for what?"

"For whatever you've been not saying since the dock." She set the cup down. "You've been carrying it like ballast. I know ballast. I spent six weeks being ballast for a machine."

Outside, rain hammered the river flat. Inside, the lantern hissed. Ondine slept on, or pretended to, and Ivo understood suddenly that this was the arrangement — that Ondine had put them in this cave together on purpose, the way she'd done everything else, reading the current before it showed itself.

"There was a fire," he said.

His own voice surprised him. He hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way water comes through a cracked hull — all at once, once the crack is there.

"Twenty-two years ago. Up north, past where the charts go honest. A refinery town called Ashfall, if you've heard of it. Nobody has. That's sort of the point."

Sela didn't move. Didn't lean forward, didn't make the encouraging noises people make when they want your story. She just watched him with that terrible borrowed patience, and so he kept going, because there was no one left alive he could lie to about this except himself, and he'd been lying to himself so long the words had worn smooth.

"I was nineteen. I went by a different name then — my mother's name, Kestrel. Ivo Kestrel. I changed it the same year, and everyone assumed it was grief, and grief is easier to forgive than guilt." He turned the tin cup in his hands, though his was empty too. "There was a depot. Fuel storage, drums of it, stacked along the rail spur. And there was a man named Harlan Voss who ran the depot, and he'd been cheating the town on fuel weights for years, shorting every drum, selling the difference downstream. My father found out. My father was the kind of man who wrote letters about things. Fourteen letters, certified mail. He kept copies."

The storm hit the cliff mouth and the whole cave seemed to inhale.

"He kept the copies in our house. Which was two hundred yards from the depot. Because my father was a careful man about everything except the things that mattered." Ivo heard his voice go flat and distant, the way it did when he read instruments. "I knew Voss was scared. Scared men do arithmetic too. So I did something clever. I told Voss that if anything happened to my father, the copies were already somewhere safe. A bluff. Nineteen years old, full of my own cleverness, and I bluffed a frightened man with a depot full of fuel."

"You didn't light it," Sela said. Not a question. She'd already followed him all the way down the road and around the bend.

"No. But I made him believe the copies existed, and I made him believe they'd surface, and I stood in the yard that night and watched the sky go orange and I knew — I *knew*, standing there, before anyone said a word — that he'd done it to destroy papers that only ever lived in my mouth." His hands had gone still on the cup. "Eleven houses burned. The Reyes family lost their boats — no relation to ours upriver, but I thought of it every time I heard the name. And a girl named Ada Quist died of smoke inhalation in a house that had nothing to do with any of it. She was sixteen. She was asleep."

The lantern flame leaned in a draft that shouldn't have reached that deep into the cave.

"My father never forgave himself for keeping the copies where they could burn. He never knew they weren't the reason. He died thinking his caution killed Ada Quist, and I let him carry that, because telling him the truth meant telling him what I'd done, and I was a coward in the specific way that clever cowards are cowards." Ivo looked at the firelight on the wet rock. "So I buried the name. Took my father's surname instead of my mother's, which felt like penance and was actually just another hiding place. And I started measuring everything in miles, because miles are honest. A mile doesn't care what you did in the last one. You just put it behind you and there's another one."

He'd expected — he didn't know what. Horror. Pity. The particular silence of someone recalculating how close to sit near you.

Instead Sela picked up her cold tea, drank it anyway, and set it down.

"Vess Corrigan knows," she said.

It wasn't a question either, and it landed harder than any question could have.

"The optimists don't hire men like you for salvage," she went on, gentle as a chart showing depth. "They hire men with hooks in them. That's why the contract is in tides — a debt measured in time you can't pay back any other way. They found the name Kestrel. They found Ashfall. And they've been letting you pull the load ever since."

"They gave me a job when nobody else would touch me."

"They gave you a leash and called it a rope." She said it with Wren's flat certainty, and then — and this was the part that undid him — her face shifted, and something younger surfaced through the captain's composure, something frightened and fierce and unmistakably *hers*. "I know what it is to have someone else's life written over yours. Mine was done with a machine. Yours was done with a signature. But it's the same document, Ivo. It says: *you are what we say you are.*"

The storm was moving off. He could hear it going — the roar thinning, the thunder stepping backward up the valley, the river settling back into its long dark argument with itself.

"If I forgot," Ivo said slowly, working it out as he said it, the way you probe a wound to find the depth, "the device could do it. That's the thing I haven't said out loud. Back in the hold, when you told me what it does — I stood there listening and some part of me was doing the arithmetic. What it would cost. What it would buy. A version of me with no Ashfall in him. No Ada Quist. No buried name. Clean." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "I looked at the machine that stole your whole self and I thought: *one file. Just take out one file.*"

"And who would he be?" Sela asked. "The man without the file?"

"What?"

"The man who wakes up tomorrow and doesn't remember the fire. Who is he?" She leaned forward, and the lantern caught the planes of her face — Sela's face, Wren's stillness, and underneath both, something that belonged to neither and was becoming its own thing. "Does he love the river? Does he keep his word? Does he sleep beside Ondine's boat all night because he trusts her watch? None of that came from the fire. The fire is one file. You are the rest of the archive."

"That's easy for the machine to say."

"It's hard for me to say," she corrected. "Because I'm the proof of the other direction. I lost files I didn't choose to lose, and I can tell you exactly what's gone: I don't know my mother's face anymore. I know Wren's mother's face instead — a stern woman in a harbor town, and I grieve her, and she was never mine." Her voice held steady, but her hands had closed around each other, knuckles white. "That's what forgetting buys, Ivo. It doesn't delete the debt. It deletes the debtor, and the debt gets paid by whoever's standing closest. Your father paid yours. Ada Quist paid Voss's. If you hand yourself to that machine, Ondine pays. I pay. Everyone who loved the man who stayed up all night doing arithmetic rather than sleep."

The last thunder was far away now, almost polite.

"So I keep it," Ivo said. "The file. The guilt. I carry it."

"You carry it *accurately*," Sela said. "There's a difference. Guilt you hide makes you a man running. Guilt you carry in the open makes you a man with a heading." She almost smiled — and for one second it was entirely her own smile, crooked and unpracticed, a stranger arriving in her own face. "Wren had a saying. He was full of them; half my head is full of them now. He said a navigator's job isn't to avoid the storm. It's to know exactly where he is inside it."

Ivo sat with that. Twenty-two years of miles behind him, and not one of them had ever asked him where he was — only how fast he was leaving.

"Kestrel," he said aloud. Testing it. It fit worse than it used to and better than he expected, like a boot resoled one too many times. "My name was Ivo Kestrel."

Across the cave, Ondine stirred under her cap. She didn't lift it. She didn't need to.

"Kestrels hunt into the wind," she said drowsily, to no one in particular. "Only bird that does. Everybody else waits for the tailwind." She resettled, and her breathing evened out again, and Ivo understood that she had been awake for all of it, and that she would say nothing further, because Ondine carried people, and people got to arrive at themselves in their own time.

Sela was watching him with an expression he couldn't file under Wren or Sela — something new, something being built in real time out of salvaged parts.

"Thank you," she said.

"For a confession?"

"For the coordinates." She tapped her own temple, lightly, where the machine had written over her. "You just gave me a map of what it looks like when a person chooses to stay himself. I'm going to need that. Because tomorrow—" 

She stopped. Outside, downriver, where the storm had gone to die, a light blinked twice against the black water. Then twice again. Deliberate. Spaced. Not lightning — lightning didn't keep time.

Ondine's cap came off.

"That's Kettle's fleet signal," she said quietly. "Three flashes means *we see you.* Two means something else." She was already moving toward the helm, all sleep gone from her. "Two means *come to terms.*"

The light blinked again. Twice. Patient as a creditor.

Sela looked at Ivo, and the new thing in her face hardened into resolve.

"Tomorrow," she finished, "they're going to offer you everything you just refused."
