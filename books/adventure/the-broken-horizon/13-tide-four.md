## 13. Tide Four

The river woke him before the radio did.

Ivo had learned to sleep in the shallows of his own mind — never deep enough to drown, never shallow enough to rest — and the sound that pulled him out was water doing something wrong. Not the steady argument of the current against Ondine's hull, but a deeper, wider voice, the river clearing its throat.

He climbed the ladder to the wheelhouse and understood immediately. The banks had narrowed overnight. Sandbars that Ondine had threaded with contemptuous ease two tides ago were gone, drowned under brown water moving with the confidence of something that had been doing this since before there were charts. The drowned villages they'd passed were more drowned now — rooftops gone, only the crowns of the tallest palms breaking the surface like the last fingers of men who'd let go.

"Seasonal flood," Ondine said from the wheel. She didn't look at him. She'd been at the wheel since before dawn, he could tell, because the coffee beside her was cold and full to the brim, untouched. "Came early. It does that some years, like a guest who can't read a clock."

"How much time before the wreck's unreachable?"

She shrugged with one shoulder, the way she did when a question deserved an answer but not a whole one. "Tide five, maybe. Tide six for certain. After that the Meridian Star is a rumor again."

Ivo did the math the way he did everything — in distance, in hours, in the shrinking margin between what he owed and what he could carry. Four tides down. Two left. Somewhere behind them, the optimists' fleet, and somewhere ahead, Bram Kettle's, and between them a woman who remembered being a dead man better than she remembered being alive.

The radio crackled.

He'd been dreading it for two days, the way you dread a tooth you know will have to come out. It went off at 0600 exactly, because the optimists were punctual the way gravity is punctual.

"Pemberton." Vess Corrigan's voice, warm as a bank lobby. "You're a hard man to raise."

"I've been busy salvaging."

"I'm sure. Put Reyes on."

The request landed wrong. Not *report your position*, not *proof of salvage*. *Put Reyes on.*

Ivo looked at Ondine. She'd gone very still, the particular stillness of a woman listening to a storm decide which way to turn. She took the handset from him with the unhurried grace of someone picking up a knife.

"Reyes."

"Captain Reyes." Vess's smile was audible, which was the worst thing about her — she could smile through a radio, through a locked door, through anything. "We understand you have family in Puerto Ceniza. A sister, I believe. Two nephews. Lovely boys. The older one plays football on the north pitch, yes? Tuesdays and Saturdays."

The wheelhouse got very quiet. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"I know what you're doing," Ondine said. Her tone didn't change. That was the frightening part. It was the same level, river-worn tone she used for everything, as if threats were just another kind of weather to be read and steered around. "You're doing it badly."

"What I'm doing," Vess said, "is reminding everyone that the optimists invest broadly. In vessels. In cargo. In people's futures. And we protect our investments. Pemberton delivers the survivor and the device at the tide-five rendezvous, and your sister's boys keep their Tuesdays. That's all. This is a friendly call, Captain. I make so few of them."

Ondine set the handset down on the console without answering, which was its own answer, and turned back to the wheel. Ivo stood there holding the silence like something hot.

"I'm sorry," he said, and heard how small it was.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She adjusted the wheel a degree, two, eyes on the water. "They've had my family's name since the day I took your money. I knew the price when I signed. What I want to know is what *you're* going to do about it, because it's your debt they're collecting, Pemberton. Not mine."

* * *

He went below to think, which was a lie he told himself. What he did below was stand in the corridor outside the cabin where Sela March slept, and listen to her hum.

That was new. Two days ago she hadn't hummed. Two days ago she'd been Wren all the way through — the captain's clipped cadence, the captain's habit of touching the doorframe twice on entering, the captain's unnerving calm in a flooded hold. But since the archive, since she'd pulled her real name out of the corrupted files like a splinter from a wound, something had been leaking back in. Fragments. She'd called him *Ivo* yesterday instead of *Mr. Pemberton*, and he'd had to turn away so she wouldn't see what it did to him.

She hummed the lullaby now. The one from the dead-air broadcast, the thirty impossible seconds that had started all of this. Her mother's melody, half-recovered, full of holes she filled with her own invention.

He knocked.

"Come in, Ivo." She was sitting on the bunk with the device's backup terminal on her knees — the portable reader, the one they'd risked their lives to keep dry. She looked up at him with eyes that were still half Wren's, level and patient, but the mouth was hers now. He was starting to be able to tell. "You have the face of a man who's been offered something terrible."

"Vess called. They know about Ondine's family."

Sela nodded, unsurprised. "And?"

"And nothing. That's the point. There's no *and*. They just — said it. Like a weather report." He sat down on the crate opposite her, elbows on knees. "I keep trying to find the version of this where nobody gets hurt, and it doesn't exist. Every branch ends with someone drowning. Ondine's nephews. You. Me. The only question is who."

"Or everyone," Sela said mildly. "That's also a branch."

"Thank you. Very comforting."

"I'm not here to comfort you. I'm here because you came to my cabin at dawn to say something you haven't said yet." She closed the terminal. Wren's patience, but pointed at him like a lamp. "So say it."

He looked at the device. The pressure case sat in the corner of the cabin where they'd chained it, dull grey, unremarkable, the most dangerous object on the river dressed like an instrument of survey. He'd been avoiding looking at it for two days, the way an alcoholic avoids the shelf, and now he made himself look.

"Last night I couldn't sleep," he said. "So I did the thing I do. I started measuring. Miles, hours, the distance between what I've done and what I'd have to do. And I thought about the fire."

Sela didn't move. She was very good at not moving.

"Twenty years ago. You know the shape of it. I told you in the cave." The words came easier now, which frightened him more than the hard ones had. "Eleven people. A coastal depot. I was nineteen and I was running cargo I shouldn't have been running for men I shouldn't have been running it for, and when it caught, I ran the other way. I didn't set the fire, Sela. But I left the door barred, because the men I'd stolen from were coming through it, and I wanted them to have to go around. I didn't know anyone was inside. That's true. I tell myself that's true every single day, and every single day it doesn't weigh less."

"And the name."

"And the name." He almost laughed. "Ivo Pemberton is a man I built out of driftwood. The man who barred that door had another name, and I buried it so deep that sometimes I have to stop and think before I remember that I'm the one who buried it. Twenty years of miles. That's the trick, you know. You keep moving fast enough, the past can't catch up. You measure your life in distance and it never has to be measured in anything else."

The river thudded against the hull. Somewhere above, Ondine's boots crossed the deck.

"Last night," Ivo said, "lying there, I had the thought. The thought you'd have to be a particular kind of ruined to have. And the thought was: the device works. It took everything from you — your name, your mother, twenty-something years of being a person — and it did it *cleanly*. No scars. You didn't suffer the loss. You can't suffer a loss you don't know happened. You woke up as someone else and the someone else was *fine*."

Sela's face had gone very still. "Ivo."

"Let me finish, because if I don't finish I never will." His hands had started shaking and he pressed them flat against his thighs. "The thought was: I could put my head under that thing and wake up as nobody. Not as someone new — I'm not stupid enough to think I'd get to choose — just as *not him*. Not the man who barred the door. The debt would still exist on paper, but the man who owed it wouldn't. The optimists would have a salvage operator with no history and no guilt and no reason to ever lose sleep again. And I would be — " His voice caught on it. "I would be *free*. That's what it would feel like from the inside. Freedom. And the eleven people in the fire would stay dead, and no one would even be sorry, because the one person left who could be sorry about it wouldn't exist."

The silence after that was the longest of his life.

Sela set the terminal aside and leaned forward, and for a moment she was neither Sela nor Wren but something older than both — a person at the bottom of a well looking up, who knew exactly what water was.

"Do you know what it's like?" she said quietly. "Being fine? I can tell you. It's like being a room after the furniture's been sold. The walls are fine. The light comes in fine. You walk through yourself and there's nothing to bump into. Wren was fine. Wren was *perfectly* fine, right up until the day his fine ran out, because a self that's been installed doesn't have roots, Ivo. It doesn't hold in weather. I watched him — I watch *me* — reach for things that aren't there. Opinions with no floor under them. Love with no address." She touched her own sternum. "You want to give away the guilt. I understand. God, I understand better than anyone alive. But the guilt is load-bearing. Take it out and the whole building comes down, and what's standing in the rubble afterward isn't you. It isn't anyone."

"And what if there's nothing worth keeping?" he said. "What if the honest accounting is that Ivo Pemberton is just the fire wearing a man's coat?"

"Then you rebuild," she said. "Badly. Slowly. From the sliver. That's what I'm doing. I have a name and a melody and maybe forty seconds of my mother's kitchen, and I am building a person out of *that*, and some days the person is mostly holes. But the holes are mine." Her voice cracked and steadied. "You don't get a clean self, Ivo. Nobody does. You get the one you can live in. That's the whole offer life makes, and it's a better offer than that machine's."

He sat with it. The temptation didn't leave — that was the thing no one told you about temptation; it didn't leave, it just got a smaller room in the house. But it got the smaller room. He felt it move, felt it sulk, felt it start planning.

"I'm not going to do it," he said.

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because you told me first." She almost smiled. "The man who does it doesn't tell anyone. The telling is the part of you that's still load-bearing."

* * *

At noon they came around a bend and found the trading post.

It sat on a bluff above the flood line — a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets and a fuel depot, the last fixed point before the river went wild into the gorges. Ondine wanted fuel and water and didn't want to stop; they had no choice. The flood had turned the next stretch into white water, and you didn't run white water on a quarter tank.

Ivo was on deck coiling line when he saw the boats.

Three of them, moored at the post's lower dock, low-slung and black-hulled, engines cowled, the kind of craft that didn't fish and didn't ferry. He knew the make. He knew the *owner*. And standing at the rail of the lead boat, hands folded, watching their approach with the unhurried attention of a man watching weather he'd ordered in advance, was a slab of a man in a salt-white coat.

Bram Kettle.

Ondine saw him at the same moment. Her hands tightened on the wheel and then, deliberately, loosened.

"Trading post or not," she said, "we can run the gorge on what we have. It'll be ugly."

Ivo watched Kettle watching them. Kettle raised one hand — not a wave, an invitation. The gesture of a man who has already counted your options and found them flattering to him.

"No," Ivo said. "He'd run us down in the narrows and call it the flood. We go in. We buy our fuel. And I hear what he's selling." He looked down at the deck, at his own hands on the line, at the grey pressure case visible through the open hatch below, and felt the temptation shift in its small room, opening a window. "Then I decide what it costs to say no."

The current took them toward the dock. Kettle smiled, and it was Vess's smile, learned from the same school, and Ivo understood that the optimists hadn't sent him.

He'd come on his own account.

"Captain Reyes," Kettle called across the water, voice carrying like something thrown. "Fuel's free today. Everything's free today. All I want is a conversation."

Ivo stepped to the rail. "Then talk fast, Bram. The river's rising."

"So it is." Kettle's smile widened. "And I'm told you're the man who knows exactly how much anything is worth. Let's find out what *she* is."

Behind him, below decks, Sela's lullaby stopped mid-phrase.
