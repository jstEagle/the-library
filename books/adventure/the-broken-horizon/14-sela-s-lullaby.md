## 14. Sela's Lullaby

The melody came to her in the dark between watches, when the river had gone quiet and the only sound was water worrying the hull of the *Vantablack*—no, that wasn't right, the boat was called something else, Ondine's boat, the *Marrow*, old as rust and twice as stubborn—and Sela lay in a bunk that smelled of diesel and mildew and did not know whose hands were folded on her chest.

The tune arrived first as rhythm. A rocking. Not the boat's rocking—something older, something that predated the river entirely. Three notes up, two notes down, a pause like a held breath, then three more. It was the kind of melody a body makes when it is trying to soothe another body. Lullaby-shaped.

She hummed it before she knew she was humming it.

And then she stopped, because Captain Aldous Wren did not hum lullabies. Wren was sixty-one years old, a widower, a man who kept his grief in a locked drawer with his dead wife's letters and never once, in forty years of commanding vessels through the drowned places of the world, had been heard to sing. Sela knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat—which was to say she knew it perfectly, completely, and could not for the life of her say how she knew it, or whether it was hers at all.

But the melody kept going. It went on without her permission, rising in her throat like floodwater, and this time she let it, lying in the dark, humming three notes up and two notes down while somewhere behind her eyes a door she hadn't known existed rattled on its hinges.

*Someone sang this to me.*

Not to Wren. To *her*. To the body. And Wren's memories, vast and orderly as a chart room, contained no such song. She searched them the way she'd learned to search them—deliberately, methodically, the way Wren himself would have searched a coastline for a safe anchorage—and found nothing. No lullaby. No mother humming over a cradle. Wren had no children. Wren had a wife named Iris who'd died of a long illness and a brother in the southern ports he hadn't spoken to since—

Since nothing. The memory frayed there, and Sela realized with a small cold shock that some of Wren's memories were fraying too, worn thin by use, by being lived in by someone they weren't made for. She was a squatter in a mansion, and the mansion was slowly learning to resist her.

Good, she thought, and then caught herself, horrified. *Good?* Part of her—the part that called itself Aldous Wren—mourned every lost detail like a man watching his house burn. The rest of her, the small dark part that hummed lullabies, wanted the whole edifice down.

She sat up in the bunk. The lamp above her swung gently with the current, throwing shadows that moved like slow animals across the bulkhead.

Three notes up. Two notes down.

Her mother's voice—if it was her mother's; she had no face to attach to it, only a warmth, a low alto roughened by cigarettes or river air—singing words she almost had: *down where the water...* no. *Down by the...* Something about a shore. Something about coming home when the tide turned. The words were gone but the shape of them remained, the way a footprint remains in mud after the foot has moved on.

She got up. She dressed in the dark—Wren's clothes, still too big at the shoulders, and beneath them, against her skin, the technician's coveralls she refused to throw away, name tag torn off, because some stubborn animal part of her believed the tag could be sewn back on someday, that the name under it might come back.

The corridor outside was narrow and dim. Ivo's light showed under his door, a thin gold line. He didn't sleep much either. She'd noticed that. He measured things constantly—distance, speed, hours until tide—with his lips moving slightly, a man doing arithmetic against drowning. She recognized the habit from the inside, because she did it too now, except what she counted was memories. How many of Wren's today. How many of hers. The ledger always came out wrong. The ledger always came out wrong in the same direction.

She climbed to the deck.

*

Night on the river was its own country. The banks dissolved into blackness and the water ran silver where the moon touched it, and the stars came down so low over the flats they seemed to trail in the current. Ondine stood at the wheel even now, though the *Marrow* was anchored for the night in the lee of a drowned church tower—the top third of it rising out of the water like a finger raised in warning. Ondine didn't trust anchors alone. She trusted herself.

"You're up," Ondine said, not turning. "Couldn't sleep either?"

"I found a song."

Ondine glanced back. In the moonlight her face was all planes and patience. "Found it where?"

"Inside me. Underneath him." Sela wrapped her arms around herself. The night air was cool and smelled of silt and green rot and, faintly, of the wreck downstream, though that was impossible; you couldn't smell a shipwreck from twenty miles. But she did anyway. She smelled the Meridian Star the way dogs smell storms. "It isn't his. That's how I know it's mine. Everything else in my head belongs to Aldous Wren. This doesn't fit anywhere. It just—" she pressed a fist lightly against her sternum— "sits here. Like a stone in a shoe."

Ondine considered her for a long moment. The wheel creaked under her hands, an idle motion, keeping time with nothing.

"My grandmother used to sing to the engine," Ondine said. "Old songs, from before the floods took the valley towns. Said engines were lonely and died young if nobody sang to them." She nodded at the drowned tower. "People think memory lives in the head. My family always believed it lives in the body. Hands remember rope work. Feet remember gangways. Maybe your mother's song didn't live in the part of you they overwrote."

"Or maybe it's nothing," Sela said. "Maybe it's a fragment of Wren's childhood that misfiled itself."

"Is that what you're hoping?"

"No." The answer came out fast and fierce and surprised them both. Sela laughed—a strange sound, borrowed lungs making an unfamiliar noise. "No. I'm hoping it's everything."

"Then follow it," Ondine said simply, as if following a half-remembered lullaby into the architecture of one's own stolen mind were the most reasonable course in the world. "You've got that machine aboard. Machines keep what people throw away."

Sela looked downstream, toward the black water and the drowned church and, beyond both, the place where the Meridian Star lay grounded in the shallows like a beached whale, its belly full of the device. They'd left it there—couldn't carry it, couldn't yet destroy it, a decision suspended like a held breath while tides counted themselves down. But the device had a backup archive. Wren's memories—her memories—had told her so, in Wren's own meticulous voice: *primary store volatile, archival layer redundant, mirrored nightly.* The archive held everything the device had ever recorded. Every experiment. Every subject.

Every overwrite.

Somewhere in that mirrored vault of someone else's yesterdays, there might be a record of the woman Sela March had been before the machine ate her. Corrupted, maybe. Fragmented. But machines kept what people threw away.

"I'll need Ivo," she said.

"He's awake," Ondine said. "He's always awake. Go knock before he wears a hole in the floor."

*

He opened the door with the light behind him, and for a moment Sela saw him as a silhouette—all sharp angles and restless weight, a man built out of miles. He looked at her the way he always did now: a flicker of fear quickly mastered, the fear of a man standing next to something that shouldn't exist.

"The archive," she said. "I need to go into the backup files. Tonight."

"It's on the wreck."

"I know where it is."

"Sela." He said her name carefully, the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing—as if the name itself might be fragile, might not survive being spoken too hard. "It's nearly slack water. The approach channels are silted. Kettle's fleet is somewhere between here and the trading post, and the optimists have divers working the northern debris line. Going out there tonight is—"

"Necessary." She stepped closer. "Ivo. I found a song tonight that isn't Wren's. Do you understand what that means? There is something underneath him. Something they didn't get. If there's one thing, there might be more—in the archive, in the raw recordings, in whatever the device kept before it wrote over me. I can find myself, Ivo. Or pieces of myself. Enough to know who I'm rebuilding."

Something moved across his face—not doubt. Recognition. She'd seen it before, in the cave, when he'd told her about the fire, about the name he'd buried, and asked her—asked *her*, the woman with no self—to tell him who he'd be if he forgot. He carried his erased life around with him voluntarily, chained to it, and she could see now that part of him envied her and part of him pitied her and neither part knew which was worse.

"Slack water turns in ninety minutes," he said finally. "After that we've got a six-hour window before the next flood stage. We take the skiff, quiet, no lights past the marker stakes. We're in and out before the water gives us away." He was already reaching for his jacket, already calculating—she could see the numbers moving behind his eyes, miles per hour, minutes per mile, the currency of his whole existence. "And Sela—if the archive shows you something you don't want to see—"

"Then it's mine to not want," she said. "That's the point."

*

The Meridian Star rose out of the shallows like the ribcage of something that had once been magnificent. Grounded on the sandbar at a list, decks awash, superstructure black against the lesser black of the sky. Ivo brought the skiff in along the leeward side with a touch so gentle the hull kissed the wreckage without a sound, and they went up the boarding ladder hand over hand, into the ship's wet throat.

Inside, the flooded corridors breathed. Water moved through the lower decks with a slow tidal pulse, and the air tasted of brine and rust and the sweetish chemical ghost of the experiments. Sela's skin crawled as they waded toward the laboratory compartment—but not, she noticed, with fear. With familiarity. Her feet remembered these passageways. Her hands reached for handrails at exactly the right heights. Whatever else they'd taken from her, the body had kept its map.

The device sat in its pressure case where they'd cached it, lashed to a stanchion above the waterline, its status lights pulsing a slow patient green. Sela knelt in front of it and felt—she hated this, hated it with everything she had, which admittedly wasn't much and none of it verifiably hers—felt *tenderness*. Wren had loved this machine. Had championed it, funded it, dreamed of it. And those feelings lived in her now, indistinguishable from her own, so that looking at the device was like looking at a beloved child and knowing the child had tried to kill you.

"Interface," she said aloud, and her voice came out in Wren's clipped command cadence, and she flinched at the sound of it.

The case unsealed with a sigh of equalizing pressure. Inside, the device waited, screens dark, coils cold. She pressed her palms to the interface pads—one of the few protocols she retained from her technician days, muscle memory surviving like the lullaby, like the handrails—and the machine woke, recognizing the pattern of her nervous system the way a lock recognizes a key. Even overwritten, she was still indexed. Still on file.

*"Archival access,"* the device said, in a voice like warm static. *"State query."*

"Backup archive," Sela said. "Full index. Chronological."

*"Archive spans eleven thousand four hundred and six recorded sessions. Display parameters?"*

"Subject files. Cross-referenced by neural signature." She closed her eyes. "Mine."

The screen bloomed with data. Ivo leaned in beside her, close enough that she could feel the heat of him, the restless energy of a man who hated standing still. Rows of entries scrolled—session numbers, dates, integrity percentages. Most of the recent files, her own sessions, glowed a sick amber: *CORRUPTED — PARTIAL OVERWRITE — DATA UNRECOVERABLE.* Page after page after page of amber. The machine had eaten her meal by meal, day by day, and what it couldn't digest it had chewed and spat back in fragments.

"There," Ivo said quietly. "Third from the bottom. Integrity eighty-one percent."

One file burned a steady, uncorrupted green.

*"Session 9,204,"* the device intoned. *"Baseline recording. Subject: March, Sela V. Recorded prior to experimental overwrite. Full-spectrum capture. Integrity: one hundred percent."*

Her real name. On the screen, in plain characters, indifferent as water: **March, Sela V.**

She had known it, of course. Ondine and Ivo had told her, had found it in crew manifests, had spoken it to her a hundred times hoping it would catch somewhere, take root, grow. But hearing it from the machine—from the witness, from the thing that had been there—was different. The device didn't believe in her name. It *knew* it. It had recorded the last hour of the person who owned it.

"Play it," she whispered.

*"Audio and neural playback will require interface depth of—"*

"Just the audio. Just the beginning."

There was a pause, a click, a breath of tape-hiss—and then a voice filled the flooded compartment, a woman's voice, bright and tired and alive:

*"Baseline log, session nine-two-zero-four. Technician Sela March, recording for posterity because Aldous bloody Wren wants baselines on everyone before we run the trial. So. Posterity: my name is Sela March. I'm thirty-four years old. I was born in Corry's Landing, which is underwater now, which is probably why I signed onto this mad expedition—figured if the river took my hometown, I'd at least get to map its grave. My mother used to sing me this song about the tide turning, and I can't remember all the words anymore, which frightens me more than the machine does, honestly, because if I'm losing it now, at thirty-four, what will be left at fifty? So here. For posterity. Before I forget."*

And then the voice began to sing.

Three notes up. Two notes down. A pause like a held breath.

*Down where the water meets the morning,*
*where the tide turns twice a day,*
*come home, come home, the light's returning,*
*the river knows the way.*

Sela's legs gave out. She went down into the flooded compartment on her knees, water soaking through Wren's trousers, and wept—except that even her tears were suspect now, because Wren had grieved too, Wren had wept at his wife's graveside, and how could she know whose sorrow was shaking her shoulders? But the song kept playing, and the song was hers, *hers*, the one artifact in the world that belonged to no captain, no experiment, no creditor or buyer or fleet—and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood, not because it will save her but because letting go is unthinkable.

Ivo crouched beside her. He didn't touch her. He understood, somehow, that touch would complicate the question of whose body was being comforted. He just stayed, a solid presence in the dark, while the recording played through to the end.

*"End baseline log,"* the voice said, brisk again, wiping its eyes audibly. *"Posterity, if you're listening: I made it. Remember me."*

Silence, except the water.

"She knew," Ivo said hoarsely. "On some level, going in—she knew what the trial might do. That's why she recorded it. Insurance."

"A message in a bottle." Sela lifted her head. Her face was wet and she didn't wipe it. "And I'm the coast it washed up on." She turned back to the screen, to the wall of amber corruption surrounding that single green flame. "How much of the rest survived? Fragments. Anything."

*"Corrupted sessions retain partial data,"* the device said. *"Estimated recoverable content across all subject-March files: between four and nine percent. Reconstruction possible but incomplete."*

"Nine percent." Ivo exhaled slowly. "Nine percent of a life."

"It's more than I have now." She looked at him. "It's more than zero, Ivo. Do you know what zero is like? It's like being a house where all the furniture belongs to someone else. Nine percent is a chair. Nine percent is a song. Nine percent is a *name*." She put her hand flat on the device's casing, and this time the tenderness that moved through her she chose to interpret as her own—claiming it, daring anyone, including Aldous Wren, to dispute it. "Copy the March baseline. All of it. And start pulling fragments from the corrupted files. Everything recoverable. I want it stored separate from the primary archive, encrypted, keyed to my neural signature."

"That'll take hours."

"We have until the tide turns." She was already standing, wiping her knees, wading toward the ladder with water streaming off her. "And Ivo—when we're done, I want you to hear something. The corrupted files aren't all mine."

He stopped. "What do you mean?"

"The index. Session numbers before mine. Hundreds of them. Other subjects, other overwrites—some marked *COMMERCIAL TRIAL*, some marked with codes I don't recognize." She looked back at him, and in the green glow of the device her borrowed face looked, for a moment, entirely her own—fierce and frightened and awake. "Wren wasn't just experimenting, Ivo. He was filling orders. And somewhere in this archive is a list of who those orders were for."

Above them, far off through the hull, thunder muttered over the flats—and beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, the river began to rise.
