## 15. Kettle's Bargain

The trading post sat where the river bent lazy and wide, a sprawl of tin roofs and rope bridges strung between drowned pilings that had once held a town. Now it was just a market on stilts: fuel drums, dried fish, satellite uplink in a cage of chicken wire, and every kind of person who came to the river to disappear or to find someone who'd tried.

Ivo saw the skiffs first.

Three of them, moored at the far end of the dock, hulls painted the flat gray of gun metal, engines cowl-tarped against the drizzle. He knew the paint job. He knew the way the crews stood — spread out, casual, each one angled toward a different approach like men who'd been told *watch the doors* without being told why. His stomach dropped the way it did before a wall cloud sheared off the mesocyclone, that cold plunge that said: *you are already inside it.*

"Ondine," he said quietly. "Don't stop at the main dock."

She was already looking. Her jaw worked once, side to side, the way it did when she was reading water she didn't like. "Too late for shy," she said. "They've seen us. If I turn away now, they know we know." She swung the boat in anyway, wide and slow, buying herself thirty seconds of thinking time, and killed the engine ten meters short of the pilings. "Bram Kettle?"

"You know him."

"I know his reputation. Ex-optimist enforcer. They cut him loose or he cut himself loose, depends which story you buy." She spat over the rail. "Both stories end with somebody drowning."

Sela came up from below deck with Wren's logbook under her arm, and Ivo watched her take in the gray skiffs, the watching men, and do nothing at all. That was the worst part of her, some days — the calm. Wren's calm. A captain's calm, worn on a body that had never earned it, fitted so well now that sometimes Ivo forgot it was stolen. She looked at him and said, "Aldous kept a list of people he trusted. It was very short."

"How short?"

"One name. And Aldous was lying about that one too."

* * *

Bram Kettle was waiting under the awning of the trading post's central hall, sitting at a table set for four with a pot of coffee and four cups, as if this were a meeting he'd scheduled weeks ago. He was a big man gone soft in the middle but not in the hands, and he had the particular stillness of someone who had done violence often enough that he no longer needed to rehearse it. When Ivo came up the ramp, Kettle poured four cups without asking.

"Pemberton." He said the name like he was checking it against a manifest. "Sit. Coffee's terrible. It's always terrible out here. Drink it anyway."

Ivo sat. Ondine stood behind his shoulder, arms crossed, close enough that Kettle's two men — one by the door, one pretending to browse a shelf of rusted tools — had to account for her. Sela took the fourth chair. Kettle's eyes went to her and stayed there a beat too long, the way a man looks at cargo.

"You're ahead of schedule," Ivo said. "Two tides ago you were a day behind us. Now you're here first. Either your pilots got good or somebody sold us."

"Somebody always sells somebody." Kettle smiled. It was a working smile, all function, no warmth. "That's the river. That's the business. You'd know, Pemberton. You used to sell weather footage to insurance companies while the same companies were paying you to chase it. Man after my own heart."

"That was a long time ago."

"It's never a long time ago. That's the thing about debt." Kettle slid a cup toward him. "It doesn't age. It just accrues interest. Speaking of which—" he turned his wrist over and read something off a battered slate, though Ivo would have bet money there was nothing on it, "—your balance with Corrigan's people stands at a number I'm going to say out loud because I want to watch your face when I do. Four hundred and twelve thousand. Plus the tide-fees they've been charging you since tide three, which is a creative piece of accounting even by their standards. Call it four-sixty by last light."

Ivo kept his face still. He'd had practice. There had been years — the fire year, the year after — when his whole life was keeping his face still while numbers were read aloud to him.

"Now," Kettle said, "here's what's interesting. Vess Corrigan sent you upriver to find a boat. A salvage job. Hull and contents, standard recovery contract, maybe forty thousand finder's fee if the optimists feel generous, which they don't." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and lowered his voice as though the rain on the tin roof might be taking notes. "But Vess didn't tell you what the contents were worth, did she. Because Vess doesn't know. Vess thinks she's recovering a memory-recording rig, some prototype, salvage value, auction it to a research house. Vess is thinking in tens of thousands."

"And you're not."

"I've seen the expedition's real manifest." Kettle tapped the table once. "Wren filed it under a shell company out of Meridian Port. The device aboard the Star isn't a recorder, Pemberton. It's a writer. It doesn't just keep what a person remembers. It can put something else there instead. Whole identity, clean install." He glanced at Sela, and his voice went almost gentle, which was worse than anything he'd said yet. "You'd be the proof of concept, ma'am. No offense intended."

Sela looked back at him with Wren's level gray patience. "None taken," she said. "You have the wrong man's face when you look at me, but none taken."

Kettle blinked — the first honest reaction Ivo had seen from him — and then laughed, short and genuine. "God. You really are him in there. That's the most unsettling thing I've seen since the flood year." He turned back to Ivo, and the humor drained out of his face like water through scoured gravel. "Here's my offer. The woman and the machine. Both, together, delivered intact to my buyers at the delta. I pay your debt in full today — all four-sixty, wired before you leave this table. And then I pay you the same amount again. Yours. Clean. No optimists, no tide-fees, no Vess smiling at you across a desk like weather about to turn. Nine hundred twenty thousand and you walk away owning yourself for the first time in your adult life."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere down the dock a generator coughed and caught.

Ivo looked at the coffee. He made himself look at the coffee, because if he looked at Sela right now he was afraid of what she'd see in his face, and worse, he was afraid she wouldn't see anything surprising at all.

Nine hundred twenty thousand. He did the arithmetic the way he did everything — in miles. At his old chase rates, at storm-season rates, at the rates he'd actually been paid before the fire and the fall and the slow sink of the years, nine hundred twenty thousand was eleven thousand miles of driving into weather that wanted to kill him. It was the rest of his life, bought back in a single transaction. It was never again standing in a dockside bar while men called themselves optimists explained, kindly, what he owed.

It was also a woman sold.

He heard himself say, "The device may not work. You've seen what it did to her—"

"It worked perfectly," Kettle said. "That's the point. Look at her. She's healthy, calm, functional. She remembers being a man named Aldous Wren down to his childhood dog. If it had failed, she'd be a vegetable or dead. Instead she's sitting here drinking terrible coffee." He shook his head slowly. "Pemberton. I've read the file. I know what you did twenty years ago."

The word *fire* rose in him like floodwater.

"I know about the dry-goods warehouse in Calder," Kettle went on, soft and relentless. "I know about the night watchman who wasn't supposed to be there. I know you drove inland afterward and changed your name and never stopped moving, because a man in motion is hard to collect from — debts, guilt, all of it. And I know that somewhere in that machine's archive there might be a version of you that never did any of it. A clean install." He spread his hands. "My buyers would pay for that too. Think about it. Not just the money. The mercy."

For one full second — one — Ivo let himself think about it.

He let himself imagine waking up tomorrow without the weight. Without the smell of smoke that lived in the back of his sinuses and came out on hot dry days. Without the name he'd buried and the one he wore over it like a tarp over a wreck. A self with no fire in it. He imagined it the way a drowning man imagines air, completely, helplessly, for exactly as long as the imagining takes.

Then he saw Sela's hands on the table. Steady. Borrowed hands. Hands that wept with someone else's grief because their own had been taken away, and he understood — with a clarity that felt like the moment a tornado drops out of a wall cloud, sudden and total — that Bram Kettle was offering to do to him what the machine had done to her. Sell him an erasure and call it mercy.

"No," Ivo said.

Kettle raised his eyebrows. "That was fast."

"It was slow," Ivo said. "Twenty years slow." He pushed the coffee away. "No sale. Not her. Not the machine. Not me."

Kettle studied him for a long moment, and Ivo watched the man reprice everything — the situation, the risk, the odds. Then Kettle smiled again, and this one had teeth in it. "Okay. Here's what happens now. You stall — and you are stalling, Pemberton, don't insult us both — and my buyers get impatient, and impatient buyers send people. I gave you the civilized version. The next version has fewer chairs." He stood, unhurried, and dropped coins on the table for the coffee neither of them had drunk. "One more thing, free of charge. The seasonal flood's coming early this year. My hydrologists say the river comes up five meters inside two days. After that, the Star's unreachable until the dry season — and so is every route south. Whatever you're going to do, you're going to do it fast, in front of witnesses, with my skiffs sitting on your wake." He paused at the top of the ramp and looked back. "Vess taught me one useful thing before we parted ways. Everybody sells. They just haven't met their price yet. You'll meet yours. They always do."

He walked out into the rain. His men folded around him, and the gray skiffs began, one by one, to cast off — but slowly, lazily, staying in sight. A shadow, not an attack. Yet.

* * *

Ondine didn't speak until they were back aboard and moving, threading upstream past the trading post's outer pilings, putting distance between them and the gray hulls.

"You considered it," she said. Not a question. She kept her eyes on the channel.

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"A second."

"A second's a long time at those prices." She adjusted the wheel a degree. "I'm not judging you, Ivo. I want that clear. I've sold things I shouldn't have. Everyone on this river has. But I need to know which man I'm carrying. The one who said no, or the one who's going to say yes later when it's harder."

Ivo stood at the rail and watched the trading post shrink behind them, its tin roofs going dull in the rain, and hated himself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional. Because the truth — the truth he couldn't say out loud, the truth that sat in his chest like ballast — was that Kettle hadn't offered him money. Money he could refuse. Kettle had offered him the fire, undone. And some part of Ivo Pemberton, some part older than his name, was still standing in that warehouse doorway wanting it.

"Sela," he said.

She was at the stern rail, wind pulling at hair that wasn't quite hers. She turned. In her arms she carried the pressure case, and she held it the way you hold a sleeping child or a bomb.

"He said there might be a version of me in there," Ivo said. "A version that never lit the match. Is that true? Can it do that?"

Sela was quiet for a long moment. Rain ran off the case's edge in a thin bright line.

"There's a backup archive," she said finally. "Expedition baseline scans. Wren scanned the whole crew the day we launched — I did the scanning, I remember doing it, I remember his hands on my shoulders telling me it was routine." Her voice stayed flat, captain-flat, but her borrowed hands tightened on the case. "If there's a scan of you in there, from some other job, some other year — then yes. It could build a you that never burned anything. It would believe it was you. It would be happier than you." She looked at him, and for half a heartbeat something surfaced through Wren's calm — something young and frightened and entirely her own. "And it would stand where you're standing and it would not know what it cost."

Behind them, far downstream, the gray skiffs held their station on the horizon like weather deciding.

Ahead, the river had begun, almost imperceptibly, to rise. Ondine saw it before any of them — the color of the water changing, tea-darkening, the current picking up a new muscle — and she rang the engine back and said, to no one and everyone:

"Flood's early. We've got one tide, maybe less. So somebody tell me right now what we're doing, because I carry people, not cargo — and I need to know if everybody on this boat is still a person."
