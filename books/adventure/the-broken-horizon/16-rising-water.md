## 16. Rising Water

Ondine Reyes had a rule about rivers: they never lied, but they never told you everything either. The water spoke in levels and currents, in the color of the foam and the smell of the mud, and if you listened long enough you could hear what it was holding back. What it was holding back right now was a flood.

She stood on the foredeck of the *Mariana's Grace* at first light with her coffee going cold in her hand and watched the river do something it shouldn't be doing yet. The seasonal rise was due in nine days — she'd counted on nine days, planned on nine days, promised her crew nine days — but the water had come up four inches overnight without a drop of rain falling locally. That meant rain somewhere far upstream, a lot of it, moving toward them with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry.

The tributaries were already running brown. She could see the seam of it from the deck: the clear green of the main channel braided through with dirty ribbons where the feeder creeks had turned. Up in the high country, the sky was doing what the sky did before it opened its throat.

"Captain." Mateo came up the ladder rubbing sleep out of his eyes, and she pointed at the seam without turning around. He looked, and she heard him stop breathing for a second. "That early?"

"That early."

"The wreck—"

"I know."

The Meridian Star lay grounded in a bend forty miles upriver, wedged into shallows that would be gone by tide five if the flood kept its new schedule. Forty miles of channel that would go from knee-deep trickles over gravel bars to a single brown throat wide as a lake. After the rise, nothing short of a submarine would reach that hull. Everything they'd come for — the device, the answers, whatever Sela March was becoming — would sit sealed under twenty feet of moving water until the optimists came back with better equipment and worse intentions.

Ondine drank the cold coffee anyway. It tasted like decisions.

* * *

She found Ivo in the wheelhouse, hunched over the chart table like a man doing arithmetic he didn't want the answer to. He'd been there since before dawn — she'd seen his light burning when she made her rounds at three — and the table around him was littered with pencil stubs and calculations crossed out so many times the paper had gone soft.

"Flood's early," she said.

He didn't look up. "I know. I've been running the numbers since midnight. If we leave now, we make the wreck by midday, load what we're taking, and get back below the narrows before—" He stopped. Picked up a pencil. Put it down. "Before it doesn't matter anymore."

"And what are we taking?"

That got him to look up. There was something raw in his face, the look of a man who'd been arguing with himself all night and losing. "The device," he said. "The pressure case comes out whole. Sela says the archive can be read portable. We take it downriver, hand it off, settle my account, and everyone walks away breathing."

Ondine leaned against the doorframe and let the silence stretch until it started to hurt him. She was good at that. Twenty years of carrying cargo and passengers up this river had taught her that most men confessed to empty rooms faster than to full ones.

"You're not going to say anything?" Ivo asked finally.

"What is it you want me to say? That handing that machine to Corrigan's people is a bad idea? You already know it is. You've known since the trading post, when Kettle put numbers in front of you and you couldn't look away from them fast enough." She held up a hand before he could speak. "No. Don't defend it. I'm not asking you to defend it. I'm asking you one question, and then I'm going to tell you how this boat works."

He waited. Outside, the current pushed a raft of broken reeds past the hull, moving faster than it should have been.

"Sela," Ondine said. "When you hand that machine over, what happens to her?"

"She's salvage. She's part of the contract. They want the survivor delivered along with the equipment — that was always the deal."

"So she gets sold."

"It's not—" He stopped himself. Ran both hands through his hair. "It's not selling. It's returning property. That's what the contract calls it."

"The contract also calls your creditors 'the optimists,' Ivo. Contracts are written by people who need lies to sound official." She pushed off the doorframe and came to stand across the chart table from him, close enough that he had to meet her eyes. "I've run this river for twenty years. I've carried contraband, fugitives, coffins, and once an entire circus troupe with two dead tigers in the hold. I don't ask what people bring aboard because it's none of my business what a man is fleeing or chasing. But I have one line, and I keep it the way I keep my channel markers — painted, lit, and non-negotiable."

"Which is?"

"I carry people, not cargo. Anything on this boat that breathes and can ask me for help is a person, and persons don't get ferried to their buyers. Not for debt relief. Not for partnership. Not because the alternative is drowning." She tapped the chart, right on the mark where the Meridian Star sat drawn in faded ink. "So understand me clearly, because I'm only saying this once. If you deliver Sela March to Vess Corrigan, you'll do it on someone else's boat. Mine goes back downriver empty of that transaction or it doesn't go at all."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Somewhere below decks, Sela was awake — Ondine could hear the faint rhythm of her pacing, the walk Wren had given her, measured and even, a captain inspecting a ship that no longer existed.

Ivo looked at the chart for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and rough. "You'd strand us. Up here. In a flood."

"I'd give you a choice, which is more than the optimists ever gave you." She straightened. "And I wouldn't strand anyone. I'd put every soul aboard ashore safe and burn this boat myself before I'd ferry a woman to the people who broke her. Ask Mateo. Ask any of my crew. They know the line. Time you knew it too."

She left him there with his arithmetic and went to check the bilge pumps, because floods didn't wait for moral clarity and neither did boats.

* * *

Midmorning brought the wind.

It came down out of the hills ahead of the rain, hot and wrong, smelling of crushed vegetation and distant lightning, and it set the river surface shivering in long cat's-paws that ran against the current. Ondine stood at the helm and read the wind the way other people read letters — every gust a sentence, every shift a warning. This was storm weather. This was the sky clearing its schedule.

Sela came up the companionway and stood beside her at the rail. She moved like Wren still — that economical, watchful gait — but lately there were interruptions in it, small stutters of someone else's body trying to remember its own habits. Yesterday she'd caught herself humming and gone white as chalk. This morning she'd called Ondine by a name that belonged to no one aboard, some ghost from the overwritten life, and apologized in two different accents inside the same breath.

"The water's rising fast," Sela said. Her voice was hers again today, mostly. Younger than Wren's. "Ivo told me about tide five."

"He tell you what happens after?"

"That the wreck becomes unreachable. That whatever's left has to be decided before then." Sela looked out at the brown seams spreading through the channel. "He didn't say what he's decided."

"No. He hasn't." Ondine adjusted the wheel a degree, feeling the current lean on the hull. "Can I tell you something about that man, since he won't tell it himself?"

"If you want."

"He's not a coward and he's not a villain. He's a man who's been paying interest on a fire for twenty years, and the optimists own the bank. Every deal he makes with them, part of him believes he deserves the terms. That's what they buy cheap — not men's loyalty, their shame." Ondine glanced sideways. "You'd know something about having your self handled by strangers. So I'll say to you what nobody said to him soon enough: what they took isn't the whole of you. Whatever's left, however small, it counts. And I count it."

Sela was quiet for a while. The wind pulled strands of her hair loose and she didn't fix them.

"There's a file," she said finally. "In the backup archive. Corrupted except for one field. A name." Her hands closed on the rail. "Mine. I haven't said it out loud yet. I'm afraid it'll sound like a stranger."

"It'll sound like whoever grows into it," Ondine said. "Names are seeds, not statues. Give it time."

Below them, the engine room hatch clanged open and Mateo shouted up that the intake strainers were clogging with silt — the river was carrying twice the sediment it should, more proof of the flood coming down from the headwaters. Ondine acknowledged it and started calculating fuel, ballast, freeboard. The *Grace* could ride dirty water if she had to; she'd been built by a drunk genius and maintained by a stubborn woman, and between the two of them she'd survived things the manufacturer would have disowned.

But the wreck was another matter. The Star sat in a bend that flooded ugly — Ondine had run that stretch in high water exactly once, thirty years ago, younger and stupider, and had spent six hours pinned against a submerged fence line praying the hull would hold. If they went up there now, they went knowing the window was closing with every hour, and coming back would mean riding the leading edge of the flood down through the narrows, where the channel constricted and the river forgot to be navigable at all.

She looked at Sela, who was watching the water with an expression that wasn't Wren's at all — something rawer, hungrier, a woman reading her own reflection in the current.

"When we get to the wreck," Ondine said, "what is it you're going to do?"

"Whatever needs doing." Sela turned. Under the grime and the borrowed posture, her eyes were steady. "Ivo thinks I'm salvage. Maybe the optimists think I'm salvage. But I was a technician on that expedition, and I remember enough of myself to know that I am the only person alive who understands that machine from the inside. Whatever happens to it — saved, scuttled, drowned — it happens through me. Not through a contract."

Ondine felt something in her chest loosen, the way it did when a channel she'd feared was silting up turned out to be deep after all. "Good," she said. "Then here's my offer, and it stands whether Ivo likes it or not. My boat carries you wherever you choose to go. Downriver, inland, back up into the hills — doesn't matter. You're a passenger with a destination, not a delivery. That's the line. Nobody crosses it on my deck."

Sela blinked, and for a moment the composure cracked clean through, and underneath it Ondine saw the thing that had been erased trying to believe it existed. "Why?" Sela asked. "You don't know me. You knew Captain Wren for six days and me for less. Why would you risk your boat, your crew—"

"Because twenty years ago somebody drew a line for me." Ondine faced the river again. "A woman named Ferro who ran supplies up this same channel, back when I was young and desperate and about to sign myself to a fleet owner who'd have worked me to bone meal. She looked at my contract, tore it up, and said 'people aren't freight.' I thought she was insane. I thought it for years. Then one day I didn't, and I bought this boat, and I've been drawing the same line ever since. Somebody drew it for me. I draw it for you. That's the whole economy of it."

The wind gusted hard enough to heel the boat a half-degree, and both of them braced against the rail. Far upstream, beyond the last visible bend, the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and beneath the wind Ondine could hear it now — faint, continuous, like the river clearing its throat a hundred miles long.

Rain on the headwaters. Coming down.

* * *

At noon she called a crew meeting on the aft deck, all seven of them including Ivo and Sela, and laid it out flat: the flood, the timeline, the window at the wreck, and the price of going after it. She didn't dress it up. Her crews never needed dressing-up; they needed facts and a reason.

"We can reach the Star by tomorrow dawn if we leave tonight," she said. "We work the wreck during tide five's last calm hours, and we come out ahead of the flood front through the narrows, which will be the worst water I've steered in a decade. Anyone who wants off does it now, at the trading post, with my blessing and a share of the pay. No shame in it. This stopped being a supply run two bends ago."

Mateo spoke first, as she knew he would. "We stay." Around him, heads nodded, one by one, the old litany. These were river people; they understood windows and floods and the arithmetic of chances.

Then Ivo stood. He looked like he hadn't slept, and like sleeping was the least of what he'd lost. "Before we commit," he said, and his voice carried the particular hoarseness of a man who'd been arguing all night and had finally picked a winner, "there's something I need to say. To all of you. But mostly to Sela."

Ondine leaned on the rail and waited, and the river rose beneath them, patient as a creditor, carrying everything downstream toward the sea — except, if she had anything to do with it, one woman who was not freight, and one man who was finally, at the edge of the flood, learning the difference.

"I'm not delivering the machine," Ivo said. "Or her. I've had it backwards from the start, and I'm done—" 

But the rest of it was interrupted, because that was when the lookout shouted from the bow: smoke, upstream, black and deliberate — signal fire, someone marking their position — and around the far bend, small and getting less small, came the angular silhouette of a boat flying no colors at all.

Kettle had found them. And behind him, riding down the rising water like it had been sent for, came the flood.
