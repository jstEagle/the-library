## 17. The Archive

The device woke like a thing that had been listening the whole time.

Sela felt it before she understood it — a pressure behind her eyes, a hum in the fillings of her teeth, the strange doubled sensation she had lived with since the hold: two lives breathing in one skull. Wren's memories lay over hers like silt over a drowned town, and beneath them, faint as footprints in wet clay, the shape of a girl who had signed onto an expedition with a duffel bag and a mother's lullaby and no idea what she was carrying home.

She sat cross-legged on the deck of Ondine's boat, the pressure case open on its stand between them, cables running from its spine to a salvaged terminal Ivo had wired together with the tenderness of a man defusing something. The cabin lamp swung on its hook. Rain needled the porthole. Somewhere above, Ondine moved through the rigging, checking lines against the rising water, and the river spoke under the hull in that low continuous voice it had kept up for days now — *I am coming, I am coming* — the flood arriving early, exactly as Ondine had said it would.

"Talk to me," Ivo said. He crouched across from her, forearms on his knees, watching her face the way he watched weather. "What do you see?"

"Directories." Her voice came out flat, technical, Wren's cadence bleeding through without permission. She hated that. She reached for her own voice and found it thin, uncertain, a reed bent by someone else's wind. "The archive is layered. Operational logs at the surface. Then personnel files. Then—" She stopped.

"Then what?"

"Then a directory called *Garden*."

Ivo went still. She had learned to read him in these weeks — the storm chaser's economy of motion, everything conserved, nothing wasted — and she knew that stillness was not calm. It was the stillness of a man seeing the supercell rotate.

"Open it," he said.

Her hands were shaking. Not Wren's hands — Wren's hands, she knew from the inside now, had been steady as a surgeon's, capable of threading a catheter or signing a death certificate with equal composure. These were her own hands, Sela March's hands, the ones that had trembled since the night in the hold when the machine had poured another man's life into her like hot lead into a mold. They shook because some part of her, buried deeper than the overwrite could reach, already knew what the Garden would contain.

She opened it.

* * *

Files bloomed across the terminal in columns. Names first — the whole crew list, every soul aboard the Meridian Star: Okonkwo, the engineer. Vashti, the medic. Little Ferro, nineteen years old, who'd been saving his wages for a boat of his own. And then, beside each name, a second designation, and Sela's breath caught in her throat because the second designations were not names at all.

They were *products*.

OKONKWO, J. — GARDEN UNIT 04 — TEMPLATE: COMPLIANT TECHNICIAN (VARIANT: CHEERFUL)
VASHTI, M. — GARDEN UNIT 05 — TEMPLATE: DEVOTED CARETAKER (VARIANT: MATERNAL)
FERRO, D. — GARDEN UNIT 06 — TEMPLATE: EAGER APPRENTICE (VARIANT: GRATEFUL)

And at the top of the list, dated earliest, flagged complete:

MARCH, S. — GARDEN UNIT 03 — TEMPLATE: LOYAL EXECUTOR (VARIANT: WREN-ADJACENT) — STATUS: DEPLOYED

"Deployed," Ivo read aloud, very quietly. "That's you."

"That's me." The word tasted like river mud. "He didn't just test it on me. He *finished*. I'm not an accident. I'm unit three."

She scrolled. Below the crew there were more names — dozens, then hundreds, a roster that unspooled past the edge of the screen and kept going. Names she didn't recognize. Designations she did. Every template a flavor of devotion. Every variant tuned, the metadata made clear, to a buyer's specification. There were purchase orders attached. There were *invoices*. And every invoice bore the same counterparty code, and when she traced the code through the expedition's encrypted correspondence, it resolved to a name she had heard Ivo say only once, in the dark, with a hatred so old it had gone smooth as sea glass:

The optimists.

"They weren't funding the research," she said. Her voice sounded far away, like it was coming up through the hull. "They were *ordering*. Wren wasn't building a memory device. He was building a factory. People who remember being loyal. People who can't be turned, can't be bought, can't run — because running would mean abandoning the person they believe themselves to be." She looked up at Ivo. "Do you understand? They don't want slaves. Slaves know they're enslaved. They want *volunteers*. A hundred thousand people who would die smiling for their owners and call it love."

The lamp swung. Rain filled the silence.

Ivo's face had gone the color of old rope. "How many names are in that directory?"

Sela scrolled to the bottom. The number sat there, patient as a headstone.

"Forty-one thousand," she said. "And a note from Wren. Dated three days before the Star went silent." She opened it. Read it. Found she couldn't speak it, and handed the terminal across instead.

Wren's handwriting translated to text was precise, unhurried, almost tender:

*Production is ahead of schedule. Unit 03's deployment exceeded all projections — subject retained full motor function, social integration, and task compliance within 48 hours. No detectable degradation at day nine. The self is not destroyed; it is archived, which is better, because archives can be sold twice. The buyers want coastal populations first — port cities, where people come and go and no one counts the missing. We will need the estuary staging grounds by tide season. Tell Vess the Garden is ready to be planted.*

"Tell Vess," Ivo repeated. He said it the way a man repeats the name of a disease.

* * *

Sela did not remember deciding to keep reading. But the hours passed, and the rain thickened, and somewhere in the middle of it Ondine came below, took one look at their faces, and put a pot of coffee on the little stove without asking questions — because that was Ondine, who carried people, not cargo, and who understood that some cargo needed carrying too.

It was Ondine who found the backup archive.

"It's nested under the maintenance partition," she said, tapping the screen with a blunt finger scarred from thirty years of line work. "Nobody hides anything in maintenance. Nobody ever looks." She glanced at Sela, and her eyes — dark, unhurried, the eyes of a woman who had piloted white water no one should survive — held something that was not pity. Pity would have been unbearable. It was recognition. "If your original self is anywhere, it's here."

Sela's heart did something complicated and painful. "You think there's a copy of me?"

"I think a man thorough enough to archive forty-one thousand souls wouldn't have left his best prototype unrestored." Ondine set the coffee down and straightened. "Search for the deployment record. Find out what he took."

So Sela searched. And there it was, buried in the maintenance partition like a body under floorboards:

UNIT 03 — PRE-DEPLOYMENT SNAPSHOT — MARCH, SELA — TIMESTAMP: 2140 HOURS, DAY OF INCIDENT — INTEGRITY: 61% — CORRUPTED SECTORS: 39% — RESTORABLE CONTENT: FRAGMENTARY

Sixty-one percent. She stared at the number until it blurred. More than half of her existed somewhere in this machine — her mother's kitchen, the smell of diesel and oranges, the lullaby, whatever she'd been at twenty-three years old when Aldous Wren had smiled at her across a lab table and told her the future was safe in their hands. But thirty-nine percent was gone, chewed to static, unrecoverable. She would never be whole. She would be a mosaic with pieces missing, a song with holes where verses used to be.

"Do it," Ivo said.

She looked at him. "What if what comes back is wrong? What if I restore myself and I'm — broken? Half a person?"

"You're asking the wrong man." His mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "I've spent twenty years being half a person on purpose. It doesn't get easier. It gets heavier." He leaned forward. "But I'll tell you what I know about storms. You don't get to choose whether the wall cloud drops. You only choose whether you're watching when it does."

Ondine snorted softly. "That's the most romantic thing I've ever heard a debt-ridden fool say."

Sela laughed — a startled, cracked sound, the first laugh she could remember that felt like it belonged to her and no one else. Then she put her hand on the key.

"Wait," she said. "If I restore this — if any of me comes back — will it push Wren out? Because right now Wren is the only reason we know how the Garden works. He's the map. If I burn the map—"

"Then we draw a new one," Ondine said flatly. "I've navigated by dead reckoning my whole life. You learn the river by being on it, not by owning the chart."

"But the buyers—"

"Are forty-one thousand reasons to stop protecting the man who sold them." Ondine's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was what made it land like a dropped anchor. "Girl. You have been wearing a dead man's life like a coat because it was warm and nobody gave you anything else. Take it off. Whatever's underneath — take it off and let us see."

Sela looked at Ivo. He nodded once, slow, the way he nodded at weather he'd decided to drive into rather than around.

She pressed the key.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the terrible part. She had braced for pain — for the tearing sensation of the overwrite in reverse, the machine ripping Wren out of her — but restoration was gentler than theft. It was more like remembering. Like walking into a house you'd forgotten you owned and finding the furniture exactly where you'd left it, dusted, waiting, patient beyond all reason.

Her mother's hands, flour to the wrist. The orange tree in the courtyard, split by lightning but still fruiting, stubborn as doctrine. The lullaby — *the river rises, the river knows, sleep my daughter where the water goes* — and suddenly she understood why the melody had surfaced in fragments for weeks, pushing up through Wren's memories like green shoots through asphalt. It hadn't been corruption. It had been *her*, insisting. Thirty-nine percent lost, and the deepest roots had held.

She came back to herself sitting on the deck of a boat in the rain, and she was crying, and the tears were falling down a face she was seeing, in some uncanny doubled way, from both inside and out — her own face, Sela March's face, wet and working and alive.

"Weeps with borrowed hands," she whispered, because she could feel it — the grief arriving through nerves that had spent weeks reporting to another man's brain, sorrow routed through circuitry that wasn't built for it, so that even her crying felt slightly foreign, slightly secondhand. "Look at that. Even my crying is salvage."

"No," Ivo said roughly. "Your crying is yours. First thing you've done in weeks that's entirely yours."

She wiped her face with her sleeve and felt the gesture land — truly land, owned, chosen — and something in her chest unlocked that she hadn't known was locked. Sixty-one percent. A mosaic with holes. But the holes were *hers* now, gaps shaped like her own missing life, and she would rather carry those empty spaces than carry one more inch of Aldous Wren.

Except—

The thought arrived cold, and she followed it down.

"Wren is still in here," she said. "Quieter. Pushed back. But he's in here, and he knows the Garden better than anyone alive." She met Ivo's eyes. "And there's something else. Something I saw in the deployment records while I was looking for my snapshot. Wren didn't just plan the coastal plantings. There's a final entry. A contingency."

Ivo went very still again. "What kind of contingency?"

Sela pulled up the file. Read it aloud, her restored voice steady now, her own steadiness, hard-won and real:

"*In event of recovery or hostile boarding: deploy seed protocol. Unit templates are portable. Any device, any recorder, any system capable of storage can host a Garden. Do not let the technology fall intact into optimistic hands — deliver it to the estuary staging ground and initiate broadcast.*"

She lowered the terminal.

"He built it to spread, Ivo. Not ship by ship. Not city by city. *Broadcast*. One signal, and every networked archive, every data vault, every memory repository on the coast becomes a planter. Forty-one thousand names is what he wrote down." Her hand was shaking again, but this time it was her own fear, clean and honest and hers. "What he *built* was unlimited."

Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the deck like fingers counting down. Above them, through the hatch, they heard Ondine shout something to the river — a curse, or a prayer, or a depth sounding — and the hull shuddered as the current strengthened, dragging them toward the drowning wreck of the Meridian Star and the last hours before tide five closed the window forever.

Ivo stood. When he spoke, his voice had the flat calm she'd learned meant a decision had already been made somewhere below language.

"Then we don't just sink the wreck," he said. "We poison the garden. Every template, every snapshot, every name in that directory — we drown it in noise so deep that nothing recoverable ever grows back." He was already reaching for the cable spool. "And we do it tonight. Before the optimists realize what their captain left lying in a flooded hold."

Sela wiped the last of the borrowed tears away, flexed her own hands, and picked up the terminal.

The river rose. The archive waited. And somewhere downstream, lights were moving against the current — too many lights, moving too fast, coming their way.
