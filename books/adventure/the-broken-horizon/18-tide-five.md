## 18. Tide Five

The river told us before the radio did.

Ondine felt it in her wheel first — a tremor through the hull at slack water, a shudder like something enormous turning over in its sleep downstream. She came aft to where I sat mending a gaff line that didn't need mending and said, "Engines. Three of them, maybe four. Big ones."

I'd heard nothing. But Ivo Pemberton had spent twenty years learning to trust other people's instruments over his own ears, and Ondine Reyes was the finest instrument on this water.

"How far?"

"Half a tide. Maybe less if they're not being polite about it." She looked at me with those flat river eyes that forgave nothing and expected less. "They're coming for you, Ivo. Not for the wreck. For you."

I tied off the line with hands that were steadier than I deserved. "Then we should talk about what happens when they get here."

"We should have talked about it two tides ago."

She was right, of course. She was always right, which was one of the many things about her I found unbearable.

Sela was below, in the galley, sitting very still with the pressure case open in front of her — not touching the device inside, just sitting near it, the way you sit near a grave or a fire. She'd been doing more of that lately. Speaking to it, almost. And lately, some of the time, she spoke as Sela March instead of Captain Aldous Wren, and the seams between the two of them showed like scar tissue: thin, pale, permanent.

I went down and told her the optimists were coming.

She didn't flinch. Wren wouldn't have; Sela might have, once, but there wasn't enough of once left to say. She just closed the case and latched it and said, "What are you going to tell them?"

"The truth," I said, and heard how strange the word sounded in my mouth, like a coin from a country I'd stopped visiting.

"They'll kill you for it."

"Probably not today."

* * *

They arrived an hour before high water, four boats in echelon formation, gunmetal gray, running lights dead until the last possible moment. The lead vessel had a name painted over with fresh black — someone had tried to burn it off and given up halfway — and standing at its rail, under a canvas awning that must have cost more than my boat, was Vess Corrigan.

She looked exactly as she had at the dockside bar, which was somehow worse. Weather about to turn. That smile with all the warmth of a barometer falling.

They came alongside without asking. Grapples bit into Ondine's rails and men in drysuits swarmed aboard with the practiced indifference of people who had done this to other boats many times. Ondine stood at her wheel with her arms crossed and her crew — both of them, the boy Teo and the old net-mender Anselm — pressed against the cabin wall. Nobody resisted. There was no version of resisting that ended anywhere good.

Vess stepped across the gap between decks like a woman stepping off a curb.

"Ivo," she said. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"That's the idea." She walked past me, trailing two enforcers — not Bram Kettle's people, these were hers, quieter, better dressed — and surveyed the deck, the wheelhouse, the river beyond, with the proprietary ease of someone who considered everything she saw already hers. "Where is she?"

"In the hold."

"And the device?"

"With her."

Vess turned and smiled at me fully then, and I understood that the smile was not for me. It was for whatever was behind me, waiting to be collected. "You've done well, Ivo. Better than I expected. Kettle said you'd fold by tide three. I said tide five. We're both right, in a way — you folded, you just did it quietly."

"I haven't folded."

"No?" She tilted her head. "Then let's talk terms."

* * *

We talked terms in Ondine's wheelhouse because Vess liked small rooms for negotiations — she'd told me that once, back at the dockside bar, over whiskey I hadn't paid for. Small rooms, she'd said, kept everyone honest. What she meant was that small rooms made it harder to run.

The two enforcers stayed outside. Ondine refused to leave her own wheelhouse, and Vess, after a moment's calculation I could almost hear ticking behind her eyes, allowed it. Maybe she wanted a witness. Maybe she wanted me to understand that even my allies were within reach.

Vess set a leather folio on the chart table. She didn't open it. Opening it would have been a concession that the terms needed explaining.

"Here's the shape of it," she said. "You deliver Sela March and the device intact — device first, her second, and I want her lucid, I want her talking, I want the Wren personality stable enough to brief our technical people. In exchange: your debt is zeroed. Not paid. Zeroed. Erased, like it never existed. And there's a position in it for you. Salvage operations lead. River division. You'd report to Kettle, but you'd outrank every other contractor we run."

I looked at the folio. "And if I don't deliver?"

Vess's smile didn't change, but something behind it did — a pressure drop, a front moving in. "Then the debt stays, and the debt collects. You know how it collects, Ivo. You've seen it collect on other people." She glanced out the wheelhouse window, at Ondine, at the boat, at the drowned villages sliding past in the brown water. "It collects on things you love, too. We know about the captain here. Her sister runs a fish camp up at Kiln Bend. Lovely spot. High ground, mostly."

There it was. Tide Four's threat, come due, wearing a suit now instead of a radio voice.

"You'd threaten a fish camp," Ondine said. Her voice was level. Her knuckles on the wheel were white. "Over a woman."

"I don't threaten anything," Vess said pleasantly. "I describe futures. People choose among them. That's why we're called the optimists — we believe everyone will make the sensible choice, eventually." She turned back to me. "So. Sensible choice, Ivo. You've been measuring your whole life in miles. This is the shortest one you'll ever be offered."

I thought about the fire.

That's the thing nobody tells you about a buried past: it doesn't stay buried, it just waits for rooms exactly like this one. Twenty years ago I had stood in a burning boathouse with a lantern in my hand and a choice, and I had chosen the thing that saved my skin and cost a man named Aldous Wren — no, that wasn't right, the name was different then, the name was mine — and I had driven away from the smoke and changed my name and started measuring life in miles so I'd never have to measure it in years again. Years asked who you were. Miles only asked how fast.

Vess was offering me miles. A whole road of them, paved, lit, going somewhere comfortable.

And Sela — Sela March, who had lost her entire self to a machine built by men like the ones Vess worked for, who was down in the hold right now trying to stitch a person back together out of corrupted files and a mother's half-remembered lullaby — Sela was the opposite of miles. She was years. She was the question I'd spent two decades outrunning.

"Yes," I said.

Vess raised an eyebrow.

"Yes," I said again, and let my shoulders do the thing shoulders do when a man gives up — the slight settling, the surrender of the spine. "You win. Deliver the girl and the case, wipe the debt, take the job. Fine. Fine." I rubbed my face with both hands, and when I lowered them I made sure my eyes were wet, which wasn't hard, because some part of me genuinely was grieving — grieving the man I was pretending to become. "When do you want them?"

"High water," Vess said. "Two hours. We'll bring her across on our launch. The device comes sealed, no tampering — my people will check the pressure case for interference. If she's damaged or the archive's compromised, the deal reverts to its other form." She picked up the folio and slid it across the table to me. Contract, signature page flagged. "Sign."

I signed. My hand barely shook. That was the worst part — how little it shook. Twenty years of practice at selling pieces of myself had made the transaction smooth as river stone.

Vess took the folio back, checked the signature, and smiled the real smile this time, the one underneath, the one that meant the weather had turned. "Knew you had it in you," she said, which was the cruelest thing anyone had ever said to me, because she meant it as praise.

She left the wheelhouse. I heard her boots on the deck, heard her give orders in that light carrying voice — launch prep, containment team, standby — and then the grapples released and the gray boats backed off to anchor in the channel mouth, close enough to watch, far enough to be safe from whatever they imagined might go wrong.

Ondine waited until they were anchored. Then she turned from the wheel and looked at me with an expression I will remember for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

"Tell me you have a plan," she said. "Tell me that was a plan."

"It was a plan."

"Then God help us, Ivo, because it looked exactly like surrender."

"Surrender is supposed to look like surrender," I said. "That's how you sell it."

* * *

Down in the hold, Sela listened to all of it — she'd heard most of it through the deck plates anyway; sound travels through a hull like guilt through a family — and when I finished telling her the shape of the lie, she was quiet for a long moment.

Then she laughed. It was a strange laugh, assembled from parts — Wren's dry command humor bolted to something younger and rawer that had to be Sela March surfacing through the seams — and it scared me more than Vess's guns had.

"You said yes," she said.

"I said yes."

"To Vess Corrigan. Who has never in her life accepted yes as a final answer, because people who accept yes stop being useful." She latched the pressure case and stood, and for a moment she stood the way Wren must have stood on his own bridge — weight forward, chin level, a woman wearing a dead man's authority like borrowed armor that fit better than it should. "Good. Then we have until high water, and they think we're beaten, and beaten people are the only kind Vess never watches closely."

"What's the play?"

"The wreck," she said. "The Meridian Star sits in six fathoms off the cut bank, hull breached at the cargo ramp, holds flooded but the frame sound. Your sonar charts show the stern section still watertight below the third bulkhead. The device goes back where it came from — deep, sealed, inside a hull that's about to be under ten more feet of river by tomorrow's tide. And the archive—" she touched the case, gently, the way you touch a wound that's yours, "—the archive gets poisoned first. Noise. Ten thousand false memories, contradictory, timestamped wrong, signed with names that don't exist. Even if they raise it someday, even if their machines can read it, what they read will be a hall of mirrors. No self in it worth stealing. No identity worth selling."

"And you? They'll want you. Vess said lucid, stable, talking."

Sela looked at me, and in her eyes — one eye Wren, one eye Sela, and the border between them shifting like the river's edge — something resolved.

"Wren planned overwrites for the whole crew," she said quietly. "Did I ever tell you that? A fleet of obedient selves, custom-built, for buyers Vess's people had already lined up. I found the order forms in the archive. Names, prices, delivery dates. I'm not cargo, Ivo. I'm inventory. And inventory that talks is worth more than inventory that doesn't — but only if it talks to spec." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "So I'll talk. I'll talk exactly to spec. Right up until the water closes over the case."

"You'd drown yourself with it."

"I'd drown *it* with myself. There's a difference, and the difference is the only thing I have left that's mine." She paused. "Besides. I've died before. Once already, in a lab, on a Tuesday, while a machine read my life and wrote someone else's over it. Drowning is slower but it's honest. Everything about that machine is lies. I choose the honest death."

"There's a sliver," I said. "Your name. The file you restored. Your mother's lullaby. If the case goes down—"

"Then I carry it up in my head, and I keep it, and I grow it back. That was always the plan, Ivo. The device was never going to give me back to me. Only I can do that. It just took me dying twice to figure out where the door was." She hefted the case — forty pounds of machined titanium and stolen souls — and settled it against her hip like a child, or a bomb. Both, maybe. "Get me to the skiff. Get me to the wreck. And Ivo — whatever happens up there on the surface, whatever Vess does when she figures it out — you don't come down after me. You keep Ondine's boat moving and you keep your promise to the river."

"What promise?"

"The one you made when you signed her contract instead of theirs. You carry people, not cargo. Start acting like it."

* * *

High water came at 4:40 by Ondine's brass clock, and with it came the optimists' launch, riding low and eager, a containment team in the bow and Vess Corrigan amidships with her hands folded and her patience visibly thinning.

By then we were ready.

The skiff had gone out an hour earlier, low against the water, running the shadow of the cut bank with its engine muffled by a blanket over the cowl — an old smuggler's trick Ondine taught me in another life, or what passed for one. Sela rode in the skiff with the case strapped between her knees and a dive bottle at her back, and I rode with her, because some promises you make out loud and some you make by being in the boat.

We went down through water the color of steeped tea, down past the cargo ramp's torn lip, down to the third bulkhead where the Star's stern still held its shape like a ribcage holding its last breath. Sela swam the case through the breach herself. I watched her work — quick, certain, Wren's hands knowing the ship's geometry from the inside — and when she'd wedged the case into the flooded compartment behind the bulkhead, she opened it one last time.

Her hands moved over the interface. From outside, through my mask, I couldn't see the screen. I saw only her face, lit faintly green, and the expression on it — grief and purpose fused into something that had no name in any language I knew.

Then she flooded the archive with noise. Ten thousand false memories, I'd later learn: weddings that never happened, confessions from men who never lived, coordinates to treasures buried in rivers that don't exist, a lullaby recorded ten thousand times in ten thousand wrong voices. A hall of mirrors, poisoned at the root.

She sealed the case. She welded the hatch dogs shut behind her — Wren's ship, Wren's seals, Wren's last order given by the ghost of himself wearing a dead technician's body.

And then Sela March kicked for the surface, and the Meridian Star began, at last and forever, to sink.

Above us, the river rose to meet the story. Above us, Vess Corrigan stood on her launch watching bubbles break the surface in a pattern that meant nothing good, and her head turned slowly toward our skiff, and her hand went to the rail, and her smile finally, finally fell away like weather clearing to reveal the storm underneath.

"IVO PEMBERTON," she called across the water, and her voice carried the way cold fronts carry, flat and total. "WHERE IS MY DEVICE?"

I looked at her across fifty feet of rising brown water, at the fleet behind her, at the guns, at the whole weight of the sensible future she'd tried to buy me with — and I measured the distance in miles, the way I'd measured everything for twenty years.

It was the shortest distance I'd ever have to cross.

"Ask the river," I said.
