## 19. Scuttle

Vess Corrigan's answer came as three shots across the water, none of them aimed to kill. Warning fire, the grammar of men who still believed I could be reasoned with.

I was already moving.

The Meridian Star lay heeled over against the drowned pilings of the old customs house, her starboard side just breaking the surface like the back of a sleeping animal. Behind me, Ondine's boat swung hard into the current, engines screaming, drawing the fleet's eyes south. Ahead of me, fifty feet of brown floodwater separated my skiff from the breach point we'd chosen at slack tide — a seam in the hull below the waterline where the plating had already begun to fail.

I'd measured that distance in miles. It came out to nothing at all.

The rain hit first, fat drops that stung like gravel, and then the wind arrived behind them, shoving the skiff sideways so I had to correct twice before I found the seam. The wreck loomed up out of the murk, rust bleeding from every rivet in long orange tears, and I killed the engine and let momentum carry me in. My shoulder struck the hull. The sound rang through the steel like a bell tolling underwater.

"Ondine," I said into the radio, "tell me you're still pretty."

"I'm gorgeous," her voice crackled back through static. "They're following. Kettle's boat is fastest — he'll be on you in four minutes."

Four minutes. I'd spent twenty years measuring life in miles, and now everything came down to minutes. There was a joke in there somewhere, but I didn't have time to laugh it.

I went over the side.

The water took me like a fist. Cold enough to stop thought, dark enough to stop time, the current dragging at my legs the moment I submerged. I'd rigged a cutting torch and a breather rig off the Star's own emergency stores two tides ago, back when this was still a plan instead of a prayer, and I found the seam by feel — my gloves tracing the rivet lines until they reached the soft spot where corrosion had done half our work for us.

The torch lit with a muffled *whump* that glowed green-gold through the floodwater. I set it against the plate and began to cut.

Sparks rose around me like inverted rain. Somewhere above, muffled by forty feet of river, I heard the fleet's engines change pitch — Kettle coming about, Vess shouting orders she expected the whole valley to obey. The plate groaned under the torch, a seam of molten metal opening like a wound, and I thought about what we were about to drown down here and whether drowning something could ever really be called mercy.

* * *

Sela March sat cross-legged in the flooded chart room with the device open in her lap, cables running from its spine to a salvaged battery bank, her fingers moving across the interface with Wren's precision and — increasingly, these last two days — with something that was only hers.

Ten thousand memories. That was the number she'd settled on. Ten thousand false lives, seeded through the archive like silt after a flood: invented childhoods, fabricated voyages, counterfeit griefs. A technician who never existed falling in love with a harbor town that wasn't on any map. A captain mourning a daughter who had never been born. Whole biographies, generated and corrupted and interleaved until any attempt to recover the device's true contents would yield nothing but noise — ten thousand plausible selves, and not one of them real.

She worked fast because the tide was working faster.

Her hands paused once, over a file she'd quarantined apart from all the rest: a sliver of melody, her mother's lullaby, recovered intact from the backup archive. Her real name lived next to it. *Sela.* Just Sela. Not Wren, not the ghost of some salvage broker's ambition — a woman who had once been afraid of thunderstorms and had learned, aboard this very ship, to love the sky anyway.

She copied the sliver to a chip no bigger than her thumbnail, sealed it in wax, and sewed it into the lining of her jacket. Then she opened the floodgates and let the noise pour through the archive like dye through water.

"It's done," she radioed. "Whatever they pull off this wreck, it'll be ten thousand lies wearing one truth's clothes."

* * *

The hull plate gave way with a shriek I felt in my teeth more than heard, and then the river was pouring into the Meridian Star's belly through a hole wide enough to drive a truck through — which, in a sense, is exactly what I'd just done.

I surfaced inside the flooding cargo hold, coughing, and found Sela waiting for me at the top of the companionway ladder, water already swirling around her ankles.

"You're late," she said.

"The traffic."

"Ondine says three minutes."

"Then we're right on schedule." I hauled myself up onto the deck plates beside her, dripping, and looked at the device sitting in its pressure case between us like a coffin built for a god. "Last chance to change your mind."

She shook her head. "It's not my mind anymore. That's rather the point."

Below us, the water climbed. Above us, boots on deck — Kettle's boarders, dropping from the fleet's lead skiff onto the Star's exposed superstructure, their shouts thin and furious through the rain. We had perhaps ninety seconds before they found the companionway.

"Sela." I caught her arm. "The archive. Once this case floods—"

"I know what I'm doing, Ivo." And she did; that was the terrible thing. She knew exactly what she was doing, because she alone among us understood what it meant to have your self overwritten, to be a stranger wearing your own face. She was choosing the noise over the name. She was choosing everyone else's future over her own past.

"Your mother's song," I said. "You kept it?"

Her hand went to her jacket, to the little wax-sealed weight over her heart. "Some things," she said, "you don't put in rivers."

* * *

Ondine Reyes drove her boat the way other people pray — total commitment, no negotiation, absolute faith in the outcome.

Kettle's skiff was faster. Kettle's skiff was always going to be faster. But Kettle was driving for the wreck, and Ondine was driving for Kettle, and there is a difference between chasing something and being chased by Ondine Reyes that Bram Kettle was learning at thirty knots through white water.

She came alongside him in the narrows below the customs house, close enough to read the fury on his face, close enough to see him understand — in one dawning, horrified instant — what she intended to do.

"You wouldn't," he shouted over the engines.

"I carry people," Ondine said, and turned her wheel hard over.

Her bow struck his port quarter amidships. Not enough to sink him — Ondine did not kill, not even enforcers, not even today — but enough to spin him broadside into the current, enough to buy ninety seconds, enough to send his boarding party scrambling for handholds while the river decided their heading for them.

Then she was through, running for the wreck, running for her people, running for the line she'd drawn in the water and would not cross.

* * *

We went over the side together as the boarders breached the companionway behind us, Sela first, me second, the device's pressure case sinking ahead of us both into the dark of the hold like a stone given permission to fall.

The water closed over the archive. Ten thousand false lives began to drown in earnest.

I got Sela to the skiff. Ondine pulled us clear as the first of Kettle's men reached the rail above us, screaming something I didn't stay to hear, and behind us the Meridian Star settled another foot into the river with a groan that sounded almost grateful.

Vess Corrigan stood on the lead skiff's deck, watching her fortune sink, and even at this distance I could see her doing the arithmetic — the divers she'd need, the weeks of recovery, the ten thousand corrupted files waiting at the bottom of every dive.

Ahead of us, the horizon was gone entirely, swallowed by storm and rising water.

Behind us, the river closed over everything we'd chosen to lose.

* * *

"We're not clear yet," Ondine said, hauling the wheel over. "Look astern."

I looked. Kettle's skiff, righted and running again, was coming about — not toward the wreck, but toward us. And beyond him, cutting across the flood from the north where no sane pilot would take a boat, came a second vessel flying no colors at all.

"That's not the fleet," Sela said quietly.

"No," I agreed, watching it come. "That's worse."

Because I recognized the silhouette. Everyone on this river did. It was the optimists' own survey vessel — the one they swore they'd decommissioned years ago — and it was making straight for the drowning wreck at full speed, as if whatever Vess Corrigan wanted salvaged from the bottom of the Meridian Star was worth risking the entire flood to reach.

Or as if someone aboard it already knew the device was empty.

And wanted to know why.
