## 20. The Broken Horizon

The water was coming for all of them, and only Sela could feel how glad it was.

She stood at the rail of the *Meridian Star*'s listing deck — or what remained of her deck above the flood line — and let the river's voice fill the spaces where her own name used to live. The wreck groaned beneath her feet like an old man turning over in bed. Below, somewhere in the drowned dark of the cargo hold, the device sat in its pressure case with ten thousand false memories flooding its archive, a poison Ivo and Ondine had spent the last tide brewing. Above, on the water, the optimists' boats idled in a crescent formation, running lights cutting the rain into slivers.

And Vess Corrigan stood six feet from Ivo Pemberton with a pistol held the way she held everything: loosely, as if the world would eventually hand her whatever she pointed at.

Sela had watched men die aboard this ship. She had watched herself die, in a sense — watched Aldous Wren's life pour over hers like concrete into a mold until there was no Sela-shaped space left to breathe in. She knew what it was to be overwritten. She knew what it was to be a copy.

Which meant she knew exactly what to tell the divers.

* * *

It had begun twenty minutes ago, when the optimists' launch came out of the rain with its engines muffled and its deck crowded with men in black rubber. Bram Kettle wasn't among them. Word of the flash storm had reached him, apparently, or word of the rising water, or perhaps he simply knew when a bargain had gone rotten. The man who stepped onto the wreck's tilted deck instead was narrow and gray-faced, with the patient hands of someone who'd spent his life inside machines — one of Vess's divers, a salvage chief named Ellery Voss, flanked by four others in dive harnesses, their rebreathers hanging at their chests like sleeping animals.

Vess Corrigan had come up the ladder last, dry under an umbrella one of her men held, smiling her weather-about-to-turn smile.

"Pemberton," she'd said. "You said yes on the radio. I do so admire a man who keeps his word."

"I said yes," Ivo agreed. His voice was steady. Sela, standing behind him in Wren's borrowed posture, could hear the hairline fracture underneath it. He meant the opposite. She had learned to read him the way Ondine read water — by what moved beneath the surface.

"Then you'll want to show me the device," Vess said, "and the woman, and then we can all be rich and gone before the river finishes its argument."

That was when Sela stepped forward.

She felt Wren rise in her like a tide of her own choosing — not the overwrite this time, but the discipline, the captain's cadence, the flat authority of a man who had signed orders that ended careers. She put it on the way another woman might put on armor. It fit badly. It fit perfectly. Both things were true.

"Captain Corrigan," she said, and watched Voss the diver go still. "You're standing on my ship."

Vess's smile flickered — recalibrated. "Captain Wren. They told me you were dead."

"They told you wrong." Sela let the silence stretch. Wren had been a master of silence; she had his silences filed away like tools. "And your divers are standing on a corpse that's about to be buried. So we should discuss what they're about to dive for."

* * *

Now, in the present, the rain thickened and the river rose, and Sela began to lie with a dead man's voice.

"You've reviewed the expedition records," she said to Voss. Not a question. Wren never asked questions of subordinates; he issued premises. "Then you know the device was never a single archive. It's a lattice. Every recording it makes is copied, and every copy is checked against the original self-map for drift."

Voss's eyes narrowed. He was a technician; technicians hated drift. "Drift?"

"Copies degrade," Sela said. "A memory recorded twice isn't the same memory. The self-map corrects for it — mostly. But the Star's unit took damage during the grounding. The correction layer is burned." She gestured, slow and captain-like, toward the flooded hold. "Whatever your people pull out of that case will contain copies of copies of copies. Identities folded through each other like wet paper. You won't get one clean self. You'll get a chorus."

"A chorus," Voss repeated.

"Voices," Sela said. "Wearing whoever wears them."

She saw it land. She saw it move through the four divers behind him like current through water — because these were men who worked with the device's outputs, who had seen what a clean copy could do, who had maybe even been paid in edited memories themselves. The optimists didn't hire believers. They hired the indebted, and the indebted know exactly what can be taken from a person.

One of the younger divers shifted his weight. Another looked at the water rising over the deck plates and did not look away fast enough.

"The buyer wants a working unit," Voss said carefully. "Not—"

"The buyer wants what he was promised." Sela turned to Vess. "What did you promise him, Captain? One identity, intact? Or did you promise him the whole archive — every self the Star ever recorded?" She let Wren's contempt surface, cold and precise. "Because the archive is poisoned now. I flooded it myself. Ten thousand fragments. False childhoods. False deaths. A hundred versions of a woman who never existed, threaded through everything. Your buyer will wear one of them and spend the rest of his life hearing the others."

"That's not possible," Vess said. "The noise protocol doesn't exist. Wren never—"

"Wren never told *you* what he told me." And here Sela did something dangerous: she smiled with Wren's mouth, and it was almost easy, because some part of her understood now that a stolen face used in defense of its victim was a kind of restitution. "I was his technician, Mr. Voss. Before I was anything else. Do you imagine he built a machine like that and left it unguarded?"

The rain came harder. Somewhere below them, the hull gave a long metallic sigh, and the deck tilted another degree, and every man on it adjusted his stance without thinking — bodies obeying the river before minds did.

Vess Corrigan hadn't moved. Her umbrella dripped a neat circle onto the deck between her boots. She was watching Sela with an expression Sela recognized from the playback files, from Wren's own memory of boardroom negotiations: the look of a person doing arithmetic and disliking the sum.

"You're not Wren," Vess said softly.

"No," Sela agreed. "I'm what's left of the woman he erased. Which makes me the only person on this river who knows exactly what your buyers bought." She spread her hands — Sela's hands, thin and salt-cracked, trembling slightly, and she let them tremble because trembling was true and truth was the one currency Vess couldn't counterfeit. "Every copy in that case is unstable. Pull it up, open it, and the first thing it does is offer to fix whoever's nearest. That's what it's *for*, Captain. It doesn't record selves. It replaces them. Ask me how I know."

The silence after that was the loudest thing on the river.

* * *

Ivo hadn't spoken through any of it. Sela could feel him beside her like a wall she was leaning against without touching — Ivo, who measured life in miles, who had confessed his fire to her in a cliff cave with the honesty of a man emptying a wound. Now he stood very still, hands visible, watching Vess's gun hand the way Ondine watched white water.

Vess raised the pistol — not at Sela. At Ivo.

"Here's where we stop performing," she said. "Pemberton signed a contract. Six tides, proof of salvage, delivery of asset and device. He's failed on every count, and failure has a price I don't set." The gun didn't waver. Her smile did — a small collapse at one corner, the first honest thing she'd shown. "But I'm generous. Hand me the woman and walk away, and I'll consider the debt settled in full."

"She's not cargo," Ondine Reyes said, from the rail. She had come up the ladder silent as silt, and now she stood with her arms crossed and the river rising around her boots. "I told Kettle. I'll tell you. I carry people."

"Then you can carry his body inland," Vess said, "or wait for the flood to do it. Either way—"

"He already gave you your answer," Sela said.

Everyone looked at her.

"On the radio. Tide five. He said yes." Sela stepped forward, deliberately, into the space between the gun and Ivo, and heard Ondine's breath catch. "You thought he was surrendering. He was counting. Ask yourself why a storm chaser — a man who calculates wind speed instead of his own age — would say yes to you and then spend two days flooding an archive you wanted intact." She let Wren's calm settle over her words like frost. "He said yes because yes kept you close enough to sink."

Below them, deep in the drowned hold, something vast and structural surrendered. The *Meridian Star* shuddered from keel to rail, and the river poured in through breaches no one could see, and the whole wreck began — slowly, majestically, irreversibly — to go down.

* * *

It happened the way disasters happen: all at once and then in pieces.

The stern lifted as the bow filled. Deck plates sang. Ondine was shouting for the boat, for everyone to get off, her voice cracking across the chaos like a whip. The divers broke first — Voss barking retreat orders in a voice stripped of all its patience, men scrambling down ladders with rebreathers clutched to their chests, and Sela shouted after them, using the last of Wren's authority like a thrown weapon:

"The case is sealed with a dead-man latch! If it floods unevenly, the archive dumps! Nobody dives on this wreck again unless they want to wear a stranger!"

Whether it was true hardly mattered. Fear doesn't check citations. She watched Voss hesitate at the rail — one glance back at the black water swallowing the hold, one glance at the young diver beside him whose hands were shaking — and then the glance became a decision, and the decision became distance.

Vess Corrigan stood alone on the tilting deck with her gun still leveled at Ivo Pemberton, and for three full seconds nobody breathed.

"Last chance," Vess said.

"You don't have a buyer anymore," Sela said. "You have a story you'd have to explain. *Why did the device fail, Captain? Why did your divers refuse the dive?*" She advanced one step, rain streaming down Wren's borrowed certainty. "The optimists don't forgive failed arithmetic. You taught me that. You taught all of us that."

The river decided it.

A wave came upriver against the current — flood water meeting flood water, the season arriving early and angry — and slammed the wreck's flank hard enough to stagger everyone on deck. Vess's boot skidded on wet plating. The gun dipped. And Ivo Pemberton, who had spent twenty years measuring distances and none of them learning to fight, did the only thing worth doing:

He grabbed Sela and jumped.

They hit the water together on the upstream side, away from the wreck's suction, and Ondine's boat was there — Ondine was always there — hauling them over the gunwale with hands like dock cable. Behind them the *Meridian Star* rolled, showed its barnacled belly to the grey sky one final time, and slid beneath the broken horizon of the flood with the device locked in its iron throat.

Vess's launch was already pulling away. Sela, coughing river water, watched the umbrella fold, watched the narrow figure at the stern turn once to look back — not at the sinking ship, Sela realized, but at her. At the woman wearing a dead captain's voice. Something passed across Vess Corrigan's face that might have been fear, or might have been the first honest envy Sela had ever seen anyone wear openly.

Then the rain closed like a door, and the optimists were gone ahead of the flood, and the river owned everything.

* * *

Ondine ran the boat hard for the high ground at Wren's Point, threading debris with the calm of a woman steering through her own kitchen. Ivo sat on the deck plates, soaked and shaking, laughing in the small broken way of a man who has just discovered he's still alive. Sela sat beside him and found she was shaking too — not with Wren's composure, which had drained away with the adrenaline, but with her own unnamed thing.

"You used him," Ivo said finally. "Wren. You wore him like—"

"Like armor." She stared at her hands. Borrowed hands, steady now, hers. "It's all I had. A dead man's manners and a liar's script."

"It was the best lie I ever heard," Ivo said. "And I'm a professional."

Ahead, through the rain, the lights of Wren's Point appeared — a trading post, a fuel depot, a roadhead where the river finally gave up its claim on the world. Ondine cut the throttle and let the current carry them the last hundred yards, and in that drifting quiet Sela felt the sliver of herself stir — the fragment she'd restored from the corrupted archive, small as a seed, warm as a coal.

Her mother's lullaby rose in her, unbidden. Half a melody. All hers.

"One more tide," Ondine said, watching the water climb the pilings. "After that, the river takes the valley, and every debt on it drowns."

Ivo reached into his jacket — into the waterproof sleeve where he'd kept it since the cave — and drew out the expedition's insurance ledger, dry as bone, heavy as a verdict.

"Then I'd better deliver the mail," he said.

Sela looked at the ledger, and at the roadhead beyond it, and at the horizon where the flood met the sky in a single unbroken line — and for the first time since the device had emptied her, she wondered what name she would choose for the woman standing on the far side of it.
