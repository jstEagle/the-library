## 21. Last Tide

The river took the *Meridian Star* the way a man takes back something that was always his — without ceremony, without malice, without so much as a ripple of apology.

Ivo watched it from the deck of the *Salt Lark*, his hands still raw from the breach work, his knuckles split where the pry bar had bitten through his gloves. Below the brown water, forty fathoms down and sinking deeper by the hour, the wreck settled into the silt with the device inside it, and inside the device, ten thousand false memories Sela had seeded like poison grain — a drowned library of lives no one had lived, waiting to corrupt any mind foolish enough to open it.

The water closed over the mast at dawn. By mid-morning even the current markers had shifted, the river rearranging its furniture, and by noon there was nothing left to see. No hull. No shadow on the sonar. Just a long brown scar of disturbed silt drifting seaward, already dissolving.

Ondine stood at the wheel and didn't look at it. She'd said her piece the night before — *the river keeps what the river keeps* — and now she kept her eyes on the channel, reading the water the way other people read scripture.

Sela stood beside Ivo at the rail. She was wearing a coat two sizes too big for her, one of Wren's from the locker, and she'd pushed the sleeves up past her elbows as if to remind herself the arms were hers. Her hands were shaking. They had been since the archive. He understood why. He'd watched a woman weep with borrowed hands, and there was no unseeing that.

"You're sure," he said. It wasn't a question, but it needed saying aloud, the way a man says a name at a graveside.

"The sliver's mine." Her voice was steady, but it was a steadiness she was building in real time, plank by plank. "It's not much. A name. A kitchen. My mother's voice singing something about a boat going out. But it's *load-bearing*, Ivo. Everything else can be built on it." She looked down at the water. "Wren's memories are still in my head. They'll fade. He said they would — wrote it in his own notes, before he stopped being careful about what he wrote. Memories that aren't fed, aren't rehearsed, aren't *wanted* — they thin out. Like paint in weather."

"And yours won't?"

"Mine will be fed." She turned and looked at him, and for the first time since the cargo hold, the look in her eyes belonged entirely to the person wearing it. "Every day. On purpose. That's the whole trick, isn't it? You don't lose yourself in one afternoon. You lose yourself by not deciding, ten thousand times, who you are."

Ivo thought about twenty years of not deciding. Twenty years of measuring his life in miles so he'd never have to measure it in anything else. He said nothing, because there was nothing in him equal to what she'd just said, and he was learning — slowly, at fifty-one, on a river that had tried to kill him twice in a week — when to keep his mouth shut.

* * *

The optimists came for him at the last tide's slack water, because of course they did. Vess Corrigan did nothing at random; she did everything at the moment of maximum leverage.

Her launch came upriver flying no colors, which was itself a kind of message — they were past pretending to be a shipping concern. Two boats, six men he could count, Bram Kettle conspicuous by his absence, and Vess standing at the bow with her hands folded, smiling like weather about to turn. She came aboard the *Salt Lark* without asking permission, because permission was a currency she'd never needed.

"Ivo Pemberton." She said his name the way she'd said it in the dockside bar, tasting it. "You've had an eventful week."

"Slow week," Ivo said. "You should see my busy ones."

Ondine had positioned herself by the wheel with her arms crossed, and the two crewmen she'd hired at the trading post — river people, loyal to her in the way river people are loyal to the one who pulls them out of floods — had drifted to either side of the deck without being asked. Vess noticed. Vess noticed everything. It was the noticing that made her dangerous.

"Where is it?" she asked.

"Gone."

The word landed on the deck between them. One of the men shifted. Vess didn't move at all.

"Gone," she repeated.

"The wreck's down past recovery, the device is breached and flooded, and the archive is salted with ten thousand fabricated memory-sets. Your divers could spend a year down there and come up with nothing but a head full of lives they never lived. Assuming they came up at all." Ivo kept his voice flat, conversational, the tone he used for wind speeds. "You trained me to read weather, Vess. This is weather. You don't sail into it."

Something moved behind her eyes — not anger. Recalculation. He watched her run the numbers the way he watched her run them in the bar, and he knew what she'd find: the salvage contract was void, the asset was unrecoverable, and the only thing of value left on this river was the man standing in front of her, who had just cost her a fortune.

"You understand," she said pleasantly, "that you owe us. The contract had performance clauses. Failure clauses. You signed them."

"I know what I signed."

"Then you know what happens to men who default."

"I brought you something instead." He reached into the waterproof bag at his feet — the one he'd pulled from Wren's hidden compartment two nights ago, the one he'd been carrying like a stone in his chest, deciding. "You wanted the *Meridian Star*'s cargo. Here's what was actually in her hold."

He handed her the ledger.

It was a plain thing — oilcloth-bound, water-stained at the corners, the kind of book a ship's purser keeps. But Vess opened it, and Ivo watched her face do something it almost never did: it stopped performing.

The ledger was the expedition's insurance record. Every policy Wren had taken out, every premium paid — and every payment received. Because the optimists hadn't just funded the *Meridian Star*'s salvage expedition. They'd insured it. Heavily. Against loss, against delay, against total casualty. And the payouts, dated and initialed in Vess's own hand, had been flowing since before the ship ever went silent. The *Meridian Star* hadn't been lost. She'd been *sunk* — the first time — on paper, for the insurance, and then quietly refloated and sent upriver to do the real work, with a crew who didn't know their own deaths had already been cashed.

There were names in it. Crew names. Sela March's name, with a payout beside it. Aldous Wren's name, marked *deceased — claim settled*, dated three weeks before the ship went silent on the radio.

Vess closed the ledger slowly. The smile was gone now, and what was underneath was worse — a cold, running arithmetic.

"Where did you get this?"

"Wren's compartment. The man kept meticulous records. He was planning to use it, eventually, I think. Leverage of his own." Ivo shrugged. "He ran out of eventually."

"You could have sold this," Vess said. Her voice had gone quiet, which from her was the sound of a storm still deciding. "To us. To the insurers. To a magistrate. This is worth more than the device."

"I know."

"So why—"

"Because you'll read it," Ivo said. "That's the thing about you, Vess. You can't not read a document. You'll read it tonight, and tomorrow, and you'll spend every hour after that figuring out which of your partners already has a copy, and which of them is planning to hand it to the insurers' auditors first, and you'll never trust any of them again. I don't have to ruin you. I just have to put this in your hands and let you do what you do best." He paused. "Worry."

The silence on the deck was long enough that the river filled it — water against hull, the creak of the wheel, somewhere downstream a bird crying its one note.

"You're a fool, Pemberton," Vess said at last. "You could have been rich."

"I was rich once," Ivo said. "It was the name I was using when the fire happened. I'm trying the other thing now."

Something flickered across her face — not recognition, not quite, but the sense of a door she'd never noticed in a wall she'd walked past for years. Then she folded the ledger into her coat, turned to her men, and said, "We're done here," in a voice that made it clear *done* was a word with a body count attached.

At the rail she stopped, half-turned. "The debt, Pemberton. All of it. Consider it—" she almost smiled, and it was the ugliest thing he'd seen all week, "—settled. In full. You understand why."

"Because hunting me costs more than I owe."

"Because men who hand you a knife instead of a bill are either saints or something worse," Vess said, "and I've decided I don't want to find out which." She stepped down to her launch. "Stay off the water, Ivo. Stay off the water, and stay small, and maybe you'll die of old age after all."

The launches pulled away downstream. Ivo stood at the rail until they were gone, and then his legs informed him, without consultation, that they were finished holding him up, and he sat down hard on a coil of rope and discovered that his hands were shaking worse than Sela's.

Ondine put a tin cup of something hot in them without a word. He drank it. It tasted like tar and kindness.

* * *

They ran downriver through the afternoon, the *Salt Lark* riding the last tide's ebb, and the water that had swallowed the wreck carried them toward the sea it had swallowed everything else into. Ivo slept four hours in the wheelhouse chair and woke to find Sela sitting across from him, going through a small canvas bag with the concentration of a surgeon.

"What's all that?"

"Everything I own," she said. "Which is one bag, apparently. I checked the manifest against what I can remember. It matches." She held up a battered tin. "My mother's. It was in my footlocker the whole time, under Wren's orders — he had the crew's personal effects cataloged. There's a photograph in it. I don't remember the people in it." She said it without self-pity, which was the remarkable thing. "But I remember that it matters. The sliver comes with hooks. Little ones. I keep finding them."

"Does it hurt? Finding them."

Sela considered the question with Wren's patience and then, visibly, deliberately, set the patience aside and answered with whatever was underneath.

"Like walking on a leg that was asleep for a year. It hurts because it's *mine* and it's coming back and the two things feel the same." She closed the tin. "Ondine says there's a town two days down, a river town, with a records office. Shipping registries, census rolls, crew manifests going back fifty years. I'm going to start there. Sela March, technician, listed on the *Meridian Star*'s articles. There'll be a home port. There'll be a next of kin, maybe. Maybe a mother who sang a lullaby about a boat going out." She looked up. "And then I'm going to spend the rest of my life feeding the sliver. Every day. On purpose."

Ivo nodded. He found that his throat had closed up around something, and it took him a moment to get past it.

"I owe you an apology," he managed. "In that cave. I told you about the fire, and you asked me who I'd be if I forgot. And I've been thinking — the answer's nobody. That's the answer. I'd be nobody. That's what forgetting buys." He turned the tin cup in his hands. "I spent twenty years thinking the guilt was the worst thing I carried. It's not. The worst thing is that I built my whole life so I'd never have to look at it. The miles weren't an escape, Sela. They were an anesthetic. And anesthetic doesn't heal anything. It just lets you keep walking on a broken leg."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to let it hurt." He said it simply, because it was simple, and because it had cost him everything he had to arrive at something so small. "There's a fire brigade pension fund in Corvess that's been paying out to a dead man's family for twenty years. A man named Tomas Ellery. Except Tomas Ellery didn't die in the fire. He set it. He walked out the back, changed his name, and went to sea." He looked at her. "I'm going to write them a letter. A confession. My real name on it. And whatever they do with it — courts, restitution, nothing at all — that's theirs. I'm done deciding who I am by deciding what to hide."

Sela reached across and put her hand over his — her hand, not Wren's; he could see the difference now, the way she held it, the way it shook and didn't stop shaking and stayed anyway.

"Ten thousand times," she said. "That's how it's done."

* * *

The roadhead was called Gray's Landing, and it was barely that — a gravel shelf where the river road met the coast road, a fuel depot, a church with a leaning steeple, and a bus that came through twice a week if the water hadn't taken the bridge. Ondine brought the *Salt Lark* alongside the pilings at golden hour, the light coming long and amber across the flats, and for a while nobody moved to tie off, because tying off meant arriving, and arriving meant the voyage was over.

Ondine lit a cigarette and leaned on the rail. "You could stay on," she said. "River's short of hands who can read water. And I'm short of crew I don't have to watch."

Ivo looked at the river — the brown, patient, keeping river — and felt the old pull, the miles, the horizon, the arithmetic of distance that had organized his whole life. It was still there. It would always be there. That was the thing about a self: you didn't get to swap it out. You just got to decide, ten thousand times, what to feed it.

"Can't," he said. "I've got a letter to write."

Ondine nodded slowly, smoke drifting off her cigarette, and Ivo understood that she'd known before he had — had read his forged papers at a glance and his whole life at a second one, and had ferried him anyway, because that was what she did. She carried people.

"Sela," she said. "You're still welcome aboard. Any tide. Any time. You know that."

"Two days down," Sela said. "The records office. You're taking me."

"I'm taking you." Ondine flicked the cigarette into the water. "Then we'll see what name you grow into."

They tied off. Ivo shouldered his bag — one bag, everything he owned, and for the first time in twenty years it felt like enough — and stood on the gravel shelf of Gray's Landing with the river at his back and a road running inland in front of him, gray and straight and going nowhere he'd ever been.

Sela stood beside him. The bus wasn't due until morning. They had a church with a leaning steeple and a night to get through, and a letter to write, and a name to say out loud, maybe, for the first time in twenty years, just to hear how it sounded.

"Tomas," Sela said.

He looked at her.

"Try it," she said. "See how it fits."

Ivo Pemberton stood on the last dry ground of his old life and said the name into the amber light, quietly, like a man testing ice — and somewhere behind him, without hurry, without malice, the last tide went out, and did not come back for him.
