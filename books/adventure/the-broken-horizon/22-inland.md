## 22. Inland

The river let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, finger by finger, until there was nothing left to grip.

Ivo stood at the rail of Ondine's boat and watched the last of the Meridian Star's shadow slide beneath the brown water. There was nothing to see now. That was the point. Somewhere down in the drowned dark, past the silt and the cold and the patience of the current, the device sat in its breached pressure case with ten thousand false lives bleeding through its archive like ink through wet paper. In a year it would be unreadable. In five, no one would believe it had ever worked at all.

He found he was counting tides out of habit and made himself stop.

"You're doing the thing," Sela said beside him.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you measure everything." She said it without looking at him, her eyes on the water too. Her voice had settled into something that was neither Wren's clipped command nor the hollow calm they'd pulled from the hold. It was newer than both. Younger. A voice still deciding what it wanted to sound like. "Miles. Tides. Wind speed. You do arithmetic instead of feeling things."

"Arithmetic is honest."

"Arithmetic is hiding." She turned then, and half her face was lit by the low morning sun, and for a moment he saw the sliver of her — the real one, the technician who had once been a girl singing a lullaby her mother taught her — looking out through whatever remained. "Ondine says we're two hours from the roadhead."

Two hours. He caught himself converting it to distance before he could stop, some old reflex dividing by wind speed or fuel burn, and Sela watched him do it and almost smiled.

"See," she said. "The thing."

* * *

The roadhead wasn't much. It never was, this far upriver where the water got shallow and the maps got honest about how little anyone cared. A gravel shelf cut back into the clay bank, a corrugated shed with a rusted sign advertising fuel and ice, and beyond it a road — actual asphalt, cracked and weed-ridden but continuous, running west into hills the color of old rope. Ivo hadn't stood on a road in twenty years. Roads went places. Rivers only went down.

Ondine killed the engine and the silence that followed had weight to it, the particular silence of a boat that has carried people through something and knows it.

"This is where you get off," she said. Not a question. She'd declared her line weeks ago — *I carry people, not cargo* — and she'd kept it, and now the keeping was done.

Ivo looked at the road. Then at her. "You could come with us."

Ondine laughed, short and real, the way she did when something amused her enough to be worth the breath. "And drive? On a road? Where there's no current to argue with?" She shook her head. "I've spent thirty years learning what the river wants. Out there—" she nodded west, at the hills, at all that dry indifferent distance, "—nobody wants anything. You just go. Sounds exhausting."

"It is," Ivo said. "That's mostly what it is."

"I know. My brother tried it. Lasted eight months, came back talking about parking lots like they were miracles." She leaned against the wheel housing and studied him with those flat river-colored eyes that had read his forged papers at a glance and said nothing, because she recognized upstream-shaped men when she ferried them. "You're not coming back, though. Are you."

It wasn't a question either. The river didn't ask questions; it made observations and let you live with them.

"No," Ivo said. "I don't think I am."

She nodded slowly, and something passed between them that neither of them would have called goodbye, because on the river goodbyes were bad luck and these two had used up more luck than most people were allotted in a lifetime.

Then Ondine looked past him, at Sela, and her expression did something complicated.

"And you. Half a person with a stolen captain rattling around in her head, driving inland toward a name she hasn't picked yet." She said it plainly, without cruelty, the way she'd say *storm coming* or *shoal ahead*. "You know what I'd tell you?"

"Tell me anyway."

"Don't pick fast. A name's like an anchor. Drop it too soon and you're stuck wherever it lands." She pushed off the rail and started gathering their bags — two duffels, one case of salvaged instruments Ivo couldn't bring himself to sell, and a single data slate wrapped in oilcloth. The backup archive. Corrupted almost beyond reading, except for one intact file, one true name, one lullaby that belonged to no captain anywhere.

Sela took the slate and held it against her chest the way you'd hold a child, or a wound.

"Thank you," she said. "For carrying me. Even when I was cargo."

Ondine stopped halfway to the wheelhouse. For a long moment she didn't turn around, and Ivo saw her shoulders move once, a breath taken and released with effort. When she spoke again her voice was steady, but only barely.

"On my boat," she said, "you were always people."

* * *

The car was Ondine's brother's, left at the roadhead years ago under a tarp that had fused itself to the paint. It started on the third try, coughed, and settled into a rattle that sounded like a man clearing his throat before a long speech. Ivo sat behind the wheel with his hands at ten and two and realized he couldn't remember the last time he'd driven. Twenty years of rivers and runways and the long gray margins between storms, and somewhere in all of it he'd forgotten what it felt like to steer something that only went where you pointed it.

Sela climbed into the passenger seat, arranged the slate on her knees, and looked out the windshield at the road unspooling west.

"How far?" she asked.

Ivo opened his mouth to calculate — fuel range, terrain, average speed — and closed it again.

"I don't know," he said, and the words came out strange and light, like something he'd been carrying in his teeth for twenty years finally set down on a table. "We'll find out."

They drove.

The hills took them in stages, first the low clay ridges with their scrub grass and standing water, then higher ground where the air changed — drier, thinner, smelling of dust and something green and stubborn growing where it shouldn't. Ivo drove the way he used to chase storms: reading the land, adjusting, hands loose on the wheel. But there was no storm ahead. There was nothing ahead but road, and after a while he understood that this was the whole difference, the entire secret the inland people knew and he'd forgotten: driving without a horizon to beat was not emptiness. It was room.

Sela slept for the first hour. When she woke, the light had gone gold and long, and she sat up blinking like someone surfacing from deep water.

"I dreamed," she said, wonder in it. "Not Wren. Not the archive. Mine." She pressed her palm flat against the window glass. "A kitchen. Yellow tiles. Someone humming." Her voice caught. "I think it was the song."

"The lullaby?"

"My mother's version of it. She got the words wrong on purpose, I think, because I always corrected her and she always laughed." Sela was quiet a moment. "Wren never had a mother. He told everyone he'd been raised by the sea. So this isn't his memory leaking through. This is mine. Coming back on its own."

Ivo glanced at her. "Like floodwater finding old channels."

"Something like that." She looked over at him, and in the gold light her face was young and unfinished and entirely her own. "What about you? What comes back first, when a man drives away from the water?"

Ivo thought about it honestly, which was new for him. Honesty had cost him everything this season — his debt paid in ledgers instead of treasure, Vess Corrigan's empire quietly rotting from the inside out because he'd handed the optimists' own insurance fraud to the people they'd insured against. Truth as currency. It shouldn't have surprised him that it spent better than salvage ever had.

"A name," he said. "There was a name before Pemberton. Before the fire. I buried it so deep I stopped hearing it even in my head." His hands flexed on the wheel. "I keep waiting for it to surface. Like your channels."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then maybe I grow a new one." He heard himself say it and was startled by how little it frightened him. "You're doing it. Why can't I?"

Sela considered this with Wren's patience — the one piece of the captain worth keeping, she'd decided, the habit of listening before answering — and then she smiled, and there was nothing borrowed in it at all.

"We could pick each other's," she said. "Names. Like witnesses. People who saw what happened."

"People who saw what happened," Ivo repeated. The road hummed under the tires. Somewhere behind them, miles back, the river bent away toward the coast and all its drowned machinery, and neither of them looked.

"That's the first sensible offer anyone's made me in twenty years," he said.

* * *

They stopped at dusk at a filling station that was also a diner that was also, according to a hand-painted sign, a motel, though the motel part appeared to be three trailers behind the building. The sky inland did something Ivo had forgotten skies could do: it simply ended, clean and enormous, no haze, no salt, no supercell stacking up purple on the western edge. Just stars arriving one by one, punctual as tide tables and twice as indifferent.

He ate eggs he didn't need and drank coffee that tasted like the pot it came from, and across the table Sela spread the oilcloth-wrapped slate and woke it, and together they read the one intact file again. Her real name. Fourteen letters, a birthdate, a mother's surname. Data, nothing more. But data was what Ivo understood, and he understood this: a file was a seed. Everything else — the woman, the voice, the wrong-words version of the lullaby — would grow from it or it wouldn't, and no one could hurry it, and no machine could overwrite it now that it lived outside the archive, in the world, in the mouths of two people who'd seen what happened.

"Say it," Sela said. "Out loud. Once. So it's in the air instead of the machine."

Ivo said it. Carefully, the way you'd carry something breakable across a flooded deck.

Sela closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was crying, quietly, with her own hands.

"There she is," Ivo said softly.

"Not yet," Sela said, wiping her face with the heel of her palm, laughing a little. "But she's on her way. And she's driving inland, apparently, with a storm chaser who pays his debts in evidence." She looked up at him. "What do we do tomorrow?"

Ivo looked out the window at the road going west into the dark, at the broken horizon where the hills gave up and the sky began, and felt the old arithmetic rise in him — miles, hours, fuel, weather — and let it pass through and out like weather moving over open country.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we measure the day in hours. All of them. Every single one."

Sela raised her coffee cup. "To hours, then."

"To hours."

Outside, the stars kept arriving, and the road waited, and for the first time in twenty years Ivo Pemberton — or whatever name was surfacing beneath that one, patient as floodwater finding its old channels — went to sleep without counting anything at all.

In the morning they would drive until the light ran out, and then stop, and then start again. It was not a plan. It was better than a plan. It was a direction, and a witness, and a name coming home slow.

The horizon stayed broken all the way west, and neither of them tried to fix it.
