## 1. What the River Keeps

The river gave Paloma Oyelaran three things in her life, and she had learned not to expect a fourth: silt, salvage, and the occasional corpse she pretended was driftwood until she'd reported it and could stop pretending.

Today it was giving her silt.

She hung forty feet down in the green murk off the drowned hulk they called the Widow of Vessarine Point, her gloved hands working a rusted cargo hatch while the current pushed at her like a bored customs officer. Above her, the *Second Chance* sat at anchor, her hull patched in six places and her engine held together mostly by Sefa's opinions. Below her, the wreck spread out across the riverbed like a lady who'd fainted and never been picked up.

The Widow was a good wreck. Honest, in the way wrecks were honest. She'd gone down thirty years back with a load of riverstone and copper ingots, and the navy's charts said so, and the navy's charts were right, and Paloma had pulled enough copper out of her over the years to keep Doss in rum and herself in rope. There was comfort in a wreck that was what it said it was. On the Vessarine coast, comfort was not a thing to be sneered at.

There were rules to salvage, and Paloma kept them the way other people kept religion — not because she believed in every word, but because the alternative was drowning. Rule one: know your wreck. Rule two: know your depth. Rule three, and this was the one that mattered: *the navy forbids discussion of certain wrecks, and a diver who discusses them stops being a diver and starts being a headline.* There were four such wrecks on the stretch of river she worked. She'd never dived them. She'd never asked why. Asking why, on the Vessarine, was like asking the tide to explain itself. You got wet, and nothing changed.

The Widow was not one of the four. The Widow was safe.

Which is why it unsettled her, forty feet down with her lamp beam swimming in the silt, when her hand came out of the cargo hold holding something that was not copper.

It was a chest. Small — small enough to lift with one hand, which no honest cargo chest ever was. She turned it in the light. It was lead-lined, she could tell by the weight, and the lead had kept the river off the wood beneath, so that it came up out of the silt looking less like it had spent decades drowned and more like it had been set down for a moment by someone who meant to come back.

And on the lid, stamped into a plate of tarnished brass, was a seal she knew the way you know a word you've never been allowed to say aloud.

A tower, broken at the middle, rising out of water.

Kethria.

Paloma had been seven years old the last time she'd heard that word spoken in her mother's kitchen, and her mother had said it the way you say the name of a dead relative at a funeral — quietly, and once, and then you change the subject. Kethria had been a nation once. Then it hadn't been. The maps in the schoolroom at Corvel's Landing had a blank space on the north bank of the river, forty miles of it, and the schoolmaster had called it *unincorporated territory* in the flat voice of a man reciting something he'd been told to recite, and nobody had ever asked him what had been incorporated out of it.

Forty years. Nobody talked about it. The navy patrolled the north bank more than the south, which was odd, because there was nothing on the north bank to patrol — no towns, no traffic, nothing the charts admitted to. Once, when Paloma was sixteen and new to diving, she'd asked old Marette why the patrol boats bothered. Marette had looked at her for a long moment and said, "Girl, the river keeps what the river keeps. You want to stay a diver, dive the wrecks that are on the charts." Paloma had stayed a diver. She had dived the wrecks that were on the charts. She had built an entire life on the principle that some questions cost more than the answers were worth.

And now she hung in the green water with Kethria's broken tower in her hand, and her heart was doing something complicated.

She should put it back. That was the thought that arrived first, clean and sensible, like a harbor pilot. Put it back in the silt, finish the copper run, surface, drink the tea Sefa would have waiting, and never think about it again. That was the life she'd built. It was a small life but it was *hers*, and it floated, and things that floated were not to be argued with.

Her hands did not put it back.

She'd like to say, later, that it was courage. It wasn't. It was simpler and more shameful than that: the chest was *beautiful*, and she was tired, and some small stubborn animal in her chest — the same animal that had made her a diver instead of a net-mender, the one that always wanted to know what was on the other side of the next bend — that animal had its teeth in the thing and would not let go.

She clipped it to her harness and went up.

* * *

The light on the surface was the flat white of late morning, and Doss was waiting on the deck of the *Second Chance* with the expression of a man who has already decided what the morning's find is worth.

"Let me see, let me see." He took the chest before her boots were fully on deck, turning it over, weighing it, running his thumb across the brass plate. Doss was a big man gone soft in the middle, with a beard he trimmed to look trustworthy and hands that were quicker than his face. "Lead-lined. That's money right there, the lead alone. And the plate —" He stopped. His thumb was still on the seal. "That's the —"

"Yes," Paloma said.

"That's the *dead country's* mark."

"Yes."

Doss looked at the chest, then at the water, then at Paloma, and Paloma watched him do the arithmetic she'd done forty feet down, except Doss's arithmetic always came out to money. "Where."

"Forward hold. Under the copper dunnage."

"The Widow?"

"The Widow."

Doss's eyebrows went up and stayed there. "The Widow's a stone hauler. Thirty years on the charts. I've pulled ingots off her myself." He turned the chest again, and his voice dropped into the register he used for haggling, which was also, unfortunately, the register he used for honesty. "Pal. A sealed lead-lined chest stamped with a country that doesn't exist, hidden in the hold of a wreck that's been on the open charts for thirty years. That's not salvage. That's *somebody's secret*, and somebody went to real trouble to keep it, and somebody else went to real trouble to lose it. Both of those somebodies pay."

"Or drown us," Paloma said.

"Or drown us," Doss agreed cheerfully. "But pay first."

She took the chest back from him. It was heavier than it looked, the way secrets are. "We don't know what's in it."

"That's the *point*, Pal. That's the whole entire point. A locked box is worth what's in it. An *unopened* locked box is worth whatever the buyer imagines is in it, and buyers imagine *beautifully*." He rubbed his hands together. "We take it to Corvel's Landing, we don't say where it came from, we let three collectors bid —"

"It's not ours to sell."

"It's in our river."

"It's not *in* it anymore. I have it." She set the chest down on the deck between them, on the boards Sefa had re-caulked last month, and found that she was standing over it slightly, the way you stand over something you mean to protect. She noticed herself doing it and made herself stop, and then did it again anyway.

Doss studied her. Under the beard and the greed and the twenty years of shared decks and shared storms, he was not a stupid man, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "You want to open it."

"I want to know what it is."

"Same thing."

"It is *not* the same thing, Doss. Selling is a decision about money. Opening is a decision about —" She gestured, and failed to find the word, and gestured again. "About everything else."

"Everything else doesn't pay the yard fees."

"No," she agreed. "It never does."

Sefa appeared at the engine hatch then, wiping her hands on a rag that had once been white, a small wiry woman with grease in the lines of her knuckles and the permanent squint of someone who listens to machines for a living. She looked at the chest. She looked at Paloma. She looked at Doss, who was wearing the face of a man who has definitely not already been arguing.

"That's Kethrian," Sefa said.

"You know the seal?" Paloma asked.

"Everybody my mother's age knows the seal." Sefa crouched, not touching it, which Paloma noticed and appreciated. "My mother used to sing songs with Kethrian words in them. She'd stop when my father came in the room." She squinted up. "Where was it?"

"The Widow."

Sefa's squint deepened. "The Widow's clean."

"That's what I said," Doss said.

"That's what worries me," Sefa said, and went back to her engine, and Paloma stood with the chest at her feet and the river slapping the hull and thought: *everybody knows more than I do, and everybody has known it longer, and they have all decided to know it quietly.*

Runi came up from the bow then — sixteen, all elbows, with her hair tied back with a piece of copper wire and the river running in her blood on her mother's side — and looked at the chest with the frank, unguarded interest that only teenagers and cats can manage.

"What's that?"

"Nothing," said Doss.

"A Kethrian seal," said Sefa, from the engine hatch.

"It's nothing," Doss repeated, louder.

Runi crouched next to Paloma. "My gran says there's towns up the north bank. Under the water. Whole towns, and the navy charts them as shoals." She said it the way you say a thing you've been told a hundred times and never believed until this exact moment. "Is that what this is?"

"It's a chest, Runi."

"It's a *Kethrian* chest." Runi looked up, and her eyes were bright with the particular hunger of the young, which is the hunger for things to *mean* something. "Aren't you going to open it?"

And there it was. The question, asked by a sixteen-year-old with copper wire in her hair, the question Paloma had spent her whole adult life not asking, laid out on the deck of her own boat in the flat white morning light.

She looked at the chest. Lead-lined, river-kept, stamped with a broken tower. Somebody had sealed it and sunk it in the hold of an honest wreck, and the honest wreck had kept it for thirty years, and the river had kept it before that, and the whole coast had kept the silence around it like a family keeps a room nobody goes into.

"I'm going to make tea," Paloma said. "Then I'm going to open it."

Doss made a noise like a kettle. "Pal —"

"You can help me or you can sulk. Those are the options. Sulking doesn't get you a cut."

"It's *my* cut too —"

"It's nobody's cut until we know what we're cutting." She picked up the chest. It was warm from the sun, which felt wrong, like a handshake from a stranger. "And Doss — if it's what it looks like, we don't sell it to collectors. Collectors talk. Whatever this is, it's been kept quiet by people with gunboats."

Doss opened his mouth. Closed it. Paloma watched him arrive, visibly, at the thought she'd had forty feet down: *somebody went to trouble to lose this, and somebody else went to trouble to sink it there, and neither of those somebodies has ever had to answer for it, which means they're still around.* Greed, in Doss, was a fast animal. Fear was faster.

"Tea first," he said finally, in the voice of a man conceding a point he intends to revisit. "Then we open it. And Pal —" He tapped the brass plate once, gently, the broken tower under his thumb. "Whatever's in there. We decide together what it's worth. Deal?"

"Deal," Paloma said, and did not believe him, and went to make the tea.

Below the hull, the river moved on over the Widow and her copper and her thirty honest years, keeping what it kept. Paloma stood at the little galley stove and watched the water heat, and caught herself, twice, glancing at the chest on the deck boards through the hatch — and each time, that small stubborn animal turned over in her chest and settled again, the way it does when it has finally been thrown something worth catching.

The tea boiled. She poured four cups.

She had a feeling they were going to need them.
