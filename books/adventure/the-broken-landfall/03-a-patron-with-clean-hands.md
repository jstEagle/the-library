## 3. A Patron with Clean Hands

The letter arrived the way money always did in Vessarine — wrapped in someone else's good intentions.

It came by courier, a boy of maybe fourteen on a bicycle too big for him, who pedaled out to the Second Chance where she lay at her moorings off Tallow Quay and held out an envelope heavy as a brick. Paloma signed for it without thinking. Salvage divers learned early that anything delivered by hand was either a bill or a summons, and both were best read standing up, so you could sit down after.

She sat down after.

*Corvel Asque requests the pleasure of your company at his residence on Meridian Hill, Thursday next, to discuss a matter of mutual interest regarding recent recoveries in the Kessel Reach.*

Paloma read it three times. The paper was thick enough to fold into a boat, cream-colored, watermarked with a crest she didn't recognize — some arrangement of keys and laurels that meant old money, the kind that had stopped needing to introduce itself generations ago. Nobody wrote to salvage divers on paper like this. Nobody wrote to salvage divers at all, except the harbormaster, the tax office, and once, memorably, a widow in Saltmarsh who wanted to know if her husband's fishing boat had gone down quick or slow.

Doss appeared at the companionway before she'd finished the third reading, because Doss had a talent for appearing whenever paper changed hands, the way gulls appeared whenever fish did.

"That's good stock," he said, nodding at the envelope. "You could sell the envelope."

"Who is Corvel Asque?"

Doss leaned against the doorframe and scratched under his cap, which was how he thought. "Financier. Backs expeditions. Dredging operations, mostly. Had a finger in the canal works upriver until the government bought him out." He squinted. "Clean hands, they say. Which around here means nobody's caught him yet."

"He wants to discuss *recent recoveries.*"

"Word travels," Doss said, and left it there, which was unlike him. Doss usually traveled with words like luggage, hauling them everywhere whether anyone wanted them or not. But he'd been there, two nights ago, when Paloma had come up the ladder with the lead-lined chest slung over her shoulder and set it down on deck with a sound like a coffin being lowered. He'd seen what was inside — or some of it. He knew why she'd slept badly since.

"Should I go?" Paloma asked.

"You should eat first," Doss said. "Decisions made hungry are decisions made twice."

* * *

Meridian Hill smelled of cut grass and other people's security. The houses up there stood back from their gates behind hedges trimmed to the width of a hymnbook, and each gate had a bell you rang and a voice that answered through a grille, so that even invitations felt like interrogations conducted politely.

Asque's house was the third one, white-fronted, with a garden that had been designed rather than grown. A woman let her in without asking her name, took her wet jacket — it had started raining on the climb, because the river kept its weather close — and showed her into a study lined with books that had clearly never been opened, spines uncracked, arranged by color. Paloma ran a thumb along one as she passed and felt the gilt flake.

Corvel Asque rose from behind a desk the size of a dinner table to greet her. He was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, dressed in gray wool that probably cost more than the Second Chance's hull. His handshake was dry and exactly calibrated — firm enough to seem honest, brief enough to seem busy.

"Diver Oyelaran," he said. "Thank you for coming. May I offer you tea? Coffee? Something stronger? It's miserable out."

"Coffee," Paloma said, because tea at a rich man's table always came with ceremony, and ceremony came with time, and time was the one thing she'd promised herself not to give him more of than necessary.

He poured it himself, which surprised her. Good coffee, too — dark, real, not the chicory stretch the quay cafés served. She drank it and hated herself a little for enjoying it.

"I'll come to the point," Asque said, sitting, folding his hands. "I understand you've recovered material from a wreck in the Kessel Reach. A chest, I'm told. Lead-lined."

"Told by whom?"

"Salvage is a small world," he said pleasantly. "Smaller than most people think. Smaller than it should be, frankly, given how much coastline we have." He smiled. "I'm told it's stamped with a seal no one has used in forty years."

"The navy forbids discussion of that wreck," Paloma said. "Officially it doesn't exist. Unofficially, divers who mention it stop getting permits."

"Yes." Asque nodded slowly, as though she'd confirmed something he'd long suspected but wanted verified by a professional. "And yet here you are, discussing it, in the house of a man who funds dredging contracts with the Admiralty Office. Doesn't that worry you?"

It should have. It was, in fact, the single most worrying sentence anyone had said to her in weeks, and she noticed that she was noticing this only afterward, in the way you notice a current pulling after you've already drifted. In the moment, she'd been distracted — by the coffee, by the room, by the strange vertigo of being treated as a person whose opinion might be worth something.

"A little," she said.

"Good. A healthy fear keeps divers alive." He leaned forward. "Now. What I propose is this: a full recovery operation. Proper equipment, proper crew, decompression support, a tender vessel, hazard pay for everyone involved. I have capital sitting idle and a reputation for backing profitable ventures. You have the find, the expertise, and the location. Between us, we do this correctly instead of the way salvage is usually done — which is to say, dangerously, illegally, and for pennies."

"And your share?"

"One third of whatever's recovered, valued at market. Standard terms. I'll draw up papers."

Paloma set down her cup. One third. For money. For equipment she could never afford, for a crew she couldn't yet pay, for legal cover against a navy that had already demonstrated its appetite for silence. It was, on its face, the fairest offer anyone had ever made her, and that was precisely what snagged in her throat like a fishhook.

"Mr. Asque," she said carefully, "why does a financier want a share of a wreck the navy says doesn't exist?"

Asque's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a small recalibration, a gear shifting somewhere behind his eyes. "Because the navy is wrong," he said. "Or lying. Or both. And because things that don't officially exist are frequently the most valuable things on the coast. Ask any smuggler." He spread his hands. "I deal in realities, diver. Maps, ledgers, salvage rights. If a nation can be erased from the maps, then a nation can be written back onto them, and whoever holds the pen sets the price. I simply intend to hold the pen."

There it was. Not hidden, not even disguised — just offered plainly, the way powerful men offered things, confident that plainness would be mistaken for honesty. He wanted the past the way he wanted everything: as an asset.

"And if the Admiralty objects?"

"The Admiralty and I have a working relationship," Asque said. "They fund my dredging surveys; I keep my findings discreet. They will object to nothing they don't learn about, and they won't learn about this unless you tell them." He tilted his head. "Unless, of course, you'd prefer to sell to them directly. I understand they pay well for discretion. Though I hear their contractors pay better for witnesses, and I use the word *pay* loosely."

Paloma stood. It wasn't a decision so much as a reflex — her body understood before she did that the conversation had reached the bottom of its useful depth, and staying longer would only mean drowning in it slowly.

"I'll consider your offer," she said.

"Take all the time you need," Asque said graciously, walking her to the door himself, which was either respect or surveillance. At the threshold he paused. "One thing, diver, as a courtesy between professionals. Whatever you found in that chest — papers, instruments, deeds — be careful who sees you carrying it. Objects like that have a way of making their owners valuable in ways they don't enjoy." He smiled again, warm as lamplight. "I'd hate to see anything happen to you. You're the most interesting thing to happen to this coast in forty years."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The walk down Meridian Hill took twenty minutes, and Paloma spent all of them doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

* * *

That night she sat in the Second Chance's cramped cabin with the deed spread on the chart table, weighted at the corners with a spanner, a tin cup, Doss's lucky bolt, and the ship's cat, who had opinions about being moved and expressed them by sitting harder.

The deed named a bearer. Not a family, not an estate — a bearer, singular, whoever carried it home. It named coordinates she'd memorized and then burned the memory of writing down. It named sums. She'd read the figures twice and still didn't believe them, the way you don't believe lottery numbers, the way you don't believe the depth gauge when it says you're deeper than the cable allows.

Enough to buy fleets, Ilya would say later, when later came. Enough to buy fleets.

And Corvel Asque had looked at her across his desk and said, essentially: *I know what you have, I know what it's worth, and I am prepared to make you rich and safe — on my terms.*

She thought about the Admiralty's silence. Forty years of it. Permits denied, wrecks struck from charts, divers warned off with quiet visits and loud consequences. Silence on that scale costs money. Somebody was paying for it, every year, decade after decade — paying surveyors, paying cartographers, paying men like the ones who'd boarded her cousin's boat off Saltmarsh and taken his charts and his livelihood in the same afternoon. The silence had a price tag. She'd found the receipt.

Which meant the inheritance was real. Which meant the claim was real. Which meant that somewhere in a frozen account, or a land registry, or a vault in a neutral country, there existed a sum of money with her name — or at least her possession — attached to it, waiting for a bearer with the nerve to walk in and say *this belongs to the dead, and I am speaking for them.*

Her first instinct, honest and immediate, was to burn it. Sink it. Weld it inside a ballast block and drop it off the continental shelf. Let the dead keep their secrets; the living had enough trouble. Doss would call that cowardice. Runi would call it sense. Paloma called it Tuesday.

But the second instinct arrived right behind the first, wearing better clothes: *use it.* Use the money. Use the claim. Use the dead, even — they'd waited forty years, what was another season — to buy something the living needed. A real expedition. Real equipment. A crew paid what they were worth. And at the end of it, justice, or at least acknowledgment, which in Vessarine was the same currency under different names.

And underneath the second instinct, quieter than either, a third voice — one she recognized because it sounded disconcertingly like Corvel Asque, smooth and reasonable and slightly amused: *You could keep it. You could be the bearer in every sense. Fleets, diver. Think what a woman with fleets could do for this coast.*

Paloma pressed her palms flat on the chart table until her fingers went white. That third voice was the dangerous one, and it hadn't come from Asque. He'd merely tuned the frequency; the signal had been broadcasting in her all along, the way ambition broadcasts in everyone, waiting for a strong enough antenna. Power didn't kick doors. It knocked politely, offered coffee, and asked if you'd considered your options.

She folded the deed along its original creases, slid it into an oilskin pouch, and did the thing she'd decided on during the walk down Meridian Hill, the decision she'd been rehearsing ever since:

She would take Asque's money.

Not his terms — those she'd amend, stall, blur. But the capital, yes. The equipment, the crew wages, the tender vessel. Because the alternative was diving on credit and hope, and hope had a worse interest rate than any financier. Because the navy would come for her regardless — the chest had already marked her, the way a bright fish marks itself against dark water — and if trouble was coming anyway, it might as well arrive while she had air tanks and a fast boat and people beside her who could hold a line.

She was using him, she told herself. Using his money to reach the archive before his kind could bury the truth. Every coin he advanced was a coin the truth would spend against him. It was elegant. It was almost true.

Almost.

Because she'd also stood in that book-lined study and felt the pull of it — the clean hands, the easy money, the version of herself that walked up Meridian Hill often enough to stop noticing the smell of cut grass. And self-deception, Paloma knew from long experience with currents, doesn't feel like lying. It feels like navigation. You adjust your heading a few degrees to catch a favorable tide, telling yourself you'll correct later, and later the shore has changed and you're somewhere you never meant to go, and the worst part is that every single degree of the journey made perfect sense at the time.

She told Doss the next morning, over coffee on the aft deck, fog burning off the harbor in slow rags.

"We take his money," she said.

Doss chewed on that the way he chewed on everything, visibly, noisily, with commentary. "His terms?"

"To be negotiated. Slowly. Paperwork takes time; I'll make sure ours takes longest."

"And when he asks what's in the chest?"

"He hasn't asked yet. When he does—" She turned her cup in her hands. "I'll tell him it's documents. Historical. Valuable. I won't lie about what it isn't. I just won't show him what it is."

Doss studied her with an expression she rarely saw on him — something older and sadder than his usual grin. "You know he'll find out eventually."

"Eventually is a destination," Paloma said. "We don't have to arrive today."

"No," Doss agreed. "Today we need an engineer, a cartographer, and somebody who knows where the river hides its rocks." He pushed back from the rail and headed below, and over his shoulder he added, cheerfully and terribly, "I'll start asking around. Everyone needs money. Nobody'll ask why the navy cares."

Paloma watched him go and felt the plan settle around her shoulders like a coat that fit in the shoulders and pulled everywhere else.

Three days later, Asque's first advance arrived — cash, in a canvas bag, counted twice by a solicitor's clerk who smelled of ink and nerves. It was more money than the Second Chance had earned in her entire working life. Paloma signed the receipt, banked half, stuffed the rest into a floorboard cavity beneath the bilge pump, and told herself, one more time, firmly, with feeling:

*I'm using him.*

The tide turned that evening. By morning, word had come down the quay that a naval patrol boat had been reassigned to the Kessel Reach, reason unstated, duration open-ended. And a man Paloma had never met — a cartographer, ex-naval, name of Ilya — had walked down to the moorings asking after work, holding his forged charts rolled under one arm like a weapon he hoped nobody would recognize.

She hired him before lunch.
