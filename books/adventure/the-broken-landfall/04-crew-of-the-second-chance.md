## 4. Crew of the Second Chance

Doss had a theory about people, and it was this: everybody was running from something, and if you could figure out what, you could figure out what they'd do next. He'd built his whole life on the theory. It had kept him fed through two famines, one war scare, and eleven years of diving wrecks that the Admiralty preferred to pretend were fish habitats.

So when Paloma told him — in her flat, careful way, the way she said things she'd already decided — that they were going upriver with Asque's money to chase whatever was in that chest, Doss started making a list.

Not a list of divers. The Vessarine coast was thick with divers; half of them could hold their breath long enough to be dangerous about it. No, what he needed was a crew. People who could keep a boat alive, read water, and ask no questions worth answering. People who needed the money more than they needed to know.

He found them the way he found everything: by sitting in the wrong taverns and listening.

* * *

Sefa was under a boat when Doss found her, which was where Sefa usually was, metaphorically and otherwise. The boat in question belonged to a fisherman named Ollik who owed Sefa three months' wages and had offered her his engine instead, which was like offering someone your toothache as payment for dental work.

Doss crouched at the transom and watched her work for a while. She was a broad, square woman with forearms like mooring line and grease worked so deep into her knuckles that no solvent on the coast would ever get it out. She talked to engines. Not sweetly, either. She insulted them, quietly and constantly, the way you'd scold a stubborn mule you secretly loved.

"That injector's lying to you," she muttered. "I know you're lying. I can hear it."

"Maybe it's shy," Doss said.

Sefa didn't startle. She just turned her head enough to give him one eye, flat as a dead calm. "People who sneak up on working engineers lose fingers."

"I'll remember." He held up both hands, all ten fingers accounted for. "Doss Merrow. I dive with Paloma Oyelaran."

That got him the other eye. Everyone on the coast knew Paloma's name; she'd pulled three bodies out of the Marrow Shoals wreck two winters back when the navy's own rescue launch couldn't be bothered to leave harbor before dark. "The one who found the Kethria box," Sefa said.

Word traveled. Doss filed that away with a small internal wince. "News does move."

"News moves. Navies move faster." She went back to the injector. "What do you want?"

"A chief engineer. Upriver run, flood season, nineteen days of window. Pay's triple salvage rates, hazard bonus on top, and Asque money means it's actually real."

"Flood season." She said it the way other people said *plague*. "Up which river?"

"The Verren."

Sefa sat back on her heels. For a moment she looked past him, out toward the gray sheet of the harbor, and Doss watched her do arithmetic he couldn't see. Then she said, "My sister lives in Verren Reach. Haven't seen her in six years because I can't afford the passage and she can't afford to leave the archive."

"You'd be going right past her door."

"I'd be going right past her door during flood crest, which is exactly when nobody gets to stop anywhere." But she was already wiping her hands, and Doss knew he had her. That was the thing about Sefa: she pretended to be all engine, but there was a whole person bolted underneath, badly maintained and running anyway.

"One condition," she said. "Nobody touches my engine room without asking. Not the patron, not the captain, not you especially."

"Not me especially," Doss agreed solemnly. "Especially not me."

"And I want to know why the navy cares about a forty-year-old box."

Ah, Doss thought. There it is. "You want to know, or you need to know?"

She considered that with the same squinting attention she gave machinery. "Want," she admitted finally. "Need's different. Need I could live without."

"Then we understand each other." He stuck out his hand. She shook it with a grip that rearranged something in his wrist.

* * *

Ilya was harder.

Ex-naval cartographer, retired sideways — that was how the story went, though Doss had heard four versions of *sideways* and none of them matched. He lived in a rented room above a chandler's shop and made his living redrawing harbor charts for private owners, correcting errors the Hydrographic Office never admitted to. His charts were famous among working captains for being honest, which was a strange thing to be famous for, and told you most of what you needed to know.

Doss found him at the chandler's counter, arguing gently with the shopkeeper about the price of lamp oil.

"Gently" was the word. Ilya argued the way water wore stone — no force at all, just patience and repetition until the other person gave up from sheer embarrassment. He was a narrow man, sixty-odd, with a cartographer's stoop and hands that trembled slightly unless they were holding a pen, in which case they went perfectly still, like they'd found their purpose.

"Lamp oil," Doss said, when the argument concluded at a price Ilya clearly considered extortionate and paid anyway. "For charts or for burning charts?"

Ilya looked at him over the top of spectacles held together with wire. "Burning, mostly. Old ones. They go out of date and then they're just paper with lies on it."

"That's a grim way to talk about your trade."

"My trade is drawing coastlines that agree with the Admiralty. My hobby is knowing where they don't." He said it mildly, almost pleasantly, and Doss felt the hook go in clean. This was a man who'd been waiting years for someone to ask.

"So here's my question," Doss said, leaning on the counter. "Flood season, the Verren, up past Kessel Bend. What do the official charts say about that stretch?"

Ilya's pen-hand twitched, just once. "They say it's unremarkable."

"And what do you say?"

"I say the last survey party that charted it came back with numbers that don't match the riverbed, and the survey leader got promoted rather than questioned." He took off the spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried. "Why do you ask, Mr. Merrow?"

"Because I'm putting together a crew for an upriver run, and I'd rather have a man aboard who knows where the maps are lying than one who trusts them."

Ilya was quiet for a while. Outside, the chandler's boy stacked crates; inside, the lamp oil sat between them like a small bribe neither had mentioned.

"I'm expensive," Ilya said at last.

"Asque's paying."

"Corvel Asque." Ilya pronounced the name carefully, tasting it. "There's a man whose ledgers I'd enjoy reading."

"He keeps them close."

"They always do." He put the spectacles back on. Behind the wire frames his eyes were tired and very awake. "I'll come. One condition of my own: I draw the route, and nobody else draws anything. If someone hands me a chart and tells me it's better than mine, I'll want to know who printed it and why."

"Fair."

"And Mr. Merrow—" he paused at the door, oil under his arm, stooped against the frame like a question mark that had given up standing straight, "—when the time comes that you need to know why the navy cares, come to me first. Before anyone else. I've been keeping answers longer than you've been keeping questions."

He left. Doss stood at the counter feeling like he'd just shaken hands with a locked drawer.

* * *

Runi was the easiest recruit Doss ever made, and the one that worried him most.

She was fifteen, maybe sixteen — nobody on the river banks counted years the way towns did — and she ran the shallows above the fishing weir in a flat-bottomed skiff her grandfather had built, ferrying goods, gossip, and the occasional fugitive for whatever people could pay. She was small and quick and brown as a nut, with a laugh that carried across water like it had a right to, and she knew the Verren's sandbars the way other children knew their own teeth.

Doss caught her at the weir at dawn, mid-negotiation with a eel-fisherman who clearly thought he was getting the better of her and clearly wasn't.

"—and if you think the second channel's open after the rain, take your skiff through it and see what the mud thinks of your keel," Runi was saying. "Or pay the fair price and live to be rude another day."

The fisherman paid the fair price.

Doss waited until he'd poled off, then said, "Good morning."

"It was," Runi said, "until a stranger started lurking by my lines. You're Doss Merrow. You dive with Paloma Oyelaran." She said it with the particular relish teenagers reserve for adults they've decided to be unimpressed by. "Everyone below the bend knows your boat. Second Chance, right? The name's a joke, isn't it? Because she sank once?"

"She ran aground once. There's a difference, and it matters to her feelings."

Runi grinned despite herself. Doss counted that as progress.

"I need a pilot," he said. "Shallows water, flood season, all the way up to Verren Reach. You know every channel the charts forgot."

"Charts forget on purpose sometimes." She said it lightly, tossing a coil of line into the skiff, and Doss felt the words land somewhere near his spine. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, and already talking like Ilya. River kids grew up fast or drowned; apparently growing up fast included learning which silences were load-bearing.

"On purpose," he repeated.

"Mm." She hopped down into the skiff and began checking her poles with the brisk economy of someone much older wearing someone much younger's body. "My grandad used to say the river keeps two sets of books. One for the tax man, one for the truth." She looked up at him, suddenly direct. "Triple rates, you said? I heard you at the market yesterday. You talk loud for a sneaky person."

"I contain multitudes."

"What's the cargo?"

"Can't say."

"Is it illegal?"

"No."

"Is it *navy*-illegal?" She drew the distinction with great precision, and Doss laughed before he could stop himself — a real laugh, surprised out of him, the kind he hadn't had since before the chest came up on the line.

"Worse," he said. "It's complicated."

"Complicated pays triple too?"

"Complicated pays triple and a bonus if we're quick."

Runi thought about it, chewing her lip, looking upriver where the water lay flat and brown and patient under the morning haze. Somewhere up there was Verren Reach, and the archive, and whatever it was that had admirals spending money to keep a nation's name off maps. Somewhere up there, in nineteen days, the flood would crest and close the window for another year.

"I'm in," she said. "But I bring my own skiff, and when we pass my auntie's landing I eat at her table, and nobody argues."

"Deal."

"And Doss Merrow?" She pushed off from the weir, steadying the skiff with one foot, entirely at home on moving water in a way Doss knew he would never be. "When people start asking why the navy cares — and they will, they always do — I won't ask. But I'll notice. We river folk notice everything. It's how we stay floating."

* * *

They gathered aboard the Second Chance on a bright, cold morning with gulls arguing overhead and the tide turning. Paloma walked the deck with the deed hidden somewhere Doss refused to imagine and Asque's advance sitting in the harbor office like a loaded thing. Sefa claimed the engine room within the hour and was heard insulting the fuel pump by noon. Ilya spread his own charts across the mess table and weighted the corners with mugs, and when Sefa came up for air and saw them, the two of them fell into a conversation about river gauges that lasted two hours and excluded everyone else completely, which Doss took as a good sign. Crews that argued about gauges stayed alive. Crews that agreed about everything were usually agreeing about nothing.

Runi arrived last, skiff in tow, a sack of clothes and one enormous knife, and immediately claimed the spot on deck with the best view, announcing that she'd take first watch out of habit and nobody should make a fuss about it.

By evening they were a crew. Doss sat on the rail with a mug of Sefa's terrible coffee — she made it strong enough to caulk seams — and looked at the four of them in the lamplight and felt the old warmth settle in his chest, familiar and dangerous. Paloma at the tiller, quiet, certain. Sefa below, muttering blessings and curses at machinery in equal measure. Ilya bent over his charts, pen steady, drawing the river as it truly was. Runi cross-legged on the foredeck, teaching herself knots she already knew, faster, for the pleasure of it.

His people. Found, not born; chosen, not assigned. The only family he'd ever trusted, and he'd trusted them with less than this.

That was the part that itched. Every one of them had signed on needing the money and none of them had asked the question — not Sefa, who wanted to know but could wait; not Ilya, who was hoarding answers like a dragon; not Runi, who'd announced in advance that she wouldn't ask but would notice. Nobody had asked *why the navy cares*, and Doss knew exactly why nobody asked. Because asking was expensive. Because on this coast, questions had a way of coming due with interest, collected by men in gray uniforms.

He took a sip of the terrible coffee and made himself look at it plainly: he'd recruited a crew of people who knew how not to ask, and he'd done it on purpose, and someday soon — maybe at Kessel Bend, maybe sooner — somebody was going to have to ask anyway, and the whole warm structure he'd just built would find out what it was made of.

Paloma came up beside him at the rail. She smelled of river water and lamp oil and the faint iron tang she always carried, like the sea had gotten into her blood and never left.

"Doss," she said, "you look like a man doing arithmetic."

"I am. I'm counting how many days until someone on this boat asks the question we're all avoiding."

"And how many did you get?"

"Fewer than nineteen." He handed her the mug. She drank the terrible coffee without complaint, which was its own kind of loyalty. "Paloma — Asque's ledger is aboard. I've seen the man carry it. Whatever he is, he isn't clean, and clean is what he pretends to be."

"I know," she said simply, and that frightened Doss more than ignorance would have, because she said it the way she said everything — already decided, already past the point where warning helped.

Below them, the tide finished turning. Upstream, far past the last lights of the harbor, the Verren waited, high and brown and rising, holding in its drowned memory a nation that the maps had agreed to forget.

"We sail on the morning tide," Paloma said. "Nineteen days."

Doss looked upriver again, at all that patient dark water, and thought: *nineteen days is plenty of time for everyone to stop not-asking.*

He hoped he was wrong. He'd bet the crew he was.
