## 5. Flood Season Rules

The morning tide came in gray and smelling of iron, and Paloma stood at the rail of the *Second Chance* watching the harbor master's boy nail up the flood-season notice like it was a death warrant he'd been paid by the word.

*VERREN RIVER CLOSED TO UNLICENSED TRAFFIC ABOVE MILE FORTY. BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY OFFICE.*

"Closed," Sefa said beside her, reading over her shoulder with her hands still black to the wrist from engine grease. "That's friendly."

"It's a courtesy," Paloma said. "They're telling us exactly where they'll be looking."

Sefa snorted. "You always find the bright side of a threat?"

"I find the useful side." She watched the boy finish nailing, salute nobody, and wander off. "A closed river means a charted river. Charts have gaps. Gaps are where we live."

Below deck, the sounds of departure were already underway — Doss arguing with the water casks, Runi somewhere overhead rigging the awning wrong in a way that would take Ilya twenty minutes to fix and forty minutes to explain why it was wrong. The *Second Chance* was a salvage barge that had been converted to an expedition vessel by people who believed enthusiasm could substitute for shipwrights, which is to say she leaked in three places and none of them were fatal yet. Paloma had named her after the only kind of luck she'd ever trusted: not the first chance, which everyone wasted, but the second one, which you earned by surviving the first.

"Nineteen days," she said to the notice, and went below to count the air tanks herself, because trust was lovely but arithmetic was honest.

* * *

They cleared the outer moorings on the turn of the tide, the city falling away behind them in its usual fashion — smoke first, then bells, then the gulls, until there was nothing left of civilization but the channel markers bobbing like patient dogs waiting to be thrown something.

The Verren at flood season was not a river. It was a rumor of a river wearing a river's clothes. Where in the dry months it ran narrow and brown-green between reed banks, now it had climbed into the fields, the orchards, the lower roads, and moved with the slow muscular confidence of something that had eaten a valley for breakfast and was still hungry. Trees stood waist-deep and pretended it was normal. A farmhouse roof passed them on the port side, tilted, with a goat standing on it — alive, unimpressed, chewing.

"That goat," Runi said from the bow, "is having a better flood than we are."

"Goats don't pay for fuel," Sefa called up from the engine hatch.

Runi, sixteen and built out of elbows and certainty, knew these waters the way other children knew their own bedrooms. Her family had worked the Verren's shallows for four generations, poling flatboats through water that drowned outsiders and forgave locals. She stood at the bow now with a pole she was too proud to use and too smart to put down, reading the current aloud like scripture.

"Green water past the willow line," she reported. "Means it's shallow under the color. We want the brown seam on the left, where it's moving fast but smooth. Fast-and-smooth means deep. Fast-and-lumpy means we're about to donate the hull to the river gods."

"And who are the river gods?" Ilya asked mildly, from his nest of charts at the stern table.

"Whoever's older than the Admiralty," Runi said. "So basically everyone."

Ilya smiled down at his charts. He was a thin man with cartographer's hands — precise, ink-stained, always slightly cold — and he'd spent eleven years drawing navy charts before deciding he preferred drawing ones that were true. He'd spread the Verren's official survey across the table that morning and then, without comment, laid his own hand-annotated copy beside it, full of small corrections in a cramped, apologetic script. *Actual depth 4.2m, not 6. Marker moved 1887, never replaced. Village of Tessel: relocated, chart not updated, do not anchor where the church used to be.*

Paloma had looked at those annotations for a long moment. "How many rivers did you correct?"

"Seven," Ilya said. "Before they asked me to stop correcting and start agreeing."

She hadn't asked anything else. There was time for the rest of it, or there wasn't, and either way the river would tell them.

By noon they'd made the first bend and lost sight of anything resembling a town. The wilderness here didn't announce itself; it simply stopped pretending to be tamed. Reeds closed in. The channel narrowed, widened, lied about itself. Somewhere inland a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, endlessly, and Sefa eventually went below just to get away from it.

Doss found Paloma at the wheel in the early afternoon. He came at the wheel the way he came at everything — sideways, hands in pockets, as if he might change his mind about arriving.

"We should talk about the chest," he said.

"No."

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"You were going to say we should talk about what happens if Asque's money runs out and the chest is the only asset left, and whether selling it sealed might be smarter than opening it, and you wanted me to say no so you'd know where the line is." She kept her eyes on the channel. "The line is: we open it when it's safe, we sell it never, and if it comes down to the chest or the crew, the chest floats."

Doss considered this. "That's a terrible business plan."

"It's a terrible business plan that keeps everyone breathing. I've seen the other kind." She glanced at him then, and softened it with the half-smile she rationed like fresh water. "Ask me again in Verren Reach. If we make Verren Reach, I'll let you be right about something. You can pick which thing."

"That's the nicest threat anyone's ever given me," Doss said, and went forward to bother the casks again.

* * *

The first night they tied off in the flooded remains of an orchard, trees standing in black water with their branches full of unripe fruit nobody would ever pick. Sefa cooked rice with too much salt and defended it as seasoning. Runi taught Ilya a card game that appeared to have no rules except that Runi won. The fireflies came up out of the reeds in such numbers that the whole orchard seemed to be thinking hard about becoming stars.

It was, Paloma thought, almost enough to forget they were being followed.

Almost.

She'd noticed it an hour past sunset — not a light, not a sound, but a wrongness in the way the reeds across the channel held their reflections. A stillness where stillness didn't belong. Then, later, when the moon rose: the faintest shape upstream, dark against dark, holding position against a current that strong meant an engine idling low, and engines idling low at night on a closed river meant one thing.

Navy patrol boat. Shadowing them. Not hailing.

She said nothing for a long while. She sat with her tea going cold and watched the dark shape hold its patient distance and felt the old familiar feeling settle over her — the one from her diving years, the one that said *something down there has noticed you, and it hasn't decided yet whether you're worth the trouble.*

"They're not coming closer," Ilya said quietly, appearing beside her with the particular silence of men who'd spent careers on ships where appearing suddenly was discouraged.

"You saw them too."

"I've been watching since the bend at Corry's Light. They keep exactly eight hundred meters. That's regulation shadow distance." He turned his cup slowly. "If they wanted to board us, they'd have done it in daylight, with witnesses. If they wanted to sink us, they'd have done it at the bend, where the current would scatter the evidence. This is something else."

"What else is there?"

Ilya looked at the dark shape for a while. "Being reminded," he said finally. "Somebody wants us to know they're there. Wants us to feel it. It's cheaper than stopping us and louder than ignoring us." He paused. "It's how you herd animals, Paloma. You don't touch them. You just stand where they shouldn't go, until standing there feels like their own idea to avoid."

Paloma drank the cold tea anyway. "And what do we do?"

"We do exactly what we were doing," Ilya said. "We keep our course, we keep our lights legal, we keep our papers ready, and we do not give them a reason to stop being polite." He hesitated, and then added, very quietly, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far already: "But I'll tell you this much for free. In eleven years of naval service, I never once saw a patrol boat waste a night shadowing a salvage barge. Whatever's in that chest, they're not worried about it. They're *invested* in it."

Across the fire, Runi laughed at something — loud, young, unguarded — and the sound crossed the black water toward the patrol boat and got no answer at all.

* * *

Paloma took the middle watch, as she always did, because sleep came hard to her on moving water and lying awake below decks was worse than being useful about it. She sat at the wheel with the boat riding at anchor, the orchard dripping softly around them, and let the river teach her its night grammar.

Flood water spoke differently than dry-season water. Dry water chattered — over rocks, around snags, all surface noise. Flood water *murmured*, deep and continuous, a conversation happening underneath about things it had already decided to swallow. She could hear it taking the orchard a little at a time. By morning, she judged, the waterline would be a handspan higher on the trunks. The river wasn't rising in anger. It was rising on schedule. That was the frightening part — the indifference. A storm you could hate. A schedule you could only respect.

Around two in the morning, the patrol boat's shape shifted.

Not approaching. Not leaving. It simply turned — slow, deliberate, its running lights winking once as it came about — and began to move back downstream, unhurried, the way a man walks away from a door he's decided isn't worth knocking on.

Paloma watched it go until the reeds took it, and found that its absence was somehow worse than its presence. While it shadowed them, they were a question. Now they were an answer — filed, forwarded, entered into some ledger in some office where men with clean hands drew lines on maps and waited to see whose little boat crossed them.

She woke Doss at dawn. He surfaced from his bunk with the grace of a landed fish.

"Patrol boat tailed us all night and turned back at two," she said. "Log it, tell Sefa, and when Runi asks, tell her it was fishing boats."

"You want me to lie to a child who can read water better than I can read words?"

"I want you to let her sleep tonight. Truth keeps. Childhood doesn't." She handed him the tea she'd saved him. "Also, we're coming up on Mile Forty. The closed water. From here on, every soul on this river is either smugglers, fools, or us."

Doss drank the tea and looked out at the flooded valley waking up — mist on the water, birds arguing in the drowned trees, the whole green world indifferent and enormous and utterly unbothered by navies, deeds, or dead nations.

"Fools, then," he said. "Mostly fools."

"Mostly," Paloma agreed, and rang the bell to raise the anchor.

Behind them, far downstream, the city was a smudge. Ahead, the river bent north into country no chart admitted to, carrying them and their sealed chest and nineteen shrinking days toward whatever the Admiralty had decided they were worth.

Somewhere in the reeds, the bird that sounded like a rusty hinge began again, patiently, as if counting.
