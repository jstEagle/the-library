## 6. The Wreck They Won't Discuss

Ilya Vess had spent thirty years making maps of places he'd never been allowed to see properly.

That was the joke of his profession, if you could call it a profession. A naval cartographer drew coastlines the way a priest drew saints — reverently, from secondhand accounts, and never quite to scale. The Admiralty gave him soundings and survey notes and told him where the water was deep and where it wasn't, and Ilya inked the charts in his careful hand, and somewhere between the sounding line and the pen, the truth got tidied up. Rivers straightened. Shoals vanished. Wrecks that didn't officially exist were left off entirely, like guests struck from a wedding list.

He'd told himself for three decades that this was simply how institutions worked. Paper needed to be orderly. The world, unfortunately, was not.

Now he sat on the deck of the *Second Chance* with a cup of Sefa's terrible coffee going cold in his hands, watching Paloma Oyelaran mark a dive site on her chart with an X so casual it might have been a doodle.

"There," she said. "Old hull, maybe sixty years down. Local fishermen won't set nets over it."

"Why not?" Runi asked, upside down on the rail, her legs hooked over the top bar like a bat at rest.

"Because their grandfathers told them not to," Paloma said. "And their grandfathers' grandfathers before that."

"And nobody ever asked why?"

"Runi." Doss didn't look up from the winch he was greasing. "Rule one of salvage. You don't ask why a wreck is empty. You ask why it's *full*."

"That's a horrible rule."

"It's kept me fed."

Ilya sipped his coffee and said nothing, which was, he reflected, more or less what he'd been doing since 1941. He was getting very good at it. If silence were a trade, he'd have his master's papers by now.

But Paloma glanced at him — just a flicker, the way she did when she wanted a professional opinion but didn't want to spend the social capital asking for one — and something in him shifted, the way ballast shifts when a boat takes water on one side.

"You'll want to check the hull integrity before anyone goes deep," he said. "A wreck that old, sitting where fishermen avoid it? Could be anything under the silt. Could be nothing. Could be a reason."

"A reason," Paloma repeated.

"For the nets staying out." He shrugged, aiming for the tone of a man discussing weather. "Rivers keep grudges. Ask any fisherman."

She looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable, then nodded and went back to her chart. Runi unhooked her legs and dropped to the deck. Doss resumed his war with the winch. The morning went on being ordinary, and Ilya sat with his cold coffee and felt the new weight settle into him, low and central, exactly where ballast belongs.

* * *

The dive itself was Sefa's idea, though she'd deny it.

They anchored over the site at midday, when the light came down through the brown water in dusty columns, and Paloma and Doss went down first with the cutting gear while Sefa ran the air lines and Ilya watched the bubbles and Runi watched everything, because watching everything was Runi's whole personality and she wore it well.

"Anything?" Sefa called into the voice tube.

A long pause. Then Doss's voice, tinny and delighted: "Cargo hold's intact! Iron ingots, maybe brass fittings — Paloma says don't get excited, which means she's excited."

"Brass fittings," Sefa murmured, patting the compressor like a beloved mule. "That's my next six months paid for."

It was on the third dive that Ilya went down himself.

He hadn't intended to. He was fifty-eight years old and his diving days were supposed to be behind him along with his naval career, his marriage, and his ability to sleep past dawn. But they were short-handed — Runi couldn't go below twenty feet without Sefa having opinions, and Sefa refused to leave the compressor, and Doss had gashed his forearm on a plate edge and was sulking on deck with a bandage and a bottle of rum he was pretending was medicinal — and Paloma had found something in the forward section she wanted a second opinion on.

"Structural," she'd said, and there had been a particular flatness in her voice that Ilya recognized the way you recognize a chord you once played yourself.

So he went down.

The river took him gently, which he resented. He'd expected cold and murk and the indignity of his knees complaining about the ladder, and instead the water parted around him warm as bathwater, green-gold, lit from above in slow shifting coins. The wreck rose out of the silt ahead of him like a sleeping animal — a coastal freighter, hundred and forty feet if she was an inch, lying on her port side with her starboard plates furred white with decades of river life.

Paloma waited at the break of the deck, her lamp beam steady. She pointed forward, toward the bow, and swam.

Ilya followed, and it took him perhaps four meters to understand why her voice had gone flat.

The hole in the hull was not damage. Damage was ragged; damage was torn metal bent outward or crushed inward by whatever had done the killing. This was neither. This was a series of openings — five of them, spaced with a regularity no accident had ever achieved — each one punched *outward*, the plate edges curled back like petals, the way steel behaves when the explosion comes from inside.

Scuttle charges. Placed against the keel from within, fired in sequence. He knew the spacing. He'd helped draft the manual diagram for it, in another life, in a drafting room that smelled of ink and floor wax, when he was twenty-three and proud of his neat hand.

His own breath sounded loud in his ears. Paloma's lamp swung to him, questioning. He gestured: *hull survey, standard checks*, the bland diver's shorthand, and she nodded and turned away, and Ilya put his gloved hand against the curled edge of the nearest charge breach and held it there while the river moved around them both.

Civilian hull. Coastal freighter — he could read her lines even under the weed, the fat working belly of a grain hauler or a general trader, nothing military about her except what men had done to her from the inside. And then sunk, and then erased — because he had checked, the night before, every chart he owned and half the ones he'd drawn, and this stretch of the Vessarine carried no notation, no hazard marker, no wreck symbol. A hundred and forty feet of dead ship and however many dead crew, and the maps said open water.

Fishermen's grandfathers had known better than the Admiralty. That was the part that made his chest hurt. The people who actually lived on the river had passed down the truth in the only archive that couldn't be redacted: *don't net here.* Grandfather to grandson, no paperwork required.

Paloma tapped his shoulder and pointed upward. Surfacing. He followed her, and all the way up he thought about how easy it would be to say nothing. How saying nothing required no effort at all. It was the path of least resistance, worn smooth by thirty years of his own feet.

* * *

He almost told her that evening.

They ate on deck — rice and dried fish and a jar of pickled vegetables Runi had traded off a bank family for "basically nothing, they were so nice, Ilya, you should've seen the baby" — and the talk was the usual salvage talk: air consumption, the ingots' likely weight, whether Doss's arm was infected (it wasn't) and whether he was milking it (he absolutely was).

"They were placed," Paloma said suddenly, to her bowl rather than the table. "The breaches. Outward curl, even spacing. Somebody sank her on purpose."

Doss stopped chewing. Sefa's ladle paused over the pot.

"You saw that too," Ilya said.

"I've seen scuttled ships before. Usually it's owners dumping insurance. You drill her, flood her, collect." Paloma frowned at the fire. "This wasn't insurance work. Insurance charges are sloppy, cheap, one or two. These were—" She shook her head. "Professional. Naval pattern."

The word hung there. *Naval.* Nobody picked it up. The fire popped. Somewhere across the dark water a night bird made its idiotic complaint.

"So somebody in uniform murdered a freighter," Doss said at last, lightly, the way you test ice. "Forty, sixty years ago. What's that to us?"

"Nothing," Sefa said quickly.

"Nothing," Doss agreed. "Brass fittings. That's our story."

Paloma's eyes came around to Ilya. Not accusing. Just asking, the way she asked everything — directly, without armor, which was either courage or naivety and he still hadn't decided which.

"You're the cartographer," she said. "You've seen the surveys. Was there ever a wreck logged on this stretch?"

And here it was. The moment, offered on a tin plate, with the fire warm and the rice going cold and everyone waiting.

"No," Ilya said. "No wreck was ever logged."

Every word true. Every word a lie. He watched her accept it, watched her turn back to the fire with a small dissatisfied line between her brows, and he understood with perfect clarity that he had just done the thing he'd been doing his whole adult life: he had given the tidy answer instead of the true one, and the tidy answer had fit so smoothly into the conversation that no one would ever know a seam had been sewn.

Later, in his bunk, he lay listening to the river slap the hull and conducted the argument he'd been conducting with himself since 1941.

*You were young,* said the old voice. *You didn't know what the omissions meant. You drew what you were given.*

*I knew enough,* said the newer voice, the one that had gotten louder lately, the one that had started speaking up around the time a salvage diver pulled a Kethrian chest out of the mud and the whole river seemed to hold its breath. *You knew the wrecks weren't on the charts. You knew why the fishermen avoided certain water. You knew, and you inked your neat little lines anyway, because the alternative was a career ending at twenty-four and a name on a list.*

*And what would telling Paloma change?*

*Nothing tonight. Everything eventually. Truth is like water — it doesn't need to be strong, it only needs to keep arriving.*

He turned over. Through the bulkhead he could hear Runi talking in her sleep, some dream-argument with a market vendor, and Sefa snoring in a key that would strip paint, and Doss's bunk creaking as he rolled over, and underneath it all the great patient heartbeat of the river going by.

Complicity, he thought, was not a single act. It was a cargo, loaded one crate at a time, none of them heavy enough to refuse, until one day you noticed your draft was too deep for the channels you needed to sail and there was nowhere left to unload.

He had thirty years of crates in his hold. He'd learned to sail heavy. He'd even gotten good at it.

But tomorrow they'd dive again, and Paloma would look at those breaches again, and she was not a woman who stopped asking questions just because the answers got expensive. He'd known her three weeks and he already knew that about her the way he knew a channel's depth: completely, and with respect.

There was a version of this where he told her everything — the manual, the drafting room, the surveys he'd cleaned, the names he'd watched vanish from charts. There was a version where she thanked him, and a version where she put him ashore at the next town, and both versions terrified him less than the third one, the one where he kept carrying the weight until the river decided his draft for him.

*Tomorrow,* he told himself. *I'll tell her tomorrow.*

He'd been telling himself that for thirty years. The remarkable thing, he thought, drifting toward sleep, was how renewable the supply of tomorrows always seemed, right up until the day the water came in over the plates.

Above him, faint through the deck, he heard Paloma still awake in the wheelhouse, chart paper rustling. Working late. Measuring the gap between what the maps said and what the river knew.

She'd find it. He was almost sure she'd find it.

And God help him, some tired honest corner of him was hoping she would — soon, and loudly, and before he had to be the one who said it first.
