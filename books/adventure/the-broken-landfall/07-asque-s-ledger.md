## 7. Asque's Ledger

Runi had three rules for living aboard the *Second Chance*, and she'd invented all of them herself, which made them law.

Rule one: never let Sefa catch you touching the engine, because Sefa talked about that engine the way other people talked about a first love, and jealousy made her lecture. Rule two: never eat the last of Doss's pickled eel no matter how much he claimed he didn't want it, because he always wanted it, and his sulking could sour a whole afternoon. Rule three: when you needed to hide something on a boat, hide it where nobody would ever think to look — somewhere boring.

Boring was why she was in the hold at all.

The trouble was this: seventeen years old, smallest person aboard, and possessed of a plan so sensible it had been rejected twice. If things went badly upriver — and Paloma's face said *when*, not *if* — Runi intended to be nowhere near whatever happened. She'd survived the Vessarine coast by being elsewhere during emergencies, a skill her mother called cowardice and Runi called strategy. All she needed was a stowaway spot. A bolt-hole. Somewhere with air, room for one skinny girl and a bag of dried fish, invisible until the shouting stopped.

The forward hold was full of rope. The aft hold was full of Asque's crates.

That was the problem with Corvel Asque, Runi had decided. Not that he was cruel or loud or stupid — he was none of those things, which was worse. He was *tidy*. His crates were stacked with corners aligned. His boots never had mud on them even after mud had definitely happened. He smiled at everyone the same amount, like a man who'd measured out exactly how much charm the situation required and refused to overspend.

And he'd brought eleven crates on a river run that Paloma had said would be lean. Eleven. Nobody brought eleven crates unless they expected to come back heavier than they left.

So Runi crouched between crate seven and crate eight, holding her little lamp low, looking for gaps. There was a gap behind the third stack, deep enough for a girl if she folded right, but it shared a wall with the bilge pump and the pump sang at night like a cat with opinions. She moved on. Behind the water barrels there was a hollow that smelled of vinegar and old wood, big enough, dry enough, boring enough —

Except the floorboards there didn't sound right.

She knew boat floors the way Doss knew card games: intimately and slightly dishonestly. This plank had a knock in it, a hollowness, and hollow planks meant space, and space meant either rot or storage, and both were interesting. She pried it up with her belt knife, careful as peeling an egg.

Underneath: a flat tin box, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string that had been knotted by someone who cared about knots.

Runi sat back on her heels.

Now. Here was a crossroads, and she knew it was a crossroads because her mother's voice arrived uninvited, the way it did whenever Runi was about to do something memorable. *Girl, you can put that plank down and go eat supper like a person with a future, or you can open that box and become part of a story.* Her mother had died believing stories were bad luck. Runi had never managed to believe it. Stories were the only inheritance her family had ever gotten that hadn't sunk.

She opened the box.

It wasn't treasure. It was paper — neat stacks of it, a ledger bound in gray leather, a bundle of letters, a small brass stamping press that looked expensive and joyless. The ledger's pages were ruled in fine columns, and the columns were full of numbers, and numbers were boring, which was rule three, except these numbers had names next to them.

She read the top page twice, because the first time her eyes had clearly lied to her.

*Admiralty Office, Disbursements Section.* That was the header. Underneath, entries in a hand so tidy it looked printed:

*To C. Asque, for survey services rendered — 400 crowns.*
*To C. Asque, chart acquisition, Kessel Bend approach — 250 crowns.*
*To C. Asque, final installment upon confirmation of site — 600 crowns.*

Dated four months ago.

Four months before Paloma Oyelaran had gone down into the forbidden wreck and come up with a chest that made grown men go quiet. Four months before Asque had appeared at the dockside tavern with his clean boots and his generous smile and his offer to fund everything, asking nothing, wanting nothing, a patron with hands so clean they squeaked.

Runi sat very still in the dark of the hold, and the river slapped against the hull below her, patient as a debt collector.

*Final installment upon confirmation of site.*

He'd known about the wreck. He'd known before any of them. Maybe he'd known before Paloma knew. And the Admiralty — the same Admiralty whose patrol boats shadowed them without hailing, whose name made Doss spit over the rail — the Admiralty had been paying him. Paying him to do what? Find the chest? Or find the people who found the chest?

Her heart was doing something complicated, half fear and half the awful electric thrill of knowing a thing nobody else knew. She'd felt it once before, aged twelve, watching a customs officer take a bribe through a warehouse window, and she'd learned then what she was learning now: secrets were heavy, and they got heavier the longer you carried them, and everybody who carried one eventually had to set it down somewhere.

The question was where.

* * *

She thought about telling Paloma first. That was the correct answer, obviously, the answer a better person would give. Paloma was captain; the boat was hers; the secret concerned her most of all. Runi even got as far as the galley door that evening, hand on the latch, hearing Paloma and Ilya arguing gently about charts inside, their voices doing the tired dance of two people who respected each other and were exhausted anyway.

Then she heard Asque's laugh from the deck above, warm and reasonable, drifting down through the boards like smoke.

And she thought: *what if he's right about her?*

Not wrong — right. What if Paloma heard the news and did the brave thing, the loud thing, marched straight up to Asque and demanded answers? Asque was rich. Rich men owned lawyers, boats, contractors, weather, practically. Paloma owned a diving suit with a patched knee and a crew she fed out of her own pocket. If it came to a fight tonight, tonight, here in the middle of the river with the flood coming — Paloma would lose, and everyone aboard would lose with her, and Runi's beautiful stowaway plan would die along with everything else.

No. Information was like the dried fish in her emergency bag. You didn't spend it all at once. You kept it dry, you kept it hidden, and you spent it when spending it would actually buy something.

But she couldn't carry it alone. That was the trap of secrets — one carrier drowned under the weight, two could swim. She needed someone who wouldn't panic, wouldn't preach, and wouldn't tell Paloma out of pure dutiful honesty before Runi had decided what the information was worth.

There was exactly one person aboard who fit that description, and he owed her a favor besides, from the eel incident at Kessel Bend, which he believed she'd forgotten and which she had absolutely not.

She found Doss at the stern that night, keeping the late watch, feet up on the rail, whittling a piece of driftwood into something that was either a bird or a failure. The moon was fat and the river ran silver and the whole world looked harmless, which Runi had learned was the river's favorite trick.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Doss said, not looking up.

"You're supposed to be whittling a bird. We're both disappointed."

He snorted. She climbed onto the rail beside him, which was against every safety rule Sefa had ever pronounced, and waited. Waiting was another skill. Doss hated silence the way cats hate closed doors; give him ten seconds of it and he'd fill it himself.

Eight seconds. "Well? You're sitting there like a gull eyeing my lunch. Out with it."

"I found something today," Runi said. "In the hold."

"Rats?"

"Worse. Paperwork."

Doss's knife paused. Doss feared many things — drowning, honesty, Sefa's opinion of his engine maintenance — but paperwork was his deepest terror, because paperwork was how the world reached out and grabbed a man who'd spent his life staying just outside its grip. "Whose paperwork?"

"That's the interesting part." She kept her voice low, though the nearest sleeper was thirty feet away and snoring. "It's a ledger. Asque's. Hidden under the floorboards, wrapped up like it mattered. And Doss—" she made herself say it plainly, because dressing it up would make it worse later, "—it's payments. From the Admiralty Office. To him. Dated months ago. Before we ever found anything. One of them says 'final installment upon confirmation of site.'"

The knife stopped moving entirely.

For a long moment Doss just looked at the water, and Runi watched his face do several things quickly, like cards being shuffled: disbelief, then arithmetic — she could see him adding up every generous thing Asque had done, every coin, every extra ration — and then a kind of grim settling, the look of a man confirming a suspicion he'd been refusing to have.

"The Admiralty," he said finally. "Paying our patron. Before the chest was ever raised." He turned to look at her, and his eyes weren't joking, which on Doss was nearly unprecedented. "You're sure it's his. Not planted. Not—"

"It was hidden under a loose board in his own crates' hold. Who else hides ledgers under floors? Paloma keeps hers in a locked drawer and dares you with her eyes to look. Sefa keeps receipts. This was hiding-hiding."

"Huh." He rubbed his jaw. "And you came to me. Not the captain."

"I came to you because you don't rattle." This was a lie dressed as a compliment; Doss rattled constantly, he just rattled privately. "And because I wanted to think before it goes to her. Once Paloma knows, it's torches and pitchforks, and I'm not sure we can afford pitchforks midriver."

Doss was quiet again. Below them the river kept moving, huge and indifferent, carrying them north toward whatever the flood season had planned. Somewhere ahead was Kessel Bend, and past that the drowned harbor, and past that everything else the charts argued about.

"What do you want to do?" he asked. "Truly."

And there it was — the question she'd been avoiding since the hold, asked kindly, which made it harder. Because the honest answer had layers, and she wasn't proud of all of them. Part of her wanted to protect Paloma. Part of her wanted to protect the crew. And a small, shameful part — the part that had grown up hungry on the coast, counting coins that never added up — part of her understood that a secret this size was worth something to somebody. Asque would pay to keep it quiet. The Admiralty would pay more. And knowledge, her mother had always said, was the only cargo that never spoiled.

"I want us to be safe," she said, which was true, and covered the rest like a tarpaulin.

Doss nodded slowly. Then he held out his hand. "Show me the page."

She'd torn the relevant leaf out before coming up — carefully, close to the binding, so the loss wouldn't show unless a man counted pages, and men like Asque counted everything, which was the point. She passed it over. Doss held it to the moonlight and read it through twice, lips moving, and she watched the same shuffle of expressions cross his face again, ending this time on something she couldn't name. Not anger. Something older and tireder.

"Four hundred crowns for survey work," he murmured. "I've done survey work. Do you know what survey work pays on the open market? A bruise and a thank-you." He laughed, short and bitter, and folded the page with great care, the way he folded losing hands. "Man's been paid more to look at a river bend than I've made in six years."

"Doss."

"I know." He tucked the page inside his coat, into the inner pocket, the one he used for things he didn't intend to show anyone. "I'm just saying it out loud so it stops rattling around. Go to sleep, Runi. I've got the watch."

She hesitated. The page was gone into his coat, and some small practical voice in her head noted that she now had no proof of anything except memory, and memory was worth exactly what people believed. But Doss was Doss — lazy, greedy for eel, soft-hearted under all the bluster — and she'd known him two years, and he'd never once sold out a crewmate for money, mostly because opportunities hadn't arisen, but still.

"Tell me before you tell anyone," she said. "Even Paloma. Promise."

"Promise," he said easily, and waved her off toward the cabins, and went back to his whittling, and the moonlight lay silver on the water, and the ledger slept under its floorboard, one page lighter.

* * *

Runi did not sleep well.

She lay in her bunk listening to the boat talk to itself — the pump singing its cat-song, the timbers ticking as the current leaned on them, Sefa turning over with a grunt two bunks down — and she replayed the evening, hunting for the flaw in her own cleverness, because there was always a flaw, and finding it yourself hurt less than having it found for you.

The flaw arrived around the third hour, quietly, the way flaws do.

Doss had asked *what do you want to do* — and then he'd taken the page. Not photographed it in his memory, not read it and handed it back. Taken it. Put it in the pocket where he kept things he didn't intend to show anyone.

And Doss, for all his virtues, was a man who'd spent his whole life one bad month from broke, and who believed — sincerely, almost religiously — that the difference between himself and the men who'd ruined the coast was simply that nobody had ever offered him enough. He said so often, usually while losing at cards. *I'd sell out anybody for the right price,* he liked to announce, *and the tragedy is nobody's ever tested the theory.* Everyone laughed. It was a bit. Bits were how Doss told the truth.

Runi stared at the ceiling beams and felt the secret shift in her chest, heavier than it had been that morning.

She'd given it to him to share the weight. That was the theory, anyway. But weight shared isn't weight halved — sometimes it's just weight, relocated, and now it sat in the coat pocket of a man on watch alone at the stern of a boat, with a folded page worth more than the vessel it rode on, and a buyer waiting somewhere behind them in the wake.

She got up quietly, stood in the corridor, and considered going back out to the stern. Just to check. Just to see him whittling his bird-or-failure, page safe, world intact.

Through the stern window she could see him, small in the moonlight, feet on the rail. He wasn't whittling. He was sitting very still, and his hand kept going to his inner pocket and resting there, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Runi went back to bed and did not check.

Some things, she told herself, you had to let a man carry until he decided what he was. And she rolled toward the wall and shut her eyes, and told herself the river would sort it out, rivers sorted everything out eventually, and if that was a lie it was at least a comfortable one, and comfort, this far upriver in flood season, was the rarest cargo aboard.

Two days later, at the fishing weir, a skiff with no colors would come alongside asking questions, and Doss's hand would drift toward his inner pocket, and Runi would remember this night — the moon, the stillness, the tongue returning to the broken tooth — and understand exactly when the betrayal had actually happened.

Not at the weir. Here. In the kindness of handing a hungry man something to hold.
