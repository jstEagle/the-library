## 8. Rapids at Kessel Bend

The river told you things the night before it tried to kill you.

Paloma had learned that on her first season diving the Vessarine coast, back when she was sixteen and too poor to be afraid. The water went quiet in a particular way — not calm, but *held*, like a breath taken behind a door — and if you were fool enough to sleep through it, you woke to find the river had rearranged your plans without consulting you.

She stood at the rail of the *Second Chance* an hour before dawn and listened to the held breath of Kessel Bend.

"You feel it too." Sefa came up beside her, wiping grease from her hands onto a rag that was more grease than rag. The engineer nodded downstream, where the dark water slid past with a smoothness that looked innocent and wasn't. "Barometer's been dropping since midnight. And the sound."

"What sound?"

"Exactly." Sefa grinned. "There's no sound. A river that big should be talking in its sleep. When it goes quiet like that, it's standing up somewhere ahead, getting ready to shout."

Behind them, the deck slept in patches: Ilya curled around his charts like a dragon on gold, Runi sprawled upside down in a hammock she'd strung between the mast and the winch because, in her words, sleeping right-side up was for people with boring dreams. Doss snored in the pilot house with one boot off and both hands still wrapped around a cold cup of tea he'd never drink.

And Corvel Asque's cabin lamp burned late, as it did every night now. Paloma had stopped pretending not to notice.

"Nineteen days," she said. "That was the window when we left. We're down to fourteen, and Kessel Bend is the worst water between here and Verren Reach."

"So we go around?"

"There is no around. Not in flood season. There's through, or there's back." She said it lightly, but the words sat heavy in her chest, because she'd caught Asque looking at her two nights ago with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — something between patience and appetite. He wanted the chest. That much was plain. What she couldn't work out was why a man rich enough to fund an expedition would care about a box of old paper unless someone richer had told him to.

Sefa followed her gaze to the lit cabin window. "Your patron burning midnight oil again?"

"He pays for the oil. Let him burn it."

"Mm." Sefa folded her rag with the precision of a woman who organized chaos for a living. "You know what my grandmother used to say? Never trust a man who's generous before breakfast. Generosity before breakfast means he wants something he doesn't want to ask for while you're awake enough to say no."

Paloma laughed despite herself. "Your grandmother sounds like my mother."

"Everyone's grandmother sounds like everyone's mother. It's how they get you." Sefa clapped her shoulder. "I'll have the engine ready by first light. You make sure our river pilot doesn't sleep through her own rapids."

* * *

Runi did not sleep through them. Runi, in fact, met them the way she met everything — standing on the bow with her arms out like she was greeting family.

The bend announced itself as a wall of noise, sudden and total, the way a crowd roars when a door opens onto a stadium. The river dropped four meters over half a kilometer, and every liter of it had opinions. White water stood up in ridges that moved against the current, and boulders that would have shown their heads in a dry year were hidden now, lurking under a meter of brown flood, waiting to open a hull like a letter.

"I count three channels!" Runi shouted back over her shoulder, hair whipping. "Left is fast but clean! Middle is suicide! Right is—"

The *Second Chance* hit the first wave and the rest of Runi's report was lost to the river.

Paloma had the wheel. Sefa had the engine, feeding it just enough power to keep steerage without driving them nose-first into anything solid. Ilya braced himself against the chart table with both arms, watching the banks with a cartographer's eyes, calling depths from memory of surveys forty years old and praying the flood hadn't rewritten them.

"Right channel's got a strainer!" Ilya bellowed. "Fallen trees — whole bank's come down!"

"LEFT!" Paloma hauled the wheel over. "Runi, get off the bow! Get inside!"

But Runi had spotted something Paloma hadn't yet — or thought she had. Later, replaying it a thousand times, Paloma would never be sure whether there'd actually been a swimmer, a shape, a flash of color in the flood, or whether Runi's eyes had simply invented a rescue because Runi's heart ran ahead of her sense the way the flood ran ahead of the rain. All Paloma knew was what happened: the girl pointed hard to starboard, shouted *"Someone's in!"*, and dove.

No rope. No vest. No hesitation. Sixteen years old and gone over the rail into water moving faster than any horse Paloma had ever seen.

The world narrowed.

"Sefa, hold us steady!" Paloma kicked off her boots, looped a line twice around her waist and once around the winch post — Doss, awake now and white-faced, threw her the coil end without being asked, his hands shaking so badly he tied it wrong and then tied it right on top of the wrong knot, which was somehow very Doss — and went over the side after her.

The river took her like a debt collector.

Cold didn't begin to describe it. Flood water in the high country was snowmelt wearing mud as a disguise, and it closed over her head with a force that shoved her down, rolled her, spat her up sideways into air she grabbed with both lungs. She caught one glimpse of the boat — Sefa at the rail paying out line, Ilya shouting bearings, Asque's face in the cabin doorway doing nothing at all — and then the water flipped her again and the world became brown roar.

She found Runi by accident, which is to say by the grace of a boulder. The girl had wedged herself against the upstream face of a submerged rock, arms locked around it, chin barely above water, and when Paloma slammed into the same stone she understood instantly why Runi had chosen it: it was the only thing in the entire bend that wasn't trying to drown them.

"STUPID," Paloma screamed, and the river ate half the word, and she screamed the other half. "BRAVE AND STUPID!"

"There WAS someone—" Runi's teeth were chattering hard enough to hear over the rapids.

"There wasn't! Or there was and they're long gone! Hold on!"

Paloma got an arm under the girl's shoulders and pinned them both against the rock, and for a moment they just breathed, cheek to cheek, while the flood leaned its whole weight against them. Above, distorted by spray, she could see the *Second Chance* fighting its way along the left channel, Sefa's engine screaming, Doss hauling the line hand over hand with Ilya helping, the two of them walking it in like men pulling a net.

The spare air tanks were lashed to the stern rack. Paloma saw them go — saw the line holding them part with a snap she felt rather than heard, saw the two steel cylinders cartwheel once, gleaming, almost beautiful, and vanish into the brown. Four hundred crowns of borrowed equipment. Her stomach turned over. Then Runi's grip slipped on the wet stone and Paloma stopped caring about equipment forever.

They came aboard at the eddy below the bend, hauled over the gunwale in a tangle of line and limbs, and collapsed on the deck in a puddle shaped like a small country. For a long moment nobody spoke. The river roared on behind them, indifferent, precise, already forgetting.

Then Doss dropped to his knees beside them, took one look at Runi's scraped-raw forearms, and burst into tears.

"Oh, don't—" Runi said.

"You absolute — you little — I thought—" Doss pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I've known you eight months and you're the most annoying person alive and I thought I'd watched you die and I don't even LIKE people—"

"Doss." Runi patted his arm with the tenderness of someone comforting a large, damp dog. "You're crying on me."

"I KNOW."

Sefa appeared with blankets. Ilya appeared with tea that was somehow hot, though no one could later explain how, given the state of the galley stove. And Paloma lay on her back watching the sky wheel slowly overhead and felt something settle in her chest that she hadn't felt since she was a child — the specific, unglamorous warmth of being part of a crew that would come in after you. Not because it was smart. Because it was you.

It was worth more than the air tanks. She was almost sure of that. She'd do the arithmetic properly later, when she'd stopped shivering.

* * *

Asque waited until evening, which Paloma had to admit showed discipline.

He came up to the wheelhouse where she stood the night watch, carrying two cups of coffee, and handed her one with the easy grace of a man who had never once entered a room uninvited and never once been asked. Below, faint through the deck, she could hear Runi retelling the day's events to Sefa with escalating heroism — by the third telling, Paloma noticed, Runi herself had acquired a rope and Paloma had acquired a heroic dive from a height of ten meters.

"That girl nearly died today," Asque said.

"Yes."

"And you lost the spare tanks, which I paid for. And we lost six hours running the bend instead of camping above it and waiting out the peak." He sipped his coffee, gazing out at the moonlit water with the expression of a man reviewing a ledger. "I'm not angry, Paloma. I want to be clear about that. I'm asking a question as an investor."

"Ask it."

"Was it worth it?" He turned to her, and his voice was gentle, reasonable, almost kind. "One girl against the expedition. Against the schedule. Against everything we're trying to reach before the flood closes the upper river. You gambled fourteen people's livelihoods — well, seven people's — on a shape in the water that probably wasn't even there."

Paloma drank her coffee and let the silence do some work. Out on the water, a night bird made its flat, mournful call, and somewhere far off thunder muttered over the hills where the rain was being born.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I worked a wreck off Corvane Shoals. My partner went down and didn't come up. His line fouled. I had maybe ninety seconds of air left in my own bottle, and everyone on the boat told me not to go — the skipper, the insurance man, everyone with a stake in the numbers. They were all right, by the way. By the numbers, going down after him was insane." She looked at Asque. "I went. He lived. He has three kids now. One of them dives."

"A touching story."

"It's not a story. It's the answer." She set her cup down on the chart table, deliberately, so the sound of it landed between them. "You're asking me to run this expedition like a ledger. But ledgers are how the Admiralty works, Corvel. Ledgers are how a whole nation gets erased and nobody goes to jail. Every terrible thing that's ever happened on this coast was justified by somebody balancing a column." She stepped closer. "So no. I won't turn back. And if you're looking for a man who'll trade people for cargo, you chartered the wrong boat."

Something moved behind Asque's eyes — quick, unreadable, gone. When he smiled it reached everything except the middle of his face.

"Loyalty," he said softly. "An expensive commodity. Rarer than brass, harder to salvage." He set his untouched coffee beside hers. "Get some sleep, Captain. We've a long river yet."

He went below. Paloma stood at the window and watched the lamplight in his cabin come on again, and burn, and burn.

* * *

Near midnight, Doss found her on deck. He didn't say anything at first — just leaned on the rail beside her, close enough that their elbows touched, which for Doss was practically a speech.

"The tanks," he said finally. "I've been doing sums. If we sell my share of the next two wrecks, and I skip buying a new coat this winter, and Runi stops eating like a dock crane—"

"She won't."

"—we can replace one tank by Verren Reach. Maybe." He picked at a splinter in the rail. "I know what you're going to say. You're going to say it doesn't matter, we'll manage, the crew comes first. And you'd be right, and it's infuriating, because I had a whole speech prepared about fiscal responsibility and now it's ruined."

Paloma smiled at the dark water. "Keep the speech. It'll keep till port."

"Captain?" It was Ilya, at the wheelhouse door, and something in the old cartographer's voice made them both straighten. He held a lantern low, and his face above it was gray. "You should see this. A light, upstream. Behind us."

Paloma took the glass. Far back along the black ribbon of the river, past the bend they'd run that morning, a white light blinked. Once. Twice. Paused. Once. Twice.

Not a patrol boat's running light. Patrol boats didn't blink in patterns.

Patrol boats didn't need to signal each other.

"They're not following us," Ilya said quietly. "They're waiting for us. There's a second light — there, on the near bank. Paloma, that's the old fishing weir ahead. Nobody's manned that weir in thirty years."

The white lights blinked at each other across the water, patient as bookends, and between them the *Second Chance* drifted on toward the narrowing dark.
