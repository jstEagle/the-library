## 9. Kethria's Last Harbor

The river widened the way a held breath widens before it's let go.

Paloma stood at the wheel of the *Second Chance* and watched the banks fall away on either side until they were no longer banks at all but suggestions — green smears through morning mist, the water spreading fat and slow over country it had swallowed decades ago and never bothered to give back.

"Chart says open water," Ilya said beside her, tapping the old survey he'd been guarding like a prayer book since Kessel Bend. "But charts lie about this stretch. Everyone knows it. Nobody says it."

"Everyone knows, nobody says," Runi repeated from the rail, swinging her bare feet. "That's the navy's favorite song."

"It's everyone's favorite song," Doss said from the deck where he was coiling line with the exaggerated care of a man avoiding conversation. "It's how you stay alive out here."

Paloma said nothing. She'd learned that much in forty years of diving these waters: silence was currency, and she'd spent most of her life hoarding it. But the chest sat below in the hold, lead-lined and patient, and every mile upriver the silence felt less like savings and more like debt.

Sefa's engine muttered contentedly beneath them — she'd rebuilt half of it since the rapids, using parts cannibalized from things no engine should legally contain — and the boat pushed on into the widening water.

It was Runi who saw it first, because Runi always saw things first when they were underwater-shaped.

"There." She pointed, not at anything above the surface but at a shadow beneath it, long and straight as a rule drawn by God. "That's not a reef."

Paloma cut the engine and let the boat drift. The mist thinned. And there, under six feet of glass-green floodwater, lay a street.

Not a wreck. A street. Cobbled, running arrow-straight into the murk, lined on both sides by the dark shapes of doorways. A lamppost stood at the corner like a man waiting for a tram that would never come again.

"Well," Doss said finally. "That's not right."

"That's Harrow Quay," Ilya said quietly.

Everyone looked at him.

"I surveyed it. Before." He didn't say *before what*. He didn't have to. "It was a town of eleven thousand people. Fishing, river trade, a famous bell foundry. It was on every map."

"And now?"

"Now it's on none of them."

* * *

They anchored over the drowned town because there was nowhere else to anchor, and because nobody could make themselves leave. That was the honest reason, though Sefa gave a practical one — "current's calmest here, might as well check the hull" — and practical reasons were easier to hold than true ones.

Paloma went down first.

The water was cold in the way deep places are cold, holding winter even in flood season. She dropped along the anchor line and the light changed around her, gold to green to a blue so old it felt like memory, and then the town rose up to meet her.

Harrow Quay was intact. Not ruined — intact. The flood had come fast, forty years ago, faster than anyone could run, and whatever had happened after, the water had done its work gently. Shopfronts still stood with their painted signs legible under a skin of silt. A child's hoop leaned against a doorstep. In a bakery window, the shapes of loaves had long since dissolved, but the trays remained, ranked and waiting, as if the baker had only stepped into the back room.

Paloma hung in the water above the street and felt something crack in her chest that she hadn't known was load-bearing.

She had dived wrecks all her life. Wrecks were honest. A ship died fighting or failing, and you read its death in the break of its hull, and you took its brass and its truth and you left. But this wasn't a death. This was a pause. Eleven thousand people had lived here, and the water had closed over their lives mid-sentence, and for forty years the whole nation had agreed not to finish reading.

Her dive light swept across a wall and stopped.

Paint. Under the silt, paint — bright, defiant, enormous. She kicked closer and brushed the wall with a gloved hand, and color bloomed up through the grey like a bruise in reverse: red, ochre, a blue so vivid it hurt.

A mural. A whole wall of mural, running the length of a building that had once been — she wiped more silt away — a guildhall, by the crest above the door.

She was down there a long time. When she surfaced, four faces were crowded at the rail, and none of them pretended they hadn't been counting the minutes.

"There's a town down there," she said. Her voice came out strange, echoing off the water. "Whole streets. Shops. And walls covered in paintings."

"Paintings of what?" Runi asked.

Paloma looked at her crew — Doss with his arms crossed, Sefa already calculating air supply in her head, Ilya gone pale and very still, Runi leaning so far over the rail she was in danger of joining the town.

"Come see," Paloma said. "Bring lights. All of them."

* * *

They went down in pairs, which was against every safety rule Paloma owned, and she broke them anyway, because some things you don't ask people to witness alone.

The guildhall mural ran thirty feet. They lit it section by section, four dive lights cutting cones through the green, and the story assembled itself out of the dark the way stories do — reluctantly, then all at once.

The first panels were ordinary joy: a market day, fish stalls and fruit carts, children racing paper boats down a gutter, the bell foundry pouring bronze in a fountain of orange sparks. Kethrian faces, broad-cheeked and laughing, painted with such affection that Paloma could almost hear them.

Then the painter's hand changed.

The next panel showed granaries — doors shut, chained, stamped with a seal Paloma recognized with a lurch, because she'd spent three weeks staring at its twin on a lead-lined chest. The Kethrian seal, but inverted, defaced, painted being nailed over by men in naval uniform.

The panel after that showed the fields. Empty. Then the queues. Long, patient, terrible queues of people outside buildings marked with the same seal, and the painter had given every face in those queues a name — tiny letters above each head, hundreds of them, a census of the starving rendered in gold leaf.

"He signed them," Ilya said. His voice came distorted through his regulator, muffled and shaking. "He painted their names. So they couldn't be made anonymous."

The final panel was the largest, and someone had painted it last, after the rest, in colors mixed with something darker than pigment. It showed a long table. Admirals in dress uniform, twelve of them, seated behind documents, and above each figure the painter had written a name in letters a foot high.

Ilya's light moved down the row of names and stopped.

Paloma watched him stop. Watched his shoulders go rigid inside his harness. She'd seen men freeze at depth before — nitrogen fear, pressure panic — but this was different. This was recognition.

"Ilya?" she said against his mask.

He turned his light away from the wall, toward her, and even through the distortion of two masks and six feet of green water, she saw that he was crying, which shouldn't be possible at twenty feet down and was happening anyway.

"The third name," he managed. "Read the third name."

She swam closer. The letters were bold, imperial, the kind of lettering that expects to be carved in stone:

**ADMIRAL E. VOSSE-KEHL, LORD OF THE ADMIRALTY OFFICE.**

Paloma knew that name. Every soul on the Vessarine coast knew that name. It was on the naval recruitment posters in every harbor town. It was on the memorial at Port Callow. Lord Vosse-Kehl, hero of the Reclamation, father of the modern fleet — whose grandson held the same office today, whose name headed every naval charter, whose signature authorized the salvage permits Paloma scraped her living from.

"He signed the famine," she said. The words came out flat, absurd, too large for the space between them. "Forty years ago. He signed it, and they painted it here, and the water kept it, and—" She stopped, because the sentence ahead of her was one she couldn't take back, and once spoken it would change everything: the chest, the money, Asque's generous smile, the nineteen days, all of it.

Doss saved her from it, in his way.

"So the admirals starved them," he said slowly, "and then flooded them, and then erased them, and now they pay divers like us to keep quiet about it." He laughed, one short ugly bark that fogged his mask. "And we wondered why the patrol boat wouldn't hail us."

Nobody answered. The mural glowed in their lights. Somewhere above, faintly, Sefa's engine ticked as it cooled, keeping its own kind of time.

Runi drifted to the last panel's edge, where the painter had left one final flourish — a small figure apart from the admirals, a woman with a diver's helmet under her arm, standing at the edge of rising water, looking back at the town. Beneath her, in the painter's careful gold:

*WE WERE HERE. THE RIVER REMEMBERS EVEN WHEN YOU DO NOT.*

"She knew," Runi said softly. "Whoever painted this — she knew the water was coming. She painted it for us."

"For whoever came looking," Paloma corrected. "She just couldn't know it would take forty years."

* * *

They surfaced into late afternoon and nobody spoke for a while, which for a crew like theirs was its own kind of ceremony. Sefa made tea without being asked, strong and over-sweetened, the way you brew tea for shock. Doss drank his and stared at nothing. Runi sat wrapped in a blanket writing in her little waterproof notebook, fast, like the names in the mural might fade if she didn't copy some of them out.

Ilya sat apart at the stern, chart case across his knees, hands folded on it like a man at a funeral.

Paloma brought him a cup and sat down beside him.

"You knew the town," she said. Not a question.

"I surveyed it." His voice was rough. "Nineteen years ago. Hydrographic office contract. We mapped the submerged structures for — I was told it was for navigation safety." He laughed, hollow. "I was young. I believed the paperwork. I measured their streets and catalogued their bell foundry and filed my report, and my report went into a drawer, and I told myself that was the end of it."

"And now you've seen what your drawer was sitting on top of."

"Yes." He looked at her, and there was something in his face she'd never seen there before — not the careful blankness of an ex-naval man, but the naked face underneath. "Paloma. The chest. Whatever's in that chest — I think I need to know. Even if knowing is the thing that gets us killed."

She studied him. Around them the river lay golden and innocent, and beneath them eleven thousand people waited in the dark with their names painted in gold leaf, patient as only the dead can afford to be.

"We open it tonight," she said. "All of us. No secrets below decks anymore — that's how they win, Ilya. One secret at a time, until everyone's carrying everyone else's ballast."

From the bow, Doss snorted. "Speak for yourself. I've got at least three secrets and they're all charming."

"Doss."

"Fine. Two charming, one relevant." He drained his tea and stood, stretching. "Well. If we're opening the dead nation's mail, somebody should check the hold isn't flooded. Sefa, with me."

They went below together, and Paloma stayed on deck a moment longer, watching the light die over Harrow Quay. The lamppost's tip broke the surface near the anchor line, black against the sunset, and she thought: *forty years, and it's still standing watch.*

Then Runi appeared at her elbow, notebook clutched to her chest, wearing the particular expression she wore when she'd found something she wasn't supposed to find.

"Paloma? When we open the chest — there's something you should know first." She glanced toward the hold, lowered her voice. "About Asque. I found papers. Weeks ago. And I think — I think he was paying the Admiralty before we ever found the chest at all."

The river lapped against the hull. Below them, a drowned town kept its vigil, and somewhere in the dark of the hold, a lead-lined chest waited with the patience of everything that had already survived the worst.

"Show me," Paloma said.
