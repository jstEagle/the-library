## 10. The Inheritance Clause

Ilya had not deciphered anything in thirty years that mattered, and he found he'd missed it the way a man misses an old coat — not the wearing of it, exactly, but the weight of it on his shoulders.

The deed came out of the chest's false bottom at dusk, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark. Paloma handed it to him without ceremony, which he appreciated. Doss would have made a speech about it. Runi would have wanted to hold it first and probably lick it.

"Cartographer's hands," Paloma said, by way of explanation. "You're the only one aboard who reads old script without moving his lips."

"My lips moved for years," Ilya said. "Naval cartography school. They beat the habit out of us between lessons on how to leave things off maps."

She almost smiled. She'd been almost smiling for three days now, ever since Kethria's Last Harbor, and Ilya had learned to read those almosts the way he read coastlines — by what was hidden just beneath the surface of them.

He took the deed to the stern rail, where the lamplight from the cabin window reached but the conversation didn't, and unrolled it flat against the chart table Sefa had bolted there two expeditions ago. The paper was Kethrian rag-stock, heavy as sailcloth, and the ink had been fixed with something that still smelled faintly of resin when he bent close. Forty years underwater in a lead-lined box, and the words stood up off the page like they were waiting for him.

*Well,* he thought. *Aren't you smug.*

He worked until midnight. It was slow going, not because the script was difficult — Kethrian civil hand was a cousin of the Vessarine merchant alphabet, and Ilya had spent his youth annotating charts in three alphabets before breakfast — but because every few lines he had to stop, put his palms flat on the table, and breathe.

*Clause One.* The bearer of this deed, being named herein as *whosoever carries it in good faith to a neutral archive*, held legal claim to the Kethrian Reparations Fund: monies escrowed in the year of the Fall under treaty guarantee, frozen pending the recognition of a legitimate claimant.

*Clause Two.* The fund's principal. He read the figure four times. Then he copied it out onto scrap paper in plain numerals, because his eyes kept refusing to accept it in the old notation, and even in plain numerals it looked like a joke someone had written very seriously.

Enough to buy fleets. Not fishing fleets. Not the sad coastal patrol boats the Vessarine navy called a fleet. Fleets like the ones in the history books, the ones nations built when they intended to be nations for a long time.

*Clause Three.* Distribution upon claim: one-fifth to the bearer, four-fifths to be administered for the surviving descendants of Kethria, per registry attached.

Ilya sat back. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere forward, Sefa was arguing in her sleep with an engine that had wronged her.

One-fifth. His mind, trained since boyhood to divide coastlines into manageable segments, did the arithmetic without being asked. One-fifth of that number was more money than the Admiralty Office spent in a decade. More than Corvel Asque's whole shipping concern, probably. More than any salvage crew could spend in forty lifetimes of honest diving, and they were not, if he was honest, an entirely honest salvage crew.

And four-fifths — four-fifths belonged to people who had been scattered, renamed, erased. Descendants who mostly didn't know they were descendants. Children and grandchildren of a nation the maps had agreed to forget, living in river towns and port slums under other flags, never told why their grandmothers went quiet whenever the navy's white hulls passed.

Ilya knew why the admirals' names in the murals at the drowned town had made his stomach drop. He'd drafted coastline surveys for some of those men. Young Lieutenant Ilya Vennerick, neat as a pin, inking in depth soundings while ashore the granaries emptied and the ledgers were burned. He had told himself for thirty years that he hadn't known what the soundings were *for*. Tonight, holding the deed, he ran out of ways to keep telling himself that.

"Still up?"

Runi, barefoot, hair a wet rope down her back, materialized at the rail the way she always did — no footsteps, no warning, just suddenly present, like weather.

"It's past your berth time," Ilya said.

"It's past *your* berth time, old man." She leaned over the table, squinting at the deed. "That the dead-nation paper? What's it say? Is it a treasure map? Please tell me it's a treasure map."

"It's a bank statement," Ilya said, "for a country that doesn't exist."

Runi considered this. "Is the country rich?"

"The country was rich," Ilya said. "Then its own admirals starved it and sank the evidence." He heard himself say it plainly for the first time, aloud, to another person, and was surprised at how little the sky fell.

Runi whistled low. Then, with the terrible practicality of the young: "So who gets the money?"

"People who don't know it exists."

"Huh." She chewed on that. "My gran says money you don't know about is the only kind that can't ruin you." She tapped the deed once, reverently, with one finger, as though it might bite. "Don't stay up all night. You get crooked when you're tired. Crookeder."

She padded off toward the bow. Ilya watched her go and thought about clause registries, and about a girl who ferried strangers through flood-season shallows for coins, and about how many Runis there must be downstream of a number like the one sitting on his chart table.

* *

He didn't sleep. He transcribed.

By grey dawn he had a fair copy of the full deed in modern Vessarine, every clause, every seal reference, every date cross-checked against the naval almanacs he still carried out of habit and self-punishment. The dates were the worst part. The fund had been escrowed eleven days before the official record said Kethria fell to "internal collapse." Eleven days. Somebody had known precisely when the nation would die, and had taken the trouble to put its money somewhere safe first, and then to write instructions for giving it back.

Somebody had also, presumably, been murdered for it, or fled, or drowned, and the deed had ended up in a lead box on a forbidden wreck where the Admiralty hoped tides and silence would do the rest.

Paloma found him at first light, two mugs of Sefa's tar-thick coffee in hand. She set one down beside the transcription without asking what it was, read the top line upside down, and went very still.

"That's the fund?"

"That's the fund."

"And that number's real."

"I checked the notation three times. I checked it a fourth time hoping I was wrong."

"Why would you want to be wrong?"

"Because numbers like that get people killed, Captain. Small numbers get people robbed. Big numbers get people disappeared." He slid the transcription toward her. "Read clause three. It matters."

She read. He watched her face do what his had done: the flicker of greed, quickly ashamed; the longer, deeper ache of the four-fifths; and finally the narrowing of the eyes that meant Paloma Oyelaran had stopped feeling and started planning.

"One-fifth to the bearer," she said slowly.

"If the bearer carries it in good faith to a neutral archive. Verren Reach qualifies, under the old treaties. Barely. Technically. There's a footnote."

"There's always a footnote."

"This one says claims must be presented before the fund's custodial banks may lawfully transfer it elsewhere — 'elsewhere' meaning the Admiralty has spent forty years trying to have it declared abandoned property. If we're late, or if we're caught, the money goes to the men who stole it in the first place." He turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table. "There's also this. The deed names no individual. 'Whosoever carries it.' Which means—"

"Whoever holds it owns the claim," Paloma finished. Her voice had gone quiet in the way that made the whole boat quiet around her. "Not me. Not the crew. Whoever's holding the paper when it reaches Verren Reach."

"Yes."

She looked at the river. Ilya watched her understand it — really understand it — that the most dangerous thing aboard the Second Chance wasn't the flood season or the patrol boats. It was the fact that any hand on this deed became, instantly, worth a fortune and a funeral.

"We tell the crew," she said at last. "All of it. Nobody makes decisions about this kind of money in the dark."

"Even Asque?"

Her jaw tightened. "Especially Asque. He paid for this expedition. He'll want to know what he paid for." A pause. "And I want to watch his face when he hears the number."

* *

Corvel Asque's face, when he heard the number, was a masterpiece.

They gathered in the cabin after breakfast — Paloma, Ilya, Doss, Sefa, Runi perched on the engine housing she wasn't supposed to sit on — and Ilya laid the transcription on the table and read the clauses aloud in his flattest surveyor's voice, because drama was Paloma's department and accuracy was his. When he reached the figure, he simply said it, the way you'd report a sounding. No emphasis. Emphasis would have been mercy.

Sefa swore with genuine admiration, the way she swore at particularly elegant machinery. Doss sat down on a crate that wasn't there and hit the deck, which broke the tension better than any speech could have. Runi said, "Told you it was a treasure map," to nobody, vindicated.

Asque did none of these things. Asque went smooth.

It happened visibly — Ilya had spent a career reading officers' faces across briefing tables, deciding what to leave off maps, and he saw the calculation move through Asque like current under ice. Surprise, mastered. Recalculation. And then warmth, deployed like canvas in fresh wind.

"Well," Asque said, spreading his clean hands. "Well. Paloma. My dear captain. Do you understand what you're carrying?"

"A great deal of trouble," Paloma said.

"A great deal of *history*," Asque corrected gently. "And a great deal of money, which is history's usual disguise." He laughed — a small, confiding laugh, man-to-man, inviting the room into the joke. "Forgive me. I funded this expedition to recover artifacts. Salvage rights, museum pieces, a modest return. Instead my captain has hauled up the estate of a murdered nation." He shook his head, marveling. "You could not have planned it better."

"No," Paloma said. "Nobody planned it at all. That's rather the point of it."

"Of course, of course." Asque paced two steps, hands clasped behind him — Ilya recognized the posture; admirals did it when they were about to reassign someone. "But consider the practicalities. That fund has been frozen for forty years because the Admiralty has lawyers older than our boat. The moment we surface anywhere near a port with a telegraph office, they will know. They will bury the claim in procedure, and bury us in something less procedural." He stopped, and turned, and his voice dropped into the register men use for offering cigars. "Unless the claim is presented by someone they can't afford to bury."

"Meaning you," Sefa said flatly.

"Meaning *my name*, my standing, my counsel in Verren Reach — yes." Asque smiled at her without a trace of offense. "I'm a civilian financier with imperial contacts, Miss Sefa. I am expensive to disappear. Whereas a salvage diver holding a piece of paper is—" he spread his hands apologetically, "—buoyant, but brief."

"And your price?" Paloma asked.

Asque's smile widened a fraction. Here it comes, Ilya thought. Watch the hands.

"A partnership. Reasonable terms. The bearer's fifth, divided fairly among the risk-takers — yourself generously foremost, naturally, and a share each for this fine crew, they've earned it. In exchange, the deed travels in my custody, under my seal, presented through my solicitors. Everyone walks away rich. Everyone walks away *alive*." He let that last word hang, then added, softly, the blade sliding in under the silk: "Or — forgive the bluntness — Captain keeps the paper, keeps the danger, and I keep my ledger balanced some other way. I'd hate that. Genuinely. I've grown fond of this floating family of yours."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the river kept on doing what rivers do, indifferent as arithmetic.

Ilya watched Paloma. He expected anger — he'd seen her angry at wrecks, at weather, at Doss's cooking. But what crossed her face instead was slower and sadder: the look of a woman watching a trap she'd half-expected close gently around her, and noting, with a professional's eye, how well-made it was.

"You knew," she said. "Before you hired us. You knew what the chest was."

Asque's pause was a masterpiece too. "Captain—"

"The payments, Corvel. Don't insult both of us at once. Did the Admiralty send you, or do you freelance?"

For one heartbeat — Ilya counted it, he counted everything, it was his curse — Asque's smoothness cracked, and underneath it was something quick and cold and quite unlike a shipping magnate. Then the silk came back down over the mechanism.

"I know people," he said, "who know things. It's how finance works. But consider, truly consider: does it matter who I answer to, if the outcome is the same? Four-fifths to Kethria's ghosts. A fifth to the living. Riches, distributed, justice done, everyone compensated for their trouble." He gestured at the transcription, at the deed, at the whole impossible morning. "You can be righteous or you can be effective, Captain. On this river, in this season, with gunboats behind us — you cannot be both."

Paloma picked up the transcription. Folded it, unhurried, along Ilya's creases. Put it inside her coat, against her chest, where a heart would be if she were the sentimental type.

"Here's my counteroffer," she said. "The deed stays on my body, in this boat, under my command. You're welcome to travel with us to Verren Reach and watch a free crew do an honest thing. And your cut, Corvel, is the satisfaction of having funded it."

Asque looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed again — warmly, easily, a man delighted by spirit — and clapped his hands once.

"Splendid! Refused like a heroine in a ballad. Very well, Captain. We proceed together to Verren Reach, and we shall see which of us the river agrees with." He bowed slightly to the room, to the crew, to the folded paper he couldn't see. "Do sleep well, all of you. Floods wait for no one."

He left the cabin whistling.

Nobody spoke until his footsteps faded up the deck. Then Sefa said, "We should throw him overboard," and Doss said, "He'd float, the bastard's mostly ledger," and Runi laughed, and the tension broke the way it does on small boats, reluctantly and temporarily.

But Ilya stayed at the table, looking at the ring left on the wood by Asque's coffee cup, and thought about the heartbeat of coldness he'd seen beneath the silk, and about the phrase *some other way*, and about how a man balances a ledger when the asset refuses to be bought.

He thought about gunboats. He thought about the nineteen days, now sixteen, now fewer.

And he thought: *he isn't going to wait for Verren Reach.*

Up on deck, Asque was speaking quietly to the pilot — not their pilot, but the hired extra, the broad silent man Asque had brought aboard at Kessel Bend who never ate with the crew and whose kit bag, Ilya now recalled with a cartographer's precision for details that arrive too late, was the wrong shape for luggage.

The right shape, in fact, for a short-barreled boat gun.

Ilya rose, stiff-kneed, and went to find Paloma, and the river carried them all northward toward the crest of the flood, at a speed nobody aboard could any longer control.
