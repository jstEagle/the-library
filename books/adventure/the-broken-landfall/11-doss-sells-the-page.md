## 11. Doss Sells the Page

Doss had always believed that a man's conscience was like his appendix — something you carried around without noticing until it went bad. He'd gone forty-one years without so much as a twinge. He'd cheated at cards, shorted weights on salvage shares, told Paloma the engine parts were "nearly new" when they'd been fished out of a wreck older than both of them, and slept like a stone every night of it.

So it came as a genuine surprise, three days after he pocketed Runi's ledger page, to find himself lying awake in his bunk listening to the river slap against the hull as if it were knocking.

He got up. Made tea on the little spirit stove because making noise was better than making thoughts. Sat in the galley with the mug going cold in his hands and tried to work out what exactly was wrong with him.

It wasn't the money. The contractor skiff had paid well — passage out of river country and a purse of coin that would keep him fed for a year in any port that didn't ask questions. It wasn't fear. Doss had been afraid plenty of times and knew its flavor; this wasn't it. This was more like the feeling of having eaten something that disagreed with him, except the something had happened four days ago and the disagreement was only now arriving.

*Guilt,* said a voice in his head that sounded suspiciously like his late mother. *That's called guilt, love.*

"Don't be ridiculous," Doss said aloud, to an empty galley, which did not help.

* * *

The trouble, he decided, was that he hadn't meant for it to happen the way it happened.

When Runi had come to him — pale, whispering, holding the page she'd torn from Asque's ledger like it might bite her — Doss had done the sensible thing. He'd taken it from her. "You shouldn't have this," he'd told her, gravely, in his best responsible-crewman voice. "Give it to me. I'll deal with it."

And then, alone in the hold with the page in his hands, he had read it.

*Admiralty Office — retainer, quarterly — C. Asque.* Dated eleven months before Paloma ever pulled that lead-lined chest out of the mud. Eleven months before anyone alive knew Kethria's seal still existed anywhere but in old men's nightmares.

Doss was not a clever man, or at least he'd built a comfortable life on people believing he wasn't, but even he could do that arithmetic. Asque hadn't funded the expedition because Paloma found the chest. Asque had funded the expedition because he already knew the chest was there. They weren't salvagers on this trip. They were retrievers. Tools. And tools, Doss knew from long experience with broken engines and discarded dive gear, got used up and left on the bottom.

So he'd kept the page. Told Runi he was "looking after it." Told himself the same. And every day since, the page had sat folded in his breast pocket, over his heart, where it weighed approximately nothing and approximately forty pounds, depending on the hour.

The skiff had come two nights ago. A navy contractor boat, running dark, no lights but a single green lantern swung twice — the signal Doss had arranged through a cousin of a cousin who worked the fuel docks at Halfway Weir. Doss had slipped over the rail while the rest of the crew slept, huddled under an oilskin in the skiff's stern, and made his trade: the page for passage, plus coin, plus the man's word that nobody would ask Doss Venn anything ever again.

"Smart," the contractor had said, counting coins into Doss's palm. His name was Brack; he had a face like a closed ledger himself. "Most of them want to be heroes right up until the water gets cold."

"I'm not most of them," Doss had said, and it had felt true, and that had been the whole problem, apparently.

The skiff put him ashore at a stilt village called Marrow's Rest, half a day behind the Second Chance, with instructions to wait. Passage out was coming. A supply barge, southbound, two days. In the meantime: coin, a dry room above a fish-smoking shed, and freedom.

Freedom smelled like smoked fish and felt like nothing at all.

* * *

On the first morning, Doss did what he always did when a plan came together: he celebrated. There was a tavern on stilts over the water, the kind of place where the floorboards had gaps you could see the river through, and the beer was served in mugs that had survived more wrecks than Doss ever had. He bought a round for the room. He told a story about a crocodile and a customs officer that got better with each telling. He tipped extravagantly. He was, by any measure, a free man enjoying his freedom.

By the second mug, a thought arrived uninvited: *Runi is sixteen years old, and you took the one thing she trusted you with.*

He ordered a third mug to drown the thought. The thought could swim.

By evening, the thought had brought friends. *Paloma pulled you out of the water off Corren Shoals when your own line fouled. She dove down with her last breath of air and cut you loose, and you never even thanked her properly, you just said "we're square" and she laughed and said "we're never square, Doss, that's what a crew is."*

He'd laughed too, that day. On the deck of the Second Chance, coughing up river water, wrapped in a blanket Sefa had heated by the engine housing because Sefa noticed things like cold crewmates and fixed them without being asked. Ilya had checked his hands for the bends with the fussy precision of a man who'd once watched a cartographer die of something preventable. Runi — who'd been thirteen then, all elbows and opinions — had made him tea so strong it stripped paint, because that was the only kind she knew how to make, and had sat with him until he drank it.

They were never square. That was the whole point of them. Nobody aboard the Second Chance had clean accounts. Everybody owed everybody, forever, in the particular currency of people who'd chosen each other over every sensible alternative.

And Doss had sold a piece of their safety for a purse of coin and a seat on a barge.

"No," he told the ceiling of his rented room, firmly. "I sold a *page*. One page. Paper. It doesn't even prove anything — it proves Asque was paid, not that we know anything. They can't hurt the crew with it. It's practically harmless."

The ceiling declined to comment, which Doss chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

The second morning, he went looking for distraction and found the fish-smoking shed instead, and the shed's owner, a wide woman named Omma who talked the way rivers run — constantly, in all directions, carrying everything along with her.

"You're the one waiting on the southbound," she said, handing him a rack of fish to turn. Not a question. Small villages on the river had one economy and no privacy.

"That's me. Passage to the coast."

"Running from something?"

"Running *to* something," Doss said. "A beach. A hammock. A complete absence of flooded rapids."

Omma snorted and fed another log to the smoke. "My nephew ran to a beach once. Lasted four months. Says the trouble with a quiet life is there's nowhere to hide from yourself." She turned fish with the ease of long practice. "River folk, we don't get quiet lives. We get weather."

"I've had enough weather."

"Mm." She looked at him sideways, and Doss had the distinct sensation of being read like a shallow draft chart — everything visible, nothing flattering. "You're the one who was on the salvage boat. The one going up to Verren Reach."

Word traveled faster than boats on the Vessarine. Of course it did. Everyone knew about the Second Chance by now — the mad salvage crew racing the flood season, the rich patron, the navy sniffing their wake.

"They say the diver woman found something belonging to Kethria," Omma went on, conversationally, turning the rack. "Old country. Gone before my mother was grown. My grandmother used to sing their songs. Said they were farmers mostly, and good neighbors, till the famine came." She paused. "Funny thing about that famine. Grandmother always said the granaries upstream were full the whole time. Full, and locked, and guarded."

Doss turned his rack of fish very carefully.

"The admirals who signed the ration orders back then," Omma said, "have grandsons with the same names running the same office now. But I expect a fellow running to a hammock doesn't care much about history."

"It's not that I don't care," Doss heard himself say, and then stopped, because the sentence had a destination he didn't want to reach.

Omma waited. She was good at waiting. You had to be, smoking fish.

"The chest has papers in it," Doss said finally. "Proof. That's what the patron wants. That's what the navy wants. And there's a deed — a claim — that legally belongs to whoever carries it home. Whoever *bears* it." He laughed, short and ugly. "Paloma's carrying it. Do you understand? She could walk into Verren Reach and walk out owning enough money to buy the whole river. Fleets. Harbors. Everything. And she talks about it like it's a hot coal. Like it's a *burden*. And I—" 

He stopped again.

"And you took money from the people who drowned them," Omma said, gently, the way you'd set down something breakable.

Doss stood there among the smoke and the drying fish and felt the full weight of the last four days arrive all at once, late, the way everything important arrived in his life — courage, regret, the understanding of other people's feelings, all of it showing up after the moment had passed, tapping him on the shoulder like a passenger who'd missed his stop.

"Yeah," he said. "I did."

* * *

Here is what Doss had expected to feel when he watched the contractor skiff pull away into the dark: triumph. Relief. The clean lightness of a man who has finally gotten off a sinking ship. He'd rehearsed it, almost — standing at the rail, coin in pocket, watching the green lantern shrink to a spark and vanish, thinking *suckers, all of you, staying aboard to drown for someone else's war.*

Instead he had felt the way you feel stepping off a boat onto a dock that isn't moving when the boat still is. Wrong-footed. Seasick on solid ground. The skiff's wake had slapped against the pilings and Doss had stood there in the dark listening to it, and what he'd felt, underneath everything, was the specific loneliness of a man who has just voluntarily left the only family that ever pretended to tolerate him.

He'd told himself it would pass. Feelings always passed. That was the beauty of them.

But this one hadn't passed. It had settled in, unpacked, and started rearranging the furniture.

That second evening, sitting on the edge of the stilt dock with his legs over the black water, Doss took out the purse of coin and counted it. Then counted it again. Then held a coin up to the last of the light, and saw, stamped in the silver, the crest of the Admiralty Office — the same crest, near enough, that had been burned into the side of the chest Paloma had pulled from the mud. The same crest that had been on the scuttle charges, if you believed Ilya's silences. The same crest whose admirals' names headed the navy that had let a nation starve with full granaries.

The coin was pretty. It caught the light nicely. It was also, Doss realized, the exact shape and weight of thirty pieces of something, and he was not the first man in history to be paid in it for pointing at a friend.

"Right," Doss said to the river. "Right. Fine."

The river said nothing. Rivers are worse than ceilings that way.

But something had shifted, and Doss recognized the feeling from the inside of engines, from the moment when a thing that has been jammed suddenly comes free — not fixed, necessarily, but moving. He had spent four days telling himself the story that made him comfortable: *one page, harmless paper, they can't hurt anyone with it.* And the story had a crack in it now, and through the crack he could see the true shape of what he'd done.

The page proved Asque was Admiralty-paid. Which meant the page, in the wrong hands, was worth more than coin. It was leverage. It was the thread that, pulled, unraveled the whole arrangement — Asque, the expedition, the reason a contractor skiff was shadowing a salvage boat upriver in flood season. Doss had handed that thread to the navy. And the navy didn't buy information to admire it. They bought it to act on it, and the acting, when it came, would come at the Second Chance, at the weir, at some narrow bend of the river where a boat couldn't turn.

Wherever Paloma was tonight, she was sleeping on a boat that was being hunted with Doss's help, and she didn't know it, and she'd cut him loose once with her own last breath of air, and he'd repaid it with a folded page and a purse of admiral's silver.

He turned the coin over in his fingers one last time. Then he flipped it out into the black water, and listened to it go, and heard nothing, because a single coin makes no sound entering a river, and somehow that was the loudest thing he'd ever heard.

* * *

He knocked on Omma's door at dawn.

She opened it already dressed, unsurprised, holding two cups of coffee as though she'd been expecting him since midnight. "Changed your mind about the hammock."

"The hammock can wait." Doss rubbed his face with both hands. "Omma. I need to get word upriver. To the Second Chance. Tonight, if possible. Faster than a barge, faster than anything."

"To tell them what?"

Doss looked at the water, then at her, and made himself say it plainly, because he'd spent his whole life saying things sideways and it had finally, at forty-one, caught up with him.

"To tell them I sold them out. And that the navy knows where they'll be. There's a fishing weir on the upper channel — old one, half-collapsed, everyone uses it as a landmark. If I were setting an ambush for a boat coming upriver ahead of the floods, that's where I'd set it. Shallow approaches, one deep channel, high banks. Perfect killing ground for a boarding action." He swallowed. "And I think that's where they'll be hit. Because of me."

Omma set down the coffees. "You understand what you're asking. River folk don't carry messages against the contractors lightly. My nephew ferried one letter for the resistance in the bad years and lost two fingers to the lesson."

"I'm not asking them to fight anyone. I'm asking them to pass a warning. And I'm asking you to help me do it fast, because whatever else I am, I'm the reason they need one." He hesitated, then reached into his shirt and brought out the rest of the purse — the admiral's silver, less one coin at the bottom of the river. "And I need to get back to that boat. Before the weir. Before it happens. Whatever they do to me when I get there, I'd rather it happen with me facing them than with me reading about it later."

Omma studied him for a long moment, the way you'd study a hull before trusting it with cargo.

"You know they might not forgive you," she said. "River folk forgive slow. Some things not at all."

"I know." Doss found, to his considerable surprise, that this was true, and that it didn't change anything. "I'm not going back to be forgiven. I'm going back because Runi gave me that page because she trusted me, and Paloma cut my line with her own air, and Sefa warmed blankets, and Ilya checks hands for the bends like it matters, because it does matter, because we were never square, none of us, and I walked out on the only account I ever wanted to keep open." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Also, frankly, the hammock sounded boring."

Something in Omma's face eased. "There's a boy runs a pirogue up the channel at first light. Fastest thing on this stretch of water, and he owes me for a winter of smoked fish. He'll take you as far as the Split. After that you're on your own." She pressed the coffees into his hands, both of them. "Drink those. You look like a man who hasn't slept honest in a week."

"I haven't," Doss said. "But I think I might tonight. First time in four days."

He drank the coffee scalding, standing on her step, watching the mist lift off the river. Somewhere upstream, past the bends and the drowned towns and the water rising day by day, the Second Chance was pushing north with a hole in its knowledge shaped exactly like him. By tomorrow, if the river allowed, he'd be standing on her deck with the truth in his mouth and no idea whether it would save them or only make him feel better saying it.

Probably both. Probably neither. Doss had learned, in forty-one years of arriving everywhere late, that you went anyway.

The pirogue boy pushed off at first light.
