## 12. Contractors at the Weir

The old fishing weir announced itself before they saw it — a long brown seam of broken stakes stretching across the river like the spine of something that had died mid-swim. Paloma had known it was there since Runi pointed it out on the chart the night before, tapping the faded ink twice and saying, in the tone of someone reporting weather, "Bad place. Grandmother says the water lies there."

"The water lies everywhere," Sefa had said.

"Not everywhere," Runi said. "Just where people used to make their living off it."

Paloma thought about that a long time after everyone had turned in. She sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low, watching the river run black and swollen past the bow, and she thought about how every drowned thing on this coast had once been somebody's livelihood. The wrecks she dove were ships first and ruins second. The towns under the floodwater had been kitchens and schoolrooms. Even Kethria, the name nobody said aloud, had been a country full of ordinary mornings before it became a silence.

And now here came the navy's contractors, riding up that same river to make sure the silence held.

She'd seen the skiff at dusk. Two of them, actually — low, fast, grey-green hulls with their running lights doused, holding back half a mile astern the way a shark holds behind a swimmer who hasn't noticed yet. She hadn't told the crew. Not out of any noble instinct to spare them; Paloma had learned long ago that noble instincts got people killed on rivers. She hadn't told them because there was nothing yet to decide. A shadow behind you wasn't a knife until it arrived.

By morning it had arrived.

* * *

They came alongside just below the weir, where the current slackened into an eddy the size of a courtyard. The skiff slid out of the mist with its engine idling so quietly that the first warning anyone got was the sound of a hull kissing the Second Chance's gunwale — a soft wooden knock, polite as a dinner guest.

There were four of them. Three wore the flat grey jackets of the Admiralty's maritime contractors, the ones whose paperwork always said *survey assistance* no matter what they were actually assisting. The fourth wore civilian clothes cut too well for the river, and he smiled the way men smile when they've already counted your money.

"Morning," he called up. "Vessel inspection. Routine."

"Routine," Paloma repeated from the rail. She kept her hands loose at her sides and her voice bored. Boredom was armor. Fear invited searching. "Since when does the Admiralty inspect fishing boats above Kessel Bend?"

"Since the floods," the man said, climbing aboard without waiting for permission, which answered her question better than any answer would have. "Debris hazard. Navigation safety. We're checking hulls for unregistered salvage." He glanced at the deck — at the dive gear racked by the mast, at the crane arm, at everything that made the Second Chance exactly what his paperwork claimed to be looking for — and his smile widened a fraction. "You do have a salvage license, Captain...?"

"Oyelaran." She let him wait for the rest. He did not get it.

"Oyelaran," he said, tasting it. "And your destination?"

"Upstream."

"Upstream is a large place."

"It's the direction the river goes," Paloma said. "I find it saves argument to go with it."

Behind the well-dressed man, one of the grey jackets snorted and then pretended he hadn't. Paloma catalogued him: young, big hands, the kind of hired muscle who still found the work funny. The other two were older and quieter, and quieter was worse.

Runi appeared at the galley hatch with a pot of coffee and an expression of perfect, weaponized innocence. "Visitors! Cups?"

"We won't be long," the well-dressed man said. "A look below decks, a check of your papers, and we'll let you get on with the river."

"Of course," Paloma said. "Papers are in the chart room. Ilya — fetch the gentleman the charts."

Ilya came forward slowly, wiping his hands on a rag, and Paloma watched him the way she'd watch a fuse burn. Because here was the thing she'd decided at three in the morning, alone in the wheelhouse with the mist coming down: the charts in the chart room were real. And the charts Ilya had spent two nights redrawing, in a hand so close to the Admiralty's own survey style that it made her skin crawl, were folded inside the real ones — and those charts showed the Second Chance as a vessel bound *downriver*, cleared at Kessel Bend two days ago, with a cargo manifest of scrap iron and a destination port forty miles south of anywhere they intended to go.

Forged documents were a hanging offense in some jurisdictions and a drowning offense in others. On the Vessarine coast they were simply Tuesday. But showing them to a contractor was a gamble, because a good forger's work didn't just lie — it *invited* the reader to feel clever for catching the lie, and then offered him a reason to keep it to himself.

Ilya handed over the folder with the mild apologetic air of a man surrendering a grocery list.

* * *

It took them eleven minutes to find what Ilya wanted them to find, and Paloma spent all eleven minutes pouring coffee and making small talk about the weather, which on the river during flood season was a rich subject. The well-dressed man — his name, when he finally gave it, was Merren, and he pronounced it like a title — stood at the chart table with the folder open, and Paloma watched his eyes move across the downriver clearance stamp, the manifest, the port of call, and then flick up once, briefly, to meet hers.

There it was. The moment. She'd seen it a hundred times across a negotiation table or a pawnshop counter: the instant a man realizes you know that he knows, and both of you agree, without a word, that knowing is worth more than telling.

Merren closed the folder. "Everything appears in order," he said.

"Good," said Paloma.

"Though I'll note the manifest lists scrap iron, and your deck is rigged for deep recovery work." He said it lightly, almost kindly. A door opened, and left open, deliberately. "Crane like that costs money, Captain Oyelaran. Somebody's investing in you."

"Somebody is," Paloma agreed. "He prefers to remain uninvested publicly."

Something moved behind Merren's eyes — not suspicion exactly, but recalculation. A patron who paid for discretion was a patron who could be squeezed, and contractors understood squeezing the way priests understood prayer. "Naturally," he said. "Discretion. Well." He tapped the folder once against the chart table, straightening pages that were already straight. "We'll leave you to your river. You'll understand if we note your position in our log."

"Note whatever you like," Paloma said. "The river moves me faster than your log can."

They went over the side. The skiff idled away downstream — *downstream*, Paloma noted, not upstream, which meant they weren't following, they were *reporting*. Somewhere ahead, at some bend or weir or flooded channel mouth, someone with more boats and less patience was going to receive a message that read: *salvage vessel, forged papers, wealthy patron, heading north with something worth forging for.*

"They bought it," Doss said, when the mist had swallowed the skiff. He sounded surprised, which offended Paloma slightly on Ilya's behalf.

"They bought what they wanted to buy," Ilya said quietly. He was folding his rag with great precision, over and over, smaller and smaller. "Men like that don't verify papers. They verify opportunities. I gave him one — a captain hiding a patron's business. Now he thinks he owns a piece of us. Men protect what they own."

"That's the most cynical useful thing I've ever heard," Sefa said from the engine hatch, where she'd been listening with her head cocked like a bird's. "How long did it buy us?"

Ilya considered. "An hour. Maybe two. Long enough to clear the weir and lose sight of the eddy. After that—" he unfolded the rag, refolded it, "—after that it depends on the river."

"And on Sefa," Paloma said.

Sefa grinned. It was not entirely a comforting grin.

* * *

Here was the problem with the weir, explained to Paloma years ago by a pilot who later drowned in it: the old fish traps had been built across the main channel, and the floods had knocked down enough of the stakes to open a gap, but the gap filled with strainers — deadfall, fence wire, a whole drowned orchard of trees standing upright in the current with their branches combed downstream like hair. Boats went through the gap the way letters go through a mail slot. What happened after the slot was between the boat and God.

But there was another way. There was always another way on a river, if you were willing to take one that didn't officially exist.

Runi had shown it to her on the chart the night before, and Paloma had stared at it for a long time, because it wasn't on the chart. It was *beside* the chart — a pencil line Runi's grandmother had drawn decades ago along the true left bank, through what the printed surveys insisted was solid ground: a slough, a cutoff, an old meander the river had abandoned and then, in flood years like this one, quietly taken back. No chart admitted it existed. The Admiralty surveys showed levee and farmland. Runi's grandmother's pencil showed water.

"Flood season rules," Runi had said, tapping the pencil line. "The river remembers where it used to go. High water, it visits."

Now, standing at the wheel with the weir's broken stakes sliding past to starboard, Paloma put the helm over and steered at what every chart in her hold swore was a cornfield.

"You're sure," she said. It wasn't a question, but she wanted to hear it anyway.

"I'm sure my grandmother was sure," Runi said. "She took timber barges through there. Loaded ones." A pause. "Also she drowned, but not there."

"*Not there* being the operative phrase," Sefa muttered, cranking the throttle. The Second Chance's engine had spent the last two days sounding like a man with a chest cold, and Sefa had spent those same two days with her arms buried in it to the shoulder, talking to it in a low voice, feeding it parts cannibalized from the backup pump and, at one memorable point, a hinge from the galley cupboard. Now it caught, coughed, and settled into a rough but willing roar. "Come on, ugly. Show the navy what a hinge can do."

The bank came at them — actual bank, willows and mud, the kind of terrain that eats hulls — and then, at the last possible moment, the willows ended and there was water where water should not have been, a broad brown lane threading back through the trees, moving fast, moving *north*, moving the way the river remembered.

"Grandmother," Runi said softly, "I owe you a candle."

They went in.

* * *

The slough was not gentle. Flood water never is; it runs with the manners of a crowd leaving a fire. The channel bent hard around a drowned farmhouse — roof just breaking the surface, chimney leaning like a drunk — and the current slammed the Second Chance's stern toward it hard enough that Paloma felt the whole hull shudder. Doss was on the bow fending off with a boat hook, shouting coordinates of debris in a voice pitched somewhere between alarm and delight, because Doss had never met a crisis he couldn't narrate.

"Tree! Tree, tree — starboard side, big one — *big* one—"

"I see it!" Paloma hauled the wheel. The trunk slid past close enough to touch, streaming, its rootball a dark island of torn earth.

"Chimney!"

"I see the chimney too!"

"Just checking! You missed the tree for a second there—"

"I did not miss the tree."

"There was a moment," Doss said, "where it looked like missing was on the table."

Below, Sefa nursed the engine through it, easing off in the bends, surging in the straights, reading the water through the propeller shaft the way a blind woman reads stairs with her foot. Twice the prop hit something soft — silt, weed, a submerged fence — and twice she brought the revs back without stalling, muttering apologies to the machinery that were somehow more tender than anything she said to people.

Ilya stood amidships with the grandmother-chart in his hands, though he barely needed it; the channel, once entered, marked itself. He looked, Paloma thought, like a man walking through a house he'd burglarized thirty years ago. Which, in a way, he was. Every mile of this river, he'd drawn for the Admiralty. Every mile of this river, he'd drawn *wrong*, in small careful ways, and Paloma was beginning to understand that the wrongness had been its own kind of testimony — a cartographer's thirty-year letter, written in omissions, addressed to whoever finally came looking.

She didn't say so. There was a time for that conversation, and it wasn't while the current was trying to fold their boat around a chimney.

They ran the slough for forty minutes. Then the trees opened, the current gentled, and the main river received them again a good six miles north of the weir — six miles the contractors would have to explain, six miles their log couldn't account for, six miles bought with a forged stamp, a borrowed hinge, and a dead woman's pencil.

Paloma killed the throttle to half and let the crew exhale. It came out of all of them at once, a great communal groan, followed immediately by Runi laughing and Doss demanding credit for the tree warnings and Sefa announcing that the engine now owed her nothing and she was keeping the hinge.

"An hour," Ilya said, coming up to the wheelhouse. He folded the chart away with those precise hands. "Maybe two. That's what the papers bought. The channel bought more, but channels close. When the flood crests, that route dies until next season."

"Then we don't come back this way," Paloma said.

"No," Ilya agreed. "We don't." He hesitated, and she saw the weight shift in him, the ballast moving, the thing he'd been carrying since the forbidden wreck getting heavier by the day. "Captain. There's something I need to tell you. About these charts. About why I know how to draw them the way the Admiralty draws them."

Paloma looked at him — at the rag in his hands, folded now to the size of a coin, at the face of a man standing at the edge of thirty silent years — and she thought of the murals in the drowned harbor, of admirals' names painted over famine, of all the ways a person can be complicit without ever firing a shot.

"After the next bend," she said gently. "Find me after the next bend. I want to hear it with clean water behind us and none ahead."

Ilya nodded once, like a man accepting a sentence, and went forward.

Behind them, far downstream, faint as a headache, she heard it: engines. More than one. Running fast against the current, which meant they were in a hurry, which meant the log had been read.

Paloma opened the throttle all the way north.
