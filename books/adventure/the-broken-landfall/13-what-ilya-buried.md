## 13. What Ilya Buried

The Second Chance ran dark that night, tied off under a curtain of hanging vines where the river widened into a slack brown pool the charts called nothing at all. No name. Ilya had checked three times, holding the lantern low over his own hand-drawn sheets, and found only blank paper where a village or a landing should have been. Blank paper, on his charts, meant one of two things: nobody had bothered to survey it, or somebody had been paid not to.

He was still staring at it when Paloma's shadow fell across the chart table.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I slept in Kessel Bend."

"You slept forty minutes in Kessel Bend, sitting up, with a wrench in your lap." She pulled the crate opposite him out with her boot and sat, elbows on knees, the way she did when she'd decided a conversation was going to happen to you whether you liked it or not. "Sefa says the engine's sound. Runi says the channel ahead is passable for two more days. Doss is — " she paused, and something crossed her face that Ilya couldn't read, some small weather front, " — Doss is keeping watch. So there's nothing left to do tonight except talk."

"I'm not much of a talker."

"No," Paloma agreed pleasantly. "You're a mapmaker who recognized scuttle charges on a civilian hull and didn't say a word about it. You're a man who reads admiralty cipher like his morning tea leaves. And you're someone whose hands shook for an hour after we passed the drowned town, which I noticed, because I notice things, it's how I've stayed alive." She leaned back against the bulkhead. "So let's try talking."

Ilya looked down at the chart. The blank space seemed to look back.

* * *

He had been twenty-three years old, the first time he drew a lie.

It had been good work, too. That was the thing he could never make anyone understand, the thing that had kept him awake through thirty years of nights in port towns from here to the Salt Reaches: the lie was *beautiful*. The survey of the Vessarine upper tributaries, commissioned by the Admiralty Office in the spring of the last year of Kethria, was the finest cartographic work of his career. Every sounding triple-checked. Every bank profiled. He'd been proud of it. He'd pinned a copy above his bunk.

"The commission came through the Hydrographic Section," he said now, and heard his own voice go flat and official, the voice of a young man reciting. "Standard flood-season mapping. Trade routes, safe anchorages. There were eleven of us. Junior men, mostly. They always took junior men for the upper rivers because the pay was poor and the senior cartographers had better offers."

Paloma said nothing. She had the sailor's gift for silence, letting a story come up on its own line like a fish that needed time.

"Halfway through the season, we were issued revised instructions. Certain features were to be omitted from the final plates. Settlements. Anchorages. A whole stretch of the Kethrian shore road." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if the evidence might be written there. "We were told it was a printing error in the source documents. Duplication. Everyone laughed about it. Duplication, of an entire nation's coastline."

"And you drew the omissions anyway."

"I drew them beautifully," Ilya said, and the old shame rose in his throat like bilge water, exactly where he'd kept it all these years. "That's what I want you to understand, Paloma. It wasn't cowardice, not at first. Cowardice would have been simpler. It was *craft*. I was good, and they used my goodness, and every year the maps got cleaner and the country got deader, and I told myself I was a technician. Technicians don't ask what the lines mean."

The lantern hissed. Out in the dark, the river moved against the hull with the patient sound of something that intended to outlast everything built beside it.

"When did you stop telling yourself that?" Paloma asked.

Ilya almost smiled. "The day the famine started. Or rather — the day I understood it had already started, and that the omissions weren't about trade routes at all. You can't starve a port city if barges can reach it. You can't quarantine a people if their harbors are on the charts. We didn't erase Kethria with guns. We erased it with *blank paper*." His voice cracked on the last word and he was grateful, absurdly, that it was dark enough in the cabin to hide it. "Forty years later, I dive into a wreck the navy forbids anyone to discuss, and I find naval demolition charges set on a civilian hull, and I think: there it is. My work. Still working. Still keeping the waters quiet."

"Why didn't you tell me?" Paloma asked. Not angry. Worse than angry — genuinely asking, the question of a woman doing arithmetic.

"Because telling you made it real again," Ilya said. "Because I spent thirty years being a small, careful man in small, careful ports, and I told myself the silence was penance. That carrying it was enough. That remembering counted for something." He shook his head. "It doesn't. Memory you bury isn't memory. It's just weight. And I put mine in your hold without asking, same as any smuggler."

* * *

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, rougher at the edges.

"My father fished the Vessarine delta," she said. "Before the salvage laws, before the navy bought up the shallows. He used to tell me there'd been a country upriver once. Everyone's parents said it. A country with glassworks and a fleet of grain barges and a university that taught navigation to anyone who could pay the fee, which was nothing." She looked at the lantern flame. "And then everyone stopped saying it. Not because they forgot. Because boats that talked about Kethria didn't get mooring rights. My father lost his berth over less."

"I know," Ilya said quietly. "I drew the regulations that cost him his berth. Page forty-one of the Coastal Access Act. My survey data underpinned the whole annex."

Paloma stared at him.

For a moment Ilya thought she was going to strike him, and he found — this surprised him most of all, thirty years late — that part of him wanted her to. It would have been simpler. Punishment was simple. What she did instead was worse: she breathed out slowly, the way she did before a deep dive, checking herself, and asked:

"The deed. The inheritance clause. When you deciphered it — did you already know what the reparations funds were? Did you know before you told us?"

"Yes."

"And you let Asque corner us with it anyway. You let me walk into that conversation blind."

"I told myself you deserved to hear it from the document, not from me. That my testimony would poison it." He laughed, short and joyless. "Thirty years of practice at telling myself things. I'm very good at it. It's the same skill as drawing a lie, in the end. Steady hand, clean line."

The cabin door scraped and Runi's head appeared, hair wild, eyes bright with the particular alarm of a teenager who has news and no tact whatsoever. "Is this the part where we throw him off the boat? Because Sefa wants to know if we're throwing him off the boat, and if so whether it should be at the next landing or—"

"*Runi.*"

"—because the current's wrong at the next landing, it'd look like an accident, which is the whole point—" She caught sight of both their faces and retreated with the dignity of a slammed door, though the door itself remained open, which somehow made it worse.

Paloma waited until the footsteps faded. Then she rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands, and when she looked up she looked tired in a way Ilya had never seen on her — tired the way divers get tired, not of the water but of the surface.

"Here's my problem, Ilya," she said. "Every instinct I have says throw you off the boat. You're a risk. You've been a risk since the day you came aboard with your clean references and your excellent charts, and I hired you anyway because I needed a cartographer and because — " she gestured vaguely at him, at the whole stooped, ink-stained fact of him, " — you seemed harmless. Which was the best forgery you ever produced."

"It was," Ilya agreed. "I worked on it for decades."

"But." She held up a finger. "But you also forged the charts at the weir that got us past the contractors. You dove a wreck you knew would hurt you, to bring back proof. And just now, when you could have stayed quiet another thirty years, you handed me the knife handle-first." She stood, joints cracking, and looked down at him. "So here's what trust looks like on my boat. It's not forgiveness. Forgiveness is for priests and mothers, and I'm neither. Trust is: I know what you did, and I'm choosing to believe what you do next matters more. For now. Provisionally. Subject to revision."

"That's the coldest grant of trust I've ever received," Ilya said.

"And the only one on offer." She paused at the door, then turned back, and her voice softened by perhaps a single degree, which from Paloma was a warm bath. "My father used to say the river keeps everything, eventually. Everything thrown in, everything sunk, everything buried. It keeps it, and it moves it, and one day it sets it down somewhere in the light where everybody can see it." She tapped the blank space on his chart, right in the middle of the erasure. "Maybe that's what you're for, old man. Maybe you're the part of the river that finally puts it down."

She left. Ilya sat alone with the lantern and the blank paper, and found that his hands, for the first time in thirty years, were steady.

* * *

He didn't sleep either. Instead he cleared the chart table, weighted down fresh paper, and began to draw.

Not the lie. The other thing. From memory — and his memory, he discovered, was a vault that had never once been locked, only painted shut — he drew the Kethrian shore road as it had been. The eleven anchorages the Admiralty had struck. The grain piers at Vessa Mouth. The ferry crossings. The little town whose name he'd had to look up in a smuggled gazetteer even then, because it had already been missing from the official plates: *Sarn Ket*, population four thousand, glassworks, chapel, one very good bakery whose proprietor had once given a hungry young surveyor a roll and refused payment.

He drew until the sky behind the portholes went grey, and then he drew the drowned town they'd seen, matching mural to memory, and wrote in the margins in his small precise hand: *witnessed, this season, by the undersigned.*

When he finally came up on deck, stiff-necked and ink to the second knuckle, the crew was gathered at the rail watching the light come up gold on the flooded forest. Sefa had coffee going. Runi was asleep sitting up against the mast. Doss was gone from his watch post, and Paloma's jaw was tight in a way that suggested that was a conversation for later.

Paloma looked at the rolled papers under Ilya's arm, then at his face.

"What's that?"

"The truth," Ilya said. "Plotted and gridded. Soundings verified." He unrolled it on the deck box: the shore road, the anchorages, Sarn Ket with its bakery marked with a small affectionate cross. "It's not complete. It can't be — half of it's underwater and the other half is in my head. But it's a start, and it's signed, and if we reach Verren Reach, it goes in the record under my name."

Sefa leaned over it and whistled. "This'll take days to fair in properly."

"We have nineteen days," Paloma said. Then, correcting herself, "Eighteen now." She studied the chart a long moment, and Ilya watched her watch it — watched her see, for the first time, the shape of the country the blank paper had eaten.

"There's something else," Ilya said. "Something I remembered around the third hour, drawing the ferry crossings. The archive at Verren Reach — the neutral one, the one Asque fears enough to hire gunboats. Do you know why it's neutral? Why the Admiralty has never been able to touch it?"

"Old treaty," Sefa said. "Everyone says so."

"Treaties are paper," Ilya said. "No. It's neutral because of what's buried under it. Verren Reach was built on the foundations of the Kethrian national archive. The Admiralty burned the town above it during the fall, but the vaults are cut into bedrock, and the flood sealed them instead of destroying them. Everything Kethria ever recorded — deeds, ledgers, the famine requisition orders, *everything* — is sitting under that archive house right now, forty feet down, behind a door only a bearer of the inheritance can lawfully open." He straightened, and felt thirty years fall off him like a coat. "Asque doesn't want the chest because of what's inside it. He wants it because it's the key. And he knows — the Admiralty knows — that if Paloma reaches Verren Reach as bearer, she doesn't just file a claim."

Paloma went very still.

"She opens the door," Ilya finished. "On the whole country. Every document they drowned. All at once."

The coffee pot hissed over the little stove, and nobody moved to take it off the heat. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came the sound none of them wanted to hear: engines. More than one. Running without lights, coming fast against the current.

Paloma was already moving. "Runi! Up! Sefa, give me everything the engine has and then some. Ilya — " she glanced back at him, and there it was, provisional and cold and real: trust, subject to revision. "Get us a route. Any route. You owe the river a truth; start paying it off."

Ilya unfolded his new chart across the deck box, heart hammering like a young man's, and began.
