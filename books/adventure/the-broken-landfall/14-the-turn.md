## 14. The Turn

The supply stop at Marrow's Landing was supposed to take forty minutes.

Paloma had said so twice — once to the crew, once to the universe, which she'd long ago learned ignored her either way. Forty minutes to buy diesel, rice, and the tinned peaches Runi had been mourning since Kessel Bend, and then back on the river before the water rose another hand's width against the Second Chance's hull.

Marrow's Landing was less a town than an argument between a trading post and a mudbank. A crooked pier, a warehouse with a roof like a dropped hat, and a woman named Odji who sold everything from engine oil to wedding rings and asked no questions about which was being bought for which occasion. The flood had already climbed the first step of her porch, and she'd moved her stock up onto pallets with the calm of someone who did this every year and expected the river to apologize eventually.

"Forty minutes," Doss said, shouldering the empty fuel drums. "You've got a look."

"What look?"

"The look you get when you're about to be wrong about something."

"I'm always wrong about something," Paloma said. "It keeps the gods bored." She counted coins into Odji's palm for the peaches first — priorities — and that was when Asque came down the pier.

He looked wrong. That was the thing she'd notice later, in the long replay of it she'd run through her head on sleepless nights: not that he *did* anything wrong yet, but that he looked wrong doing nothing at all. Corvel Asque had spent three weeks aboard cultivating a particular softness — the patron who rolled his sleeves, who laughed at Sefa's jokes, who carried his own bag like a man proving he wasn't what he was. Now he stood at the foot of the gangplank with his sleeves buttoned and his coat closed, and his hands were empty in the way hands are empty right before they aren't.

"Paloma." His voice was pleasant. It was always pleasant. That had been the whole trick of him. "A word before we cast off?"

"We're casting off in thirty-five minutes."

"This won't take thirty-five minutes." He smiled, and the smile had all its teeth but none of its warmth, like a lantern with the candle taken out. Behind him, two men came off the moored skiff at the pier's end. They wore plain canvas jackets and moved like men who'd been paid to move like that. One of them had a boat hook. The other had something under his jacket that didn't need describing; Paloma had grown up around the navy's contractors, and you learned to read the shapes of guns the way other people read weather.

Her mouth went dry in the specific way it did before dives — the body knowing before the mind agreed to know.

"Asque," she said carefully. "Whatever this is—"

"It's very simple." He gestured toward the Second Chance, riding low and patient at the pier. "The chest. You'll have your crew bring it down, we'll transfer it to my skiff, and you'll all go on to Verren Reach with my blessing and the balance of your pay. Everyone eats. Everyone floats. It's the gentlest version of this day that exists, I promise you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then it becomes a different day." He said it without heat, almost apologetically, the way a man describes rain. "I'd rather not. I've genuinely enjoyed your company. Runi especially — that child is going to be a terror in ten years, and I mean that as a compliment."

Paloma's eyes flicked past him. Doss had stopped mid-stride with the fuel drums, twenty feet away, frozen in the particular stillness of a man calculating distances he didn't like. Odji had quietly stepped inside her warehouse, which told Paloma that Odji had seen this play before and knew which role the audience survived by not playing.

"Runi," Paloma called, without taking her eyes off Asque. "Go get Sefa."

"She's in the engine room."

"Go get her anyway."

Runi, bless her, didn't ask. She dropped the shopping list and went, quick and quiet along the pier's far rail, and Paloma saw one of the canvas-jacketed men track her and decide she wasn't worth tracking. That was their mistake, or would be. River kids were never worth tracking until suddenly they were everywhere.

"Paloma," Asque said, gently chiding. "Don't make this complicated."

"You're making it complicated. You're standing on my pier with hired guns asking me to hand over the one thing keeping your patron's name out of the mud, and you're calling it simple." She kept her voice level, conversational, loud enough for Doss to hear every word. "Here's what I know about you now, Corvel. Your ledger says the Admiralty was paying you before we ever found that chest. Which means you weren't funding an expedition. You were running a recovery operation, and we were your dredge."

Something flickered across his face — not guilt. Regret, maybe, of the shallowest kind: regret that the mask had been necessary at all.

"The Admiralty pays for silence," he said. "That's most of what it pays for, if you look closely at any budget. And the chest is silence, Paloma. It's forty years of silence sitting in a lead-lined box, and there are people whose entire lives are built on top of it. You think they'll let a salvage diver carry it upriver? They let me hire you because I promised them you'd never reach Verren Reach. I'm renegotiating that promise. This is me, being kind."

"You're being paid."

"I'm being *paid*," he agreed, "and I'm also being watched, and the difference matters more than you'd think. Give me the chest. Keep the deed, if you like — burn it, frame it, I don't care. The paper means nothing without the evidence in the box. Lawyers need exhibits, Paloma. Archivists need artifacts. You have neither if I'm gone, and I will be gone, one way or the other, in the next four minutes."

*Four minutes.* Her mind was moving fast now, diver-fast, that cold clear place where panic gets filed under later. The chest was aboard, lashed in the hold under Sefa's spare chain — Sefa, who was currently elbow-deep in the engine and hadn't heard a word of this. Ilya was ashore buying charts from Odji's back room. The gun under the jacket hadn't come out yet, which meant Asque still believed he wouldn't need it, which meant there was still a version of this where nobody bled.

There was also the fact that the Second Chance sat in six feet of brown floodwater, and Paloma Oyelaran had spent her whole life learning what water could be talked into.

"Doss," she said. "Bring up the chest."

Doss's head came around. For one second his face was naked — shock, betrayal, the question *what are you doing* — and then he read whatever was in hers, and his jaw set, and he went up the gangplank slow, dragging his feet like a man carrying grief instead of drums.

Asque exhaled, satisfied. "Sensible. Truly. When this is over, if you ever want honest work, my office—"

"Your office hires dredges."

"My office hires survivors," Asque said. "There's a difference, and it's mostly a matter of who writes the definition."

Doss came back down the plank with the chest in his arms, and Paloma watched Asque's eyes go to it — hungry, finally unhidden, the want showing through like a lamp through a cracked shade — and she thought, with a strange floating clarity: *there it is. Forty years of dead nation, and he looks at it like a man looking at a purse.*

The chest reached the bottom of the plank. The canvas-jacketed man with the hidden gun stepped forward to take it, and his jacket shifted, and Paloma saw the grip, black and practical, and the moment stretched thin as wire.

And that was when the Second Chance's bilge alarm started screaming.

* * *

Sefa's alarms were famous. She'd built them herself from salvaged parts, and each one had a personality: the fire alarm wailed like a widow, the collision horn barked twice like an insulted dog, and the bilge alarm — the one that meant water coming in faster than the pumps liked — shrieked in a rising spiral that sounded like the ship itself being murdered.

Every head on that pier turned toward it. Every head except Paloma's, which stayed exactly where it was, because she had been watching Sefa's shadow in the engine room hatchway for the last ninety seconds, and ninety seconds ago that shadow had picked up a hammer.

"Gods," Sefa's voice floated out over the alarm, pitched beautifully, terribly, at full theatrical panic. "She's taking water! Hull breach, aft section, it's coming in like a tap — somebody get down here or say goodbye to your deposit!"

"She's holed?" Asque's composure cracked, just a hairline. "How is she holed? We haven't—"

"Flood debris," Paloma said instantly, smoothly, lying with the ease of long practice. "Submerged log took us in the night. We've been pumping since dawn. It's why I wanted a fast turnaround." She spread her hands. "You can see why I can't linger, Corvel. Take the chest. Go. We'll limp to Verren Reach or we'll swim, but either way we're done here."

For a moment — one long, swaying moment — she thought it might actually work. Asque looked at the ship, at the pier, at the rising river, and Paloma could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes: the chest secured versus a client waiting versus a boat that might sink and take his alibi down with it.

Then the canvas-jacketed man with the gun made his own arithmetic, and it was shorter.

"Breach is aft," he said. Flat voice, river-flat. "Aft's where the chest was stowed. Funny thing about lead lining — heavy stuff. If she's holed aft and listing, that box just got worth more than the boat." He turned to Asque. "We're on a clock either way, sir. Take it and go."

The gun came out.

Not pointed at anyone, not yet — held low, angled at the planks, the way a man holds a tool he's been authorized to use. But it was out, and the air changed the way air changes before a squall, and Odji's warehouse door shut fully, and somewhere upstream a bird complained about all of it.

"Last time," Asque said, and all the pleasantness had drained out of him now, leaving something older and barer, a man who had always been this underneath and was tired of the costume. "Nobody needs to be a hero today. Heroes make work for everyone."

Paloma looked at the gun. She looked at the chest, sitting on the planks between Doss's boots. She looked at the river, high and brown and indifferent, and at her ship, shrieking its beautiful fake death, and she felt the despair arrive — really arrive, cold and total, the way it does when you understand that the story has left your hands.

They were going to take it. There was no clever line, no trick left, no amount of talking that would talk a bullet sideways. Asque had simply waited until the moment when the mask cost more than the mission, and now the mission wore the mask's face, and the chest was going onto that skiff, and Verren Reach was going to hear nothing but the Admiralty's version, again, forever, the way it had heard nothing for forty years.

*All this way,* she thought. *All this river.*

"Take it," she said.

Asque blinked. "Just like that?"

"Just like that." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "You're right about one thing, Corvel. Paper means nothing without the exhibit. So take your exhibit. Take your silence. Put it on your shelf next to your ledger." She stepped back from the chest, deliberately, formally, like a woman ceding ground in a dance. "But hear me, and hear me clearly, because I'll only spend these words once. What's in that box proves what happened. What's in *me* knows what happened. You can't dredge that up. You can't buy it, sink it, or scuttle it. There are five people on this pier and a girl running errands who will carry Kethria's name the rest of their lives, and every one of them is a bearer now. You've just multiplied your problem by six."

Something crossed Asque's face then that she'd remember longer than the gun — a flicker of genuine unease, the first honest thing he'd shown her. Because she was right, and he knew she was right, and men like Corvel Asque built their whole architecture on the assumption that truth lived in objects, in vaults, in things that could be owned and therefore buried.

The canvas-jacketed man picked up the chest. It was heavier than it looked; Paloma saw his knees take the weight and felt a small, vicious satisfaction.

"To the skiff," Asque said. He paused beside Paloma on his way past, and for a heartbeat the old warmth flickered back on, reflexive, almost sad. "You were the best of them, you know. In thirty years of hiring people to not find things, you're the only one who found them anyway." He touched the brim of his hat, an absurd little courtesy. "Get your ship patched. The river's rising."

Then they were walking down the pier, two canvas jackets and a patron with clean hands and forty years of a murdered nation swinging between them, and the skiff's engine caught on the first try because of course it did, because the world was not fair, and Paloma stood on the planks and listened to the sound of the chest getting smaller.

Behind her, the bilge alarm kept screaming.

* * *

It stopped screaming the moment the skiff cleared the point, which told Paloma everything about how much water the Second Chance was actually taking on, which was none.

"Well," Sefa said, emerging from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag, hammer still tucked in her belt like a sidearm. "That went well."

"We lost the chest," Doss said. He was staring down the empty river. "We lost the *chest*, Sefa."

"We lost a box," Sefa said. "Full of paper and a fancy clock. I can rebuild a clock. Can't rebuild a nation, but I couldn't do that with the box either."

"Sefa—"

"He's right," Paloma said quietly.

They all turned. She was still looking at the river, at the wake the skiff had left, already smoothing itself flat as if nothing had crossed it. And underneath the despair — underneath the cold sick weight of it — something was turning over, slowly, the way silt turns over in a current and suddenly shows you the shape of the wreck beneath.

"Ilya," she said. "Come here. All of you. Listen to me, because I've only just heard it myself."

Ilya came up from Odji's back room with a rolled chart under his arm and his face pale. Runi appeared from wherever Runi went, which was everywhere. The five of them stood on the pier of Marrow's Landing in the flood-season light, and Paloma took a breath.

"When I found that chest, what did I actually find? Think. Not the documents — those are copies of copies, and the Admiralty has the originals of everything, that's half the crime. Not the astrolabe. Not even the seal." She held up her hand, ticking fingers. "What I found was a *deed*. A legal instrument. Naming a bearer. Naming coordinates. Naming a claim that stands in law whether the chest exists or not — because the claim isn't stored in the box, Ilya. Where is it stored?"

Ilya's eyes widened. She watched him get there, watch the old cartographer's mind run ahead of her words.

"In the registry," he said slowly. "In the archive. The claim was *filed*, Paloma. Forty years ago. Frozen, pending presentation by a named bearer. The chest is evidence. The deed is—" He stopped. Swallowed. "*The deed is the key. And the key doesn't care where the lock is.*"

"The chest proves what they did," Paloma said. "The deed says someone has the *right* to make them answer for it. And that right is sitting right here." She pressed her fist, gently, against her own sternum. "Asque took a box. He thinks he took the inheritance. Let him haul it. Let him explain to his masters why the weight on his manifest is the least valuable thing on this pier."

Doss made a sound that was half laugh, half groan. "You let him take it. On purpose. You stood there and *handed it to him*—"

"I stalled him while Sefa screamed and Runi ran, yes. And I meant every word besides." She turned to Sefa. "How bad is our real situation? Truth, not theater."

Sefa's grin faded into honesty. "We're sound. But we burned the reserve pump running that stunt, the starboard seam's weeping where it's been weeping since Kessel Bend, and we've got maybe nine days of food if we don't eat like Runi."

"I eat normally," Runi said, with dignity.

"You eat like a shrimping season."

"Nine days," Paloma said. "Verren Reach is eight, if the Divide doesn't eat us. And Asque will report the chest recovered, which buys us maybe two days before someone realizes the deed never came ashore with it — and then every contractor on this river stops chasing a box and starts chasing *us*." She looked at her crew — her impossible, broke, loyal crew — and felt the last of the despair drain out and leave something harder and cleaner behind. "So we don't go the way they'll expect. Runi. Your kin on the banks. The ones you said would shelter a stranger with a good story."

Runi's chin lifted, and there was something new in her face — the kid falling away and the river daughter standing up. "My aunt holds the ferry rights at Serpent Narrows. My cousins net the shallows past the old weir. Word travels bank to bank faster than any skiff, and nobody on this river loves the navy's contractors." She grinned, sharp as a fishhook. "You want to disappear upriver, Captain? I can make us fog."

"Then we're not stranded," Paloma said. "We're *ahead* of them, and they just don't know it yet." She clapped her hands once, briskly, the sound cracking across the pier like a starting pistol. "Doss — fuel, now, drums moving. Ilya — charts, the real ones, and every ghost channel you ever forged. Sefa — that seam, before we're a submarine. We cast off in twenty minutes."

"And the peaches?" Runi asked.

Paloma looked at the sack sitting untouched by Odji's door, and despite everything — the gun, the theft, the eight days of river and ruin ahead — she laughed. It came up out of her rough and real, and it felt like surfacing.

"Grab the peaches," she said. "We're going to need the sugar."

Downstream, around the bend, a skiff carrying forty years of silence ran dark toward the coast — and never noticed the small, battered, stubborn boat already turning its bow upriver, into the flood, toward home.
