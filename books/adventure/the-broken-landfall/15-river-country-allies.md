## 15. River Country Allies

Runi smelled her aunt's cooking before she saw the house.

That shouldn't have been possible. The Second Chance was still a half-mile downstream, wallowing in the brown flood-water like a wounded ox, and the wind was coming off the river, not toward it. But Aunt Besha's smokehouse had a smell that traveled the way gossip did — sideways, uphill, and against all sense — and Runi had spent eleven years of her life being pulled home by that smell against her own better judgment.

"There," she said, pointing at the bend ahead where the flooded river had swallowed the old ferry landing and crept up into the orchard. A stilt-house stood hip-deep in water, its veranda railing trailing green fronds of something that used to be a garden. Smoke rose from behind it. "That's my aunt's place."

Paloma stood at the rail beside her, arms crossed, watching the shoreline with the expression she wore when she was deciding whether to trust something. It was an expression Runi had seen a lot lately. It didn't change much between navy patrol boats and aunts.

"You said your family ran this stretch," Paloma said.

"I said my family *is* this stretch." Runi counted on her fingers. "Aunt Besha at the bend. The Okonkwos at the weir — well, what's left of the weir. My cousin Teyo fishes the narrows with his three brothers and a dog that bites engineers, so warn Sefa. And past the divide there's old Nana Ireti, who is older than the flood season and meaner than the Admiralty, which is saying something these days."

"And they'll help us? Just like that?"

Runi looked at her. Paloma Oyelaran was the toughest person Runi knew, and also somehow the one who asked the most naive questions.

"They're river people," Runi said. "The navy has boats. We have cousins. Boats sink, Captain."

* * *

Aunt Besha met them at the landing with a boat-hook in one hand and a wooden spoon in the other, and for one alarming moment it looked like she intended to use both.

"Runiola Oyelaran-Beshe." She pronounced every syllable like a sentence being handed down. "You disappear for two years. Two years. Not a letter, not a pigeon, not so much as a fish with a note tied to it. And now you come back up my river on a leaking hulk full of strangers with navy contractors two days behind you, and you want — what? Breakfast?"

"Breakfast would be great," Runi said.

Besha stared at her for a long moment. Then the spoon lowered, and something in her face cracked open the way river ice does — suddenly, all at once, and warmer underneath than anyone expected.

"Come up before you drown," she said. "All of you. Even the one who looks like he sleeps standing up." She pointed the spoon at Doss, who did, in fact, look like he slept standing up. "Especially him. He looks hungry enough to eat the spoon."

The hull of the Second Chance had taken a gouge below the waterline during the run through the shallows — not fatal, but the kind of wound that got worse if you ignored it, which was true of hulls and people alike. Sefa had been nursing it with tar and prayer for two days, and prayer was losing.

While Sefa and Teyo — summoned by the smell of trouble, or possibly the smell of breakfast — went to argue about patching compounds, Runi sat on Besha's veranda with her boots off and her feet in a basin of hot water that appeared without her asking, because that was how things worked here. Paloma sat across from her, holding a cup of bitter leaf tea like she wasn't sure whether to drink it or apologize to it.

"Your aunt knows why we're here?" Paloma asked quietly.

"She knows we're running from the navy." Runi shrugged. "She doesn't need more than that. Nobody on this stretch needs more than that. The navy stopped asking nicely forty years ago, and everybody remembers whose fault that was."

Paloma's cup paused halfway to her mouth. "Kethria."

"Nobody says the name out loud on the water," Runi said. "But everybody knows it. You don't live on a river and forget what drowned upstream."

Inside the house, through the open window, Doss was eating his third bowl of stew and telling Besha's youngest daughter a story about the rapids at Kessel Bend in which he was, apparently, the hero. Runi caught Paloma listening too, and caught the small smile Paloma tried to drown in tea.

"He stayed behind at the decoy light," Paloma said softly. "We don't even know if—"

"We know," Runi said. "Teyo's brother saw a skiff go down past the narrows yesterday flying no colors and running dark. And this morning a fisherman found a man walking the bank with a burned sleeve and an attitude, heading downstream toward the ferry crossing." She grinned. "Doss walks fast when he's feeling guilty. He'll be here by dark."

Paloma set down her cup. For a moment she looked younger than Runi had ever seen her — just tired, just human, just a woman waiting for word about her friend.

"Your family," Paloma said. "They'd hide us. Knowing the navy's involved. Knowing what could come down on them."

Runi pulled her feet out of the basin and looked her captain dead in the eye.

"Captain. When the floods come, does the river ask the valley's permission?"

* * *

By mid-afternoon the veranda had filled.

This was how it worked in river country, Runi knew: you didn't send invitations, you sent smells and rumors, and the right people arrived. Teyo came with his brothers and the dog (which bit Sefa, as prophesied, though only once and mostly out of professional courtesy). Old Nana Ireti arrived by dugout, poled by a grand-nephew, and installed herself in the best chair without a word, where she proceeded to listen to everything and say nothing, which everyone understood to be her way of conducting state business.

Sefa came up from the boat with tar on her hands and hope in her voice, which was a new combination for her.

"The hull's fixable," she announced. "Not with my materials — I need proper pitch and a sheet of hammered copper, and maybe two days dry time, which we don't have unless—" She stopped, recalculating. "Unless somebody knows where to get pitch and copper in the next hour."

"Teyo," Runi said.

"Copper's at the Okonkwo place," Teyo said. "Pitch we render ourselves. Give me till sundown." He looked around the veranda, at the strangers, at the armed tension sitting among them like an uninvited guest, and added, "And nobody asks me why."

"Nobody ever asks anybody why here," Nana Ireti said, from her chair, without opening her eyes. "Asking why is how the navy got in."

It was the first thing she'd said. The whole porch went quiet.

Then the old woman opened one eye, fixed it on Paloma, and said: "You're carrying something heavy, girl. I can see it sitting on your shoulders like a second head. River took something from you?"

"River gave me something," Paloma said carefully. "I'm trying to get it somewhere safe."

"Mm." Nana Ireti closed the eye again. "Verren Reach."

Runi felt the whole porch lean forward.

"The archive?" Paloma said. "How did you—"

"I'm ninety-one years old and I live on a river where the navy pretends nothing happened. What do you think I do all day? Think?" The old woman snorted. "Word came upriver two days ago, before you did. The flood's going to crest at Verren Reach in six days, maybe seven. When it crests, the lower town goes under, and the archive closes its doors and seals its records until the water drops. That's their law. Water over the threshold, doors shut, no exceptions, not for kings, not for admirals, not for girls with heavy shoulders."

"So we have six days," Paloma said.

"You have six days," Nana Ireti agreed. "And here is the other thing, since nobody else will say it plainly. The archive doesn't care about your chest. Chests are boxes. Boxes burn, sink, get stolen by men with clean hands." One wrinkled finger rose and pointed, blind and accurate, at the spot where Asque had last been seen. "The archive cares about a *bearer*. Their oldest law, older than the flood law: a claim must be carried by a living person who stands in the hall and speaks it aloud. Paper can be forged. People have to show up." The finger swung to point at Paloma's chest. "If that deed names you, then you are not a courier, girl. You are the claim. Get yourself to Verren Reach before the water does, and stand up straight when you speak."

The porch was very quiet. Somewhere below, Sefa was already hammering.

"I never asked to be anybody's claim," Paloma said quietly.

"No," Nana Ireti said. "Nobody ever does. That's how you know it's real."

* * *

They worked through the evening, and this was the part Runi loved, the part she'd missed for two years without knowing it: the way river country turned strangers into crew before nightfall.

Teyo's brothers rendered pitch in a blackened kettle behind the smokehouse, stirring with a broken oar and arguing about proportions in the ancient, furious way of brothers everywhere. The Okonkwo copper arrived wrapped in oilcloth, hammered thin by someone's grandfather for some long-forgotten purpose, and Sefa held it up to the last light like it was a holy relic. Besha fed everyone twice. The dog adopted Doss, for reasons no one could explain and Doss refused to encourage, though Runi saw him slip it a rib bone when he thought nobody was looking.

Ilya sat apart, as he usually did, but not far apart — near enough to the firelight to be counted, far enough to keep his own counsel. Runi brought him a bowl without being asked and sat down beside him.

"You've been quiet," she said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You've been quiet *differently*." She nudged him. "My aunt thinks you're a spy. Teyo thinks you're a defrocked admiral. I told them you're just an old man who draws good maps."

Ilya almost smiled. Almost. "Your family," he said, after a while. "They're doing all this for people they met this morning."

"That's not strange."

"It's the strangest thing I've seen in thirty years," Ilya said. "In the service, everything cost something. Every favor had a ledger line. Here—" he gestured at the yard, the kettle, the dog, the whole smoking, boiling, arguing economy of goodwill — "nobody's written anything down."

"That's because Aunt Besha keeps it all in her head," Runi said. "And she charges interest. In visits. You'll owe her a visit after all this."

"I haven't had anywhere worth visiting in a long time," Ilya said, and went back to studying the river as if it were a chart he was still learning to read.

* * *

Doss came up the landing at full dark, exactly as predicted, singed, limping slightly, and pretending it was nothing.

He made it four steps onto the dock before Runi hit him at a dead run hard enough to knock them both into the pilings.

"Ow," Doss said. "Ow. Okay. Ow."

"You *idiot*," Runi said into his shoulder. "You absolute — you stayed behind — you could have — we thought—"

"I know," Doss said. Patting her back awkwardly, the way men pat things they love and don't understand. "I know. I'm sorry. Also I'm starving, is there food?"

"There's always food," Runi said, wiping her face furiously on his ruined sleeve. "There's a whole aunt of food. Come on."

From the top of the dock, Paloma watched them come, and Runi saw her captain take a breath and let it go — the breath she'd probably been holding for two days. Besha appeared in the doorway with a bowl the size of a washbasin, took one look at Doss, and said, "Sit. Eat. Then you'll tell me honestly what happened to my niece's boat, because I can see the lie forming already and I want to enjoy catching it."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had gone down to coals and the crew of the Second Chance lay scattered across the veranda and the loft like the survivors of something, which they were — Runi couldn't sleep.

She climbed to the roof of the woodshed, her old spot, and sat with her knees drawn up, looking downriver. The flood had risen another hand's width since sunset; she could tell by the orchard fence posts disappearing. Six days. Maybe seven. Somewhere down that black water, gunboats were moving, and somewhere up it, an archive was counting the hours until it shut its doors on the world.

Footsteps on the shed ladder. Paloma's head appeared over the eaves.

"This is trespassing," Runi said. "This is my spot."

"I know. Your aunt told me. She also told me where you'd be." Paloma hauled herself up and settled beside her, close enough for warmth. For a while neither spoke. Below, the river moved in the dark, enormous and patient, carrying everything toward the sea.

"They're going to help us all the way up," Paloma said finally. "Teyo's brothers will pole us through the narrows. The Okonkwos will watch the weir and signal if contractors come. Nana Ireti sent a bird ahead to Verren Reach tonight — a *bird*, Runi, I haven't seen a message bird in twenty years — telling the archivists a bearer is coming." She shook her head slowly. "I spent weeks thinking we were alone against the Admiralty. One family, one boat, one stolen box. And it turns out the whole river's been waiting for someone to carry it."

"Not waiting," Runi said. "Remembering. There's a difference. Waiting means you think somebody might come. Remembering just means you refuse to forget." She hugged her knees. "The navy erased Kethria from the maps. But you can't erase a river. The river remembers where everything drowned. My family just... lives along the memory."

Paloma was quiet a long moment. "Is that what we're doing? Carrying a memory?"

"You're carrying a deed," Runi said. "Same thing, if you do it right."

Downriver, far off, a light blinked twice against the black — the Okonkwos' signal lamp, at the weir. Two blinks: traffic sighted, moving slow, no colors shown.

Runi was on her feet before the second blink faded.

"Contractors?" Paloma asked, already rising.

"Maybe. Or maybe just a smuggler running dark." Runi grabbed the ladder. "Either way — Aunt Besha says we launch at first light. Looks like first light just got here early."
