## 16. Chase Through the Divide

The canyon announced itself before they saw it — a low, grinding hum in the hull, like the river clearing its throat.

Paloma stood at the wheel of the *Second Chance* and watched the walls close in ahead of them: two cliffs of red stone leaning toward each other across a channel of green water gone brown with flood. The Divide. Every river rat from here to Verren Reach had a story about it, and most of those stories ended with somebody's boat in pieces on the rocks.

Behind them, three gunboats came around the last bend in loose formation, black-hulled, engines running hot enough that she could smell the exhaust over the wet stone. Asque's money. Asque's hired teeth.

"Sefa," she called down the hatch. "How's our patient?"

Sefa's head appeared, streaked with grease, one eyebrow raised in permanent skepticism. "She'll hold. Probably. If you don't ask her to do anything heroic."

"No promises."

"Of course not." Sefa vanished again, and something clanged with the cheerful violence of a woman who had made peace with her circumstances.

Runi scrambled up onto the foredeck, hair plastered to her forehead, and squinted back at the gunboats. "They're gaining."

"They're always gaining. That's what makes it a chase." Paloma eased the wheel a hand's width to port, feeling for the current's spine. "What I want to know is whether we're faster than the water."

Runi looked at the canyon mouth, then at the river, then at Paloma, and did the arithmetic every child of the banks learned before they learned letters. "Nineteen days ago you said the window was closing. It's closed. Crest is tomorrow night, maybe the night after."

"So the river's on their side too."

"The river isn't on anybody's side." Runi said it without drama, the way she said everything about the water, and Paloma loved her a little for it.

Ilya emerged from below with his charts rolled under one arm like a man carrying an accusation. He'd barely slept since the contractors at the weir, and it showed in the gray under his eyes. "There's a narrows eleven kilometers in," he said. "Kessel's Teeth, the old charts call it. After that the canyon opens for a stretch, then pinches again at the Singing Gates. Two days through, if the water lets us."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then the water decides, and we find out what it decided afterward." He spread the chart on the deck box and weighted the corners with bolts. "There are side channels. Most of them are lies this time of year — drowned, or worse than drowned. But there's one, here—" his finger stopped on a smudge of blue west of the main channel, "—the Lantern Cut. My survey never confirmed it. The old Kethrian charts show it, though. They marked it with a light."

Paloma studied the smudge. "A light?"

"A beacon. The Kethrians lit beacons in the divide during flood season. Guided traffic through the cuts when the main channel was suicide." Ilya's voice went quiet in the middle of it, the way it always did when he touched anything Kethrian. Like speaking softly near a grave.

Doss came up the ladder then, moving slow, favoring the ribs he'd cracked at the weir. He looked at the chart, at the gunboats, at Paloma's face, and Paloma watched him do the same math Runi had done, only Doss's version had more exits in it. She'd seen him counting doors ever since the ledger page. Maybe before.

"So we run the main channel," Doss said, "and hope we're prettier than the rocks."

"We run the main channel until we're not," Paloma said. "And if the Teeth close on us, we take the Lantern Cut."

"Which nobody has sailed in forty years."

"Which the Kethrians lit with beacons because they wanted people to live." She met his eyes. "Somebody kept those lights. Somebody will have taught their grandchildren."

Doss held her gaze a moment longer than was comfortable, then nodded once and went to check the lines. Paloma watched him go and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the coffee.

* * *

The first day in the Divide was a lesson in humility delivered by geology.

The canyon took the wind away and gave back echoes, so that every sound arrived twice — once from ahead, once from the stone. Sefa ran the engine at a pitch that hurt to hear, coaxing power out of machinery that had already given more than its warranty promised. The *Second Chance* threaded the bends with maybe a boat-length to spare on either side, and twice the current grabbed her stern and swung her like a door, and both times Paloma caught her on the wheel with her arms burning and her teeth locked, and both times Runi shouted depth readings from the bow in a flat, unhurried voice that steadied everyone including, somehow, the river.

The gunboats gained. Of course they gained. They drew less water, carried less weight, and their pilots didn't love their vessel; they were paid to spend it.

By late afternoon the lead gunboat was close enough that Paloma could see the men on her deck — four of them, bored-looking, one eating something out of a tin. That was the thing about hired guns nobody warned you about: the banality. They weren't monsters. They were men with wages, doing a job, and the job was her.

"Paloma." Ilya's voice from behind her, careful. "The Teeth are ahead. You can hear them."

She could. Under the engine note, under the echo, there was a new sound building — a white, seething roar, the sound of a channel trying to pass a flood through a throat too narrow for it.

"Options," she said.

"Main channel through the Teeth in this water is a coin flip," Ilya said. "Lantern Cut branches off in eight hundred meters. If the beacon's lit."

"If it's not?"

"Then we're trading a coin flip for a cliff."

Sefa climbed up, wiping her hands. "For what it's worth, the engine would prefer the cut. Less hammering." A pause. "The engine and I have discussed it."

Runi, at the rail, suddenly pointed. "Smoke."

Not smoke — spray, rising off the Teeth like breath off a beast. And above it, faint against the darkening stone, a flicker. Yellow. Rhythmic.

Paloma's heart did something complicated. "That's not possible."

"Somebody lit it," Runi said simply. "River country remembers."

Eight hundred meters. Six hundred. The lead gunboat's skipper, seeing the spray, began to fall back — hired men were brave up to the exact price of their contract, and the Teeth exceeded it. But not far enough back. Not out of sight.

"Lantern Cut," Paloma said. "Sefa, ease her down. Runi, read me the water. Ilya — pray to whoever keeps dead nations, and make it loud."

The turn into the cut's mouth nearly took the starboard rail off. The water in there was different — faster, narrower, shot through with crosscurrents that fought each other like cats in a sack. The beacon light strobed yellow off the wet stone, and by it Paloma saw the channel: a slot of green water threading between black rock, marked, impossibly, with a line of small floating lamps — oil lanterns on cork rafts, renewed, tended, alive.

"Forty years," Ilya whispered beside her. "Forty years, and someone still lights them."

"Somebody's grandmother made them promise," Runi said.

The *Second Chance* went through the Lantern Cut with her crew holding their breath as one body, and came out the far side into a long, open reach of canyon where the walls fell back and the sky showed a strip of bruised evening violet, and the gunboats were gone — swallowed by the Teeth or turned back, it didn't matter which.

For ten minutes, nobody spoke. Then Sefa started laughing, low and helpless, and couldn't stop, and that broke the rest of them open too.

* * *

They anchored that night in a side slough behind a gravel bar, running no lights, sharing cold rice and the last of the smoked fish. The relief sat among them like a fifth crew member, warm and slightly drunk-making.

It was Doss who finally said what Paloma had been turning over since the beacon.

"That light wasn't luck," he said, poking at the fire with a stick. "Somebody knew we'd come through here. Somebody lit it for us."

"River country," Runi said, mouth full. "Word travels ahead of boats. My aunties' network moves faster than any navy skiff. Someone heard we were coming and walked the cut lighting lamps."

"For strangers."

"For people carrying Kethria home." Runi shrugged, as if that explained everything, and Paloma supposed it did.

But Doss wasn't looking at Runi. He was looking at the dark water, and his face had the particular stillness he got when he was deciding something and didn't want witnesses. Paloma had known him eleven years. She knew all his faces. This one scared her more than the gunboats had.

"Doss," she said quietly, later, when the others had turned in. He was sitting on the rail, looking upstream. "Talk to me."

"Nothing to say."

"You've been saying nothing loudly for three days."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You remember what I did. With the ledger page."

"I remember."

"I keep telling myself it bought us time. That selling Asque's dirty paper to the contractor was — tactical." He turned the stick in his fingers. "It wasn't. It was me buying myself a door, same as always. And now there's three gunboats full of men who know exactly where we are because I drew them a map with my own stupid hands."

"The beacon got us past the Teeth anyway."

"This time." He looked at her then, and in the firelight his face was older than eleven years of knowing could account for. "Paloma. There's a place coming up. The Narrows at Grayfall — you remember, Ilya's chart. Single channel, high walls, no side cuts. If they catch us in there, we're done. No beacon, no trick, no Sefa miracle."

"I know."

"There's a shelf rock halfway through. Old watchpost. You can see the whole run of the narrows from it." He said it lightly, too lightly, the way a man lays cards down when he doesn't want anyone to see how much they cost him. "A decoy light on that shelf, rigged to look like a boat running dark — engine noise on a timer, a lantern swinging. Gunboats see it, they commit to the narrows thinking it's us, they meet the flood crest in the worst water on the river."

Paloma felt it before she understood it, cold walking up her spine. "Who rigs it?"

"Me."

"No."

"You haven't let me finish."

"There's nothing to finish. You rig the light, and then what? You swim after us in flood water with cracked ribs?"

"I go in before you enter the narrows. Climb to the shelf. Spring the decoy when you're through. Then I come down the far side and follow the bank. Runi's kin have camps all through here — I've seen their fires. I walk out."

"The crest comes tomorrow night. The bank will be underwater."

"Then I climb higher." He smiled for real this time, and it was the worst smile she'd ever seen on him, because it was calm. "Paloma. I sold a page to save my own skin and it nearly killed everybody I — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Let me carry something for once that isn't mine to keep."

She wanted to say no. She opened her mouth to say no, and what came out instead was: "Why unasked? Why now?"

"Because asking means you'd have to decide, and I've watched you decide things for eleven years, and you always decide for other people first. I'm tired of being the reason you lose sleep." He hopped down off the rail. "Besides. Sefa needs help rigging the timer, and I'm the only one aboard who can't fix an engine, so I'm dead weight otherwise. Might as well be useful dead weight."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

* * *

They argued until midnight, the whole crew, in the low fierce way families argue — everyone talking over everyone, Runi insisting she could guide him along the high ledges partway, Sefa insisting the decoy needed two hands and offering herself, Ilya silently producing a coil of rope and setting it on the deck box, which ended the argument more effectively than any speech.

In the end it went the way Doss had planned it, because the plan was good, and because good plans don't care whose guilt powers them.

Sefa built the decoy through the small hours: a lantern on a pendulum arm driven by a clockwork timer scavenged from the bilge pump governor, a sheet-iron reflector to throw the beam wide, and — her masterstroke — a hollow steel drum half-submerged and fitted with a paddle wheel, so that the current would turn it and knock against the rock in a slow, irregular boom. Engine noise, or near enough in the dark. "It'll fool a tired skipper at two hundred meters," she said. "Beyond that, pray."

At gray dawn they put Doss ashore below the narrows, on a shingle spit still dry, though the water had climbed the stones overnight like something patient. He stood on the spit with the decoy rig bundled on his back and the rope coil over his shoulder, and he looked smaller than Paloma had ever seen him, and larger too.

"Grayfall Narrows," he said. "You'll come through around midday. I spring it when your stern clears the second bend. Then I'm down the far side and gone, and I'll meet you at the ferry ruin below the Gates, or I'll meet you at Verren Reach, or — " he shrugged — "you'll carve my name on something and complain about my handwriting."

"Your handwriting is terrible," Runi said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it.

"See? Family." He gripped Paloma's forearm, hard, diver to diver, the old grip. "Open the chest's truth, Paloma. Don't you dare keep it. Don't you dare become them."

"I won't."

"Promise me the boring version. Promise me archive stamps and paperwork and lawyers. Promise me nothing heroic."

"I promise you paperwork," she said, and meant it with her whole chest, and saw him believe it, and that belief was the heaviest thing either of them carried that morning.

They pushed off. Paloma did not look back, because looking back is how helmsmen hit rocks, and also because she knew if she looked back she'd order the boat turned around, and he'd have jumped overboard rather than let her.

* * *

Grayfall Narrows swallowed the *Second Chance* at noon.

The walls rose sheer on either side, close enough some places that the rail nearly brushed stone, and the river inside them was a single muscular animal, deep and fast and utterly committed. Paloma steered by feel and faith, Runi calling the water, Ilya braced at the chart table calling distances from memory, Sefa below decks holding the engine at a scream.

First bend. Second bend. The stern cleared the rock shelf, and somewhere above and behind them, right on time, a lantern bloomed against the canyon wall — swinging, warm, unmistakably a boat running dark — and a moment later the drum began its slow false heartbeat against the stone.

*Boom.* Pause. *Boom-boom.*

Behind them, faint but growing, the gunboat engines changed pitch. Committed. Hungry. Chasing a ghost of iron and clockwork into the worst water on the river while the real quarry slipped out the far end into the widening light.

Paloma held the wheel steady and did not celebrate, because celebration is for people whose crew is all accounted for, and hers was standing alone on a shelf of rock above a rising flood with a clockwork lantern and a promise.

They cleared the narrows as the sun went gold. The canyon opened. Ahead, far downstream, the first smudge of the Singing Gates showed against the sky, and beyond it, two days' hard running, Verren Reach.

Runi appeared at her elbow, watching the water rise along the banks. "Crest is tomorrow night," she said quietly. "My kin's message said the archive closes its doors at crest. Whatever's inside stays; whatever's outside waits till the water drops."

"Then we're ahead of it."

"Barely." Runi looked back upriver, toward the narrows, toward the shelf. "He'll make it," she said, in the tone of someone ordering the river to agree.

Paloma put her hand on the girl's shoulder and said nothing, because there was nothing true enough yet to say.

Then Sefa's head came up the hatch, and her face was wrong.

"Paloma. Listen."

Paloma listened. Under the engine, under the water, from far behind — from the direction of the narrows — a sound was rolling down the canyon. Not the drum. Not the flood.

Three gunboats, running the narrows at full throttle, and above their engines, thin and rising over the stone, a fourth sound that made her blood stop:

A whistle. Long-short-long. River country signal. *Man overboard.*

And then, underneath it, almost lost, another whistle — weaker, farther upstream, from the high dark water where no boat should be.

Long-long-short.

*I'm alive. Come find me.*
