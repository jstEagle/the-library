## 17. Verren Reach

They came down the last stretch of the river with the water chasing them like a debt collector.

Paloma had seen floods before — every salvage diver on the Vessarine coast had a flood story, and most of them ended with *and that's why I don't dive in flood season* — but she had never seen anything like this. The river had spent nineteen days gathering itself in the high country, swallowing tributaries whole, and now it arrived at Verren Reach all at once, brown and muscular and utterly without patience.

The lower town was already going under.

From the deck of the *Second Chance*, Paloma watched the water take it street by street. First the market stalls along the quay, their awnings blooming up like drowned flowers. Then the warehouse district, the river sliding through doorways with the courtesy of an invited guest. A man rowed past them hauling a goat in his boat, the goat looking profoundly unimpressed by the entire enterprise. Somewhere behind the levee wall, a bell rang — not an alarm, Paloma realized, but a kind of clock. The town rang it at every hand's-breadth of rise, so people could keep score against the water.

"Eight bells," Runi said quietly. She stood at the bow rail with her elbows on the wood, watching her kin's country drown for the fortieth time in recorded history. "That means the archive steps are gone."

"The archive steps are *gone*?" Sefa called from the engine hatch, her voice pitched to carry over the diesel clatter. "Then how do we get in? Swim?"

"You go up," Runi said. "Archive's built on the high knoll. Whole building's on stilts, like a heron. The archivists plan for this. They've got boats."

"They've got *boats*?"

"It's Verren Reach," Runi said, with the tone of someone explaining rain to a fish. "Everyone here has boats."

Ilya stood at the wheel beside Paloma, one hand light on the spokes, his eyes doing the thing they always did — reading the water, measuring it, filing it away. He'd been quiet since the canyon. Quieter than usual, which for Ilya was saying something. But when he spoke now, his voice was steady.

"Approach channel runs along the north side of the knoll. Old ferry cut. It'll have two meters even at crest, if the charts are honest." He glanced sideways at her. "They were honest forty years ago. We'll find out about today."

"Take us in," Paloma said.

The *Second Chance* turned toward the drowning town, and Paloma felt something loosen in her chest that she didn't entirely trust. Nineteen days of rapids and contractors and Asque's smiling treachery, and here was the end of it: a neutral archive on a hill, where truth went to be stamped, indexed, and shelved. She'd imagined this moment a hundred times since Kethria's Last Harbor. In most versions, someone wept. In several, there was applause.

In none of them, she would later reflect, had she imagined a man named Undersecretary Pell.

* * *

The ferry cut held, barely. Sefa brought them alongside a stone stair that rose out of the floodwater like the spine of some submerged animal, and a flat-bottomed skiff put out from an upper landing to meet them — poled by two young women in oilskins who looked at the battered salvage tug, its patched hull and salt-stained rails, with expressions of polite, professional dismay.

"Berthing is closed due to crest conditions," said the taller one, in the tone of someone reciting scripture. "You'll need to anchor mid-channel."

"We've come from the Vessarine coast," Paloma said. "Nineteen days upriver. We're carrying a claim for the archive. An inheritance claim. Kethrian deed."

Something flickered across the young woman's face — interest, quickly filed away behind procedure like a coin into a locked drawer. "Claims are received at the Receiving Office. The Receiving Office is open until the sixth bell of afternoon. It is currently—" she consulted a watch chained inside her oilskin— "the fourth bell. You have time, if you hurry. The stairs are flooded above the third landing; you'll want the gantry walkway, east side."

She paused, then added, almost as an afterthought: "Welcome to Verren Reach."

They anchored mid-channel because there was nothing else to do, and took the skiff in with the deed wrapped in oilcloth against Paloma's chest, where it had lived for eleven days, warm as a second heartbeat. Doss insisted on coming ashore too, though he could barely walk straight after two days without sleep, and Runi came to translate the local dialect of bureaucracy, which she claimed was worse than the river dialect and considerably more dangerous.

The gantry walkway swayed over the flood like a rope bridge in a fever dream. Below it, the water slid past carrying fence posts, a washbasin, half a rowboat, and — memorably — a single kitchen chair, upright and dignified, as though its owner had simply stepped away for a moment. The archive building loomed ahead on its great timber stilts, grey stone and dark wood, older than any nation Paloma could name. Carved over its entrance, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, ran an inscription that Runi translated aloud:

*"What is given to the water may be kept by the word."*

"That's either very beautiful or very ominous," Sefa said.

"With archivists," Runi said, "it's usually both."

Inside, the Receiving Office smelled of lamp oil, paper, and the particular dustiness of a place where nothing has been thrown away in four hundred years. Shelves rose to the ceiling in ranks, ladders on wheels between them, and clerks moved through the aisles with the unhurried gravity of herons wading. At a long counter of black oak sat a man of perhaps sixty, silver-haired, spectacles on a ribbon around his neck, engaged in the act of writing something in a ledger with a pen that must have cost more than the *Second Chance*'s anchor chain.

He finished his line before he looked up. Paloma respected that, in a way. It meant he wasn't pretending.

"State your business," he said.

Paloma set the oilcloth bundle on the counter and unwrapped it. The deed of inheritance lay there, its Kethrian seal dark as dried blood, the brass astrolabe beside it catching the lamplight. "My name is Paloma Oyelaran. Salvage diver, licensed out of Corvessa Harbor. Eleven days ago I recovered this from a wreck off the Vessarine coast — a chest bearing the seal of Kethria. Inside was this deed of inheritance, naming a bearer. I'm the bearer. I'm here to make a claim."

There. She'd said it. Nineteen days of blood and flood water, compressed into six sentences.

Undersecretary Pell — for it was he; a small brass plate on the counter said so, and Paloma would learn that Pell never went anywhere without his plate, in case someone mistook him for a mere secretary — regarded the deed for a long moment. Then he put on his spectacles, drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, and said:

"Form Fourteen-C."

"I'm sorry?"

"Recovery of historical materials in navigable waters. Form Fourteen-C, in triplicate. You'll also need Form Nine for declaration of bearer status, Form Twenty-Two for provenance attestation, and — " he peered at the astrolabe — "if that instrument predates the Kethrian dissolution, Form Thirty-One, Cultural Artifacts, which requires a separate schedule of descriptive detail. Do you have the chest itself?"

"The chest was stolen," Paloma said. "By the man who financed our expedition. Working for your Admiralty, or very close to it. There are naval contractors behind us on the river right now, probably no more than a day back, and —"

"Was the theft reported to any civil authority?"

"There *is* no civil authority out there! That's rather the point!"

Pell made a small note. "So the answer is no."

Paloma became aware that her hands had curled into fists on the counter, and that Sefa had drifted closer in the way she did when she thought Paloma might hit someone. Behind her, Runi was reading the posted fee schedule with mounting horror.

"Undersecretary," Ilya said, stepping forward with the mild, reasonable air he wore like armor, "perhaps you could help us understand the sequence. If we complete the forms tonight, when would the claim be heard?"

"The Claims Panel convenes weekly," Pell said. "Next session is in five days."

"The flood crests in three," Runi said. "After that, the river road's gone for a month. Anyone coming up from the coast — say, contractors with gunboats and a stolen chest — would be cut off. So would any evidence they bring. Or any witnesses."

"Yes," Pell agreed pleasantly. "That is a known feature of the calendar."

Paloma stared at him. "You're telling me the timing works in their favor."

"I'm telling you the timing is the timing." Pell removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, and for just a moment — a flicker, gone as fast as it came — his eyes over the rims were not pleasant at all. They were tired, and sharp, and very old. "Miss Oyelaran. I have sat at this counter for thirty-one years. In that time I have received claims carried by war heroes, smugglers, one pirate queen, a woman who swam the estuary in winter holding the documents above her head, and a man who arrived with a parrot that recited the deed from memory. Every single one of them believed their urgency was exceptional. Every single one of them wanted the forms waived." He set the spectacles back on his nose. "And every single one of them, eventually, filled out the forms. Because the forms are not the enemy of the truth, Miss Oyelaran. The forms are the reason the truth survives. Passion burns. Procedure endures. Now — Form Fourteen-C. Shall I fetch you a pen, or will you be needing a moment first?"

Paloma needed a moment.

* * *

They took the moment in the archive's reading room, a long gallery of windows overlooking the flood, where other claimants and petitioners sat among the shelves waiting out the water the way people wait out weather. An old woman was teaching a boy his letters from a ledger of shipping manifests. Two merchants argued in whispers over a map. Someone had left a pot of tea on the communal table, and Sefa poured cups for everyone with the solemnity of a sacrament.

"Five days," Sefa said, dropping into a chair. "We hold this room for five days while the river does whatever it's going to do, and then we stand in front of a panel and hope they care."

"They'll care," Ilya said. "That's not the problem. The problem is they'll care *correctly*. Slowly. In order."

"And meanwhile Asque—" Paloma stopped. Saying his name still tasted wrong, like drinking from a cup you'd found out someone else had used. "Meanwhile Asque is a day behind us with the chest, Admiralty lawyers, and a head start on knowing exactly which forms to fill out. His people wrote half the procedures in this country. They know how to make procedure a weapon."

"Then we'd better learn faster," Ilya said.

It was Runi, surprisingly, who broke the gloom. She'd wandered off along the shelves and come back cradling a slim volume like a rescued kitten. "Look at this. *A Citizen's Companion to Archive Procedure.* Written two hundred years ago by somebody who clearly hated archivists." She flipped it open. "Listen to this bit. 'The Panel shall hear any bearer's claim presented with proper form, but the Panel is bound also to receive testimony relevant to the provenance of the claim, such testimony to be entered on the record whether or not the Panel finds it convenient.'" She looked up, grinning. "Testimony. On the record. Whether or not they find it *convenient*."

"So?" Sefa said.

"So — Ilya." Runi turned to him, and her grin softened into something steadier. "Thirty years of what you know. The survey. The scuttle charges. All of it. That's not just background anymore. That's *testimony*, and once it's on the record, nobody can take it back. Not the Admiralty, not lawyers, not anybody. It becomes part of the word."

The reading room went quiet around their table. Ilya looked down at his tea for a long moment, and Paloma watched him carry the weight of it — thirty years of silence, about to become thirty years of record.

"I'll need," he said finally, "a great deal of paper."

"Form Forty-Four," said a dry voice behind them.

They turned. Pell stood there, ledger under his arm, having materialized with the soundless competence of a man who had spent three decades learning where everyone in his building was at all times. He set a thick stack of blank forms on their table, aligned precisely with its edge.

"For extended testimony," he said. "You'll want to begin tonight. The Panel respects thoroughness." He paused, and adjusted his spectacles, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to something almost — almost — human. "For what it is worth, Miss Oyelaran, the woman who swam the estuary? Her claim succeeded. It took nine years, and she filled out four hundred forms, and the parrot man failed because his bird died mid-recitation and he hadn't kept a copy. But hers succeeded. The archive remembers what everyone else forgets. That is the entire point of the building." He gathered his ledger. "Fifth bell rings in twenty minutes. The Receiving Office closes. I suggest you start writing."

He turned to go, then stopped, and added without turning around:

"Oh — and Miss Oyelaran. The contractors you mentioned. Gunboats, Admiralty lawyers, a day behind you." Now he did turn, and his face was perfectly, bureaucratically bland. "If they arrive during crest, they will be required to anchor mid-channel and wait like everyone else. If they arrive with improperly completed forms, they will wait considerably longer. Verren Reach has been neutral for four hundred years precisely because we are *extremely annoying* to powerful people. Good evening."

He left. Sefa stared after him.

"I think," she said slowly, "that was encouragement."

"I think," Paloma said, picking up a pen, "that was the strangest ally we've got. And I'll take it."

* * *

They wrote through the night.

The reading room lamps burned low and golden against the dark windows, where the flood pressed its black face to the glass, patient as everything else in this country. Runi fetched tea and bread and, at midnight, a plate of honey cakes from a clerk who had taken a liking to her. Sefa drafted diagrams of the engine work and the hull patch, documenting every repair with the loving precision of a woman who believed machines deserved honest paperwork even when people didn't. Doss, who had slept twelve hours and woken looking almost like himself, copied out the ledger page — the one he'd pulled from Asque's papers, the payments from the Admiralty Office dated before the chest was ever found — in careful, deliberate script, and when he handed it to Paloma he wouldn't meet her eyes, and she didn't make him.

"He sold information once already," Doss said quietly, when the others were out of earshot. "Me. To the contractor skiff. For passage out." He swallowed. "I never told you."

"I know," Paloma said.

"You *know*?"

"Doss. You came back. You stayed behind in the canyon to spring a decoy light for strangers on a boat you'd only known a month." She pushed the honey cakes toward him. "People are allowed to be the worst version of themselves for a while, as long as they don't stay there. Fill out Form Forty-Four."

Outside, the bell rang the hours, and the river rose, and somewhere downriver in the dark, Paloma knew, boats were fighting upstream through the flood with lawyers aboard and a lead-lined chest lashed below decks, carrying the past toward this building like a siege army.

Let them come, she thought, dipping her pen again. Let them anchor mid-channel. Let them wait.

At dawn, gray light came up over the water, and with it a knock at the reading room door — one of the skiff girls from the landing, wet to the waist, breathless.

"Beg pardon. Message from the watch post downstream." She caught her breath. "There's boats coming up the ferry cut. Flying no colors. And the tall one at the front — the watch says he asked for you by name, miss. Asked for the bearer." She swallowed. "Said he'd come to settle the estate."

Paloma set down her pen.

"Well," she said. "I suppose we'd better finish the forms."
