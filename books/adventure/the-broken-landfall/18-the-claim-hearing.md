## 18. The Claim Hearing

The archive at Verren Reach smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the river itself — as though forty years of floodwater had soaked into the stone and never quite left. Paloma sat on a bench outside the hearing chamber with her hands flat on her knees, watching a clerk dust a shelf of ledgers that had probably not been opened since before she was born.

"You're doing the thing," Runi said beside her.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you breathe like you're hauling up an anchor."

Paloma exhaled slowly through her nose. "I'm breathing like a person who is about to argue with lawyers."

"Lawyers," Runi said, with the contempt only a seventeen-year-old river rat could pack into two syllables. "They can't even swim."

"They don't need to swim, Runi. That's rather the point of them."

Sefa came down the corridor at her usual rolling gait, wiping engine grease off her hands with a rag that was mostly grease by now. She'd spent the morning trying to make herself presentable and had achieved something Paloma privately thought of as *drowned mechanic formal*: clean shirt, hair tied back, one smear of oil along her jaw like a beauty mark gone wrong.

"Chamber's open," Sefa said. "Archivist Vess wants us in ten minutes. And — heads up." She lowered her voice. "There's a boat at the lower dock. Navy grey, no flag flying, but I know the hull lines. And Asque was seen walking up from it an hour ago, dry as a sermon."

Paloma felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it did when a wreck shifted under her. "He has the chest."

"He has the chest," Sefa confirmed. "And he has company. A lot of very well-dressed company."

Ilya was already inside when they filed in. He sat at the long table on the claimants' side, his forged charts stacked neatly before him, his testimony written out in the small, precise hand of a man who had spent thirty years making maps nobody was supposed to question. He looked, Paloma thought, older than he had yesterday. Lighter, too, in some strange way — like a man who had finally set down ballast and wasn't sure yet whether the ship would float without it.

"Paloma." He nodded to her. Then, to Runi: "Younger Oyelaran. Sefa." He hesitated. "Is Doss—"

"No," Paloma said quietly.

Ilya closed his eyes for a moment. "Then we speak for him too."

* * *

Archivist Vess turned out to be a narrow woman of about sixty with spectacles on a chain and the unhurried air of someone who had outlasted four governments and expected to outlast this one. She did not sit so much as settle, arranging her papers with the tenderness other people reserved for grandchildren.

"This is a claim hearing under the Verren Protocols," she said, once everyone was seated. "Which means three things. One: this chamber is neutral ground. No navy, no merchant house, no patron holds authority here. Two:" — her gaze traveled, without hurry, across the Admiralty lawyers who had entered behind Corvel Asque, five of them in dove-grey coats, carrying document cases like ammunition boxes — "everything said in this room becomes public record. Permanently. Irrevocably. Some people find that inconvenient. Three: I will decide what constitutes evidence, and I do not care how expensive your coats are."

One of the lawyers, a silver-haired man with a voice like oiled hinges, cleared his throat. "Archivist, we object to the composition of—"

"Objection noted," said Vess, and began reading Paloma's filing.

Asque sat across the table, and Paloma made herself look at him properly for the first time since the supply stop where he'd put a gun in Doss's ribs and taken the chest. He looked exactly as he always had: immaculate, reasonable, a man whose hands were so clean you could see your future in them. Only his eyes moved too fast now, flicking to the door, to the lawyers, to the chest — which sat between his side and hers on the table like a guest invited by both families.

He caught her looking and gave her a small, regretful smile. The kind of smile that said *none of this was personal, and also I will win.*

"Ms. Oyelaran," Vess said. "Your filing claims bearer's inheritance under the Kethrian Succession Statutes. Present your instrument."

Paloma stood. Her legs held, which surprised her. She unrolled the deed — wrapped now in oilcloth, because some habits keep you alive — and laid it flat.

"I recovered this chest," she said, "from a wreck in the Vessarine shallows, on a dive paid for by me and my crew, on a wreck the Admiralty pays boats to stay away from. The chest is lead-lined. The seal is Kethria's. The lock opens to a date, not a key — the date the nation fell." She let that sit. "Inside were documents, an astrolabe, and this deed. It names a bearer. It says the bearer may carry home a claim. I am the bearer. Not because I was chosen. Because I was the one who picked it up."

"Recovered from open water," said the silver-haired lawyer smoothly. "Salvage law is clear. Abandoned property belongs to the salvor or the crown. There is no living Kethria to hold title, therefore there is no inheritance. Therefore this woman holds a box of old paper and nothing more."

"That's the argument?" Runi whispered from the bench behind Paloma. "That's their whole argument? They murdered a whole country and their best line is *finders keepers*?"

"Runi," Sefa murmured, "let the professionals flounder."

Vess looked over her spectacles at the lawyer. "And your client's interest in a box of old paper, Master Quill? The Admiralty dispatched three vessels and retained private contractors to recover it. That is a considerable expenditure for nothing more."

Quill's smile didn't waver, but Paloma saw his hand pause on his document case. "The Admiralty recovers its own property wherever it finds it."

"Its own property," Vess repeated, writing something down. "How interesting. Ms. Oyelaran, continue."

"Ilya," Paloma said. "Tell them what you told me."

Ilya rose. His hands weren't steady, but his voice was. Thirty years of silence had apparently been rehearsing this.

"My name is Ilya Vosk. I was a cartographer with the Naval Survey Office. In the year of the fall, I helped chart the Kethrian coast for a blockade that was officially a quarantine. I surveyed the harbors while the grain ships were turned away. I mapped the river mouths while the interior starved." He laid his written testimony on the table, square to its edges. "The famine was not war. It was policy. It was signed. I have spent three decades drawing coastlines around the hole where a country used to be, and I am done."

He read. He read the convoy schedules, the requisition orders, the names of the admirals — names that still headed the navy, carved into buildings Paloma had walked past all her life without reading them properly. The chamber was very quiet. Even the lawyers stopped shuffling.

When he finished, Vess said, "This testimony will be entered in full."

"Archivist—" Quill began.

"In full," Vess repeated, in a tone that ended conversations.

* * *

It was Asque who changed the shape of the room.

He waited until the afternoon session, until the light through the high windows had gone amber and everyone's patience was worn thin, and then he stood — unhurried, apologetic, the picture of a reasonable man forced into unreasonable straits.

"Archivist. Before this goes further, I should correct the record. Because the record, I'm afraid, has been fed a romance." He gestured, and one of the lawyers unlocked the chest and lifted out the documents, fanning them on the table like a card trick. "These instruments were stolen from my expedition. From my vessel. By Ms. Oyelaran and her crew, who were in my employ. I funded the dive. I bore the risk. The salvage is mine under every statute this chamber recognizes — and these people are thieves who happened to be standing closer to the truth than I was."

"There it is," Sefa muttered. "Clean hands Corvel. Never gets them dirty. Just hires someone else's."

"And I'll go further," Asque continued, warming, turning to address the chamber as if it were a shareholders' meeting. "Look at them. A diver, an engineer, a runaway cartographer, a child. Honest people, desperate people — I hired them because desperation is cheap. They didn't find this inheritance. They *stumbled into it*, and everything since has been them deciding that a fortune belongs to whoever wants it most." He spread his hands. "I don't blame them. I offered them money. They wanted meaning instead. Meaning doesn't feed anyone, Archivist. I've watched that lesson taught on every coast from here to the Vessarine."

Paloma listened to all of it with a strange calm settling over her, the calm she got forty feet down, when the current tried to tell her which way to think.

Because he was right about one thing, and it was the wrong thing, and she could feel the trap in it. If this became a dispute about ownership — his claim versus hers, theft versus salvage, his money versus her luck — then the room would spend its days arguing about possession, and the admirals' signatures would gather dust in a footnote.

She stood.

"Archivist Vess. May I ask the chamber a question?"

Vess inclined her head, wary and interested at once.

Paloma turned to face Asque fully. "Corvel. You're right. I stumbled into it. So answer me honestly, in front of the record." She tapped the deed. "Who does an inheritance belong to when its owners were murdered?"

The chamber went still.

"Because that's the actual question," Paloma said. "Not who found the box. Not who paid for the boat. Every person named in that deed is dead. Every heir listed in those documents was starved by men whose names are still on the Admiralty rolls. There is no one left to inherit — except everyone they left behind. The descendants. The survivors' children, scattered up and down this coast, carrying surnames the maps forgot." She looked at the lawyers, at Vess, at Asque. "You want to call it salvage. Salvage is what you take off a wreck nobody's mourning. This isn't salvage. This is a debt, and debts don't belong to whoever's holding the paper. They belong to whoever was owed."

Asque smiled the regretful smile again. "A moving speech, Ms. Oyelaran. But sentiment isn't a legal theory. The Succession Statutes require a bearer. Fine — you're the bearer. Then claim it. Claim the frozen funds, the reparations estate, all of it. Take your fortune." His voice dropped, went almost gentle, and Paloma understood suddenly that this was the real blade, unsheathed at last. "That's all I ever asked. Take it, and withdraw the allegations against the Admiralty. Sign a settlement. You'd be the wealthiest woman on the coast, and everyone walks away whole. Isn't that kinder than a war you can't win?"

And there it was — the same quiet offer power had been making her since the day she hauled that chest aboard, dressed now in lawyers and lamplight. *Take the money. Stop asking questions. Be rich instead of right.*

Behind her, she heard Runi shift, ready to say something catastrophic, and Sefa's hand land gently on the girl's shoulder.

Paloma looked at the chest. At the astrolabe that had crossed a drowned harbor to reach her. At Ilya, who had carried his silence for thirty years and set it down in front of strangers. At the empty chair where Doss should have been, arguing loudly for the wrong side of everything.

"No," she said.

Quill leaned forward. "Ms. Oyelaran, if you refuse the claim—"

"I'm not refusing the claim." She turned to Vess. "Archivist. I haven't decided yet what I'm going to do with it. But I know what it isn't. It isn't his. And it isn't a settlement price." She held Asque's gaze. "You keep asking what the inheritance is worth. You've never once asked what it cost. That's why you'll never own it."

For the first time since the supply stop, Corvel Asque had nothing prepared. She watched him search for the next reasonable sentence and not find it, and she thought: *there you are. The man under the clean hands.*

Vess wrote for a long moment. Then she removed her spectacles.

"This chamber will recess until tomorrow morning," she said. "At which time the bearer will state her disposition of the claim, and this archive will rule on the criminal referrals arising from Mr. Vosk's testimony." Her eyes rested briefly, coolly, on the Admiralty benches. "All of them."

The lawyers gathered their cases like men collecting hailstones. Asque paused beside Paloma on his way out, close enough that only she heard him.

"You could still be rich," he said softly.

"I know," Paloma said. "That's how I know I don't want it."

He looked at her for a moment — really looked, the way a man looks at a current he has misjudged — and then he walked out into the amber light with his lawyers folding around him like grey water closing over a hull.

Runi let out a breath that seemed to have been held since breakfast. "So what *are* you going to do tomorrow?"

Paloma looked at the deed, at the dead nation's seal, at the name of a bearer who was never meant to be her.

"I'm going to ask the archivist," she said, "who keeps things when nobody's allowed to own them."

From the doorway, a clerk appeared, pale and quick. "Archivist Vess requests the bearer," he said. "Now, if you please. There's been a development at the dock."
