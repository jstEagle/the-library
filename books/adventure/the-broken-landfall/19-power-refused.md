## 19. Power Refused

The night before the hearing, Paloma did not sleep. She sat in the archive's reading room with the deed spread out before her under a lamp the archivists had lit for her — a courtesy, they said, and also a way of keeping an eye on her, though they were polite enough not to say so.

The archive at Verren Reach was built like a fortress that had decided, somewhere along the way, that it would rather be a library. The walls were a meter thick. The doors were iron. But the windows were tall and generous, and the shelves went up so far that young archivists climbed ladders on wheeled rails, and the whole place smelled of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of the river, which had swallowed the lower town and now lapped politely at the foundation stones as if asking to be let in.

Paloma had spent the evening reading the deed again. Not because she needed to. Because it was the last night it would be hers, whatever *hers* meant, and she wanted to understand what she was giving away.

The inheritance clause was written in the careful, formal hand of a dead nation's lawyers. *To the bearer of this instrument, duly presented before a neutral archive of record, is conveyed the whole of the Kethrian Restitution Fund, frozen in trust these forty years, together with all claims, titles, and standing arising therefrom.* There was a number. She had made Ilya say it out loud, twice, because she hadn't believed it the first time. Enough to buy fleets. Enough to buy the Admiralty and the dockyards and half the Vessarine coast and still have change for a new engine for the *Second Chance*.

Enough, said a small voice in her that sounded uncomfortably like Corvel Asque, to never dive a cold wreck again. To never count pennies for diesel. To buy Doss his own boat. To put Runi through whatever school took fancy to her. To fix Sefa's hands, which ached in the rain, with the best surgeons money could hire.

"You're doing it again," said Sefa, from the doorway.

Paloma looked up. Sefa had a way of arriving with two mugs and no announcement. She set one down next to the deed and lowered herself into the opposite chair with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions about stone floors.

"Doing what?"

"Negotiating with yourself. I can see the numbers moving behind your eyes. Like a ledger with a leak."

Paloma almost smiled. "Is it that obvious?"

"You've been staring at that page for four hours and you've turned it exactly twice. A woman reading reads. A woman arguing turns pages like she's looking for the exit." Sefa blew on her tea. "Which side are you arguing for?"

Paloma didn't answer right away. Outside, the river made its low, patient sound against the archive walls — the sound it had made her whole life, the sound of the world's largest creditor waiting to be paid.

"When I was eleven," Paloma said, "my mother took a contract to survey the navy's new breakwater. Good money. She came back with a story she never told twice — about how the survey crew found old foundations under the harbor silt, Kethrian foundations, and the naval officer in charge made them log them as natural rock. Made them sign for it. My mother signed. She said it was that or the breakwater contract, and the breakwater contract fed us for a year." She turned the page, as if looking for the exit. "She never dove the same way after. Like the water knew."

Sefa said nothing, which was one of the things Paloma loved about her. Sefa understood that some silences were load-bearing.

"Forty years," Paloma went on. "Forty years of divers like me finding Kethrian things and logging them as natural rock. That's what the silence costs. It's not one big lie. It's ten thousand small ones, and every one of them had a family to feed." She looked up. "And tomorrow they're going to offer me a fortune to keep telling them."

"They already tried the other way," Sefa said. "Gunboats. Contractors. A hole in our hull."

"Yes, and look how well that went for them." Paloma did smile this time. "The river's on our side. Or at least it's on nobody's side, which comes to the same thing."

Sefa wrapped her hands around her mug. "You know what you're going to do."

"I know what I'm going to do. I'm trying to find out if I'm brave enough to mean it."

"And?"

Paloma looked at the deed. At the dead nation's careful hand. At the name of the bearer, which was hers now, and had been hers since she hauled a lead-lined chest out of water that the Admiralty pretended held nothing at all.

"And I think," she said, "that power is like salvage. You can keep a wreck's bell on your mantel and tell yourself you earned it. Or you can return it to the families of the drowned and let them decide what it was worth." She pushed the mug aside and squared the deed's corners. "I've spent my whole life taking things off the dead, Sefa. Tomorrow I'm going to give something back."

* * *

The hearing was held in the archive's Great Registry Room, which had clearly been designed to make everyone in it feel like a document being filed. The benches were oak. The archivists sat at a long table beneath the seal of the Concordance — the treaty of neutral houses that had kept the archive sovereign through two wars and one flood. There were nine of them, and they looked like nine variations on the same disapproving grandmother.

Paloma wore her good coat, which was to say the coat with only one patch. Ilya sat beside her in a suit he had borrowed from an archivist two sizes too large, looking like a cartographer dressed by a generous ghost. Runi, Sefa, and Doss filled the bench behind them. Doss had shaved. Nobody commented on it, but everybody noticed.

Across the aisle sat the other side: Corvel Asque, gray and immaculate, flanked by three Admiralty lawyers whose uniforms were so crisp they seemed to have been ironed by machinery. Behind them, on a wheeled trolley, sat the chest — recovered, sealed with fresh wax, stamped with the Admiralty's own evidence mark, as though the navy had always been its rightful keeper.

Paloma looked at the chest for a long moment. Asque caught her looking and inclined his head, very slightly, the way a man tips his hat to a horse he means to buy.

The senior archivist — a small, dry woman named Prendel, whose spectacles appeared to be older than the Concordance — opened the session with the enthusiasm of a woman reading a shipping manifest.

"This is a claim hearing under Article Fourteen of the Concordance," she said. "The claimant is one bearer, Paloma Oyelaran, presenting an instrument of inheritance originating from the sovereign nation of Kethria. The archive will hear testimony. The archive will not hear theater." She looked, without appearing to look, at the Admiralty lawyers. "Claimant. Present your instrument."

Paloma stood. Her hands were steady, which surprised her.

"I present the deed of inheritance of the nation of Kethria," she said, and laid it on the table. "Recovered from the wreck of the *Kethrian Grace* in the Vessarine shallows, in a lead-lined chest bearing the Kethrian seal. I'll let the document speak for itself. It's better at this than I am."

Prendel read. The room waited. Somewhere above them, a ladder rolled along a shelf; a young archivist, unable to help herself, was watching from the gallery.

"The instrument appears authentic," Prendel said at last, in the tone of a woman conceding a point to a fish. "Provisionally. The archive will require the testimony of the surveying witness." Her eyes went to Ilya. "You are Ilya Vosk. Formerly of the Admiralty Cartographic Office."

"I am."

"You surveyed the Vessarine shallows thirty-one years ago."

"I did." Ilya stood. His borrowed suit made him look like a man standing inside his own apology. "And I am prepared to testify to what that survey was actually for."

The Admiralty lawyers stirred. The senior one — a compact woman named Halloran, whose name Paloma had learned from the contractors' manifest they'd captured at the weir — rose with the smoothness of someone who had rehearsed rising.

"Before the witness testifies," Halloran said, "the Admiralty wishes to address the question of standing."

"Standing," Prendel repeated, without inflection.

"The chest was recovered from Admiralty-restricted waters. Its contents were removed without permit. The Admiralty asserts that the instrument is evidence in an ongoing theft investigation, that the claimant is a person of interest in that investigation, and that any claim founded upon stolen property is void." Halloran let that settle, then turned, and here it came — the thing Paloma had known was coming since the gunboats. "However. The Admiralty is prepared to be generous."

"Generous," said Prendel.

"The Admiralty recognizes the claimant's... difficult circumstances. It is prepared to withdraw the criminal investigation entirely. No charges. No pursuit. Full amnesty for the claimant and her crew. In exchange, the claimant will withdraw this claim and surrender the instrument to Admiralty custody." Halloran paused, and then, with the delicacy of a surgeon naming the fee: "Additionally, the Admiralty is authorized to offer the claimant a personal settlement. One million marks. Paid within the week."

The room did not gasp, because archivists do not gasp, but Paloma felt the whole registry lean toward her like a tide.

One million marks. She thought of the *Second Chance*'s hull, patched with river-kin lumber and Sefa's stubbornness. She thought of Doss's face when she'd told him what the chest might be worth — the way want had moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice. She thought of her mother, signing the false survey, feeding her family on the price of a lie.

"May I ask a question?" Paloma said.

Prendel nodded. "The claimant may address the room."

Paloma turned to Halloran. "Whose money is it? The settlement. Where does a million marks come from, the week before a hearing, from an office that's spent forty years pretending Kethria never existed?"

Halloran's expression did not change, which was itself an answer. "The settlement is a matter of Admiralty internal accounts."

"Internal accounts." Paloma nodded slowly. "That's what I thought." She turned to the archivists. "Then let me answer the question this hearing is actually about. You've heard the Admiralty's position: the chest was stolen, so the claim is void. But here's what the deed says, and here's what Ilya Vosk will swear to, and here's what the murals in Kethria's last harbor show: the owners of this inheritance weren't ruined by war or weather. They were murdered. A famine, manufactured, signed by admirals whose names are still on the navy's letterhead. So the real question isn't whether I stole a chest." She looked at Halloran. "It's who an inheritance belongs to when its owners were killed for it. And I'll tell you what I think. It belongs to the survivors. To their children, and their children's children, scattered up and down this coast for forty years, diving other people's wrecks and logging other people's lies."

She turned back to Prendel.

"So here is my claim. Not the Admiralty's version, and not mine."

She picked up the deed, and her hands were still steady, and she was grateful for that, because inside she was anything but.

"I surrender the entire inheritance to this archive. As public record. The full fund — every mark of it — to be distributed as reparations to the descendants of Kethria's survivors, per the terms of the original trust. None of it to me. Not one mark. I don't withdraw my allegations; I add to them. I ask this archive to enter into the record the testimony of Ilya Vosk regarding the thirty-one-year-old survey, the testimony of my crew regarding the Admiralty's payments to Corvel Asque, and the murals of Kethria's last harbor, which I will gladly show any archivist with a diving certificate and a strong stomach."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere in the gallery, the young archivist had stopped pretending to shelve anything.

Prendel removed her spectacles, cleaned them with a handkerchief that had clearly been waiting forty years for this moment, and put them back on.

"Claimant," she said. "You understand that you are refusing one million marks and full amnesty."

"I understand I'm refusing to be bought twice," Paloma said. "The Admiralty already bought my mother. It didn't take."

Halloran was on her feet. "The archive cannot seriously entertain — the claimant has no standing to surrender what she cannot prove she owns —"

"She owns it," Prendel said. "That is precisely what the instrument says. The bearer holds the whole of it, with all claims and standing arising therefrom. Including," and here the old woman's voice acquired, for the first time, a blade's worth of edge, "the standing to surrender it to a neutral archive of record, which is what she is doing, in front of nine witnesses, in the most public room in Verren Reach." She folded her hands. "The Admiralty's offer of settlement is declined. The offer of amnesty is declined — the claimant and her crew will stand or fall by the truth, like everyone else in this room. The hearing will proceed. Witness Vosk, you may testify."

Ilya stood. And Paloma, sitting back down beside him, felt Sefa's hand land briefly on her shoulder — one squeeze, no more, the exact weight of it calibrated like everything Sefa did.

"You all right?" Sefa murmured.

"I just gave away enough money to buy a fleet," Paloma murmured back.

"I know. You look terrible. It's very becoming."

Behind them, Runi was grinning so widely Doss had to nudge her. Doss himself wore an expression Paloma had never seen on him before — something between grief and relief, like a man watching a boat he'd been secretly afraid to board sail away without him.

Across the aisle, Asque had not moved. He sat with his hands folded, gray and composed, watching Paloma with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life: not rage, not even disappointment. Recalculation. A man discovering that the ledger he'd built his life on had a currency he'd never bothered to learn.

He leaned, very slightly, toward his lawyers. He murmured something. And Paloma, who had spent her life reading water, read the set of his shoulders and knew: he was not finished. Men like Asque did not lose hearings. They lost *lawyers* — and then they did something desperate.

Ilya began to testify. His voice was quiet and it did not shake, and thirty-one years came out of him in careful, measured sentences, like a diver bringing up artifacts one at a time.

Paloma listened, and let the river sound against the walls, and waited.

* * *

The hearing ran until the light through the tall windows went from gold to gray to the blue of early evening. Prendel called a recess at dusk — "The archive does not deliberate by lamplight; the archive has standards" — and the room emptied in the slow, reluctant way of a room that knows the story isn't over.

The crew gathered on the archive's upper terrace, which looked out over the flooded lower town: rooftops just breaking the surface, a church spire standing in the current like a mast, lanterns in the upper windows of houses whose owners had moved uphill generations ago and still, apparently, paid their taxes.

"So," Doss said. He was turning his unlit pipe over and over. "A million marks."

"Yes."

"You gave away a million marks."

"I gave away *their* marks," Paloma said. "They were never mine. I was just the diver."

Doss was quiet for a while. The pipe went around and around.

"I'd have kept it," he said finally. "You know that."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway, with me sitting right there." He looked out at the water. "That's either the stupidest thing I've ever seen or the first honest thing. I haven't decided."

"Take your time," Paloma said. "It's been forty years in the making."

Runi was hanging over the terrace rail, watching the current slide past the church spire. "My gran says the river remembers everything it swallows," she said. "She says that's why it floods. It's not angry. It's just bringing things back."

"Your gran," Sefa said, "sounds like she should testify."

"She charges."

Ilya came out onto the terrace last, moving like a man who had set down something he'd carried so long his arms had forgotten their own weight. He stood next to Paloma at the rail.

"Thirty-one years," he said. "And it took you one afternoon to do what I couldn't."

"It took you thirty-one years to make my one afternoon possible," Paloma said. "Your maps. Your testimony. Your silence, even — you kept the shape of the lie intact until someone could break it properly." She shrugged. "Memory's a salvage job, Ilya. Nobody brings it up alone."

He looked at her then, and there was water in his eyes, and he was too old and too tired to be ashamed of it.

Below them, at the archive's water-gate, a lantern bobbed. A boat was coming through the flooded streets — a navy launch, moving fast, too fast for the debris-choked current, with a wake that slapped against the drowned houses. It carried no running lights. It carried men.

Paloma watched it slide into the shadow of the archive's foundation and stop.

"That's not a recess," she said quietly. "That's a flanking maneuver."

Sefa was already moving for the door. "I'll wake the archivists."

"No," Paloma said, watching the launch, counting silhouettes, finding the tall gray one in the stern who sat the way Asque sat, who was not aboard by accident. "Wake the archivists, yes. But somebody should also go down to the water-gate and greet our guest. Because whatever he's planning—" she turned, and the last light caught the set of her jaw, "—he's about to find out that this archive has walls a meter thick, nine grandmothers with the Concordance behind them, and a claimant who has already given away everything he could possibly threaten her with."

She started for the stairs, and the crew fell in behind her, and somewhere below, in the dark water at the archive's foundations, the river waited with the patience of everything that remembers.
