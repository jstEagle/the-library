## 21. High Water, Clear Sky

The river crested at dawn on the fourth day after the hearing, and Paloma knew it the way she knew most things about water — not by looking at it but by listening.

For two weeks the Vessarine had roared past Verren Reach like a freight train with no brakes and nowhere to be, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees and once, memorably, an entire chicken coop with three indignant survivors still aboard. It had swallowed the lower town as promised, lapping at the archive's stone steps like a patient animal testing whether the door would open. But that morning, standing on the archive's balcony with a cup of terrible coffee, Paloma heard the pitch of it change — drop by a half-tone, the way a singer's voice drops when the song is finally over.

"Runi!" she called through the doorway. "It's turning."

Runi appeared in a heartbeat, hair still wet from washing, wearing Sefa's spare shirt because her own had been lost somewhere between Kessel Bend and the weir and nobody had asked questions about that in weeks. She leaned over the rail, cocked her head, listened the way her river-kin listened — with her whole body, eyes half-closed — and grinned.

"Crested," she confirmed. "Falling by tonight. Give it four days and we can take the *Second Chance* down to the shallows without scraping her belly."

"Four days," Sefa said from inside, where she was doing something unspeakable to a kettle with a screwdriver. "Give me four days and I'll have the port engine purring instead of growling. It's growling right now. I want you all to know that. It's a personal insult."

"It's always been a personal insult," Ilya said mildly, from his corner, where he sat every morning now with the deed documents spread out before him like a man saying prayers he no longer believed in but recited anyway out of habit. Since the hearing, since his testimony, since thirty years of silence had come out of him in one long exhale before the archivists' tribunal, he had seemed both lighter and more fragile — a knot untied that wasn't sure what shape to hold. He spent hours with the papers anyway. Paloma suspected it was less about the documents than about being allowed to look at them openly, without flinching.

The kettle screamed. Sefa swore at it with real affection and poured for everyone.

They had been living in the archive's guest quarters for eleven days — narrow bunks, a shared washroom with a door that stuck, and a landlady-archivist named Mistress Ovvel who fed them stew every evening and pretended not to know who they were while asking increasingly specific questions about their adventures. The criminal allegations were resolved; Asque was detained at the dock, coldly and administratively, exactly as promised; the chest and its contents were public record now, catalogued under glass in the archive's long hall where any descendant of Kethria could come read them, and already had — three families in the first week, one of them an old woman who had stood in front of the famine ledgers for six hours without speaking, and left a sprig of dried rosemary on the case when she went.

Paloma thought about that woman often. More than she thought about the fortune she hadn't taken, which was almost never, which surprised her more than anything else about the whole affair.

"So," Runi said, dropping into a chair and hooking one foot over its arm. "Four days. Which means tomorrow we do it."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone has been waiting for someone else to say the thing.

Tomorrow. The lantern. Doss.

"Yeah," Paloma said. "Tomorrow."

* * *

They went upriver in the morning, the five of them — Paloma, Runi, Sefa, Ilya, and Mistress Ovvel's nephew Tobb, who had appointed himself crew-of-honor for the day and brought a basket of food nobody had asked for and everybody ate. The *Second Chance* ran easy on the falling water, Sefa's repaired engine purring so smoothly that she kept glancing back at it with the expression other people reserved for newborns.

The place where Doss had stayed behind was a shelf of rock above the canyon narrows, maybe forty feet above the waterline even now, with the flood marks high on the stone above it like tide lines on a beach. From there you could see the whole sweep of the divide — the channel where the gunboats had come hunting, the bend where Sefa's decoy light had blinked and winked and lied beautifully, the dark water where the pursuit had turned away toward a light that wasn't a boat at all.

Doss had rigged that light himself. He'd volunteered — unasked, unthanked, in that grumbling way of his that had never once fooled anybody — and then he'd held the position alone through the worst night of the chase, blinking a lamp on a rock shelf until the gunboats committed to the wrong channel, until the *Second Chance* was through the narrows and gone.

He'd made it off the shelf. That was the part Paloma still couldn't make sit straight in her chest. He'd gotten down, gotten to a fisher's skiff, gotten most of the way back to them. The river took him in the last hundred yards, in the dark, in flood water moving faster than anything alive should argue with. They'd found the skiff. They hadn't found him. Runi's kin along the banks were still watching the eddies for him, in the patient way river people watched, because river people did not say *lost*, they said *not yet given back*.

So today they weren't burying Doss. Today they were burying the lantern.

Sefa had cleaned it up. That had taken her three days and she didn't talk about why, and nobody asked, because some things you understood without a conversation. The decoy lamp was dented, salt-crusted, its brass housing scarred where shrapnel or rock had kissed it during the chase. But she'd polished the lens until it caught the morning sun and threw a small bright coin of light onto the rock, and when she set it down on the shelf it looked less like wreckage than like something waiting to be lit again.

"He'd hate this," Sefa said. "You know that, right? He'd stand right there—" she pointed at an imaginary Doss-shaped spot, "—and complain about the ceremony. Say we were wasting good daylight. Ask if there was food."

"There's food," Tobb offered helpfully, holding up the basket.

"See? The only part he'd approve of."

Paloma crouched by the lantern. Up close, in daylight, without the river roaring, the canyon was almost gentle — red stone, white birds riding the thermals, the water below running high but honest. She remembered it only at night, only in terror, only with engines behind them and Runi's small hand gripping hers and the decoy light burning steady on this shelf like a promise somebody was keeping at gunpoint.

"Doss," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat and started over. "Doss. You sold us out once. You know that. We all know that. You sold a page from a ledger to a contractor skiff for passage out, and then you came back, and you never explained it, and you never apologized, and I think — I think the coming back *was* the apology. I think it was the only kind you knew how to write."

Ilya made a small sound beside her. She glanced over. His eyes were wet, and he wasn't hiding it, which for Ilya was practically a public address.

"You were a pain in the neck," she went on. "You complained about everything. You ate more than your share. You once tried to convince Runi that eels were attracted to singing so she'd serenade the catch nets." Runi snorted. "And when it mattered — when the boats were behind us and the river was ahead of us and somebody had to stay on this rock and lie with a lamp until the lying worked — you raised your hand. Nobody asked you. You just did it."

She picked up a flat stone and began scooping out a hollow in the gravel at the base of the shelf, where the flood couldn't reach, where the ground was dry even in the worst season. One by one the others joined her — Sefa digging with her hands because tools were for people with patience, Runi working fast and silent, Ilya moving stones with careful old-man deliberation, Tobb digging enthusiastically and mostly in the wrong direction until Runi redirected him.

When the hollow was deep enough, Paloma lifted the lantern and set it in.

"Light," she said. "That's what you gave us. So that's what goes in the ground."

Sefa produced a striker. For a moment nobody moved. Then Sefa reached into the lantern and, with the tenderness she usually reserved exclusively for engines, turned the wick and lit it.

The flame caught. Small, yellow, ordinary — a workmanlike little light, the kind Doss himself would have approved of, nothing fancy, nothing wasted. It burned in its housing in the shallow grave, and the wind off the river made it lean and sway and refuse, stubbornly, to go out.

They filled it in together. Paloma placed the last stones herself, arranging them in a rough circle, and then they all stood around it in the sunshine feeling awkward, because grief doesn't come with instructions and funerals on riverbanks don't come with priests.

Tobb broke the silence by opening the basket. "Food," he said, with the gravity of a man performing a sacrament.

They ate on the rocks above the grave — bread, hard cheese, river plums, a jar of honey that Runi guarded with open hostility. And the stories started, the way stories do, one leading into another: Doss arguing with a customs officer in Port Merren and winning by pretending to be deaf until the officer gave up; Doss teaching Runi to cheat at cards and then being outraged, genuinely outraged, when she beat him with his own technique; Doss drunk on festival wine standing on the *Second Chance*'s roof declaring that he had never once in his life been wrong, only early.

"I miss him," Runi said suddenly, mid-story, mid-laugh, and then her face crumpled and she cried the way teenagers cry — all at once, furiously, as if betrayed by her own face. Sefa pulled her in without a word. Paloma put a hand on her back and felt the sobs come up through her spine like the river's pulse.

"We all do," Paloma said. "That's the tax. That's what loving people costs. You pay it and you'd pay it again."

"And he'd complain about the funeral," Sefa said into Runi's hair, and Runi laughed wetly, and the laugh and the crying tangled together and neither won, and that felt right.

They stayed until the lantern's flame guttered naturally down inside its stony bed — Sefa had rigged the air hole so it would burn itself out clean, no smoke, no mess, just a light spending itself where it had been spent before. When it finally dimmed and died, the canyon didn't seem darker. It seemed, if anything, more lit, the way a room is brighter after a candle goes out and your eyes adjust to how much sun there was all along.

Walking back down to the boat, Ilya fell into step beside Paloma. He walked slower these days, but steadier, like a man who had finally set down weight and was learning the strange mechanics of walking without it.

"The archivist asked me yesterday," he said quietly, "whether I regretted the thirty years."

"What did you tell her?"

"That regret is a luxury for people who still have time to spend. That what I feel is closer to debt." He looked out at the water. "Doss carried his debt badly and paid it late and paid it all at once. I'm trying to carry mine better. Paying it in installments." A pause. "Testimony. Maps corrected. Names restored to records. It's slow work. It turns out the truth is less like a wreck you raise and more like a garden you keep."

Paloma smiled. "You've been talking to the old woman with the rosemary."

"Twice," Ilya admitted. "She's teaching me Kethrian mourning songs. I'm terrible at them. She says that's appropriate; the songs are supposed to sound like grief."

At the boat, Runi was already waiting, and she was holding the ship's knife, and she had the look of someone who had decided something and would not be argued with.

"No," Paloma said immediately.

"You haven't heard—"

"You want to carve his name into the rail. The answer is no, because the rail is structural, and because Sefa will—"

"Sefa already said yes," Runi said.

All heads turned to Sefa, who examined the sky with great interest. "Did I," Paloma said.

"I may have mentioned," Sefa said, "that certain modifications to the rail were structurally acceptable, provided they were performed by someone who knew what they were doing, and provided they were done properly, and provided I supervised."

"You absolute traitor."

"She asked me three days ago," Sefa said. "She cried. I'm strong, Paloma, but I'm not made of stone. Also she'd already practiced on a plank. It's good work. I checked."

Paloma looked at Runi — at the set jaw, the knife, the fierce wet eyes — and felt the last of her resistance go the way the flood had gone: not defeated, just finished, its work done.

"Where?" she said.

Runi's whole face changed. "Starboard side. By the stern cleat. Where he used to sit and complain about the view."

"That's not a view, that's the engine exhaust."

"Exactly where he used to sit and complain about the engine exhaust," Runi said solemnly, and Paloma laughed, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been locked since the canyon, and she had to turn away and pretend to check a mooring line for a minute while it worked itself out.

Runi carved slowly, tongue between her teeth, the knife whispering through the wood. The others gathered around. She carved letters deep enough to last — deep enough to survive weather, paint, years, whatever came — and when she was done she brushed the curls of wood away and stepped back.

**DOSS**
*He was early.*

Paloma read it twice before she got it, and when she got it she had to press her knuckles to her mouth. *Never wrong, only early.* Runi had heard the story too. Had kept it. Had turned his stupid drunken boast into an epitaph, and somehow it was perfect — funny and sad and true, the way the best things about Doss had always been all three at once.

Sefa ran her thumb over the letters. "Structurally sound," she pronounced, voice thick.

"Ship's certified," Runi agreed.

Ilya said nothing at all. He just stood there with his hand resting flat on the rail beside the name, the way you'd rest a hand on a friend's shoulder, and Paloma saw his lips move once, silently, shaping words in a language only three families in the world still spoke.

* * *

They cast off the next morning on falling water under a sky scrubbed clean by the flood — that particular washed blue that only comes after a river has finished throwing its tantrum, high thin clouds, sunlight on the water like scattered coins.

The *Second Chance* ran sweet. Both engines purred. The rail bore its new name and the whole boat seemed to carry itself a little differently, the way a person walks differently after a funeral — lighter and heavier at once, grieving and grateful in the same stride.

Paloma stood at the helm with the current helping them home and let herself feel it, all of it, without flinching: the canyon, the hearing, the old woman's rosemary, the fortune sitting in a public archive belonging to people she'd never meet, the lantern buried under stones with its light spent. Somewhere behind them Asque was in a detention cell learning what consequence tasted like when it was administered coldly, by committee, without drama. Somewhere ahead the maps were being redrawn — literally, she'd seen the proof sheets at the archive, cartographers inking KETHRIA back onto the coast in letters that had been blank for forty years.

"Paloma." Runi appeared at her elbow, and there was something in her voice — excitement carefully leashed, the leash straining. "Message came upriver with Tobb's cousin's ferry this morning. Sealed. Addressed to you. To *the bearer of the Second Chance*, actually, which is weird phrasing, but river post does what river post wants."

"A letter addressed to a boat."

"To you *by way of* the boat. Open it, I'm dying."

Paloma took the oilskin packet. The seal on it was plain wax, unmarked, which meant either nothing or everything. She broke it and unfolded a single sheet in a neat, old-fashioned hand:

*To the bearer of the inheritance of Kethria —*

*We use the old term deliberately. The record shows you declined the claim, and the record is correct, and the record also shows what the claim was, which means you are the only living soul who knows its full shape and chose to set it down. That makes you a keeper, whether you accept the title or not.*

*Keepers get letters.*

*Ours is short. In the drowned harbor south of the divide — the town you found, the one with the murals — our divers report a second chamber beneath the customs house, opened by the recent floods, sealed for longer than Kethria has been dead. What's inside, we don't know. What we know is that the seals bear a mark matching the astrolabe in your chest, and the archive says the astrolabe answers to no catalogue they hold.*

*The water over the chamber will be clear for eleven days. After that, the silts return for a year.*

*No obligation. Keepers choose. But we thought you'd want to know the river isn't finished giving things back.*

*— Friends of the Rosemary*

Paloma read it twice. Then she folded it, slowly, and looked up.

Runi was watching her with naked hope. Sefa had drifted near, wiping her hands on a rag, radiating studied indifference. Ilya stood at the rail beside Doss's name, one eyebrow raised in the closest thing to a grin his face permitted.

"Well?" Runi demanded.

Paloma looked at the river — high, clear, running gold in the morning light, full of everything it had ever swallowed and everything it might yet give back.

"Someone get me my dive bag," she said.
