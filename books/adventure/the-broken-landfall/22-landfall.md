## 22. Landfall

The wreck of the *Merciful Ann* had been down for sixty years, and in all that time nobody had bothered to lie about her. She was a grain barge that had struck the old shoal off Corren's Point in a fog, plain and simple, and the river had folded her into the mud with no ceremony and no secrets. Paloma loved her for that.

She worked the hold in the green half-light, gloved hands moving cargo stone by stone. Grain sacks, long since become silt and shadow. A brass lamp, worth a week's bread. A child's tin soldier, worth nothing, which she tucked into her belt pouch anyway because Runi collected them and would trade her a terrible joke for it. The water pressed in around her, patient and cool, and for the first time in longer than she could remember, it asked nothing of her.

No navy skiffs patrolling the channel. No contractors with their polite, hungry questions. No sealed chests with dead nations stamped on their lids. Just a barge, a lamp, and a tin soldier, and above her — when she finally tilted her head back — a coin of morning sun wobbling on the surface like a promise kept.

She surfaced, spat her regulator, and hauled herself up the Second Chance's ladder with the easy ache of a body that had been used well.

"Anything?" Doss called from the rail, where he sat mending net with the concentration of a man who had discovered, late in life, that his hands were happiest when they were busy.

"Brass lamp. Some chain. And your tin soldier's cousin."

Doss looked up, and the grin that split his face was the same grin that had once talked her into hauling up a chest she should have left on the riverbed — except now it reached all the way to his eyes, and there was nothing behind it but a man glad to be exactly where he was. "A cousin! What regiment?"

"Cavalry. Missing an arm."

"They all are, eventually." He took the little figure, turned it over in his palm, and set it carefully on the rail beside the eleven others Runi had lined up there like a honor guard. "Honest pay for honest mud. I could get used to it."

"You are used to it. You've been used to it since spring."

"A man can still be surprised by his own good fortune. It keeps the humility fresh."

Sefa's head appeared through the engine hatch, streaked with grease, one eyebrow raised in the expression that served her for both skepticism and affection. "If you two are done celebrating mud, the charter party's coming up the dock in twenty minutes, and I'd like the deck to look like we're professionals and not a floating yard sale."

"We are a floating yard sale," Doss said. "That's our brand."

"It's a *terrible* brand."

Paloma laughed — actually laughed, out loud, which still surprised her sometimes, the way a scar surprises you when you press it and it doesn't hurt — and went below to change out of her kit.

* * *

The charter was a survey job. A merchant consortium out of Verren Reach wanted the old shipping channels remapped now that the Admiralty's monopoly on hydrographic charts had been broken into about nine hundred public pieces. Honest work, honest rates, and the consortium's representative was a round, cheerful man named Pell who shook everyone's hand twice and called the Second Chance "a vessel with *character*," which everyone aboard understood to mean *rust*.

He spread the charts on the cabin table while Sefa made tea and Doss pretended not to listen from the doorway.

"Corren's Point to Kessel Bend, the whole middle reach," Pell said, tapping the paper. "The old charts are Admiralty work, which as we now know means roughly one chart in three was drawn to hide something rather than show it."

"Mostly to hide sandbars," Paloma said.

"Mostly," Pell agreed, with a small smile that suggested he knew about the other ones too.

Runi, who was sixteen now and had grown three inches and a permanent air of grievance about it, leaned over the table. "The flooded channel past the fishing weir's on there now. The one that didn't exist."

"It exists," Paloma said.

"It didn't, and then Sefa made it exist, and now it does. That's the most interesting thing on the whole chart and nobody's even marked who found it."

"Sefa," Paloma said. "It says so. Right there."

Sefa, pouring tea, did not look up, but her mouth did something at the corner that on anyone else would have been a smile.

Pell counted out the advance, and Paloma signed, and the pen scratched on the paper with a small, ordinary sound — the sound of a living being made, one honest job at a time. She had signed a great many documents in the past year. Testimony. Depositions. Her name on the public record of the Verren Reach archive, forty-one times by Ilya's count, and Ilya counted everything. But this was the first signature in all that pile that had been nobody's business but her own, and she found she liked the weight of it.

"There's one more thing," Pell said, gathering his papers. He hesitated, which round cheerful men rarely did. "You'll have seen the news."

"The news," Paloma said.

"The new charts." He said it carefully, the way people had learned to say a great many things carefully in the past months. "The ones coming out of the Reach this autumn. The full survey."

"I've heard."

"Have you *seen* one, though?"

He laid a proof sheet on the table. It was a chart of the middle coast, the Vessarine waters, the drowned country — and Paloma looked at it for a long moment before she understood what was different, and when she understood, her throat went tight in a way she hadn't planned for.

There, where the charts had shown only open water and a dotted hazard line and no name at all for forty years — where the ink itself had conspired in the famine, in the erasure, in the quiet — there was a label. Small. Neat. Official.

*KEETHRIA SOUND (historic)*.

Spelled wrong. The archive's clerks had spelled it wrong, and Runi was already pointing at it with the outrage of the young confronted by institutional typography.

"That's not — it's *Kethria*, with one E, everyone knows that —"

"They'll fix it in the next printing," Pell said. "There's going to be a next printing. There's going to be a lot of next printings." He shrugged, apologetic and pleased at once. "First time in forty years the name's been on anything official. The clerks were apparently working from a naval document that had it wrong. The navy, it turns out, misspelled it in their own cover-up correspondence, and nobody dared correct them for four decades."

Doss had come fully into the cabin now, and he was looking at the chart the way he'd once looked at the sealed chest — except this time there was no hunger in it, no calculation. Just something quiet and a little stunned, like a man seeing a grave finally given its stone.

"Forty years," he said, "and they couldn't even spell it."

"They didn't need to spell it," Paloma said. "That was the whole point. You can't mourn a place that isn't on the map."

* * *

They went out that afternoon, the five of them, because it was the anniversary — or near enough; the archive's records had settled on a date and the date was today — and because Runi had declared that anniversaries were for visiting, not for avoiding.

Kethria's last harbor lay a half-day downriver, drowned and whole. The town had been flooded when the admirals broke the levees — not in war, the murals said, never in war, only in *management* — and the water had preserved what it was given. They anchored over the main street in the late light, and Paloma went down alone.

She'd been down four times since spring. The first time, she'd cried into her mask and been furious about it. The second time, she'd brought flowers, which the river ate by morning, and decided that was correct — the river kept what it kept. The third time, she'd brought Ilya, who had stood in the drowned square where the mural of the admirals' signatures curled off the wall, and had said, in the flat voice of a man setting down thirty years of ballast, "I surveyed this coastline when I was twenty-three. I drew the waterline. I drew it *accurately*. That was the only honest thing I did for thirty years, and I didn't know it was honest until you made it so." She had not forgiven him that day. She had forgiven him somewhere around the fiftieth time he'd stayed up past midnight cross-referencing testimony, and she had never told him which day it was, and she suspected he knew anyway.

Now she swam the main street slow, fins barely moving. The murals glowed in her lamp — the harvest that never failed until it was made to fail, the ships that came and went, the names. The names had been repointed this spring by a team from the archive, fresh mortar in the old grooves, so that the admirals' signatures — the ones that still headed the navy, or had, until the hearings — stood out clean and legible for any diver to read. *Let them sign it forever*, the archivists had said. *In the water, where they sent it.*

Paloma rested her glove on the wall, over a painted child holding a painted fish, and stayed there a while, breathing.

Then she took the tin soldier from her belt pouch — the cavalry one, the armless cousin, which she'd kept back from Runi's collection without explaining why — and set it on the windowsill of a drowned house, facing the street, standing guard.

"For you," she said, and the river took the words the way it took everything, gently, and kept them.

* * *

The celebration that evening was small and imperfect, which is to say it was a family dinner. Sefa cooked on the deck stove and pronounced everything underdone, which was her way of saying she was happy. Ilya brought out a bottle he'd been saving since the hearings and could not explain the provenance of, which everyone drank anyway. Runi held forth on the spelling error until Doss threatened to have the new chart tattooed on her arm with the extra E included, purely to watch her suffer.

Paloma sat at the rail with her cup and let the talk wash over her, and Doss drifted over the way he did now, without agenda, without ledger, just a man leaning on a rail next to a friend.

"Thought about it?" he asked. "The claim. The money's still sitting there. Reparations fund, frozen forty years, and the archive keeps writing to you about it. Ilya showed me the letters. They're very polite. They're *extremely* polite. Politeness like that always has numbers in it somewhere."

"I know what's in them."

"You could take it, you know. Nobody'd think less of you. Half the coast would call you a saint and the other half would call you a fool, and both halves would be right, which is how you know it's a good decision either way." He turned his cup. "Asque would've taken it. Asque would've taken it so fast he'd have left scorch marks."

"Asque is currently being detained at a dock, awaiting trial, having been cut loose by the people he served." Paloma said it without heat. The news had come weeks ago, via Ilya's endless correspondence: the Admiralty, hemorrhaging, had sacrificed its contractor the way you jettison ballast, and the contractors' own people had picked him up at the pier before the navy could even pretend it wasn't involved. Justice, Ilya had written, arriving cold, administrative, and on schedule. "He took everything he was ever offered. Look where it kept him."

"And you? You were offered it too. Same money. Same moment."

"I was offered it and I gave it away, and look where it kept me." She gestured — at the deck, the rust, the tin soldiers, Sefa burning dinner, Runi arguing, Ilya quietly refilling cups, the river going gold and slow toward the sea. "Best trade I ever made."

Doss was quiet a moment. "The letters," he said. "The archive's letters. There's one thing in the latest one Ilya hasn't told you yet, because he's been waiting for the right moment, and because he's Ilya, and the right moment is a concept he believes in the way other people believe in weather."

Paloma looked at him. "Doss."

"He'll tell you tonight. But since you're looking at me like that —" he grinned, the old grin, the honest one now — "the reparations fund isn't just money. It never was. The deed's inheritance clause, the one you surrendered — the archive's been administering it as a *trust*. Public record. Survivor descendants get the reparations, that's settled, that's done, that's the part you already won. But the trust has a provision for administration. Stewardship. Somebody to oversee the recovery work — the drowned towns, the records, the murals, the whole drowned country. A salaried position. A *charter*." He paused, savoring it, because Doss had always known how to land a thing. "They want it to be you. The bearer who wouldn't be paid. Who else would they trust with it?"

Paloma set down her cup. The river moved beneath them, dark coming on, carrying everything it carried — the grain barges and the tin soldiers, the murals and the mortar, the name coming back to the maps one printing at a time — out toward the sound where a nation was learning, slowly, in fresh ink, to be remembered.

"Ilya," she called across the deck. "Where's that letter?"

Ilya smiled his small, precise smile, and produced it from inside his coat, because of course he had it on him, because of course the right moment was now.

Paloma read it twice, by lamplight, while her family went quiet around her. Then she looked up at the water, and the water looked back, and she thought: *you kept it all. Every secret, every name, every honest thing. You kept it until we were ready to carry it.*

"Steward of the Kethrian Recovery," she read aloud, testing the words. "Diving, survey, preservation. Salaried." She glanced at Sefa. "There's a budget for a crew."

"There's always a budget for a crew," Sefa said. "The question is whether the crew gets hazard pay."

"The question," Runi said, "is whether I'm old enough, and the answer is yes, and I've already packed."

Paloma laughed, and took the pen Ilya was already holding out — because of course he had a pen, because of course — and signed the charter at the rail of the Second Chance, under the first star, with the river running silver toward the sea.

The future, like the water, was deep, and uncharted in places, and hers to dive.

She was already looking forward to the finding out.
