## 1. Twelve Miles Down

The freighter *Cormorant Star* had been dead for six years, and in that time the sea had done what the sea always does: it had begun to take her apart with terrible patience.

Rosalind Hartwell hung forty meters down in the green half-light, her rebreather hissing softly at her ear, and watched a school of barracuda wheel through the cargo hold like silver smoke. Below her, the wreck's spine curved away into darkness, furred with coral, draped in fishing net that some long-ago trawler had lost and never come back for. It was, by any honest measure, a beautiful grave. She'd worked uglier ones. She'd made some of them herself, though that was a story she kept locked in a box at the bottom of her chest, weighted down, marked on no chart she'd ever show anyone.

"Talk to me, Ros," Mateo's voice crackled through the comm, warm and slightly too loud, the way it always was when he was nervous and pretending not to be. "Your heart rate's doing that thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you're down there enjoying yourself instead of working, and I have to pretend I'm not jealous."

"I'm assessing structural integrity," she said, and hooked her light onto a stanchion so both hands were free. "Structural integrity is a meditative state."

"She's philosophizing," Mateo said, to someone else on deck. "Priya, she's philosophizing again. Do we dock her pay for philosophy or—"

"Dock it," Priya said, dry as ballast sand. "Time on bottom is money, Hartwell. The *Cormorant* isn't going to get any deader while you admire the fish."

Rosalind smiled inside her mask, which was one of the small luxuries of diving alone: nobody could see you smile at their expense. She turned back to the work. That was the thing about salvage—the work was honest even when everything around it wasn't. A wreck didn't lie about what it was. It told you exactly how it died, if you knew how to listen: here the hull plate that failed, there the fire scar, over there the place where the crew had understood, all at once and forever, that the ocean does not negotiate.

She'd spent twelve years learning to listen. Twelve years since she'd been anyone else, someone with another name and a life that fit together like a well-built hull, before the day it all tore open and took people down with it. Twelve years of measuring her existence the only way that seemed to make sense anymore—not in birthdays, not in anniversaries, but in nautical miles. Miles behind her. Miles of water between her and the person she used to be. If you kept moving, the miles accumulated like armor plating, and nobody ever got close enough to read the name painted on your stern.

*Two hundred and eleven miles since Port Maren,* she thought, checking her gauge out of habit rather than need. *Forty meters down. Six years of rust between me and a payday.*

The payday was modest, as paydays went. The *Cormorant Star*'s insurers had finally given up on legal wrangling and sold the recovery rights to a consortium, and the consortium wanted the ship's safe and its manifest records—boring things, paper-things, the kind of salvage that paid for diesel and rice and the slow, patient repair of a boat named the *Kestrel*. Rosalind had found the safe an hour ago, wedged behind a collapsed bulkhead in the purser's office, and now she set about the delicate business of persuading it free without bringing three hundred tons of corroding steel down on her head.

This was the part she loved, though she'd have denied it under torture. The quiet. The focus. The way the world narrowed to the width of a cutting torch and the rhythm of her own breathing. Down here there were no names, no pasts, no debts—just physics and patience, and physics never asked who you used to be.

"Rosalind." Priya's voice this time, stripped of its usual irony. "We've got company."

"How much company?"

"Navy patrol boat. Designation *Corvus*-class, hull number—" a pause, the sound of keys clicking, "—hull number I can't find in any registry, actually, which is charming. Eight hundred meters off our port quarter. They've been holding station for twenty minutes."

Rosalind's hands went still on the cutting torch.

Twenty minutes. She'd been on bottom for fifty-eight. Which meant the patrol boat had arrived while she was already down, had slowed, had matched their drift, and had said nothing. Warships did not drift alongside salvage vessels for twenty minutes out of loneliness.

"Are they hailing us?"

"No. No radio contact, no running lights change, nothing. Just sitting there." A beat. "Like they're watching."

"They're always watching," Mateo said lightly, but she could hear the brightness in his voice thinning. "We're a fascinating vessel. We've got a crane that only works on Tuesdays."

"It's Wednesday," said Priya.

"Then we're extra mysterious."

Rosalind exhaled slowly, watching her bubbles rise and flatten against the overhead of the hold. Patrol boats shadowed salvage operations sometimes—it happened, especially near shipping lanes, especially in waters where somebody's government was touchy about somebody else's government doing something. Usually it meant paperwork. Usually it meant a bored lieutenant wanting to see your permits and your manifests and your face, comparing all three against a database somewhere, finding nothing worth remembering, and moving on.

Usually.

But usually they hailed you first.

"Keep cutting," Priya said, reading her silence correctly, as she always did. "I'll keep an eye on them. If they close to five hundred meters, I'm waking Sam."

Sam Okonkwo slept like a man who'd earned it, which he had, most nights, in the medic's berth he'd claimed within an hour of coming aboard. He was the newest member of the crew—three months now—and like everyone on the *Kestrel*, he had arrived without a history and been welcomed precisely because of it. That was the unspoken covenant of Rosalind's hiring practices: no questions about where you came from, no questions about why you left, and in exchange, absolute loyalty to the people currently sharing your deck. It wasn't trust, exactly. It was something better than trust. It was the understanding that everyone aboard had something they were swimming from, and therefore nobody was going to be the one to ask.

She got the safe free on the fourth attempt, easing it out of the bulkhead with a lift bag and a patience that would have made her old instructors proud. The bag swelled, the safe rose, and Rosalind rose with it, hand over hand along the guide line, watching the *Cormorant Star* shrink beneath her into a pale suggestion of itself, then a shadow, then a rumor.

At fifteen meters she paused at her decompression stop and let the sea hold her, weightless, suspended between the dead ship below and the living problem above.

Through the shimmering ceiling of the surface she could just make out the *Kestrel*'s hull—and beyond it, gray and sharp-edged against the morning glare, the patrol boat. Closer than eight hundred meters now. Close enough that she could see the wake it was throwing, holding position against the current with the arrogant ease of machinery that cost more than her entire operation earned in a decade.

*Five hundred meters,* she thought. *That's the line Priya drew.*

The patrol boat crossed it without slowing.

By the time Rosalind broke the surface, the boarding ladder already rising to meet her, she had composed three different explanations for their presence and discarded all of them, because none of them accounted for the silence. Ships that meant to inspect you announced themselves. Ships that meant to warn you announced themselves loudly. Only ships that meant to *study* you stayed quiet, and being studied was a sensation Rosalind Hartwell had spent twelve years perfecting the art of avoiding.

She came over the rail, pulled her hood back, and stood dripping on the deck while Mateo wrestled the lift bag's line and Priya stood at the rail with binoculars, very still.

"Well?" Rosalind said.

"Gray hull, no visible pennant numbers, no transponder answering our queries." Priya lowered the binoculars. "They're holding at four hundred meters. There's a man on their bridge wing with optics, and Rosalind—I'd bet my engine spares he's not looking at the ship."

"Looking at what, then?"

"At you."

Rosalind wiped salt spray from her face and considered the gray shape across the water. Four hundred meters was close enough to count rivets. Close enough to read a nameboard, if they'd cared to show one. Close enough, she thought, for a camera lens to do its quiet work, filing away her face in whatever archive collected faces like hers—people who had slipped their registries, changed their names, become smooth water where a wake should be.

Twelve years of miles, and she had always assumed the miles were working. That distance was a kind of camouflage, that if you moved fast enough and stayed small enough, the world's great indifferent machinery would slide right past you. Standing on her own deck with salt drying on her skin and a stranger's optics fixed on her stern, she felt the assumption shift under her feet like a deck plate coming loose.

Invisibility, it turned out, was not a wall. It was paint. And paint wore.

"Options," she said, because her crew expected her to have them, and because saying the word aloud kept the other feeling—the cold one, the one with teeth—at bay.

"We run," Mateo offered. "We're loaded light, we could make Port Vessa by nightfall."

"And they run faster, and then we look guilty, which we are not, mostly." She turned to Priya. "Other options."

"We finish the job. We've got the safe aboard; the manifest records can wait for a second dive tomorrow. We act boring. Boring is armor."

"Boring is armor," Rosalind agreed. "Sam!"

From the galley doorway, Sam raised a mug of coffee in salute. He'd appeared sometime during the last minute, as he tended to, drawn by the specific frequency of tension that he claimed he could smell. "Before anyone asks: no, nobody's hurt, yes, I heard everything, and yes, I think we should also act boring. Also, breakfast is ready, and tense people who skip breakfast make bad decisions."

"He's not wrong," Mateo said, already drifting toward the galley.

Rosalind lingered at the rail a moment longer. The patrol boat held its station, patient as a headstone. On the bridge wing, the man with the optics had lowered them—or turned away, or stepped back into the shadow of the superstructure; she couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like swallowed anchor chain.

*You're being paranoid,* she told herself. *You've been paranoid for twelve years and it's kept you alive. Those aren't mutually exclusive.*

She went below, ate the eggs Sam pushed at her, laughed at Mateo's impression of Priya's engine-room vocabulary, and let the morning fold itself back into something almost ordinary. Almost. Because underneath the ordinary, in the place where she kept her ledgers, a new entry had opened itself:

*Miles of water between me and whatever that boat wants: four hundred, and closing.*

They finished the second dive that afternoon without incident. The manifest records came up clean and dry in their case, and by dusk the *Kestrel* was making nine knots toward Port Vessa with her crane lashed and her deck washed down, and the patrol boat was gone—simply gone, sometime between Rosalind's watch and Priya's, vanished without a farewell or a wake, the way large gray things do when they've decided you're not interesting after all.

Or when they've decided you are.

Rosalind took the midnight watch alone, as she preferred, with the autopilot murmuring and the stars doing their ancient, indifferent bookkeeping overhead. Two hundred miles to port. Three days of ordinary work ahead, invoices to send, a crane to fix. Life measured out in miles, steady as a heartbeat.

It was Priya who brought the message up from the radio room at 0300, barefoot, unsmiling, a printout in her hand.

"You'll want to see this," was all she said.

Rosalind read it twice. It was addressed to the *Kestrel* by name—a name that existed in exactly one registry, under exactly one owner, a shell company three layers deep that Rosalind had built herself, brick by careful brick, over seven years. Nobody knew that name. Nobody *should* know that name.

The message was brief. Coordinates she didn't recognize. A sum of money that made her sit down slowly on the chart table's bench. And at the bottom, in place of a signature, a single line:

*Recovery of survey equipment. Discretion expected. Compensation tripled for urgency.*

And beneath that, in handwriting she hadn't seen in twelve years but would have known anywhere—in handwriting she had once trusted with her life, and had, in every way that mattered, lost it—

*E.V.*

Rosalind stared at those two letters until the cabin lights blurred, while somewhere behind her the autopilot held their course through the dark, and two hundred miles of ordinary sea rolled patiently by, carrying her toward a decision she could already feel closing around her like deep water.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Sleep was for people whose pasts stayed politely in the past, and hers had apparently learned to send telegrams.

At dawn she stood at the galley table with the printout flat between her palms, and her crew assembled around it the way crews do—drawn by coffee, then by silence, then by the particular gravity of their captain standing very still.

Mateo read it upside down, which was his habit and his protest against literacy before sunrise. Priya read it once, fast, the way she read engine diagnostics. Sam read it slowly, twice, and then looked at Rosalind with the gentle, terrible attention of a man whose entire profession was noticing what people tried to hide.

"E.V.," Priya said. "Friend of yours?"

"A ghost," Rosalind said.

"The coordinates," Priya tapped the page, "plot to open ocean. Not near a lane, not near a shelf, not near anything. I checked three charts. The charts say there's nothing there."

"There's always something there," Rosalind said quietly. "Charts just decide what's worth writing down."

Mateo looked up. "So do we go? Triple pay. That's the crane fixed, that's the hull job, that's—"

"That's a stranger who knows a name nobody knows, offering money for equipment on a patch of sea that officially doesn't exist," Sam said mildly. "Speaking as the crew's designated pessimist: that's not a contract, that's bait."

"It might be both," Rosalind said.

She looked down at the letters *E.V.* and felt twelve years of carefully accumulated miles tremble, all at once, like a cable taking strain. Elias Vane had taught her to dive. Elias Vane had taught her to disappear. And Elias Vane was the only person alive who knew both names she carried—the one on her papers, and the one at the bottom of the sea.

Debt and curiosity outweighed fear. They always had. That was the flaw in her hull, and she'd known it since she was nineteen years old.

"Prep the ship," she said. "We're going to have a look."
