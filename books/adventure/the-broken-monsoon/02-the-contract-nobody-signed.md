## 2. The Contract Nobody Signed

The broker found me in Suva, which should have been my first warning. Good news doesn't go looking for people in Suva. Good news waits politely at home like a cat that's decided to forgive you.

I was sitting on the veranda of the Captain's Rest with a plate of overcooked fish and a beer sweating rings onto the table, doing what I always did between jobs: nothing, aggressively. Twelve years of practice had made me excellent at it. There is an art to being nobody in a port town—wear the same salt-stiff shirt three days running, tip exactly right but not memorably, learn the names of no one whose name could get you asked about later. I had the art down to a science.

He sat down across from me without asking, which was either very rude or very deliberate, and I couldn't decide which until he ordered a beer for himself and one for me without looking at the menu. He knew what I drank. That was the second warning, and I catalogued it the way I catalogue everything now: calmly, completely, and with the quiet certainty that sooner or later it would matter.

"You're Hartwell," he said. Not a question. People who say *you're Hartwell* instead of *are you Rosalind?* have already done their homework, and homework means someone paid for it.

"I'm whoever you need me to be if the rate's right," I said, which was a lie dressed up as flexibility. I was Rosalind Hartwell all the way down, and underneath her was somebody else entirely, and neither of us was available for conversation with strangers.

"Delacroix." He offered a hand. His grip was dry and brief, a handshake conducted by a man who had shaken ten thousand hands and remembered none of them. "Marine survey and recovery, out of Brisbane."

"Never heard of you."

"Nobody has. That's rather the point of us." He smiled like a man sharing an inside joke with himself. "We have a client with a problem. A research vessel lost some equipment off a site east of here. Sensitive instruments. Expensive. The insurance company wants them recovered quietly, before word gets out and the vultures start circling."

"Vultures don't circle wrecks, Mr. Delacroix. They circle payouts."

"Then we're agreed there won't be a payout worth circling." He set a folder on the table between us, square and heavy, the kind of folder that had been printed by someone with a budget. "Coordinates, manifest of the equipment, salvage terms. Triple your last job. Cash advance on signature."

Triple. I didn't touch the folder. I looked at it the way you look at a dog that's too friendly—the ones that bite are always the ones wagging hardest.

"Lost equipment," I said. "From a research vessel."

"A survey vessel."

"A survey vessel doing research in waters where—" I turned my beer glass slowly, watching the condensation trace a line through the ring it had already left. "Mr. Delacroix, I've been doing this twelve years. When a man offers triple pay for 'equipment,' the equipment is usually a story. What's actually down there?"

"Instruments. Data cores. The usual."

"And the vessel?"

"The vessel is accounted for."

That sentence had a shape I recognized. *Accounted for* is what people say when they mean *don't ask about the vessel.* It sits in the air the way a wrong note sits in a chord. I'd heard it from smugglers, from insurers, once from a coast guard officer who was lying so badly I almost respected him. The sea is full of vessels that are accounted for.

* * *

Here is something about me that the brochures don't mention: I am a coward with excellent instincts. Every instinct I own was screaming at me to finish my beer, wish Mr. Delacroix a pleasant afternoon, and be three nautical miles gone before he finished his. Instincts have kept me alive through things I don't tell stories about at parties, mostly because I don't get invited to parties.

But instinct isn't the only voice at the table. There's also debt. Debt has a voice like a landlord—patient, procedural, and utterly without imagination. My boat, the *Kestrel*, needed a new hydraulic ram for the crane arm, a hull inspection I'd been postponing with the dedication of a woman avoiding a diagnosis, and roughly four hundred liters of diesel that currently existed only as a number in my head that made me wince. Salvage pays well when it pays, but between jobs it eats you alive, and I'd been between jobs for six weeks.

And then there was the third voice, the quiet one, the one I liked least because it sounded most like me. Curiosity. Twelve years ago curiosity had ruined my life, and I still hadn't learned to kill it. It just kept coming back, like a weed with good intentions.

I opened the folder.

The coordinates were printed clean and centered, formatted by someone who understood that confidence is a font choice. I read them twice, and both times my eyes snagged on the same thing: the numbers described a patch of ocean that did not exist. Not *unexplored*—the sea is full of unexplored, and unexplored is just unmapped money waiting for someone brave or stupid enough to collect it. These coordinates marked water that the charts specifically declined to discuss. I'd seen the region once, years back, on a hydrographic office chart with a blank space where depth soundings should have been, annotated in small type: *insufficient data.* Which is chart-speak for *we looked, and then we were told to stop looking.*

"Your research vessel," I said slowly, "was surveying a place with insufficient data."

"That's generally what survey vessels do."

"It's not generally what they do when the charts have been deliberately left empty. Insufficient data means nobody funded the mapping. Deliberately empty means somebody funded the *not*-mapping." I closed the folder and slid it back across the table an inch, not far enough to surrender it, just far enough to make a point. "I want to know who your client is."

"You know I can't tell you that."

"Then let me tell you what I know, free of charge, because I'm generous that way." I leaned forward. "Somebody with a great deal of money and a great deal to lose lost something—or someone—in a trench the government pretends isn't there. They want it recovered by a civilian, quietly, because anything official leaves fingerprints. And they're paying triple because the going rate for silence is whatever the market will bear. Have I got the shape of it?"

Delacroix's smile didn't move, but something behind his eyes recalculated. "You're more perceptive than your file suggests."

"My file?"

"Your reputation, I meant."

"Mm." I picked up my beer and drank, giving myself time, because the other thing about me—the thing under the cowardice and the curiosity—is that I notice paperwork. It's a diver's habit. Down there, everything is written in pressure and current and rust, and reading it correctly is the difference between coming up and not. Paperwork talks the same way, if you listen.

And this paperwork was talking in a voice I knew.

It wasn't anything obvious. The contract language was boilerplate, admiralty-standard, the kind of clause stack any maritime lawyer could produce overnight. But the boilerplate had been edited. Someone had struck out the standard force majeure language and replaced it with a custom clause, and the replacement clause had a particular rhythm to it—a way of stacking contingencies, each one nested inside the last like diving bells, redundancy layered on redundancy, every failure mode anticipated and provided for. Most contracts assume things will go right and litigate about the rest. This contract assumed things would go wrong and had already forgiven itself in advance.

I'd only ever known one person who wrote clauses like that. A man who used to say that a plan without a retreat wasn't a plan, it was a eulogy with better formatting.

Elias Vane.

My mentor. My teacher. The man who taught me to read water and write contracts and lie beautifully to harbormasters. The man whose boat I'd crewed on for four formative years, who taught me that the sea forgives nothing but forgets even less. The man I hadn't spoken to in nine years, since the night I walked off his deck in Darwin with everything I owned in two bags and a name I was about to bury.

Either Elias Vane was behind this contract, or someone had hired his ghostwriter, and both possibilities made my skin prickle in the exact same place.

* * *

"You've gone somewhere," Delacroix observed. He's the sort of man who narrates other people's faces; I made a mental note to keep mine boring around him.

"I'm deciding whether to take the job."

"Take it. The advance alone covers whatever that boat of yours needs."

"You don't know what my boat needs."

"A hydraulic ram, a hull inspection you've been avoiding, and diesel." He sipped his beer. "We do our homework, Ms. Hartwell. It's how we keep our clients confident."

There it was again—that phrase, *our clients*, deployed like a shield. I filed it. "And if I say no?"

"Then I'll thank you for your time and buy the next round, and we'll never speak again." He said it lightly, but underneath the lightness was something with weight. Men like Delacroix don't waste trips. If he'd flown to Suva—and his shoes said he had, shoes like that don't survive ferries—he had a list, and I was on it, and the list had alternates. "Though I'll confess I'd be disappointed. You come highly recommended."

"By whom?"

"By people who prefer not to be named. You seem to attract that quality."

"I cultivate it," I said. "It's a garden. Takes years."

He laughed, genuinely, and for a moment he seemed almost human, which I distrusted immediately. Then he tapped the folder. "The terms are generous because the work is delicate. Recover the equipment. Don't discuss the site. Don't discuss the client. Sign, take your advance, and in six weeks you're paid in full and nobody remembers your name—which, I understand, is how you like it."

That landed harder than he could have known, or perhaps exactly as hard as he'd intended. *Nobody remembers your name.* Twelve years I'd spent making sure of it. There was a comfort in invisibility that I'd built my whole life around, brick by careful brick: no records, no questions, no past reaching up out of the water to grab my ankle. And here was a stranger offering to sell me my own anonymity back to me at triple rates.

The trap in that offer was so elegant I almost applauded.

Because that's the thing about buying silence—you don't own it afterward. It owns you. Every dollar of that advance would be a hook, and hooks don't come out backward. I knew that. I'd watched men learn it. I'd *been* a man learning it, once, under another name, in another life, and the lesson had cost me everything I'd had at the time.

So why was I still holding the pen?

* * *

Delacroix produced the pen from his breast pocket like a magician producing a coin, and I stared at it for a long moment, and I'd like to tell you I was weighing justice against greed, principle against survival, the great moral scales of a complicated woman's soul. But honestly? Mostly I was thinking about the hydraulic ram. And about the fact that the *Kestrel*'s bilge pump had developed a stutter that sounded suspiciously like a countdown. And about the cold, simple arithmetic of a woman alone in the world with a boat, a reputation, and a bank account that cleared its throat nervously whenever she opened the app.

Debt and curiosity outweighed fear. That's the honest ledger. Fear put up a good fight—it always does—but debt fights dirty and curiosity doesn't know when it's beaten.

"One condition," I said.

Delacroix raised an eyebrow, inviting.

"I pick my own crew, I run my own dive plan, and if I get down there and find anything that isn't 'survey equipment'—bodies, weapons, government hardware, anything with a flag on it—I surface, I leave it where it lies, and I still get paid in full. All of it. No renegotiation."

"That's an unusual clause."

"I'm an unusual contractor. Also, I learned contract-writing from a paranoid genius, so I know exactly how much trouble a badly-worded clause can buy." I held out my hand for the pen. "Put it in writing. Your style of writing. You people are good at that."

Something flickered across his face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. As if I'd said a password I didn't know I knew. For half a second, Delacroix looked less like a broker and more like a messenger who had just realized the recipient understood the message.

"He did teach you well," he said quietly.

The veranda noise—gulls, guitar from the bar, some tourist arguing cheerfully about the price of a fishing charter—all of it seemed to step back and give us room.

"So it *is* him," I said.

"I said nothing."

"You said enough. 'He did teach you well' is a confession wearing a shrug." My heart was doing something complicated, part anger, part something older and softer that I refused to examine at the table. Nine years of silence, and the first word from Elias Vane arrives via a man in expensive shoes carrying a contract full of retreat clauses. "Tell him I want to hear his voice. Not a message. His actual voice. Before I sign anything else, before I'm three hundred miles offshore over a trench that officially isn't there. Tell him Rosalind says he owes me a phone call, and he knows exactly why."

"I'll pass along the request." Delacroix stood, straightened a jacket that didn't need straightening, and left the pen on the table in front of me. "The advance transfers on signature. The window closes Friday. After that, the client finds someone less... particular."

He walked out into the heat, and I sat there with the folder and the pen and my cooling beer, and I thought about all the versions of me that were about to stop existing the moment ink touched paper.

There was the version that walked away. She'd keep the *Kestrel* afloat on small jobs and stubbornness, stay invisible, stay safe, stay small. She'd die someday in a rented room with an unremarkable name and a head full of stories nobody would ever hear, and the sea would keep its secret, and so would I.

There was the version that signed. She was already leaning over the page. She wanted to know what was in the trench, wanted to know why Elias Vane—who had spent forty years teaching people to avoid exactly this kind of job—had put his fingerprints on it. She wanted the money, yes, but underneath the money she wanted the answer to a question she'd been carrying since Darwin: *what happened to the man who taught me everything, and what has he gotten himself into?*

And there was a third version, the one I didn't look at directly. The one that suspected this contract wasn't a job at all. That it was a door, and doors opened in both directions, and somewhere on the other side was a past with my real name on it, patient as pressure, waiting.

I signed.

The pen scratched the way pens do, ordinary and final. Delacroix's associate materialized from nowhere to collect the countersigned copy, shook my hand with professional warmth, and vanished into the afternoon. I finished my beer. I paid my tab. I walked down to the harbor in the gold light, past the fish sellers and the fuel dock and the church with the blue roof, and there she sat at her berth, my whole world bobbing gently: sixty feet of stubborn steel and bad paint, the *Kestrel*, needing everything and promising nothing.

"Okay," I told her, resting my hand on the rail the way you'd touch a horse you'd just entered in a race it shouldn't be running. "We're doing something stupid. Try to act surprised."

She creaked. Boats are honest that way.

Three days. That's what I had to find a crew willing to dive into a hole the government pretended wasn't there, for a client who didn't exist, on a contract written by a mentor who wouldn't call. Three days to find three people with clean hands and dirty pasts, the only kind of people who say yes to work like this.

I took out my phone and scrolled to a contact list that was short even by hermit standards, and I started with the deckhand who owed me six months of wages and an apology, and whose last known location was a bar in Lautoka with excellent taste in music and terrible taste in life choices.

Mateo Cruz answered on the fourth ring.

"Cruz," I said. "How do you feel about trenches?"
