## 3. Crew of Strangers

*Rosalind*

The trick to hiring a crew for a job nobody should take was to interview them in a place where they couldn't ask questions. Rosalind had learned this the hard way, back when she still asked questions herself and had gotten answers that cost her everything.

So she conducted her interviews at The Drowned Rat, a dockside bar in Puerto Caldera where the music was loud enough to drown out curiosity and the lighting was bad enough to hide hesitation. She'd rented the corner booth for the evening. The bartender owed her a favor from a salvage job two years ago, and favors were the only currency she trusted more than cash.

First up: Mateo Cruz.

She'd seen his name on three different crew manifests over five years—deckhand, rigger, diver's tender—and never once seen him attached to a ship longer than four months. That kind of drift usually meant one of two things: trouble, or someone running from it. Rosalind recognized the second one because she owned it.

He slid into the booth across from her like he'd been invited, which he hadn't, exactly—he'd been told *booth in the back, eight o'clock*—and set down a duffel bag that clinked.

"You bring tools to a job interview?" she said.

"I bring tools everywhere." He unzipped it far enough to show her a coil of braided line, a wrench wrapped in electrical tape, and what appeared to be half a sandwich. "The sandwich is not part of my kit. It's dinner. I wasn't sure how long this would take."

"How long do you think it will take?"

"Depends." He took a bite of the sandwich anyway, chewing thoughtfully. "You're Rosalind Hartwell. Twelve years of salvage work, mostly solo or short-crew, no incidents on record, which is either very good or very suspicious. You pay on time—I checked with two people who've worked for you. And you just took a contract through a broker who doesn't exist."

Rosalind kept her face still. "Where'd you hear about the broker?"

"Same place you did, probably. A man who talks like a lawyer and dresses like a fisherman." Mateo brushed crumbs off his hands. "I'm not asking what the job is. I'm telling you I already know it's strange. Strange pays triple. Triple pays for things."

"Things," she repeated.

"My mother's dialysis. In Cebu." He said it plainly, without performance, which is why she believed him. Men who performed their sob stories wanted pity; men who stated them wanted work. "So. What's the job?"

"The job is you don't ask me that until we're at sea."

"That's fair." He stuck out his hand. "When do we sail?"

She shook it. His grip was calloused in the right places, and he didn't try to prove anything with it. "Thursday. Pier nine. Don't be late, don't be drunk, and don't tell anyone where you're going."

"I haven't told anyone anything since 2019," Mateo said cheerfully, and finished his sandwich.

* * *

*Mateo*

Mateo had worked for captains who shouted, captains who sulked, and one captain in the Gulf of Thailand who communicated exclusively through whistling. Captain Hartwell was none of these. She was worse, in a way: she was quiet, and quiet captains expected you to think.

His first morning aboard the *Magpie's Rest*—a name he found deeply ominous for a salvage vessel, since magpies collected shiny things and so, apparently, did she—he spent three hours checking rigging while she stood at the rail watching him without speaking.

Finally he couldn't stand it. "Is there a problem, Captain?"

"No. You tied off the port winch correctly. Most people don't."

"Most people are wrong about many things."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished, like a fish surfacing. "Engineer arrives tonight. Priya Nair. Treat her well; she's the only reason this rust bucket floats."

"And after her?"

"A medic. Sam Okonkwo."

Mateo nodded slowly. Deckhand, engineer, medic. Four people total, including the captain, for a job paying triple rates. He'd done the math on the pier before boarding, and the math said one of two things: either the job was small, or the job was dangerous and small crews were easier to keep quiet. Given the broker who didn't exist, he was betting on door number two.

He didn't mind. That was the thing nobody understood about people like him—people who moved from ship to ship, country to country, leaving nothing behind but a forwarding address that stopped forwarding. Danger wasn't the problem. The problem was being known. On a ship of strangers, nobody knew you, and nobody knowing you was its own kind of safety.

Though he had to admit, watching Hartwell check the anchor chain link by link in the fading light, that some part of him was tired of safety. It was a lonely meal.

* * *

*Rosalind*

Priya Nair arrived at midnight with two sea bags, a toolbox that weighed more than Mateo, and an expression that suggested she had already diagnosed every mechanical failure the *Magpie's Rest* would suffer over the next decade and found them all insulting.

"You're burning oil in the number two generator," she said, before hello. "And your bilge pump has been screaming for a new impeller for approximately six weeks. I could hear it from the dock."

"It has a certain charm," Rosalind offered.

"It has a certain death wish." Priya dropped her bags on the deck and put out her hand. Her handshake was brief and firm, a business transaction. "Priya Nair. You can call me Priya if we survive the first week."

"What happens after the first week?"

"Then we're family, and families get to complain."

Rosalind liked her immediately, which was inconvenient. Liking people made them liabilities, and liabilities made mistakes, and mistakes made graves. She filed the liking away in the compartment where she kept all the other things she refused to feel, a compartment that had grown crowded over twelve years and was starting, lately, to rattle.

They walked the engine room together by lamplight. Priya ran her hands along pipes like a doctor palpating a patient, murmuring diagnoses. Halfway through, she paused at a bulkhead panel Rosalind had hoped nobody would notice.

"This patchwork here. Fire damage?" Priya glanced up. "Old?"

"Before my time."

"Mm." Priya let it go, which told Rosalind something important: the woman had her own locked compartments. People who pressed didn't have any. "I'll rewire the panel anyway. Fire damage makes me nervous."

"Noted."

"One question, though." Priya wiped grease on a rag, unhurried. "Triple rate, four crew, coordinates I'm not allowed to see yet. I've sailed under captains running guns and captains running people and one captain, God rest him, running a floating casino. This feels like all three combined into something new. So I'll ask once, and then never again: when it goes wrong—and jobs like this always go wrong—do we run, or do we finish?"

Rosalind met her eyes across the lamplit engine room. It was the right question. It was the only question.

"We finish," she said. "We finish or we die trying, and I intend for us to do neither."

Priya considered this for a long moment, then nodded once, sharply, like a judge accepting a plea. "Good answer. Bad plan. When do we sail?"

"Thursday."

"Then I have three days to keep this ship from embarrassing us both."

* * *

*Mateo*

Sam Okonkwo came aboard last, on Thursday morning, twenty minutes before departure, carrying a single battered leather case and apologizing to everyone he passed.

"Sorry—sorry, the ferry was delayed—sorry, is this the galley? Lovely galley—" He stuck his head into the wheelhouse, where Rosalind stood at the chart table. "Captain Hartwell? Sam Okonkwo. Your medic. Sorry I'm late."

"You're not late. We sail when I say we sail." She looked him over. Mateo, coiling line nearby, watched too, because Sam Okonkwo did not look like a ship's medic. He looked like a professor who'd lost an argument with a laundry machine—rumpled shirt, reading glasses pushed up into graying hair, hands soft around the edges except for a fresh rope burn across one palm.

"You dive?" Rosalind asked.

"When required. I prefer not to drown, but I'm flexible."

"Dive experience?"

"Commercial certification, saturation training, and one incident in the Red Sea that I'd rather discuss after we've known each other longer." He smiled—an easy, warm smile that seemed genuinely apologetic about the mystery. "The case stays dry-locked in the med bay. I assume there is a med bay?"

"There's a closet with a cot in it."

"Perfect. I've worked in worse." He extended his hand to Rosalind, then turned and offered it to Mateo as well. "You must be the deckhand. Mateo, yes? The broker mentioned you. Well—not mentioned, precisely. More like read off a list with visible distaste."

"He does talk like that," Mateo agreed.

Over the next hour, as the *Magpie's Rest* cleared the harbor and pointed south into open water, Mateo catalogued his new shipmates the way he catalogued every crew: by what they hid. The captain hid behind silence and a habit of touching the rail at the bow every time they crossed a mile marker, like she was counting something. Priya hid behind competence, talking machinery so fluently and so constantly that no one could get a word in edgewise about anything else. And Sam—

Sam hid behind kindness. It was a good disguise. Mateo had used it himself in leaner years. You could be the warmest person on any ship, the one who remembered birthdays and patched up cuts and listened to homesickness, and if you did it well enough, nobody ever wondered what you were warm in order to avoid.

By noon they'd fallen into a rhythm that surprised him with how quickly it came. Priya sang under her breath in Tamil while she worked the generators, something with a driving beat that Mateo caught himself nodding along to. Sam brewed tea for the whole deck using a kettle he'd brought personally, "because ship's kettles are crimes against humanity." The captain stood her watch and said perhaps forty words total, but they were the right forty words, and twice she almost smiled, which Mateo was beginning to suspect was the local equivalent of a standing ovation.

At dusk, the four of them ended up in the galley together, eating stew out of mismatched bowls, the first meal of the voyage. Nobody spoke for a while. The engine hummed. The sea knocked politely against the hull.

"So," Sam said at last, ladling seconds. "Are we doing the thing where we all pretend we signed onto this job for normal reasons? Because I find those conversations go faster if we skip them."

Silence. Mateo exchanged a glance with Priya, who raised one eyebrow a quarter inch, which he was learning was her version of a monologue.

"I'm here for my mother's medical bills," Mateo said. "That part's true. The rest of my reasons are mine."

"Fair," Sam said. "Priya?"

"I'm here because the money's obscene and the ship needs me." A pause. "Also the last captain I worked for turned out to be smuggling antiquities, and I'd rather be complicit in something interesting."

"Captain?" Sam turned to Rosalind, gentle as anything. "No pressure."

Rosalind looked at her stew for a long moment. Mateo braced for the wall to come up—the silence, the deflection, the captain-shaped armor.

"I'm here," she said finally, "because somebody asked me to find something, and I need to know what happened to the people who went looking first."

It wasn't the whole truth. Mateo could see that—it sat in her face like a stone under a tarp, changing the shape of everything around it. But it was more truth than he'd expected, and he found, unexpectedly, that he respected her more for the piece than he would have for the whole.

"Well," Sam said, raising his tea. "To partial honesty among friends."

"To finishing the job," Priya said.

"To my mother," Mateo added, and they drank.

Later that night, Mateo stood his watch alone on the aft deck, the stars thick overhead and the water black beneath. Behind him, through the porthole, he could see lamplight in the wheelhouse—the captain, awake at midnight, studying charts she wouldn't show anyone.

Ahead of them, somewhere south, lay coordinates that didn't exist and a paycheck that didn't make sense. Every instinct he'd sharpened over ten drifting years told him to jump ship at the next port.

But his instincts had also told him, back in another life, that staying invisible meant staying safe, and his instincts had been wrong about that too. Sometimes the safest thing was a ship full of strangers who understood exactly why you couldn't go home.

In the wheelhouse, the light went out. Then, a moment later, it came back on.

Mateo frowned. He watched it flicker twice more, steady, then dim again—and heard, faintly over the engine noise, the captain's voice, low and clipped, saying words he couldn't make out to someone who wasn't aboard.

He decided, for now, not to wonder about it.

The wondering would come soon enough, whether he wanted it or not.
