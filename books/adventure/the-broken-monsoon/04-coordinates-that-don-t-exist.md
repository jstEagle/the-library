## 4. Coordinates That Don't Exist

Priya Nair had a rule about the ocean: it was allowed to be big, but it wasn't allowed to lie.

She'd held to that rule for eleven years, through two refits, three captains, and one memorable hurricane off Mindanao that had taught her exactly how much water a bilge pump could move when properly motivated. The sea did what physics said it would do. Currents ran where temperature and pressure told them to run. Radio waves propagated at the speed of light and degraded on a predictable curve. Satellites orbited on schedules you could set a watch by. Everything out here could be measured, modeled, and trusted, and Priya had built her whole life on the quiet comfort of that arithmetic.

Which was why she was currently sitting in the engine room of the *Magpie's Fortune* at two in the morning, staring at a screen full of numbers that refused to behave, feeling like the floor had quietly been pulled out from under her.

The generator hummed behind her, steady as a heartbeat. She'd rebuilt that generator herself—new bearings, rewound stator, a fuel governor she'd machined from scrap because the original part cost more than Mateo's annual salary. It purred like a well-fed cat. She loved it the way some people loved dogs. And normally, when the world got strange, she'd come down here and put her hands on something honest until the strangeness passed.

But the strangeness was on her laptop screen, and no amount of generator maintenance was going to fix it.

She'd found the survey vessel's transponder data by accident, mostly. That was the thing she kept circling back to. Accident. Rosalind had given her the job coordinates—a latitude, a longitude, and a date range—and asked her to plot the approach, check weather windows, and generally do the boring, sensible work of getting them there without sinking. Priya was good at boring and sensible. She'd pulled the regional AIS archives—the automatic identification system feeds that every commercial vessel over a certain size was legally required to broadcast—to see what traffic moved through the area, because knowing your neighbors was half of staying alive at sea.

And there it was. The *RV Corvus*. Survey vessel, research registry, forty-one meters, last known position almost exactly on top of their destination coordinates.

Priya had expected that much. Rosalind had told them they were recovering equipment from an abandoned survey operation. Fine. Surveys came and went. Funding dried up, contracts ended, ships sailed home. There should have been a tidy little track: the *Corvus* arriving, loitering on station for however long the survey lasted, then a clean line back to port somewhere.

Instead, the track just stopped.

Not faded. Not degraded. Stopped. Mid-broadcast.

She zoomed in, and the numbers resolved into something that made the hair on her arms stand up despite the engine room's warmth. The *Corvus*'s final transmission had begun at 0347 local time and terminated at 0351—four minutes into what should have been a continuous broadcast cycle. Transponders didn't work that way. They were designed to be boring. They repeated their identity and position every few seconds, all day, every day, whether anyone was listening or not. For one to cut out mid-cycle meant either total power loss, physical destruction of the unit, or someone switching it off.

And here was the part Priya kept reading four times, hoping it would change: there was no distress call before it. None. No Mayday on 156.8 MHz, no EPIRB activation, no digital selective calling alert. She'd pulled every frequency record she could access from the archive aggregators, cross-checked with the coastal station logs she still had login credentials for from a job two years ago that she was fairly sure she shouldn't mention to anyone. Nothing. A forty-one-meter vessel with, presumably, a crew aboard had simply ceased to exist in the data, between one ping and the next, without so much as a cough.

Ships sank slowly or they sank loudly. Even a catastrophic event—a gas explosion, a hull breach, a rogue wave—left fingerprints. Debris fields drifted and got reported. Life rafts broadcast. Crews got off radio calls, sometimes garbled, sometimes terrified, but present. The ocean took plenty of people, but it took them messily, and the mess left traces.

There were no traces.

Priya pushed her glasses up her nose and rubbed her eyes. Around her, the engine room ticked and settled, the familiar sounds of a ship underway doing ordinary things. Somewhere above her, Mateo was on watch, probably drinking coffee he'd loaded with enough sugar to qualify as dessert and talking to himself in Spanish when he thought no one could hear. Sam was asleep, or pretending to be. Rosalind—

Rosalind was the reason she hadn't gone straight up on deck and said something. Because Priya had done what she always did when data looked wrong: she'd checked it against everything else she knew. And the more she checked, the more wrong it looked.

The *Corvus* hadn't filed a float plan amendment. Hadn't requested weather routing. Hadn't logged a single port call in the eleven days before its disappearance, which was odd for a survey vessel working remote stations—they burned fuel, needed provisions, rotated personnel. Eleven days of silence, then nothing. As if the ship had been fed, fueled, and provisioned for a mission nobody wanted discussed on any open channel.

And the coordinates themselves. Priya had charted them three times, because the first two times she assumed she'd made an error. The destination Rosalind had given them sat in a stretch of ocean where the charts showed depth soundings so sparse they might as well have been decorative. Survey coverage thinned to nothing. One old British admiralty chart from decades ago marked the area with a single cryptic notation—"dbs unexamined"—and otherwise offered blank blue space. Modern databases listed the seafloor there as "unassessed." Not shallow, not deep, not dangerous. Just *unknown*, in an era when most of the planet's seabed had been mapped to within a few meters.

Somebody had kept this patch of ocean off the map. Deliberately. Charts didn't develop holes like that by accident; holes like that took paperwork.

"You're going to give yourself a headache," she muttered to herself, and reached for her tea, which had gone cold hours ago. She drank it anyway, because waste offended her more than cold tea did.

The thing was—she'd known this job was strange when she signed on. Everyone had. That was the unwritten contract of the *Magpie's Fortune*: good pay, no questions, everyone's past stays in their own bunk. Priya had her own locked drawer, thank you very much—an engineering license under a name that wasn't quite hers, a former employer whose sudden bankruptcy had coincided suspiciously with an audit, a talent for making machinery do things its manufacturers swore it couldn't. She'd chosen this crew precisely because none of them would ever ask about the drawer. She'd assumed Rosalind's drawer contained the usual: a lawsuit, a debt, maybe a death she'd rather not discuss.

But there was a difference, Priya was realizing, between a crew that didn't ask questions and a job that answered them anyway. She'd signed on to salvage a wreck and recover some equipment. She had not signed on to sail toward a hole in the world's maps where a ship full of scientists had stopped existing between one minute and the next.

She thought about the others. Mateo, who read romance novels with the covers bent back and pretended he didn't, who had cried once—genuinely, unashamedly—over a stranded seabird they'd nursed for a week. Sam, who kept a photograph taped inside his medical kit that he turned face-down whenever anyone came near, and who treated every injury, however minor, with the gravity of a man performing penance. Rosalind, who moved through the world like someone walking on a frozen lake, testing every step, listening for cracks.

People came out here to hide. Priya knew that better than anyone. But hiding people didn't usually attract the attention of whoever had erased the *RV Corvus* from the sea.

Her screen saver kicked in—a rotating wireframe of a turbine she'd been designing in her head for six years and would probably never build. She watched it spin and let herself think the thought she'd been avoiding.

If the *Corvus* hadn't sunk—if it had been *made* to disappear—then whoever had done it was capable of erasing a ship and, presumably, its crew. And if they'd done it once, quietly, with no distress call and no debris and no follow-up inquiry in any database she could find, then they were good at it. Practiced. And practiced organizations did not leave loose ends floating around with salvage divers poking at their work site.

The generator shifted pitch as the autopilot adjusted course, and Priya glanced at the navigation repeater out of habit. Two hundred and six nautical miles to go. A day and a half, at their current pace, into blank blue space.

She could say something now. Wake Rosalind, lay the data out, let the captain decide. That was the correct chain of command, the professional move. Or she could keep digging first, because showing up with half a picture only ever produced half a decision, and Priya had learned the hard way that half decisions at sea got people killed.

Digging, then. She cracked her knuckles and got back to it.

It took her another ninety minutes to find the second anomaly, and this one was worse.

Buried in the same archive feed, filtered out by a routine quality-control flag she nearly dismissed, was a fragment of acoustic telemetry. Hydrophone arrays—underwater listening stations—sometimes leaked their metadata into public scientific repositories, sloppy archiving, grant-funded researchers not scrubbing their uploads. Most of it was noise: shipping traffic, seismic surveys, whales. Priya had combed such data before, hunting for the source of a vibration that had been shaking apart a client's equipment.

This fragment was tagged to a listening station four hundred kilometers northeast of their destination. Timestamped nine days after the *Corvus* vanished. And in it, beneath the whale song and the noise floor, was a sound the classification algorithm had labeled, with the serene confidence of a machine that had never lost sleep in its life, as "biologic—unidentified."

Priya played it through her headphones, volume low.

It didn't sound biological. It sounded mechanical—a slow, rhythmic pulse, deep and regular, like something enormous breathing through machinery. It repeated on a cycle of roughly forty seconds. Then, near the end of the fragment, the rhythm changed. Sped up. Doubled. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable to anyone who'd spent a decade listening to engines, came a second sound: metal, moving against metal. Grinding. Deliberate.

Then the fragment ended. Nine seconds of recording, plucked out of an ocean full of secrets, and the archive had no more.

Priya sat very still. In the silence, she could hear everything: the generator's purr, the hull's creak, the water sliding past outside, Mateo's footsteps crossing the deck overhead, unhurried, ordinary. The ship around her was warm and lit and full of people she'd come, without ever saying it aloud, to care about.

And beyond the hull, in every direction, was the ocean—vast, patient, and apparently willing to hold whatever it had been given. She'd spent her whole career thinking of the sea as honest. Big, indifferent, occasionally murderous, but honest. Now she sat inside its belly and understood, with creeping, crawling certainty, that the honesty had never belonged to the ocean at all. The ocean didn't keep secrets. People used it to keep secrets. The ocean was just the vault door, and somewhere ahead of them, someone had locked it and thrown away the charts.

Forty seconds. Something down there pulsed on a forty-second cycle, and no whale on earth breathed like that.

She saved everything—three copies, encrypted, one disguised inside the turbine design file where no one would ever think to look—and closed the laptop. Then she climbed the ladder to the deck, because there was one more thing she wanted to see with her own eyes.

The night was clear, stars scattered thick across black water. Mateo stood at the helm, silhouetted against the instrument glow, humming something off-key. He nodded when she appeared beside him.

"Couldn't sleep? The sea's showing off tonight."

"Mm." Priya looked north, toward coordinates that didn't exist on any map worth trusting. The horizon there was empty. Perfectly, completely empty—no lights, no traffic, no smudge of smoke on the stars. An entire missing survey team, and the sea wore no mark of them at all.

"Hey, Mateo," she said quietly. "When we get there tomorrow... stay close to the ship, okay?"

He glanced at her, and even in the dark she could see the question form behind his eyes. To his credit—and it was a great deal of credit—he didn't ask it. He just looked back at the empty northern horizon, and after a moment he said, "Okay, Priya."

Below their feet, the engine hummed its steady forty-second rhythm, and Priya realized she was counting along with it without meaning to.

One. Two. Three.

Two hundred and six miles to go, and the ocean keeping its mouth shut the whole way.
