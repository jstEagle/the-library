## 5. Gear and No Tracks

The atoll didn't exist on any chart Priya trusted, which was how Rosalind knew it was exactly where they were supposed to be.

It was barely an atoll at all—a crescent of coral and scrub no bigger than a football pitch, ringed by a lagoon the color of bottle glass. The survey team had set up camp on the leeward spit, tucked behind a ridge of black volcanic rock, and from the deck of the *Magpie* you could just make out the orange domes of their tents, the glint of solar panels, the lazy turn of a wind generator.

"Looks cozy," Mateo said beside her. He had the binoculars up and was working them slowly across the camp, the way he did everything—thorough, unhurried, like a man reading a letter from an old friend. "Little beach. Nice shade. Honestly, better accommodations than your boat."

"My boat has charm."

"Your boat has a galley that smells like burnt rice and regret."

"That's Sam's cooking, not the boat's fault."

Sam, within earshot as always, raised one finger without looking up from the med kit he was repacking. "That rice fed a crew of four for six days, and I heard no complaints."

"You heard one complaint," Mateo said. "Mine. Repeatedly."

"Repeated complaints are still complaints I heard *no* complaints about the second pot."

Rosalind let them run with it. The banter had gotten better over the six days out from port—looser, more automatic, the kind of noise a crew makes when it's starting to believe in itself. She'd worked with teams that never got there, and she'd worked with teams that got there too fast, all trust and no edges, and those were the ones that got people killed. This one felt about right. Four strangers who were becoming, cautiously, a unit.

Still, something in her chest had been tight since dawn, and it wasn't the coffee.

"Anything moving?" she asked.

Mateo kept the glasses steady. "Wind generator's turning. Solar array's angled for morning sun. I see... laundry." He paused. "There's laundry on a line."

"Laundry," Priya repeated from the helm, flat as a chart symbol.

"Dry laundry. Not moving. And the tide's been running for—" he checked his watch, "—three hours. That line should be swinging."

Rosalind took the binoculars. She swept the camp slowly, letting each detail arrive and be counted. Two orange tents, guy-lines taut. The main dome shelter, its flap half open. A generator shed with its vent door closed. A skiff pulled up above the tideline, bow anchor set, fuel canister standing beside it like a soldier at attention. A folding table outside the dome with what looked like instruments on it, covered by a tarp weighted at the corners.

And the laundry. Four shirts and a pair of trousers, hung neat as church, hanging dead still in a wind that was, she could see now, lifting the surface of the lagoon into little moving diamonds.

"Nobody's home," she said quietly. "But nobody left, either."

"That's a contradiction," Priya said.

"Welcome to this job."

* * *

They took the skiff in—Rosalind, Mateo, and Sam, because Priya refused on principle to leave the ship unattended in waters where a navy patrol boat had already found them once, and because someone had to be the adult, and Priya had claimed the title by seniority of temperament.

The lagoon was knee-to-waist deep over sand so white it hurt to look at. Rosalind waded the last stretch, and the moment her boots hit dry coral she felt it—the wrongness arriving through her feet before her eyes could confirm it.

The camp was *running*.

She heard it before she saw it: the low diesel hum of the primary generator, steady as a sleeping animal's breathing. The wind generator turning overhead with a soft rhythmic *whump, whump*. Inside the main dome, a laptop screen glowed on a folding desk, a chart program idling, cursor blinking. A radio sat in its charging cradle, battery light green. Fully charged.

"Coffee," Sam said.

She followed his gaze to the camp stove. A pot sat on the burner, maybe two-thirds full, a skin across its surface like a thin grey lake. Cold. Not hours cold, either. The burner was set to low, the way you'd leave it if you meant to come back in five minutes.

"Sam. How long does coffee take to grow a skin like that?"

He crouched, touched the pot, touched the stove's residual warmth with the back of his hand the way a doctor takes a pulse. "Twelve hours, give or take. Longer if the pot's full. Ambient's warm here, so—" He straightened. "Overnight. This happened overnight."

"Overnight they all walked away from a running generator, a charging radio, and a pot of coffee," Mateo said. He'd drifted to the tents and stood at the flap of the nearest one, not going in, hands loose at his sides. "In the middle of the night. Without turning anything off."

"Or without being allowed to," Rosalind said.

She went into the tent.

It was tidy in the way of people who expected to return. Sleeping bags unrolled but not slept in—she checked, and the bags were still strapped with their compression bands, which meant they'd been set out for show or for later, and neither made sense. Personal effects arranged on a shelf made from a plank and two crates: a paperback novel with a boarding pass for a bookmark, a photograph of a woman and two kids on a beach, a hairbrush, a tin of mints, half-finished. A pair of reading glasses folded on top of the book. Boots by the tent door, three pairs, laces loosened, ready to be stepped into.

Nobody leaves without their boots. Not in the middle of the night. Not anywhere.

"Rosalind." Mateo's voice from outside, and it had lost all its humor. "You need to see the ground."

She came out. He was crouched at the edge of camp where the coral spit gave way to a strip of grey sand leading toward the ridge and the far side of the atoll. The sand was perfect. Wind-rippled, unbroken, a clean page.

Except it wasn't quite perfect. There were tracks—she could see them now that she knew to look—faint depressions leading *into* camp from the skiff landing. Dozens of them. Survey boots, different sizes, the scuffs of daily traffic, all of them pointing inward, none outward. The team had arrived. The team had lived here. And then—

Nothing. The sand simply refused to say what happened next.

"Wind could have erased them," Sam offered, but his voice said he didn't believe it.

"Wind doesn't erase tracks selectively." Rosalind walked the perimeter of the camp, slow, reading the ground the way she read a wreck—by what was missing. The tide line was clean. No boat tracks at the water except the skiff's, days old, crusted with dried sand. No drag marks. No scuff of struggle. No blood, no dropped gear, no trampled scrub beyond the camp's edge. Eight people—Priya had pulled the manifest, eight names—had stood in this camp drinking coffee the previous evening, and then the island had swallowed them like they'd never been.

She stopped at the folding table under the tarp. Instruments: a gravimeter, a magnetometer, a laptop tethered to them both. The laptop was asleep. She woke it, and it asked her for a password, and the cursor blinked at her with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

"Eight people," Mateo said behind her. He'd gone quiet in the way that made her like him most—his noise was a choice, and when he stopped choosing it, it meant something. "Eight. No boat left. No tracks. No distress call—Priya said the last transponder ping just cut off. So either they all grew gills—"

"Or something came that didn't need to walk," Rosalind finished.

The wind generator went *whump* overhead. The generator hummed. The coffee sat in its pot, cold, patient, waiting for owners who were not coming back.

Sam had drifted to the medical bag he'd found clipped inside the dome—standard survey issue, and untouched. He held up a small bottle of painkillers. "Prescription label. Dr. L. Marsh. She'd have had these on her person if she left under her own power. People don't forget their own medication."

"Unless they left fast."

"Unless they left *carried*," Sam said, "by someone who didn't know what mattered to them."

Rosalind stood in the middle of that tidy, humming, abandoned camp and let the horror arrive properly. She'd seen drowned ships and drowned men, and grief had a shape she knew. This was different. This was a camp that expected its people back. A coffee pot set to low. A radio charged and ready. Boots by the door. It was the domesticity of it that got under her skin—the small ordinary faith of eight people in their own safety, arranged neatly on a shelf, waiting.

And underneath the horror, something older and colder in her, the part that had spent twelve years reading situations fast because reading them slow had once cost a life. That part was already working.

*They didn't panic. They didn't run. Whatever came, came quietly, and it came for all of them at once, and it took them somewhere that didn't leave tracks.* A ship that ran dark. A helicopter at altitude. Something that rose out of the lagoon. She didn't know which, and not knowing was its own kind of pressure, pressing on her eardrums like depth.

"Okay," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, which surprised her. "New terms. We're not here for equipment anymore."

Mateo looked at her. "We're here for them."

"We're here for them. Eight people walked into a hole in the ocean and haven't walked out. Whatever the contract says, whatever Vane's paperwork says, that's the job now. Find them. Bring them home if they're alive." She paused. "Find out what happened if they're not."

"And if whoever took them comes back?" Priya's voice, crackling through the handheld radio Rosalind had propped on the table. She'd been listening the whole time, of course. Priya missed nothing.

"Then they find us ready," Rosalind said. "Sam, bag everything personal—IDs, photos, anything that tells us who these people are. Mateo, check the skiff and the fuel. Priya—" she picked up the handheld, "—start pulling that transponder history apart. I want to know exactly where that ping died, to the meter."

"Already on it," Priya said. "And Rosalind?"

"Yeah."

"The ping didn't fade. It didn't degrade. It ended. Mid-broadcast, clean cut, like someone reached out and turned off the ocean." A beat. "I don't like it here."

"Nobody likes it here," Rosalind said. "That's how you know it matters."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the camp's ordinariness kept finding new ways to be terrible.

Mateo found a deck of cards in the dome, mid-game, four hands dealt face-down, a score pad with a running tally and a small argument recorded in the margins in two different handwritings. He photographed it without touching it, and Rosalind saw him stand over it a long moment, and she knew what he was thinking, because she was thinking it too: *they were coming back to finish the hand.*

Sam catalogued the medical bag and found, tucked behind the gauze, a child's drawing—crayon, a boat, three stick figures, *COME HOME SOON DADDY* in the wobbling capitals of a six-year-old. He put it in the evidence bag with a care that made Rosalind's throat tight, and said nothing, and she loved him a little for that.

Rosalind herself walked the tide line as the sun went low, and it was there, at the northern point where the black rock ridge met the water, that she found the only thing in the entire camp that was out of place.

A boot print. Single. Pressed deep into the wet sand at the base of the rocks, half-erased by the tide, but fresh—fresher than anything else on the island. Larger than any of the survey boots inside. A military pattern, if she was any judge, and she was. Whoever wore it had stood there, facing the water, for long enough to sink in.

Facing the water. Not the camp.

She crouched beside it as the light went amber, and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Someone had come ashore here, quietly, recently. Someone had stood and watched the camp. And then eight people had vanished, and the watcher's tracks led only down into the sea.

"Rosalind!" Priya's voice on the handheld, sharp, stripped of everything flat. "I've got the survey team's last transmission. It wasn't telemetry. It was a voice log—a message they were sending to their handler. Lena Marsh's voice. She was mid-sentence, Rosalind. She said—"

Priya stopped.

"Priya. She said what?"

A breath came through the speaker, and when Priya spoke again her voice had dropped, like she was afraid of being overheard by the water itself.

"She said, *'We found it. God help us, we found what they buried down there—and there are names in the files, names that go all the way—'* and then it cuts. That's the last thing any of them ever said out loud."

Rosalind stood in the dying light, one boot print at her feet pointing down into the darkening sea, and felt the last of the salvage job slip off her shoulders like an old coat.

Somewhere out there, under that water, was a trench with something buried in it—and eight people who had seen the names.

"Pack it up," she said. "We're going down."
