## 6. The Logbook

Sam Okonkwo had three rules for medicine at sea, and the first was: never diagnose a patient you can't sit down with. The second was: coffee is a food group. The third, learned the hard way during a cholera outbreak off Mombasa, was that the truth always arrives at the worst possible hour, wearing its worst possible clothes.

The logbook arrived at 2:47 in the morning, which meant it was batting a thousand.

He'd been in the galley pretending to sleep. This was a skill he'd perfected over twenty years of fieldwork—lying in a bunk with his eyes closed while his brain ran triage on everything that had happened that day. Rosalind's face when they'd found the camp. The coffee cups still half-full. The generator humming to itself like a dog waiting at a door that wasn't going to open.

Nobody had slept. That was the thing nobody said out loud. He could hear Priya's keyboard clicking through the bulkhead, patient as rain. Mateo was up on deck, ostensibly on watch, actually smoking his fourth cigarette and staring at the water like it owed him an apology.

So when Rosalind knocked on his cabin door—two soft raps, the way you knock on a patient's door when you already know the prognosis—Sam was awake, dressed, and pouring coffee before she finished saying his name.

* * *

"You're the one with the patience," she said, setting the survey team's field case on his desk.

"I'm the one with the least patience of anyone on this boat. Ask anyone."

"Exactly. Which means if *you* can sit with this thing for six hours, it's because it needs sitting with." She pushed the case toward him. "The encryption is medical."

Sam raised an eyebrow. "Medical."

"Field-hospital grade. The kind they issue to trauma units and epidemiological response teams. Priya cracked the outer layer in twenty minutes. The inner layer is keyed to biometric data—heart rate, specifically. It only opens for someone who knows how to read a pulse under stress."

He looked at the case. Grey composite, scuffed at the corners, a white stenciled cross half worn away. Someone had loved this equipment once. Someone had packed it carefully, the way you pack things you expect to need again.

"And you can't do it?" he asked.

"I could learn. In a week. Maybe." Rosalind's jaw did the thing it did when she was choosing her words like steps across ice. "Priya says the decryption window is tied to a live reading. Whoever opens it has to do it in one sitting. One attempt. If the biometrics don't match the profile—someone calm, someone trained, someone used to keeping a steady hand while everything goes wrong—it wipes."

"Wipes."

"Permanently. Self-securing archive. Whoever built it assumed the people carrying it might be captured." She met his eyes. "They built it for someone exactly like you, Sam. That's not an accident. That's a message."

He turned the case over in his hands. It was heavier than it looked, the way all important things are.

"You've been up for how long?" he asked her.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters because you're asking me to do the one thing I tell every patient never to do—open something I can't close." He tapped the case. "But you already know I'm going to say yes, so let's skip the part where you pretend I have a choice and I pretend I'm thinking about it."

The ghost of a smile crossed her face. First one in two days. He counted it as a vital sign.

* * *

The galley at three in the morning had its own weather. Sam cleared the table, laid out his kit like he was prepping for a procedure—case in the center, monitoring clip for his fingertip, Priya's decryption tablet propped where he could see the readout. Mateo appeared in the doorway with a fresh pot and the expression of a man who had decided that if the boat was going to sink, he'd rather go down caffeinated.

"I'm just here for the coffee," Mateo said.

"You're here because you can't stand not knowing," Sam said. "That's fine. Sit. But whatever's in this thing, you don't touch it, you don't photograph it, and you don't say a word to anyone until Rosalind decides what happens next. Understood?"

Mateo held up his hands. "Doctor's orders."

Priya came last, carrying her tablet and a look Sam recognized from triage tents: the careful blankness of someone about to learn something they can't unlearn. She plugged her equipment into the case, checked the biometric feed twice, and nodded at him.

"Whenever you're ready," she said. "Steady hands, steady heart. If your pulse spikes past one-forty, it locks."

"One-forty," Sam said. "I've kept a steadier pulse than that delivering a baby in a force-nine gale. Although, in fairness, the mother was calmer than I was."

He clipped the sensor to his finger, took a breath the way he'd teach a patient to—four counts in, six counts out—and said, "Go."

The screen bloomed.

* * *

The first files were boring, and Sam had never been so grateful for boring in his life. Supply manifests. Dive schedules. Water quality readings from a survey vessel called the *MV Corvus*, a government-contracted oceanographic team, seven personnel, routine bathymetric mapping of a trench system that the charts—Priya pulled them up side by side—marked as unremarkable.

"Routine," Priya murmured. "Routine, routine. Nobody encrypts routine."

The entries were dated across eleven days. Sam read them in order, aloud in a low voice, because there was something in the tone of the writing that deserved a human voice.

Day one through four: standard. A team lead named Okafor—Sam noted the name, filed it, the way you note a patient's name because names matter—writing with the dry humor of a man who'd been at sea too long. *Position confirmed. Priyanka seasick again. Vasquez ate the last of the good coffee and has been banned from the mess.*

Mateo snorted. Then stopped, because the humor was doing what humor does on ships: holding the roof up.

Day five: the sonar anomaly. Okafor's voice shifted on the page, the way a patient's voice shifts when they've decided to tell you something. A return signature at depth that didn't match any geological feature. Geometric. Regular. *Man-made*, Okafor wrote, and underlined it twice.

Day six: they took the submersible down.

Sam read the entry twice, because the first time his eyes kept sliding off it, the way they slide off a wound you don't want to look at directly.

*A structure in the trench wall. Flooded, but maintained—power draw, heat signature, active systems. Not a wreck. A facility. Construction maybe eight to ten years old, built in sections, sunk and assembled. We found the access lock and the designation stenciled beside it. Project name: TIDEGATE. I have never heard of it. I have been in this field for twenty-two years, and I have never heard of it, which is itself the most frightening data point of my career.*

"Tidegate," Priya repeated. She'd gone very still. "That's not in any registry. I've read the defense procurement databases for three countries as a hobby—"

"You have a *hobby*?" Mateo said.

"—and that name doesn't exist. Which means it was funded outside normal channels. Which means someone didn't want a paper trail."

Sam kept reading.

Day seven: they went inside. The entry was longer, and Okafor's careful irony had burned away entirely. What remained was a list, written with the terrible precision of a man who understood that lists were the only form of testimony that couldn't be argued with.

Test bays. Pressure-rated weapons testing—warheads designed to detonate at depth, tuned to generate shockwaves that would cripple submarines through hull compression rather than direct impact. Sam read the physics twice and understood maybe a third of it, and the third he understood was enough: it was a weapon built to kill people in the dark, underwater, where no one would ever see the bodies rise.

And beneath the test results, a second list. Names. Officials. A deputy minister of defense. Two admirals. A director of maritime procurement whose signature appeared on the funding authorizations, dated and countersigned, over and over, a rhythm of approval stretching back nine years.

Beside one of the names, Okafor had written a single line: *I sat across a table from this man at a conference in Lisbon. He shook my hand. He asked about my daughter.*

Sam stopped reading aloud at that point. Some things the room didn't need to hear in his voice. He read the rest in silence, his pulse steady on the monitor, his hands steady on the tablet, and only his eyes moving.

Casualty lists. Test results that included *occupancy estimates*. Civilian fishing lanes marked and noted. A trial in the Coral Sea three years ago that had been written up publicly as a "seismic event," with a fishing boat lost, four dead, attributed to a gas release. The logbook's attached analysis said otherwise. The analysis was annotated in Okafor's hand: *They knew. They knew the drift pattern. They tested anyway.*

"Sam," Priya said quietly. "Your pulse."

He looked down. One-twelve. Steady. He breathed out, long and slow, the way he'd tell a patient to.

"I'm fine," he said, which was a lie doctors are permitted, and kept reading.

* * *

Day eight was when the entries changed.

The handwriting—scanned pages, the tablet rendering them in grey ghost-images—got smaller. Tighter. Okafor had started writing the way Sam wrote notes in a war zone: fast, compressed, every word load-bearing.

*Someone pinged us from the facility. Not an automated signal. A pattern. Deliberate. Vasquez thinks it's an identification challenge. We did not answer. We withdrew to the surface and did not log the contact with the home office. I do not know who at the home office I can trust. Read that sentence again. I have worked for the government for two decades and I do not know who I can trust, and that is not paranoia, that is arithmetic.*

Day nine: they began copying everything. Redundant drives. Printed hard copies sealed in waterproof tubes. Okafor wrote about splitting the evidence three ways—*so that no single capture, no single sinking, no single accident can end this.*

Day ten: *A navy vessel on the horizon for six hours. Not the patrol pattern. Holding station. Watching. We have not transmitted. We are running dark. Lena says I'm being paranoid. I hope to God she's right. I have never less wanted to be right about something in my life.*

Sam knew the name. Lena. Dr. Lena Marsh. The woman they hadn't found. The woman they hadn't found *anywhere*—no tracks, no bodies, no sign.

Day eleven. The final entry.

It was short. Sam had read a thousand final entries in his career—final notes in charts, final letters from patients, final messages from colleagues who didn't come back—and they all had the same architecture. The noise falls away. What's left is the load-bearing wall.

*They know we know. A launch approached at 0400. Not navy livery. Armed. We are scattering the copies. I am keeping the primary log with me. If you are reading this, we did not make it to the rendezvous, and you are holding the only complete record. Do not come looking for us. Do not trust the sea service, the home office, or anyone with a government voice. Go to the press. Go wide. Go loud.*

*And if any of my people are alive—if you find them—tell them the coffee was bad and the company was good, and I would sail with them again in any sea God saw fit to make.*

*—E. Okafor, MV Corvus, final entry.*

The screen dimmed. The decryption complete, the archive open, the whole rotten architecture of it laid out on a galley table between a coffee pot and a bowl of limes.

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

Mateo broke it, because Mateo always broke it, and Sam could have kissed him for it. "The coffee was bad," he said hoarsely. "The company was good." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, quick and embarrassed, like a man caught doing something private.

"They're alive," Priya said. It wasn't hope in her voice. It was arithmetic, the same arithmetic Okafor had used. "You don't scatter evidence and evacuate a team you've already killed. You kill them and take the copies. They took the *people*. People who can testify. People who can be interrogated, or—" She stopped.

"Or held," Sam finished quietly. "Held as witnesses. Kept alive because dead men can't confirm what you're afraid of, but living ones can be managed." He'd seen it before, in another life, in another country. He'd treated the ones who got out. He knew what nine days of it did to a person, and the person who came out the other side.

"Nine days," Mateo said. "If they're alive. In that place."

"Then nine days is the number we work with," Sam said. "Not the number we grieve. Not yet." He closed the tablet with a click that sounded very loud. "I've spent twenty years learning the difference between a patient who's dying and a patient who's just hard to reach. The second kind is why I still do this job."

Priya was already pulling up the trench charts, her fingers moving fast, her face lit blue and set. Mateo had stopped smoking somewhere around day nine of the log and hadn't noticed. And Sam sat with his hand still in the biometric clip, his heart rate finally climbing past one-twelve, because now—*now*—it was allowed to.

The weight of it settled over all three of them, the way ballast settles a hull. Knowledge like this didn't make you free. It made you *responsible*. Somewhere in the dark below them, seven people had done the brave thing and paid for it, and the bill had just been handed across the table to four strangers on a salvage boat.

The galley door opened. Rosalind stood there, hair damp from the deck, eyes going from the open tablet to their faces one by one, reading them the way Sam read a chart at the start of a shift.

"Well?" she said.

Sam slid the tablet across the table to her, the final entry glowing on the screen.

"You'd better sit down, Captain," he said. "And then you'd better decide fast. Because the last line of this log isn't a warning about what they knew." He tapped the screen, on the words that had been sitting in his chest since he read them. "It's a countdown. *They know we know*—which means somewhere out there, someone is already doing the arithmetic on whether four more people on this boat are a problem to be solved."

Rosalind looked down at the screen. Above them, through the deck plates, they all heard it at the same moment: the low, distant thud of engines. Not one set. Several. Big ones, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once needed to hide.

Priya was first to the porthole. When she turned around, her face had gone the grey of deep water.

"That'll be the navy," she said. "And Captain—they're not passing through."
