## 7. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The frigate came out of the haze at 0640, the way bad news always does—early, and pretending to be something else.

Rosalind was on deck with a mug of coffee gone lukewarm, watching the survey camp's generators burn fuel they'd stolen from a government that no longer existed in any useful sense, when Priya's voice crackled through the wheelhouse speaker.

"Ros. Contact bearing two-seven-zero. Warship profile. She's got us on her radar whether she admits it or not."

"How big?"

"Big enough to have a name longer than our boat."

Rosalind set down the coffee. On the horizon, gray resolved into steel: a frigate, low and mean, its superstructure bristling with the kind of equipment that didn't get sold to salvage outfits. No flag flying yet. That was almost polite. The really dangerous ones announced themselves.

"Mateo," she called down the hatch. "Wake Sam. And put the kettle on."

"Is this a kettle situation?" Mateo's head appeared, hair flattened on one side, eyes still soft with sleep.

"It's about to be."

* * *

By seven-thirty the frigate had closed to two miles and was holding there, patient as a shark that had already eaten. A rigid-hull inflatable dropped from its side and came across the swell toward them, white wake stitching a straight line, four figures aboard in dark uniforms.

Rosalind stood at the rail with her hands visible and her heart doing arithmetic. Behind her, the *Kestrel's Wake*—a hundred and ten feet of refitted trawler held together by welding beads and stubbornness—smelled of diesel, instant coffee, and Sam's attempt at pancakes, which had been abandoned halfway through and now sat congealing in the galley like a small domestic tragedy.

The logbook was in the wheelhouse, wrapped in a garbage bag inside a toolbox under a coil of spare anchor line. It had taken Rosalind eleven minutes at dawn to decide that hiding it there was better than hiding it anywhere cleverer, because clever places were the first places professionals looked. She'd learned that lesson the expensive way, years ago, in a life she did not discuss.

"Easy morning for them," Mateo murmured beside her. He'd shaved, she noticed. For the navy. That was Mateo all over—he met authority the way he met weather, with respect and a clean shirt.

"Don't jinx it."

"I don't believe in jinxes."

"You believe in saints."

"Different department entirely."

The inflatable bumped against their hull. A sailor in a flak vest caught the lines, and then an officer climbed aboard with the unhurried precision of a man who had never once been told no by the ocean.

He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, jaw like a mooring cleat. His uniform fit him the way good decisions fit people—seamlessly. He removed his cap, tucked it under his arm, and smiled a smile that had been issued to him along with his commission.

"Good morning. Commander Hale, Royal Australian Navy." A pause, calibrated. "You're a long way from any survey grid, Miss...?"

"Hartwell. Rosalind Hartwell." The name sat in her mouth like a coin she'd bitten too many times. "Salvage contractor. We're recovering equipment for a client."

"What client?"

"The kind who pays triple and doesn't ask for receipts."

Hale's smile didn't move, but something behind his eyes did—a small adjustment, like a lens focusing. "May I see your paperwork?"

"You may not," Rosalind said pleasantly. "Commercially sensitive. But I'll tell you what I can tell any naval officer: we're here legally, we're insured, and we're leaving as soon as the job's done."

"And when will that be?"

"When it's done."

Behind her, very quietly, Sam made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a prayer.

* * *

They gave Hale coffee anyway, because refusing would have been memorable, and the last thing Rosalind wanted to be was memorable. They sat in the cramped mess where the ceiling was low enough that even the shortest of them had to duck, and the pancake casualty had been discreetly buried in the bin.

Priya poured with the serenity of a woman who could sink a warship with a wrench and had decided, for now, against it. Mateo produced biscuits from somewhere, because Mateo always produced biscuits from somewhere; Rosalind had stopped investigating it the way you stop investigating magic tricks you need to keep working.

"So," Hale said, accepting his cup. "Miss Hartwell. Salvage. How long in the trade?"

"Twelve years."

"That's a specific number."

"Most numbers are."

He laughed—warmly, convincingly—and Rosalind felt the cold start behind her sternum, because she knew that laugh. She'd heard it once before, eight years ago, in a port office in Darwin, attached to a man in civilian clothes who'd been asking careful questions about a wreck she'd dived and a cargo manifest she'd kept her mouth shut about. He hadn't introduced himself then either. But she remembered the voice the way you remember a hand that's been in your pocket.

Commander Hale. The name meant nothing to her that morning. But the face, older now, harder around the eyes—the face was a signature, and she'd seen it on the periphery of documents Sam had spent three nights decrypting. Not prominently. Prominence was for people who expected to be investigated. Hale's name appeared the way water stains appear on a ceiling: small, repeated, deniable.

And now he was sitting in her mess drinking her coffee, asking how long she'd been in the trade, and the answer to why was sitting in a garbage bag under the anchor line.

"We're curious about your coordinates," Hale said. "This area's restricted. Naval exercise zone."

"Since when?"

"Since before your contract was signed, I'd imagine."

"Then whoever signed my contract didn't get the memo."

"No," Hale agreed softly. "I imagine they didn't." He sipped. Set the cup down with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a latch closing. "Here's my difficulty, Miss Hartwell. There is no record of a salvage contract for these waters. No client of record. No survey vessel scheduled—which is itself strange, because a survey team was operating here three weeks ago, and they've gone rather quiet."

"They finished up," Rosalind said. "We're collecting their gear."

"Did you speak with them?"

"On the radio. Standard handover."

"What frequency?"

Rosalind named one. It was a guess dressed as confidence, and she watched Hale receive it the way a bank teller receives a note passed under the glass—with perfect courtesy and total awareness that something was wrong.

"I see," he said. "Well. In any case, I'm instructed to ask you to vacate the area. There are operations underway below that are—" he paused, choosing the word with tongs, "—sensitive."

"What kind of operations?"

"The kind the navy won't discuss."

It was such an honest answer, delivered so dishonestly, that Rosalind almost liked him for it.

* * *

He wanted to tour the boat. Of course he did. Rosalind said yes, because saying no would have been memorable, and she walked him through the *Kestrel's Wake* like a woman showing off a secondhand car: engine room, where Priya stood amid her machinery looking bored enough to be innocent; the dive locker, racks of tanks, the survey submersible's cradle empty and explained away with a shrug—*client's asset, already recovered*; the wheelhouse, where the toolbox sat under the anchor line and Rosalind's pulse performed a slow drumroll while Hale glanced around with the idle thoroughness of a man cataloguing everything without appearing to look at anything.

"Nice boat," he said.

"She gets me where I'm going."

"That's all any of us want, isn't it." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on her a half-second past comfortable, and Rosalind understood that the sentence wasn't about boats at all. It was an offer shaped like small talk, or a warning shaped like an offer. *Tell me what you want, and perhaps we can arrange for you to keep wanting it.*

"Where are you going, Commander?"

"Wherever I'm sent." He replaced his cap. At the rail, waiting for the inflatable, he turned back. "One more thing, Miss Hartwell. The survey team. Six people. If you hear anything—anything at all—on your radios, your hydrophones, anything washed ashore..." He handed her a card. Plain white. A phone number, nothing else. "We'd be grateful."

"And if I find their gear?"

"Leave it. We'll collect it."

"You said there were no records out here."

"There aren't." The smile again, issued and official. "That's rather the point."

The inflatable took him away across the swell, and Rosalind stood at the rail until the frigate began to turn, slow and enormous, back toward its holding pattern two miles out. It didn't leave. She noted that. It didn't leave, and it didn't pretend to.

* * *

She found them all in the mess, waiting, wearing the particular silence of a crew that had watched its captain lie to a warship and wanted to know whether to be terrified or impressed.

"Well," Mateo said. "That went well."

"He drank our coffee," Priya said. "I feel violated."

Sam was turning the plain white card over in his fingers. "No name. No rank. Just a number."

"That's the point of the card," Rosalind said. She took it from him, looked at it, and dropped it into the pancake bin. "It's not contact information. It's a leash. He wants us to call it. Calling means we know we're being watched. Calling means we've accepted the terms."

"And if we don't call?" Priya asked.

"Then eventually someone calls us."

Nobody laughed. Outside, through the salt-fogged portlight, the frigate sat on the horizon like a verdict deferred.

Rosalind leaned against the bulkhead and let herself think clearly for the first time since dawn. Here was the shape of it: Hale knew a survey team had vanished. Hale knew they'd been here. Hale had come aboard looking for exactly what Rosalind had hidden, and had left satisfied—or pretending to be, which amounted to the same thing in men like him. And his name, his careful, deniable, water-stained name, was in the logbook wrapped in a garbage bag twelve feet from where he'd drunk his coffee.

Staying invisible had been the plan. Twelve years of plan. Keep your head down, keep your name clean, keep moving, measure your life in miles and never in years. And it had worked, mostly, right up until the moment a polite man with a mooring-cleat jaw stepped aboard and made invisibility impossible—because now they'd been seen. Logged. Filed. Somewhere in a building with no windows, there was a folder with *Kestrel's Wake* written on it, and folders like that didn't get closed. They got acted on.

"You recognized him," Sam said quietly. It wasn't a question. Sam missed nothing; it was the medic in him.

"His face. From the files." She watched their faces absorb that. "Not just his face. His name's in there. Three times. Small print. The kind of small print that runs programs nobody discusses."

Mateo exhaled slowly. "So the man telling us to leave is one of the men who made them disappear."

"Or one of the men who ordered it done. There's usually a chain, and chains have hands at both ends." Rosalind rubbed her eyes. "He offered me a card instead of a threat. That's worse. Threats are for people who matter. Cards are for people being given a chance to matter less."

"So what do we do?" Priya asked. "We can't outrun a frigate. We can't outgun one. We can't even out-caffeinate one, apparently, since he took the last of the good beans."

"He didn't take the beans."

"He took the *good* beans, Rosalind."

Despite everything, something in Rosalind's chest loosened half a degree. This was the thing about crews built from people with things to hide: fear never quite got the whole room. There was always someone defending the coffee.

"We do what we came to do," she said. "We finish the job. We find those six people, or we find out what happened to them, and we bring home whatever truth fits in a boat this size." She looked at each of them in turn. "But understand what today was. Today we stopped being invisible. Whatever happens next, there are men on that ship whose entire profession is making problems stop existing. As of this morning, we're a problem."

Silence, of the working kind. Priya nodded slowly, already halfway into some engineering calculation of escape velocity versus displacement. Mateo cracked his knuckles, one hand at a time, a habit Rosalind had come to read as his version of prayer. Sam simply looked at her with those steady doctor's eyes and said, "Then we'd best be worth the trouble."

"Amen," said Mateo.

Rosalind pushed off the bulkhead and headed for the wheelhouse, and only when the door was shut behind her did she allow herself the luxury of pressing her forehead against the cool chart table and breathing. Under the anchor line, the toolbox waited. Inside the toolbox, the logbook waited. Inside the logbook, six names and a trench and a program that ate people.

She pulled the chart drawer open and spread the local charts across the table, and her eye went where it had been going for two days—to the trench line, to the depth soundings that fell away beneath the survey camp like a staircase going down into the world's throat. The navy wouldn't discuss what was down there. Fine. Discussion was overrated.

Down there, six people had stopped broadcasting mid-sentence. Down there, a facility breathed in the dark. And down there, sooner or later, Rosalind Hartwell was going to have to go, because staying on the surface had just become the most dangerous place of all.

Through the wheelhouse glass, the frigate's running lights blinked on against the graying afternoon—steady, patient, unblinking.

Watching.

Waiting.

Counting, she was quite sure, the same miles she was.
