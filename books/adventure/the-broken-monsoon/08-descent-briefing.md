## 8. Descent Briefing

Mateo Cruz had learned a long time ago that the worst meetings always started with someone saying *I'm going to be honest with you.*

His mother used to say it before telling him his father wasn't coming back. His first captain said it before announcing the engine was held together with prayer and wire. And now Rosalind Hartwell stood at the head of the *Magpie*'s cramped galley table, a chart weighted down with coffee mugs, and said, "I'm going to be honest with you, because you've earned it."

Mateo's stomach did the thing it always did when the truth was coming. He braced his elbows on the table and waited.

Rosalind looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. She had that quality Mateo had noticed in people who'd been carrying something heavy for a very long time—shoulders set like they were braced for a blow, eyes that kept checking exits even in a room with one door.

"The survey team didn't lose their equipment," she said. "They found something. And then they disappeared."

She laid the logbook on the table. Sam had spent most of the previous night on it, and it showed—he had the hollowed-out look of a man who'd argued with a cipher and won, barely. Priya sat across from Mateo, arms crossed, one boot bouncing against the deck plate in a rhythm Mateo had come to think of as her thinking tempo.

"Eleven days ago, a government survey vessel anchored at coordinates that don't appear on any chart," Rosalind continued. "Their last transponder ping cut off mid-broadcast. No distress call. No debris. We found their camp intact—generators running, coffee in the cups. Nobody walked out. Nobody was dragged out. They were just *gone*."

"And the logbook?" Priya asked, though her bouncing boot said she already suspected.

"The logbook says they found a structure in the trench. A facility. And it names the people who built it—officials, military, contractors—running an illegal deep-sea weapons program. The last entry is four words." Rosalind's jaw tightened. "*They know we know.*"

The galley was quiet except for the tick of the cooling coffee pot and the eternal complaint of the *Magpie*'s hull, which creaked when it was happy and groaned when it wasn't. Right now it was groaning, which Mateo chose to interpret as agreement with the mood of the room.

"So," Sam said at last, rubbing his eyes behind his glasses. "We're not salvaging equipment."

"No."

"We're diving into a black site at the bottom of an ocean trench, to rescue people the government made vanish, carrying evidence the government will kill for."

"That's the shape of it, yes."

"And a navy frigate is currently sitting out there," Priya said, jerking a thumb toward the bulkhead, "close enough that I can read the hull number through the scope, waiting for us to do something stupid."

"Close enough that I could wave at them," Mateo said. "I did wave. Nobody waved back. Very rude."

Nobody laughed, which Mateo felt was unfair, because the joke had been load-bearing. He'd learned that about fear—if nobody laughed at it, it got bigger. He laughed a little himself, alone, and it helped, the way a single match helps in a large dark room. Not much. But it's a start.

Rosalind let the silence sit for a moment, then did the thing that Mateo would think about for years afterward. She straightened up, put her hands flat on the table, and said:

"Here's the way out. Any of you can take it. No questions, no hard feelings. Priya can have us back to port in thirty-six hours. I'll pay everyone in full for the contract, plus a bonus for the trouble. You walk away clean, and whatever happens next happens to me and this ship."

Mateo blinked. He'd expected a speech about duty. About courage. He hadn't expected her to mean it—and she did mean it, that was the thing. He could see it in the set of her mouth. She was offering them the door with the handle already turning.

"Why?" Priya asked, and her voice had an edge on it. "Why would you give us the out? You need a crew."

"Because I've done this before," Rosalind said quietly. "Followed someone into something because I trusted them, and found out too late what I was actually carrying. I won't do that to you. What you know now, you can't unknow. So the choice has to be real."

*The choice has to be real.* Mateo turned the phrase over. It sounded like something a person says when they've made a thousand unreal choices themselves.

Priya looked at the logbook. Then at the chart. Then at her own hands, which had gone still, which Mateo had never once seen.

"I have a question," she said. "The submersible. The survey team's. Can it actually make the dive?"

"Two-seat, rated for full trench depth," Rosalind said. "It's sitting in their flooded moon pool right now, and from what Sam decrypted, it's intact. We'd take it down the canyon line to the facility."

"Life support?"

"Seventy-two hours, four people in shifts. It'll be tight."

"It'll be a coffin with a coffee maker," Priya said. "Fine. I'm in."

Mateo stared at her. "That's it? *Fine, I'm in?* You spent an hour yesterday explaining to me why the bilge pump is a death trap, and this—this is *fine?*"

"The bilge pump is a death trap *I* built out of parts I trust," Priya said. "This is different. This is—" She stopped, and for a moment the engineer's armor slipped and Mateo saw what was underneath, which was a woman who had crossed an ocean to get away from something and had been running ever since. "I spent six years making myself too useful to be noticed. Turns out you can hide in plain sight your whole life and it doesn't mean you were ever actually *alive*. I'd like to be alive for something that matters. So. Fine. I'm in."

Sam took off his glasses and cleaned them on his shirt, which was what he did instead of sighing.

"I'm a medic," he said. "There are eleven missing people down there, and at least one of them—if the logbook's right, if any of it's right—might be alive. I don't have a choice, professionally speaking. My oath doesn't have an exit clause." He put the glasses back on. "Also, I've seen Rosalind dive. If anyone's getting people out of a flooded structure at depth, it's her. I'm in."

Three faces turned to Mateo.

He hadn't expected that. He was the deckhand. He tied knots, painted rust, and made the coffee that everyone complained about and everyone drank. He was, in the strict hierarchy of the *Magpie*, the person whose opinion was sought least and whose gossip was sought most.

But they were looking at him like it mattered.

Mateo thought about his mother, who had worked thirty years cleaning rooms in a hotel in Veracruz and had once told him, folding towels with the serenity of a saint, *Mijo, the world is full of people who look away. Be expensive to look away from.* He thought about eleven people who'd drunk their coffee one morning and vanished from the face of the sea, and about how somewhere down in that trench there was probably a woman like his mother, or a kid like he'd been, waiting for someone to not look away.

He thought, too—and this was the honest part, the part he'd never say out loud—about how he'd spent three years on this crew of strangers, eating at this table, complaining about this coffee, and how somewhere in there the strangers had stopped being strangers. Priya had taught him to solder. Sam had stitched up his hand and lectured him about tetanus for forty minutes. Rosalind had stood beside him in a storm off Guam and taught him that fear was just excitement that hadn't decided what it was yet.

You didn't leave family in a hole in the bottom of the ocean. Even family you'd only had for three years. Even family that was currently proposing to take all of you into that hole on purpose.

"I have one condition," Mateo said.

Rosalind raised an eyebrow. "Name it."

"Nobody drinks the last of the coffee without making more. Not even during a crisis. *Especially* not during a crisis."

"That's the condition," Sam said flatly. "We're planning a dive into a classified military installation and your condition is about coffee."

"Coffee is morale," Mateo said with dignity. "Morale is survival. I've read books."

Rosalind's mouth did the thing it almost never did—the thing that was maybe a quarter of a smile, the ghost of one, like a fish surfacing briefly before deciding the air wasn't for it. "Deal," she said. "Anyone else?"

Silence. Priya shook her head. Sam shook his. Mateo shook his, and then, because the moment seemed to require it, he put his hand flat on the table, palm down, in the middle of the chart.

It was a stupid gesture. He knew it was a stupid gesture. But Sam put his hand on top of it, and then Priya sighed the sigh of a woman who found rituals embarrassing but people indispensable, and put hers on too, and then Rosalind looked at all of them for a long moment with an expression Mateo couldn't read—something old and startled, like a woman being handed something she'd stopped expecting to be given—and she put her hand down last.

"Okay," she said, and her voice was rough at the edges. "Okay. Then here's how we do this."

* * *

They worked through the night, and the *Magpie* became a different ship.

Priya stripped the survey submersible's schematics out of Sam's decrypted files and spread them across the engine room like a general planning a campaign. She talked to the schematics the way other people talked to pets. "You're a lovely machine and I'm going to keep you alive if it kills me," she told the pressure hull diagram, and Mateo, hauling spares down the ladder, decided not to ask.

The plan, as it emerged, had the elegance of all desperate plans: simple in outline, terrifying in detail.

Rosalind and Sam would take the submersible down the canyon line—two thousand meters, then four, then deeper, following the trench wall to the facility's maintenance lock, which the survey logbook described in enough detail to find it. Priya would hold the *Magpie* on station above as surface relay, monitoring their tether and air, running the radio silence drill they'd rehearsed a dozen times. Mateo would ride the submersible's support skiff down to the launch point and handle the launch itself, then return to the surface.

"Four people in a two-seat submersible is not a plan," Priya said, "it's a dare."

"Marsh might be alive down there," Rosalind said. "If she is, she comes up with us. That's why the numbers are ugly."

"Marsh," Sam said. "Dr. Lena Marsh. The logbook's author. She signed the last three entries."

"If she's alive, she's been down there eleven days," Rosalind said. "If she's alive, she's the only person on this planet who's seen the inside of that place and walked out. She's worth ugly numbers."

Mateo spent his shift prepping the launch skiff, checking fuel lines and winch cables with the particular devotion of a man who understood that his entire job was the two minutes of work that would put his people into the dark. It was strange, he thought, how a job could be ninety-nine percent waiting and one percent everything. He'd been a deckhand long enough to know that the one percent was why the ninety-nine existed.

Around two in the morning, he found Rosalind alone on the aft deck, checking the submersible's lifting points by flashlight, and he did the thing that was maybe the bravest thing he did all week, braver than anything that would happen in the trench.

He asked her a personal question.

"Boss. The out you gave us. Did anyone ever give you one?"

The flashlight paused. The sea breathed against the hull, slow and enormous.

"Once," she said. "A long time ago. I didn't take it." A pause. "I've regretted it in different ways ever since. Some days I regret taking it. Some days I regret what I was running from. It's complicated."

"Is that why you offered it to us?"

"It's why I *meant* it." She looked at him then, and in the flashlight's spill her face was open in a way he'd never seen—tired, and honest, and a little frightened, which somehow made him trust her more than all her competence ever had. "You should know something, Mateo. Whatever's down there—whatever Hale's people do when they find us—I'm going to do my best to bring everyone home. But I can't promise it. I can only promise that I won't ask any of you to go somewhere I won't go first."

"I know," Mateo said. "That's why nobody left."

She looked at him like she wanted to say something else, and then the quarter-smile came back, the fish surfacing again. "Go sleep. We dive at first light."

"Yes, boss." He got as far as the hatch, then turned back. "Rosalind? For what it's worth—my mother says the world is full of people who look away. She'd have liked you. You look *at* things."

He didn't wait for an answer, because he'd learned that some moments are better left unclosed, like a good knot with a working end left loose. But as he climbed down into the galley to make the last pot of coffee—because morale, because survival, because he'd read books—he heard her boots on the deck above, going back to work, and he thought: *strangers, three years ago. Strangers, and now I'd follow her into a hole in the ocean, and the terrible thing is it doesn't feel like bravery. It feels like Tuesday.*

* * *

First light came gray and windless, the sea like poured metal.

They gathered on the aft deck around the submersible, which sat in its cradle like a fat yellow beetle—*Nautilus*-ugly, survey-stamped, and, according to Priya's three a.m. inspection, "sound enough, which is the best I can honestly say about any of us."

Rosalind stood at the rail in her dive gear, and Mateo watched her look at each of them in turn, and he understood that she was doing what captains do at moments like this: memorizing faces. Just in case.

"Comms check," she said.

"Priya, surface relay, reading you clear."

"Sam, dive medic, clear."

"Mateo, launch, clear." His voice came out steadier than he felt. Good. That was the trick of it. The voice goes first, and the rest of you follows.

Rosalind nodded once. "Priya—if we miss our first check-in by more than thirty minutes, you take the *Magpie* and you go. That's not a request. You go, you keep the evidence safe, and you get it to people who can use it. Swear it."

Priya's jaw worked. "Sworn," she said, in the tone of a woman swearing something she intended to interpret very loosely.

"Sam—nobody dies of something I could have prevented. That's your whole job down there. Mateo—" She stopped. For a moment the captain's voice wavered, just a hair, like a compass needle finding north after a shock. "Make the launch clean."

"Always clean," Mateo said. "I'm offended you'd suggest otherwise."

The winch took the strain. The submersible swung out over the gray water, and Mateo stood at the release lever with Sam beside him and Priya's voice in his ear running the final checks, and below them the sea waited, flat and vast and keeping its secret, two thousand meters of darkness between the daylight and the thing they'd come to find.

"Launching in thirty seconds," Rosalind's voice said through the hull, calm as a woman reading a grocery list. "See you at the bottom."

Mateo gripped the lever. He thought of his mother's towels, folded into perfect squares; of Priya's soldering iron; of Sam's endless tetanus lectures; of eleven people who'd vanished with their coffee still warm.

"See you at the bottom," he repeated, and pulled the release, and the sea took them.
