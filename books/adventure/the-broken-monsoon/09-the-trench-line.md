## 9. The Trench Line

The submersible had a name painted on its flank in cheerful yellow letters—*Puffin*—which struck me as the kind of joke engineers tell when they've spent too long in a dry dock. Puffins are small, stubborn birds that dive into black water and come back up again. I suppose someone thought that was comforting. At four hundred meters down, it was not.

"Comms check," Priya's voice said in my ear, thin and crackling through the tether. "Rosalind, say something so I know you haven't been eaten."

"I'm being eaten," I said. "It's fine. Very polite about it."

"Good. Mateo?"

From the seat behind me, wedged between the oxygen scrubbers and a crate of emergency flares he'd insisted on bringing because, in his words, "if we die down here we should at least die colorfully," Mateo said, "Present and terrified, as requested."

Sam's voice came last from the surface ship, *Meridian*, where she'd drawn the short straw of staying topside. "All systems green. Water temperature dropping. You're crossing into the thermocline now—expect some fogging on the viewport."

She was right. Within minutes the water outside went from deep blue to a bruised, murky gray-green, and then to something that wasn't a color at all. It was like descending through the skin of the world into whatever lay beneath it. The *Puffin*'s floodlights carved cones out of the dark, and the dark took them and gave back nothing.

I checked my gauges more often than I needed to. It gave my hands something to do.

Depth: 620 meters. Outside pressure: sixty-three atmospheres. Inside the hull: one atmosphere, kept there by eleven centimeters of titanium and a great deal of faith. Somewhere below us, at the bottom of a canyon system the charts called nothing at all, was a facility the navy didn't have, built by money that didn't exist, holding people who had officially never been there.

And somewhere above us, forty kilometers north, a frigate sat on the surface pretending to be weather.

"You're quiet," Mateo said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet *differently* right now." He leaned forward until his chin nearly rested on my shoulder, the way he did when he wanted to read my instruments without asking. He'd learned the habit from watching me do it to Priya. "Talk to me. What does quiet-differently mean?"

"It means I'm counting," I said.

"Counting what? Miles?"

I almost smiled. Twelve years of measuring my life in nautical miles—the distance from the accident, the distance from the name I used to be—and Mateo had picked up on it inside a month. "Meters, this time. Every meter down is a meter we'll have to climb back up. And every meter has a price tag attached."

"What's the price at six hundred meters?"

"About four minutes of consciousness if the hull goes," I said. "Give or take."

There was a pause. Then Mateo said, cheerfully, "Well. Good thing the hull isn't going," and settled back into his seat, and I understood that he knew exactly what I'd told him and had decided, deliberately, to answer fear with jokes. It was a good strategy. I'd used it myself for twelve years.

* * *

The trench line opened beneath us at 780 meters like a wound in the seafloor.

Priya had described it from the sonar data: a canyon network running along an old fault, walls plunging another kilometer and a half into sediment and basalt, branching like the roots of a tree nobody had ever seen. The survey team's last recorded position sat near the third branch, close against the eastern wall, where the sonar had shown an anomaly too regular to be geology.

Regular things at the bottom of trenches are either shipwrecks or secrets. We were hoping for both.

"Floodlights to full," I said. "Slow to half a knot. I want eyes on everything before anything has eyes on us."

"That's not how eyes work," Mateo said.

"Humor me."

The *Puffin* crept forward into the canyon mouth, and the walls rose around us on either side—black rock furred with pale bacterial mats that glowed faintly under our lights, like frost that had forgotten which way was up. Small fish hung motionless in the beams, eyeless or nearly so, drifting like scraps of paper. The water was utterly still. No current reached this deep; the trench held its breath the way a held room does.

It was beautiful. That was the worst part. Twelve years of diving wrecks had taught me that the ocean's cruelest trick is making its worst places gorgeous, so that you relax, so that you look instead of watch.

"Sonar contact," Priya said suddenly, her voice sharpening. "Bearing two-one-five, range nine hundred meters. Stationary. Consistent with the survey anomaly."

"Copy. Bring us around."

I swung the *Puffin*'s nose toward the contact, and for perhaps ten seconds everything was ordinary—just rock and darkness and the slow arithmetic of depth.

Then the contact moved.

Not much. Not fast. But it moved *against* the current, and there was no current, and stationary objects at the bottom of canyons do not, as a rule, decide to become mobile ones.

"Priya," I said carefully. "Tell me that's an echo."

Her silence lasted three seconds, which from Priya was a scream. "It's... not an echo. Contact is maintaining bearing but closing range. Slowly. Maybe two knots." A pause. "Rosalind, it's bigger than we are."

My hands were steady on the controls. I noted that with distant interest, the way you notice someone else's hands. "Mateo. Kill the exterior floods. Running lights only."

Darkness swallowed us whole. In the viewport, the world became a suggestion—faint bioluminescent scatter, the ghost-glow of the bacterial mats, nothing else. I cut our speed to a drift and let the *Puffin* hang in the water column like a jellyfish, small and soft and hopefully uninteresting.

"Range?" I whispered, though whispering underwater was pointless and we both knew it.

"Six hundred meters. Closing." Another pause. "Four hundred."

Mateo's voice came low beside my ear. "Submarine?"

"At this depth? Nothing military runs a hull through a canyon this narrow." My mouth had gone dry. "Nothing *manned*, anyway."

"Three hundred meters. It's—" Priya stopped. "It stopped. Contact is holding station at three hundred meters, bearing two-one-five. It's just... sitting there."

We sat there too. Two shapes in the black, regarding each other across three hundred meters of water older than language. I found I was breathing very slowly and very deliberately, the way Sam had taught all of us during the medical drills—not to calm the body, but to give the mind a job.

"Any transponder ping?" I asked. "Active sonar? Anything?"

"Nothing. Passive only. Whatever it is, it's listening."

Listening. Not hunting. Listening—the way you listen at a door.

"Rosalind," Mateo said quietly. "The survey team's last position is bearing zero-nine-zero from here. Away from it."

"I know."

"We could go around. Come at the site from the north branch. Add twenty minutes to the dive."

I looked at the sonar display, at that single patient blip holding station in the dark like a sentry who had all the time in the world. Everything in me that had spent twelve years surviving said: go around. Go around, go home, stay small, stay invisible, stay alive.

Everything else said: they put a guard on that canyon for a reason, and the reason is sitting at the bottom of it.

"We go around," I said. "Nice and slow. And Mateo—if that thing moves toward us, you say so immediately, even if you have to interrupt me mid-sentence."

"Even if you're saying something important?"

"*Especially* then."

* * *

The northern branch of the canyon was narrower, and flying the *Puffin* through it was like threading a needle in a dream—every surface the same black, every shadow the same shape, my depth perception dissolving after the first hundred meters until I was flying by sonar overlay and prayer. Mateo narrated the clearance margins in a low, steady voice, calling out "two meters port, one and a half starboard, easy, easy," like a man talking a spooked horse over a bridge, and I loved him a little for it, in the way you love people who make themselves useful in exactly the moment usefulness matters.

Twenty-three minutes later, Priya said, "Contact astern. It's moving again—following the main channel. Heading..." She exhaled. "Heading away from us. Toward the facility approach."

It was escorting the door. Or guarding it. Either way, we'd slipped past it, and none of us said what we were all thinking, which was that eventually we would have to come back out the same way.

The survey site appeared at 840 meters, tucked into a shelf of the eastern wall where the canyon widened into a hollow like the cupped palm of a giant hand.

I saw it before the sonar confirmed it: a shape wedged against the rock face, half-shrouded in drifting silt, our floodlights catching the curve of a sail and the dull gleam of a viewport.

"There," I said. "That's them."

The survey submersible was a *Nautilus*-class workhorse, thirty feet of orange-painted steel, the kind of vehicle that had hauled scientists and equipment into inconvenient places for twenty years without drama. This one had drama written all over it. It lay canted hard to port, jammed bow-first into a fissure in the canyon wall, its stern hanging out over the drop like a man leaning too far over a railing. Silt had begun the long process of burying it. Our lights showed the survey team's markings still crisp on the hull—a white triangle, a registration number—and beneath them, scrawled across the flank in what looked like paint pen, a message:

**DO NOT SURFACE. THEY ARE LISTENING UP THERE TOO.**

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

"Well," Mateo said finally, very quietly. "That's not ominous at all."

"Approach from the stern," I said. "Slow. I want to see the damage before we commit to docking."

I brought us in along the wrecked submersible's flank, and the floodlights walked across the hull section by section, and I began, piece by piece, to understand what had happened down here in the dark nine days ago.

The *Puffin*'s lights found the first breach aft of the sail: a jagged star of torn metal, edges bent *outward*. I felt my stomach tighten. Outward meant from within. Outward meant pressure from inside a pressurized hull, which meant—

"Mateo," I said. "Get the cutting kit ready. And the med kit. Both."

"Why both?"

"Because there's a second breach amidships, and a third by the viewport, and all three of them blew outward." I kept my voice level, professional, the voice I used for bad news delivered in a steady drip rather than a flood. "Something got out of this submarine, Mateo. Or someone made sure it could."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "People don't blow holes in their own hull at eight hundred meters."

"No," I agreed. "They don't."

Unless they were trying to open the door for something. Unless they were trying to get *out*. Unless the alternative to a hole in the hull was worse than eight hundred meters of black water coming in.

I brought the *Puffin* alongside the breach amidships—roughly man-sized, ragged, the metal curled back like petals. Through it, our lights pierced the flooded interior of the survey craft, and the beam caught loose equipment turning slowly in the water: a clipboard, a thermal mug, a jacket with a name patch I couldn't quite read. Sediment drifted like snow that had fallen indoors.

And then the light swept across the aft compartment, and I saw the hand.

It floated just inside the breach, pale, unmoving, fingers slightly curled—an arm in a torn survey-team jumpsuit, attached to a shoulder, attached to a body pressed back against the far bulkhead as if whoever it was had been trying, in the last moments, to get away from the hole they'd cut themselves.

Except the suit was empty at the chest. Except the body had been down here nine days in water that does not preserve, and what our lights showed was not a person anymore but the evidence of one, and I heard Mateo make a small sound behind me that I will hear for the rest of my life.

"Oh God," he whispered. "Oh God, Rosalind, is that—is he—"

"He's gone," I said. "Nine days gone. They all might be." Even as I said it, I didn't believe it, because the logbook had said *they know we know*, and people who write warnings like that are usually trying to warn someone still alive. "But the others weren't in this sub when it went down. Six crew, one submersible rated for four. Someone stayed on the surface boat. Someone was taken elsewhere."

"The facility," Mateo said.

"The facility."

I held the *Puffin* steady in the currentless dark, our lights full on the dead man's outstretched hand, and I made myself look. Twelve years of salvage had taught me that the dead deserve witnesses—that looking is a kind of respect, maybe the only kind left to give. His name patch had come loose and hung by one thread, turning slowly. I memorized the registration number on the hull instead. Somebody topside could match it to a name. Somebody should.

"Rosalind," Priya said in my ear, gentle. "Your heart rate's up. I can see it on the telemetry."

"I'm fine."

"You're grieving," she corrected, "and fine. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," I admitted. "They're not."

I reached for the external manipulator controls and eased the *Puffin*'s mechanical arm toward the loose name patch, meaning to retrieve it, meaning to bring something of him back into the daylight. And that was when the sonar alarm sounded—one sharp chirp, then another.

"Contact," Priya said, and her voice had gone flat and fast, the voice she used when there was no time for feeling. "Bearing two-one-five. It's moving. Fast. Closing on our position—it's coming *around the canyon wall*—"

On the display, the blip that had guarded the facility approach was sweeping toward us at better than eight knots, and at eight knots, in this canyon, with a wreck jammed against one wall and a drop-off on the other, we had nowhere to go and no time to get there.

"Mateo," I said, hands already moving on the controls, "how colorful can those flares get?"
