## 10. Survivor

I have been a medic long enough to know that the dead are honest and the living lie. The dead tell you exactly what happened to them—broken bones, water in the lungs, the clean arithmetic of trauma. The living tell you what they can bear to say. Nine days alone in the dark, I suspected, would make Dr. Lena Marsh a very unreliable narrator indeed, if she was alive to narrate anything at all.

"Talk to me, Sam," Rosalind's voice came through my helmet, tinny and close. "You're quiet, and quiet from you is worse than Priya swearing."

"Priya swearing means something's broken," I said. "Me being quiet means I'm thinking."

"Terrifying. Think faster."

We were forty meters down the canyon wall, three of us in the water and Mateo holding station above with the umbilicals, and the survey submersible was wedged into a fissure like a coin dropped between sofa cushions. From a distance, in the lights, it looked almost intact. Up close, the story was written all over it: the forward viewport starred with cracks radiating from a single point, the hatch deformed outward, the hull scored along its flank in long parallel grooves.

"Those grooves," Priya said over the comm. She had a way of talking when she was frightened, very flat and very precise, like a woman reading her own autopsy. "Those are from the inside. Something was dragged across the hull from within."

"Or someone," Mateo said.

Nobody answered that. We hung in the green-black water and let the silence do its work, and I watched Rosalind's lights move toward the hatch with the unhurried steadiness of a woman who had decided, somewhere in the last hour, that fear was a luxury billed by the minute and she was on a budget.

* * *

Getting the hatch open took twenty minutes and most of Priya's vocabulary. The mechanism was jammed by the deformation, and in the end Rosalind braced her feet against the hull and hauled while Priya worked the wheel, and when it gave way it came with a groan of metal and a slow exhale of trapped air that bloomed up past our lights like a silver jellyfish.

The interior was cold. I felt it before I saw anything—the water inside the cabin was several degrees colder than the sea around it, which meant the submersible had been venting its own heat for days, which meant its power cells were nearly dead, which meant—

"Sam," Rosalind said quietly. "You're on."

Because there, crammed into the aft compartment behind the equipment racks, curled into a space no larger than a shipping crate, was a person.

I want to be honest about my first thought, because medics are supposed to be calm and I was not. My first thought was: *she's dead*. The figure was so still, so folded in on herself, so gray in our lights, that everything in me began composing the report before I'd even crossed the threshold. *Recovered remains of survey team member, female, approximately—*

Then the figure flinched from my light, and my heart did something acrobatic and unprofessional.

"Dr. Marsh?" I said. "Dr. Lena Marsh? My name is Sam Okonkwo. I'm a medic. I'm here to help you."

She didn't answer. She pressed herself harder into the corner, and I saw the details now: a woman in her fifties, maybe, in a thermal underlayer gone to rags at the elbows, her hair matted, her lips cracked and white. Her eyes were the worst part. Not wild, exactly. *Wary*. The eyes of an animal that had learned, over nine days, exactly what light meant and had stopped believing any of it was friendly.

"Sam," Rosalind said, "slow approach. Hands where she can see them."

"I know how to do this, Ros."

"I know. I'm saying it anyway, because I need to hear it too."

I moved forward a meter at a time, keeping my lights low and off to the side so they didn't stab at her face. "Dr. Marsh. We found your camp on the ridge. The generators were still running. Your coffee got cold, which frankly as a doctor I have to tell you is a crime against caffeine, and we've been looking for you ever since."

Nothing. Her breath came fast and shallow, fogging faintly in the cold water.

"You've been down here nine days," I went on. "Nine days is a very long time. I've seen people come back from nine days. I've seen people come back from worse. But they all needed the same thing first, and that thing is water and warmth, and I have both, right here, in this bag, and I am going to set it down between us and then I am going to stop talking, which my crew will tell you is a medical miracle in its own right."

I set the bag down. I retreated a meter. And I stopped talking, which was harder than it sounded, because every second of silence I could hear my own pulse in my ears and Priya's breathing on the comm and the small, wet tick of the submersible's hull adjusting to the pressure.

After a long time—minutes, maybe—Dr. Marsh moved. She uncurled one arm, slow as a hermit crab testing the water, and dragged the bag toward her. She got it open. She found the water bottle. And she drank with the desperate, graceless greed of the truly dehydrated, spilling half of it down her chin, and I felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched.

"Okay," Rosalind said softly, to all of us. "Okay. Sam, assessment. Gentle."

* * *

Gentle turned out to be the operative word. Dr. Marsh let me come close enough to examine her the way you'd let a stray dog close enough to sniff your hand—watching, coiled, ready to bolt into a corner that didn't exist. Her vitals were bad in the way I expected: severe dehydration, hypothermia bordering on serious, a left wrist that had been broken and splinted with a torn strap and a length of cable, badly, by her own hand, in the dark. There were pressure sores where she'd lain against the deck. There was a laceration on her scalp, healing ragged, from the breach event itself.

And there was the mind. I shone a penlight in her eyes and she tracked it, but when I asked her the date she stared at me for four full seconds and said, "Which one?"

"The current one."

"The current one." She said it like I'd asked her the current name of God. "They change it. When you're down here. The days don't—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "I counted. I counted by the battery gauge. Every time it dropped a percent, that was a day, roughly, because it was losing about a percent a day to the heater I wasn't using, except I was using it, at night, so it was more like—" Her breath hitched. "I counted. I counted the whole time. I'm not crazy. I want that on the record. I counted."

"You're not crazy," I said, and I meant it, and I made sure my voice carried it. "You counted. That's the sanest thing a person could have done. Dr. Marsh, can you tell me your full name and where you're from?"

"Lena Adeyemi Marsh. University of Cape Town. I'm the— I was the lead geologist on the Kestrel survey." Her voice was gaining a kind of scaffolding as she used it, plank by plank. "And you're not navy."

"No."

"The navy doesn't send medics who apologize to their crew for talking too much. I've met the navy." Something moved behind her eyes, something old and cold. "The navy doesn't send anyone. That's the point."

"Dr. Marsh," Rosalind said, easing into the compartment beside me, "my name is Rosalind Hartwell. We were hired to recover your team's equipment. We didn't know you were here. But now that we are here, we're getting you out. I need you to tell me about the others. Six people came ashore at your camp. Where are the other five?"

Marsh went very still. It was the stillness of prey, and I put a hand lightly on her good arm—*grounding*, we call it, though what I was really doing was telling her body that another human's touch could mean something other than violence—and after a moment she turned to look at Rosalind, and whatever she saw in Rosalind's face must have been enough, because she started to talk.

* * *

What she told us, she told in pieces, in the flat reciting voice of someone who has rehearsed a horror so many times it has worn smooth, like sea glass.

The survey team had found the anomaly on day three. A structure in the trench wall, below the depth their equipment was rated for, emitting power signatures no natural formation produces. They'd reported it. Within thirty-six hours, the navy frigate had arrived—not with questions, she said, but with a *tone*. Two officers came aboard. They were polite. They took copies of everything. And then the team's own support vessel had been ordered away for "resupply," leaving the six of them at the camp with a submersible, a generator, and a growing conviction, in Marsh's case at least, that the resupply was never coming back.

"So we dove," she said. "I know. I know how that sounds. But Okafor—Dr. Okafor, our team lead—said if we came home with nothing, the story would be whatever they said it was. And if we came home with proof—" She laughed, a dry, cracked sound with no humor in it. "We were academics. We thought proof was a shield. Nobody ever told us proof is a target."

The submersible had made two dives. On the second, returning from the structure, something had struck them—or they had struck something; Marsh still wasn't sure, and the not-sure was clearly one of the things that gnawed at her. The hull had breached. The pilot, a quiet man named Ferreira, had gotten them wedged into this canyon before he drowned, or before he died, Marsh couldn't say which and the not-knowing was another thing that gnawed.

"The others got out," she said. "Through the forward hatch. Four of them. They tried to get me—my wrist was broken, I was slow—and then the lights came. From the structure. Not lights like lamps. Lights like—" She searched for it. "Like eyes. Searchlights that *knew where to look*. And there was a vehicle, a small submersible, black, no markings, and men in hard suits, and the others swam toward them because what else do you do, what else do you *do*—" Her voice cracked down the middle. "They thought they were being rescued. They went toward the lights. And the lights took them."

"Took them where?" Rosalind asked, very quietly.

Marsh turned her head. She looked at Rosalind for a long moment, and I watched her decide something, watch the decision move through her face like weather.

"Up the mountain," she whispered. "They took them up the mountain."

"The trench wall," Priya said over the comm, hushed. "The facility."

"There is no facility," Marsh said. "There's what they let people believe, and there's the mountain. And everyone who goes up the mountain doesn't come back down, except me, because I was under a rack with a dead man and a broken wrist and they counted five and left." Her breath shuddered. "They counted five. They counted, and I heard one of them say *all accounted for*, and I lay there under Ferreira with his blood still warm in the water, and I said nothing. Nine days I said nothing. I want that understood too. I'm not brave. I'm the one who stayed under the body."

"Dr. Marsh." Rosalind's voice, when it came, was gentler than I had ever heard it, and I have heard Rosalind Hartwell talk to a frightened green deckhand through his first storm, and to a dying engine at three in the morning, and to a broker who thought triple pay bought him a diver's spine. "I have spent twelve years being the one who stayed hidden. It is not the same thing as cowardice. It is the thing you do when staying alive is the only way anyone's story gets told." A pause. "Your story is about to get told. All of it."

Marsh looked at her. And I swear on my stethoscope, which I do not actually carry anymore but swear on anyway, something in the woman's face reorganized itself—not hope, not yet, hope is a big word and she'd been down here nine days counting battery percentages—but the *possibility* of hope, the way the sky looks an hour before dawn, when it's still dark but no longer committed to it.

* * *

Getting her out was its own operation. She weighed almost nothing, which frightened me more than any symptom—nine days on whatever emergency rations the submersible carried and terror besides, terror burns calories like a furnace. We wrapped her in the thermal blanket from my kit, clipped her to the recovery line, and Mateo brought her up slow, talking to her the whole way in his low, musical voice, telling her about the ship, about the coffee—everyone tells survivors about the coffee, it's a superstition among us, coffee means the surface, coffee means people—and I watched her eyes through the water, watching him, listening, holding on to the sound of a stranger's kindness like a rope.

On the deck of the *Magpie's Fortune*, we got her warm. Priya had the galley heater going full blast and had, in a gesture that told me everything about Priya Nair, made actual coffee, not the instant sachets, the good stuff she hoards like a dragon. Mateo wrapped her in dry clothes that swallowed her. Sam—that is, I—ran the full workup: fluids, warmth, the wrist re-splinted properly, her scalp cleaned, her vitals charted.

And then I sat with her while she drank the coffee, because the body was only half the patient.

"They'll come for us," she said, after a long silence. "The frigate. It was already coming when we dove. It'll have found the camp by now. It'll find you." She looked up at me, and her eyes were clear and terrible. "You have to understand what you've picked up. I'm not a survivor. I'm *evidence*. The moment they know I'm alive, you're not a salvage crew anymore. You're accomplices."

I thought about telling her something reassuring. I'm a medic; reassurance is in the job description, right there between *bedside manner* and *don't faint at the sight of blood*. But she'd spent nine days in the dark being lied to by silence, and I thought she'd earned the truth.

"Dr. Marsh," I said, "our captain signed a contract nobody would explain, to a place that isn't on the charts, for equipment nobody wanted back. Our engineer found your transponder died mid-broadcast with no distress call. Our deckhand hasn't asked a single question since we left port, which for Mateo is either wisdom or a medical condition. And me—" I smiled at her. "I came because when someone's out there, you go. That's the whole of my philosophy. It's not a good philosophy. It's gotten me yelled at by three separate employers. But it means nobody on this ship is going to hand you to a frigate to save their own skins. I'd stake my license on it."

"You don't have a license," she said.

"I'd stake my *future* license on it."

Something happened to her face then—something that on a healthy person I'd have called a laugh. On Lena Marsh, nine days out of the dark, it was more like a tectonic event, small and deep and genuine.

"Sam," Rosalind called from the doorway, and I heard it in her voice before I turned, the particular flat calm she gets when the horizon has gone the wrong color. "You'll want to see this. Priya's got the radar."

I looked at Marsh. She looked back at me, and she didn't have to say it, because we both already knew.

"They're here," she whispered.

On the radar, at the edge of the screen, moving toward us with the patient inevitability of a tide, was a contact the size of a frigate, broadcasting a polite, official, utterly chilling hail: *Salvage vessel, this is HMS Dauntless. Prepare to receive a boarding party.*
