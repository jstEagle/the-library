## 12. Twelve Years of Miles

Sleep didn't come that night. It rarely did after a man like Hale put his boots on your deck and his hand around your throat without touching you.

Rosalind sat in the galley with the logbook locked in the safe behind her and a mug of tea going cold in her hands, and she let the memory come, because it always came eventually, and fighting it was like swimming against an undertow—you only exhausted yourself before it took you anyway.

It started, as these things did, with a name.

Not hers.

* * *

Twelve years ago, her name had been Rosalind Vance.

She'd been twenty-six and invincible, the way all divers are at twenty-six, the way the sea permits no one to remain for long. She was working the North Atlantic rigs then—saturation diving, living weeks at a time in a pressurized can the size of a school bus, breathing heliox that made her laugh sound like a cartoon duck. She'd been good. Not competent-good, not journeyman-good, but the kind of good that made old hands stop chewing their tobacco to watch her work. Her hands were steady at three hundred feet. Her mind stayed quiet when the water went black. Elias Vane used to say she had "cold blood and warm judgment," which was the nicest thing anyone had ever said about her, and she'd carried it like a coin in her pocket for years afterward.

Elias. That was where the story bent.

He'd taught her everything worth knowing—how to read a wreck's bones by the way silt settled on them, how to trust the itch at the back of your neck before your instruments confirmed it, how to drink coffee so bad it built character. He'd pulled her out of a training program that was going to wash her out for insubordination and told the review board, with a perfectly straight face, that what they called insubordination he called initiative. She'd have followed him into a volcano. Which was, in retrospect, roughly what happened.

The job was supposed to be simple. A construction barge off the Hebrides, a collapsed cofferdam, a body recovery—not salvage, not treasure, just the grim arithmetic of bringing two men home to their families. Weather window: narrow. Client: impatient. Elias's plan: aggressive.

"We go down together," he'd said, tapping the chart. "Two sets of hands, half the time. You take the port side, I take the starboard."

"Regulations say solo recovery dives," Rosalind had said, because regulations were one of the few gods she still attended.

"Regulations also say the weather holds for six hours, and it won't." He'd smiled at her, that crooked smile that had gotten him out of more trouble than it had gotten him into. "Rosie. Two families are waiting on us. You want to tell them we waited out a squall?"

She hadn't wanted to tell them anything. She'd wanted to be the diver who brought their people home. So she'd said yes, and that yes had cost more than any word she'd ever spoken since.

The dive went wrong in the way dives go wrong—not all at once but in increments, each one small enough to explain away. The current arrived early. The visibility dropped from ten meters to two to nothing. And somewhere in that gray blindness, working by feel along the port side, Rosalind made a decision.

The structure was unstable. She knew it—the groan in the steel had a pitch she recognized, the sound metal makes when it's done pretending. Protocol said withdraw. But protocol assumed you could see, and she couldn't, and the last section of debris was right there, and the second body was under it, and she could feel him through her gloves, cold and waiting, and she thought: *thirty more seconds.*

Thirty seconds.

The collapse didn't announce itself. It simply happened, the way the sea does things—a shift of weight, a shriek of steel, and then the world turned over and something enormous struck her across the shoulder and drove her down into the dark, and when she clawed back to the surface of consciousness, her air line was snagged, her left arm wouldn't answer, and Elias was calling her name on the comm in a voice she had never heard him use before. Not angry. Not afraid for himself.

Afraid for her.

"I'm here," she'd managed. "I'm pinned. Line's fouled. I need—"

"I'm coming to you."

"No—" Because she could hear it in his breathing, the strain, the depth, the distance he'd already covered against the current to reach her side of the wreck. "Elias, don't cross. Wait for the tender, wait for the—"

"The tender's twelve minutes out. You've got four minutes of air."

And there it was. The whole equation, laid bare in the dark. Four minutes of air, twelve minutes of help, and one man who loved her enough to do the math wrong.

"Elias." She heard her own voice go flat and calm, the way it went underwater, the way it never went on land. "Listen to me. If you cross that gap, we both die. If you stay, I might make it. Stay."

"Rosie—"

"*Stay.*"

Silence on the line. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy: "I'm staying. Hold on, sweetheart. Hold on."

He held the line open while her gauge crawled toward zero. He talked to her—about nothing, about the terrible coffee on the tender, about the review board he was going to lie to again, about how she should take that wreck-survey certification she kept putting off. He talked until her gauge read zero and her vision went silver at the edges, and then he said, very clearly, "I've got you, Rosie," and cut his own umbilical, and swam through the blind collapsing dark to share his air with her.

They made it up together. His air lasted almost exactly long enough.

Almost.

* * *

The inquiry found what inquiries find. An unauthorized buddy dive. A structural assessment ignored. A supervisor who crossed a hazard zone against his own standing order. They were very thorough about Elias's decisions, because Elias was dead and dead men absorb blame the way sand absorbs water.

They were less thorough about hers. Why would they be? She'd told them she'd ordered him to stay. She'd told them he disobeyed. Both true. What she hadn't told anyone—not the board, not his widow, not the priest at the funeral who'd asked if she wanted to talk—was the part that lived in her chest like a splinter worked too deep to grip:

If she hadn't gone for that last section, he would never have crossed.

Her order to stay had been correct. It had also been a lie of timing, because the honest order—the one owed to a man about to die for her—was "*Don't come, because this is my fault.*" Instead she'd let him believe she was a victim of circumstance, let him spend his life believing he was saving her from the sea, when really he was saving her from herself. He'd died thinking he was the rescuer. She'd let him die comfortable in that belief, because the truth would have poisoned his last four minutes, and she'd told herself that was mercy.

Maybe it was. She'd spent twelve years failing to decide.

* * *

What came after was simpler to explain and harder to survive.

She changed her surname. Not a dramatic vanishing—no fake passports, no midnight flights. Just paperwork, a move, a new certification under Hartwell, a career rebuilt one anonymous contract at a time. Salvage, not rescue. Wrecks nobody loved, cargoes nobody claimed. Work where the only lives at stake were her own and everyone else's stayed safely theoretical.

And she started measuring in miles.

It had begun as a joke she told herself. Distance was the one honest unit left. Relationships were measured in risk; reputations in exposure; but miles were clean. Twelve years, four hundred and some-odd thousand nautical miles, and not once had she stayed anywhere long enough for anyone to learn the shape of her guilt. Mateo thought she was allergic to commitment. Priya thought she was a loner by temperament. Sam, who noticed things medics notice, had once said gently, over cards, "You know, most people run *toward*, Roz."

"And look where that gets them," she'd said, and dealt the next hand, and he'd let it go, because Sam understood that some doors you knock on only when invited.

The miles had worked. That was the obscene part. Invisibility wasn't a punishment; it was a functioning system. No roots, no records, no one close enough to lose. She'd traded love for safety at a fair market rate and paid promptly every year since.

Until a navy frigate had appeared on her horizon with Commander Hale aboard, holding out a contract written in the exact currency of her oldest debt.

* * *

Because that was the thing she'd understood the moment Hale smiled at her on her own deck—that courteous, surgical smile—and said, "Ms. Hartwell. Or shall we use the other name? I understand it opens certain doors."

He hadn't been guessing. He'd known. Someone had dug through twelve years of carefully scattered gravel and found the one stone she'd buried deepest, and polished it, and set it in front of her like a jeweler presenting a ring.

Immunity. Erasure. A clean record for a dirty past. All she had to do was hand over the logbook and a half-mad survivor, and walk away from the missing, and let the trench keep its secrets the way she'd kept hers.

That was what made it unbearable—not the threat, but the tailoring. Anyone could threaten her. Only someone who truly understood her could *offer* her this. Hale's people hadn't just researched her file. They'd understood its architecture. They knew the accident was the load-bearing wall of her whole constructed life, and they'd designed their offer to slip into that exact crack and pry.

And the cruelest elegance of it: the offer was even sincere. He'd keep his word. Men like Hale always kept their word; it was the only currency they couldn't print. She could take it. She could be Rosalind Hartwell, pardoned and unremarkable, back on the water within the month, miles accumulating again like sand in an hourglass, and no one would ever know what she'd traded away to get there.

Except her.

* * *

The galley clock read 3:40 a.m. The tea had gone fully cold, which felt appropriate. Rosalind got up, poured it out, and stood at the little porthole watching the black water slide past, and did the math she'd been avoiding.

Elias had died buying her four minutes with his own. Hale was offering to sell her back her entire life for the price of six strangers in a trench and one witness who'd survived nine days alone in a dead submersible by talking to the dark. Lena Marsh. Sam had her sedated and stable now, but Rosalind had heard her, before the sedation, whispering the same phrase over and over: *"Up the mountain. They took them up the mountain."*

Six people. Maybe alive. Held by men who scuttled evidence as a matter of routine.

And her crew—her ridiculous, question-not-asking, card-playing, engine-blessing crew—who had signed on for a simple salvage and were now anchored above a weapons facility with a warship on the horizon. Whatever she decided, they'd inherit it. That was the tax on command that nobody mentioned in the brochures.

She thought about what Elias would say. Not the crooked-smile Elias of the chart table, but the Elias of the last four minutes, the one who'd talked to her about coffee and certifications while his gauge ran dry, keeping her calm with ordinary words because ordinary words were ballast. He'd say something infuriatingly gentle. He'd say, *Rosie, you can't buy back your soul twice. First purchase cost me everything. Second one costs them.*

She pressed her forehead against the cold glass of the porthole.

Here was the truth she'd sailed four hundred thousand miles to avoid: invisibility had never been penance. Penance would have been staying—staying near his widow, staying in the trade where lives hung in balance, spending her skill on other people's four-minute emergencies. Running wasn't atonement. Running was just running, dressed in work clothes. She'd called it measuring her life in miles, but a mile is just a mile. It doesn't redeem anything. It doesn't weigh anything. It's the unit of cowards, because it's the only measurement that always comes out the same no matter what you're fleeing.

Hale thought he'd found her price. He'd found her wound, yes—but wounds aren't prices. Prices are what you'll accept. And sitting there in the dark with the logbook humming faintly behind the safe door like something alive, Rosalind discovered that her price had changed without her noticing. Somewhere between a cold cup of coffee in an abandoned survey camp and a woman whispering *up the mountain,* the exchange rate had shifted.

She couldn't bring Elias back. Twelve years of miles had proven that beyond all argument. But she could refuse to spend his last four minutes a second time—could refuse to let his death become, retroactively, the down payment on her silence. He'd bought her life. What she did with it was the only eulogy he'd ever get.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the porthole, to the water, to a dead man who'd once lied beautifully to a review board on her behalf. "Okay, Elias."

Then she straightened up, wiped her face with the heel of her hand—once, efficiently, the way she did everything—and went to wake her crew.

They gathered in the galley at 4 a.m., rumpled and wary, Mateo clutching his lucky cap, Priya already suspicious, Sam alert in the way of a man who'd slept with one ear open. Rosalind put the kettle on first, because whatever she was about to ask of them, they deserved decent coffee while hearing it.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said. "About a different name, a collapsed cofferdam, and the worst decision I ever made. And then I'm going to tell you what Hale offered me, and what I intend to do about it. And then—" she set down four mugs, her hands steadier than they had any right to be— "you're going to vote. Because this stopped being my ship the moment you all refused to leave it."

Mateo looked at Priya. Priya looked at Sam. Sam nodded slowly, the way men nod at the start of things they know will hurt.

"Tell us the story," Priya said.

So Rosalind Hartwell—born Vance, widowed of her own history, four hundred thousand miles from where she started—took a breath, and for the first time in twelve years, stopped running mid-stride.
