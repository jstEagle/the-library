## 13. Mutiny of Conscience

Priya Nair had rebuilt engines in war zones, monsoons, and one memorable week in a dry dock in Colombo where the entire harbor had lost power and she'd fixed the generator by candlelight using a hairpin and spite. She was not, by any reasonable measure, an alarmist.

So when she found herself standing in the galley of the *Kestrel's Grace* at two in the morning, unable to sleep because her hands kept rehearsing the motion of packing her tools, she paid attention.

The offer sat on the table between them like something washed up dead. Hale's terms, transcribed by Rosalind in her flat, careful handwriting: surrender the logbook and Dr. Marsh, receive payment, immunity, and—this was the part that made Priya's teeth ache—the complete erasure of Rosalind Hartwell's past. A new name, clean records, a life with no twelve-year trail behind it.

"It's generous," Priya said to the empty galley. "That's what scares me."

Generous offers from men who commanded frigates were not generosity. They were invoices. And somewhere in the fine print of this one, Priya was fairly certain, was a line item for four other people who'd seen too much and would eventually need to be balanced out.

She put the kettle on. In her experience, no crisis worth having could survive contact with proper tea.

*

By morning, everyone had read the transcription. By noon, nobody had said a word about it, which aboard a forty-meter salvage vessel was roughly equivalent to a parliamentary shouting match.

It was Mateo who finally broke, as he always did, because Mateo Cruz had never met a silence he couldn't improve.

"Okay." He set down his cards mid-game—he'd been losing to Sam anyway, which Priya suspected was deliberate on Sam's part, a kindness disguised as card play. "We're doing this. The thing where we all pretend we're not thinking about it until somebody explodes."

"I'm not going to explode," said Sam, laying down a pair of sevens without looking up. "I'm a medical professional. I contain my explosions."

"You alphabetized the med bay twice yesterday," Mateo said. "The second time backwards."

"That's called thoroughness."

Priya watched Rosalind at the chart table, ostensibly correcting their position but actually staring at the same square centimeter of paper she'd been staring at since dawn. The captain had that quality Priya both admired and distrusted: the ability to look calm while internally running calculations that would frighten a supercomputer.

"We should vote," Mateo said.

Rosalind's pen stopped. "On what?"

"On *that*." He jabbed a finger at the transcription. "Hale's offer. Whether we take it. Whether we run. Whether we—" His voice caught on the last option, and Priya saw him swallow it whole. "Whether we go down there after them."

The cabin went quiet enough that Priya could hear the generator's rhythm change as the compressor cycled. She'd have to look at that later. Third time this week.

"There's nothing to vote on," Rosalind said quietly. "I'm the captain. I decide."

"No," said Mateo—and here was the thing about Mateo, the thing Priya had clocked in the first week and loved him for ever since: he was built entirely of warmth and stubbornness in equal parts, and when the stubbornness came up, the warmth didn't leave. It just got more determined. "No, Captain. You told us in the briefing we could walk away. Nobody walked. That means we're crew, and crew gets a say when the ship's headed somewhere with a frigate parked over it."

"He's right," Priya heard herself say.

Everyone turned. She hadn't exactly planned to speak; her mouth had simply gone ahead of her, the way it did around engines and injustice.

"Priya," Rosalind said, a warning note in it.

"Don't 'Priya' me. I've been thinking about this since two in the morning, and I've done the arithmetic, so let me do it out loud before Mateo makes it a speech." She held up one hand and began counting fingers. "One: Hale knows our names, our faces, and our position. If we take his deal, we're witnesses who took money, which is a category of witness that historically does not enjoy long retirements. Two: if we refuse and run, we're witnesses who refused money, which is worse. Three: if we dive, we're witnesses who became participants, and there is no version of the paperwork that makes us safe after that." She folded her hand closed. "I'm telling you the math says survival means taking the deal or running now, tonight, while the frigate's fueling or whatever frigates do."

"Frigates refuel," said Sam mildly.

"Thank you. My point stands."

Mateo was shaking his head before she finished. "You're counting the wrong things."

"Oh, here we go."

"No, listen." He stood up, and Priya noticed his hands were trembling slightly, and noticed also that he wasn't trying to hide it. "There are six people down in that trench. Maybe alive, maybe not—I know. But Lena Marsh spent nine days alone in a broken submersible listening to the dark, and she's up in the sickbay right now because we went and got her. We *went and got her*, Priya. That's the only reason we know anything. And you want to do the math on whether the next five deserve the same trip?"

"The math isn't about whether they deserve it. Of course they deserve it. The math is about whether we survive long enough to help anyone else, ever again." Priya heard her own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "I grew up in a country where people disappeared for knowing things. Whole families. You don't beat that kind of power by being brave in the wrong order. You beat it by being alive and smart and *patient*—"

"And how's patience worked out for you?" Mateo asked, not cruelly, which somehow made it land harder. "Twelve years, Rosalind. How many miles?"

Rosalind didn't answer. The silence answered for her.

Sam cleared his throat. All three of them turned to him, because Sam Okonkwo occupied a strange gravitational position on the ship: the youngest-seeming and oldest-seeming person aboard simultaneously, a man whose opinion arrived rarely and landed heavily.

"For the record," Sam said, "I'm not voting on strategy. I'm voting on medicine. Dr. Marsh has a concussion, dehydration, hypothermia sequelae, and a psychological injury I haven't fully assessed. She is my patient. There are five more people down there who may be injured, may be held, may be—" He paused, chose his words with visible care. "May be in conditions I can't treat from up here. I don't abandon patients. It's the one rule I have that I've never once been tempted to break." He looked at Priya, and his voice gentled. "I understand your math. I do. But some things aren't variables."

"They're all variables," Priya said, "until you're dead."

"Then I'll be a variable with a clear conscience."

Despite everything—a frigate on the horizon, a trench full of ghosts, an offer of erasure sitting on the table like a loaded gun—Priya felt her mouth twitch. "That's the stupidest heroic thing anyone's said on this boat, and Mateo once told a customs officer his cargo was 'personal items' while standing next to a crane full of mining equipment."

"It was personal," Mateo protested. "To me."

And then, absurdly, they were almost laughing, the three of them, the way people laugh in hospitals and lifeboats—not because anything is funny but because the alternative is screaming, and laughter at least keeps your hands free.

Rosalind still hadn't spoken.

*

They gave her the afternoon. Nobody decided to give it to her; it just happened, the way weather happens. Mateo went topside and coiled lines that were already coiled. Sam sat with Lena Marsh, whose whispers had started forming sentences again that morning. Priya shut herself in the engine room with the compressor that had been cycling wrong, because there was comfort in problems with actual solutions, in machines that only wanted to be understood rather than survived.

She was elbow-deep in the intake manifold when Rosalind found her.

The captain didn't announce herself. She just appeared at the edge of the work light, oil-stained rag in hand, holding it out. Priya took it and wiped her forearms and waited.

"You think I should take the deal," Rosalind said.

"I think you should take the deal, burn the logbook copy, drop Marsh at a hospital in a neutral port, and disappear so thoroughly that even I forget your face." Priya met her eyes. "And I think you're not going to do any of that, because I've watched you for six weeks, and I know what you do with exits. You stand near them. You never use them."

A ghost of a smile crossed Rosalind's face and vanished. "Is that an observation or a diagnosis?"

"It's a maintenance report. You're a machine that runs on guilt, Captain, and guilt doesn't have an off switch. Believe me, I've checked."

Rosalind leaned against the bulkhead, and for a moment the captain-shaped armor slipped, and underneath was just a tired woman in her forties who had been carrying something heavy for longer than Priya had been fixing engines. "When I was twenty-six," Rosalind said, "I made a choice to save myself. One choice. People died because of it. I've been paying it off ever since, mile by mile, and I thought—that was the debt. That was the whole balance. Then Hale puts a number on it and says it's forgivable, and I realized—" Her jaw tightened. "He's not offering forgiveness. He's offering to sell me back my own soul at a discount, and then own the receipt."

"What are you asking me, Rosalind?"

"I'm asking what the vote looks like. Honestly. Not what you think I want to hear."

Priya considered lying. She genuinely did. But there was something about a woman who'd just admitted her soul was mortgaged that made lying feel like kicking someone on the ground.

"Mateo's a yes for the dive. Sam's a yes. Marsh will say yes to anything that gets her people back, but she shouldn't count—she's not fit. I'm a no." She held up her hands, palms out. "Two yeses, one no, one abstention, one captain. Which means, mathematically, it comes down to you. Again. Same as everything on this boat."

"That's not a democracy."

"No. It's worse. It's a family." Priya tossed the rag into the bin. "Families don't vote. They argue until somebody breaks, and then they live with whoever breaks first."

*

They gathered in the galley at dusk, all five of them, because Marsh insisted on being carried up from sickbay in the deck chair and nobody had the heart—or frankly the strength—to stop her. She looked terrible and fierce, like a candle burning at both ends out of pure spite, and she wrapped a blanket around her shoulders and said, "Well? Someone say something dramatic. I've been rehearsing my objections all day and I hate to waste them."

Rosalind laid the transcription on the table where everyone could see it.

"One last time," she said. "Hale's offer. Full immunity, real money, clean records—for me, and I believe he'd extend it, in his way, to anyone who helps deliver the logbook and Dr. Marsh. Against that: a dive to a facility we've never mapped, guarded by a navy that's already fired on civilian ships this week, to rescue five people who may already be dead, carrying evidence that powerful men have killed to protect." She looked around the table. "I'm not ordering anything. Mateo's right—we're past orders. So we settle it here, and whatever we settle, we settle together, and nobody carries it alone afterward. Say your piece."

Mateo went first, because of course he did. "Five people," he said simply. "Alive or dead, they get brought home or we die finding out why not. That's it. That's my piece."

Sam went second. "My patient needs me below. I go where my patient needs me."

Marsh raised one skeletal hand. "Objection: I'm coming with you, and none of you can stop me, because I'm the only one who's seen the facility's interior layout, and also I bite."

"Noted," said Rosalind, with the faintest warmth. "Priya."

Priya took a breath. She'd prepared arguments on the way up—three of them, well-structured, each with supporting data. What came out instead was quieter than any of them.

"When I was nineteen, my uncle was taken from his shop for printing pamphlets. Just pamphlets. Jokes, mostly, about a minister. They never returned him, and my aunt spent thirty years setting a place for him at every meal." She looked down at her hands, engine-stained, steady. "I came out to sea because the sea doesn't disappear people. It kills you honestly, in front of God and everyone, and leaves wreckage you can hold. And now I'm sitting over a trench where the government does what governments do, and every instinct I have is screaming *run*. So my vote is no. My vote is run tonight, scatter, survive, and find another way another day." She lifted her chin. "But I want it on record that if the captain says dive, I will have the submersible's systems ready in four hours, because I am a professional, and because—" her voice cracked, just barely, and she hated it, "—because my aunt set that table for thirty years, and I never once helped her carry the plates. I'm tired of watching. Whatever we choose, I'm done watching."

The galley was silent. Somewhere above, the wind shifted and the rigging sang its low note.

"All votes but one," Rosalind said softly. "Mine."

She stood. She picked up Hale's transcription, and Priya watched her fold it once, twice, precisely, and then—without ceremony, without drama—she tore it in half, and in half again, and dropped the pieces into the galley's little stove, where the paper curled and flared and was gone.

"Here's my vote," Rosalind said. "Twelve years ago I chose myself, and it cost me everything I've been running from since. I'm not making that trade twice. We dive. Tonight. Four hours, Priya."

Priya was already standing. "Three and a half. I've been prepping since lunch."

Mateo let out a breath that sounded like it had been held for days, and Sam smiled his slow, unshakeable smile, and Marsh said, "Finally. Do you know how boring convalescence is?"—and just like that it was done. No one had won. No one had lost. They had argued themselves into a single shape, the way pressure argues carbon into diamond, and Priya understood suddenly that this—this ragged, reluctant, laughing agreement—was worth more than any immunity Hale could print.

She was halfway to the engine room ladder when Rosalind caught her arm.

"Priya. Your vote was no."

"My vote was no," Priya agreed. "And my hands are yours. That's how families work."

Rosalind held her gaze a moment longer, and something passed between them that needed no paperwork—gratitude, maybe, or recognition, diver to diver in deep water.

Then the captain turned to the rest of them, and the tired woman was gone, and the captain stood in her place, lit by the stove's dying embers.

"Get your gear," Rosalind Hartwell said. "In four hours we go down the mountain."

Above them, through the porthole, the lights of the frigate hung on the horizon like a patient eye—and beneath the keel, twelve thousand feet down, something in the dark switched on a light of its own.
