## 14. Into the Facility

The maintenance lock was exactly where Lena Marsh said it would be, which was the first genuinely good news in nine days, and I intended to enjoy it for at least four seconds.

"There," Mateo said, his voice tinny through the comm. His helmet lamp swept across the trench wall and caught it: a recessed hatch, oval, furred with two decades of marine growth but unmistakably man-made. "Like a door in a hillside. A very deep, very wet, very illegal hillside."

"Nobody's calling it illegal until we're inside," Priya said. "Legally speaking, right now we're just trespassing."

"Legally speaking," Sam said, "right now we're dead. We're past the depth where the navy admits people exist. Whatever happens next is between us and the fish."

I let them talk. It was how they kept their hands steady, and God knew mine needed the help. The *Vela's* dive bell hung at the end of its umbilical like a lantern on a string, swaying gently in a current that had no business being this gentle this far down. Above us: three thousand meters of black water, a frigate named *Corvus* sitting on it like a lid on a pot, and Commander Hale presumably watching our transponder do something dishonest that Priya had spent six hours teaching it to do.

Below us: the door.

"Pressure's holding," Priya reported. "Bell's happy. I'm not, but the bell is."

"Bell's smarter than you," Mateo said.

"Bell doesn't have to live with your jokes."

I keyed my mic. "Talk less, breathe more. Lena said the lock cycles on an old failsafe—manual wheel from outside, pressure equalizes automatically. She used it when she got out." I paused. "When she was let out," I corrected myself, because that was closer to the truth. Nobody escapes a place like this. You're released, or you're discarded.

The wheel was crusted but turned. Of course it did. Hale wanted this facility to be enterable by exactly one kind of person—the kind he could control—and a salvage diver with a grudge apparently qualified.

The lock swallowed us in stages. First me, then Mateo, then Sam, while Priya held the bell at station with the survey team's submersible tethered alongside like a loyal dog tied outside a shop. The inner chamber filled around us with groans and sighs of equalizing pressure, water draining through grates beneath our boots, and then—a sound I will hear in my dreams for the rest of my life—the hiss of the inner door unsealing into air.

Air. At this depth. That meant the facility wasn't just built here. It was *defended* here, engineered against every law of the ocean, and every rivet of that engineering had been paid for out of budgets no committee had ever voted on.

"Lights," I said softly, and three beams cut the dark.

We stood in a maintenance gallery, low-ceilinged, ribbed with pipes that hummed faintly overhead. The air tasted recycled and mineral-sweet, like drinking water left too long in a canteen. Condensation beaded on everything. Somewhere deeper in, machinery breathed in long slow rhythms.

"Okay," Mateo whispered. "Okay okay okay. This is either amazing or the last mistake I ever make, and honestly the suspense is killing me faster than the pressure would."

"That's the spirit," Sam murmured. "Keep talking. If you stop, I'll worry."

"I never stop. Ask anyone. Ask the sea itself."

* * *

We moved the way Lena had described the layout, her words scratched into the margins of the logbook in handwriting that had started neat and ended jagged: *Maintenance gallery south. Junction. Dry corridor to hab ring. They kept us in the observation module. Don't take the central shaft—it has cameras.*

Cameras. I'd dressed for them, in a way. We all wore the survey team's own gear, recovered from their camp topside and from the wedged submersible—coveralls with the program's insignia stripped off, the generic gray of government contract work. To any lens down here, we might read as crew. Might. For about ninety seconds, if nobody looked closely.

The junction opened into a corridor that stopped me cold, and I don't stop cold easily. Twelve years of wrecks have taught me that human spaces underwater fall into two kinds: drowned ones, where the water came in and took everything, and preserved ones, where the water was kept out and the people left in a hurry. This was neither. This was *lived in*. Boots by a bulkhead, lined up neatly. A mug on a rail, half-full of something long since evaporated to a dark ring. A wall calendar, still turned to a month two years gone, with someone's birthday circled in red pen and annotated—"R. turns 40, bring the good coffee"—in handwriting that made my chest ache for a stranger I'd never meet.

"They live here," Sam said quietly. He touched the calendar without taking his eyes off it. "Not staffed. Inhabited. There are kids' drawings, Rosalind. Look."

There were. Crayon on butcher paper, taped above what must have been a family bunkroom door: a lopsided submarine, a sun with sunglasses, three stick figures holding hands. One of them was labeled *DADDY* in letters that got prouder as they went.

"So it's not just a weapons site," Mateo said. His usual brightness had gone thin. "It's a town. A secret town at the bottom of the sea, and somebody's kid thinks this is normal."

"Somebody's kid thinks the sun wears sunglasses," I said. "Kids believe what they're shown. That's rather the point of all this, isn't it? Show people nothing, and they'll believe there's nothing."

We found the first cache of survey gear forty meters along, stuffed behind a pipe junction as if hidden in haste: sample cases stenciled with the government survey crest, a geologist's hammer, a hard hat with a name taped inside the brim—*OKAFOR, D.* Dr. Daniel Okafor. Lena had spoken his name like a prayer. *Danny kept everyone calm. Danny counted days out loud so the rest of us didn't have to.*

And beside the gear, scratched into the pipe insulation with something sharp, in letters small enough to be missed by anyone not crawling along at eye level:

**DAY 6 — STILL ALIVE — 7 OF US — D.O.**

Seven. Lena had been the eighth, the one they'd put in the submersible—or the one who'd escaped into it, the truth probably lived somewhere crueler between those two sentences. Seven still alive, six days ago. The scratch marks continued, shallower, further along:

**DAY 8**
**DAY 9 — TAKEN FOR QUESTIONING — BACK AFTER**

Back after. My stomach did something complicated. Taken for questioning and brought back meant they were worth keeping alive, which meant they were witnesses, which meant they were leverage, which meant—as long as Hale thought the evidence walked and talked—they were breathing. It was a horrible arithmetic and I clung to it anyway. Hope is a calculation too. You run it with whatever numbers you have.

"He's counting," Sam said. His voice had gone rough. "Nine days alone in a flooded submersible, and Okafor's still counting for his people. Marking the walls so whoever came looking would know they lasted."

"We came looking," Mateo said.

"We came looking," I agreed, and heard something in my own voice I hadn't heard in twelve years. Not the flat competence I wore like a diving suit. Something underneath it, older, with teeth.

* * *

The hab ring was where awe curdled.

I want to be fair, even now, even after everything. What they built down here was magnificent. The corridor widened into a vaulted gallery with a viewing window the size of a chapel wall, looking out onto the trench through glass thick as a man's torso, and beyond the glass the abyssal plain stretched pale and endless under our floodlights, and I understood—instantly, unwillingly—why someone had drawn a sun with sunglasses on the other side of that window. From inside, the deep didn't look like a grave. It looked like a frontier. It looked like the most beautiful thing humanity had ever stolen.

Because that's what it was. Stolen. Every meter of it.

The gallery ran past laboratories behind glass—dry labs, wet labs, a machine hall where something vast and segmented sat in a cradle of scaffolding, partially assembled, its purpose legible in its shape the way a gun's purpose is legible in its shape. Long. Cylindrical. Made to travel fast through water and deliver something terrible to somewhere worse. Beside it, a test-results board still bore grease-pencil figures, ranges and depths and casualty projections written in a cheerful administrative hand, as though the numbers were quarterly earnings instead of bodies.

Mateo stood in front of the machine for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped all its music. "My cousin was on a ferry off Mindanao. Eleven years ago. Official cause: hull failure, unexplained." He didn't look at me. "You think this thing has sisters?"

"I think," I said, "that the logbook names the men who signed for it, and the archive room holds the receipts, and in about an hour the whole world gets to read both." I put my hand on his shoulder. "Your cousin's ferry gets its real answer. That's not nothing. That's the whole reason we're standing in this ridiculous aquarium."

"The whole reason," Sam echoed, "plus the seven people scratching messages on pipes."

"Plus them," I said. "Always plus them."

It was Sam who spotted the sign of life—not dramatic, nothing hammered home in a thriller. Just a chalkboard mounted beside the galley hatch, wiped mostly clean, with one line left in the corner in a tired hand:

**QUIET HOURS 2100–0500 — PLEASE RESPECT THE NIGHT SHIFT**

And beneath it, fresher, in different chalk, smudged but recent—days, not months:

**DR. M — IF YOU CAN READ THIS, WE ARE IN MODULE C. THEY MOVE US AT NIGHT. LEAVE A MARK SO WE KNOW SOMEONE SAW.**

Lena Marsh had told us she'd been held in Module C before they put her in the submersible. She'd told us the others were taken "up the mountain"—her word for the facility, seen from below through the viewport, lit against the dark like a lit city on a hill. She'd been right about everything else. The lock, the layout, the cameras. Why not this?

I dug a grease pencil from my chest pocket—the same kind the engineers used on their test boards, invisible in its ordinariness—and wrote beneath the message, in block letters steady enough to pass for staff:

**SEEN. FRIENDS OF DR. M. COMING. STAY ALIVE.**

Then, because I am who I am, because twelve years of habit die hard, I added a small mark in the corner. A single slash, the tally-mark of a woman who once measured her whole life in miles. One more mile. Maybe the last one I'd ever count.

"Module C," Mateo whispered. "Which way?"

"North spine, past the machine hall," I said. "Lena's map says there's a service corridor behind the labs. And it says—" I checked the page again, though I knew it by heart, "—it says the corridor runs *under* the observation windows. Which means we're going to walk past that beautiful view with nowhere to hide."

"Story of my life," Mateo said, and some of his music came back, and I loved him a little for it.

We were fifty meters into the service corridor—shoulder-width, lit at long intervals by amber emergency strips, the hum of the facility's lungs pressing in around us—when Sam, walking drag, made a small sound. Not a word. A sound, like a man finding a word stuck in his throat.

Ahead of us, around the next bend, light spilled across the deck plates. Warm light. Lamplight. And with it, faint but unmistakable, carried down the corridor on the facility's circulating air:

Singing. Low, ragged, half-remembered—some old hymn or work song, sung by more than one voice, badly, beautifully, the way people sing when they're keeping each other alive.

Seven voices. Or fewer. It didn't matter. They were alive, they were close, and they were singing in the dark like they believed someone might hear.

I killed my lamp, raised my fist, and felt my heart go loud enough to give us away all by itself.

Then, from somewhere behind us—back toward the galley, back toward the chalkboard, back toward the mark I'd left only minutes ago—came the heavy, unhurried clang of a hatch opening, and boots on metal, and a voice amplified flat and official through the facility's intercom:

"All personnel, Module C movement in ten minutes. Escort teams to stations."

They moved the prisoners at night. And night, down here, was whenever they said it was.
