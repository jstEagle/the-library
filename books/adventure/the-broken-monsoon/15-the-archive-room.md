## 15. The Archive Room

The first thing I did when I could finally hold a cup without my hands shaking was apologize to the coffee.

It had gone cold in the galley of the *Meridian Grace* while Sam Okonkwo sat across from me, patient as a tide, watching me drink it. Nine days in a breached submersible at the edge of a trench will teach you things about coffee. It teaches you that warmth is a language, and that you have been speaking it to no one for a very long time.

"Take your time," Sam said.

"I've had nine days of time," I said. "I'd like to spend the next hour being useful instead."

That got a small smile out of him. He had that way — a medic's economy of kindness, nothing wasted, everything exactly where it needed to be.

Rosalind Hartwell came into the galley then, and I watched her the way I'd been watching her since they pulled me out of that crushed tin can: carefully, the way you watch weather. She moved like someone who had practiced not being noticed until the practice became her skeleton. But there was nothing forgettable about her eyes. They were the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"You said you could find the archive," she said. No preamble. I liked that about her too. The sea had scoured the politeness out of both of us.

"I can." I set down the cup. My hands were steady enough now, mostly. "But you should understand what you're asking me to walk back into."

"I'm asking you to point," Rosalind said. "We'll do the walking."

"That's what I told my team," I said, and the galley went quiet around us. Mateo Cruz, who had been pretending to fix something that didn't need fixing by the door, stopped pretending. Priya Nair looked up from her tablet. Even the ship seemed to lean in, the hull ticking softly against the swell.

I hadn't told them yet. Not all of it. I'd given Sam the shape of it — six people went down the mountain, one came back up — but shapes aren't the same as names.

"Six," I said. "Okafor. Lindqvist. Reyes. Tanaka. Bright. And Devi Anand, who was twenty-six years old and kept a photograph of her mother taped inside her dive helmet because she said the trench was the only place dark enough that she couldn't see how much she missed her." I made myself keep going. "They were taken up the mountain. To the facility. I heard the doors. I heard the boots on the deck plates. And then I spent nine days listening to my own oxygen scrubber tick, wondering if anyone would ever say their names again."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Mateo, bless him, said quietly: "We're saying them now."

And that — that was the moment I understood these people were going to be all right. Not safe. All right is different from safe. Safe is what Hale's people promised each other every morning in that concrete cathedral under the sea, and look where it got them.

* * *

The descent took four hours, which gave me four hours to remember how to be Dr. Lena Marsh again instead of the thing I'd been in that submersible — a woman who talked to a dead radio and rationed her own sanity in twelve-hour shifts.

I rode in the survey submersible's sister craft, the one Priya had coaxed back to life with what she described as "aggressive optimism and two bypassed safety interlocks." Rosalind piloted. Sam rode with us, because he refused to let me out of arm's reach, and because — though he'd never say it this way — he knew that a witness returning to the scene of her survival needs a witness of her own.

"Talk me through it," Rosalind said as the light died above us, layer by layer, blue to black.

"The facility sits on a shelf at three thousand meters, tucked against the canyon wall. Officially it doesn't exist. Unofficially it has a name nobody uses out loud — Meridian Station, which I always found funny, because your ship is called—"

"I know," Rosalind said. Something flickered across her face. "Somebody named it after the place. Or the place after somebody."

"Either way, it means whoever built it wanted it found eventually. People who truly want to hide don't leave nameplates." I traced the route on the chart between us. "Maintenance lock on the eastern face, service tunnel C. That's how we got in the first time. We were supposed to be mapping a geological anomaly. Instead we mapped a weapons program."

"And the archive?"

"Level two. Behind the server hall. There's a cold room — literally cold, climate-controlled, the only warm-blooded thing in that whole facility is the ambition." I heard Sam huff a laugh beside me. Good. Someone had to. "Test results. Deployment records. Casualty lists. Everything indexed by a man named Okafor who thought he was cataloguing oceanographic data right up until the day he read his own index."

The sonar painted the canyon wall as we dropped, and there it was — the mountain, rising out of the dark like something the sea had decided to keep. My chest tightened. Nine days of silence lived in that wall. But so did Devi's photograph, and Okafor's index cards, and the truth, sitting in its cold room like a patient thing, waiting for someone brave or foolish enough to come collect it.

Brave or foolish. On this boat, I was learning, the categories overlapped almost completely.

* * *

The maintenance lock opened the way it had opened for us eleven days ago — too easily, with the soft hydraulic sigh of a door that had never once expected to be challenged. That ease frightened me more than any alarm would have. Alarms mean someone cares whether you come in.

Inside, the facility breathed. Pumps. Ventilation. The hum of a place that considered itself permanent.

We moved through service tunnel C with Rosalind in the lead, and I navigated from memory, calling turns before we reached them: "Left at the junction. Stairwell past the coolant lines. Don't touch the pipes — they run hot enough to ruin a glove and the hand inside it."

"How do you know all this after one visit?" Mateo asked over the comm. He'd insisted on coming despite his ribs, which Rosalind had strapped and he had lied about.

"Because I spent four days memorizing it," I said. "Right up until the night they came for us. Fear is an excellent study aid."

The corridors held our ghosts. In a supply alcove off tunnel C, I found Lindqvist's sample case, standing open, his calipers still laid out in their foam bed like he'd be back in five minutes. Further along, Tanaka's jacket hung on a hook by the airlock, her credentials still clipped to the pocket. Nobody had even taken the badge. Either they were arrogant, or they intended for the badges to stay here with everything else that officially didn't exist.

Rosalind stopped at Tanaka's jacket. She touched the credential clip once, lightly, the way you'd touch a headstone.

"They're alive," she said. It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because we both needed to hear it said aloud.

"There are signs," I said. "Fresh food packaging in the mess. Laundry cycles logged in the last week. And the holding wing on level three has beds made up for eight people. Six of ours, plus two they brought in later. Whoever runs this place wants witnesses kept breathing. Witnesses are leverage. Dead ones are just martyrs with better press."

"Then we get them out before the leverage expires," Rosalind said, and there was something in her voice I recognized from the mirror, from years ago, from the person I'd been before the trench taught me humility. Fury with a plan attached. The most dangerous and most useful emotion a human being can carry.

* * *

The archive room announced itself the way archives always do — by being aggressively boring. A gray door. A keypad. A placard reading DATA RETENTION — LEVEL 2 in the same font as every other sign in the building, as if genocide and inventory shared a stationery budget.

Devi would have laughed at that. I let myself laugh at it, briefly, and Sam put a hand on my shoulder and let me.

"Code?" Rosalind asked.

"Okafor's birthday," I said. "He changed it from the default the first day and told all of us, because he trusted everyone on that team with his life and turned out to be half right." I keyed it in. The lock clicked with a sound so ordinary it felt obscene.

The cold room opened onto rows of server racks and, mercifully, physical media — because whoever built Meridian Station trusted machines more than networks, and networks can be scrubbed remotely, but a shelf of hardened drives and printed indices requires a human being with a shredder and a conscience.

Priya had come down with us after all — Rosalind had overruled her own caution, saying if anyone could copy a data center before anyone noticed, it was her. Priya took one look at the racks and made a sound I can only describe as reverent greed.

"Oh, this is beautiful," she whispered. "Unencrypted local storage. Redundant physical backups. They built a fortress for the evidence against themselves. Do you know how often criminals do my job for me?"

"Every time," Rosalind said, "if you believe in justice. Which I'm starting to, again, against my better judgment."

They worked. I watched them work, and I read.

That was my job, in the end. Rosalind copied; I verified. Drive by drive, folder by folder, I walked back through everything my team had died finding, and I made myself read every line, because a witness who flinches isn't a witness, she's just a survivor.

Project CHARYBDIS. Test detonations in water columns designated uninhabited, which was a lie so tidy it deserved a frame — the columns weren't uninhabited, they were unmonitored, which is a different word for the same fishing villages. Depth-charge variants tuned for pressure hulls at three thousand meters. Deployment schedules. Procurement chains that climbed upward through defense contractors and naval offices and finally, in a memo dated fourteen months ago, to a signature block I had seen before, on funding approvals, on the order that sent my team into the trench:

Commander R. Hale, Office of Special Programs.

"He signed it," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "He signed the deployment order. He shook my hand at the dock in Darwin and told me to bring back good data. He meant this data. He meant for us to find it and for him to decide what happened next."

Rosalind came to stand beside me. She didn't take the printout from my hands. She just read it alongside me, close enough that I could hear her breathing change.

Then I found the casualty lists.

Not projections. Actuals. Test series 4 through 11, with columns for yield, depth, and — in Okafor's careful, horrified handwriting, added after the fact in red — confirmed civilian proximity. A trawler, the *Anak Perkasa*, listed as "environmental loss" with nine souls aboard. A research platform. Two coastal settlements whose water tables showed contamination the official reports attributed to "natural seismic activity."

Natural seismic activity. As if the earth had done this to itself. As if the ocean simply woke up one morning angry.

I had imagined, in nine days of darkness, what I would feel if I ever saw proof. I had imagined vindication, maybe, or grief. What I felt instead was colder and hotter than either: clarity. Every name on those lists stopped being an abstraction and became a specific person who had woken up on a specific morning and gone out onto a specific sea, trusting it, the way my team had trusted the mountain, the way I had trusted Commander Hale's handshake.

"This goes further than the navy," Rosalind said quietly. "Contractors. Civilian oversight committees. Names I recognize from congressional testimony."

"It always goes further," I said. "That's how it survives. Everyone upstream assumes someone downstream will take the fall. Nobody volunteers to be the bottom of the river." I looked at her. "Except you people. You seem to have volunteered."

"We're not bottom of anything," Rosalind said. "We're the flood."

Sam, copying the medical test results with hands that had gone very still, said softly: "Series nine. They tested on their own divers. Their own people." He set the page down like it might bite. "Lena, some of these casualty entries — they match the missing persons files I cross-referenced before we left port. Sailors reported lost in exercises. Divers written off as equipment failures."

"Equipment failures," Priya repeated, from behind a rack, her voice shaking with something that wasn't fear anymore. "They wrote human beings into the maintenance logs."

"Yes," I said. "Now you understand why I stopped sleeping."

* * *

It took ninety minutes to copy everything worth having. Priya worked through three drives and a portable array, muttering checksums like prayers, while Rosalind photographed the printed indices page by page — redundancy upon redundancy, because she planned like someone who had already survived losing everything once.

Near the end, I found one more thing. A single folder, misfiled, in Okafor's own indexing system. Labeled PERSONAL — DO NOT ARCHIVE.

Inside was a letter, unsent, addressed to his daughter. And beneath it, a handwritten note in his cramped engineer's script:

*Lena — if you're reading this, I didn't make it back up the mountain. Take the red-index pages first. They're the ones with faces. Everything else is money and machinery; the red pages are why it matters. Tell my girl the ocean isn't cruel. It's just honest. Cruelty required paperwork.*

I stood there in the cold room, holding a dead man's handwriting, and I did not cry, because I had used up nine days of crying in a tin can, and what was left in me now was load-bearing.

"Lena?" Rosalind said gently.

"He left me instructions," I said. "Red pages first. The ones with faces." I gathered them — the casualty actuals, the civilian proximity reports, the diver test series — and handed them to Rosalind personally, one stack, sealed in a waterproof case she'd brought for exactly this purpose. "These go on your body, not in a bag. Bags get left behind. Bodies, apparently, come back for each other. I've seen yours do it."

Rosalind took the case and, after a moment, did something I didn't expect. She tucked it inside her dive vest, against her chest, and said, "There. Now it's where my heart would be if I hadn't pawned it years ago."

"Ma'am," Mateo said over the comm, and even through the static I could hear the grin, "that's the saddest romantic thing anybody's ever said on this boat, and Sam proposed to a dolphin once."

"That was a rescue," Sam said with dignity, "and she said yes."

For one moment — one small, warm, impossible moment — we stood in the cold heart of a secret weapons facility, surrounded by the paperwork of atrocities, laughing. Because that's what people do when they've decided to be unafraid together. They laugh in ugly places. It's how you mark territory.

Then Priya's tablet chirped, and the laughter died in the air like a snuffed candle.

She stared at the screen. Looked up. Her face had gone the color of the corridor walls.

"Motion sensors," she said. "Just tripped on level three. The holding wing." A pause, and her voice dropped. "Someone just opened the cell doors. All of them. At once."

Rosalind's hand went flat against the evidence case over her heart.

"That's not a release," I said, and my nine days of darkness rose up in my throat. "That's a move. When they want prisoners somewhere else before visitors arrive—"

"They're relocating them," Rosalind finished. Her eyes had gone bright and terrible. "How long do we have?"

Priya checked the sensor log, and I watched her do the math, and I watched the math hurt her.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "Maybe less. And the transfer route runs straight through the level-two junction — which means whatever's coming up that corridor is coming straight toward us."

Somewhere below us, deep in the mountain, boots began to ring on steel deck plates. I knew that sound. I had spent nine days hearing it in my sleep.

This time, I was not alone, and neither were they.

Rosalind Hartwell rolled her shoulders, touched the case one more time, and said the sentence I suspect she'd been carrying since the day she signed a contract nobody should have signed:

"Everybody pick a weapon and a prayer. We're meeting them halfway."
