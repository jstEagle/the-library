## 16. Taken

Mateo Cruz had a theory about fear. He'd developed it over years of working boats too small for the weather they sailed into, and it went like this: fear was just your body doing math faster than your brain could. Your hands knew before you did that something was wrong. Your stomach filed the paperwork first.

His stomach had been filing paperwork all morning.

They were three hours into the facility—three hours of flooded corridors and emergency lighting the color of old bruises—and Mateo had been carrying the archive copy in a waterproof case strapped to his chest like a very serious baby. Rosalind's orders had been simple: *You don't let go of that case unless you're dead, and even then, think hard about it.*

He'd laughed at the time. He wasn't laughing now.

"Copy's complete," Priya's voice crackled through his earpiece, thin and staticky through the facility's drowned wiring. "Every file, every casualty list, every signature. It's all on this drive."

"And the original?" Rosalind asked.

"Left exactly where we found it. Let them come home to their own crime scene."

Sam's voice joined in, low and steady the way it always was, like a man reading bedtime stories to a nervous child. "Marsh is stable. She walked most of the way herself. She keeps saying thank you. I keep telling her she can thank me by not dying."

"She has a gift for understatement," Lena Marsh said faintly in the background, and Mateo smiled despite everything. Nine days alone in a breached submersible, half-mad from cold and dark, and the woman still had her dry wit intact. There was hope for all of them.

They were moving back toward the maintenance lock, single file along a catwalk above a flooded junction where the water glowed faintly green with whatever the facility pumped into it. Mateo took up the rear, because he was biggest and because someone had to watch their backs, and because—though he'd never say it out loud—he trusted himself least with the case. If he carried it, he'd only worry about dropping it. If he didn't carry it, he'd only worry about whoever did.

Rosalind led. Of course she led. The woman had a compass in her chest where other people kept a heart.

* * *

It started as a sound.

Not a loud sound. That was the terrible part. Just a soft metallic *tick*, somewhere below them, like a cooling engine. Then another. Then a rhythm.

Priya stopped so fast Sam nearly walked into her. "Do you hear—"

"I hear it," Rosalind said.

Mateo's body finished its math before his brain caught up: boots. Metal decking. More than two. Coming fast.

"Floodlights," he said, and then the floodlights came.

The corridor ahead of them detonated into white. Every fixture along the junction blazed at once, and Mateo threw an arm across his eyes, and in that one blind second he heard the thing he would hear for the rest of his life, however long or short it turned out to be: the flat, amplified voice of a man who had rehearsed this announcement.

*"This facility is under security control. You will drop your equipment and place your hands on your heads."*

"They found the maintenance lock," Priya breathed. "How did they—the lock was sealed—"

"Hale had the codes," Rosalind said. Her voice was calm in the way ice is calm. "Of course he had the codes. It's his facility."

Behind them, at the far end of the catwalk, more lights. More boots. The rhythm of them closing in from both directions, patient as tide.

*Mateo.* His own name arrived in his head uninvited, in his mother's voice, the way it always did when things went truly wrong. *Mijo, what have you gotten into now?*

He thought about the answer honestly, which was a mistake, because the honest answer was: *a family, Mamá. I finally found one, and it's about to be arrested or worse.*

"Options," Sam said quietly. "Quickly."

"The water," Priya said. "The junction below—we came through it. If we can get down to the lock—"

"It's forty meters behind us," Rosalind said, "and they're between us and it."

"No," Mateo said.

Everyone stopped. Even, it seemed, the boots behind them paused, though that was probably his imagination.

"There's a second way," Mateo said. "When we came in, I mapped it. Habit. You spend enough years on boats falling apart, you learn where the exits are before you learn where the coffee is." He pointed back the way they'd come, past the archive room, toward a service ladder he'd noticed half-buried in conduit. "Maintenance shaft runs up to a ventilation platform. From there there's a crawlway that should come out near the dry dock gallery. It's tight. Too tight for all of us with gear."

"It's also unmapped," Rosalind said. "We don't know where it ends."

"We know where it *starts*," Mateo said. "That's more than the other option gives us."

Below and behind, the voice again, closer now, losing its patience: *"This is your final warning."*

Rosalind looked at him. In the white glare her face was all planes and shadow, and her eyes did the thing they did when she was measuring a person against a problem and finding the arithmetic acceptable.

"You'll take the rear," she said.

"I'll take the rear," he agreed.

"Mateo—" Sam started.

"Go," Mateo said, gently, the way you'd say it to a patient who wanted to argue about discharge papers. "Somebody has to make sure our new friends don't hurry you. I'm very good at being slow and annoying. Ask anyone."

"That's true," Priya said. "He is."

"See? Character witness."

Lena Marsh made a small sound that might have been protest, but Sam's hand was already on her shoulder, steering her forward, and the column began to move—Rosalind first, case transferred to Priya with a handoff so smooth and solemn it deserved music, then Marsh, then Sam, then Priya clutching the drive against her chest like it could feel gratitude.

Mateo stayed at the catwalk's end and watched them go, and felt the strangest thing settle over him, which was peace. Not bravery—he wanted to be clear with himself about that, because Mateo Cruz was many things but not a liar, at least not to himself. Bravery was for people who weren't afraid. This was just clarity. For twelve years he'd been running from a marriage that ended, a brother who wouldn't call him back, a version of himself he didn't much like. And then a woman with a haunted face had hired him onto a salvage boat full of strangers, and somewhere between the bad coffee and the night watches and Sam's terrible singing, the strangers had become the best thing he had.

So when the first soldier came around the corner—a big man in black gear, rifle leveled, shouting words Mateo didn't process—Mateo did the only reasonable thing available to a man protecting his family.

He picked up a fire extinguisher off the wall and threw it at him.

* * *

Afterward, he would remember it in pieces, the way you remember any storm.

The extinguisher spinning end over end, catching the floodlight. The soldier ducking, the canister clanging off the rail and dropping into the green water below with a splash that sounded almost polite. Mateo turning and running—not away, he'd tell himself later, *toward*, toward the ladder, drawing them, making himself the loudest, largest, most annoying target on the entire seafloor.

Shouts behind him. A warning shot that sparked off the deck a meter from his heel, close enough that he felt the heat of it through his boot sole.

"Stop!" someone yelled. "Stop or we shoot!"

"You're shooting anyway!" Mateo yelled back, which was fair, and also bought him four seconds.

The ladder was where he remembered it, rusted and narrow, bolted into the shaft wall. He went up it hand over hand, lungs burning, and the shaft swallowed the light behind him until he was climbing in the dark by memory and faith. Below, voices argued—one wanting to follow, one saying the shaft was a choke point, one radioing somebody named Vasquez—and Mateo climbed, and climbed, and permitted himself one small, stupid, joyful thought:

*I'm going to make it. I'm actually going to—*

The hatch at the top opened before he reached it.

Light poured down. A silhouette stood against it, rifle slung, holding out a hand—not to help him up, but palm-out, in the universal gesture of *that's far enough.*

"Slowly," the silhouette said.

Mateo hung there on the ladder, halfway between the dark and the light, and did some fast math of his own. Above: one soldier, maybe two, a crawlway beyond them that might lead anywhere. Behind and below: the team, escaping, if his noise had bought them enough time. In his chest: no case, no drive, nothing they needed except himself.

*Trade,* said the math. *You were always going to be the trade. You volunteered. You just didn't use those words.*

He thought about Rosalind's face when she'd handed off the case. He thought about Sam's hand on Marsh's shoulder. He thought about his mother, and how she'd once told him that the Cruz men had two settings—stubborn and stupider—and that his whole life's work was choosing stubborn.

He chose stubborn.

"Well," Mateo said, in his best conversational Spanish-inflected English, the one he used for calming drunks and frightened passengers, "this is embarrassing. I appear to be lost. Is this the way to the gift shop?"

The silhouette didn't laugh. Somewhere behind it, a second figure stepped into view, and Mateo's stomach filed its final paperwork of the day.

"Hands," the first one said. "Now."

Mateo raised them. "For the record," he said, "I want it noted that I got further than anyone expected."

* * *

They zip-tied his wrists in front of him, which he chose to interpret as a compliment—they expected him to need his hands for something—and marched him down a corridor he hadn't seen before, deeper into the facility, away from the maintenance lock, away from the sea, away from every direction that meant *home*. The archive copy was gone with Priya; he held onto that. Whatever happened next, the truth was moving. It was in Priya's arms and Rosalind's plan and Sam's stubborn medic's heart, and it was swimming toward daylight while he swam the other way.

That was the deal. That had always been the deal. He'd just never said it out loud, and now he supposed he never would, and there was a strange grief in that—unfinished sentences, goodbyes he'd have to deliver telepathically.

*Tell my brother,* he thought, absurdly. *No. Don't tell my brother anything. Tell Rosalind the bilge pump on the boat is older than she thinks and lies about its output. Tell Priya her coffee is a war crime. Tell Sam—*

They pushed him through a door into a room with a table, two chairs, and a camera bolted to the wall, and Mateo Cruz, deckhand, cook, occasional philosopher, sat down in the chair he was pointed to and decided that if they were going to interrogate him, the least he could do was be interesting.

A man entered. Not a soldier—wrong posture, right confidence. Gray at the temples, uniform pressed to the precision of a man who ironed his own shirts, and a face Mateo recognized from the frigate's deck, from photographs in a decrypted logbook, from nightmares he hadn't yet had.

Commander Hale closed the door behind him, folded his hands, and smiled the smile of a man about to discuss a business opportunity.

"Mateo Cruz," Hale said, reading from a tablet. "Deckhand. Cook. Former crew of the *Andrea's Promise*, out of Curaçao. No criminal record—which must have taken effort." He set the tablet down. "You're the one who threw the fire extinguisher."

"Somebody had to," Mateo said. "It was a good extinguisher. It deserved a purpose."

Hale studied him for a long moment, and Mateo had the uncomfortable sensation of being weighed, priced, and appraised. "Your friends are still loose in my facility," Hale said. "With property that doesn't belong to them. Now—I'm a reasonable man, Mr. Cruz. I don't enjoy hurting people. So here's what's going to happen. You're going to tell me where they're taking that data."

Mateo leaned back in the chair as far as the zip ties and his dignity allowed, and grinned the grin that had gotten him thrown out of exactly two bars and one wedding.

"Commander," he said, "I have been waiting my whole life for someone to ask me that question, because I have prepared *nothing*."

Hale's smile thinned. Outside the door, boots were running—fast, urgent, wrong-direction urgent—and a red light began to pulse slowly along the ceiling seam, washing the little room pink and dark, pink and dark, like a heartbeat.

"What's that?" Mateo asked, though his stomach already knew.

Hale checked his tablet, and for the first time since entering, the commander's composure flickered—just a hairline crack, but Mateo saw it, and clung to it like a man clinging to a rail in heavy seas.

"That," Hale said quietly, "is a scuttle order."

Somewhere deep beneath them, vast and patient, the facility began to die—and Mateo understood, with perfect clarity, that whatever happened next, the clock was no longer on anyone's side but the ocean's.
