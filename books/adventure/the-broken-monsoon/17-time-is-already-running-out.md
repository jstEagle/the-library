## 17. Time Is Already Running Out

The klaxon started as a whisper.

That was the thing about the facility—everything down here was muffled, softened by steel and water and the sheer weight of the ocean pressing in from every direction. Even catastrophe had to be polite about it. The alarm came through the bulkheads like someone tapping a spoon against a coffee cup three rooms away, and for a full five seconds Rosalind Hartwell stood in the corridor with the archive drive clutched to her chest, listening, trying to convince herself it was nothing.

Then the lights went amber.

Not red. Amber. A soft, apologetic amber, the color of a hotel hallway at midnight, and somehow that was worse. Red meant panic. Amber meant *procedure*. Amber meant that somewhere above them, in an office with a view of nothing but ocean, a man had signed off on a checklist.

"They're scuttling it," Lena Marsh said. She was leaning against the wall, still gray from her nine days in the wrecked submersible, but her voice had found its footing again, the way a sailor's does once the deck stops moving. "Controlled flood. Ballast tanks open, compartments sealed in sequence. It'll settle into the trench floor and—" she made a small, terrible gesture with one hand, like she was tucking something into bed, "—and then it's just another anomaly on a sonar map. Buried. Forever."

"How long?" Rosalind asked.

Lena checked her watch, which had stopped sometime during her ordeal and now served only as a reminder that time could abandon you entirely. She looked at the amber lights instead, counting them as they cycled along the ceiling. "The survey team's engineers estimated the full sequence at forty minutes, if they built it to spec. They build everything to spec." Her mouth twisted. "It's the one thing they're good at."

Forty minutes. Rosalind did the math the way she did all her math—in miles, in minutes, in the distance between where you were and where you needed to be and whether your body would forgive you for the trip.

The archive drive weighed almost nothing. That was the joke of it. Everything they'd come for—the test results, the casualty lists, the names of civilians who'd never been told their husbands' fishing boats had been sunk as target practice—all of it fit in a case smaller than a lunchbox, and it weighed less than the coffee thermos Sam Okonkwo carried everywhere like a security blanket. The truth was light. It was only the carrying of it that got heavy.

"Rosalind." Priya Nair's voice crackled through the comm unit at her collar, thin and strained by distance and water and whatever the facility's walls did to radio signals. "Say again your position. We've got movement on the upper levels. Lots of it."

"We have it," Rosalind said. "We're coming up."

There was a pause. In that pause, Rosalind heard the thing Priya wasn't saying, which was that Priya always said things eventually, because silence between friends was just honesty waiting its turn.

"Rosalind," Priya said again. "Mateo's beacon stopped transmitting four minutes ago."

* * *

She made herself walk, not run.

That was a discipline older than her name change, older than the twelve years of miles: when the world starts screaming, you lower your voice. Panic spends oxygen. Panic spends judgment. Panic spends the exact currency you'll need most when the clock runs out, and the clock, she knew with the certainty of a woman who had watched clocks run out before, was already running.

But God, it was hard. Every compartment they passed showed signs of the evacuation—or whatever this was, this controlled drowning dressed up as housekeeping. Chairs pushed back from consoles. A mug left on its side, coffee dried to a brown ring years old or hours old, impossible to tell. And in the third corridor, propped against a bulkhead with the casualness of discarded luggage, a survey team's dive bag. Rosalind recognized the patch on it. Chen. One of the missing. Which meant the missing weren't all missing anymore. Some of them were here, or had been here, and were being moved—or being left.

"The holding cells are on level two," Lena said behind her, breathing hard. Sam had wanted to leave her aboard the submersible and she'd refused with a ferocity that had surprised everyone, including, judging by her face, herself. "They took us there after the submersible. Interrogation rooms, then cells. Mateo—they'd have taken him there too."

"You don't know that."

"I know what I saw them do to my team." Lena's voice didn't rise. It went flat and cold, which was scarier. "I know exactly what they do with people who've seen the archive room."

Rosalind adjusted her grip on the drive. Level two was below them. The scuttle sequence, according to the schematic Sam had pulled from a maintenance terminal before the screens died, flooded bottom-up. Level two would take water first.

Which meant the math had changed. Which meant the math was now this: the proof in her arms, the exit ladder forty meters up and aft, and Mateo Cruz somewhere beneath her feet in a compartment filling with seawater on a schedule set by men who had never once in their lives been inconvenienced by the consequences of their decisions.

Sam caught up with her at the junction, medical kit bouncing against his hip. He'd been quiet since the archive room, in the way he got quiet—not absent, but gathered, like a man rolling up his sleeves inside himself. "I heard the beacon report," he said.

"Priya talks too much."

"Priya talks exactly enough." He fell into step beside her. "Rosalind. The submersible can't wait past twenty-five minutes. Decompression alone—"

"I know the tables, Sam."

"You know the tables. You also know you've never once followed them." He said it without heat, almost gently, the way you'd remind a friend she'd left her stove on. "I'm not telling you what to do. I'm telling you what the numbers say, so that whatever you decide, you decide it with the numbers in the room."

And there it was. The choice, laid out on the deck between them like instruments before surgery.

Go up. Take the proof, take Lena, take themselves, and surface with everything they'd come for. Let the facility close over Mateo like a book. Grieve him properly, later, in the warm air, with the evidence safe and the mission accomplished and a funeral where nobody would ever find a body. That was the duty answer. That was the answer the mission demanded, the answer Elias Vane would have called *professional*, the answer that kept the crew alive and the truth intact and cost exactly one deckhand, who had known the risks, who had signed on anyway, who had laughed at her terrible jokes and fixed her engines and covered their escape with his own body—

Or go down. Into a flooding tomb, after a man she'd known for eleven days, carrying the single most dangerous object in the southern hemisphere, spending minutes she did not have on a maybe.

"Rosalind." Sam again, softer. "Whatever you choose, we're with you. But you should know you're choosing out loud. Everyone's watching what you do next. Including yourself."

She thought about twelve years of miles. Twelve years of putting distance between herself and the accident, the name, the girl she'd been who'd made one catastrophic decision and spent a decade paying interest on it. She had learned every trick of leaving. She could leave anything—a port, a job, a life, a name. She was the best leaver in the business.

And she understood, standing in an amber-lit corridor with the ocean already testing the seals below her feet, that this was the same choice she'd made twelve years ago, inverted. Back then she'd chosen herself. Chosen survival, chosen silence, chosen the long swim away. It hadn't even felt like a choice; that was the horror of it. It had felt like the only sane option, right up until it became the shape of her whole life.

She could not buy back her soul twice. She'd known that since chapter—since the frigate, since Hale's offer, since the moment she'd understood that the commander had studied her file the way she studied tide charts, looking for the exact place to sink the hook.

"Mateo stayed behind so we could carry this out," she said. "If I leave him, then what he bought with those four minutes is a woman who ran anyway. Then he's just a delay, not a sacrifice." She looked at the drive in her hands, then held it out—to Sam. "So we split the difference. Nobody carries everything alone."

Sam took the case. His eyebrows went up. "You're trusting me with—"

"I'm trusting you with the part of the job that matters most, which is why it goes to the steadiest hands on this boat." She turned to Lena. "Can you make the climb to the submersible?"

"I climbed out of a dead submarine nine days ago," Lena said. "I can climb a ladder."

"Sam, get her topside. Get to the ship. Tell Priya to prep for emergency ascent and to keep her opinions to herself until I'm aboard, because I know what they'll be and she'll be right about all of them." Rosalind was already moving toward the maintenance shaft, the one the schematic marked in cheerful green as an alternate route to level two. Green, in navy schematics, apparently meant *technically survivable*. "I'm going down after our boy."

"And if you're not back in twenty minutes?" Sam called after her.

Rosalind paused at the shaft opening. Below her, faint through the metal, she could hear it now—not a whisper anymore but a voice, the deep groan of ballast tanks surrendering, water finding its level in a structure that was never meant to hold it.

"Then broadcast the archive," she said. "Every name. Every number. Don't let them bury it with me."

"That's not what I asked."

She smiled at him, and it was the real smile, the rare one, the one that cost something. "I know. Twenty minutes, Sam. Count them slow."

* * *

The maintenance shaft was exactly as bad as green suggested.

Rosalind went down it hand over hand, rung by rust-flecked rung, the amber emergency lights strobing through grating at each level, and the deeper she went the louder the ocean became. Not waves—it had no patience for waves—but a pressure, a presence, the sound of tons of black water leaning patiently against thirty-year-old welds, saying *take your time*, saying *I'm not going anywhere*, saying the thing the ocean says to everyone eventually, which is *you belong to me, you just don't know the date yet*.

Level two's landing was wet.

That was the detail that stopped her heart for a full second. Ankle-deep, clear, still rising, and coming—as far as she could tell in the strobing light—from the open mouth of the main corridor, where the flood valves stood wide and the water slid in smooth and silent as spilled oil. Somewhere in the facility's bowels, a pump was working against it, but the pumps had lost their argument hours ago. This was just cleanup now. Tidying. The sea reclaiming a room it had been promised all along.

"Mateo!" Her voice bounced down the corridor and came back thinner.

Nothing. Then—faint, muffled, from somewhere to her left, behind a sealed hatch—a sound she'd have known anywhere, in any language, in any ocean. Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks.

Their signal. Established on day two, as a joke, after Mateo had locked himself in the head for twenty minutes and Priya had threatened to cut the door off with a plasma torch while narrating his eulogy. *Three knocks means alive,* he'd said, wheezing with laughter. *Four means bring snacks.*

Alive. Rosalind pressed her forehead against the hatch for half a second—one half-second, borrowed against the budget—and knocked back three times.

Then she looked at the hatch controls. Manual override, red-painted, stenciled with a warning in Spanish and English both. And beside it, a status display, its screen cracked but stubbornly lit, showing the compartment's atmospheric readout.

Breathable. For now. The compartment was the last in the flood sequence—which meant whoever had locked it had done it deliberately, sealing Mateo in a dry box with a countdown instead of letting the water take him quick. Either mercy or cruelty, and with these people Rosalind had learned there was rarely a difference.

The override needed power. The power was out on this level. Which meant the wheel, the manual backup, the kind of mechanism designed by engineers who assumed that someday the men in charge would do something stupid enough to require it.

She put both hands on the wheel and pulled.

It didn't move. Of course it didn't move. Nothing in this facility moved except water and consequences. She braced her boots against the frame, wrapped her grip in the fabric of her sleeves, and hauled with her shoulders, her back, her whole history of hauling things out of wrecks—the freighters, the trawlers, the twelve years of miles—and the wheel gave with a shriek that she felt in her teeth, a quarter turn, then another.

Through the little porthole in the hatch, a face appeared.

Mateo Cruz looked terrible. Split lip, bruised jaw, eyes swollen but blazing, and when he saw her he did the most Mateo thing possible: he grinned, and mouthed something through the glass that was either *took you long enough* or *I love you*, and with Mateo it had always been genuinely difficult to tell the difference.

"Hold on," she mouthed back, and kept turning.

The water on the landing crept up her shins. Cold as a verdict. Somewhere above, faintly, she heard the facility groan—a long structural complaint, the sound of a building reconsidering its career. The amber lights flickered, steadied, flickered again.

The wheel turned. Turned. Turned.

And then, from the dark end of the corridor, footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. The footsteps of a man who has never once been late for anything, because everything else arrives on his schedule.

Rosalind didn't turn around. She kept her hands on the wheel and her eyes on the porthole, on Mateo's face going from triumph to alarm, and she said, without raising her voice, because the world was screaming and she had learned long ago to lower hers:

"Commander Hale. You're standing in a flooding room, and I'm about to be very busy. So whatever you've come to say—I'd say it fast."
