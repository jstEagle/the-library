## 18. The Long Way Up

I have been a medic for eleven years, and in that time I have learned that the human body keeps its own ledger. Every debt comes due eventually. Decompression is just the ocean's way of sending an invoice.

So when Rosalind Hartwell looked at me across the flooded corridor of a dying weapons facility and said, "Sam, I need you to do the math," I already knew I was going to hate the answer.

"Talk to me," she said.

"Mateo's been held on the lower deck for—" I checked my watch and hated the number there too. "Ninety minutes at depth. He's breathing their air, so he's saturated whether we like it or not. If we bring him up fast, we're looking at a fifty-fifty shot of his blood turning to soda water. If we do it right—staged stops, proper decompression—we're looking at four hours hanging in open water while Hale's frigate decides whether we're worth a torpedo."

"And if we leave him?"

The question hung there like something drowned. Priya, crouched by the maintenance lock with her tablet glowing against her face, didn't look up. Lena Marsh did. The doctor had come back to herself somewhere between the archive room and this corridor, and what had returned was a woman with a spine made of rebar.

"Then we're the kind of people who leave people," Lena said quietly. "And everything we're doing here stops meaning anything."

Rosalind nodded once, like a woman confirming a diagnosis she'd already made herself. "Then we go back."

"Roz." Priya finally raised her head. Her voice had the careful flatness she used when she was frightened and refusing to admit it. "The scuttle sequence has the lower decks flooding in twenty-six minutes. The facility's own systems are going to drown him before Hale gets the chance."

"Then we've got twenty-five minutes of margin," Rosalind said. "That's plenty."

It was not plenty. It was the opposite of plenty. But I had watched this woman measure her whole life in nautical miles, keeping everyone at exactly one safe distance, and I had watched her stop measuring three days ago, somewhere around the moment Mateo Cruz got dragged out of an archive room by men with guns. People don't change all at once. They change in the direction they choose when the clock is running.

"Okay," I said, and started pulling gear. "But I'm coming with you."

"No."

"Yes. You'll need someone to run a line, monitor his vitals, keep him conscious. And you'll need someone down here rigging the broadcast anyway, which means you need me in two places, which means—" I shrugged into my harness. "Which means you don't get to be noble alone. That's not how triage works."

For a second I thought she'd argue. Then something moved behind her eyes—not gratitude exactly, more like relief wearing gratitude's coat—and she said, "Fine. But if I tell you to surface, you surface."

"Sure," I said, in the tone of a man agreeing to eat more vegetables.

* * *

The facility was dying around us in stages, and each stage had its own voice. First the lights went amber, then red, then settled into a sullen emergency glow that turned every corridor into a photograph from some sunken century. Then the pumps began their long descending moan, like a whale giving up on a song. Water came next, ankle-deep, then knee-deep, sliding along the deck plates with a patience that felt personal.

We found the holding cells off the old barracks wing—a row of six rooms with porthole windows welded shut and electronic locks that Priya defeated from a wall panel with the serene competence of a woman picking a lock she'd designed herself.

"Third door," she said. "Life sign. Weak but steady."

The second door held two life signs, clustered together, and when it slid open a thin man in a ruined survey uniform flinched back against the far wall like we were the ones with guns. His name tag said OKAFOR, and beside him sat a woman I recognized from the survey team's personnel file—Dr. Adeyemi, the geophysicist—holding his hand with both of hers.

"We're with the salvage vessel *Kestrel*," Rosalind said, and her voice did something I'd never heard it do: it softened without losing any of its steel. "You're Dr. Okafor? We came for you. All of you."

"They told us nobody was coming," Okafor whispered. His lips were gray. "They told us the sea would just... close over it."

"The sea doesn't get a vote today," Rosalind said. "Can you walk?"

Adeyemi answered for both of them, hauling her colleague upright. "He can walk if I'm insulting him the entire time."

"Perfect," I said. "Insult him continuously. It's medically indicated."

* * *

Mateo was in the third cell, and for one horrible heartbeat I thought we were too late, because he wasn't moving.

Then he heard our voices and lifted his head off the bunk with the exaggerated care of a man who had recently lost an argument with a rifle butt, and grinned at us through a split lip.

"Took you long enough," he croaked. "I was starting to think I'd have to rescue myself, and honestly? My plan involved a lot of complaining."

"You're bleeding," Rosalind said. Her hands were already checking his pupils, his pulse, the back of his skull where the hair had gone stiff and dark.

"I'm *distinguished*," Mateo corrected. "There's a difference. Sam, tell her there's a difference."

"There's a difference," I agreed, pressing a dressing to his scalp, "and yours is going to need stitches. Can you stand?"

He stood. It cost him—I saw the wobble he tried to hide, the way his hand found the wall—but he stood, and he even managed a joke about the facility's interior design ("brutalist, two stars, would not recommend"), because that was Mateo Cruz all the way down: a man who treated fear like a houseguest he intended to annoy until it left.

Rosalind put his arm over her shoulders and took his weight without being asked, and I watched her do it, and I understood that whatever else happened tonight—whatever the frigate upstairs decided, whatever the ocean invoiced us—she had already paid for something. Twelve years of miles, spent in a single decision to turn around.

"Twenty-two minutes," Priya said in my earpiece. "Lower decks flood in twenty-two. And Sam—the archive uplink. Tell me you've got it."

"On it," I said. "Go. Get them to the submersible dock. I'll catch up."

"That's what people say right before they don't," Mateo observed.

"Move," Rosalind told him, gently, and they moved.

* * *

The archive room was where I'd left it: humming, half-lit, full of the accumulated sins of important men. My job was simple in description and miserable in execution. The survey team's data, the program records, the casualty lists—all of it was copied onto a transmitter module no bigger than a lunchbox, and my job was to make sure that if the *Kestrel* went down, if we all went down, the truth would climb out of our wreck and keep talking.

Priya had built the fail-safe into the ship's emergency beacon. Dead-man's logic: if the beacon stopped receiving our hourly handshake, it assumed the worst and started broadcasting everything, on every frequency, to every listening station in the hemisphere. Elegant. Ugly. Effective.

All I had to do was finish the handshake protocol, test it twice, and not think too hard about the fact that I was wiring our own deaths into the machinery of our vindication.

"Come on," I muttered at the coupling, which was refusing to seat properly because seawater had gotten into the port and salt does not respect deadlines. "Come on, sweetheart. Don't do this to me. I'm a medic, not an engineer. This is above my pay grade, and technically I don't have a pay grade."

The coupling clicked home on the word *grade*, as if it had only needed to hear how pathetic I was.

"Thank you," I told it sincerely.

First test: green light. Second test: green light. Third test, because I am paranoid and eleven years of medicine will make you that way: green.

"Sam," Priya's voice in my ear, tight. "Water's at the junction. You have maybe four minutes to reach the dock corridor, and Sam—it's rising faster than the model says. Something's opened a breach on D-deck."

"Copy," I said, and ran.

* * *

I caught them at the submersible dock, and the picture there was one I expect to keep for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be.

The dock was a cathedral of rusted girders with black water climbing the walls. The survey team's battered submersible sat cradled in its launch frame, running lights on, hatch open. Rosalind was handing Okafor down into it like she'd done it ten thousand times, steady hands, calm voice, while Adeyemi climbed the ladder behind him. Lena Marsh stood at the frame controls with her ruined hands moving over them like she'd been born there—nine days alone in a dead submersible had apparently included a great deal of studying the manual.

And Mateo was braced against a girder, white-faced, arguing.

"—not leaving without you, that's the whole point, that's the *entire* point—"

"The math doesn't care about your feelings," Rosalind was saying. "Four seats. Five of us. Someone rides the tether sled, and the sled can't take weight in this current, so it's me or—"

"It's you," Mateo said. "Obviously it's you. You're the captain."

"I'm the one who caused this," Rosalind said, and there it was again, that thing underneath her voice, twelve years deep. "Hale's here because of what I took. Mateo—"

"Hey." Mateo pushed off the girder, swayed, steadied himself against her shoulder. Even concussed, even gray-lipped, he managed to look her dead in the eye. "You came back. Nobody made you. So we're not doing the arithmetic where you're the one who gets subtracted. Sam! Back me up. Medically."

"Medically," I said, wading toward them through thigh-deep water, "the captain should ride the sled, because the sled puts her outside first, where she can help haul us aboard, and also because if anyone argues with me I'm prescribing bed rest and nobody wants that."

Lena Marsh snorted. It was the most human sound I'd heard from her in days.

Rosalind looked at the four of us—at Mateo's stubborn face, at Lena's steady hands, at me dripping and out of breath—and something in her jaw unclenched by a single degree, which for Rosalind Hartwell was roughly equivalent to weeping.

"Sled," she said. "Fine. But Sam rides with Mateo in the sub. If his pressure symptoms spike, you treat him, you don't negotiate with him."

"He never negotiates anyway," Mateo said. "It's very frustrating."

"Fifteen seconds!" Priya, in all our ears now, from the *Kestrel* above. "D-deck breach is accelerating. Get in the water, all of you, or the water gets in you, and I promise it's ruder about it."

* * *

The launch itself was ugly and perfect. Lena rode the sled up the tether line into the dark, then Rosalind, swallowed by the black water so fast it seemed like the sea had simply taken her back. Mateo went through the submersible hatch with my hand between his shoulder blades, and I followed, and Priya blew the clamps, and the facility that had eaten a survey team and nearly eaten us let go of its grip with a shudder that I felt through the hull like a sigh.

Then we were rising, and the trench fell away beneath us, and the only sound was the sub's tired motors and Mateo breathing beside me—too fast, too shallow.

"Hey," I said. "Look at me. Slow it down. In for four, out for six. You're saturated, and panic is just nitrogen with ambition."

"In for four," he managed. "Out for six. That's... that's a lot of counting, Sam."

"I know. Count with me anyway. Four in. Six out. There you go."

His color came back by degrees. Outside the viewport, the water went from black to bruise-purple to a deep, impossible blue, and I kept one hand on his wrist and counted his pulse against my watch and thought about ledgers. About debts. About how the body keeps score no matter what we promise it.

Two hours of staged decompression waited for us in the water column above—hangs at forty meters, thirty, twenty, ten, the whole tedious liturgy. And above that, somewhere in the grey-green light, the *Kestrel*, and above *her*, on the surface, a frigate with guns and a commander who had offered Rosalind her own soul back at a discount.

"Sam," Mateo said quietly, somewhere around the thirty-meter hang, his voice slurred with exhaustion and nitrogen in equal parts. "If Roz hadn't come back..."

"She came back."

"Yeah. But if she hadn't." He was quiet a moment. "I'd have understood, you know? If she'd taken the deal. Everyone's got a thing they're running from. Hers just had better tailoring than most."

"That's why it mattered," I said. "Easy courage is cheap. She bought the expensive kind."

He smiled, winced at whatever that did to his lip, and closed his eyes. "Put that on my chart. 'Patient displayed excellent taste in captains.'"

"Noted," I said, and wrote it down, because sometimes the chart is allowed to be true.

* * *

We broke the surface ninety minutes later, into weather that could not decide what it wanted to be—low cloud, flat swell, a wind with teeth in it. The *Kestrel* sat a quarter mile off, and she looked wrong before I could say why. Then I saw why. She was listing. Not badly, not yet, but unmistakably, the way a man lists when he's trying to keep weight off a broken foot.

And beyond her, gray against the gray, close enough that I could see the wake boiling off her bow, came the frigate.

"Kestrel, Kestrel, this is the sub," Priya's voice crackled, stripped of everything except function. "What's your status?"

A pause. Then Rosalind's voice, and I will give her this: she sounded calm. She sounded like a woman reading a grocery list.

"Hull breach below the waterline, patched but failing. Priya, I need you to listen carefully. The beacon handshake is live?"

"Confirmed. Fail-safe armed. If we sink, the world hears everything."

"Good." Another pause, longer. Through the viewport I watched the frigate grow, and I watched the *Kestrel* sit in its path like a stubborn animal that had decided the road belonged to it. "Because I don't think they're going to let us finish the decompression stops. And I don't think they're going to accept our surrender, either. Hale's past accepting things."

"Roz," Priya said. "What are you telling me?"

Rosalind was quiet for three full seconds. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—not harder, exactly. Clearer. Like glass after firing.

"I'm telling you to get everyone topside and hold onto something. And I'm telling you that whatever happens in the next hour—we came back for each other, and that was the part that mattered. Everything else is just weather."

The frigate's bow wave spread wide and white, closing the distance.

"Sam," Mateo murmured beside me, eyes still closed, hand finding my sleeve. "Tell me you rigged that beacon good."

"Three tests," I said. "All green."

"Then let's go be weather."
