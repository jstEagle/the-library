## 19. Surface Fire

Priya Nair had always believed that the ocean was honest. It killed you quickly or it didn't kill you at all, and it never pretended to be anything other than what it was. Machines, in her experience, were nearly as honest—give her a seized bearing or a cracked weld any day over a human being with a secret.

It was people who lied. And it was people, specifically the ones aboard the frigate sitting four hundred meters off the *Kestrel's* port bow, who were about to make liars of them all.

She stood on the aft deck with a mug of chai gone cold in her hand and watched the warship through the morning haze. It was beautiful, in the way that things built to destroy things are beautiful: gray hull like a blade, radar mast turning its slow patient circles, the white wake curling off its bow like a signature on a contract nobody had read. The name on the stern was too far away to read, but she didn't need to read it. She'd spent the last hour running its silhouette against every naval registry she could pull from the satellite uplink, and the answer had come back the way answers always came back when Rosalind Hartwell was involved: *vessel not found*.

A three-hundred-foot warship that did not exist. Priya had laughed out loud alone in the engine room when she saw it, a short ugly laugh that echoed off the generator housing. Of course. Of course the navy that denied the wreck denied the frigate too. Denial was clearly their entire operating budget.

"Status?" Rosalind's voice came through the deck speaker, tinny and compressed, rising up from eleven fathoms of water and one very bad decision.

Priya keyed the mic. "Our friend is still parked where he was. No movement. No hailing. Just sitting there like a cat outside a mouse hole."

"How's our girl?"

"Our girl," Priya said, glancing down at the dive station, at the umbilical gauges and the ballast readouts and the little green light that meant Sam was breathing at the end of two hundred meters of hose, "is holding together better than the rest of us. Comms are clean. Life support nominal. I've got the winch warmed for a fast pull if you need it."

"We'll need it," Rosalind said, and there was something in her voice that Priya had learned to hear over twelve days of shared coffee and shared fear—the sound of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "We're coming up with more than we went down with. Two survivors plus Mateo. Watch your trim."

"Watch my—" Priya looked at the sky, at the frigate, at the cold tea. "Rosalind. The frigate."

"I see it."

"Do you? Because I've been watching it for an hour and it has moved forty meters closer and turned its flight deck into our wind, which is either a coincidence or a man positioning himself to do something unpleasant without looking like he's doing it."

A pause. Static. Somewhere below them, in the dark, Rosalind Hartwell was deciding how much truth to spend.

"Priya," she said finally, "if they come for us before we're up—"

"I know."

"—you take the *Kestrel* and you go. You don't wait. You don't be clever. You go."

"Mm," said Priya, who had absolutely no intention of going anywhere, and who suspected Rosalind knew it, because Rosalind sighed the sigh of a woman who has given an order she already knows is dead.

"Priya."

"Yes, Captain."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you say 'yes' in a voice that means 'no.'"

"That's not a thing."

"It's absolutely a thing. Mateo does it. You do it. I'm surrounded by people who treat my orders as suggestions with extra steps."

Priya smiled despite everything, which she was learning was simply what happened around these people. Twelve days ago they had been strangers hired off a broker's list, each carrying luggage they never opened in front of each other. Now Mateo was at the bottom of a trench buying them time with his body, Sam was down there keeping him alive, and she was standing on a rust-streaked salvage tub arguing about obedience with a woman who'd spent twelve years being no one. Family, she thought. Apparently this was what family felt like. Loud, terrified, and held together with baling wire.

* * *

The first shell came nine minutes later.

She heard it before she understood it—a flat crack rolling across the water, wrong somehow, too casual, like someone slamming a car door three streets away. Then the sea two hundred meters off their starboard bow stood up in a column of white and fell back down, and the sound arrived properly this time, and Priya's body did what bodies do: it dropped her behind the winch housing before her brain had finished the sentence *that was a warning shot.*

Her ears rang. Her chai, abandoned on the rail, shivered in rings.

The frigate's bridge lit up with signal lamps. Priya grabbed the binoculars and read the flashing, translating with hands that wanted very much to shake: **VESSEL KESTREL. YOU ARE IN A RESTRICTED MILITARY EXERCISE AREA. HEAVE TO AND PREPARE FOR BOARDING OR YOU WILL BE FIRED UPON.**

"Fired upon," Priya repeated aloud. "Fired *upon.* As opposed to the warm, friendly firing you've just done at our bow."

She keyed the mic. "Rosalind. They've announced boarding. And they've redefined the word 'upon.'"

Static. Then, distantly, through the comm relay patched down to the dive bell: Rosalind's voice, tight and level. "How long until we can pull the bell?"

"Four minutes if nothing goes wrong. Twenty if it does."

"Make it four."

"Captain, respectfully, I am an engineer. Four minutes means six. Six means eight. Physics doesn't take requests."

"Then start lying to physics," Rosalind said, and cut the channel.

The second shell took the *Kestrel's* forward mast off.

It wasn't even aimed well—that was the thing Priya would remember later, telling herself over and over, *they weren't even trying*—a round meant to miss close enough to terrify, except terror is imprecise and masts are tall. The report came, then the shriek of steel, then the mast leaned with terrible dignity to starboard and lay down across the foredeck like a tired man onto a bed, taking the antenna array, the navigation lights, and Priya's entire satellite uplink with it.

She stood up from behind the winch. Her heart was going like a hammer drill, but her hands, her blessed traitor hands, were already cataloguing damage. Mast: gone. Antenna: gone. Forward deck plating: holed, below the waterline by perhaps half a meter. Fuel line to the auxiliary generator: nicked and weeping diesel in a bright rainbow thread.

And the archive data—the copy of everything, the proof, the names, the dead civilians, the whole rotten architecture of it—was queued on her local drive waiting for an uplink that no longer existed.

"Oh," Priya said quietly, to the mast, to the frigate, to God if He was taking calls. "Oh, you absolute bastards. You've made me important."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about courage: it looks exactly like competence from the inside. Priya didn't feel brave. She felt busy. There is no room in a busy mind for fear; there is only the next task, and the next, and the next, and somewhere in the chain of nexts the fear gets skipped like a bad sector.

Task one: stop the boat from sinking. She ran aft, threw the bilge pumps to full, and jammed a tapered wooden plug—she kept plugs stowed everywhere, a habit from a childhood spent around her uncle's trawler, where sinking was considered a normal Tuesday—into the deck hole. Water slowed to a trickle. Good. Task two: the fuel line. She crimped it with a pair of locking pliers and a prayer to no particular deity. Task three—

Task three was the problem. Task three was the fact that the only way to broadcast the archive now was line-of-sight radio, and line-of-sight radio needed altitude, and their altitude was currently lying on the foredeck pointing at the fish.

Unless.

Priya looked at the fallen mast. Looked at the boom crane on the aft deck—the big yellow hydraulic arm they used for hauling wrecks and engines and once, memorably, a very angry sea lion. Looked at the antenna, still cabled, still intact, dangling among the wreckage like a kite string.

"Okay," she told the crane. "You and me. We're going to commit crimes against marine engineering."

The crane's controls were simple enough: two joysticks, a load gauge, and a safety interlock that Priya bypassed in eleven seconds with a screwdriver and a total absence of guilt. She swung the boom out over the stern, hooked the fallen antenna assembly with a cargo strap, and began to raise it—not upright, the mast foot was shattered, it would never stand—but trailing. Towed behind the ship on the crane arm, thirty meters above the water, cable taut, the antenna might just hold a line-of-sight path to the relay buoy she'd spotted on the chart, fourteen nautical miles east. If it held. If the sea cooperated. If the frigate let them.

The frigate did not intend to let them.

The third shell hit the water fifty meters short, and the fourth hit the *Kestrel*.

It struck aft, high, punching through the wheelhouse roof and out through the side in a cough of splinters and glass. Priya felt the deck jump under her boots. The wheelhouse—their wheelhouse, with its peeling paint and its kettle and the photo Mateo had taped above the helm of his mother's house in Cebu—shuddered and came apart in slow pieces. The autopilot died with a sad electronic sigh. The rudder indicator froze at fifteen degrees port.

No fire, thank God. Small mercies. But the *Kestrel* was now circling slowly to port like a drunk looking for her keys, dragging a wounded antenna behind her, while a warship that officially did not exist walked its shells toward her with the unhurried confidence of an institution that had never once been punished.

Priya stood in the wreckage of the aft deck, and something in her chest went very quiet and very clear.

They thought the *Kestrel* was a target. They thought she was cargo—a slow, fat, unarmed salvage boat full of evidence to be sunk and a story to be ended. They had done the math: one frigate versus one tramp steamer, and the math was a massacre.

But Priya Nair had grown up on her uncle's trawler in monsoon seas, and she knew something the officers on that gray blade of a ship did not know, something they had never had to learn because no one had ever shot at them: she knew what a working boat could *do*. A salvage vessel was not a weapon, true. But neither was it helpless. It was sixty meters of stubborn steel designed to survive storms that sank yachts, built to drag impossible loads, fitted with cranes and winches and cables rated for tons, crewed—well, captained, at present—by a woman who had spent her whole life making broken machines do things their designers would have found embarrassing.

They want a shield fight, Priya thought. Fine. I grew up playing this game.

She put the *Kestrel's* remaining engine to full ahead, hauled the wheel hard to starboard to break out of the circle, and set her course directly at the frigate.

Not at its waterline. She wasn't suicidal. At its *bow wave*—at the space just ahead of it, the place where a warship's own speed and pride would force it to turn, because no captain alive will ram a civilian vessel if he can avoid the inquiry. Every meter the frigate spent turning was a meter it wasn't shooting. Every second of confusion on that bridge was a second the dive bell rose unbothered. She couldn't outrun them and she couldn't outgun them, but she could *annoy* them, and annoyance, at the right scale, was a weapon.

The frigate's guns went silent. Signal lamps flashed, faster now, less polite: **ALTER COURSE IMMEDIATELY. FINAL WARNING.**

"No," Priya said pleasantly, and held her course, and keyed the intercom to the dive station with her free hand. "Rosalind. Status. And I apologize in advance for the noise."

"What noise—" The deck bucked as a near-miss slapped the sea alongside. "PRIYA. What are you DOING?"

"Driving."

"You're driving AT them?"

"I'm driving at the space in front of them. It's called aggressive incompetence. It's very effective. How's my bell?"

A breath. Below and behind them, the winch drum began to turn, cable paying up out of the dark, and Priya allowed herself one full second of joy so fierce it nearly knocked her over. Coming up. They were coming up. Mateo, Sam, Lena Marsh, the survey survivor—all of them, rising through the cold water toward a burning ship, because apparently that was the kind of ship she ran.

"Bell's at forty meters and climbing," Rosalind said. "Priya, listen to me. That frigate will sink you."

"Probably."

"Priya—"

"But here's my thinking." She swung the wheel, threading the *Kestrel* across the frigate's bow at a distance that would give the other captain nightmares for years. The great gray ship heeled slightly, turning away, its guns tracking and hesitating, tracking and hesitating—an institution discovering, in real time, that shooting a civilian salvage boat full of witnesses on open water in front of its own crew was a different act than shooting at an empty hull in a restricted zone. Hesitation. Beautiful, bureaucratic hesitation. "Every minute they spend dealing with me is a minute you spend coming up. I am not trying to survive this, Captain. I'm trying to *cost* them time. Spend me wisely."

There was a silence on the channel that Priya chose to interpret as respect.

"Priya Nair," Rosalind said at last, and her voice had gone rough in a way Priya had never heard, "when we get through this, I'm promoting you."

"To what? You don't have anyone above engineer."

"I'll invent it. First Officer of Doing Whatever She Wants."

"Tempting. Bell's at twenty-five. Keep pulling."

* * *

The fifth shell found the engine room.

Priya felt it as much as heard it—a deep concussive thud through the hull plates, and then the sound every engineer knows like a mother knows a crying child: the sound of a machine dying. The main engine note sagged, stuttered, caught, sagged again. Steam pressure bled somewhere. The lights browned out and came back amber.

She left the wheel lashed and went below, into heat and smoke and the smell of scorched insulation, and found the number two fuel pump spraying a fine mist of diesel across a casing hot enough to ignite it. Thirty seconds, maybe less, before the whole compartment became a bomb.

She didn't think. Thinking was for people with time. She grabbed the emergency halon bottle off its bracket, tore the seal with her teeth, and emptied it into the spray, then slammed the fuel shutoff and stood in the sudden chemical silence, coughing, blind, listening to the engine turn over and over and refuse to catch.

Come on, she begged it. Come on, old girl. Not today. Today we are inconvenient.

The engine caught on the fourth try with a bang that rattled her fillings, surged, smoothed, and roared back to life, and Priya sat down on the grating beside it and laughed until she cried, which she told herself was the smoke.

When she climbed back up, blinking, the world had changed.

The frigate had stopped shooting.

It hung off their quarter, dead in the water, signal lamps flashing in a pattern Priya didn't recognize—and then did: not navy procedure at all, but plain text, repeated over and over, urgent and strange. **CEASE FIRE CEASE FIRE STAND BY ALL** — and beneath it, from the frigate's own bridge, the unmistakable chaos of a ship receiving news it had not been built to survive. Through the binoculars she could see figures moving on the gray deck, clustered around a console, and one officer standing very still, staring at something on a screen.

At something streaming.

Priya's eyes went to the towed antenna, still riding high on the crane arm, cable humming, link light blinking steady green. Green. Steady green. The relay buoy had taken the feed. Somewhere, right now, the archive was pouring itself into the world—every name, every test result, every civilian casualty, every crime committed in the dark under twelve miles of honest water.

"They know," she whispered. "Oh, they *know*."

And then the winch brake screamed, and she spun toward the stern, where the dive station gauges were swinging wild, where the cable was coming up fast, too fast, where Rosalind's voice burst through the intercom cracked and breathless and urgent:

"Priya! Get ready to receive—we've got company on the bell, and Hale's men are right behind us—get everyone up NOW!"

Priya Nair wiped her face, put both hands on the winch controls, and grinned at the smoking, listing, magnificent ruin of her ship.

"Ladies and gentlemen," she announced to no one, to everyone, to the engine room and the sea and the frigate full of suddenly uncertain men, "the *Kestrel* is now accepting passengers."

She pulled the lever, and the cable came home.
