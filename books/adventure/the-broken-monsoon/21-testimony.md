## 21. Testimony

The hearing room in Portsmouth had once been a customs hall, back when the empire still bothered to count what came through its ports. Now it was a theater of folding chairs and microphones, and Rosalind Hartwell sat in the third row with her hands folded like a schoolgirl waiting to be called on, which, in a sense, she was.

She had dressed carefully. Not the borrowed blazer Sam had suggested—she'd looked at herself in the mirror and seen a woman impersonating respectability, and the mirror had laughed at both of them. Instead she wore the clothes she actually owned: canvas trousers, a shirt with salt faded into the seams, and the one decent jacket Mateo had insisted on buying her from a secondhand shop the week before, because, as he put it, "You can't testify against the government looking like you lost a fight with a winch."

"You look fine," Priya murmured beside her. "Terrified, but fine."

"Terrified is accurate."

"Good. Anyone who isn't terrified in there is lying, and they'll be able to tell."

The room was full. Reporters, mostly, packed into the gallery with the hungry stillness of gulls over a working deck. Behind the long table at the front sat the inquiry panel: three civilians, two military officers who kept their faces arranged into expressions of professional neutrality, and a stenographer whose fingers hovered over her machine like a pianist waiting for the downbeat.

On the table in front of the panel, in a plain evidence case that had cost more to transport securely than Rosalind's entire boat was worth, sat the archive.

Fourteen terabytes. Test results. Budget trails. Casualty lists with civilian names on them—fishermen, a ferry crew, a research vessel that had "encountered uncharted ordnance" eleven years ago and never come home. The names of the officials who had signed for all of it, in some cases literally; one of them had initialled a page authorizing the disposal of "nonessential personnel" in handwriting so casual it might have been approving a lunch order.

Rosalind had read that page four times. She suspected she would read it a hundred more before the thing was done with her.

The doors opened at nine sharp, and the room stood, and the panel filed in, and the machinery of justice—slow, grinding, occasionally sincere—began to turn.

* * *

They called her name at half past ten.

Not "Rosalind Hartwell." The clerk read out the name she had surrendered three days earlier in a solicitor's office with Sam holding her hand and Mateo pretending very hard to study a fire evacuation diagram, because he knew, the way her crew always knew, that some things a person needed to do with witnesses and some things needed doing with the witnesses politely looking elsewhere.

Margaret Anne Rosalind Voss.

Twelve years since anyone had said it aloud in a room where she could hear it. She had buried it the way you sink a hull—weighted, deep, far enough from any shipping lane that no one would ever drag for it. The accident had happened on her watch. A training dive off the Hebrides, a checklist she'd signed as complete that wasn't, a faulty regulator she should have caught, and Danny Achebe—nineteen years old, laughing about the sandwich he was going to eat when they surfaced—gone in forty seconds of green water and silence.

She hadn't lied about it. That was the thing people never understood when she tried to explain the years of running. She had simply never corrected anyone. The inquiry had found "operator error, contributing circumstances unclear," and Margaret Voss had paid her fines, served her suspension, and then walked into the sea with a new name and let the old one stay down there with Danny.

The clerk said her name, and she stood, and her legs worked, which surprised her.

She walked to the witness stand. The room did the thing rooms do when a person walks toward a microphone—went quiet in that particular attentive way, forty pairs of eyes and a dozen lenses all leaning in. She put her hand on the book and swore to tell the truth, and the strange, vertiginous thought passed through her that this was the first oath in twelve years she hadn't been technically breaking by taking it.

"State your name for the record," said the chairwoman, a dry, unhurried woman named Justice Ferro who had the eyes of someone who had spent thirty years listening to powerful men explain why the rules didn't apply to them.

Rosalind looked at the microphone. It was old and dented and it had a strip of tape on it marking it PROPERTY OF HM GOVERNMENT, and she thought, absurdly, of the salvage tag she'd once tied to a recovered engine block.

"Margaret Anne Rosalind Voss," she said.

The name went out over the room like a stone into still water. She watched it land. A reporter in the second row sat up straight. One of the military officers on the panel stopped writing. Somewhere in the gallery, a camera shutter clicked, and then clicked again, faster.

"For the benefit of the record," Justice Ferro said, "you are also known as Rosalind Hartwell, salvage diver, master of the vessel *Kestrel's Wake*."

"I am."

"When did you adopt that name?"

"Twelve years ago. The week after the Achebe inquiry closed."

"And why?"

Rosalind had rehearsed this. She'd rehearsed it in the shower, on the dock, at three in the morning staring at the cabin ceiling while Mateo's snoring came through the bulkhead like a friendly foghorn. She had a clean answer prepared, something about wanting a fresh start, something measured and dignified.

What came out instead was the truth.

"Because I couldn't stand to be the person who killed Danny Achebe. And I couldn't stand to be anyone else, either. So I became nobody, and I measured my life in nautical miles, because miles don't ask who you are." She paused. "It worked for a long time. I'd recommend it to no one."

There was a ripple in the gallery—something between a laugh and a breath. Justice Ferro's mouth did something at the corner that on a less dignified face would have been a smile.

"Take your time," she said. "We have the whole day, and, I suspect, a good deal more."

* * *

They took the whole day.

They took the coordinates, and the contract with its impossible numbers, and the survey camp with its cold coffee and its missing footprints. They took the logbook and the trench and the facility with its maintenance lock and its corridors full of the vanished team's gear. They took Hale—Commander Hale, currently residing in a cell in Portsmouth, currently declining to comment—and his offer of money and immunity and erasure, and they made her repeat it twice so the stenographer could get every poisoned word.

They took the scuttle order. They took Mateo, captured in the dark holding a doorway so that strangers could live. They took the broadcast, fourteen terabytes of truth streaming out of a burning ship while a frigate tried to sink it, and the moment Hale's own officers saw the evidence on their screens and lowered their weapons—not because they were cowards, Rosalind made clear, but because they were, underneath the uniforms, still the kind of people who could be shown something true and refuse to fire on it.

And at the very end, when the light through the tall windows had gone long and gold and the stenographer's fingers were visibly tired, Justice Ferro asked the question Rosalind had known was coming since the moment she'd decided to come ashore.

"Ms. Voss. You have confessed today to a twelve-year-old offense—the falsification of a dive checklist that contributed to a death. This inquiry has no jurisdiction over that matter, and I must warn you that by stating it here, under oath, on the record, you have placed it beyond your power to unsay. Do you understand that?"

"I understand it."

"You could have declined. Counsel advised you to decline. The name was not required for this inquiry's purposes."

"No," Rosalind said. "It wasn't."

"Then why?"

She looked out at the room. Mateo was in the front row, and he'd stopped pretending to study anything and was just looking at her, steady as a mooring line. Priya sat beside him with her arms crossed and her chin up, wearing the expression she wore when an engine finally turned over. Sam had a notebook full of notes he wasn't reading. And at the back, in a wheelchair, wrapped in a blanket against the chill of a building she'd been carried into for the first time in her life, sat Dr. Lena Marsh, who had spent nine days alone in a broken submarine at the bottom of the world, and who was watching Rosalind with the fierce, exhausted attention of someone who understood exactly what it cost to say a true thing out loud.

"Because I spent twelve years learning what silence costs," Rosalind said. "I know the price of it better than anyone in this room. I paid it in miles. Other people paid it in—" she gestured, and couldn't finish, and Justice Ferro waited, and finally she finished. "In Danny Achebe. In the survey team. In the names on those casualty lists. Silence is never free. Someone always pays the invoice; it just doesn't always come to the person who placed the order." She straightened. "So no. I won't decline. I'm done hiding. Margaret Voss did a terrible thing and ran from it, and Rosalind Hartwell did a good thing and nearly died for it, and they're both me, and I'm not asking anyone's forgiveness. I'm just done paying interest on a debt I could have settled years ago."

The room was very quiet.

Justice Ferro looked at her for a long moment over her glasses. Then she said, mildly, "The inquiry will recess until Monday. Ms. Voss, you are reminded that you remain under subpoena." And then, as the room began to move and murmur and explode into the corridor, she added, almost too quietly to hear, "That was well said. It usually isn't."

* * *

The arrests came in waves, over the following week, like weather fronts.

Two deputy ministers first, taken from their offices in the gray morning with coats over their heads. Then a procurement director, then a rear admiral, then—on a Thursday that Mateo declared should be a national holiday—a lord, an actual lord, escorted out of a club where he'd been having lunch and complaining, witnesses said, about the price of claret.

The *Kestrel's Wake* crew watched it all on a television in the pub on the corner, a pub that had, in the space of a week, gone from regarding them with the polite suspicion reserved for strangers to adopting them with the ferocious pride reserved for locals. The landlord had named a drink after the boat. It was terrible. They drank it anyway.

"Turn it up," Priya said. "Turn it—there. Look at his face."

On the screen, the lord was being put into a car, and his face was doing the thing that faces did in these moments: the baffled outrage of a man who had genuinely, sincerely believed for his entire life that the rules were a thing that happened to other people.

"He's going to say it," Mateo predicted. "He's going to say, do you know who I am."

"He's going to say it," Sam agreed.

"Do you know who I—" the lord began, and the door shut, and the pub cheered, and the landlord, unbidden, brought over five of the terrible drinks on the house.

It wasn't all pub victories. There were harder evenings. The Achebe family had been contacted, because of course they had; the inquiry could hardly reopen one case without touching the other. Rosalind had written to them—a letter she'd rewritten eleven times and finally sent in its simplest version—and had asked for nothing, offered nothing, except the truth that their son's death had never stopped being carried by someone. She did not expect an answer. She wasn't sure what answer she'd want. But the letter was out in the world now, honest and unhidden, and whatever came back, she would be standing in the open when it arrived.

"They might hate you," Sam had said gently, the night she mailed it.

"They might," Rosalind agreed. "Hate needs a name to aim at. They've had twelve years of aiming at a fog. At least now I'm something they can hit."

"That's the most depressing thing I've ever heard someone say with hope in their voice."

"I'm a complex woman."

* * *

The survey team came home on a bright, cold morning with a wind that smelled like salt and diesel and, to Rosalind, like every good day she'd ever had at sea.

Five of the six, alive. Held in the facility's lower levels for nineteen days—"processing," the files had called it—fed, unharmed, and interrogated with a bureaucratic thoroughness that was somehow more chilling than violence would have been. The sixth, Dr. Osei, had died in the initial incident when the survey submersible was rammed; his body had been recovered during the facility's evacuation, and he came home with the others, under a flag, with an honor guard that none of the officials who'd signed for his death bothered to attend.

The crew of the *Kestrel's Wake* stood at the dock in civilian clothes, at the edge of the crowd, because this was not their day. This was for the families, who ran down the pier when the ferry came in, and for the survivors, who came down the gangway blinking in the light like creatures raised in the dark, which was exactly what they were.

Lena Marsh walked down last, on her own feet, refusing the arm offered to her. She was thinner than when they'd pulled her from the wreck, but her eyes were clear now, and she scanned the crowd with a purpose, and when she found what she was looking for she changed direction with the decisiveness of a woman who had spent nine days learning that you do not waste time.

She stopped in front of Rosalind.

"You gave your real name," Lena said. "In there. On the record."

"I did."

"They told me you would. In the facility. One of the guards, a young one, he talked when he wasn't supposed to. He said there was a salvage captain out there who'd found the camp, and that she was going to come for us, and that she'd already given up her own name to do it." Lena's voice was steady, but her hands weren't; Rosalind took one of them without thinking about it, the way you'd take a line in rough water. "I didn't believe him. Nine days in that sub, you stop believing in rescue. It's a story you tell yourself so the dark doesn't win." She squeezed Rosalind's hand. "Thank you for being true anyway."

Rosalind found, to her considerable embarrassment, that she couldn't speak. Behind her, she heard Mateo blow his nose with the subtlety of a foghorn, and Priya mutter, "Not one word," and Sam say, "I said nothing."

"The inquiry's going to call you," Rosalind managed. "When you're ready."

"I know." Lena looked out at the water, at the gray-green glitter of it, the same sea that had tried to keep her. "And I'm going to tell them everything. Every page. Every name." She turned back, and there was something in her face that Rosalind recognized from her own mirror—the look of a person who had decided that the weight was worth carrying if it was carried honestly. "You know what I kept thinking about, down there? In the sub. Nine days. I thought about how I'd spent my whole career being careful. Careful with the data, careful with the funding, careful with what I noticed and what I let myself not notice. Careful all the way into a trench." She laughed, a small dry sound. "You weren't careful. You were just stubborn. I think the world might need more stubborn."

"It has a surplus," Rosalind said. "Most of it is standing behind you on this pier."

* * *

That evening, the four of them sat on the deck of the *Kestrel's Wake*, which was still officially impounded as evidence and unofficially guarded by a bored young constable who had, by the third day, accepted Sam's offer of tea and was now functionally crew.

The harbor lights came on one by one. Somewhere across the water, a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and Priya, who could identify any vessel by its horn the way other people identified birds by their song, said, "Container ship, Greek registry, needs her anodes done," and nobody doubted her.

"So," Mateo said. He was sitting on the hatch cover with his injured arm—mending well, the medic in Sam pronounced weekly, the patient in Mateo ignored entirely—propped on a cushion he pretended not to need. "They're going to give you a medal."

"I am not going to get a medal."

"You are. Priya read it in the paper. 'The government is considering formal recognition.'"

"The government," Rosalind said, "can consider it until the sea boils."

"You have to take it," Priya said, with the tone of someone laying out an engineering argument. "Think about it. They hand you a medal, cameras everywhere, and you stand up and say, 'Thank you, and by the way, here's the full list of everyone who hasn't been arrested yet.' It's a delivery system, Rosalind. It's a podium with a ribbon on it."

Rosalind opened her mouth to object and then closed it, because the woman had a point, and they all knew it, and Sam was already laughing.

"God help them," Sam said. "They think they're rewarding her. They have no idea what they're unleashing."

"I'm a complex woman," Rosalind said.

"You're a menace," Mateo said fondly. "A decorated menace."

The laughter went around the deck and came back, and for a while nobody said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying. The water slapped the hull. The constable on the dock pretended not to be listening and was clearly taking notes. The town went about its evening around them, ordinary and lit and safe, and Rosalind sat in the middle of it with her crew and felt the strange new thing that had been growing in her chest since the day she'd signed a contract she should have burned.

It took her a while to identify it, because she'd never had it before. It wasn't safety. Safety was the thing she'd spent twelve years mistaking for a life, and it had never once felt like this.

It was being known. All of it—the name, the crime, the miles, the choice, the whole wreck of her and whatever seaworthy vessel she'd built out of the remains—and being known, and staying. Being moored.

"Rosalind." Priya, quietly, nodding toward the gangway. "You've got a visitor."

A young woman stood at the bottom of the ramp, holding a letter in both hands, looking up at the boat with an expression Rosalind knew from the inside out: the look of someone carrying something heavy and not knowing where to put it down. She had Danny Achebe's smile. Rosalind knew it instantly, the way you know a coastline you've only ever seen in charts, and her heart went still and then very, very loud.

"She says her name is Grace," the constable reported, slightly flustered. "Grace Achebe. She says—" he glanced at the letter, uncomfortable, "—she says she's been looking for Margaret Voss. She says she has questions. And she says—" he hesitated.

"Say it," Rosalind said. She was already standing. "It's all right. Whatever it is, it's all right."

"She says she thinks it's time somebody told her the truth about her brother," the constable read, "and she thinks you're the only one who has all of it."

The deck was silent. Mateo started to rise; Priya put a hand on his good arm. Sam looked at Rosalind with his calm, steady eyes and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that she didn't already know.

Rosalind looked at the young woman at the bottom of the ramp—at the letter, at the smile, at the twelve years of silence standing between them like a stretch of open water—and found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid.

"Show her up," Rosalind said. "And somebody put the kettle on."
