## 22. Measuring in Years

The *Broken Monsoon* — Rosalind had finally given the salvage vessel a name worth saying aloud — sat in dry dock at Port Calloway with her belly open to the sky like a patient who'd stopped being embarrassed about it.

Rosalind stood on the staging platform beside her hull, coffee in one hand, paint scraper in the other, and watched Priya argue with an outboard motor as though it had personally insulted her family.

"It's not the carburetor," Priya said, to the motor, to the yard, to God.

"You said that about the generator," Mateo called down from the rail above, where he was re-tarring seams with his arm still in its sling, because Mateo Cruz had never once in his life accepted help gracefully.

"The generator was the carburetor's fault."

"That's not how machines work."

"That's how *that* machine works."

Rosalind sipped her coffee and let them have it. Six months ago she would have been down there elbow-deep in fuel lines herself, alone, at dawn, before anyone could offer to share the work. Now she'd learned something harder than diving: how to stand still while other people did things badly near her ship.

Mostly. She was still holding the paint scraper.

* * *

The refit had started as a practical matter and become something else entirely. The inquiry money — witness compensation, they called it, as if a check could be weighed against nine days in a flooded trench — had covered the hull work. The rest came from honest contracts: channel dredging, wreck survey for the harbor authority, a job pulling crab pots off a fishing cooperative whose boats kept fouling them. Small work. Legal work. Work where nobody asked you to recover equipment from coordinates that didn't exist.

Sam had taken over the medical bay, which was really just the forward cabin with a proper cot and a cabinet he'd bolted to the bulkhead himself, level within a millimeter, because Sam Okonkwo did nothing approximately. He'd also, without discussion, appointed himself keeper of the crew's sleep, their meals, and their feelings, whether or not they consented to be kept.

"Eat," he said now, appearing at Rosalind's elbow with a plate of eggs he had definitely not cooked on the boat's galley stove, which meant he'd walked to the chowder shack and back before seven.

"I'm working."

"You're standing." He set the plate on a bollard. "There's a difference, and the difference is breakfast."

She took the plate. It was easier than arguing, and besides, the eggs were good. Everything Sam made was good; it was one of the many things about him that made people underestimate how stubborn he was.

"Postcard came," he said, more casually than the words deserved.

Her chest did the thing it always did now — a small lift, like a diver's first breath after long bottom time. "Lena?"

"Lena." He handed it over.

The front was a photograph of a lighthouse somewhere green and northern, absurdly picturesque. The back read, in Dr. Lena Marsh's precise, slightly tilted hand:

*Rosalind — Third week of the fellowship. They gave me a lab with a window, which I am told is standard, but I keep checking it for pressure seals. Presented my trench data yesterday. A man in the third row asked if my account of the facility could be independently verified. I told him I had witnesses. He looked nervous. Good. Tell Mateo his sister writes to me now too, which I suspect is your doing, and tell Priya the coffee here is an insult to the bean. Counting days until I can come see the ship. — L.*

P.S. *I slept eight hours last night. Eight. In a row.*

That last line was new. For months, the postcards had been short, careful things — weather reports, professional updates, the occasional dark joke that landed a half-second late because you could feel her testing whether jokes were safe again. The sleeping line was different. The sleeping line was a woman reporting her own repair.

Rosalind read it twice and put it in the tin box under the wheelhouse chart table, where eleven others already lived, arranged by date, because some archives you kept on purpose.

* * *

Mateo's sister had come aboard in the spring.

Rosalind remembered the visit the way you remember a stitch going in — sharp, then strangely relieving. The young woman had climbed the ramp with her brother's same dark eyes and none of his swagger, and she had sat across from Rosalind in the wheelhouse with her hands folded, waiting.

So Rosalind had told her all of it. The truth about Tomás — that he hadn't run off with the insurance money, hadn't abandoned her, hadn't died a stranger. That he'd signed onto a salvage crew captained by a woman named something else entirely, and that when the storm took him, he'd spent his last hour above deck securing another man's lifeline instead of his own. That Rosalind had lied on the report afterward — small lies, coward's lies, the kind that calcify — and had carried his name like ballast ever since, unable to surface it and unable to jettison it.

The sister had listened without moving. Then she'd said, "He tied off someone else first?"

"Before anything else. Before his own gear."

She nodded slowly, and something in her face unclenched that had been clenched, Rosalind realized, for twelve years. "Then he was who I thought he was." She'd looked up. "You lied about him. Why?"

"Because telling the truth meant telling everything. And I wasn't ready to exist yet."

The sister had considered this with terrible patience. "And now?"

"Now I'm testifying."

They'd shaken hands on the ramp, and the sister had said, "Tomás would've liked your crew. He liked people who argued." And Mateo, eavesdropping shamelessly from the deck below, had shouted, "We do NOT argue!" and Priya had thrown a rag at him.

After that day, the letters between Lena and Mateo's family had started, and Rosalind understood she'd done a small, permanent thing, the kind that doesn't make headlines but holds weight anyway.

* * *

The trial had ended in June. Rosalind had testified for two days under her real name — Rosalind Hartwell, spoken aloud in a courtroom, on the record, into microphones, the syllables feeling foreign in her own mouth like a language she'd studied as a child. Commander Hale had sat at the defense table with his posture immaculate and his future leaking out of him one exhibit at a time. When the verdicts came down — program dismantled, arrests cascading upward through three agencies like water finding its level — Hale had looked across the chamber at her, and she'd braced for hatred.

What she'd seen instead was almost worse: relief. A man setting down something heavy. She'd thought about that for a long time afterward, walking the dock at night, and decided it wasn't her business anymore. Some cargoes you deliver. You don't get to open them.

Her own old crime — the falsified report, the buried name — she'd surrendered voluntarily, in writing, before anyone asked. The penalty had been modest: a fine, a suspended license for ninety days, a stern paragraph in the maritime register. It should have felt like ruin. Instead, on the morning the suspension lifted and she walked back up her own gangway legally, under her own name, Mateo had strung a banner across the wheelhouse that read WELCOME BACK CAPTAIN NOBODY, and she had laughed until her ribs hurt, and that was the whole ceremony.

Twelve years of miles, paid off. Not forgiven — she didn't believe in forgiveness as a transaction — but *settled*, the way a debt settles: accounted, closed, no longer accruing interest in the dark.

* * *

In August they took a contract that made Priya suspicious on principle: a marine archaeology survey, university-affiliated, mapping a colonial-era wreck site off the southern shoals. "Academics," Priya muttered, reviewing the terms. "No budget discipline. No schedule discipline. They'll want to touch everything."

"They'll pay on time," Rosalind said.

"They'll pay *late* on time."

But the academics turned out to be a single exhausted professor and two graduate students who were so grateful for competent help they nearly wept, and the work was slow and sunlit and utterly harmless, and somewhere over those three weeks Rosalind noticed a change she couldn't date precisely: she had stopped checking the horizon for patrol boats.

It happened the way tides happen — too gradual to observe directly, undeniable in retrospect. One evening she realized she'd gone a full day without once scanning the waterline for a gray hull, and the realization landed not as triumph but as quiet, like snowfall. The vigilance didn't vanish; she doubted it ever fully would; a diver keeps her gauges even in shallow water. But it moved from the front of her mind to the back, from a way of living to a habit of craft. She was no longer hiding. She was working.

Sam noticed first, naturally. "You hum," he said one night in the galley.

"I don't hum."

"You hum. Badly. Something that might be a sea shanty or might be a distress signal."

"It's a work song."

"It's a war crime against music. Keep doing it."

* * *

September brought the first cold snap, and with it, Lena.

She arrived by ferry with one duffel bag and a look of fierce determination, as though stepping onto a civilian dock required the same courage as the trench, and maybe for her it did. She stood at the bottom of the gangway for a moment, looking up at the ship — at the fresh paint, at Mateo waving with both arms despite the sling being long since retired, at Sam already calculating her meals — and Rosalind saw her take one deliberate breath before climbing.

"Permission to come aboard," Lena said formally.

"Granted," Rosalind said. "Permanently, if you want it. We've got a bunk."

"I have a fellowship."

"Fellowships end."

"Bunks are forever?" Lena raised an eyebrow. "That's either very generous or very ominous."

"Yes," said Priya, passing with a coil of rope, and Lena laughed — a real laugh, quick and surprised out of her, the first one Rosalind had heard from her that wasn't armor.

They gave her the survey work, the gentle kind, cataloguing artifacts in the hold-turned-lab, and she took to it with the methodical hunger of a woman rebuilding a relationship with her own competence. On her second evening aboard, she stood at the rail watching the light go gold on the water and said, without preamble:

"I used to count exits. In rooms. Where the doors were, how fast I could reach them." She turned her coffee mug in her hands. "Last week I caught myself counting windows instead. How much light each one let in."

Rosalind nodded. She knew exactly what Lena meant, and exactly what it cost to say it, and exactly why you said it anyway — because naming the change helped nail it down.

"Seasons," Rosalind said. "That's the trick. You stop measuring distance and start measuring turns. The year goes around, and you're still here, and eventually you notice you're planning for spring instead of surviving till morning."

Lena looked at her sidelong. "How long did it take you?"

"Ask me again next spring."

* * *

October. The last coat of antifouling paint went on, deep blue-black, and Mateo insisted on painting the name by hand rather than letting the stencil do it, because "a ship's name should look like a person wrote it." He worked all afternoon on the transom, tongue between his teeth, and when he stepped back, the letters curved across the stern in strong, slightly uneven strokes:

*BROKEN MONSOON*

"Should've been something hopeful," Priya said, though she was smiling. "Something forward-looking."

"It IS forward-looking," Mateo said. "A monsoon breaks. Then it passes. Then you go outside and everything's washed clean and smells like rain. It's the most hopeful word there is. You're all just pessimists with toolkits."

Nobody argued, which among this crew constituted unanimous ratification.

That night they ate on deck — Sam's stew, Priya's contraband good bread, a bottle of something Mateo produced from nowhere and refused to explain — and the harbor lights came on one by one across the water, and the conversation drifted through the small, warm territories of people who know each other's silences: the professor's thank-you letter (framed, ironically, by Sam), the winter contract options, whether Mateo's mother would actually visit or simply continue sending enough food to provision a battalion.

"So," Priya said eventually, leaning back on her elbows. "Spring. Big refit's done. Crew's intact. Reputation's technically legal. What does Captain Hartwell want next?"

Rosalind looked around the deck. Sam, steady as bedrock, already dishing seconds without being asked. Priya, whose suspicion had softened into something like faith without ever losing its edge. Mateo, who had stayed — who had stayed through the trial and the threats and the months of reconstruction, who had stayed when staying was the hardest thing, and who was watching her now with that open, unguarded expression he'd never quite learned to hide.

Once, love had been the thing she ran from — Tomás's ghost, her crew's trust, every attachment a liability in a life built on leaving. She'd measured her safety in nautical miles put between herself and everyone who might care about her.

Now the miles were behind her, and what remained was this: four chairs, one deck, a ship with her wounds welded shut and her name freshly written, and people who had voted — literally voted, in a flooding trench-adjacent crisis — to dive back for each other.

"I want," Rosalind said slowly, "to see what the ship looks like in winter. And then in spring. And then—" she shrugged, and found herself smiling without permission, "—then I want to keep finding out."

"That's not a plan," Priya said.

"It's better than a plan. It's a *schedule*."

Mateo raised his bottle. "To the schedule."

"To the schedule," they echoed, and glasses touched, and out past the breakwater the sea breathed its long slow breath against the hull — not keeping secrets anymore, just keeping time.

Later, alone at the rail while the others turned in, Rosalind took out the tin box and added Lena's twelfth postcard to the archive. Then she stood a moment longer, hands on the rail, watching the water.

Somewhere out there was a trench where she had nearly lost everything, and a frigate that had fired on them, and a version of herself who believed invisibility was the only safe harbor. All of it felt far away now — not gone, never gone, but distant, the way a coast is distant once you've rounded it: still real, still shaping the currents, no longer the destination.

She counted, idly, the way she used to count miles. Not distance. Turns. Two seasons since the trial. Three since the trench. Four since a broker slid a contract across a table and she'd signed her way back into the world.

Four seasons. One crew. Zero miles remaining.

Rosalind Hartwell went below to sleep eight hours, in a row, and dreamed — for the first time in twelve years — about absolutely nothing at all.
