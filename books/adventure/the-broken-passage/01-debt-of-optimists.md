## 1. Debt of Optimists

The storm had a shape Juniper Okafor knew the way other people knew their mother's face: a broad comma of convection dragging its tail across the delta like a finger through spilled ink. On the radar screen mounted above the wheel of the *Tern*, it glowed in colors that meant money — red and magenta cores, a hook echo curling off the cell's southern flank like a question mark asking *are you really going to do this again?*

Juniper was going to do it again.

"Come on," they murmured to the sky, to the boat, to the bank account that lived in their head and never stopped bleeding. "Be beautiful for me one more time."

The delta unrolled ahead — brown water braiding through green islands, fishing stilt-houses leaning at angles that suggested prayer more than engineering, the drowned church spire out past Serpent Bend poking up like a broken tooth. Rain hit the windshield in fat, horizontal slaps. The wipers had given up an hour ago; Juniper had given up on them shortly after.

They were chasing a cell that the weather service had already written off as disorganized, which was exactly why it was worth anything. The big, obvious storms got bought up by the insurance consortiums and the agribusiness hydrology desks before dawn. The ugly little cells nobody wanted — those still had data in them, mesocyclone rotations and rain-fall rates and wind profiles that some university lab or private forecaster would pay for, if you could get close enough to taste them without dying.

Close enough was a relative term. Juniper's relative terms had been getting closer and closer to absolute ones for three years now.

The *Tern* bucked over a swell that wasn't supposed to exist this far inland, and the anemometer on the mast shrieked — sixty knots, gusting higher. Juniper's hands did what they always did, adjusting throttle and heading without consulting the part of the brain that was afraid. That was the trick of it, the thing nobody understood about storm work: fear wasn't the problem. Fear was fuel. The problem was everything fear was standing in front of.

Like the number. Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six dollars, plus accruing interest calculated in a way Juniper was fairly sure was illegal in at least two countries, owed to people who had never once raised their voices.

The probe launcher thumped twice as Juniper fired soundings into the cell's inflow. Data streamed back — pressure drops, humidity spikes, the rotation tightening at four thousand feet like a fist closing. It was gorgeous. It was worth maybe eight hundred dollars to the right buyer. It would cost perhaps two hundred in fuel, wear, and the slow grinding tax of keeping a twenty-year-old riverboat alive in salt-rotted air.

Net profit on today's terror: six hundred dollars, give or take.

At that rate, Juniper thought, watching lightning stitch the cloud base to the water in violet threads, I'll be free in roughly fifty-seven years. If the interest doesn't eat faster than I earn. It does. It always does.

The cell core passed half a mile to starboard, close enough that the air went green and the rain turned briefly to something that stung. Juniper held position, held course, held the kind of focus that felt less like courage than like anesthesia. The hook echo swung. The rotation dropped. And then, with the casual cruelty of all weather everywhere, the whole structure sheared apart into rain — ordinary, worthless, honest rain — and drifted east to die over the mangroves.

Juniper sat in the sudden quiet and let the adrenaline drain out through their boots.

"Six hundred dollars," they said aloud, because saying it aloud made it real, and making it real was a form of punishment they'd apparently decided they deserved. "Six hundred dollars."

The radio crackled with a mayday from a fishing skiff somewhere near the second channel. Juniper weighed it for exactly one second — the *Tern* wasn't a rescue boat, the seas were wrong, every minute cost fuel — and then turned toward the coordinates anyway, because there were debts and there were debts, and some kinds you couldn't put on a ledger.

They found the skiff swamped but afloat, two brothers clinging to the hull, and hauled them aboard shivering and swearing gratitude in three languages. One of them pressed a crumpled handful of bills into Juniper's pocket before Juniper could refuse. Eleven dollars. Juniper kept it. That was the year they were having.

* * *

Port Calloway at dusk looked like a place that had been drowning slowly for so long it had made peace with it. Boardwalk planks gone soft as bread. Neon signs where only half the letters worked, so the fish cannery advertised itself as F SH and the bar next door promised DR NKS. The air smelled of diesel, brine, and fried something-or-other that Juniper had long ago stopped trying to identify.

They tied off the *Tern* at slip fourteen, same as always, and walked the long way around to the Voss Syndicate's office, which occupied the ground floor of a building that used to be a shipping concern and now, officially, was a "maritime financial services consultancy." Unofficially, everyone on the boardwalk knew what it was. Everyone knew, and everyone smiled at the people coming out of it, and everyone was very careful about what they said within earshot of its windows.

Marisol Vega was waiting inside, behind a desk arranged with the precision of an altar. She was small and neat and forty-ish, with reading glasses pushed up into silver-streaked hair and a cardigan the color of weak tea. She looked like someone's favorite aunt. She looked like she baked things. This was, Juniper understood, the entire architecture of her effectiveness.

"Juniper." Her smile arrived a half-second before her voice, like thunder after lightning, except inverted — the soft part first, so you'd relax right before the impact. "Sit. You're soaked. There's coffee. It's terrible, but it's hot."

Juniper sat. Didn't touch the coffee. "I've got data from today's cell. Rotation profiles, full inflow sounding—"

"I'm sure it's lovely data." Marisol folded her hands. Behind her, by the filing cabinets, stood a man Juniper knew only as Teodor, who was large in the way furniture is large, and who never spoke, and whose presence was itself a sentence being served. "But we're not here to talk about your data, are we? We're here to talk about the number."

The number. Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six dollars, and today it had grown. Juniper watched Marisol write the new figure on a card in neat, unhurried handwriting, and felt the walls of the room move inward a few inches.

"Seventy-two hours," she said, sliding the card across the desk. "That's what I've been authorized to offer you. Seventy-two hours to bring the balance under thirty-five, or the arrangement changes."

"The arrangement," Juniper repeated. Their voice came out flat. They'd learned flatness here, in this room, over eighteen months of these meetings. Flatness was armor. "Meaning what, specifically?"

Marisol took off her glasses and cleaned them on her cardigan, unhurried, giving the question all the time it deserved. "Meaning the *Tern* becomes ours, obviously. That's collateral; you signed for it. Meaning any equipment aboard becomes ours. Meaning—" and here she looked up, and her eyes were warm and terrible, "—meaning we start finding other uses for you. Uses that don't involve weather. I'd hate that, Juniper. Genuinely. You're one of my favorite accounts. You're interesting. Most of my accounts are just sad. You're *interesting*."

"That's the nicest threat anyone's ever made me."

"It's not a threat, it's a schedule." She smiled again, wider, and for a moment the smile didn't reach anywhere near her eyes, and then it did again, seamlessly, like a shutter opening. "I like you. That's why you're getting seventy-two hours instead of twenty-four. Do you understand the difference between me and the people I answer to? The people I answer to don't like anyone. They wouldn't have given you eighteen months of patience. They gave you eighteen months because I asked, and I asked because you're good at what you do, and what you do is rare, and rare things are worth keeping intact." She tapped the card once with one neat fingernail. "But intact is a condition, not a guarantee."

Teodor shifted his weight by the filing cabinets. The floor creaked. That was his entire contribution to the meeting, and it was enough.

Juniper picked up the card. Thirty-four thousand, nine hundred and change, due down to thirty-five in seventy-two hours. Which meant finding — what, seven thousand dollars in three days? From data sales that netted six hundred on a *good* day?

"I'm doing everything I can," Juniper said. "You know that. The storms this season have been—"

"Poor, yes. I follow the forecasts." Marisol tilted her head. "Isn't that funny? I follow the forecasts now. You did that. You made a debt collector care about convective outlooks." For one strange moment she sounded almost fond, almost human, and Juniper hated how much they wanted that fondness to be real. "Seventy-two hours, Juniper. Come back with a plan or come back with money. Those are the options. I'm rooting for you — I want you to know that. I'm genuinely rooting for you."

It was the *genuinely* that made Juniper's skin crawl on the way out. Because the horror of it, the thing that lived in Juniper's chest like a swallowed stone, was that Marisol probably did mean it. That was the design. The Voss Syndicate didn't send monsters for accounts like this one; monsters made people run, and running people can't be harvested. They sent smiling women with terrible coffee who rooted for you, who liked you, who remembered your birthday last year and had a cupcake brought out — God, the cupcake, Juniper still couldn't think about the cupcake — while the interest compounded and the walls moved in and the exits quietly disappeared one by one.

Owing money to people who smile while they threaten you was worse than owing money to people who hurt you. Hurt gives you something to push against. A smile gives you nothing. A smile makes the trap feel like a favor.

Outside, the boardwalk lights smeared in the drizzle. Juniper stood at the rail over black water and did the arithmetic again, hoping the numbers had changed, knowing they hadn't. Seven thousand dollars. Three days. Six hundred dollars per nearly-dying.

They could sell the radar unit. That was two thousand, maybe, and then no more chasing, no more income at all, a candle burning from both ends and the middle too. They could sell the boat outright to a third party before the syndicate took it — except the lien made that impossible, and anyway Marisol would know by nightfall; she always knew.

They could run. People ran. There was a dredge captain two slips down who'd run from Voss three years ago, and everyone spoke of him in the present tense out of politeness, and everyone knew he was at the bottom of the channel with new concrete shoes, though no one official had ever said so.

Juniper looked at the *Tern*, riding low and tired at slip fourteen, rain beading on her patched deck. Every scar on that boat was a day Juniper had refused to quit. It was the only thing they owned that had never lied to them.

"Seventy-two hours," they told it.

The boat said nothing. Boats were honest that way.

They were still standing there, doing the arithmetic against the dark, when footsteps came up the boardwalk behind them — unhurried, deliberate, the steps of someone who had been looking for them and was pleased to have found them. Juniper turned.

A man in a dry coat that cost more than the *Tern*'s engine stood under the flickering light, holding a leather folder the way other men held out a hand to shake.

"Juniper Okafor?" he said, smiling. "I think I might have a solution to your problem."
