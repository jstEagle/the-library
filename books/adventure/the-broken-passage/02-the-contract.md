## 2. The Contract

The man's name was Dessen, and he said it like a brand — *Dessen*, with the weight on the second syllable, as if he'd practiced making it sound expensive. He did not offer to shake hands. He offered the folder instead, holding it just far enough out that taking it meant stepping toward him.

Juniper didn't step. Not yet.

"You have me at a disadvantage," they said. "You know my name. I don't know who sent you."

"Does it matter?" Dessen smiled. Rain dripped off the boardwalk awning between them, ticking against the planks like a slow clock. "You owe the Voss Syndicate two hundred and forty thousand. You have seventy-two hours. I know this because I make it my business to know things about people in your particular situation." He tilted his head toward the *Tern*, tied down and shuddering under the storm shutters. "Nice boat. Older model. The storm-chasing rig is clever — third-party sensors, home-built telemetry. Clever people are usually worth talking to."

"Clever people are usually broke," Juniper said. "That's how you found me."

"That's how everyone finds everyone, these days." He let the folder drift a little closer. "May I?"

Juniper took it. Inside: a single sheet of heavy paper, printed on one side, no letterhead, no name. Just terms.

*SALVAGE CONTRACT — RECOVERY OF GOVERNMENT SURVEY EQUIPMENT*

*Location: Upper Kessari Delta, above the Serrano confluence.*

*Scope: All instruments, charts, data cores, and personal effects belonging to the Ministry Hydrographic Survey Team Seven, missing eleven days as of this date. Recovery of remains, if encountered, is discretionary but compensated.*

*Compensation: One hundred twenty thousand, half on departure, half on verified return of equipment.*

*Conditions: Contractor operates independently. No official channels. No press. Discretion absolute.*

One hundred twenty thousand. Half the debt, gone, in a single trip upriver. Juniper read the sheet twice, looking for the trap, because there was always a trap, and the better the numbers the worse the trap. This was arithmetic they knew by heart.

"Team Seven," Juniper said. "The survey that went quiet three weeks ago. The Ministry's been very quiet about it."

"The Ministry is often quiet."

"People say the river took them."

"People say many things." Dessen's smile didn't move. "The river takes boats. It rarely takes *equipment*. Instruments are built to survive flooding — sealed cases, buoyant mounts. If the team drowned, their gear is sitting on a sandbar somewhere waiting for someone with a boat and a sonar unit. You have both."

"And if they didn't drown?"

"Then you'll be recovering equipment from people who no longer need it." He said it lightly, the way a man flicks ash off a coat sleeve. "I won't insult you by pretending there's no risk. There's risk. That's why the pay is what it is. But you're a storm chaser, Okafor. Risk is your trade. You've simply been selling it to the wrong buyers."

Juniper looked at the paper again. *No official channels.* A government team goes missing, and the recovery contract comes through a fixer in a dry coat, off the books, no names. Either someone in the Ministry wanted this handled quietly — which meant embarrassment, scandal, something buried — or someone who wasn't the Ministry wanted the gear, and was using the missing-team story as wrapping paper.

Both possibilities were bad. Both paid one hundred twenty thousand.

"Who's the client?" Juniper asked.

"I represent interests," Dessen said. "Interests prefer anonymity. It's nothing personal — you'll notice the contract doesn't require you to trust me. It requires you to find equipment and bring it back. Trust is not among the deliverables."

"And if I find out what happened to them? Bodies, survivors, evidence of anything?"

"Discretion absolute," Dessen repeated, tapping the line with one finger. "You report what you recover. What you *learn* is yours to keep or discard as you see fit. Though—" and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost kind, which from a fixer was more frightening than a threat— "I'd advise discarding. Curious contractors develop complications. Compliant ones get paid. You seem like a man who wants to get paid."

"I'm not a man," Juniper said, flatly, and watched him absorb it without blinking, recalibrating nothing, because men like Dessen didn't care what you were, only what you owed.

"A person who wants to get paid," he amended smoothly. "Half up front. Cash transfer within the hour of signature. The other half on delivery — verified by inventory, not by faith."

Juniper stood there in the rain-smell and the lamplight and did the math again, slower this time. Half the debt cleared would buy them time — real time, months of it, enough to get the *Tern* back on contract chasing cells for the weather consortiums, enough to stop flinching every time a stranger smiled. And the alternative was Marisol's collectors, who did not offer contracts. They offered schedules.

It was a door opening. Juniper had spent enough years reading weather to know that a door opening in a storm can mean shelter, or it can mean the pressure differential that takes the roof off.

"Where's the boat?" they asked.

Dessen's smile widened a fraction. He'd known they'd say yes. He'd probably known before he left wherever dry, expensive place he'd come from. "This way."

* * *

The boat was moored two piers down, in the shadow of the customs house, under a tarp that had seen better decades.

Dessen pulled the tarp back with a flourish that the boat did not deserve. She was maybe nine meters, aluminum hull gone dull and patchy with age, riveted seams weeping orange rust-stains down her flanks like old scars. A cabin squat amidships, windows fogged with salt-haze. An outboard — an old Kestrel 40, the kind they stopped making when Juniper was a kid — hung off the transom on a bracket bent slightly to starboard. The name on the bow had been painted over twice; you could still read the ghosts of the older names underneath, like sediment layers.

"She doesn't look like much," Dessen admitted.

"She looks like a lawsuit," Juniper said, and stepped aboard anyway.

The deck flexed underfoot in a way decks shouldn't. Juniper walked it slowly, heel to toe, feeling for soft spots, listening to the hull answer each step. Forward of the cabin they crouched and pressed a palm flat against the plating near the waterline. Cold. Damp. They pulled back fingers dotted with moisture that seeped through a seam where the rivets had given up arguing with the river years ago.

"She leaks," Juniper said.

"All boats leak."

"These leak *differently*." Juniper moved aft, opened the transom locker. Inside: a hand bilge pump, its handle wrapped in fresh tape. Two buckets. A coil of patching rubber, self-curing, still in its wrapper. A small wooden mallet and a box of tapered softwood plugs, sorted by size.

They stared at the kit for a long moment. Then they laughed — one short breath, no humor in it.

"He knows," Juniper said, mostly to themselves. "Your client knows she leaks. He's patched the pump handles and packed plugs. He's not selling me a boat. He's selling me a *bail-out routine*."

Dessen, on the pier, spread his hands. "A working vessel for working conditions. The upper delta isn't kind to hulls. Snags, shallows, debris fields after every blow. Every boat up there takes water. The question is whether the crew manages it."

Juniper straightened up and gave the boat a longer look — a professional look now, the way they'd look at a cell building on the horizon. Bad structure, yes. But bad structure could be read. Could be *managed*, if you respected it. The hull was thick old-gauge aluminum, none of this modern eggshell stuff; she'd take a scrape that would gut a newer boat. The Kestrel 40 was a legendary engine precisely because it was simple — carbureted, air-cooled fins exposed, repairable with a leatherman and profanity. The bent bracket was a problem. The seeping seams were a problem. The soft deck forward was a problem.

Three problems. Manageable problems. The kind of boat you didn't trust so much as *babied* — ran her slow, bailed her often, never asked her for anything heroic, and she might, might, carry you two hundred kilometers upriver and back.

"She's seaworthy," Juniper said finally, "if babied. Which means whoever crews her has to be the kind of person who babies boats. Who's that supposed to be?"

"You," Dessen said. "Solo contract. The terms specify independent operation."

Of course they did. Solo meant no witnesses. Solo meant whatever happened up there happened to Juniper alone, and the story told afterward would be whatever story the client wanted told. Juniper filed that away next to the other bad signs, in the drawer marked *money*.

They dropped down into the cabin. Smelled of diesel and damp canvas and something older, fishier, the ghost smell of a hundred previous owners' catches. A chart table with a folding top. A bunk narrow as a coffin. A VHF set, older than the outboard, and — Juniper's eyebrows went up — a satellite phone bolted to the bulkhead, new, clean, its power cable still bright.

"The sat phone stays," Dessen said from the doorway. "Client's courtesy. So we can reach you. Check-ins every evening at eighteen hundred. Miss two check-ins and the second payment converts to a recovery fee — we send people to collect the equipment, and you. Nobody wants that."

"There it is," Juniper murmured. The leash. Of course there was a leash. They touched the phone once, lightly, the way you'd touch a snake to confirm it was a snake. "And if I use it to call someone other than you?"

"It makes and receives exactly two numbers," Dessen said. "Ours, and emergency services. We're generous, not stupid."

Juniper checked the fuel: two tanks, maybe sixty liters total, half of what the round trip needed. "Fuel?"

"Provisioned at Serrano Landing, last fuel dock before the upper delta. Account's already opened in the boat's name." He paused, and for the first time something almost human crossed his face — not warmth, but the memory of warmth, worn smooth like everything else about him. "Okafor. Look at me a moment."

Juniper looked.

"I deal in desperate people. It's most of my trade. And I'll tell you what I tell all of them, though few listen." He gestured at the rain coming down harder now, drumming the tarp he'd folded back, the gray river sliding past the pilings. "Desperation makes people sign things. But desperation also makes people *readable* — they grab the first rope thrown, no questions, and then they're surprised by where the rope leads. You don't strike me as surprised-by-ropes. So ask your questions now. Whatever they are. I'll answer what I can."

It was, Juniper thought later, the most dangerous thing he could have done. Honesty from a fixer is a lure dressed as a lantern.

One question rose above the others, and it wasn't about the money.

"The survey team," Juniper said. "Eleven days missing. Ministry's said nothing. No search parties that anyone's heard of. Families haven't spoken to press. That's not grief. Grief talks. That's *containment*." They stepped closer, boots wet on the deck plates. "So before I sign: is anyone up there alive? Because if I'm sailing into a crime scene, I want to know whose crime."

For a moment — a real moment, two heartbeats, maybe three — Dessen's composure shifted. Not guilt. Something smaller and more tired. The look of a man who had answered this question before, internally, and settled his accounts with it long ago.

"I genuinely don't know," he said. "That's the truth. My client tells me equipment recovery. The Ministry tells the client the team is missing. What's actually up that river—" he shrugged, a small, closed motion— "is above my clearance, and I am a man who made peace with above-my-clearance a long time ago. You want certainty, take a job forecasting weather. You want one hundred twenty thousand, take the boat."

The rain filled the silence between them.

Juniper looked at the boat. At the bail-out kit, thoughtful and grim. At the sat phone with its two permitted numbers. At the chart table where, somewhere beneath the folding top, the charts of the upper delta would be waiting — waters Juniper had never run, in a season when the rivers were already climbing.

Seventy-two hours. One hundred twenty thousand. Half the weight lifted off their chest, and the other half waiting, patient as water finding a crack.

Every instinct they owned — the same instincts that had kept them alive through twelve seasons of cells that killed better-funded chasers — said *trap*. Said the numbers were too clean, the boat too conveniently flawed, the leash too neatly fitted. Said that nobody pays half a debt's worth of money for rusty instruments unless the instruments aren't really what's being recovered.

But another voice, quieter, older, the voice of every night they'd lain awake doing arithmetic against the dark, said: *and what else is on offer?*

Marisol's smile. The collectors' schedule. The *Tern* sold out from under them piece by piece until even the debt had nothing left to attach to.

Juniper held out their hand.

"Pen," they said.

Dessen produced one from inside that dry, expensive coat, and as Juniper signed — the scratch of nib on heavy paper loud under the rain on the tarp — he watched with the mild satisfaction of a man closing a ledger.

"Departure at first light," he said. "Serrano Landing by tomorrow evening. Check-in at eighteen hundred." He tucked the contract away and paused at the gangway, one foot on the pier, and turned back with that smile that had started all of this. "One piece of advice, contractor, free of charge. The upper delta in flood season — the locals don't go up past the church spire at Serrano Narrows. Old superstition. Drowned parish, they say. If you hear stories, mind them or don't. But do me one favor."

"What's that?"

"When you find whatever's up there—" his eyes flicked, briefly, toward the cabin, toward the sat phone with its two permitted numbers— "decide quickly what you saw. Deciding slowly is how curious people become complications."

He tipped an imaginary hat and walked away down the pier, unhurried, deliberate, a dry man in a wet world.

Juniper stood alone on the deck of the unnamed boat, the contract's copy warm in their pocket, and listened to the rain come down. Sixty liters of fuel. A leaking hull. A leash disguised as a courtesy. Half a debt's weight promised against a river full of questions nobody wanted answered.

They looked north, upriver, where the dark was coming down over the water in sheets.

"Well," they said to the boat, because boats were honest and deserved honesty. "Let's see what you're worth."

The boat, being honest, said nothing. But below the waterline, in the dark, one of her old seams wept a thin bright thread of river into her belly — patient, steady, keeping its own count of the hours.

And forty meters from the pier, in a parked skiff with its running lights off, a woman Juniper had met only once — Marisol, smiling — watched the whole transaction through a pair of night glasses, and spoke three words into her own phone:

"He took it."
