## 3. Gear With No Tracks

The river changed character an hour past the last stilt village.

Juniper noticed it the way they noticed everything about water — not with their eyes first but with their hands on the wheel, a subtle thickening in the way the *Marrow* shouldered through the current. Upstream of the villages the delta had been a busy thing, braided and chattering, all its channels arguing with each other. Here it went quiet and wide and brown-green, carrying whole trees sideways like it was moving furniture in a house where nobody lived anymore. The banks pulled back into walls of mangrove and standing water, and the air took on a smell Juniper knew from a hundred storm chases: wet stone, rot, ozone. The smell of something old being uncovered.

They ran the boat slow. The fixer's contract had been specific about that — *no wake above the church reach*, which was an odd instruction to put in writing, and odder still because Juniper had looked at the charts and there was no church marked anywhere on this stretch. Just a bend, a drowned tributary mouth, and a coordinate.

Coordinates were honest, at least. Coordinates didn't smile at you the way Marisol had smiled, seventy-two hours ticking down behind her teeth.

Juniper checked the fuel gauge for the fourth time since dawn. Half a tank, if they babied it. The *Marrow* was a salvage skiff older than Juniper's career, patched in three places with epoxy and stubbornness, and she drank fuel the way debt drank everything else — steadily, without gratitude. Every liter burned upriver was a liter they'd have to earn back. That was the arithmetic Juniper had been running their whole adult life: everything cost, nothing paid, and the only question was what you could afford to lose today.

The GPS said four kilometers to the survey camp when the first piece of gear appeared.

It came floating downstream, snagged briefly against a half-submerged log — a hard-shell equipment case, government gray, latched shut. Juniper killed the engine and drifted alongside it. The case was dry inside when they hauled it up. Intact. A seismic data logger, by the stencil on the lid, serial number matching the manifest the fixer had given them. Not damaged. Not scavenged. Just — traveling.

Juniper sat with one hand on the case and one on the wheel and let the current carry them both, and thought about what it meant for a sealed, working instrument to be floating downriver toward civilization while its owners were nowhere to be found.

Either someone had dropped it overboard deliberately. Or the river had taken it.

Neither answer was good.

* *

The camp announced itself by smell before sight: diesel, canvas, and under it that same wet-stone rot, stronger here, like the ground itself was exhaling. Then the tents came around the bend — three of them, khaki and institutional, pitched on the highest shelf of the bank above a broad mudflat. A generator hummed somewhere behind the tree line, steady as a heartbeat. An antenna mast leaned against a dead palm, cable still running taut into the largest tent.

Juniper brought the *Marrow* in against the mudflat's edge and did not step off immediately. Old habit from chasing cells across country that didn't want you in it: you read a place before you entered it. You counted exits. You listened for what was missing.

What was missing was people. And what was present was everything else.

They waded the last few meters through shin-deep water warm as bathwater and stood dripping on the shelf, and the camp just went on existing around them. The nearest tent's flap was tied open. Inside, two cots, both slept-in, bedrolls thrown back like the occupants had stepped out for a minute. A folding table held a deck of cards mid-game — Juniper counted later, out of some compulsive need for order — twenty-three cards spread across a half-finished hand of something, a mug of coffee beside them with a skin on its surface so thick it had gone the color and texture of leather. Days old. More than days.

The second tent was the mess. Three plates on the table. One still had food on it — rice gone gray and swollen, a fish skeleton picked clean, flies doing their patient work. The other two were empty, pushed back neatly, chairs tucked. Someone had finished eating. Someone hadn't started. Nobody had washed anything.

The third tent was the operations room, and that was where the instruments were running.

Juniper stood in the entrance with rain starting to tick on the canvas overhead and felt the hair rise on their arms, and it wasn't fear exactly, or not only. It was the professional recognition of a thing that should not be. Every instrument in the tent was live. The seismic logger from the floating case had a twin here, screen glowing, trace scrolling. A water-level gauge chattered quietly to itself, printing a rising line on a paper drum. The radio base station's power light burned steady green. The battery bank read full charge. The solar array outside was angled correctly for the season. Everything maintained. Everything working. Everything waiting for hands that had stopped coming.

Eleven days, the fixer had said. Missing eleven days. In eleven days of monsoon humidity, unattended electronics died. Batteries drained, connections corroded, ants got into everything. But this gear had been kept alive, or had been left in perfect order, or —

Or someone was still keeping it.

"Hello?" Juniper said, and hated how loud their own voice was, how it fell flat into the canvas and died without echo. "Survey team! Anyone?"

The generator hummed. The printer arm swept. Somewhere out over the mudflat, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

Juniper spent two hours going through the camp the way they'd go through a wrecked chase vehicle — methodically, gently, cataloguing. It helped to have a task. Tasks kept the dread in its cage. They photographed everything with the cheap waterproof camera from their kit, because documentation was money now; the contract paid per item recovered and verified, and beyond that, if anyone ever asked questions about this place, Juniper intended to have answers that weren't just their own skin crawling.

The team had been six people, according to the manifest. Director Hale, who signed the contracts and did not appear in person. Four field surveyors. And a cartographer named Elias Wren, whose handwriting Juniper found on the chart table notes — small, precise, slanting slightly left, the letters of a man who drew maps for a living and couldn't help making even his grocery lists beautiful.

Six people. Six cots' worth of living, though the third tent only had four bunks; two of the surveyors had clearly been billeted in the mess tent, bedrolls visible behind a partition. Six sets of gear, mostly present. Boots by the tent doors. Rain shells on hooks. A rifle rack holding one rifle, unfired, bolt open and empty.

No bodies. No blood. No signs of struggle — and Juniper had seen struggle, had once spent a night in a ditch in Ossory County next to a chaser who'd argued with a tornado and lost, and struggle had a look, a smell, a violence of scattered things. This place had none of it. This place had the opposite of it. This place looked like six people had evaporated between one heartbeat and the next, leaving their coffee to skin over and their cards to wait.

* *

The mud told the story that the tents wouldn't.

Juniper found it late in the afternoon, when the light went copper and the rain paused, and they walked out onto the flat below the camp partly to check the boat's mooring and partly because they couldn't stand being inside the canvas any longer. The mudflat was a hundred meters of exposed grey-brown silt at low water, firm enough to walk on, soft enough to record. And it recorded beautifully.

There were footprints everywhere — six sets at least, maybe more, churned and overlapped in the zone between camp and waterline where people had loaded boats and hauled gear. Boot prints, hip-wader prints, the long drag-marks of equipment cases. All of it leading one direction.

Down. Toward the water.

Juniper walked the flat slowly, squinting, reading it the way they read cloud structure — looking for the pattern underneath the noise. Prints toward the waterline, dozens of them. Prints along the waterline, east and west, where people had paced or worked. And then—

Nothing.

That was the thing that stopped Juniper's breath. There were no prints coming back. No prints leading away from the waterline in any direction except back to the camp itself, and those were older, weathered soft, days-old at least. Whatever had happened on this mudflat, six people had walked down onto it, toward the river, and had not walked away. Not inland. Not along the bank. Not anywhere. The mud kept its records clean and complete, and the record ended at the water's edge like a sentence cut off mid-word.

Juniper stood at the waterline where the prints stopped and looked out at the brown-green channel, at the slow freight of debris riding the current, at the drowned tributary mouth yawning off to the northeast toward the coordinates on the contract.

People don't vanish. Juniper had chased storms for nine years and buried two friends and sat through more memorial services than weddings, and the one law they'd never seen broken was that people leave evidence. Water takes bodies, yes. Water takes boats. But water doesn't take six sets of footprints pointing home.

Unless they'd gone into the water on purpose. Unless they'd found something under it worth going into.

Juniper turned and looked back at the camp, at the antenna mast and the humming generator and the instruments faithfully logging data for no one, and understood, finally, why the fixer had written *no wake above the church reach*. Why the contract specified recovery of equipment but said nothing about personnel. Why the pay was enough to clear half a debt that had seemed designed never to clear.

This wasn't a salvage job. It was a retrieval of a scene before anyone else read it.

* *

Dusk came fast, the way it did in delta country — no twilight to speak of, just the light quitting like a debtor skipping town. Juniper made camp aboard the *Marrow*, tied double, anchor set, running lights off because running lights made you a target and something about this stretch of river already felt watched. They ate a ration bar they couldn't really afford and drank rain caught in a tarp, and told themselves the tightness in their chest was just the debt, just the usual animal arithmetic, seventy-two hours minus one.

The chart table in the operations tent kept pulling at them.

Juniper had saved it for last, professionally, the way you save the locked room. Now, with the flashlight in their teeth, they went back ashore one more time and stood over it. The table was a proper surveyor's chart table, glass-topped, and under the glass lay the working charts of the survey — bathymetric strips of the channel, annotated in Wren's beautiful left-slanting hand, depth soundings in neat columns, and here and there small circles inked around anomalies with question marks beside them. A dozen circles. Two dozen. Clustered strangely, Juniper thought, not random at all — clustered along a line, as if the anomalies themselves formed a shape, a thread running northeast from the camp toward the drowned tributary and beyond, off the edge of the chart.

On top of the glass, centered on the table with a precision that raised the hair on Juniper's neck, sat a brass compass.

It was old. Older than the survey, older than the republic probably — heavy in the hand, its casing worn smooth and dark at the edges where thumbs had worried it for decades, a hairline crack across the crystal mended so finely you'd miss it unless you were looking. No maker's mark. No government stencil. Nothing about it belonged to this camp, and yet it sat at the exact center of the chart table like a paperweight placed by someone with strong feelings about symmetry.

Juniper picked it up. The needle swung, settled, steadied.

Pointing south-southeast. Downriver. Back the way Juniper had come — no. Juniper checked twice, turning in place, watching the needle hold its bearing relative to the world. Not south. Not north. Downriver, toward the deep channel, toward the drowned tributary mouth, toward the line of circled anomalies marching off the edge of Wren's chart.

Compasses pointed north. That was the entire deal with compasses. Juniper had navigated by magnetic north through whiteout mesocyclones and hail that could kill a horse, and every compass they'd ever trusted had pointed north, and this one pointed downriver with the calm insistence of a thing that had never once considered lying.

They set it back on the glass, dead center, exactly where it had been, and immediately wished they hadn't, because now the chart table looked like an altar and the compass looked like an offering and Juniper Okafor did not believe in altars.

But they believed in patterns. And the pattern was this: six people had walked into the water and not walked out. Their instruments were still running, tended or untouched. Their work pointed northeast in a straight line of questions. And someone — one of them, or whoever came after — had left a broken old compass pointing at the same spot on the map where all the question marks gathered, centered, deliberate, like a message for whoever came looking.

Like a message for a salvager with debts.

Juniper stood a long time in the dark tent with the rain starting again on the canvas, drumming, patient, and felt the dread and the hope in them lean against each other like two prisoners sharing a wall. Because here was the truth they kept circling: a normal salvage job paid a fraction of this. A normal missing-team job came with search parties, official inquiries, families asking questions. This job came with silence, and silence was expensive, and somebody was paying Juniper to keep it.

And yet. Half the debt. Half. Enough to buy back seventy-two hours and then some, enough to sleep a whole night without calculating interest. The door was right there, and doors that opened this easily were either gifts or mouths.

Outside, the river moved its freight of trees in the dark. The instruments logged their data for no one. And on the chart table, under the glass, the brass compass pointed steadily downriver toward whatever the survey team had found at the bottom of the world — pointing, Juniper thought as they clicked off the flashlight, like it was inviting them.

In the morning, they would follow it.
