## 4. The Compass Lies South

Juniper didn't sleep so much as hover above sleep, the way a boat hovers above its anchor chain — connected, but never resting. The survey camp sat behind them in the gray morning like a sentence left unfinished: tents zipped against weather no one had stayed to feel, instruments humming on their tripods, a kettle of tea gone cold and thick with mosquitoes. And no tracks out. That was the part that kept surfacing between breaths. Mud holds everything. Mud had held Juniper's own boots to the ankle when they'd stepped off the boat yesterday, had recorded every step they took toward that camp in perfect, damning detail — and it had recorded nothing leaving.

They made coffee on the boat's single burner and watched the river do what rivers do, which is pretend to be patient.

The compass sat on the chart table where Juniper had left it, centered, deliberate, the way you leave something for someone to find. Brass casing gone green at the seams, glass scratched into a fine web by years of pockets and grit. The needle lay steady under the glass, pointing not north but downriver, southeast by Juniper's reckoning, straight down the channel toward the drowned country where the maps went soft and the government's ink gave up.

"Okay," Juniper said to it. "Let's find out what kind of liar you are."

They started with the obvious. Their phone's compass app agreed with itself, which proved nothing except that phones are confident creatures. The boat's handheld GPS showed true north without complaint. So Juniper set the brass compass beside the GPS on the chart table and rotated the whole arrangement ninety degrees, then one-eighty, watching for lag, for drag, for any sign the old needle was simply stuck or magnetized wrong.

It wasn't stuck. When Juniper turned the compass, the needle swung freely, smoothly, and settled back — always back — onto the same bearing. Southeast. Downriver. It found its heading again within two seconds every time, which meant it was responding to something, and whatever it was responding to was strong enough to argue with the entire planet.

Juniper had chased storms for eleven years. They knew magnetic weirdness the way sailors know weather — iron in the hills, basalt dikes, the occasional ore body that made a compass drunk. But this was delta country. Silt and clay and water, mile after mile after mile, nothing under the river but more river. There was no geology here that should have been shouting louder than the earth's own field.

Unless the thing doing the shouting wasn't geological.

They ate breakfast standing up, because sitting still felt like agreeing to be afraid. Then Juniper did what they always did when a mystery got too large to hold in their hands: they broke it into measurements.

The bearing from the camp was 143 degrees. They plotted it on the hydrographic chart the fixer had provided — a government sheet, three years out of date, the delta drawn in careful blue lines that the river had already rewritten. The line ran down the main channel past the collapsed ferry landing, past the mangrove shoals, and terminated — Juniper tapped the pencil twice to be sure — at a point marked with a small, obsolete symbol. A cross with a circle around it.

A church.

The chart note read, in faded type: *SPIRE SUBMERGED 1931 FLOOD. HAZARD TO NAVIGATION.*

Juniper stared at it for a long moment. Somewhere out there, under six meters of brown water, stood the top of a church that had drowned before either of their parents was born, and a hundred-year-old compass recovered from an abandoned camp wanted to walk straight to it.

"That's not creepy at all," they told the coffee, and drank it anyway.

* * *

The run downriver took forty minutes, and Juniper spent all of them talking to themselves, which was a habit from storm work — narrating conditions aloud kept panic from filling the silence. *Wind out of the east-southeast, fifteen knots, building. Water level up maybe ten centimeters since yesterday; monsoon's early this year. Fuel at three-quarters. Hull seeping at the garboard seam, pump cycling every four minutes, watch it.* The boat was named the *Half Promise*, which the fixer had not chosen and Juniper refused to change, because it was the most honest thing anyone had ever said about their life.

The spire announced itself as a ripple first. Then a dark shape under the surface, like a whale's back that never breathed. Then, as Juniper throttled down and let the boat drift, it resolved: a stone point breaking the water by less than half a meter, crusted white with bird droppings, leaning perhaps five degrees off true. Around it, the river slid past in long brown muscles, and the current bent — visibly bent — around the submerged mass below, the way water bends around a piling.

Juniper killed the engine and held up the compass.

The needle didn't waver. It pointed at the spire the way a dog points at a bird: total commitment, no ambiguity, tail and eyes and spine all saying *there*. Juniper walked it around the deck — bow, starboard, stern, port — and the needle tracked the spire through the full circle, swinging smoothly to keep its fix, correcting instantly whenever Juniper stopped moving.

"It's not pointing south," Juniper said slowly. "It's pointing *at* it."

That was worse. A compass that lied about north was broken. A compass that pointed at a specific thing was *answering a question*, and Juniper hadn't asked it anything.

They anchored — carefully, upstream of the spire, with a scope of line that would keep them clear of the hazard — and got out the dive gear. This was the part of the job they'd actually been paid for: locate equipment, assess recovery. The contract said gear. The contract did not say churches. But the contract also didn't say anything about camps with no footprints, and Juniper had learned a long time ago that the gap between what a job says and what a job is, is exactly where people drown.

So they checked their gear twice. Tank pressure, regulator, weights, knife, dive light even though it was midmorning — because if there was a hole in the world under that spire, Juniper intended to see into it before it saw into them.

The water hit like a cold hand. Visibility was a meter, then half a meter, then nothing but amber murk and drifting silt, the dive light reduced to a pale coin held at arm's length. Juniper followed the anchor line down, equalizing, counting depth — three meters, five — and the bottom came up suddenly, a shelf of silted stone.

The church roof.

Tiles still clung to the ridge in places, furred with algae, and the silt had drifted against the slopes like snow against a barn. Juniper swam the ridge line, gloved hand trailing along the tiles, and the structure beneath them felt enormous — not a chapel but a proper church, built by people who expected to be remembered. At the eastern end, where the roof sloped down toward what would have been the apse, the tiles ended in a ragged break.

And below the break, where the ceiling should have continued, there was dark.

Not shadow-dark. *Opening*-dark. Juniper swept the light across it and caught the edge of dressed stone — a carved arch, blocks fitted without mortar, the kind of work that predates the colonial maps by centuries. The archway gaped downward and inward, its lower half choked with silt, its upper half breathing a current Juniper could feel against their face mask: a slow, steady outflow of cooler water, like the building was exhaling.

Something about that current made the hair stand up on Juniper's arms inside the wetsuit. Rivers don't breathe. Rivers push. For water to flow steadily *out* of a submerged structure meant there was volume behind it — space, air pressure, a system. Meant the church wasn't just drowned.

It was connected.

Juniper hovered at the arch for a full minute, light playing into a darkness the beam couldn't finish, and thought about footprints that ended. Thought about eleven missing people whose instruments were still running, whose kettle was still on the table, who had walked into the delta and then, apparently, walked *underneath* it.

Every instinct they owned said go back up, radio it in, get paid, get clear.

But the debt had other opinions, and debts always speak last. Half the balance. Seventy-two hours. Marisol's smile, which was the worst thing about her — the genuine warmth in it, the way she seemed to actually like Juniper while explaining what would happen if the numbers didn't move. Salvage meant salvage. If there was government equipment down there — sonar units, survey gear, hard drives — it was worth money, and money was the only language Juniper currently spoke fluently.

They marked the arch on the slate strapped to their wrist, shot a compass bearing from the spire, noted the depth, and ascended.

* * *

Back on deck, dripping, they keyed the satellite radio and called it in. The check-in protocol from the contract was simple: report position, report findings, await instructions. The voice on the other end belonged to a man called Deverell, who represented the ministry office that had posted the salvage notice, and who had the flat, unhurried cadence of someone reading from a script he'd written himself.

"*Half Promise*, this is Control. Go for your report."

Juniper gave position, gave conditions, gave the state of the camp — intact, abandoned, no sign of struggle, no tracks out. A pause on the line, long enough to have texture.

"And the instruments?"

"Still running. Data loggers active. Whatever happened, they didn't pack out."

Another pause. "Understood. Continue."

Juniper looked at the compass lying on the chart table, needle steady as a heartbeat, pointing at the water over the arch. Here it was, then. The moment where the job either became a job or became something else.

"One more item," Juniper said. "Found a brass compass at the camp. Old piece, survey-era. Except it doesn't point north. I've tested it against GPS and a known bearing — it's holding a fixed line on the drowned church spire at the marked hazard, southeast of the camp. I dove the location this morning." They made themselves say the next part plainly, no drama, because drama would make it easier to dismiss. "There's a breached archway in the structure below the waterline. Dressed stone, pre-colonial construction by the look of it. There's a measurable outflow current, which means significant void space behind it. I believe the survey team found an entrance to a submerged passage system, and I believe that compass is pointing at the door."

Silence. Not the thoughtful silence of a man considering — the *mechanical* silence of a transmission being monitored by more than one person. Juniper could almost hear the room on the far end changing shape.

When Deverell spoke again, his cadence had changed. Flatter. Faster. The script was gone.

"*Half Promise*, new instructions. Secure nothing further. Do not dive the site again. Leave the compass where found — return it to the camp table, exact position — and return to port immediately. Empty-handed. You will be compensated for time and fuel at the agreed day rate plus twenty percent."

Juniper sat down slowly on the chart bench. "Say again? You're canceling the salvage?"

"The salvage operation is concluded. Confirm you copy."

"I copy," Juniper said. "Help me understand, though. The contract says recover equipment. The equipment is sitting right there. The team is missing, the monsoon is coming in early, and you're telling me to put a compass back on a table and row home?"

"You are instructed," Deverell said, each word placed like a brick, "to leave the compass and come back empty-handed. Confirmation of compliance is required within the hour. Further noncompliance will be noted in your file."

"My file," Juniper repeated. "Deverell, I don't have a file. I'm a contractor off a bulletin board."

A pause. When the voice came back, it had dropped the bureaucratic mask entirely, and what was underneath was almost gentle, which was somehow the most frightening register yet.

"Mr. Okafor. Ms. Okafor. Listen to me. Some things on that river cost more than they pay. Take the day rate. Take the twenty percent. Go home." A beat. "This frequency will not be monitored after eighteen hundred hours."

The line clicked dead.

Juniper sat with the handset in their hand for a long time, listening to the static where a human voice had been, and felt the shape of the thing finally press against the inside of the story. Because here was what they knew now, assembled from pieces that had seemed harmless alone:

A government survey team had vanished so completely that they'd left their instruments running and their tea poured. Someone had posted a public salvage contract to recover their gear — generous pay, fast timeline, no questions. And the moment Juniper reported the actual finding, the same office had ordered them to abandon it, restore the scene, and forget it, with a compensation bump that wasn't gratitude. It was a *price*. Twenty percent was what you paid a witness to stop being one.

You didn't pay people to stop looking for something unless finding it was dangerous to you.

Juniper looked at the compass. It lay on the chart table, green-brassed, patient, its needle aimed through the hull, through the brown water, through the breathing arch, at whatever was down there in the dark with eleven missing people — or with whatever remained of them.

The instructions were simple. Put it back. Row away. Take the money.

Juniper picked it up. It was warm, though it had been sitting in shade, though the morning was cool, though there was no reason on earth for a brass compass to hold heat like a held hand. The needle pressed against the underside of its glass, straining downriver, wanting to go.

"Twenty percent," Juniper said aloud, to no one. "He offered me twenty percent to un-know this."

Outside, far to the east, the sky had begun to organize itself — clouds stacking into towers with that particular bruised architecture Juniper had spent a decade learning to read. The monsoon, arriving ahead of schedule, like everything else in this story.

Juniper opened the chart drawer, took out the waterproof case, and put the compass inside it. Not back on the camp table. Not in the river. In the case, next to their own instruments, where it could lie to them both equally.

Then they started the engine and turned the *Half Promise* downriver — toward the spire, toward the arch, toward the bearing the needle had been holding all along.

If someone was willing to pay extra for this to stay lost, then lost was the one place it had value. And Juniper Okafor had seventy-two hours, half a debt, and a lifetime of experience knowing exactly what it meant when powerful people smiled and told you to go home.

It meant the real cargo was still aboard.
