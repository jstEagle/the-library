## 5. Monsoon Warning

Eleven days.

Elias Wren had stopped counting them on his wrist in ink after the fourth day, because the numbers had started to feel like a countdown instead of a record — like something being subtracted from him rather than added to. Now he counted the way a cartographer counts anything: by features. By landmarks of the dark.

There was the ledge where he slept, a shelf of old coral limestone above the waterline, dry enough to keep the shivering at bay if he curled tight and tucked his hands into his armpits. There was the drip line, three meters to his left, where the ceiling wept a slow bead of groundwater into a pool that never seemed to rise or fall — a metronome, one drop every eleven seconds when the river outside was calm, faster when it rained. He had timed it against his watch until the watch died on day six, and now he timed it against himself, against his own pulse, and the pulse was always faster than the drip and that was how he knew he was still afraid.

Fear, he had decided somewhere around day eight, was not the problem. Fear was fuel. The problem was what fear did to time — how it stretched an hour into a season, how it made a man want to *do* something, anything, even if the anything got him killed. He had watched that impulse kill better men than himself. He had watched it kill Ada, though she would have argued the word *kill* all night, would have said she chose her ground, would have been right, God help him, right as she always was, and dead anyway.

So he practiced patience the way other men practiced scales. He sat on his ledge in the dark and he named things.

The passage system, for instance. He could have drawn it from memory with his eyes closed, which was fortunate, because paper was a liability now and he knew it. The main gallery ran east-northeast beneath the drowned church, its vaulted ceiling lost somewhere above the black water in a darkness so complete it felt architectural, built rather than found. Three side branches off the main gallery: the low crawl they'd nicknamed the Keyhole, half-flooded even then; the chimney shaft that went up toward the roots of some enormous riverside tree, breathing cool air on rainy nights; and the long southern run that sloped down into flooded sump and kept going, according to Ada's soundings, farther than any of them had dared follow.

Ada's soundings. He caught himself doing this — thinking in pairs, in *we* — and let the thought pass through him without stopping, the way you let a cramp pass through a muscle. Grief was a thing you metabolized or it metabolized you. Eleven days in, he was still deciding which.

He ate a quarter of a ration bar. Peanut-flavored, technically. It tasted like sweetened chalk and he chewed it slowly, deliberately, making it last, because hunger was another instrument and he intended to keep it calibrated. Four bars left after this. Rainwater he had plenty of — the drip line filled a dented mess tin overnight, and he'd learned to skim the surface scum off with a strip of cloth before drinking. His stomach had settled into a low constant complaint, like a dog too well-trained to bark.

The dark did not bother him anymore. That surprised him. He had expected the dark to be the enemy, but the dark turned out to be honest. It didn't pretend. It was the light he no longer trusted — the flare of a flashlight beam down a gallery, the white slash of a boat's running lamp through water. Light meant someone was looking. And someone was looking for him.

He knew because they came.

The first time had been day two, or maybe three — he'd still been counting in ink then. Voices above, distorted through stone and water, and then a diver's light sweeping past the submerged archway like a searchlight through fog, close enough that Elias had slid off his ledge into the pool without a splash and hung there in the cold, face up under a shelf of rock, watching the beam wander the ceiling overhead while his lungs burned and his heart slammed and some cold clerk in the back of his mind noted, calmly, *air pocket at ten o'clock, thirty seconds' swim, you can make it.* The light moved on. He'd surfaced gasping into a silence that felt like a held breath.

They came again on day five. Two boats, engines idling, and voices — he'd heard enough to know they weren't police. Police announced themselves. These men spoke in the low, unhurried tones of people doing a chore, and one of them had laughed at something, and the laugh had told Elias more than any interrogation. You don't laugh while searching for a missing man unless you already know how the file reads. Unless the missing man is a formality.

Day nine, they'd come with sonar. He'd felt it before he understood it — a pressure in his back teeth, a faint ticking in the water, pings arriving at intervals like a slow mechanical heartbeat crawling along the channel outside. They were mapping the entrances. He'd lain flat on the limestone with his ear to the stone and counted the pings as they passed, north to south, and then, forty minutes later, south to north, slightly offset, and he had recognized the pattern with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with authorship.

It was their own survey protocol. Grid-offset acoustic sweep, two passes, forty-minute interval. Hale had written it. Hale had made Elias drill the team on it in a conference room in the capital until they could recite the parameters in their sleep. Whoever was running that sonar was running it out of Hale's playbook, which meant either Hale's people were searching for a body — or Hale's people were making sure of one.

Elias lay in the dark and thought about Director Hale's voice on the radio, eleven days ago, saying *stand by* in a tone that had sounded like patience and had actually been a decision being made somewhere above his pay grade. He thought about the grinding boom he'd felt through the stone that same evening, deep and final, dust sifting down through the drip water for an hour afterward. The Keyhole entrance. Sealed. Not collapsed — *sealed*, placed, engineered, because a collapse is random and Hale did not tolerate randomness.

Two surveyors had objected. Elias had been one. The other had been Ada.

He did not let himself finish the sentence. He rolled the arithmetic around instead, the way he used to roll a pencil between his fingers: four ration bars, one mess tin, one dead watch, one flare gun with a single cartridge he'd taken from the survey kit on the worst night of his life. One route out — the southern run, the deep sump, Ada's sounding line — except the monsoon was coming and the monsoon would raise the river and raise the water in the galleries with it, and the mathematics of that were simple and merciless. Every centimeter the river rose was a centimeter less air. He had perhaps a week of air pockets left. Less, if the rains came early.

Patience, he told himself. Patience and arithmetic. A man could live on those a long time, if the arithmetic held.

The drip fell. One. Two. Three.

He named the sounds of the cavern the way he named everything else, because naming was how he kept the fear from curdling into panic. Water moving in the main gallery — normal, tidal breathing, the whole system inhaling and exhaling with the river. Stone settling somewhere far off — normal, thermal creak, louder at dusk. The soft wet slap of something small crossing the pool — a frog, probably, or a fish stranded by high water; life persisted down here in ways the surveys hadn't predicted, and he took an unreasonable comfort in that. Even drowned places stayed inhabited.

Then, on the eleventh day, near what he judged to be midmorning — the cavern had no dawn, but his body kept one anyway, a gray ghost-light behind his eyes — he heard a new sound.

An engine.

Not the heavy diesel idle of the patrol boats. Something smaller, higher, working hard against current — a shallow-draft outboard, single engine, pushed to its limit. It came up the channel from the south, and Elias tracked it by ear the way he'd once tracked thunderstorms across the delta as a boy, lying in the grass of his grandmother's compound, counting the gap between flash and sound. The engine grew, passed almost directly overhead — he could feel the vibration come down through the limestone, through the water, into the shelf beneath him, a fine tremor in his back teeth — and then it slowed.

And stopped.

Silence poured back into the space the engine had occupied, and the silence was worse. Engines passing were weather. Engines stopping were intention.

He slid off the ledge without a sound, into water so cold it closed around his chest like a hand, and pulled himself beneath the overhang where he'd survived the first search. From here he could see, across eight meters of black water, the faint gray suggestion of the submerged archway — the breach in the church foundation wall that the flood years had opened, the wound in the world that Ada had found first, because Ada found everything first. If a diver came through that archway, Elias would see the light before the light saw him. It wasn't much. It was what he had.

Minutes passed. The drip counted them out. One drop every nine seconds now — faster than baseline, which meant rain somewhere above, rain in the hills, the leading edge of something large. His skin prickled. He'd grown up on monsoon seasons; he could feel one coming through stone the way sailors felt a storm in their joints. When it arrived in earnest, the river would climb, the galleries would drown, and the clock that had been running quietly inside his chest since day one would begin to audibly tick.

A rope splashed. He heard it clearly — the wet whip-crack of a line hitting water, then the scrape of a hull against stone, then boots on rock, muffled, above and to the north. Someone had moored a boat directly over the church ruins. Someone was getting out.

Elias pressed himself deeper into the overhang and did the thing that eleven days had trained into him, the thing that had replaced every instinct he'd arrived with: he went still. Completely still. Breath shallow, hands open, mind open, listening past his own heartbeat the way you listen past wind for a footstep.

Voices. One voice only, it seemed — talking to itself, or to a radio, low and indistinct through the stone. Then a longer silence. Then a sound that raised every hair on his body despite the cold:

A splash. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

Someone was swimming the flooded gallery.

The gray shape of the archway stayed empty. The swimmer was coming from the north approach, then — the chimney side, the route through the tree roots, the route Elias had catalogued and measured and drawn and never once swum himself because the ceiling pinched shut underwater for nearly four meters and he was not the swimmer Ada had been.

Ada had been. Ada had mapped that pinch by feel, on a dare, laughing, coming up on the far side with river weed in her hair and the whole team cheering.

The swimming sounds stopped. Water dripped. Somewhere in the black galleries of the drowned church, footsteps — real ones now, on stone, squelching, cautious — began to move through the dark toward him.

Elias drew the flare gun from the plastic bag at his waist and held it against his chest, one cartridge, one chance, and made himself a promise older than the survey, older than the debt, older than the map that had gotten everyone hurt: *not today.* Whatever came out of that dark — Hale's divers, Okonjo's quiet professionals, the syndicate's smiling collectors — it would find nothing. No breath, no shape, no man. It would find a ledge, a mess tin, a drip line, and a darkness that gave up nothing, because Elias Wren had spent eleven days becoming part of this place, patient as water, and water does not announce itself.

The footsteps came closer. Ten meters. Eight.

The drip fell. Faster now. Nine seconds. Eight.

Rain, he thought, in the hills. The monsoon, arriving ahead of schedule, filling the sky above the church, filling the river, filling the galleries — and somewhere in that rising black water, a stranger who did not know these passages was swimming blind toward a starving man with one flare and nothing left to lose.

Six seconds between drops.

Elias Wren raised the flare gun and waited for the light.
