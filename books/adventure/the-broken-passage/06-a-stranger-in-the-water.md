## 6. A Stranger in the Water

The spire came up out of the river like a finger accusing the sky.

Juniper cut the engine a hundred meters out and let the boat drift, watching it through the rain-smeared windshield. It had been a church once — you could still read the bones of it, the buttresses drowned to their shoulders, the bell arch open and empty. Now only the top three meters of stone stood above the brown water, furred with moss, streaked white by decades of bird droppings. The compass on the chart table beside Juniper pointed at it the way a dog points at a rabbit: steady, certain, indifferent to everything else in the world.

They'd been arguing with themselves about it for two days.

"Nobody leaves a compass behind," Juniper said aloud, because the boat was small and silence made the leak sound louder. "Nobody leaves a whole camp behind. Nobody eats half a meal." The government's radio order sat in their memory like a fishhook: *Leave the compass. Come back empty-handed.* Empty-handed meant unpaid. Unpaid meant Marisol's seventy-two hours kept burning down, and Marisol did not extend credit, she extended funerals.

Juniper killed the drift with a short burst of reverse thrust and began rigging the dive gear they'd found in the survey camp's equipment locker — regulator serviced recently, tank at two-thirds, mask scratched but clear. Government gear. The kind of thing that got issued to people who were supposed to come back.

The water was the color of steeped tea and about as transparent. Juniper tested it with a weighted line first: eight meters to something solid near the spire's base, then nothing — no bottom, just depth going down into black. That was wrong for this stretch of the delta. The charts said four meters, silt and sandbar. Eight meters of water over nothing meant a hole. Holes in delta country meant old channels, collapsed cellars, wells — or doors.

The compass lay on the chart table, needle trembling with the boat's motion but never wavering from its bearing. Pointing downriver. Pointing here. Pointing, if Juniper was honest about the geometry, *down*.

"You're not a compass," Juniper told it. "You're a lure."

It kept pointing.

Juniper checked the radio one more time — static, the storm band building to the east, the monsoon's leading edge still a day out but already stacking clouds like furniture against a door — then shrugged into the harness and went over the side feet-first.

* * *

Underwater, the world became sound and pressure.

Visibility was a meter, maybe less. Juniper descended hand over hand along the spire itself, gloves dragging over stone that shifted from dressed blocks to rougher work as they went down — colonial masonry giving way to something older, something that had been old when the colonists arrived. At six meters the light turned the color of bruise. At seven, Juniper's trailing hand found an edge where there shouldn't have been one: a lip of carved stone, a molding, the ghost of an archway laid into the spire's base like a sealed throat.

And the seal was broken. The keystone had been pried or blasted loose — the fracture faces were pale, unweathered, recent — and beyond it yawned a gap wide enough for a body, exhaling cold water from somewhere deeper. Juniper hung there in the murk, regulator hissing, and felt the current. Not the river's lazy surface roll. A directed pull, steady as breath, drawing inward and slightly up.

Air on the other side. Air meant a chamber. A chamber meant whoever had broken this seal had gone through it, and whatever the compass wanted Juniper to find was in there with them.

Every instinct Juniper owned, the ones that had kept them alive through eleven years of chasing storms across three continents, said *turn around*. Storm chasers survived by respecting the difference between data worth having and holes worth dying in.

But storm chasers also knew that the moment you most wanted to turn around was usually the moment the story started.

Juniper went through.

* * *

Elias Wren heard the engine die and counted to six hundred.

He'd learned the rhythm of it over eleven days. Boats passed on the main channel all the time — fishing crews, the patrol launches, once a barge riding so low it seemed to be drowning in slow motion. But the boats that mattered slowed first. They'd throttle down a quarter mile out, drift, go quiet. Then men would come to the spire in masks and lights, and Elias would press himself into the deep crack between two fallen vault ribs with his flare gun held against his chest and count, because counting kept him from thinking about how long he could hold still before his shaking gave him away.

Six hundred seconds. Sometimes they left in two hundred. Once they'd stayed past nine hundred, and he'd bitten through the skin of his own lip keeping quiet, and the taste of blood had been almost comforting because it proved he was still a body and not just a fear wearing one.

This time the wait was shorter. Splashes — two, maybe three entries. Then a long silence, longer than swimming to the spire should take.

Then, faintly, carried up through the flooded gallery in that strange way water had of delivering sound like a letter: a regulator's hiss, coming closer. Coming *in*.

Elias's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Nobody came in. In eleven days, none of them had come in. They searched the spire, they probed the outer galleries with hooks and sonar pings that made his teeth ache, but they never entered the passage — because Hale's own survey notes marked the inner sections unstable, and Hale did not send men places he couldn't guarantee retrieving them from. Whoever was swimming in now was either insane or —

Or sent by someone who didn't care if they came back.

Elias moved. His body knew the route now the way it knew its own heartbeat: along the rib of fallen masonry, down into the flooded corridor, to the pocket where the air dome met the water and the passage narrowed enough that anyone surfacing would have to do it right in front of him. He crouched on a submerged ledge, water to his chest, and raised the flare gun with both hands because one hand wasn't steady anymore.

The bubbles came up silver in the dark. A head broke the surface.

* * *

Juniper came up into blackness and warm dead air and a voice like a blade being drawn.

"Don't move."

The voice cracked on the last word, which was the only reason Juniper didn't move anyway — cracks like that belonged to people at the end of their rope, and people at the end of their rope fired flares indoors without doing the math on ricochets. Juniper lifted the mask onto their forehead and went very still, treading water, eyes straining.

A shape resolved out of the dark. A man, standing chest-deep on a ledge, holding a flare gun in a two-handed grip that would have been textbook if his arms weren't shivering. He was gaunt the way famine is gaunt — cheekbones like architecture, beard grown wild, clothes hanging off him like flags of surrender. Eleven days of ration bars, Juniper thought. Maybe less food than that.

"Elias Wren?" Juniper said.

The flare gun didn't waver. "Who sent you?"

"The question I was going to ask you."

"Hale?"

"I've never met Hale. I'm not government." Juniper kept their voice low and even, the tone they used talking people out of cars in rising floodwater. "I'm a contractor. Salvage job. Recover the survey team's equipment." A beat. "You're the equipment, apparently. Or you were supposed to be."

Something moved behind the man's eyes — not softening, recalculating. "Contractor." He said the word like he was tasting it for poison. "Hale uses contractors. Deniable people. People whose disappearances don't generate paperwork."

"That's flattering."

"It's not flattery. It's how he thinks." The flare gun stayed level, but the man's breathing was ragged now, the adrenaline spending itself. "Turn out your pockets. Slowly."

Juniper did. Into the water-light they brought out, one at a time: a dive light, a waterproof notebook, a folding knife — the man's jaw tightened at that one — and finally, folded into a plastic sleeve, the document Juniper had been carrying against their chest since port.

"What is that?"

"My debt notice." Juniper held it up. "Voss Syndicate. Forty-one thousand, seven hundred and change, plus interest accruing daily. Seventy-two hours ago it was ninety-six hours. You want to see the payment schedule? It's very thorough. There's a section about what happens to my boat."

The man stared at the paper. Whatever he'd expected — a badge, a government ID, Hale's face on a warrant — it wasn't this. "You're showing me your debts."

"You asked who sent me. Nobody sent me. I'm upriver because owing money to the Voss Syndicate is a slow-motion way of dying, and this salvage contract was the only exit on offer." Juniper tucked the notice away. "If I were Hale's, I'd have shown you something official. That's the whole point of official. So." They spread empty hands. "Starving man with a flare gun in a flooded church basement. Contractor with a debt notice. Which of us sounds like the trap?"

A long silence. Somewhere above them, muffled through meters of stone and water, thunder rolled — the monsoon clearing its throat.

Then the flare gun lowered, an inch, then another, until the man simply let his arm fall, too tired to hold it up anymore. He sat down heavily on the ledge, water lapping at his shoulders, and put his free hand over his face.

"Eleven days," he said, from behind his hand. "Eleven days I've been counting boats."

* * *

They got him up onto the dry shelf where he'd been living — a vaulted pocket above the flood line, littered with ration wrappers stacked with obsessive neatness, a rain-catchment sheet rigged from torn tent fabric, and a wall covered in pencil.

Juniper played the dive light across the wall and stopped breathing for a second.

Charts. Hand-drawn, obsessively detailed, covering every flat surface the man could reach: the passage system rendered in elevation and plan, water levels marked day by day, and threaded through it all — in a different, surer hand — annotations in a tight feminine script. *Ada* was written at the top of one sheet, underlined twice. Below it, a network of lines spread across the page like veins. Like roots. Like a map of the delta's secret circulatory system drawn from underneath.

"You mapped this," Juniper said. "Down here. In the dark."

"We mapped it before." Elias Wren lowered himself onto a ration crate with the care of a man whose body had filed formal complaints. "Up there. My team. We spent fourteen months sounding the delta and we kept finding anomalies — currents that ran against the tide, salinity gradients that made no sense, sonar returns under the riverbed where there should have been clay. Ada figured it out first. She always figured it out first." His voice caught on the name and smoothed itself over with visible effort. "There's a waterway under the delta. Older than the colony, older than the maps. A whole secondary river system, braided through the limestone, and the monsoon floods move through it like blood through a heart. If you know the system, you can predict the floods to the meter. If you can control the gates—" He stopped.

"If you can control the gates," Juniper said slowly, "you can decide who floods and who doesn't."

"And land that floods is cheap, and land that doesn't is worth ten times more, and if you can choose which is which—" Elias made a small, exhausted gesture at the walls, at the dark, at everything. "Hale understood it in about nine minutes. I watched it happen. One minute he was a scientist, and then he did the arithmetic, and after that he was something else."

"What happened to your team?"

The silence stretched long enough that Juniper thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice was flat, sanded down, a man reporting weather.

"He sealed us in. The two of us who objected. Me and Ada. Blast charge at the archway — you came through the hole it left, incidentally; thank you for that, it saved me digging. He told the ministry we were lost in a flood event. Clean story. Floods eat people all the time; nobody audits a river." He looked at his hands. "Ada got me out through a collapse she'd mapped on instinct, months before, because she never trusted paper and she never trusted Hale. She got me to this chamber. Then she went back for the master charts — the originals, the ones with the gate locations — because she said evidence was the only weapon that worked on men like him." His voice did not break. It simply stopped, the way a clock stops. "She didn't come back. The water rose. I've been listening to it rise for eleven days."

Juniper stood very still in the dive light. Above them, faint through stone, the river went on with its business.

"The compass," Juniper said at last. "In the camp. It pointed here."

"Ada's. She left it on the table deliberately — she thought if anyone honest ever came looking, the compass would bring them to me instead of to the charts." A ghost of a smile crossed the ruined landscape of his face. "She trusted a brass needle more than any institution on earth. So far she's been right."

* * *

They talked logistics by dive light while Elias ate — Juniper had protein bars in the boat's emergency kit, and watching him eat was like watching a drought end. Between mouthfuls he sketched in the air with his hands: the passage layout, the flooded sections, the air pockets, the distances.

"How much of this is passable by boat?" Juniper asked.

"Some. The lower galleries were navigable when the team sounded them. The water's higher now — my marks show a thirty-centimeter rise per day since the rains started, accelerating." He tapped the wall chart, the tight script annotations. "Ada's numbers say the monsoon peak puts the whole lower system under. Every air pocket except the big ones floods. After that, the only entrances are underwater and the only people getting in are divers with a reason."

"So there's a window."

"There's a window, and it's closing, and both of us are inside it." Elias looked up, and for the first time his eyes were clear — not calm, nothing so kind as calm, but focused, the focus of a man who had spent eleven days converting terror into patience and had finally been given something to aim it at. "The originals are gone. Burned or buried or locked in Hale's safe, I don't know. But I have what's on these walls, and I have what's in my head, and Ada trained me until I could redraw the whole system from memory. That's worth something. That's worth a great deal to the wrong people, which is why nobody can know I'm alive yet."

"Too late," Juniper said. "I reported the compass find. Got ordered to leave it and come back empty-handed."

Elias closed his eyes briefly. "Then they know someone's close."

"They know *someone* found something. They don't know what, and they don't know about you — unless they do, and if they do, they'll be back to the spire, and next time they won't just probe the outer galleries." Juniper glanced toward the black water of the entry pool. "My boat's anchored off the spire. Old hull, leaks when you look at her wrong, but she floats. Charts, bars, fuel, a bilge pump that works about sixty percent of the time."

"You're proposing we leave."

"I'm proposing we leave *now*, ahead of the monsoon, through the passage if we have to, because sitting in a flooding cave waiting for Hale's divers is the single worst option on the table, and I've been making a living ranking bad options for eleven years." Juniper extended a hand. "I'm Juniper Okafor. And to be completely honest with you — because we're apparently doing honesty — half my reason for pulling you out of here is that delivering you might clear my debt, and the other half is that leaving a man to drown in a church feels like the kind of thing I'd have to answer for later."

Elias studied the offered hand. Then, slowly, he took it, and his grip was stronger than the rest of him suggested.

"Reluctant alliance," he said. "The best kind. No illusions on either side."

"No illusions," Juniper agreed, and hauled him to his feet.

Above them, distant but no longer distant enough, thunder rolled again — closer now, and underneath it, faint as a pulse through stone, came another sound that made them both go still.

An engine. Out on the channel. Slowing down.
