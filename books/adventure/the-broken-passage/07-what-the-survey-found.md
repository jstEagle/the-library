## 7. What the Survey Found

Elias Wren had learned, over eleven days in the dark, that hunger made a strange kind of clock. Not the frantic kind — the slow one. The kind that ticked in the stomach instead of the chest. He had eaten half a ration bar an hour ago, or what felt like an hour ago, and now he sat on the stone lip of the flooded gallery with his legs in the black water and told a stranger with a storm-chaser's calloused hands how nine people had become one.

The stranger — Okafor — sat cross-legged on the wet stone across from him, flare gun still within reach, and listened. That was the thing Elias noticed first, after the face and the debt notice folded into a plastic sleeve. They listened the way surveyors listen to water: patiently, as if the sound itself were data.

"You want the beginning," Elias said.

"I want what happened to your team."

"The beginning is the team." He looked at the water. Somewhere below, the flooded passage went down and down, and the compass in his pocket — no, Okafor's compass, the one from the chart table — pointed at all of it. "Nine of us. Director Hale, two hydrologists, a geologist, two technicians, Ada, me, and a security contractor named Brandt who mostly napped and drank. We were supposed to be in the delta for six weeks. Bathymetric survey, channel mapping, sediment cores. The kind of work that produces reports nobody reads and budgets get renewed on."

"And it wasn't."

Elias smiled. It felt strange on his face, like a tool borrowed from someone else. "The first week was exactly that. Boring. Beautiful, actually — the delta at dawn, mist on the water, the church spire standing out of the flood like a finger pointing at God and getting the address wrong. We mapped the river. Then Ada found the anomaly."

"Your partner."

"Ada Reyes. Cartographer. Better than me, which I told her constantly, because it was true and because she got insufferable when I did." He said it evenly. He had rehearsed this, in the dark, saying her name without his voice breaking. Eleven days of rehearsal. "She was running a side-scan pass near the drowned church — the old colonial one, San something, the river took it in the eighteen-fifties — and the sonar came back wrong. Not noise. Structure. Corridors. She thought it was a collapsed crypt system, colonial-era, maybe a smuggler's tunnel. That happens. Rivers eat buildings and buildings leave ghosts."

"But it wasn't a crypt."

"No. We dove it. Brandt and I did the first penetration because we were the two stupidest swimmers on the team, and Hale wanted volunteers, and volunteering was how you got on Hale's good side." Elias turned his hands over, looked at the blisters, the rope burns half-healed. "It was a passage. A real one. Cut stone, dressed blocks, older than the colony by centuries — pre-colonial, maybe pre-everything we had a name for. And it wasn't a tunnel. It was a *network*. Galleries, sluice channels, cisterns, branching waterways that ran for kilometers under the delta in every direction. Somebody had built a water management system down there and then let the river take it, or the river had taken it and history had agreed to forget."

Okafor shifted. "How big?"

"We mapped eleven kilometers before we stopped mapping. It kept going." He held up a hand before the question came. "And before you ask — yes, it's connected to the flood cycle. That's the whole thing. That's the whole *horrible* thing, and I'll get there, but you need the shape of it first."

He pulled himself forward, sat closer to the water's edge, and drew on the stone with a wet finger — a habit, Ada's habit, sketching when words failed. The water left a dark line that faded almost as soon as he made it.

"The delta floods every monsoon. Everyone knows that. The government's model says it's rainfall plus river volume plus tidal push, and the model is *fine*, it predicts within tolerances, it's just — incomplete. Because the delta isn't just a plain the river spills across. It's a plain sitting on top of a sponge. This network, these galleries — when the river rises, they fill. When it falls, they drain. They're a buffer the size of a city that nobody has known about for four hundred years. The floods aren't just water coming down. They're water coming *up*, out of a system we built our towns on top of without knowing it was there."

The flare gun in Okafor's hand shifted, just slightly. Elias watched them do the arithmetic. He'd seen it on Hale's face too, that first night in the survey tent, when the sonar overlays went up on the screen and the network's extent became real. The arithmetic was the same on every face. It was only the conclusions that differed.

"So whoever holds the map," Okafor said slowly.

"—holds the delta. Yes." Elias wiped his hand on his ruined trousers. "Say you're Director Hale. Say you've spent fifteen years in the water-resources directorate, smart, ambitious, perpetually second-tier, watching lesser men get ministries. And then your survey team hands you a map of a hidden hydraulic system under the most flood-prone, most politically expendable, most *poor* region in the country. What do you see?"

"A weapon."

"A ledger," Elias said. "A weapon is for enemies. This is for everyone. You see land values. You see which districts flood and which can be made not to flood. The network has gates — old ones, stone, but the channels are intact, and with modern engineering you could open some and close others. Redirect the water. Spare one town, drown another. And if you know, a year in advance, which is which—" He let the sentence die the way it deserved to, unfinished, because finishing it was the same as saying it.

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. The water in the gallery lapped against the stone with a sound like slow applause. "The flood season. The land values after."

"Now you have it. Hale didn't say any of it out loud, not at first. He's careful. He started small — restricted the data. Moved the copies to his own encrypted drive. Told us the survey was extended, then that it was reclassified, then that our findings were 'preliminary and unstable' in the official log while telling a different story on a channel we weren't supposed to know existed. Ada cracked his comms schedule in nine days. She was like that. She could not leave a locked door locked; it was a moral defect." His voice caught on the tense, the past tense, and he let it, because hiding it would have been worse. "She showed me the traffic. Hale wasn't reporting to the directorate. He was reporting to a private channel. Shell-company routing. And there was a second party on the calls — a woman's voice, clipped, patient, the kind of voice that gives instructions instead of opinions."

"Marisol."

Elias looked up. "You know her."

"Everyone in debt knows her." Okafor's mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "Keep going."

"Ada and I went to the two hydrologists. Okoro and Lindqvist. Good people. The kind who check each other's numbers, who'd already noticed Hale's models had been quietly re-tuned — flood projections for certain districts revised *upward*, others down, with no new data to justify it. We four agreed to file a dissent. Formal, documented, through proper channels. Ada said it herself: sunlight is the only disinfectant that works on water." He laughed, short and terrible. "She said that. I've had eleven days to remember every stupid beautiful thing she said."

The gallery was cold. Elias pulled his jacket tighter around himself, more ritual than warmth; the jacket had stopped holding warmth days ago.

"Hale found out. Of course he did — Brandt was his, had been all along, the nap and the bottle were a costume. On the last night, Hale called a full-team briefing at the main gallery. Said we'd be packing out in the morning, said there'd been a change in scope, said it with that flat administrative voice he used when the thing underneath the words was rotten. We went down in two boats. Nine of us." He stopped. Made himself go on. "He sealed the upper arch."

"Sealed it how?"

"Explosive. Small, precise — Brandt had demolitions training nobody had ever needed an excuse for. The charge dropped a section of the gallery roof across the main channel entrance. Not enough to kill us outright. Enough to trap us." Elias's voice stayed level. He had practiced this too, the levelness. The horror wasn't in the volume; the horror was that Hale had done the math on how much explosive was needed and had chosen the smaller number, because dead surveyors raised questions and trapped ones could be handled later, or not at all, because who would ever come looking in a place that wasn't on any map? "Okoro was hurt in the collapse. His leg. Lindqvist stayed with him. Ada got me into the water through a side channel she'd found on her own dives — she'd mapped a loop Hale didn't know about, because of course she had, because she never trusted a door she hadn't opened herself. She got me out into the deep galleries. And then—"

He didn't finish it. He let the water finish it, the black water lapping, the sound of a place that had taken her.

"She went back for the others," Okafor said. Not a question.

"She went back for the others." Elias nodded, once. "The collapse zone was unstable. She knew it. She went anyway. I heard the second charge through the stone. Hours later, maybe a day — time doesn't keep honest hours down here — I got back to the junction and found the loop sealed from the other side. Cut off. She was on the wrong side of it. So were Okoro and Lindqvist and the technicians and—" He breathed. "Hale sealed them in with the first collapse and then sealed the *evidence* in with the second. Two collapses, one story: the survey team was lost in a flood event. Tragic. Unrecoverable. The delta eats people; it always has; the paperwork writes itself."

"And you?"

"I ran. That's the whole of my heroism — I ran deeper instead of back, into the sections we'd mapped and then into the ones we hadn't, following air, following the sound of the river through stone, because Ada had trained me that air pockets follow the old construction joints and the construction joints follow the ridge line. I found this gallery. I found the skylight shaft — that crack above, the one you came down through. I found the ration cache we'd staged at the junction for the extended survey, and I found the rainwater that comes through the shaft when it storms. And I stayed hidden, because on the fourth day, men came."

Okafor went still. "Hale's men."

"Two of them. Dive gear, lights, survey-grade sonar on a tow fish. They came through the breach Hale made and they *searched*. Methodically. They had our own survey maps — our maps, the ones that were supposed to be locked — and they were checking the galleries against them, and marking things. I watched them from a ledge in the dark for six hours with my hand over my own mouth so my breathing wouldn't carry." His hands were shaking now. He looked at them with mild surprise, as if they belonged to someone less composed. "They weren't looking for survivors. You don't search for survivors with a tow-fish sonar grid. They were surveying the *gates*. The old stone sluices. Taking measurements. Preparing the network for whoever Hale's buyer is — the woman on the calls, the one whose money smells like your debt."

The flare gun came up — not aimed, just held, the way a man holds something when the world has confirmed his worst reading of it. "The syndicate financed the survey."

"Or Hale financed it through them, or they through him. Down here, after eleven days, I've stopped caring whose hand was on top. They sealed eight people in a hole in the world to own a map of water, and they sent you — a stranger with debts — to find the site, and I'd bet my last ration bar they didn't tell you why." Elias stood, slowly, knees cracking, and gestured at the dark gallery, the drowned network beyond it, the whole buried country of it. "So. That's what the survey found. A pre-colonial waterway under the delta that can be turned into a weapon against everyone living on top of it. That's what I've been guarding. That's what Ada died—" The word snagged. He pushed through it. "—died *keeping*. And now you know it too, and I'm starving, and I'm unarmed except for a flare gun with two cartridges, and you have a boat."

Okafor stood too. For a moment they just looked at each other across the black water, two people calculating what the other was worth, and Elias watched the calculation happen and waited for it to come out the way it always came out — self-interest, self-preservation, the arithmetic Hale had taught the whole world to do.

Then Okafor looked away first. Looked down at the water, at the drowned passage, and said, quietly, almost to themselves: "The compass. It wasn't pointing at the site. It was pointing at the *gates*."

Elias felt something in his chest loosen half a degree — the first degree in eleven days. "You noticed that too."

"I noticed someone wanted it pointing there." Okafor's head came up. Above them, faint through the skylight shaft, the sound of the river had changed — deeper, faster, a new note in it that Elias had heard once before, on the last night, and had hoped never to hear again. Okafor heard it at the same moment. Their eyes met.

"The monsoon," Elias said. "It's early."

"And if the river rises," Okafor said, "this whole network starts filling."

"From the bottom up," Elias agreed. "The galleries flood in sequence. We mapped the sequence. We have—" he did the arithmetic and didn't like any of the numbers "—days. Maybe less."

Okafor was already moving toward the shaft, toward the boat, toward the surface where the storm was gathering. "Then we'd better talk about how we get out," they said, "before the water decides for us."
