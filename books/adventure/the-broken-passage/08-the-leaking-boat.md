## 8. The Leaking Boat

The sky went the color of wet slate while we were still loading.

I'd seen a thousand storm cells build over the delta — the green-black bruise on the horizon, the wind that stopped before it screamed — but this was different. This was the monsoon's first true band, the leading edge of a system that had been assembling itself over the ocean for two weeks like something with intent, and it was coming down the river valley straight at us.

"We're not going to make the marina," I said, cinching the last strap on the gear locker. "We're not even going to make the bend."

Elias Wren stood at the top of the stone stairs with the chart case strapped to his back, looking upriver at the wall of weather, and I watched him do the same math I was doing. Eleven days in the dark had carved him down to tendons and stubbornness, but his eyes were still a cartographer's eyes — measuring, plotting, finding the line through.

"Then don't try for the marina," he said. His voice was rough from disuse and cave damp. "There's an oxbow three kilometers downstream. Old channel, cut off from the main current. The banks are high clay. If we tuck into it, we're out of the worst of the fetch."

"You know that from memory?"

"I know it because Ada surveyed it." Something crossed his face when he said her name — quick, swallowed whole. "She used to say the river remembers every channel it's ever loved. We just have to ask nicely."

I didn't love taking navigation advice from a man who'd spent eleven days eating ration bars in the dark, but my own charts were forty years old and the delta redrew itself every season. And there was no time to argue. The first fat drops hit the water around us like someone testing a microphone, big and slow and widely spaced, and then the sky opened its hand.

* * *

Getting him into the boat was ugly. His legs had been standing in cold water for days and they shook on the ladder rungs; I ended up hauling him over the gunwale by the collar of his jacket while he cursed me in three languages, one of which I was pretty sure was surveyor's shorthand. He collapsed into the stern among the fuel cans and I threw him a foil blanket from the ditch bag.

"Charts," I said, holding out my hand.

He unslung the case — a hard-sided map tube wrapped in plastic sheeting and sealed with more tape than I'd ever seen on anything — and held onto it a beat longer than made me comfortable.

"These are copies," he said. "Hand-drawn. From memory and from the originals. The originals are—" He stopped. "The originals are gone. These are what exist."

"Good enough." I stowed them under the helm console, in the driest spot on the boat, which on my boat was a relative term. "You bail, you stay low, and if I tell you to hold onto something, you hold onto it like it owes you money."

"That's an odd thing to say."

"I'm an odd person. Sit down."

I got us moving before the rain reached full violence, running the channel by eye and by the compass — the brass compass, riding in my jacket pocket now, because I'd decided somewhere in the last hour that I didn't trust it out of my sight. It still pointed south-southeast, steady as a lie, toward the drowned spire and everything beneath it. Elias had watched me check it twice without comment, which told me he'd already worked out what it was pointing at and had opinions about it he wasn't ready to share.

The river came apart around us.

I've chased storms across four countries and I want to be clear about what a monsoon band does to open water: it doesn't fall so much as arrive. The rain came sideways in sheets so dense the far bank vanished, and the wind walked across the surface in long white scars, and the channel I'd been reading by instinct filled with chop that came from three directions at once because the wind couldn't decide either. My boat — the fixer's boat, technically, until I paid for it, which meant never — was a sixteen-foot aluminum skiff with a sixty-horse outboard and a hull that had opinions about its own structural integrity. She rode it about as well as a filing cabinet would.

Two hundred meters into the worst of it, she started shipping water faster than she should.

I felt it before I saw it — the deck tilting a half-degree wrong, the boat sitting lower at the bow, that subtle dead weight in the handling that every skipper learns to feel in their spine before their eyes confirm it. I looked down between my feet. Water was coming through the forward bilge seam in a thin silver rope, pulsing with each wave slap, and the bilge pump — the bilge pump that had been making a sound I'd been carefully not thinking about since day one — chose that exact moment to give a grinding cough and die.

"Wren!" I shouted over the wind. "How are you with manual labor?"

He'd already seen it. He was already moving, dragging the bail bucket out of the locker, bracing himself against the console with his bad legs screaming, scooping water over the side in long practiced strokes. For a man who could barely walk, he bailed like he'd been born in a leaking hull.

"Seam's opening," he called back. "Bow seam. It'll get worse when we come off waves — the slamming works it."

"I know how hulls work!"

"Then you know you can't outrun this!" He jabbed the bucket toward the oxbow, invisible now behind the rain. "Get us behind those banks or we sink in open water!"

He was right, and I hated it, and I throttled down anyway — because he was right. Running flat-out in this chop was hammering the seam wider with every impact. I eased her to half power, quartering the waves at an angle that kept the bow from slamming, trading speed for mercy, and let the river push us downstream toward a bank neither of us could see.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about competence under pressure: it isn't calm. Calm is a story people tell afterward. What it actually is, is narrow. The world shrinks to the size of the next problem, and then the next, and your hands do work your brain only audits later, and somewhere in there you stop being afraid because fear takes space you don't have.

My space looked like this: a dying bilge pump, a widening seam, a bailer operated by a starving cartographer, and a hidden channel I had to find blind.

I killed the engine for ten seconds — ten seconds of just wind and rain and the sound of Elias bailing, rhythmic, furious — and listened. Under the storm, if you knew how to hear it, water moving through a cut bank sounded different from water moving past one. Smoother. A held note instead of static. Ada's river remembering its old channels, maybe. Or maybe just hydrology. Either way, I heard it off the port bow and turned toward it.

"There," I said. "Port side. Tell me when you see the bank."

"I see reeds." His voice had gone tight with effort. "Reeds and — yes. Cut bank. Clay. There's a gap in the trees — Juniper, there's a gap—"

"I see it."

The entrance to the oxbow was maybe fifteen meters wide, choked with reed beds on both sides, invisible from the main channel unless you already knew. I brought us in on idle, using the current itself to steer where the wind fought the rudder, and the moment we cleared the tree line the world changed. The wind dropped by half. The chop died to nothing. Rain still hammered down in ropes, but it fell on still water, and the tall clay banks on either side stood up like walls and took the storm's worst and gave it back to the sky.

Elias kept bailing for another full minute before his body caught up with the fact that they weren't sinking anymore. Then he sat down hard in the bilge water, breathing like a bellows, and laughed — one short, cracked bark of a laugh with nothing funny in it.

"Ada found this," he said. "Third week of the survey. She named it after her mother."

"What was her mother's name?"

"Esther." He looked around at the high banks, the rain silvering down through the canopy, the little pocket of almost-calm in the middle of a monster. "Hello, Esther."

* * *

I anchored us fore and aft — two lines, mud hooks, set well up the banks where the flood rise wouldn't lift them — and then I went to war with the hull.

The seam ran along the forward port bilge, maybe a meter of it, and the caulking had dried and shrunk sometime in the boat's previous life and now the monsoon had finished the job. I stripped to a T-shirt in the cold and got into the bilge with a headlamp clamped between my teeth, because the light in the skiff was a joke. Elias knelt on the deck above me, feeding me tools, and after the third time I asked for something and he handed me exactly the right thing without being told its name, I realized he'd worked boats before. Real boats. Survey boats.

"Research vessel," he said, when I asked. "Three seasons, Bay of Bengal coastal mapping. I was the guy who fixed things so the scientists could break them."

"And then you became the scientist."

"And then I became the scientist." A pause. "Worst career move I ever made."

The repair was improvisation, which is a polite word for desperation with tools. Marine epoxy first, worked into the seam with a putty knife, pressed in deep. Then — because epoxy alone on a flexing aluminum seam is a prayer, not a patch — I cut strips off the spare canvas tarp, saturated them in epoxy, and laid them over the joint like bandages, smoothing them down against the hull while the resin grabbed. Then a sheet of rubber gasket material over that, screwed down through the hull lip with self-tappers slathered in sealant, every hole a new potential leak that I was choosing to believe wouldn't leak. It was ugly. It was the kind of repair that makes a marine surveyor weep. But when I pumped the bilge dry and watched for five minutes, the water crept back at a fraction of the old rate — a seep, not a stream. Manageable. Babied, like the fixer said, she'd hold.

Elias bailed the rest of the way out while I lay in the bilge watching the patch, and at some point I became aware that he'd stopped talking. Not gone quiet — stopped talking *to fill silence*. He was working in rhythm with me, handing tools before I asked, shifting his weight to counterbalance mine, and neither of us had discussed any of it. When I finally crawled out, soaked in epoxy and river water, he handed me the foil blanket back without being asked.

"You trust me," I said. It came out more surprised than I meant it to.

"I trust that you want to live," he said. "That's been sufficient so far." He nodded at the chart case under the console. "And I trust that you haven't sold me yet, or you'd have done it before the storm, when the water was calm."

The words landed harder than he probably intended. Or exactly as hard as he intended. I thought about the satellite phone in the dry bag, and Marisol's seventy-two hours ticking down, and the number that half my debt would vanish for if a certain government witness arrived at a certain dock. I hadn't decided anything. That was the truth I was carrying. I hadn't decided, and every hour I didn't decide was itself a kind of decision.

"Eat something," I said, and threw him a ration bar, and changed the subject with both hands.

* * *

We rode out the worst of the band in Esther's oxbow, and it was during the worst of it — rain hitting the canopy like gravel, the river outside rising visibly along the clay banks, a centimeter every few minutes — that Elias finally talked about what happened. Not all of it. A piece. Enough.

"Hale didn't lose us," he said. He was sitting with his back against the console, the blanket around his shoulders, watching the water climb. "That's the thing everyone will get wrong. Hale always knows where his people are. He sealed the east gallery with a blast charge — a *survey* charge, from our own magazine — and then reported us missing in a flood event. Clean. Tragic. Unrecoverable." His hands were very still in his lap. "Okafor. Why did you really take this contract?"

"Money."

"Why did you really keep going after you found the camp? No footprints. Instruments running. Any sane person turns around."

I checked the anchor lines. They didn't need checking. "The compass pointed somewhere," I said finally. "Nobody spends that much effort making a compass point somewhere unless the somewhere matters."

He studied me for a long moment, rain drumming on the canopy above us, and whatever he saw in my face seemed to settle something for him.

"The compass belonged to Ada," he said quietly. "It was hers. She left it on the table for whoever came next." He looked down at his hands. "She left it because she knew someone would come. And she wanted that someone to find the passage, and find me, and get the maps out. That was the whole plan. That was her last plan." His voice caught and steadied. "So you see, Okafor — you're not here because of a salvage contract. You're here because a dead woman aimed you like an arrow."

The river rose. The storm leaned on us. And under the console, wrapped in tape and plastic, the copied charts waited — the thing two governments' worth of rotten men would burn the delta down to possess, and one dead cartographer had trusted to a stranger with a leaking boat.

I was still turning that over — *aimed like an arrow*, and at what target, exactly, and who got to decide — when the satellite phone buzzed against my hip.

I hadn't given anyone this number. Nobody knew where I was. On the caller ID, one word:

**MARISOL.**
