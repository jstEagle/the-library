## 9. Marisol Calls

The storm band passed an hour before dusk, and what it left behind was a river gone strange — flat as poured glass, the color of old tea, steaming faintly where the sun broke through in ragged columns. Juniper had seen this kind of calm before, over the Gulf, over the flats east of Port Meridian: the held breath between movements of something enormous. It made their teeth ache.

They'd anchored in the lee of a drowned mangrove island, just a crown of black roots breaking the surface like the ribs of some drowned animal. The *Cormorant* rode at her lines with her new patch sweating but holding, and Elias Wren slept in the cockpit, curled under a tarp with his knees drawn up to his chest, one hand still closed around the flare gun even in sleep. He'd eaten two ration bars and half a tin of condensed milk and then gone down like a cut line. Eleven days in the dark did that. Eleven days in the dark, and then the sky.

Juniper should have been sleeping too. Instead they sat at the helm with the satellite phone in their lap, weighing it.

The phone was syndicate hardware. That was the thing Juniper kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Marisol had issued it herself, three months ago, sliding it across the table of the Blue Anchor with two fingers, like a dealer pushing chips forward. *"You're going upriver where cell towers don't bother,"* she'd said, smiling that smile that never reached anything above her cheekbones. *"So you'll call me on this. Weekly check-ins. Non-negotiable."*

Weekly check-ins. Juniper had made four of them. Weather reports, position reports, nothing about the salvage, because there hadn't been anything to report until now. And now there was a man asleep under a tarp who was supposed to be dead, and a set of charts folded in a waterproof sleeve under the helm seat, and a brass compass pointing at things compasses shouldn't point at.

And now there was the message light blinking on the phone. Red, patient, steady as a pulse. It had been blinking since noon.

Juniper looked at Elias. He didn't stir. His breathing had the deep, mechanical rhythm of genuine exhaustion, and Juniper found they couldn't bring themselves to wake him — not because he needed the sleep, though he did, but because Juniper wasn't sure yet what they were going to say. To him or to her.

They pressed play on the voicemail first. Coward's move, maybe. Or smart.

*"Okafor."* Marisol's voice came through the little speaker crisp and close, as if she were sitting in the cockpit instead of wherever she sat — some office with good air conditioning, Juniper guessed, somewhere the rain never got in. *"Your last three check-ins have been thin. Thin even for you. And now I'm hearing things from people upstream that I don't love. A government patrol boat working the channel. Sonar sweeps. That's not search-and-rescue posture, that's recovery posture, and recoveries mean somebody thinks there's something left to find."*

A pause on the recording. Juniper could picture her exactly: leaning back in her chair, pen turning between her fingers, that patient predator stillness.

*"Call me tonight, Okafor. Not tomorrow night. Tonight."*

Click.

Juniper sat with it. The river breathed against the hull. Somewhere across the water a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, once, and stopped.

There was no version of this where they didn't make the call. That was the trap of debt — it didn't need to threaten you every time. It just needed to exist, and you would do its threatening for it. Juniper had learned that lesson in the Blue Anchor, watching Marisol smile, and they had apparently not learned it well enough, because here they were, thumb hovering over the button.

They dialed.

Two rings. She picked up like she'd been holding the phone.

*"Okafor."* Same voice, live now, and warmer than the voicemail, which from Marisol was always worse. Warmth from Marisol meant she thought she was winning. *"Good. You're alive. I was starting to wonder."*

"I've been busy."

*"I heard. Word travels upriver faster than boats do, if you know which radios to listen to. A survey camp with no tracks out of it. A church spire. And now, according to my friend on the patrol boat — and yes, Okafor, I have a friend on the patrol boat, I have friends everywhere, that's most of what money is —"* a small laugh, dry as paper — *"now there are two heat signatures where there used to be none."*

Juniper's stomach dropped, slow and cold, like stepping off a dock into dark water. "Your friend has good equipment."

*"My friend has excellent equipment. Government issue. Which is funny,"* she said, and Juniper heard her settle in, heard the creak of her chair, the pen clicking once, twice, *"because so is yours."*

"What's that supposed to mean?"

*"It means let's stop pretending, you and I. It's exhausting, and honestly you're bad at it. You were always bad at it. It's one of the things I liked about you."* Click went the pen. *"The Voss Syndicate financed the Meridian Delta Hydrographic Survey. Through a shell company — Corvus Water Resources, registered in Cayenne, directors who don't exist. We paid for the boats, the sonar, the salaries. All of it laundered clean as spring water. And when the survey stopped reporting, when Director Hale's team went quiet eleven days ago, we didn't panic. We didn't send our own people. Do you know why?"*

Juniper said nothing. The river said nothing. Elias slept.

*"Because we already had someone on the river,"* Marisol said. *"Someone with a boat, a debt, and a salvage contract we wrote ourselves. Someone deniable. If you found nothing, you were a failed contractor chasing ghost gear, and we were out the cost of your contract, which was pocket change. But if you found something —"* the pen clicked again, and Juniper realized with a lurch that the sound had been keeping time with their own heartbeat — *"then whatever you found belonged to us. Chain of custody, Okafor. Everything you recover under contract is syndicate property. Read page nine of your agreement sometime. Nobody ever reads page nine."*

The world tilted slightly, the way it did below decks in a swell. Juniper gripped the wheel with their free hand, not for balance, just to hold something.

"The fixer," they said. Their voice came out flat, distant, someone else's voice. "Dane. The man who offered me the contract."

*"Worked for us for six years. Good man. Dull, but good."*

"The camp. The compass. The order to leave it and come back empty-handed — that radio reply—"

*"Was ours too. Yes."* And now there was something almost gentle in her voice, which was the worst thing yet, because gentleness from Marisol meant she was explaining something to a child. *"We wanted you curious, Okafor. Curiosity is cheaper than loyalty and lasts longer. We wanted you poking around that spire with your cheap instruments and your expensive stubbornness, and we wanted you doing it while believing you were doing it for yourself. Because a person who thinks they're acting for themselves doesn't ask who else is benefiting. You've been pulling our cart for three months and calling it freedom."*

Juniper looked down at their hands on the wheel. They were steady. That surprised them. Inside, something was coming apart with a sound like wet rope under strain.

"Elias Wren," they said. "Is that what he is? Property? Page nine?"

*"Elias Wren is a problem. A witness who won't drown, which is inconvenient for everyone involved — Hale wants him buried, we want him contained, and neither of us can afford the other getting to him first. But mostly?"* A breath. When she spoke again, the warmth was gone, and what was underneath was worse: simple arithmetic. *"Mostly he's leverage. Bring me Wren alive and the original charts — not copies, Okafor, I know about the copies, my friend on the patrol boat knows about the copies, everyone knows about the copies — bring me those, and your debt dies. All of it. Two hundred and forty thousand, gone. Clean slate. You could chase storms for the rest of your life and owe nobody."*

The number landed in Juniper's chest like ballast. Two hundred and forty thousand. They knew every digit of it the way they knew their own blood pressure, the way they knew the exact pitch the *Cormorant*'s engine made at seventy percent load. Two hundred and forty thousand dollars, itemized in a folder they'd read so many times the staples had rusted. Interest accruing daily. Marisol's smile attached to every line.

"No one gets hurt," Juniper said. "That's the deal. Wren comes with me, unharmed, and walks away after."

*"After what?"*

"After I say so."

Marisol laughed — genuinely, briefly, and it was somehow more frightening than the arithmetic. *"Oh, Okafor. You sweet, doomed optimist. Fine. Fine. Unharmed, delivered, and then we'll discuss his retirement package. I'm generous with people who deliver. You have no idea how generous."* The pen clicked. *"But hear me clearly, because I'm only saying this once, and I'm only ever honest once per customer: the window is closing. Hale's patrol is running sonar grids over every entrance to that passage system, and when they finish mapping it — and they will, they wrote the protocols, they know it better than the water does — they'll seal it with Wren inside or without him. And my collectors are already moving. Three boats out of Port Meridian as of this morning. If they reach you before you reach me, the deal changes shape. Debtors who find things and don't surrender them aren't contractors anymore, Okafor. They're competitors. Ask anyone in the delta what we do with competitors."*

"And if I just give the charts away," Juniper said quietly. "If I put them on the open channel. Every hydrology office, every news desk, every village council with a radio."

Silence. Long enough that Juniper checked the signal indicator. Still full bars. Marisol was just letting it sit there, the way she let everything sit there, making other people marinate in her pauses.

*"Then you'd be a dead person who did a noble thing,"* she said at last, without heat, without threat, which was how you knew it was real. *"And the charts would be public, and Hale's project would die in an inquiry, and the villages would flood anyway, because maps don't move water, Okafor, money moves water. But go ahead. Be romantic. I've buried romantics. They're easier to bury than accountants — they die smiling."*

She paused again, and when she resumed, the warmth was back, oiled and professional, the transactional voice.

*"Seventy-two hours, Okafor. Same as before. You've had that deadline once; you know I keep my clocks. Rendezvous is the fuel dock at Halfway Light, day after tomorrow, eighteen hundred hours. Come alone in matters of cargo. Bring Wren. Bring the originals. Leave owing nothing."* A beat. *"Or don't come, and leave owing everything, plus interest, plus my personal attention. I do so enjoy giving my personal attention."*

Click. The line went dead, and the red light on the phone blinked itself out, and the river lay there under the dying light, flat and glassy and indifferent, as if the whole conversation had happened inside Juniper's skull.

They sat for a long time.

* * *

The stars came out hard after the storm, the way they always did, the whole spilled vault of them doubled in the still water so that the *Cormorant* seemed to hang suspended in black space rather than float on a river. Juniper sat in the cockpit with a cup of reconstituted coffee going cold in their hands and watched Elias sleep, and turned the evening over and over like a stone with something living under it.

*You've been pulling our cart for three months and calling it freedom.*

The worst part — the part that kept snagging like a nail head in a bare foot — was that it explained everything. The too-generous contract. Dane's eagerness. The boat, old and leaking but seaworthy, *if babied* — a boat chosen to fail slowly enough to keep them committed and fast enough to keep them desperate. The compass left centered on the chart table like bait on a hook. Even the order to come back empty-handed: not a wall, a funnel. Every door had opened exactly far enough to admit one stubborn, drowning storm chaser, and Juniper had walked through all of them grateful for the light.

They thought of the debt notice they'd shown Elias in the dark of the passage, holding it up like a lantern, like proof of something. *I'm not one of them.* And Elias had looked at the paper, and at them, and had decided — God help him, starving, half-blind, holding a flare gun on a stranger — had decided to believe it.

Juniper drained the coffee. It tasted like burnt grain and salt.

"You're going to wear a hole in the deck," Elias said.

Juniper startled badly enough to slosh the cup. He was awake, sitting up under the tarp, eyes catching the starlight. He looked better than he had that morning — color in his face, some weight behind the eyes besides terror — but he was still watching Juniper the way he must have watched the darkness for eleven days: tracking movement, calculating exits.

"How long have you been awake?"

"Since the phone lit up." He nodded at it, lying dark on the console. "Satellite phones glow. Didn't you know that?"

"It doesn't glow."

"Everything glows, down there, after enough days in the dark." He pushed the tarp back and swung his legs around, wincing. His boots were still ruined; Juniper had given him a pair of deck shoes two sizes too big, stuffed with newspaper. He looked like a scarecrow dressed by committee. "Whoever it was. They want something."

"They want you."

He took that in without flinching, which told Juniper something about what eleven days underground does to a person's expectations. "And?"

"And they want the originals. The charts. Ada's route, the whole network." Juniper set the cup down and made themselves look at him straight. "In exchange, they erase my debt. All of it. Two hundred and forty thousand dollars, gone, and I walk away owning nothing to anyone."

Elias was quiet for a moment. Out on the water, something surfaced and rolled — a fish, a log, impossible to tell — and sank again without a ripple reaching shore.

"Will you do it?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

"It usually is." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied Juniper with that cartographer's gaze, the one that measured and triangulated everything, that had spent eleven days mapping a prison by echo. "You could. You'd be rich in the only currency that matters to people like that, which is being left alone. And I'd be—" He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "Contained. Buried. Whatever word they use in the paperwork. I've read enough paperwork to know the words are all the same word."

"I'm not going to do it."

"You nearly sold me already." He said it without accusation, flat as a depth sounding. "You told me. In the church. So don't tell me what you're not going to do. Tell me what you are."

Juniper opened their mouth and found nothing there. The truth was a mess of half-things: that they'd stood in the drowned nave with the waterproof case in their hands and calculated, actually calculated, what Wren's delivery would be worth against what his testimony might buy; that the calculation had lasted perhaps four seconds and had felt like years; that they'd hated themselves with a purity they hadn't managed since their mother's funeral. That they'd confessed it to him there among the drowned pews because lying to a man who'd watched his partner die for a piece of paper had been physically impossible.

"What I am," Juniper said finally, "is someone who's been a weapon for three months without noticing the weight of it. And I don't—" Their voice caught, and they pushed through it. "I don't intend to stay one. That's all I've got. It's not a plan. It's barely a principle. But it's mine, and nobody wrote it into a contract."

Elias held their gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and reached into the breast pocket of the borrowed jacket, and brought out the brass compass.

"Then we should talk about what happens next," he said, "because Marisol's clock isn't the only one running. And hers, at least, points somewhere you can see."

He opened the lid. In the starlight the needle trembled, settled, steadied — and pointed, unmistakably, not north but south-southeast. Downriver. Toward the drowned church. Toward the passage.

Toward the sound, faint and rhythmic beneath the river's silence, that Juniper had been telling themselves for the last ten minutes was imagination:

An engine. More than one. Running without lights, coming up the channel with the current.

Juniper killed the anchor light and put a hand over the compass, and the needle beat against their palm like a second heart.
