## 10. Hale's Men

Elias heard the engine before he saw anything — a low diesel thrum rolling down the channel like weather that had learned to think.

He was in the reed shallows at the mouth of the secondary channel, waist-deep in warm water, holding the boat's bow line while Juniper wrestled the fuel drum forward. The storm band had passed an hour ago and left the sky scrubbed and pale, the kind of light that made everything visible and nothing safe. He went still, one hand raised, and Juniper read him fast enough to stop moving mid-stride, the drum's edge cutting a bright line across her shoulder.

*Don't talk,* Elias mouthed.

The sound grew. Not the arrhythmic stutter of a fishing boat, not the high whine of a skiff running from the rain. This was steady, disciplined, government-issued patience. He knew it the way you know a voice on the phone before the caller identifies himself — by its cadence, by what it refuses to hurry for.

Juniper eased the boat behind a curtain of mangrove roots, and Elias sank lower until only his chin broke the surface. The water tasted of silt and rot and, faintly, of the passage below them — that mineral coldness that rose through cracks in the delta floor like breath from something sleeping.

The patrol boat came around the bend at survey speed. That was the first wrong thing. Nobody patrolled this channel at survey speed; the sandbars shifted weekly and even the locals ran it fast and shallow, reading the water by instinct. But this vessel moved with the deliberate, grid-like precision of a machine following coordinates. It was white-hulled, unmarked except for a registration number too clean to be real, with a sensor mast folded down amidships and two figures at the rail wearing rain gear instead of uniforms. Deniable men. Hale had always preferred deniable men — they photographed better in reports and disappeared better afterward.

Elias watched them the way he'd once watched weather fronts cross the survey camp: professionally, hungrily, cataloguing.

Then the mast came up.

It unfolded in three segments, smooth as a heron's neck, and the array at its tip began to sweep. Sonar. Side-scan, by the rhythm of it — wide arcs fanning out to either side of the hull, painting the river bottom in strokes Elias couldn't see but could reconstruct perfectly, because he had helped write the software parameters that shaped those arcs.

His stomach dropped through the warm water into the cold dark underneath.

* * *

Eleven days ago — twelve? He'd stopped counting honestly somewhere around day nine — Elias Wren had been a cartographer with a government contract, a dry bunk, and a partner who hummed off-key when she plotted bathymetry. Now he was a starving animal floating in a reed bed watching his own methodology hunt him.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing that made his hands shake against the current, made his breath come shallow and careful so no ripple betrayed him.

The sweep pattern was wrong for a search. It was right for a *sweep*. A disappearance investigation would run transects over the last known position, then spiral outward, looking for debris, bodies, dropped equipment. What the boat was doing instead was a ladder pattern — parallel passes spaced exactly forty meters apart, each pass offset by half a swath width, guaranteeing full coverage with zero overlap waste. It was the pattern you used when you weren't looking for something lost. It was the pattern you used when you were looking for something *specific*, something whose dimensions you already knew, and you wanted to find every entrance to it before anyone else did.

Protocol Seven. Hale's own protocol. Elias had sat in the briefing tent at Kiln Point and watched Director Hale tap the laminated diagram with a fountain pen — the man never used markers, markers were for people who expected to be corrected — and explain, in his soft, reasonable voice, how a submerged structure should be surveyed for structural integrity. Full coverage. Half-swath offsets. Two passes minimum, second pass perpendicular to the first, to resolve vertical features.

The boat finished its east-west ladder and turned. Began the north-south set.

Perpendicular confirmation. Vertical resolution.

They weren't mapping the river bottom. They were mapping the *doorways*.

Elias thought about Ada, and had to stop thinking about Ada, because if he let her face arrive fully in his mind he would make a sound, and the sound would kill them both. He thought instead about numbers, about the sonar's effective range in turbid water, about the acoustic shadow cast by the drowned church spire, about whether the mangrove roots were dense enough here to scatter a return. His mind did what it always did under terror: it became a map. It was the only prayer he knew.

* * *

The second pass took twenty minutes. Elias spent all of them underwater to the ears, watching the boat crawl the grid, watching the two figures at the rail lean into screens he couldn't see. At some point Juniper's hand found his ankle beneath the waterline and gripped it — not pulling, just anchoring, telling him *I'm here, I see it too, we wait.* He hadn't heard her move. She was better at this than she pretended to be. Storm chasers, he was learning, were people who had made a career of standing very still in front of enormous things.

When the patrol boat finally completed the box and swung toward the main channel, it didn't leave. It slowed near the spire — the spire, their spire, the church tower leaning out of the water like a broken finger accusing the sky — and held station there for six full minutes. Six minutes. Long enough to drop something. Long enough to mark something. Long enough for a diver, if they carried divers, which Hale's budget had always allowed for.

Then it moved off downstream at cruising speed, unhurried, and the engine note faded until the channel belonged again to wind and birds and the small wet sounds of a delta pretending nothing had happened.

Elias didn't speak until the sound was fully gone. Even then he kept his voice below the wind.

"It's Protocol Seven."

Juniper surfaced beside him, hair plastered flat, eyes narrowed. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning that's not a search pattern." He hauled himself toward the boat, arms trembling, and Juniper caught his elbow and lifted him over the gunwale like he weighed nothing, which was its own small humiliation. He knelt on the deck, dripping, and pulled the chart case open with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. "A missing-persons survey runs spirals. Debris drift modeling. They look *outward* from where the team vanished." He spread his hand-drawn chart of the passage entrances across the thwart, weighted the corners with the compass housing and a coil of rope. "This is a coverage grid. Ladder pattern, half-swath offsets, perpendicular confirmation pass. It's how you map a known structure. It's how you find every way in."

Juniper stared at the chart, then at the empty channel, doing the arithmetic. She was quick. He'd noticed that on the first night — quick the way weather is quick, arriving before you've finished bracing.

"So they know the passage exists," she said.

"Hale has known since the beginning. He commissioned the survey." Elias traced the grid lines the patrol boat had just walked, invisible in the air above the paper. "But this isn't a cover-up, Juniper. I need you to understand the difference, because it changes everything. A cover-up looks backward. It erases. It finds evidence and destroys it." His finger stopped on the spire. On the secondary entrance. On the collapsed gallery where Ada had — where the rockfall had — where he had crawled away alone. "This looks *forward*. They're cataloguing entrances. All of them. And then—"

"And then they close them," Juniper said quietly.

"Or they wait at them." He looked up. The pale post-storm light made her face hard to read, all shadow and angle. "Hale doesn't erase witnesses. I told myself for eleven days that's what happened — that the team died in the flood, that Hale sealed the site to avoid liability, that the silence was bureaucracy. Bureaucracy I could survive. Bureaucracy gets bored." He laughed, and hated the sound of it, thin and cracked as old paper. "Bureaucracy doesn't run Protocol Seven twice."

"You're saying he wants you found."

"I'm saying he wants me *located*. There's a difference, and the difference is what happens after." Elias folded the chart with exaggerated care, because if his hands had nothing to do they would shake visibly, and he needed Juniper to keep believing he was useful. "A body is a problem. A witness is an asset — until he's testified, or signed something, or been discredited, or drowned in a way the report calls accidental. Hale will want me alive until the moment I stop being useful. Then the river does the rest. Rivers are very cooperative that way. No one audits a monsoon."

* * *

They ran upstream through the afternoon, hugging the western bank where the mangroves grew thick enough to break up their silhouette, Juniper handling the throttle with a delicacy that surprised him — keeping the wake low, killing the engine whenever the channel straightened and sightlines lengthened, drifting through the open stretches like flotsam. She'd rigged a tarp over the cargo well in gray-green canvas that matched the water. None of it had been aboard when they started. She'd built it all in the hour since the patrol boat passed, working in silence, and Elias understood that this was her version of what his mind did with maps: fear converted directly into craft.

They ate ration bars in the root-shadow of a flooded copse. Elias managed half of his. His body had begun rejecting food it couldn't spare the warmth to digest, a fact he kept to himself. Twelve days of cold and hunger had moved his thermostat somewhere he couldn't follow, and the shivering came now in waves he had to ride out with his jaw clenched, because Juniper kept glancing at him with an expression he refused to name.

"The sonar," she said, around the last corner of her bar. "How good is it? In water like this?"

"Turbidity kills optical everything, but sonar doesn't care about mud. It cares about density and geometry." He wiped his mouth. "Stone archways under six meters of river? It'll image them beautifully. It'll have imaged them today. Which means somewhere on that boat there's a screen showing my church, my passages, my—" he almost said *Ada's route*, and rerouted the sentence, "—every gap in the rock wide enough to admit a boat."

"Then why didn't they come in after us?"

"Three reasons, maybe." He held up fingers, cartographer's habit, inventorying. "One: they don't have divers aboard, and the entrances that fit a boat are deep. Two: they're not sure which entrance we used, and going into a flooded colonial passage blind is how you add your own bodies to the mystery. Three—" and here his voice did something he didn't authorize, dropping low, going flat, "—three: they don't need to come in. They just needed to know the doors. You seal a tomb from the outside, Juniper. Always from the outside."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment. Water ticked against the hull. Somewhere across the channel a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

"When I radioed in," she said finally, "after I found the compass. The reply told me to leave it and come back empty-handed. I thought that was a cover-up order. Leave the weird artifact, forget the site." Her jaw worked. "That wasn't a cover-up order, was it."

"No."

"That was a *herding* order."

"They wanted you away from it before you got curious. Or—" Elias hesitated. Honesty had become, in the last few days, a discipline he practiced like bailing: constant, unglamorous, necessary. "Or they wanted you clear of the site so that whatever they did next had no civilian witnesses on the water. Either way. You were never being protected. You were being *positioned*."

She absorbed that without flinching, which he was coming to recognize as her defining feature. Most people, given bad news, flinched first and thought second. Juniper thought first and let the flinch happen later, privately, where it couldn't be used.

"So we can't go back in," she said. "Not through any of the entrances they mapped today."

"Not through any of the entrances *I* knew about, either. Because they'll have compared their sonar returns against the original survey data — Hale has all of it — and any opening we used, they've now got coordinates for." He pulled the chart back out and laid it flat, and his finger hovered over it the way a man's finger hovers over a wound. "Which leaves the routes that aren't on any survey. The ones that exist in exactly one place."

"Where's that?"

He tapped his own temple. "Ada never wrote them down. She said paper talks." His throat closed around the next words and he pushed through it. "She taught me the backup routes verbally, over months, the way you'd teach someone a language. She didn't trust the archive. She didn't trust Hale. She didn't trust—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "She trusted me to remember. So I remembered. Every fork, every depth sounding, every air pocket between here and the far side of the delta. It's all up here. It exists nowhere else in the world."

Juniper looked at him for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed register — lower, stripped of the storm-chaser bravado she wore like oilskin.

"And the deepest route. The one through the flooded sections. The one you haven't mentioned because you're not sure I'd say yes."

"That's the one they'll never find," Elias said. "Because Ada found it herself, and she never told anyone but me, and I'm the only copy." He met her eyes. "And it's also the one that will probably kill us."

* * *

Dusk came down purple and total, the way it did in delta country — no twilight worth the name, just day switching off. They tied up in a dead slough behind a spoil bank, engine cold, running no lights. Juniper took first watch. Elias lay in the cargo well under the tarp, shivering through another wave, listening to the frogs resume their business and trying to calculate how long they had.

The answer kept coming back ugly.

The patrol boat had run a full coverage grid over the primary channels in a single afternoon. That meant resources. That meant Hale had committed — not probing, not delegating, but committing assets to the delta with the specific purpose of controlling access to the passage. Men like that didn't commit assets to guard a secret. They committed assets to *harvest* one. The map existed — Elias carried it in his skull, and copies of fragments rode in the waterproof case lashed under Juniper's thwart — and Hale wanted it, and Marisol's syndicate wanted it, and the two of them wanted it for different reasons that would eventually point guns at each other, and he and Juniper were the narrow bridge over which the map had to travel from the dark into somebody's hands.

Every plan ended the same way: with Elias Wren being either delivered or destroyed, and the only variable was which hands did the delivering.

Unless the map traveled a road nobody owned.

He thought about Ada's route. The deep one. Chest-deep water for two hundred meters past the Third Gallery, air pockets shrinking to lungfuls, a squeeze at the far end that Ada had once described — laughing, actually laughing, plotting bathymetry in the mess tent with her pencil behind her ear — as "a birth canal with opinions." She'd gone through it twice. She'd said a person could do it three times in a lifetime, and she'd done two, and she'd said it like a joke, and now the third transit was his, and hers was spent, and the unfairness of that was a weight he carried in his chest alongside the hunger.

The shivering peaked and broke. In the lull he heard Juniper shift above him, and her voice came down through the tarp, barely a breath:

"Elias. Port side. You seeing that?"

He slid out from under the canvas. Far down the main channel, maybe two kilometers, a light was moving. Not a navigation light — it swept, slow and systematic, arcing across the water and up the banks in long patient strokes. A searchlight, run by someone in no hurry at all. Behind it, fainter, the diesel thrum he'd hoped he'd never hear again.

But it wasn't the same boat. This one was bigger, and the light swept from the *bow*, and there were three lights, staggered, sweeping in coordination — a line abreast, a net thrown across the whole width of the delta's throat.

Two boats. Maybe three. Running a coordinated sweep at dusk, when anyone sensible would be anchored.

"They're not searching anymore," Elias whispered. The cold in him had nothing to do with the water now. "Searching happens in daylight. This is a *drive*. They're pushing everything on this river toward a choke point downstream." He swallowed. "There's a confluence four miles south. Deep water, one channel in, one channel out. If I were sealing a tomb, that's where I'd stand."

Juniper was already moving, coiling line, unshipping the tarp, her hands quick and certain in the dark. "Then we don't go south."

"We can't go north — that's back toward the spire, and the spire is mapped now. We can't go south." Elias felt the map unfold in his head, the whole delta lighting up in his mind like a circuit board, every channel a wire, every wire cut or watched. Except one. Except the road through the mountain's bones that a dead woman had walked twice and trusted him to carry.

He put his hand flat on the chart case, on the hidden copies of everything Hale wanted, and heard himself say the words he'd been circling for two days:

"There's an entrance. Half a mile west of here, behind the collapsed ferry landing. Too small for the sonar to love, too shallow for their boats, and it's not on any government survey because Ada found it and never reported it." He looked up at Juniper, and the hunted-animal part of him went quiet, replaced by something older and colder and calmer. "We go in tonight. We take the deep route. And we don't come out until we're on the other side of everything they own."
