## 12. The Drowned Church

The air pocket announced itself before we saw it — a change in the sound of the water, the slap of it against stone going hollow, resonant, the way a drumhead sounds different from a wall. Juniper killed the engine and let the boat drift, and then there it was: a vaulted ceiling rising out of the black like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down in the earth and never gotten up again.

"Church," Elias said. His voice came back to him doubled, tripled, off stone he hadn't seen in eleven days except by flare light. "Or what's left of one."

The colonial engineers had built their passage through the bones of an older world. The vaulting overhead was brick and coral mortar, furred with white mineral bloom, and where the floodlight touched it Elias could make out the ghost of fresco — a saint or a river god, features dissolved, one raised hand still visible as if blessing the water below. The air pocket was maybe thirty meters long, ten wide, a lens of trapped atmosphere between the flooded nave below and the collapsed roof above. Their boat sat in it like a toy in a bowl.

Juniper cut the engine fully and the silence that followed had weight. After two days of storm noise and bilge pumps and the constant conversation of a leaking hull, silence felt like a held breath.

"How long does it hold?" Juniper asked.

Elias had done the math already, because math was what he did when fear got loud. "The waterline's been climbing maybe four centimeters a day since the monsoon started. This pocket has about three meters of headroom at its crown." He didn't finish the arithmetic. He didn't need to. He saw Juniper do it — the flicker behind the eyes, the same flicker every surveyor learned to hide.

Two weeks. Less if the river kept rising. Less if the collapse zone upstream decided to become a non-collapse zone.

"We'll be out before then," Juniper said, and it wasn't quite a promise and it wasn't quite a lie, and Elias appreciated that they hadn't tried to make it either.

They tied off to a stub of iron — an old mooring ring driven into the masonry by hands a century dead — and Elias unrolled the charts.

* * *

He'd drawn them by flare light on the backs of ration wrappers and a torn survey field book, over eleven days in the dark, working from memory because memory was all Ada had left him. Now, spread across the boat's thwart under the floodlamp, they looked pitiful: forty-odd sheets of grease-stained paper covered in his cramped cartographer's hand, elevations and bearings and little marginal notes in two handwritings — his, and hers, hers in red pencil, quick and sure, correcting him even now, even dead.

"This is it," he said. "All of it."

Juniper leaned over the sheets and went very still, the way people go still when they're looking at something worth more than they'd imagined. Elias had seen that stillness before — on Director Hale's face, the day the first full composite of the subterranean network came together in the survey tent. He hated seeing it now. He hated that he understood it.

Because the map *was* beautiful. He could admit that. Eleven days of darkness hadn't burned the wonder out of him, only buried it. The delta above them was a maze, but the delta beneath was an architecture: pre-colonial canal works, older than the colony, older than the maps in any archive, a engineered hydrology that the river had swallowed and half-forgotten. Cisterns the size of cathedrals. Sluice galleries cut through bedrock. And threaded through all of it, the thing Hale had gone quiet and soft-voiced about when he understood what he was looking at: natural channels that connected the monsoon floodplain to the sea in ways no modern model accounted for.

"You could bleed a flood," Juniper said quietly. They traced a channel with one finger, not touching the paper. "You could open these and the water goes to the coast instead of the villages. Or you close them, and—" They stopped.

"And you decide who drowns," Elias finished. "Yes. That's the whole map. That's what Ada died for."

* * *

He told it plainly, because he was too tired to tell it any other way, and because he had learned in the dark that plainness was the only honest register left to him.

"The team found the first archway on day six. Routine — a sonar anomaly near the drowned spire, we went down expecting silt and rubble. Instead we found this." He gestured at the vault overhead. "Colonial records mentioned a 'subterranean drain' but nobody had ever located it. We spent four days mapping. It should have been the career highlight of my life. Instead—"

He stopped. The silence filled up with water sounds.

"Hale flew in on day ten. He'd been getting our data feeds the whole time, but he wanted to see it. I watched him stand where you're standing and look at the composite map, and I watched something happen in his face. Not greed exactly. Greed is loud. This was quieter. He looked like a man reading his own obituary and deciding to write a better one." Elias turned a sheet over. Red pencil annotations marched down the margin — Ada's hand. *Flow rates don't match colonial records. Older system underneath? Ask E. to check cistern 3.* "He asked us to classify everything. Eyes only. He said the ministry would want 'strategic control of the hydrological asset.' Ada asked him what that meant for the villages downstream of the eastern sluices. He said the villages were a policy question, not a survey question."

"He answered her question with a non-answer," Juniper said.

"He answered her question with a job offer. For her. Not for me." Elias heard how flat his own voice came out and made himself go on. "She declined. Politely. She was always polite. Two days later she and Tomas went back down to verify the cistern measurements — just the two of them, routine. Hale ordered the entrance scaffold sealed while they were inside. Maintenance, he said. Structural concern. By the time anyone admitted what had happened, the water had come up through the lower gallery and—" His hands did something he didn't authorize them to do. He folded them flat against his knees. "Tomas's body came out three days later, downstream. Ada's never did. I got into the western service tunnel during the confusion and I've been in here since. Eleven days. I memorized the originals' location and I burned the physical copies in a fuel drum so there would be nothing left to find but me."

Juniper was looking at him with an expression Elias couldn't read at first, and then could: it was the look of someone recalculating the price of a thing they'd planned to sell.

And that look — that small, human, terrible flicker — cracked something open in the boat between them.

* * *

"I need to tell you something," Juniper said.

Elias waited. In the dark he'd learned to wait well; waiting was most of survival.

"When I found your camp, the compass on the chart table, the gear — I radioed it in. Standard salvage protocol. And the reply told me to leave the compass and come back empty-handed, and I knew right then that somebody didn't want this site found. Any reasonable person would have turned around." Juniper's jaw worked. "I didn't turn around. And when I surfaced inside the passage and you put a flare gun in my face, my first thought — my actual first thought, before I even knew your name — was that a live witness from a vanished government survey team might be worth more than the salvage fee. To the syndicate. To whoever. There are people who pay for men like you, Elias. I know because I owe money to some of them, and I've watched what happens to assets that appreciate."

The floodlamp hummed. Somewhere above them, muffled through meters of stone and river, thunder moved like furniture being dragged across the sky.

"So when I showed you the debt notice instead of a badge," Juniper went on, "that was true, but it wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth is I spent the first hour after we met doing arithmetic. Your life versus my debt. Seventy-two hours Marisol gave me, and I spent sixty of them doing that arithmetic while you slept in the bow, trusting me." They laughed, short and without any humor in it. "I nearly sold you. I want you to know that. Not because I'm proud of telling you, but because if I don't, and you find out later — and you would find out later, people always do — then every other true thing between us becomes suspect. So. That's my confession. You were cargo to me for one hour and eleven minutes. I timed it."

"Why timed?" Elias asked. It was the only question he could find.

"Because at some point I realized I'd stopped checking the clock. And I figured the moment I stopped was probably the moment it stopped being arithmetic." Juniper looked at the charts instead of at him. "I've owed people things since I was nineteen years old. Voss, before Voss it was a charter outfit, before that a university I flunked out of owing lab fees. I don't remember what it feels like to touch something without calculating what it costs or what it brings. When I dove the spire, I told myself it was for the contract. It wasn't. It was because the compass pointed somewhere and for once I wanted to follow something that wasn't a payment schedule."

Elias sat with that. Around them the drowned church held its breath, and the saint's dissolved face watched from the brickwork with its one surviving hand raised, and Elias thought about how strange it was that honesty should feel more dangerous than the dark had. In the dark, the worst thing that could happen was death, and death was simple. This — sitting in a leaking boat across from a person who had just told him they'd priced his life and found the number wanting — this was harder, and also, absurdly, better.

"Ada used to say paper lies because paper can be taken," he said finally. "That's why she taught me her routes verbally. Why she never wrote down the deep way through. She said the only safe archive is a living memory attached to a person who chooses." He met Juniper's eyes. "I think she meant exactly this. Someone choosing to tell the truth when lying would be cheaper."

"I'm not a good bet," Juniper said. "My track record is debt."

"My track record is a burned map and a dead partner," Elias said. "We're both poor collateral. But we're the only collateral each other has."

Juniper huffed — almost a laugh. "That's the bleakest vow I've ever heard."

"It's the only kind I trust."

* * *

They worked after that, because grief and honesty both go stale if you don't give them labor. Juniper bailed the boat while Elias reassembled the chart sequence, and then they bent their heads together over the route forward — the collapse zone, the deep section, the exit that the rising water was eating centimeter by centimeter. Juniper's finger moved along the channels with a storm-chaser's instinct for systems, asking questions Elias hadn't thought to ask, and twice caught errors that eleven days of darkness had printed into his memory, and corrected them gently, in pencil, in the margin, next to Ada's red.

It was the closest thing to partnership Elias had felt since the survey tent. He didn't say so. He suspected Juniper knew.

Then, as he reached for the last sheet — the one covering the approach to the collapse zone — he stopped.

The sheet was wrong.

Not the drawing. The paper. It had been folded differently than he'd left it, and there was a faint gray smear along the crease, like graphite, like the dust a rope leaves when it runs over stone under load. He stared at it for a long moment, cold walking up his spine one vertebra at a time, and then he lifted the sheet to the floodlamp and turned it over.

On the back, in the corner, small and precise: a chalk mark. A surveyor's mark. Three vertical strokes and a diagonal — the standard notation for *section closed, proceed alternate route.*

Hale's own protocol. Elias knew it the way you know your abuser's handwriting.

"Juniper," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Someone else has been reading my map."
