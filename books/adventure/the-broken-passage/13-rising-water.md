## 13. Rising Water

Juniper woke to the sound of the river breathing.

That was the only way to describe it — a slow, tidal exhalation through the passage behind the drowned church, the water in the flooded gallery rising and falling against the stone with a rhythm that had nothing to do with tides and everything to do with pressure somewhere far above them. The monsoon was pushing its weight down on the delta like a thumb on a wound, and every drop it squeezed out of the sky had to go somewhere. Some of it was going here.

They'd moored the boat inside the vaulted air pocket Elias called the nave, tied off to an iron ring some colonial engineer had driven into the rock a century and a half ago. The ring was rusted to lace. Juniper had doubled the line anyway, then tripled it, because the alternative was thinking about what happened if the boat drifted while they slept.

Elias was already awake. He sat at the boat's stern with the chart case open across his knees, his headlamp turned low, red-lensed so it wouldn't kill their night vision. He'd been drawing again — Juniper could see the fresh graphite over his shoulder, the careful hatch marks of a man who thought in contours.

"You hear it too," Elias said. Not a question.

"The river's up." Juniper swung their legs over the gunwale and into two inches of standing water that hadn't been there when they'd anchored. Their boots found the deck plating with a slosh that said everything their mouth didn't want to say yet. "When we came in yesterday, this section was dry."

"It was dry eleven days ago too." Elias closed the chart case. His voice was flat in the way Juniper had learned meant the opposite of calm — it meant he'd already done the arithmetic and was deciding whether to share the answer. "The gallery where I hid the first week. It floods at high water now. I watched it happen from the ledge above. Took about six hours from first seep to full submersion."

Juniper looked at the water around their ankles. Then at the ceiling of the nave, twenty feet up, ribbed with old masonry, dark stains climbing the stone like a tide chart written by the building itself.

"How many air pockets between here and your way out?"

"Four." Elias held up four fingers, then folded one down. "Three, if the second one's gone. It was shallow even in dry season."

"And your exit?"

He was quiet for a moment. In the red light his face looked older than it had in daylight, which wasn't saying much — eleven days underground and half-starved had aged him a decade in all lights — but there was something else in it now. Something Juniper recognized from mirrors. The look of a person who has just remembered a piece of bad news they'd been successfully not remembering.

"I went out through the collapse," Elias said. "The east breach. Where the survey flagged the instability. It was tight but passable — you could wade it, chest-deep, maybe forty meters of rubble, and come up on the far side of the spire foundation." He paused. "I've been listening to the water since we got here. That rhythm? It's coming from the east."

Juniper felt it land in their chest before their head caught up: the specific cold weight of a door closing. "It's underwater."

"Or close to it. Close enough that 'chest-deep' is a memory." Elias unfolded from the stern, joints cracking, and waded forward along the gunwale to where the passage opened eastward. His headlamp swept the black water. "There might be an air gap still. A few inches. You could swim it with a light in your teeth and pray."

"You don't believe that."

"No," Elias said. "I don't."

* * *

They spent the morning doing what storm chasers and cartographers do best: measuring the thing they were afraid of.

Juniper rigged a gauge out of a broken antenna mast and a grease pencil, marking the waterline on a pillar every hour. Elias walked the nave with a sounding line, dropping it into every visible opening, calling depths back in a cartographer's flat cadence — *four meters, bottom soft; seven meters, no return; three-point-two, hard substrate, possible footing.* Between them they built a picture of the drowning room, and the picture got worse with every data point, which was the one universal law of fieldwork Juniper had never once seen violated.

By noon the marks on the pillar read:

*06:00 — 0 cm*
*09:00 — 11 cm*
*12:00 — 34 cm*

Thirty-four centimeters in twelve hours. And outside, through forty feet of stone and floodwater, the rain was still going — not the monsoon's opening bands anymore but the main body of it, the wall, the part of the storm that didn't pass through so much as settle in like an occupant. Juniper had chased cells like this across three basins. They knew the shape of it. This wasn't the peak. This was the approach to the peak.

"Do the math out loud," Juniper said. "I want to hear you say it."

Elias leaned against the pillar, next to his own grease-pencil marks, and looked at the numbers the way he probably looked at faulty survey data — without flinching, because flinching didn't fix instruments.

"Rate's accelerating. Call it five centimeters an hour now, doubling as the crest moves upstream. The nave holds air until roughly tomorrow night. Maybe thirty-six hours if I'm optimistic, and I'm not paid to be optimistic." He tapped the lowest mark. "After that, this room is part of the river. Everything after that is diving, and we have maybe one working dive light between us and no tanks."

"And the other pockets?"

"Smaller volumes, higher ceilings in two cases. They'll last longer once the system equalizes — the air gets pushed up and compressed, trapped under the old vault lines. Could stay breathable for days." He met Juniper's eyes. "Breathable isn't reachable. The connecting galleries between them are already silted with flow. You couldn't swim those. The current in a filling conduit will hold a body against a grate until it stops being a body."

"So we're not drowning tomorrow. We're drowning the day after, in the dark, spread across four rooms."

"That's the honest version, yes."

Juniper laughed — one short, ugly syllable that bounced off the vaulting and came back wrong. Thirty years of chasing storms and here was the punchline: they'd finally found weather they couldn't outrun, because it was rising underneath them instead of falling on top.

Except.

They turned the thought over, the way you turn a hailstone in your glove before you commit to licking it.

"Elias. Your charts. Ada's route."

His jaw tightened. He'd told them about it two nights ago in this same room — the route his dead partner had carried in her head because she trusted nothing she couldn't recite, the deep line through the flooded sections, the way she'd mapped by swimming it herself with a slate strapped to her wrist. He'd started reconstructing it from memory, fragment by fragment, the night before last, and he'd gotten maybe a third of it down before exhaustion took him mid-sentence.

"It runs deeper than this," Elias said. "That's the whole point of it. She designed it for exactly this scenario — high water. It uses the flooding instead of fighting it. But Juniper—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Ada died on that route. Not on it, exactly. Coming back from surveying it. But the sections she described — the cathedral run, the siphon under the old millrace — those are places where the water does things water shouldn't do. Siphons pull. If you get the timing wrong, they don't let go."

"What's the timing?"

"Tide-independent. It's all barometric." He pointed upward, toward the stone and the storm above it. "Pressure drops ahead of the storm core, the whole hydraulic system surges, and for a window — her notes said ninety minutes, give or take — the siphon reverses. Blows instead of sucks. You ride the surge through the deep section and come out past the collapse zone entirely. Skip it. Never touch the unstable ground."

Juniper stared at him. "You're telling me the safe route is riding a pressure wave through a drowned cathedral during the peak of a category-level monsoon."

"I'm telling you it's the *only* route. Whether it's safe depends entirely on whether my memory of a dead woman's verbal map survives contact with reality." He rubbed his face with both hands. "And on the window. Ninety minutes, centered on minimum barometric pressure. We'd need to be in position before the surge starts, because the entry gallery fills fast — if we're late, we're not late, we're dead."

Juniper pulled the barometer out of their kit — the good one, the one they'd bought with money they didn't have, the instrument that had kept them alive through more storms than any person deserved. The needle sat at 981 millibars and falling, steady, patient, like something walking toward you without hurrying because it already knew where you lived.

"How long until minimum?"

Elias did the calculation the way he did everything, silently and completely, before speaking. "Based on the pressure trace and the storm's ground speed — eighteen hours. Maybe twenty."

Eighteen hours to finish reconstructing a dead woman's map, load and prep the boat, and get into position at the mouth of a route neither of them had ever traveled, inside a maze that someone else was actively trying to seal shut.

The clock, Juniper thought, had just become audible. Not ticking. Rising.

* * *

They worked.

There wasn't anything else to do, and work was its own mercy — it filled the space where panic wanted to live. Juniper stripped the boat to essentials: charts, water, ration bars, the tool roll, the patched fuel can, the brass compass wrapped in cloth in their breast pocket where it had ridden since the camp. Everything else went into a dry bag to be abandoned or portaged, whichever the route demanded. They re-wrapped the hull patch below the waterline with the last of the epoxy putty, working by feel in the green-black water, fingers reading the seam like braille. The leak had been patient for days. It would stay patient. Juniper informed it of this out loud, and Elias, hauling rope ten feet away, snorted the first almost-laugh either of them had produced in hours.

Elias drew. That was the harder labor to watch — him sitting cross-legged on the foredeck with the slate balanced on his thigh, eyes closed, lips moving, dragging fragments of a dead woman's voice up out of his memory one at a time and pinning them to paper before they dissolved. *Left at the fallen column, count eight strokes. The ceiling comes down to a hand's width — breathe flat, exhale first. When the water tastes like iron, you're under the millrace; stop paddling, let it carry you, fight it and it takes your air.* He wrote until his hand shook, slept ninety minutes with his head on the chart case, woke, and wrote again.

Midway through the afternoon Juniper climbed to the highest ledge in the nave — the one Elias had survived on for a week — and looked back the way they'd come.

The western gallery, the route in, was gone.

Not hidden. Gone. Where two days ago there had been a walkable lip of stone above black water, there was now only surface, moving, purposeful, the river flowing *through* the building like blood through a heart. Somewhere beyond it, the breached archway under the spire — the way Juniper had entered, the way out they'd never once stopped assuming existed — was doing whatever submerged things did. Elias's exit was already a rumor. Theirs was already a fact.

They stood on the ledge for a long moment, letting the truth settle into the body the way cold water does, layer by layer, until it reached the bones.

One way out. One window. Eighteen hours.

Below, Elias's headlamp swung toward the ledge. "Juniper." His voice carried strangely in the vaulting, flattened by stone. "Come look at this."

He was crouched at the eastern edge of the nave, where the water slid into the passage that led toward the collapse zone. His lamp was aimed not at the water but at the wall beside it — at a section of old brick facing, colonial work, mortared and sound for as far as the light reached except for one spot where the mortar had been recently, deliberately, raked out.

Fresh rope hung from an iron piton driven into the joint. White poly, clean-sheathed, no rot, no silt. And knotted into its end, dripping, swaying gently with the current —

A cut end. Cut recently enough that the fibers hadn't frayed.

Juniper cycled through it fast, the way they read radar returns: rope means climber, climber means recent, recent means someone else is in the passage, and the only people who came into this passage came in for them. Okonjo. Hale's advance man, the one Elias had sworn he heard working in the galleries two nights ago, sealing exits, draining fuel drums, closing the maze one door at a time.

But this knot was different from sealing. Sealing was blocking. This was rigging. Someone had descended into the eastern passage — toward the collapse zone, toward the deep route, toward the only way out — and then cut their own rope behind them.

Which meant they weren't planning to climb back up.

Which meant they were already down there. Ahead of them. In the dark, in the rising water, somewhere along the only road left in the world.

The river breathed against the stone. The barometer needle eased down another millibar. And Juniper Okafor, who had spent a lifetime reading storms, realized that the worst number in the delta wasn't the centimeters per hour on the pillar.

It was one. As in, one exit. One window. And one person in the water ahead of them, waiting.
