## 14. Sabotage

Elias Wren had learned, in eleven days of darkness, to read a cavern the way other men read faces.

The passage had moods. It breathed with the tide of the river above — a slow exhalation through cracks too fine to see, carrying the smell of silt and drowned vegetation. It groaned when the water shifted its weight against old stone. It went silent in ways that meant nothing and silences that meant everything, and the difference between them was the difference between sleeping and being hunted.

So when he woke in the vaulted air pocket beneath the drowned church and the passage's breathing was wrong — too shallow, too quick, as if something upstream had pinched it shut — he knew before his eyes opened that they were no longer alone down here.

He lay still on the flat rock where Juniper had spread their one dry tarp, listening. The boat was moored twenty meters back along the gallery, tied to an iron ring some colonial engineer had driven into the stone a century and a half ago. Juniper slept badly even on good nights, and this had not been a good night; they'd taken the first watch and then refused to surrender the second, and eventually Elias had heard them lose the argument with sleep mid-sentence, their head tipping forward over the chart case like a man fainting at his own funeral.

Juniper was still asleep now. That was the first wrong thing. Elias counted the seconds of their breathing — steady, deep, unguarded — and felt the old fear rise in him with a terrible familiarity. He had slept like that once, in the first days after Hale sealed the others in. Ada had shaken him awake by the collar and told him: *In here, you don't get to be unconscious. Unconscious is how they find you.*

Ada. Her name arrived the way it always did now, without warning and without mercy, and Elias let it pass through him because there was no time to do anything else. He sat up. The dark pressed close but not total — the chemical lightstick Juniper had cracked before bedding down threw a sickly green wash across the vaulted ceiling, enough to make out shapes, not enough to trust them.

The passage was breathing wrong. He needed to know why.

"Juniper," he said quietly. Nothing. He said it again, firmer, and this time got a grunt, then movement — Juniper surfacing from sleep the way they did everything, all at once and slightly annoyed about it.

"What. What time is it. Is the boat sinking."

"The boat is fine." He was already pulling his boots on, laces stiff with damp. "Something's changed. The airflow."

Juniper blinked at him. Even half-asleep, half-drowned in exhaustion, there was a working mind behind those eyes — he'd watched it solve problems for three days now, watched it treat catastrophe as a puzzle with a solution somewhere if you just refused to panic long enough. It took perhaps four seconds for the meaning to land. Then Juniper was up, checking the lightstick, checking the chart case, checking the flare gun with the economy of someone who had learned the hard way what happens when you don't.

"You're sure?"

"I've lived in this passage longer than anyone alive should have. I know how it sounds." He stood, joints protesting. "Air moves through here from the north galleries toward the church shaft. If it's slowed, something's blocking the northern route. Or—" and he hated saying it, hated the shape of it in his mouth, "—someone's changed something."

"Changed it how? With what?" But Juniper's voice had already gone flat and careful, which meant they'd gotten there ahead of him. "Rope. Tools. A door."

"A door," Elias agreed grimly. "Hale's survey flagged three collapse zones as sealable. It was in the engineering appendix. I read every page of that report twice, looking for—" *for a way out*, he didn't say, "looking for errors. Okonjo wrote that appendix. He was proud of it. He used to talk about how a little shaped charge in the right throat of rock could turn a cave system into a tomb."

Juniper looked at him. "You knew the man who wrote the manual for burying people."

"I worked under him for six weeks." Elias picked up the flare gun, weighed it, put it down. It had one cartridge left and he'd been rationing the thought of using it the way he rationed the ration bars. "I thought I was learning cartography. Turns out I was learning demolition."

* * *

They went north along the gallery in single file, Juniper leading with the lightstick held low — Elias had taught them that, light low so your eyes keep the dark adapted, though he suspected Juniper had known it already and simply let him have the small authority of it. The water rose from ankle to knee as they progressed, cold enough to make his teeth want to chatter, and he clamped down on it because chattering teeth echo in limestone and echoes travel further than voices.

Forty meters on, Juniper stopped so abruptly that Elias nearly walked into their back.

"Tell me I'm seeing this wrong," Juniper said.

The lightstick found it: a coil of rope, modern, synthetic, its sheath still bright, looped around the same kind of iron mooring ring they'd used for the boat. Fresh rope. Not colonial, not salvage, not theirs. Beside it, wedged into a shelf of rock above the waterline, two steel drums lay on their sides, caps off, tilted so the last film of fuel had drained into a puddle that still gleamed rainbow-dark on the stone.

Elias crouched, not touching anything. His hands were shaking and he made fists of them inside the water where Juniper couldn't see. "This isn't old. Weeks, maybe less. The rope hasn't bleached. The drums haven't rusted past surface scale." He looked up the gallery, into the black beyond the lightstick's reach. "Someone brought fuel down here. Someone staged equipment. And then—" he turned, following the rope's line to where it ran taut across the gallery and vanished around a bend, "—they left it. Or they're still using it."

"Or they cut something," Juniper said. "Where's our mooring line?"

It took them nine minutes to reach the boat, wading fast, and Elias spent all nine minutes cataloguing the sounds of the passage and finding them wrong in new ways. A drip that had been regular for three days was gone. A crack in the eastern wall that used to whistle faintly on each tidal breath whistled no more. Small things. The kind of things a man could convince himself were imagination right up until the moment they weren't.

The boat sat where they'd left it, riding low and patient on its line. Juniper grabbed the mooring line and hauled it hand over hand to check the knot, and stopped.

"Elias."

The line had been cut. Not frayed, not chewed through by time or gnawed by some blind pale thing — cut, one clean stroke of a blade, the severed end floating beside the hull like a snake that had given up. The boat had been held only by the secondary line Juniper had rigged out of professional paranoia, and that secondary line was the only reason it hadn't drifted off into the flooded galleries while they slept.

Juniper stood very still, holding the two ends of the cut line, and Elias watched something move behind their face that he recognized from mirrors: the specific cold that arrives when you understand that the danger has hands.

"They were on the boat," Juniper said. "Or next to it. While we slept forty meters away." They looked at the cut end, turned it over. "One stroke. Confident. They knew exactly what they were doing and exactly how much noise it would make, which is none."

"Why not take the boat?"

"Because a missing boat tells us they're here." Juniper's voice was level, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "A drifting boat tells us we're careless. They don't want us warned. They want us comfortable until the walls close." They looked up the gallery, toward the north, where the air had stopped moving. "How many exits does this system have?"

"Four that we mapped. The church shaft we came in through. The north gallery. The eastern fissure Ada—" his voice caught on her name and he pushed through it, "—the eastern fissure Ada found. And the collapse zone Hale flagged as unstable, which runs down to the old millrace. Four."

"And now?"

Elias closed his eyes and listened to the passage breathe its shallow, strangled breath. "Now I need to check."

* * *

The north gallery was sealed.

Not collapsed — *sealed*. When the lightstick's glow finally reached the far throat of the passage, fifty meters on, Elias saw the change in the rock's silhouette and understood what he was looking at before his mind consent to name it. A collapse zone had been encouraged. The rubble cone that choked the passage was fresh — angular blocks, dust still hanging in the water around them, edges unsoftened by silt. Someone had placed charges with the precision of a surgeon closing a wound, and the wound had closed, and the patient was the way out.

Elias stood chest-deep in the cold water and stared at it and felt eleven days of darkness close around him like a fist.

"No," he said. It came out smaller than he intended. He cleared his throat. "No. This was open yesterday. I heard the draft from the camp. I *marked* the draft on the chart—" He was talking too fast and he knew it and couldn't stop. "Charges take time to place. Drilling the shot holes, tamping them, running the wire back to a safe distance. That's hours of work. Hours, in here, while we were—"

"While we were sleeping," Juniper finished. Their voice had gone quiet in the way Elias was beginning to understand meant fury banked down to coals. "While one of us kept watch, and the watch was me, and I fell asleep anyway." They turned, sweeping the lightstick back along the gallery. "They waited for us to bed down. They came in quiet — paddled, maybe, or swam the last stretch. Placed the charges hours upstream where the sound wouldn't carry past the bends. Blew it. Cut our line on the way out. All in one visit." A pause. "And they left the drums and the rope because taking them costs time and leaving them costs nothing."

"They're sealing us in," Elias said. The words tasted like the first night, like the dark after the blast that killed Ada, like Director Hale's calm voice explaining that the survey data required *containment of variables*. "Same as before. Same exact method. Seal the exits, control the timeline, let the water do the work. He doesn't even have to dirty his hands twice — he just reuses the recipe."

"Hale?"

"Hale's protocol." Elias started back down the gallery, faster than the water wanted to allow, because standing still near a fresh collapse in rising water was a way of volunteering to join it. "Search pattern, containment procedure, the engineering appendix — it's all his doctrine. Okonjo just executes it. Which means either Okonjo is down here personally, or someone trained to his standard is." He glanced back at Juniper, treading the black water behind him, face green-lit and unreadable. "Your collector. Marisol. How good are her people?"

"Good enough that I never saw one unless she wanted me to." Juniper's jaw tightened. "You think it's hers."

"I think the syndicate financed the survey through a shell company. I think whoever sealed the north gallery had access to Hale's demolition specifications, which means government or syndicate, and I think—" he stopped, because the eastern branch opened off to their left here, a low slot of black water breathing its own faint current, and the current was wrong too. Weaker. Much weaker.

They checked it together. The eastern fissure, Ada's fissure, the route she'd found and loved and described to him in the dark with such fierce tenderness — its mouth was obstructed. Not blown. *Netted.* Someone had stretched a cargo net across the underwater opening and weighted it with chain, and the chain was new, galvanized links still bright, and the net was anchored at four points with pins drilled into the limestone. Drilled. Recent. You could still smell the stone dust.

Elias hung in the water in front of it for a long time. Behind him he heard Juniper swear, softly, comprehensively, in at least two languages.

"She found this route," Elias said finally. "She kept it secret. She didn't write it down because she didn't trust paper, and she told me pieces of it, verbal only, because she said paper can be seized but memory has to be taken out of you piece by piece." His hand found the netting, the bright chain, and gripped it until the links bit his palm. "They didn't know about this one. They couldn't have. So they swept every route they *did* know and netted the ones they weren't sure of, and they caught hers in the sweep by accident, and now—"

"Now the maze is closing," Juniper said. "One exit blown, one netted. Church shaft behind us. That leaves the millrace. Through the collapse zone Hale himself flagged as unstable."

"Yes."

"How unstable?"

"'Structurally compromised. Non-traversable. Recommend permanent closure.'" Elias recited it from memory, because he recited everything from memory now; memory was the only filing cabinet he had left. "Which, coming from Okonjo's appendix, could mean genuinely lethal or could mean *we want you to think it's lethal*. There's no way to know except to go and look."

* * *

They retreated to the vaulted pocket beneath the church to think, because thinking in a moving current was a way of making bad decisions feel decisive. Juniper rigged the boat on doubled lines — three lines, actually, after Elias insisted, tied with knots he'd learned from a man who'd died doing this job — and they sat on the flat rock with the lightstick between them and the chart case open, and Elias laid out what remained of the world.

"Church shaft," he said, tapping the map. "Our entrance. It's still open — the draft comes down it. But it opens directly onto the river channel where the patrol boat runs sonar sweeps. Going out that way is walking into Hale's hand."

"North gallery. Blown."

"Eastern fissure. Netted, and even cut free, it runs shallow and twisting — the boat doesn't fit, and portaging gear through it would take a day we may not have."

"Millrace."

"Through the flagged collapse zone. Longest route. Deepest. Sections where the ceiling comes down to within a meter of the water, sections where nobody knows what the monsoon's done to the structure in eighty years." He looked up. "And it's the only exit they haven't touched."

Juniper was quiet for a moment. "That bothers me more than the ones they did touch."

"It should." Elias drew a slow breath. "Either they missed it, or they left it open on purpose. A trap needs a door that looks like hope."

Outside the vault, the passage breathed its strangled breath, and beneath it, fainter, Elias caught another sound and went rigid — a rhythmic knock, distant, arrhythmic, traveling through the stone itself rather than the air. Juniper heard it too; he saw it land in their shoulders.

"What is that?"

"Drilling," Elias said. His mouth had gone dry. "Shot holes. Somewhere south, toward the millrace." He met Juniper's eyes across the green light. "They're not sealing the exits behind us anymore, Juniper. They're starting on the one in front of us. Whatever window we had—"

The knocking came again, patient as a heartbeat, and both of them understood at the same moment that the maze was not merely closing around them. It was being closed by hands that knew exactly where they were, and the last door was already being nailed shut.
