## 15. Okonjo

The gallery narrowed the way a throat does before it swallows.

Juniper went first, wading chest-deep through water that smelled of stone and old rain, one hand trailing the wall to keep their bearings. The passage here was barely wide enough for their shoulders — colonial masonry on one side, raw karst on the other, the ceiling dropping until Elias had to duck his head behind them. Their headlamp beam bounced off the water's surface and threw jittering shadows up the walls, so that the whole corridor seemed to be moving even when they stood still.

"Thirty meters," Elias said quietly. "Then it opens into the siphon chamber. Ada's route starts past that."

"You're sure Okonjo came this way?"

"I'm sure someone did." Elias lifted the coil of fresh rope he'd found coiled around a stalagmite fifty meters back. It was synthetic, bright orange, still stiff from its packaging. "Nobody brings rope like that into a cave unless they plan to hang something from it. Or seal something off."

Juniper had stopped talking about it out loud after that. Some part of them had been waiting for this since Marisol's voice had come crackling through the satellite phone — *the syndicate financed the survey, Juniper, you were always the asset* — because if the syndicate had partners inside the government, then those partners would have people too. And those people would not wait politely at the surface for the flood to do their work.

They heard him before they saw him. Not footsteps — the water was too deep for quiet walking — but the click of a carbineer against a carabiner, metal on metal, deliberate as a knock on a door.

"Don't." The voice was calm, educated, pitched to carry in stone. "The gallery ahead of you is rigged. Old scaffolding from the survey team, plus some additions of mine. One pull on that tripwire and four tons of limestone comes down between you and your boat."

Juniper froze, hand still on the wall. Water lapped at their sternum. Behind them, Elias went very still in the way of a man who had spent eleven days learning how to be invisible.

"Come into the light," the voice said. "Slowly. Hands where I can see them."

A headlamp flared ahead — mounted on a helmet, the beam angled down at the water so it wouldn't blind them, which was either courtesy or professionalism, and Juniper suspected the latter. The man standing on a shelf of collapsed masonry was tall, gray at the temples, wearing a dry suit that fit him the way his posture did: precisely, without waste. He held no weapon. He didn't need one. He had the ceiling.

"Okonjo," Elias breathed behind Juniper. "Hale's advance man."

"Cartographer Wren." Okonjo inclined his head slightly, the way you'd greet a colleague at a conference. "You look terrible. Eleven days underground will do that. I'm impressed, frankly. We assumed the river took you."

"The river tried," Elias said.

"And yet." Okonjo turned his attention to Juniper, and Juniper got their first good look at his eyes — dark, patient, entirely without cruelty, which was worse than cruelty. Cruelty wanted something from you. This man simply wanted the outcome. "And you must be Okafor. The storm chaser. I'll admit, when Hale told me the syndicate had put a weather junkie on the river as deniable salvage, I expected someone rougher."

"They were out of rougher," Juniper said. "Budget cuts."

Something flickered at the corner of Okonjo's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that the joke had been received and filed. "I'm going to make this simple, because we have perhaps forty minutes before the next band hits and the water in this gallery rises half a meter. You've spoken to Marisol Voss. She offered you debt forgiveness for the originals and the witness. I'm offering you the same thing."

"From Hale?"

"From the partnership." Okonjo crouched, unhurried, resting one forearm on his knee. In the headlamp light his face was all planes and patience. "Which I suspect Marisol didn't mention, because she's planning to betray us, just as we're planning to betray her. That's what partnerships are, Okafor. Two knives agreeing to share a sheath."

Juniper heard Elias shift behind them — a small sound, weight moving from one foot to the other, the sound of a man calculating distances to an exit that didn't exist.

"The survey wasn't Hale's idea," Okonjo continued. "It was seed money from a shell company the syndicate owns. Hale provided the permits, the personnel, the official cover. The syndicate provided capital and, when the time came, a disposable contractor already drowning in debt who could plausibly be blamed for everything — the theft of the charts, the deaths, all of it. A storm chaser lost in monsoon season. Tragic. Unprecedented. No one asks questions about the weather."

"You're describing yourself," Juniper said. "Right now. Standing in the thing you're blaming me for."

"I'm describing the architecture," Okonjo said mildly. "I'm merely a load-bearing element. Here is my offer, and unlike Marisol's, it doesn't require you to trust me further than arm's length. Give me the charts and Wren. Walk away. Your debt is erased — I have the authority to transmit that to Marisol's office myself, which means you don't have to take her word for anything. And in six months, when the delta's water rights are restructured and the land along the new channels changes hands, you'll find that a certain storm-chasing outfit has new equipment, new insurance, and no creditors. You can go back to chasing cells over open water instead of running from them."

The water rose. Juniper felt it — a slow, cold climb, a centimeter, two, the river above pushing down through cracks in the world like pressure through a bruise. Their heart was doing something strange, not racing but heavy, each beat landing like a dropped anchor.

Because the offer was good. That was the obscene part. It was better than good; it was everything they'd walked onto the river wanting. Debt gone. Boat replaced. A life handed back, intact, with interest. All it cost was a starving man behind them and a map that would decide whether a hundred thousand people drowned on schedule every year or got to keep their towns.

"You killed Ada," Elias said from behind Juniper. His voice was flat, scraped thin, but steady. "Or Hale did, on your schedule. She went back for the originals and the passage sealed behind her."

"We sealed the entrance," Okonjo said, and there it was — the first crack in the calm, not guilt exactly, but the effort of holding the guilt down, like ballast. "Director Hale gave the order. I executed it. I believed the two dissenters were going to sell the survey data to a rival consortium, which would have resulted in the same land grab by worse hands. I still believe that. If it helps, Ms. Ferro's death was never the intent. The intent was containment."

"It doesn't help," Elias said.

"No," Okonjo agreed quietly. "It rarely does."

Juniper's mind was working now, fast and cold, the way it worked inside a storm cell when the mesocyclone tightened and every second of hesitation cost you options. They catalogued the gallery. The rigged scaffolding overhead — old survey timber braced against the limestone, plus newer line, plus whatever charges or counterweights Okonjo had hung in the dark. The tripwire he'd mentioned, presumably strung across the water somewhere in the black ahead of them. The shelf Okonjo stood on, maybe a meter above the waterline, close enough to the rigged section that he trusted it completely.

That was the tell. He was standing under his own trap because he was certain of it. Certain men stand under their own traps.

"Say I believe you," Juniper said. "About the rival consortium. About the worse hands. Say the map ends up owned by someone either way — isn't it better it's someone who at least kept me alive to negotiate?"

Okonjo tilted his head. "Now you're thinking like a partner."

"I'm thinking like a debtor. There's a difference, but it's narrow." Juniper let their shoulders drop, let exhaustion into their frame, real exhaustion, no acting required. "Here's my problem. The charts are copied. Elias has the route memorized — you know that, right? You can't contain him by containing paper. Kill him and the map dies with him, fine, but then you've spent a corpse and gotten nothing, and Hale's whole project stalls anyway. So what I actually hold is leverage, and leverage doesn't get sold in a flooded hallway. It gets sold at a table."

"Then we walk to a table."

"After the storm. After the water drops. You give me a channel frequency, I surface when it's safe, we talk terms with lawyers and witnesses and all the civilized furniture." Juniper paused. "Unless, of course, the plan is that I don't surface. Which, given the mooring line someone cut, given the exits someone's been sealing — fresh rope, drained fuel drums, Okonjo, I've been reading your trail for two days — I'm inclined to think the table doesn't exist."

The silence stretched. Water dripped somewhere deep in the dark, patient as a metronome.

"There is no table," Okonjo said finally, and his voice hadn't changed at all, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. "There was never going to be a table. But I want you to understand that I'm sorry about that. You were supposed to be simpler. Marisol assured us you were motivated purely by solvency."

"She was right," Juniper said. "For most of my life, she was right."

"But?"

"But I dove into a drowned church last week and found a man guarding a dead woman's map with a flare gun, and something about that rearranged my accounting." Juniper shifted their weight, subtle, testing the bottom — gravel and broken masonry, ankle-deep footing under the chest-deep water. "So here's my counteroffer. You let us pass. You report to Hale that the gallery collapsed early, that we're sealed in, that the river solved your problem. The monsoon does the rest. Nobody checks a collapse zone during flood season. You get your containment, I get my life, and Elias—" Juniper glanced back, just briefly. "Elias gets to decide what happens to Ada's work, which is more than Ada got."

Okonjo looked at them for a long moment. Then he sighed, and it was almost genuine.

"I admire the negotiation," he said. "Truly. But you've just told me you won't surface to any table I control, which means you're never surfacing where I can reach you, which means the only rational move left is the one I've been avoiding." He reached up, slowly, and touched something at his collar — a radio, or a trigger, Juniper couldn't see which. "I really did hope you'd be simple."

"Everyone keeps saying that," Juniper said, and pulled the wall brace.

It wasn't courage. Juniper would spend years refusing to call it courage. It was arithmetic. They had spent three days studying the survey scaffolding as they passed it — Elias narrating, *that's original 1970s timber, that brace is cracked, that joint is dry-rotted* — the way a storm chaser studies a wall cloud, looking for the flaw, the shear, the place where the structure stops believing in itself. And twenty minutes ago, wading past Okonjo's rigging, they had seen it: the advance man had anchored his new lines to the old survey braces, because the old braces were solid and convenient, and one of them — the third one, the cracked one, the one Elias had called out with a touch and a look — was holding both his trap and his confidence.

Juniper's hand found it in the dark and shoved sideways with everything they had.

The crack had been waiting years for permission. The timber split with a sound like a rifle shot, and the geometry of the gallery changed — Okonjo's rigging, built on the assumption that the brace would hold, did not hold. Lines snapped taut and then slack. Somewhere overhead a counterweight released, and the scaffolding — decades-old timber and Okonjo's careful modern additions — folded inward with a groan that became a roar.

"Limestone!" Elias screamed, and tackled Juniper sideways into deeper water as the first chunk came down.

The world became noise and white spray. Four tons of ceiling, Okonjo had said, and Juniper believed every gram of it now, felt the shockwave of it through the water, through their ribs, through the stone under their feet. Dust exploded outward in a choking wall, turning their headlamp beam into a brown fog. Something the size of a torso hit the water two meters away and the wave it threw knocked them fully under.

For a moment there was nothing but black water and the ringing aftershock of stone, and Juniper thought, distantly, with the strange calm of the nearly dead: *I just killed a man.*

Then they surfaced, coughing, and Elias hauled them toward the downstream side of the rubble by the collar of their jacket.

"Move," he rasped. "Dust settles, water rises, and if any piece of him is alive over there, he'll be angry."

"Don't—" Juniper choked. "Don't say it like that."

"Like what? Like it's true?" Elias pushed them forward through the fog, one arm hooked under theirs, dragging them through water suddenly thick with silt. "You dropped a ceiling on a man, Okafor. Own the sentence. It's the price of the exit."

The gallery beyond the collapse opened exactly as Elias had promised — into the siphon chamber, a cathedral of wet stone where their boat waited where they'd cached it, tied to a masonry stub, riding low on accumulated seepage. Juniper climbed aboard on shaking arms and turned, sweeping the headlamp back toward the rubble field.

The dust was settling in slow curtains. Under it, half-buried at the edge of the fall zone, Okonjo lay on his side on a shelf of fallen masonry, above the waterline, and he was moving. One leg bent wrong beneath him — the dry suit torn at the thigh, dark spreading — but his hands were working at the rubble, pushing a slab off his chest, and when the headlamp caught his face he looked directly into the light.

He didn't shout. He didn't curse. He raised one bloodied hand — not a plea, Juniper realized. A salute. Or a promise. With a man like Okonjo, Juniper suspected there was no difference.

"He's alive," Juniper said. Their voice came out strange, thin, belonging to someone else.

"Yes," Elias said. He was already untying the boat, movements quick and mechanical, the efficiency of a man who had decided something. "His radio survived, probably. Which means Hale knows within the hour that we're in the passage, and which exit we're taking, and exactly how desperate we are." He looked up, and in the lamplight his face was hollowed and grim and absolutely certain. "Ada's route runs through the deep section. Six hours, maybe eight, chest-deep the whole way, one air pocket at the halfway mark the size of a coffin. We need to start now."

Juniper stood in the boat, engine cord in their hand, and looked back one more time at the small figure in the dust, at the raised hand, at the leg bent the wrong way — a violence they had chosen, that couldn't be chosen back, that would follow them out of the dark and into whatever came after.

*I owed everyone,* they thought. *Now I owe him.*

They pulled the cord. The engine coughed, caught, and carried them into the black.
