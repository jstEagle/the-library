## 16. Ada's Route

Elias Wren had not slept in a place with a ceiling he trusted in nineteen days, and the vaulted air pocket above the drowned church was the closest thing to a room he'd had since Ada died. The water below them lay black and still, holding their boat like a held breath. Somewhere behind them, through two galleries and a collapse zone, Okonjo was either dead under rock or dragging himself toward a radio, and both possibilities meant the same thing: they could not go back the way they came.

Juniper sat on the gunwale, wringing out their shirt, saying nothing. They had said nothing for an hour, which Elias understood. There was a particular silence that followed violence done out of necessity — not guilt exactly, not yet, just the raw awareness that you had become someone who could do it. He'd worn that silence himself once, eleven days ago, swimming away from the sound of Hale's men sealing the south gallery with his colleagues on the wrong side of it.

He broke the silence because someone had to, and because there was no version of the next six hours that didn't require him to say what he was about to say.

"There's another way out," he said.

Juniper looked up. In the flare-light — they were rationing the lantern, down to its last hours of charge — their face was all angles and salt water. "The exit you used."

"Is under four meters of river now. Maybe five. It's gone." He let that land. "But Ada mapped a second route. Verbally. She never wrote it down."

"Why wouldn't she write it down?"

Elias almost smiled. It hurt his cracked lips. "Because Ada Osei did not trust paper. Paper can be seized. Paper can be subpoenaed. Paper tells whoever finds it exactly how smart you were." He tapped his own temple. "She kept the dangerous things here, and she made me keep them here too, drilled into me over three years of fieldwork until I could recite them in my sleep. She called it her insurance policy against being murdered by her employers." His voice caught on the last word, and he let it catch. He was done smoothing himself over for anyone's comfort.

Juniper went still. "You're telling me she expected this."

"I'm telling you Ada was the best hydrographic surveyor I ever met, and she grew up in a country where maps got people killed, and she planned accordingly." He stood, carefully, the boat rocking under him. "I need your chart table. Or whatever flat surface we have. And I need you to not touch anything while I work."

* * *

What he drew, he drew from memory, and the memory was a mosaic of a hundred small moments he had never realized he was memorizing.

Ada teaching him knots in the survey camp at dusk, using a length of paracord and a lantern: *"If we're ever running dark, you'll want the bowline one-handed, Elias. Cold hands. Wet hands. Practice until your fingers are bored of it."*

Ada standing at the rail of the *Kestrel*, their survey launch, pointing at a seam in the water where two currents braided together: *"See that line? That's not surface noise. That's a pressure differential. Under us, right now, there's a channel the old colonials never charted because their instruments couldn't read still water. Remember the feel of it. Instruments break. Feelings don't."*

Ada, three weeks ago, the night before everything went wrong, sitting across from him in the mess tent with a cup of cold tea she never drank, speaking low and fast like a woman defusing something: *"If this goes sideways — and I think it might, Elias, I think Hale is going to do something stupid and permanent — don't take the south gallery. Everyone will take the south gallery because it's the way you came in and panic runs backward. Take the deep route. Down past the cisterns, through the flooded colonnade, up the ventilation shaft at the old customs house. It's longer. It's wetter. It's the only way out that Hale doesn't know exists, because I found it alone and told no one but you."*

*"Why tell only me?"* he'd asked.

And she had looked at him with that expression — patient, tired, fond, the expression of a woman carrying weight she refused to set down — and said: *"Because paper burns, and people drown, and the only archive that survives is the one walking around inside a friend."*

He had laughed, then. Three weeks ago. He had thought she was being dramatic, the way brilliant people sometimes were, the way you let yourself be dramatic when the stakes were still theoretical.

Now he drew her route on the back of Juniper's waterproof chart case, in grease pencil, from a memory that felt less like remembering and more like excavation.

"The cisterns first," he murmured, sketching. "Beneath the church. Colonial-era reservoirs, brick vaulting, probably intact — the arches we passed were load-bearing and dry on top, which means the vaults below are holding. We drop through a shaft into the first cistern, transit submerged — fully submerged, Juniper, twenty meters, maybe thirty, no air pockets — and come up in the second cistern through a flooded stairwell."

Juniper stared at the grease-pencil lines. "Submerged transit. In this water. At night. With a boat."

"Not the boat. Not through the cisterns." He kept drawing. "We portage. Break the boat down — you said the engine unbolts, you said you've done it before — carry everything through on our backs, reassemble on the far side of the second cistern where the colonnade opens up. Then the boat again, through the flooded colonnade, which Ada said has a navigable ceiling for two hundred meters before the ventilation shaft."

"And after the shaft?"

"After the shaft is the old customs house. Pre-colonial foundations, colonial superstructure, abandoned since before the war. It sits on high ground — relatively high ground — north of the flood line." He sat back. His hand was shaking, and he watched it shake with a kind of detached interest. "That's the route. That's all of it. Every turn, every depth, every hazard, exactly as she gave it to me."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You've never run it."

"No."

"You're asking me to dive blind through a submerged cistern based on a route a dead woman recited over tea."

"Yes." He met their eyes. "I'm asking you to trust her the way I trusted her. Which is to say completely, and without proof, and because the alternative is staying here until the monsoon fills this room like a bathtub."

* * *

They prepared in the dark, mostly by touch, because the lantern was dying and they needed its last hour for the transit itself.

Elias broke down the boat's engine while Juniper stripped the hull fittings, and the work steadied him. There was a mercy in tasks — Ada had taught him that too, early on, when he'd frozen during his first flood-season survey and she'd put a clipboard in his hands and said, *hands remember what the mind forgets, so give the mind something to hold.* His fingers knew the bolts. His fingers knew the sequence. He worked and let the grief move through him like current through a channel, unstoppable but navigable, and somewhere in the middle of the third bolt he realized he was crying without any interruption to the work at all, tears simply joining the river water already everywhere, indistinguishable, private.

"Tell me about her," Juniper said quietly. Not looking at him. Giving him the courtesy of a sidelong conversation, the kind you could have with your hands busy.

"She was born in Accra. Hydrology degree in Kumasi, doctorate in Delft. She came to delta work because—" he laughed, wet and short, "—because she said every government that ever ruined a delta did it with a map, so the honest maps had to come from somewhere else. She believed cartography was a moral act. She believed a map was a promise you made to strangers." He seated the last bolt and wrapped the engine in the tarp. "Hale wanted a map as a weapon. Ada spent three years making sure the honest version existed too. She hid it inside herself and inside me, and she died before she could—"

He stopped. Corrected himself. Because it mattered, suddenly, to get it exactly right.

"She didn't die before she could finish it. She finished it. She finished it in me. That was the whole design." He cinched the tarp line one-handed, bowline, cold hands, wet hands, fingers bored of it. "I'm not carrying her memory, Juniper. I'm carrying her work. There's a difference. Memory is a thing you grieve. Work is a thing you do."

Juniper looked at him then, really looked, and something passed between them that had no words attached — recognition, maybe, of two people who had both been built into instruments by other people's designs, and were choosing, now, what to be instruments of.

"Okay," Juniper said. "Cisterns. Tell me the dive."

* * *

The shaft down into the first cistern was a brick throat barely wider than their shoulders, and the water at its bottom was cold enough to stop breath in the chest.

They went one at a time. Juniper first, with the gear bag lashed tight and the compass hanging around their neck on its cord — Elias had insisted on that, though neither of them said why out loud. Some part of both of them had begun to treat the brass compass as a talisman, and Elias saw no reason to argue with superstition that improved morale.

Then it was his turn, and he stood at the edge of the black water with the engine wrapped on his back and thought about Ada's voice one more time. Not the tea, not the warnings. The rail of the *Kestrel*, the braided currents, her hand steady on the rail: *instruments break, feelings don't.*

"Elias." Juniper's voice floated up the shaft, distorted by brick and water. "Thirty meters. Count your kicks. I'll be at the stairwell light-gap — I'll have the lantern on its last charge pointed at the ceiling. You swim at the gap. Don't think. Swim."

"Don't think," he repeated. "Swim."

He went under.

The cold hit him like a verdict. The dark was total — not cave-dark, not night-dark, but the dark of water that had been sitting underground for two hundred years, water that had never known a single photon. He kicked, counting, and the engine dragged at his shoulders and the cold closed around his throat and somewhere around kick twelve the animal part of his brain began screaming that he was going to die here, that this was exactly how Ada's route ended, that he had traded a certain death by drowning-in-a-room for an uncertain death by drowning-in-the-dark—

*Instruments break,* Ada said, in the voice she used when he was panicking, calm as bedrock. *Feelings don't.*

He swam at the feeling. The feeling said: left, gently left, follow the curve of the vault, the current wants to help you, let it. Kick fifteen. Twenty. His lungs began to burn and he made himself exhale a thin stream of bubbles, watching them rise and vanish, spending his air like a man spending his last coins. Twenty-five. The cold had passed through him and out the other side into a strange clarity, and in that clarity he could feel it — the pressure differential, the braided seam, the thing Ada had pointed at from a boat deck three years ago. The cistern's far wall was near. The stairwell was near. She had been right. She had been right about everything, and she was dead, and he was alive, and both facts were the same fact: the archive walks.

Kick twenty-nine. A gray smudge above. Kick thirty-one — light, actual light, the lantern's dying beam smeared across a brick ceiling — and he surfaced into the second cistern gasping, hauling himself onto a submerged step, and Juniper's hands were on his pack strap pulling him up, and for a moment neither of them spoke because speech would have cost air they might need later.

Then Juniper said, "You're blue."

"I'm efficient," Elias managed.

* * *

The colonnade was easier, and easier was its own kind of terror, because after the cisterns his body kept waiting for the easy part to kill him too.

They reassembled the boat on a wide landing where the cistern opened into a hall of columns — pre-colonial stone, older than anything the Dutch or the British had raised, columns carved with river glyphs that the flood had swallowed to their capitals. Elias ran the engine's fuel line with numb fingers while Juniper checked the patched hull, and the boat sat on black water between the pillars like a small stubborn animal in a cathedral.

"Two hundred meters," Elias said, consulting the grease-pencil map on the chart case. "Ceiling stays high. Then the ventilation shaft rises on the left — Ada said look for a masonry lip, a ledge, like a shelf cut into the column face."

"And if the ledge isn't there?"

"Then Ada lied to me over tea, which she never did, because lying was the one thing she considered worse than murder." He said it flatly, and heard what it sounded like, and didn't apologize for it. Grief had burned off his diplomacy days ago. What was left was the ore.

They motored slow through the colonnade, Juniper at the tiller, Elias at the bow with the compass open on his palm. The needle didn't point north. It hadn't pointed north since the chart table in the abandoned camp. It pointed forward and slightly down, along the axis of the passage, faithful as a dog, and Elias had stopped trying to explain it and started simply trusting it, the way he trusted Ada, the way Juniper was learning to trust him — completely, and without proof.

At a hundred and eighty meters, the masonry lip appeared on the left, exactly as described, exactly as remembered, and something in Elias's chest unlocked that he hadn't known was locked.

"There," he said, and his voice cracked clean in half. "There it is. She wasn't wrong. She wasn't—" He pressed the back of his wrist against his mouth. Above them, through the shaft, a thread of gray daylight was visible. Actual sky. Storm-colored, bruised, but real.

Juniper brought the boat alongside the ledge and held it there with the throttle, and looked at him with an expression that was, for once, entirely unguarded.

"She trained you well," Juniper said.

"She trained me for this," Elias said. "I just didn't know what *this* was." He looked up the shaft, at the thread of stormlight, and made himself say the rest of it, the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. "Customs house puts us on high ground north of the flood line. But Juniper — the monsoon peaks tomorrow. Whatever's waiting up there, Hale's people, Marisol's people, the storm itself — we surface into all of it, with a broken boat, a copied map, and nothing else."

Juniper killed the engine. In the sudden quiet, the water lapped against the old stones, patient as time.

"Then we'd better decide what we're willing to trade for it," Juniper said, "before we climb."

Above them, the thread of daylight flickered — once, twice — dimmed, and brightened again. Not clouds, Elias thought, feeling the cold walk up his spine. Something moving between the shaft and the sky. Something up there, at the customs house, casting a shadow across the only way out.

Someone had reached the exit first.
