## 17. The Long Dark

The water in Ada's Route came up to Juniper's sternum and stayed there.

They had measured it with a knotted line at the last air pocket — the vaulted chamber Elias called the Vestry — and the number had not improved since. If anything it had climbed two fingers' width while they worked, the monsoon pressing down through the limestone like a thumb on a wound. Juniper stood chest-deep in black water beside the disassembled boat and tried to think of it as a job. A salvage contract. Gear to move from point A to point B before the site flooded out entirely.

Except the gear was a boat in eleven pieces, and point B was four hundred meters of drowned gallery that no one had ever portaged on purpose, and the other person doing the carrying was shivering so hard his teeth clicked like a telegraph key.

"Elias." Juniper snapped their fingers twice, the way you did to cut through hypothermia fog. "Talk to me. What's the water temperature feel like?"

"Cold," Elias said, with the flat precision of a man conserving words like fuel.

"Good answer. Wrong question." Juniper hauled the outboard's lower unit up onto the improvised raft — three lashed sections of hull plank riding on two inflated fenders — and cinched the strap. "Try again. Numb, or burning?"

"Burning."

"Burning's good. Burning means your nerves are still filing complaints." They pressed the second flare into his hand — the last one — and closed his fingers around it. "You hold this. You don't use it unless the ceiling comes down or I say the word *Vestry* backward. Those are the only two reasons. Everything else, we walk through."

Elias looked at the flare, then at Juniper. In the beam of the headlamp his face was gray as old paper, lips blued at the edges, but his eyes were still doing cartography — measuring the gallery ahead, the waterline against the wall, the slope of the ceiling where it dipped toward its next submerged throat. Eleven days alone in the dark had built a man who trusted measurements more than people. Juniper was beginning to understand that this was not cynicism. It was how he'd stayed alive.

"Four hundred meters," Elias said. "Three low sections. The deepest is here—" he touched his own sternum, marking the depth on his body the way he marked it on charts, "—maybe two meters of headroom gone. We swim it or we don't."

"We swim it. Boat goes over us on the raft, we go under, we come up in the pocket past it. You showed me on the chart. Pocket's got air, pocket's got a shelf, shelf gets you out of the water for ninety seconds while you stop shaking." Juniper shouldered the first bundle — the wrapped chart case, waterproofed twice, strapped high across their back where the water couldn't argue with it. "Ninety seconds. That's the whole plan. Ninety seconds at a time until we're somewhere the sky can find us."

"And if there's no air in the pocket?"

"Then I'll be very embarrassed, because I already told you the whole plan."

Something moved at the corner of Elias's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one, maybe, some fossil humor preserved in a starving man's face. He picked up his end of the raft line.

"All right," he said. "Ninety seconds at a time."

* * *

The dark in the passage was not the dark of night. Night had edges — horizon, starlight, the memory of weather. This dark was total, a substance, something the water carried and the stone confirmed. Their headlamps carved cones out of it and the cones ended abruptly, as if the darkness had been poured and set.

Juniper went first, towing the raft by a short line, calling the bottom to Elias behind them. "Rock shelf, left side, hip height. Drop-off after — half a meter, soft silt, keep your feet moving. Debris field coming up, watch the rebar." Old scaffolding from some colonial-era attempt to shore the tunnel, rusted to lace, sharp as broken promises. Juniper had fouled a propeller on it once already, in another life, another section of the passage. Now they stepped over its bones carefully, reverently, the way you stepped over anything that had nearly killed you.

The patter helped. Juniper knew it helped because it helped them — storm chasers talked constantly in the field, a running narration against fear, data read aloud into the wind so the wind couldn't fill the silence with worse things. So they narrated.

"You know what a supercell looks like on radar?" they called back, voice bouncing flat off the low ceiling. "It looks like a fist. A hook echo, we call it — the rotation wraps around and draws this little comma shape, and every chaser who's ever died looked at that comma and thought, *that's beautiful, let's get closer.*"

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because right now you're looking at four hundred meters of flooded cave thinking about drowning, and I need you thinking about weather instead. Weather's honest. Weather doesn't care about you, which means it isn't *against* you. This water isn't against you either. It's just deep. There's a difference."

Elias made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed along with everything else. "You're a strange person, Okafor."

"I'm a professional. There's overlap."

The water rose as they went deeper — waist, then chest, then the awful moment when it lifted them both onto their toes and the bottom became a rumor. The gallery narrowed. Ahead, the ceiling met the water in a black seam, and beyond the seam the passage bent away into nothing their lamps could reach.

Juniper stopped at the seam and checked the raft: chart case secured, engine parts lashed, the coil of rope and the spare air tank — salvaged from the survey camp, half-full, better than nothing — slung where they could grab it blind. Then they turned and looked at Elias.

He was shaking badly now. Not the fine tremor of an hour ago but full-body shudders, jaw clenched to keep the teeth quiet, hands white-knuckled on the line. His lips had gone from blue toward gray. Juniper had seen hypothermia walk people into bad decisions before; the cold didn't make you stupid, it made you *agreeable*, willing to lie down, willing to stop, and stopping in two meters of flooded cave was just drowning with extra steps.

"Last chance for the easy version," Juniper said. "We turn around, we go back to the Vestry, we wait for the water to drop."

"It won't drop." Elias's voice was steady, which frightened Juniper more than the shaking. "Monsoon peaks tomorrow. You said it yourself. This route closes tonight whether we're in it or not." He pulled the flare against his chest like a talisman. "Ada ran this stretch twice. She said the pocket past the seam has air and a shelf. She never lied about a measurement in her life."

"She's dead, Elias."

"I know what she is." For a moment the cartographer looked out from behind the exhaustion, the man who had memorized a map because he didn't trust paper, who had watched his partner die protecting lines drawn in her head instead of ink. "That's why I trust her."

There was nothing to say to that. Juniper nodded once, clipped the tank harness across their own shoulders, and took a breath that felt like borrowing.

"On three," they said. "One. Two—"

They went under together.

* * *

The submerged throat was eleven meters long. Juniper knew because they'd paced it on the chart, converted Elias's hand-drawn scale into body-lengths, drilled the number into themselves the way you drilled a bearing before flying into cloud. Eleven meters. Fifteen seconds at a hard swim, dragging a raft over your head through a gap with less than half a meter of clearance, in water so cold it rang against your eardrums, in dark so complete that the headlamp showed nothing but suspended silt turning in the beam like dirty snow.

At meter seven, the raft snagged.

Juniper felt it happen — the line going taut above them, the drag suddenly wrong, the whole fragile convoy halting in the throat of the stone. Their lungs were already burning. They had perhaps eight seconds of margin left, and the arithmetic of it arrived all at once, clean and merciless: go back, or go up, and up was stone.

They went forward.

Hands found the snag blind — the raft's corner wedged under a nub of rock, a knob of flowstone grown down from the ceiling like a tooth. Juniper braced one foot against the wall, hooked the line, and *pulled*, shoulders screaming, vision starting to prickle at the edges, and the raft tore free with a scrape that sounded enormous underwater and probably wasn't, and then the seam of gray light was ahead — the air pocket, the shelf, the ninety seconds — and they broke the surface gasping like landed fish, hauling the raft up after them, reaching back down into the black for the line, for Elias—

His hand found theirs.

Juniper pulled, and Elias Wren came up out of the dark coughing and alive, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all. They just breathed. The pocket was small — a dome of stone maybe two meters across, the shelf a sloping ledge barely big enough for both of them — and the sound of their breathing filled it completely, ragged and human and loud.

"Ninety seconds," Juniper managed finally. "Starting now."

Elias dragged himself onto the shelf and lay there streaming, shivering in great rolling waves, and Juniper got the emergency blanket out of the dry bag and wrapped him in it without asking, the way you patched a hull without asking, because the boat was the mission and the mission didn't negotiate. Under the foil he was still trembling, but slower now. His eyes tracked up to the dome of rock overhead, measuring it, cataloguing it, and Juniper realized he was mapping even now — adding this pocket to the map in his head, the map that existed nowhere else on earth.

"There's a calcite vein," he said hoarsely. "Runs up through the dome. Ada used it as a landmark. Said it looked like lightning frozen in the stone."

Juniper looked. The vein caught the lamplight and threw it back in white branches, yes — like lightning, like a hook echo, like every beautiful dangerous thing they had ever driven toward. "She had an eye for it."

"She had an eye for everything. She just didn't write any of it down." Elias's voice cracked, and he covered it with a cough. "People thought she was careless. She wasn't careless. She was careful about exactly one thing — who could steal what she knew. Paper can be taken. A dead man's memory can't be interrogated." He looked at Juniper sideways. "Though I suppose Hale's people would disagree. They've been trying to interrogate mine for two weeks."

"Two weeks," Juniper said. "Eleven days in the dark, and now this. You should be dead three times over."

"I did the math on that too." A ghost of the almost-smile again. "I decided to be inconvenient instead."

Juniper laughed — a short, startled bark that echoed strangely in the stone dome — and then checked the time, because devotion was fine but the clock was real. Sixty seconds gone. The water below the shelf had risen visibly since they surfaced, licking higher up the walls, patient as interest accruing.

"Next section's shorter," they said, helping Elias up. "Sixty meters, mostly wading, one swim under a collapse pile. After that the gallery climbs — Elias, listen to me — after that the gallery *climbs*. Real air. Room to stand. Maybe even room to get the boat back together."

"And after the climb?"

"After the climb, the sky." Juniper shouldered the chart case, tested the raft line, and found Elias watching them with an expression that had shed most of its suspicion somewhere back in the black water. It looked, now, disconcertingly like trust.

"Okafor," he said. "Back at the survey camp. Why did you come down? The radio told you to leave. Anyone sensible would have left."

Juniper thought about Marisol's smile, the seventy-two hours, the debt notice folded in their jacket like a second heartbeat. Thought about the compass pointing south, pointing *down*, pointing at something someone wanted buried.

"Because everyone kept telling me what to do with it," they said. "And I've spent two years being paid to be sensible. Figured I'd try one thing I wasn't paid for."

Elias held their gaze a moment longer, then nodded, once, the way he must have nodded at Ada's measurements — acceptance of a reading, entry into the record.

"Together, then," he said, and stepped off the shelf into the rising water, and Juniper followed him down into the long dark, talking the whole way — about hook echoes and barometric drops and the exact color the sky turns before a wall cloud eats the horizon — filling the silence so it couldn't fill itself with worse things, until the gallery began, at last, to climb.

They were fifty meters from the climb's crest, close enough to taste the cleaner air moving down from above, when the light reached them.

Not their light. A thin, sourceless glow bleeding around a bend in the upper gallery — electric white, steady, wrong. Juniper killed their headlamp instantly and pulled Elias against the wall, and they stood together in the dark, chests heaving, listening.

Voices. Faint, distorted by stone, but unmistakable. More than one. And beneath them, a rhythmic mechanical knock — metal on rock, patient, methodical.

Someone was drilling.

Elias's hand found Juniper's arm in the dark and gripped it, and his whisper was barely breath: "That's the north gallery. There's no way in from the river. Someone's opening it from the *other side*."
