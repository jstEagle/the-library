## 21. Flood Season

The eye was closing.

Juniper felt it the way they felt every storm they'd ever chased — a pressure change in the teeth, a hush that wasn't really a hush but the sky drawing breath. The calm had lasted forty minutes. Long enough for Hale's patrol boat and Marisol's collectors to find each other in the middle of the drowned delta and start tearing each other apart. Long enough for Juniper to get Elias over the side with the charts strapped to his chest, both of them swimming through water the color of old tea toward the shoal where the boat lay half-swamped and still, impossibly, theirs.

Long enough to make a decision.

Now the western horizon had gone the color of a bruise going bad — a wall of cloud from the waterline to the top of the sky, stacked and turning, lit from inside by lightning that flickered like something trying to escape. The monsoon's final band. The last wall. Every forecast model Juniper had ever run said the same thing: when this hit, nothing on the surface of the delta would be safe, and nothing beneath it either.

The air pocket where Elias had lived for eleven days would fill like a lung drowning.

"Tell me you're getting signal," Elias said. He was on his knees in the bilge, wringing out the waterproof case he'd carried through the flood, his hands shaking so hard the case rattled against the deck boards. Eleven days underground, hypothermia twice survived, and now this. "Tell me the antenna survived."

Juniper didn't answer right away. They were standing at the console, one hand braced against the frame as the boat rocked on the shoal, looking at the mast. It leaned. Not broken — bent, maybe fifteen degrees off true, the whip antenna still attached, still trailing its guy wire into the water where it had come loose during the swim. They'd hauled it back up with numb fingers while the eye held. The transceiver's power light glowed a steady, stupid green.

"We've got signal," Juniper said. "Question is how much time."

Elias looked up at them. His face was hollowed out, cheekbones like a diagram of themselves, and there was something in his eyes Juniper had learned to read over the past two days in the dark: not fear exactly. Readiness. The look of a man who had already lost everything once and found out he could survive it.

"The map," he said. "You've decided."

Juniper had. Somewhere between the vaulted ceiling of the drowned church and Okonjo's blood on the gallery stones, somewhere between Marisol's voice saying *you were always ours* and the eye opening above them like a hole punched in the world, the decision had made itself. They just hadn't said it out loud yet, because saying it made it real, and real things could be taken away.

They said it anyway.

"I'm sending it to everyone."

* * *

The list took four minutes to build, and Juniper built it the way they'd built chase routes for nine years: fast, precise, no second-guessing. Every news desk within satellite range — the coastal broadcasters, the national wire services, the independent hydrology bloggers who'd been screaming about delta mismanagement for a decade. Every regional water authority office whose email addresses were public record. Every village council in the lower basin; Elias had recited those from memory, council by council, names of chairmen and schoolteachers who doubled as radio operators, because Ada had known them all and Ada had never trusted paper and so neither did Elias until the paper stopped mattering.

The files went out in three formats. The full survey charts, Hale's own scans, unaltered. Elias's annotations — the sections Hale had flagged, the waterway junctions where control structures could turn a flood left or right, upstream or down, onto farmland or away from it, onto villages or around them. And the audio. Marisol's voice from the satellite phone, *the syndicate financed your survey through Meridian Holdings, Director Hale signed the work order himself,* and beneath it, recovered from the survey team's own field recorder, which Elias had carried out of the dark wrapped in oilcloth: Hale's voice, calm as a weather report, ordering the passage sealed with two of his own surveyors inside it.

"You're sure about the audio?" Elias asked quietly. He stood behind Juniper's shoulder, dripping, watching the screen. "Once it's out—"

"It's already out." Juniper pressed send.

The progress bar crawled. Signal strength flickered — 40 percent, 55, 31 — and outside the eye's edge the first outrider gusts came across the water, flattening the rain-fog into streaks, rocking the boat against the shoal with a grinding shudder of hull on sand. Juniper watched each packet confirm. News desk. News desk. Water authority. Village council, Kessuma Ward. Village council, Lower Oshun. The wire service bounced once, retried, confirmed.

Eighty-one recipients. Eighty-one doors opened at once, and no way to close any of them.

The last confirmation landed at 61 percent signal, and then the bar vanished and the outbox sat empty and the map of the broken passage belonged to the world.

Elias exhaled. It sounded like it had been held for eleven days.

"They'll spin it," he said. "Hale will say the data's fabricated. The syndicate will bury it under lawyers by Monday."

"Probably." Juniper killed the transmitter to save power. "But the village councils have it. You can't lawyer a thousand people who already know which way the water's going to be pointed at their houses. Hale can't redirect a flood everyone's watching for. That's the whole point of doing it this way — not justice. Sunlight."

"And us?"

Juniper looked at him. Rain was starting to spit across the deck, fat warm drops, the leading edge of the wall. "Us is a separate problem."

* * *

The problem arrived twenty minutes later, and it arrived talking.

The radio crackled alive mid-squelch — the marine band, channel 16, the frequency every boat in the delta monitored and nobody honest used in a storm like this. Juniper's hand froze over the volume dial. They knew the voice before the words resolved. Everyone who'd ever owed money knew that voice.

"*Juniper.*" Marisol. Calm, almost gentle, the way she'd been gentle in the port office with her folded hands and her seventy-two hours. Static chewed at her edges; she was transmitting from somewhere close, close enough that her signal didn't have to fight. "*I know you can hear me. I watched your antenna go up on the radar before the front ate it. Clever girl. Clever boy. Sending the maps out in the middle of an eye — very dramatic.*"

Elias moved to the bow, scanning the white-gray murk for hulls. Juniper keyed the mic and didn't press it. Just listened.

"*Here's what happens now,*" Marisol said. "*In about six hours, the inquiry board convenes emergency session because half the government just got flooded with evidence of what Hale did. By morning, Hale is suspended, and everything he touched gets audited — including Meridian Holdings, including the paper trail that leads to certain interested parties. Those parties would very much prefer that trail stayed cold. So here's my offer, one more time, final version: you come in, you tell the board you acted alone, you give back whatever copies you kept, and the debt dies with the storm. Clean slate. Or—*" A pause, filled with static and wind. "*—or you stay out there, broke, and we wait. Debts don't drown, Juniper. People do.*"

Juniper finally keyed the mic.

"Marisol." Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Did you hear the audio? The part where Hale orders two people sealed in a cave?"

Silence. Then, softly: "*Business is business.*"

"That's what I thought." Juniper looked at Elias, who was watching them with an expression that had gone very still. "Here's my counteroffer. Everything you just heard me send — the charts, the recordings, the whole architecture — there are eighty-one copies of it now, and I sent them encrypted with a release key that publishes automatically if anything happens to either of us. Boat sinks, we drown, someone clips us in a port bar next month, doesn't matter. Dead man's switch. Ask your technical people how those work."

Another silence, longer. When Marisol spoke again, the gentleness was gone, and underneath it, faintly, almost admiring: "*You understand what you've done. You'll never work this coast again. No charter will carry you, no insurer will touch you, no fixer will return your calls. You've traded everything for a map.*"

"Yeah," Juniper said. "That was the price."

"*And the debt?*"

"The debt's yours now. All of it. I'm done carrying it." Juniper set down the mic, then picked it up one more time. "Marisol — the storm's coming in. Get your boats out of the delta or lose them. I'd hate for anyone else to drown for your business."

They switched the radio off.

The quiet afterward lasted three seconds before the world ended it.

* * *

The wall hit at 16:40 local, and it hit like a door slamming.

Wind first — a flat physical shove that laid the boat over thirty degrees and drove rain horizontally across the shoal in sheets so dense the water surface turned to smoke. Then the sea rose. Not waves — a general lifting, the whole delta swelling as the storm surge pushed upriver against the outgoing tide, and the shoal that had held them through the eye began to disappear inch by inch beneath brown churning water.

Juniper had ridden out eleven named storms in boats better than this one. This was not a named storm. This was the monsoon's closing argument.

"Elias!" They had to shout over the roar. "Bilge! Now!"

He was already there, bailing with the plastic case lid because the pump had died an hour ago, throwing water over the leeward rail in practiced arcs. Juniper fought the boat itself — engine off, anchor line paid out to its last ten meters, everything lashed that could be lashed, the two of them wedged in the cockpit with the swamped deck washing ankle-deep around their boots. Lightning strobed. Thunder arrived not as sound but as pressure, a fist against the chest.

At some point — Juniper would never be able to say when — the anchor dragged.

It happened slowly enough to feel like a dream: the grinding of the keel on sand fading, the tilt changing character, the water around them deepening from churned tan to open gray-green. The shoal let them go. The storm took them.

For four hours they went wherever the delta wanted. Juniper steered by instinct alone, no instruments worth trusting, keeping the bow quarter-on to the seas because beam-on meant capsizing and head-on meant swamping and quarter-on was the only prayer the boat would answer. Elias bailed. Bailed, and when his arms gave out, Juniper took the bail bucket and Elias took the helm with shaking hands, and they traded back and forth through the dark like men passing a coal that must not drop. Once a wave broke clean over the stern and tore the fuel can off its lashings and Juniper heard it go and thought, distantly, *there goes the rest of my life,* and then bailed harder.

Once, Elias grabbed their arm and pointed aft, shouting something, and Juniper followed his finger and saw — for one lightning flash — a shape in the chaos. A patrol boat, or what was left of one, listing hard, running lights dead, being driven ashore somewhere east of them. Hale's hunters, caught by the same storm they'd used as a weapon. Juniper felt nothing about it that they were proud of.

Toward dawn the wind began to lie down. Not stop — storms like this didn't stop, they degraded, the wall breaking into ragged bands, the bands into squalls, the squalls into ordinary rain. But the sea flattened from mountains into hills, and hills into long gray swells, and Juniper realized the boat was drifting in shallow water again, and that the light coming up through the rain was the color of pearl, and that they were alive.

They grounded on a mudbank two miles south of where the shoal had been, in the drowned ruins of a palm grove, the trunks sticking out of the floodwater like the masts of a sunken fleet. The boat settled with a sigh. The bilge held four inches of water and no more. The engine was drowned, the radio was dead, the fuel was gone, and the mast hung at its broken angle like a snapped wing.

Elias slid down the cockpit wall until he was sitting in the water, and started laughing — a cracked, disbelieving sound, half sob. Juniper sat down next to him. The rain fell gently now, almost apologetic, pocking the flood around them.

"Eighty-one copies," Elias said finally. "Out there somewhere. Being read."

"Being argued over," Juniper said. "Being lawyered. Being buried and dug up and buried again." They tipped their head back against the gunwale. "But being read."

"And us?"

Juniper took stock, the way they took stock after every storm, the old ritual: boat wrecked. Money gone — worse than gone, owed. Career burned. Reputation, such as it was, now attached to the biggest document leak in the delta's history. Ahead of them: two miles of floodwater, a drowned engine, and a coastline full of people who wanted things from them.

And the compass, somehow, in their jacket pocket, dry, because Juniper had checked it three times in the night without quite knowing why.

"No one to owe," they said.

Elias looked at them. "That's not the same as free."

Juniper watched the rain move across the drowned palms, silver curtains walking north, and thought about the difference, and found they couldn't name it yet — only that it mattered, and that they'd crossed it, and that on the far side of it the water was rising fast and the nearest help was a day's walk through a flooded country that now, thanks to them, knew exactly where its rivers wanted to go.

"No," Juniper agreed. "It's not." They stood, joints screaming, and looked south along the mudbank toward a smudge of rooftops above the floodline. "But it's a start. Come on. We've got a long walk, and I want to hear what the radio stations are saying before Hale's people get ahead of the story."

Elias hauled himself up beside them. Far off, through the thinning rain, a church bell began to ring — some village on high ground, ringing the storm's end the way they'd rung it for two hundred years, one bell, then another, then another, taking the news down the coast.

Juniper pulled out the compass and checked it out of habit.

It pointed north. Steady, certain, indifferent — north, and nothing else.

They put it away and started walking.
