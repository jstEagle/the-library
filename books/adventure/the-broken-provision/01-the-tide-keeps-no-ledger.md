## 1. The Tide Keeps No Ledger

The tide at Saltmere went out farther than it had any right to.

Sebastian Dravenmoor noticed it the way they noticed most things now—sideways, warily, the way a man notices a door left open in a house he's been hiding in. They had come down to the shingle at dawn with a tin cup of tea going cold in their hand, not for any purpose but the old habit of watching water, which was the closest thing to prayer an engineer allowed herself. And the sea was simply *leaving*. Peeling back from the harbor stones in long silver sheets, uncovering weed-slicked boulders and the ribs of boats that had drowned before anyone now living was born.

The gulls were screaming about it. Gulls always knew first.

"Far out," said a voice behind them—old Tam Beckett, mending nets on his doorstep as he did every morning of his life, his fingers moving through the ritual without his eyes ever leaving the water. "Farther than I've seen in forty years."

"Spring tide?" Sebastian asked.

"No spring tide." Tam's needle paused. "And no reason." He said it flatly, the way men in Saltmere said things they'd decided not to think about, and then he looked at Sebastian with an expression that was almost apologetic. "You'll want to stay off the flats today, engineer. Whatever the tide shows you, it isn't showing you for free."

Sebastian smiled the smile they'd learned to wear in boarding houses and queue lines and the offices of men who wanted money—pleasant, empty, a door closed gently from the inside. "I only came for the air."

Tam nodded slowly, unconvinced, and went back to his nets. But his eyes followed Sebastian down the shingle, and so did the eyes of two other fishermen who had appeared in their doorways with the silent, synchronized instinct of people who lived by reading weather and strangers alike.

*They know,* Sebastian thought. Not what, precisely—but that something was wrong with the morning, and that the stranger lodging above the cooper's shop had arrived three weeks ago with one valise, no history, and a habit of flinching whenever anyone knocked.

Three weeks. It felt like three years. It felt like no time at all since the night Sebastian had walked out of Meridian Station with nothing but a grip bag, a forged reference letter, and the particular shame of a woman whose name still opened doors she could no longer afford to walk through. Dravenmoor. The name was worth something in the valley still—not to banks, not anymore, but to men like Marlowe Vane, who collected debts the way other men collected debts' *interests*, patiently, across years, across counties.

Sebastian drank the cold tea and watched the sea go on abandoning the land.

By midmorning the flats lay bare for nearly half a mile, a landscape nobody in Saltmere would walk. Sebastian told themselves they were only strolling the harbor wall. Told themselves the pull was professional curiosity—the exposed seabed was a geological section, after all, strata and scour channels, the kind of thing they'd once have sketched for the sheer pleasure of it. Told themselves the direction their feet chose, east along the wall toward the black headland, meant nothing.

The headland was called nothing. That was the point of it. On every chart Sebastian had ever seen—and they had seen charts of this coast professionally, once, in another life—it was marked with a blank stretch and a depth sounding and no name at all, as though the cartographers had agreed among themselves to look elsewhere. The locals called it *the Old Man's Tooth*, but never in front of children, and never after dark, and when Sebastian had asked the cooper's wife about it her ladle had stopped moving for a full three seconds before she said, "We don't go round there. The tide keeps no ledger there, and neither do we."

At its base, where the retreating water had pulled back like a held breath released, there was a cave.

Not a crack, not a hollow—a proper mouth, tall as a station arch, walled in wet black stone that gleamed like coal. The seabed sloped down into it, ribbed with sandbars, and from somewhere inside came a sound Sebastian felt in their sternum before they heard it with their ears: a low, rhythmic exhalation, the cave breathing with the swell far out beyond the bar. Air moving through stone the way air moves through a tunnel. Through a bore. Through anything long enough and deep enough to have a pulse.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the last dry shingle for a long time.

Every sensible instinct they owned—and those instincts had been sharpened to a fine edge these past three years, honed on bailiffs and landladies and the specific dread of hearing your own name spoken in a public room—every one of them said *walk away*. The village says don't. The tide is already turning; you can feel it in the changed pitch of the gulls. You are a fugitive with forty-one pounds to your name and a debt collector who has been asking after a "railway man" in every port town from here to Greywater Landing.

But underneath all of that, older than the fear, was the thing that had made Sebastian Dravenmoor an engineer in the first place: a tunnel was breathing, and no one had ever told them why, and some part of them had been waiting twenty years for someone to ask.

They went.

* * *

The floor of the cave was firm gravel, rising gently, and within thirty paces the daylight behind them had shrunk to a grey coin and the dark ahead had taken on texture—damp stone, the mineral smell of deep water, and something else, faint and wrong: lamp oil. Old lamp oil, and under it, the sweetish staleness Sebastian knew from disaster inquiries and wreck sites, the smell that meant a body had been in an enclosed space for a while.

Their hand found the wall. Their heart kept its own ledger: *turn back, turn back, turn back.*

Then the light changed ahead—a thin, sourceless glow—and Sebastian rounded a bend of rock and stopped.

A lantern sat on a flat stone, burned down to a stub, its glass smoked amber. Beside it, propped against the wall with terrible care, as though someone had arranged him to be found, sat a dead man.

He had been dead for days—maybe a week. The cave's chill had kept him from the worst of it, but the sea does not negotiate, and time had done its patient work. He was dressed for travel: good boots, a wool coat gone stiff with salt, a canvas satchel still buckled across his chest. His back was against the stone and his head was bowed forward, and his right hand was closed around something at his breast with a grip that death had only tightened.

Sebastian should have turned around then. Every debt they owed, every fear they carried, said *this is not yours, this is not your business, dead men are the business of magistrates and you cannot afford magistrates.*

Instead they knelt, because the man's other hand was resting on the gravel beside him, and beneath it, half-covered, was paper.

It took both hands and a gentleness Sebastian didn't know they still possessed to work the sheet free. A map. Hand-drawn, ink faded to brown, on paper soft as cloth from handling—handled hundreds of times, folded and refolded along the same creases until the folds themselves had begun to tear. Sebastian tilted it toward the dying lantern's glow.

And the world went quiet in the way it goes quiet just before a bridge lets go.

Rail lines. Eleven of them, drawn in a sure draftsman's hand across the valley's whole length, following the river up from the coast into the mountains. Sebastian knew every one of them. Had walked every one of them. Had *drawn* four of them, at a drafting table in the Dravenmoor & Vale offices, at twenty-three, believing they were building a future.

Every line was struck through. Not crossed out carelessly—*sealed*, each one annotated in the same small, precise script: dates, gate numbers, order references. Closure Order 44-C. Closure Order 51-A. Sebastian's own signature was on two of the closure certificates; they remembered signing them, remembered being told the lines were failures, uneconomic, unstable ground. Remembered the strange hollowness of it, because the ground had never been unstable. Because the tunnels had been sound. Because they'd said so, and been overruled by an uncle whose smile never reached his eyes.

Eleven closed lines. All running the same direction. All running *upriver*.

Toward the source of the Greywater. Toward the mountain where the Dravenmoor fortune had its roots, in the springs and works the family had owned since before anyone could remember why the family owned them.

"Oh," Sebastian said aloud, to no one, to the dead man. "Oh, you poor stubborn fool. What did you find?"

The dead man did not answer, but his closed fist seemed to. Sebastian worked the fingers loose—apologizing under their breath, the way you apologize to machinery you're about to strip—and opened them.

A compass. Brass, heavy, old-fashioned, its lid engraved with a monogram worn nearly smooth. Sebastian turned it to the light and their breath stopped in their throat, because they knew that engraving. Had traced it as a child, sitting on the floor of a small rented house in Lowmarsh while their mother told stories about the family she never spoke of otherwise. A river coiled around a mountain peak, and beneath it two letters:

*D.D.* — *Dravenmoor.*

Their mother's compass. Her father's before hers. The one that had vanished—along with her letters, her papers, her whole disowned history—the winter she died, when the family's lawyers had come with their lists and their seals and taken everything that wasn't nailed down, and several things that were.

"This was hers," Sebastian whispered. The cave breathed around them, slow and enormous. "How did *you* have this?"

The map answered before the silence could. Because now, holding it closer to the lantern, Sebastian saw what they'd missed: in the corner, in different ink, newer ink, a single line of handwriting. Not annotations. An address. A date three weeks past. And five words that landed in Sebastian's chest like a coupling slamming home:

*Find S. Dravenmoor. Tell them everything.*

The lantern guttered. Somewhere behind them, far back toward the mouth of the cave, the light of day had changed—thinned, shifted, gone the color of evening.

The tide was coming back.

Sebastian rose too fast, knees cracking on stone, map and compass crushed against their chest, and ran for the grey coin of daylight that was shrinking even as they watched it. Behind them the cave exhaled, long and low, like something settling back to sleep—or like something that had finished saying what it needed to say, and would keep the rest until it was asked properly.

They came out onto the flats with the water already sliding in around their boots, cold as a creditor's hand, and ran the whole half mile to the harbor wall without looking back.

That night, above the cooper's shop, Sebastian locked the door, lit no lamp, and spread the map on the floor by moonlight. Eleven sealed lines, all pointing upriver. A compass that had belonged to a mother who had died owing the truth. Five words from a dead man who had known Sebastian's name.

*Tell them everything.*

Well, then, Sebastian thought, pressing their thumb flat against the worn brass, feeling the small cold weight of it like a promise or a sentence. Start telling.

They were still awake at dawn when the knock came—three measured raps, unhurried, the knock of a man who has never once in his life been kept waiting.

Through the door, a pleasant voice said: "Miss Dravenmoor. My name is Vane. Might we discuss the weather? It's been behaving *so* strangely lately."
