## 2. A Debt Written in Water

The tide brought Marlowe Vane the way it brought everything else to Saltmere—without asking, without apology, and without any intention of leaving things as it found them.

Sebastian saw him first from the window of Gull's Rest, the boarding house where they'd taken a room under a name that wasn't theirs. Vane came up the shingle road on a gray horse, dressed in gray, his coat cut with the kind of precision that made the fishermen's oilskins look like rags by comparison. He did not ride so much as arrive, unhurried, as though the debt he carried had been accruing interest for years and could afford one more morning.

Sebastian's hand went to their breast pocket before their mind caught up. The map was still there. The compass too, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboard under the bed, because Sebastian had learned long ago that pockets were for things you were willing to lose.

*He can't know,* Sebastian told themselves. *No one knows.*

But Vane dismounted at the door of Gull's Rest and looked up—not at the windows, not at the sign, but directly at the spot where Sebastian stood—and tipped his hat. A small, courteous gesture. The courtesy of a man who had already won.

Downstairs, the landlady was pretending very hard to scrub a pot that did not need scrubbing. "Gray gentleman," she muttered. "Came off the packet boat yesterday. Been asking after an engineer."

"Did you tell him?"

"I told him I don't hold with engineers." She wrung the cloth. "He said that was all right. He said he only wanted to talk about water."

The word landed in Sebastian's stomach like a dropped anchor. Water. Not rail. Not debt. *Water.*

The door opened without a knock.

Marlowe Vane was younger than his reputation and older than his face, if such a thing could be true. He had the smooth, unlined look of a man who never did anything himself, and the eyes of a man who had watched many things be done. He set his hat on the table with care, as though the table were an altar and he a respectful guest.

"Sebastian Dravenmoor," he said. It wasn't a question. He pronounced the name the way the family used to—*Draven-moor*, two full beats, no apology for its length. "You've been hard to find. Though in fairness, you've had help. This village is very good at not knowing things."

"This village is very good at minding its own business," Sebastian said. "Which is more than I can say for you."

"Oh, I don't mind anyone's business." Vane drew out a chair and sat, uninvited, folding his hands. "I mind ledgers. Yours has reached a figure I won't insult us both by saying aloud. Nine days. That is what my employers have authorized me to offer—the standard term. Pay in nine days, or the creditors take the workshop tools, the patents, and whatever else the courts decide a disgraced engineer owns. Which, I'm told, is very little, and mostly books."

Nine days. Sebastian had known the number was coming; they had simply hoped for a larger one. "And if I don't have it?"

"Then you'll have less than nothing, which is a condition most people find clarifying." Vane smiled faintly, as though at a private joke. "But I haven't come all this way to ruin you, Mr. Dravenmoor. Ruining people is tedious work. Anyone can do it. I came because I'd rather *buy* something from you."

"I don't own anything worth buying."

"You own a dead man's map."

The fire popped in the grate. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

Sebastian kept their face still, which took effort, because behind the stillness everything was moving—recalculating, retracing, trying to find the seam where this man had learned what he knew. "I don't know what you mean."

"No?" Vane leaned back. "Two nights ago, the tide went out farther than it has in eleven years. An old fisherman named Corben watched you go into the cave the village pretends isn't there. Corben also watches the packet boats. Corben watches everything, and reports to me, because Corben owes money too. Everyone owes money, Mr. Dravenmoor. It's the great democracy of our age." He let that sit a moment. "A man washed into that cave with the last spring tide. Dead some weeks, by the state of him. Clutching a brass compass and carrying papers. You took them. Don't trouble yourself denying it—I've read the coroner's report from upriver, and I know exactly whose corpse that was."

"Whose, then?"

Vane studied them for a long moment, and something in his smoothness shifted—not softening, but focusing, like a lens finding its subject. "His name was Aldous Wren. He was a surveyor. And forty years ago, he worked for your mother."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts when the sea changes its mind. Sebastian gripped the back of a chair. "My mother died when I was nine."

"Yes. In disgrace, according to your father's family. In *truth*—" Vane spread his hands, "—in exile. There is a difference, and the difference is a river."

"What are you talking about?"

"I'm talking about the closed lines." Vane's voice dropped, and now there was nothing casual in it at all. "The Merrowcut line. The Hollowford spur. The deep tunnel above Grayling Lock that officially collapsed in the flood year. You helped design the survey that closed them—you were young, ambitious, and you believed the engineering reports, because why wouldn't you? They were excellent reports. Your own family wrote them." He paused. "They were lies, Mr. Dravenmoor. Every one. Those lines weren't closed because they failed. They were closed because they *succeeded*—because they all ran toward the same place, upriver, toward the source of the valley's water. And the Dravenmoors have spent three generations making sure no one follows them."

Sebastian heard their own heartbeat. The map lay against their ribs like a second skeleton. Closed lines drawn in a dead surveyor's careful hand—lines they themselves had walked, measured, signed off on. Lines that had seemed to die of natural causes.

"The map shows those lines," Sebastian said quietly. "Marked in sequence. Like stations on a route."

"Because it *is* a route." Vane stood, retrieving his hat, turning it once in his hands. "Wren spent his whole life trying to reach the end of it. My employers want what he sought—the source itself—retrieved, secured, documented. Bring back proof of what's up there, and your debt vanishes. Every figure. Forgiven, in full, today's date, my signature. You walk away owing nothing to anyone." He settled the hat on his head. "Or refuse, keep your nine days, and lose everything to men who will then go up that river anyway, with worse manners and better funding."

"And if I say yes," Sebastian asked, "what happens to the source? To the river?"

Vane paused at the door, and for just a moment the collector's mask slipped enough to show the man beneath it—a tired man, perhaps, or merely an honest one, which in his trade amounted to the same deformity.

"That," he said, "is not my department."

He left the way he'd come, unhurried, and the tide-sound filled the room behind him like a held breath released.

* * *

Sebastian didn't sleep. They sat by the fire until it burned down to orange bones, turning the compass over and over, watching the needle swing and settle, swing and settle, always north-northwest, always upriver. Wren had carried this thing through sealed tunnels and drowned stations and however many years of exile, and it had never stopped pointing. There was a lesson in that, probably. Sebastian wasn't sure they liked it.

*Forgiven in full.* Two words, and every wall around their life would come down. No more borrowed names. No more flinching at gray coats. They could rebuild the workshop. They could put their real name on real work again—except the name was the problem, wasn't it? The name was the chain. Vane hadn't offered freedom. He'd offered a longer leash, held by the same family that had written their mother out of the world and called it grief.

And the alternative was nine days and ruin, while men with worse manners went up the river regardless.

Sebastian slept two hours against the wall, sitting up, the compass in their fist like a stone.

Dawn came gray and thin. Sebastian went down to check the floorboard out of habit rather than fear—fear would come later, they'd learn, when habit had already failed them.

The board was loose. Not pried—lifted, properly, by someone who knew about floorboards. The oilcloth was there. The spare coin purse was there, lighter by half. And the space where the map had been folded was empty.

For a moment the world went very simple and very cold. Sebastian knelt there on the bare boards of a rented room in a town that didn't know their name, and felt the specific vertigo of a person whose last possession has just grown legs.

Then they saw the paper on the pillow. Small, torn from a ledger, folded twice. In a cramped, childish, fiercely careful hand:

*Didn't steal it. Borrowed. Needed it more than you. — P.*

Sebastian read it four times. Then they laughed—one short, cracked sound with no humor in it at all, aimed at the ceiling, at the sea, at the entire absurd arrangement of the universe.

Somewhere out there, a stowaway orphan with quick fingers and a slow conscience was heading upriver with a dead surveyor's map and a brass compass, toward a source that three generations of Dravenmoors had killed to keep secret, pursued—if Vane was honest, and he might even have been—by men with worse manners and better funding.

Nine days. One map. One thief. One river.

Sebastian stood, pocketed the note, and went downstairs to find out who in Saltmere had seen a child with salt-stained cuffs and clever hands—and to ask Odile, the lockkeeper's daughter who knew the river better than her own prayers, exactly how fast a small boat could be made ready.

The tide was coming in. It occurred to Sebastian, climbing the shingle road against the wind, that the tide kept no ledger at all—that it simply took what it wanted and gave back what it chose, and that maybe the only way to survive a family like theirs was to learn to move the same way.

Upstream, the river waited. So, presumably, did everyone else who wanted it.

Sebastian meant to get there first.
