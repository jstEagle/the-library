## 3. The Crew Nobody Wanted

*Sebastian*

The lockkeeper's daughter smelled of tar and river mud, and she looked at Sebastian the way one looks at a ledger with a suspicious entry.

"You're the one who found the dead man," she said. Not a question. Word traveled fast in Saltmere, faster than the tide itself.

"I'm the one who found a dead man," Sebastian corrected. "Whether he was mine to find is another matter."

Odile Vasseur leaned against the pilings of her father's lock, arms crossed, watching the water churn through the gates below. She was perhaps five-and-twenty, with rope-scarred hands and eyes the flat grey of the river in November. "My father says the cave opened for you. Says the tide pulled back farther than it has in forty years, just long enough."

"Your father believes in coincidences of that scale?"

"My father believes in nothing but the schedule of the tides." She turned to face them fully. "But I believe the river's been running thin. Three weeks now, low water where there should be high. The mills upstream are choking. And you're carrying a map that shows every closed line feeding into this valley like veins into a heart." She held out her hand. "Show me."

Sebastian hesitated. The map had not left their breast pocket since the cave—had barely left their thoughts. But there was something in Odile's voice, a weariness worn smooth as river stone, that said she had been asking these questions long before a dead man washed ashore.

They unrolled it on the planking between them, weighting the corners with two bolts and a piece of sea glass.

Odile studied it in silence for a long moment. Her finger traced the blue-inked lines—the ones Sebastian had drawn themselves, years ago, young and ambitious and certain every line they laid was progress. Then her finger moved to the red lines. The sealed ones. Seven of them, branching upriver like the roots of an inverted tree.

"These aren't failures," she said quietly. "I've run barges past the mouths of these tunnels my whole life. They weren't collapsed. They were *closed*. Bricked, gated, posted with signs that say 'unstable.' But the stone around them doesn't crumble like unstable stone. It's cut clean."

Sebastian's throat tightened. They had told themselves the same thing in the dark of the cave, and had refused to finish the thought. "Someone wanted those lines shut."

"Someone wanted what's at the end of them shut *in*." Odile rolled the map and pressed it back against Sebastian's chest, holding it there a moment longer than necessary. "You'll need someone who knows the river above the last lock. Every shoal, every drowned landing, every stretch where the current turns mean after dark. That's me. I'm coming."

"I can't pay you."

"Good. Then you can't order me about." She almost smiled. It transformed her face entirely, and then it was gone. "We launch tomorrow, on the morning tide. Bring your dead man's compass. Rivers respect the dead more than the living."

* * *

*Bramble* came to them that evening, or rather, Sebastian found him already sitting in the boarding house kitchen when they returned, enormous and apologetic, his knees jammed under a table built for smaller men. He had the look of a man who had once filled doorways with authority and now asked permission to stand in them.

"Bramble Hodge," he said, rising halfway and then thinking better of it. "Tunnel foreman. Formerly. You'd know the name from the Meridian Line reports."

Sebastian did know it. Everyone in the engineering trade had known it, before. Bramble Hodge had signed off on the Meridian tunnel the same year Sebastian's own designs were being praised in the same journals—and Bramble's tunnel had failed, or been declared to fail, burying three men and ending two careers. One of them his. The other—

"You designed the approaches," Bramble said, reading Sebastian's face. "I never blamed you. I want that said first. What happened down there wasn't your doing."

"And yours?"

Bramble's hands, vast as dinner plates, folded together on the table. "That's the thing I've had nine years to wonder about. Because I inspected that rock face myself, Mr. Dravenmoor. Twice. It was sound. Sound as church stone. And then it wasn't, and everyone said Hodge got greedy, cut corners, falsified his surveys." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "I never falsified anything in my life. So when I heard a Dravenmoor was asking questions about the closed lines—" He stopped. Started again. "I want to be there when we find out who made my name a lie. I'll dig, haul, hold a gate, whatever the river asks. I don't need paying. I need an answer."

Sebastian thought of their mother, dead eleven years, disowned for marrying beneath the family's idea of itself. Thought of the map in their pocket drawn in her ally's hand. Thought: *everyone in this story has been broken by the same machine.*

"We launch at dawn," they said. "Can you handle a boat?"

"No," Bramble admitted. "But I can bail one out splendidly."

* * *

*Odile*

The stowaway made herself known an hour out of Saltmere, when Odile heard a sneeze from inside the cargo of dried cod.

She hauled the hatch open and found a child—a girl, ten at most, all elbows and salt-matted hair, wedged between crates with the compass clutched to her chest like a stolen heart. Which, Odile noted, it was.

"That's not yours," Odile said.

"It belonged to a dead man," the girl said, chin up. "Dead men don't need it."

"Dead men have heirs. Heirs have claims."

"Not this one." The girl scrambled out of the hold with the agility of a cat crossing ice. "I asked in the village. Nobody claimed him. Nobody even knew his name till the surveyor's clerk dug it out of an old letter. And besides—" here her voice dropped, and for the first time she looked less like a thief and more like what she was, "—the wells downstream are going dry. My village sent me. Well, they didn't send me, exactly, I volunteered before they could vote on it. But the river's dying and everyone upstream keeps saying it isn't, and I'm going to find out why if I have to stow away on every boat in the valley."

Odile should have put her off at the next landing. Any sensible person would have. Instead she looked at the small fierce face, at the compass held in white-knuckled fingers, and felt something twist in her chest that she chose not to examine.

"What's your name?"

"Pip."

"Pip. Rules. You stay in sight. You touch nothing that isn't given to you. And you give back what you took."

Pip's jaw set. Then, grudgingly, she held out the compass.

Odile didn't take it. "Keep it till Mr. Dravenmoor asks for it back. Let them see what kind of crew they've hired."

* * *

*Sebastian*

Sebastian discovered the theft at midday, discovered the thief at midday-and-a-quarter, and discovered by evening that they had somehow acquired a crew.

It should have been a disaster. It was, in most measurable ways, a disaster. Bramble bailed constantly and steered badly. Pip asked questions in an endless stream—*why do the red lines stop at the mountain, what's a catchment, have you ever been in a cave that flooded, how do you know you're not going to die like the dead man*—none of which Sebastian answered well. Odile gave orders in a voice that brooked no argument and accepted none, including Sebastian's, twice.

And yet.

By the second evening, moored below the last working lock with its lanterns burning yellow against the dusk, Sebastian noticed things. Noticed that Bramble had quietly re-caulked the leaking seam while the others slept. That Odile had taken the night watch without being asked, and that Pip had curled up beside her on the deck, and that Odile—who trusted no one, who had said so plainly within the first hour—had let her.

They sat together at the rail, Sebastian and Odile, passing a flask of something rough between them while the lockkeeper's lamps glimmered astern.

"They'll come after us," Odile said. "Whoever sealed those lines. A map like that is worth killing for. Possibly worth the killing already done."

"I know."

"And you're still going."

Sebastian watched the black water slide past, carrying them toward the headwaters and whatever waited there. "I spent six years hiding from my debts," they said. "Running from town to town, changing my name on the registers, sleeping with my boots on. And the whole time I told myself I was alone because alone was safe." They turned the flask in their fingers. "Then a dead man handed me a map, and three strangers climbed aboard my disaster, and I find I can't remember why alone ever seemed like the better bargain."

Odile said nothing for a while. Then: "The lock ahead opens at first light. After that, no more scheduled water. No more other boats. Just us and whatever the river's hiding." She stood, stretched, and paused at the cabin door. "Get some sleep, Dravenmoor. Tomorrow we go where the maps stop."

Sebastian stayed at the rail a while longer. Below deck, he could hear Bramble's deep, rumbling snore, and Pip talking in her sleep, and the small sounds of people becoming, without anyone's permission, a crew.

In the last grey light, far upstream where the river narrowed toward the mountains, something flashed—not water catching the sun, but metal. Brief and deliberate. Like a signal.

Like someone letting them know they were seen.
