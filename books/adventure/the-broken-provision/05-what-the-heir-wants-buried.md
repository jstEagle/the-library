## 5. What the Heir Wants Buried

The river told Odile everything, if she knew how to listen. It had been telling her all morning that something was wrong.

It started with the birds. Gulls don't fly this far upriver, but there they were at dawn, wheeling over the water in ragged spirals, screaming at nothing. Then the current changed its mind twice in an hour—first sluggish as a swamp, then quick and nervous, like a man walking fast past a door he doesn't want to look at. By the time the drowned station appeared around the bend, Odile had already decided she didn't like what the river was saying.

* * *

They smelled it before they saw it: coal smoke. Real smoke, from a real fire, where no fire should be.

The station rose out of the floodwater on stone stilts, its lower half swallowed to the second-story windows. Someone had built a walkway from the surviving platform out over the green water, and lanterns hung along it, burning yellow against the grey morning. A boat was moored beneath. A good boat. New rope. Brass fittings gone dull with spray but not with age.

"Nobody lives here," Bramble said flatly. "Nobody's lived here in twenty years."

"Somebody's living here now," Pip said, and Odile heard the hunger in the girl's voice—the hunger for warmth, for food that wasn't dried eel, for anything that looked like civilization. Odile felt it too. Six days upstream on cold rations and colder water will do that to a person.

Sebastian stood at the bow, compass in hand, and Odile watched their face do something complicated. The map had shown this station. The map had marked it with a small, careful symbol Sebastian hadn't explained. And now there were lights burning in a place that should have been dead.

"We can go around," Odile said quietly. "There's a portage on the north bank. Adds half a day."

Sebastian didn't answer right away. The lantern light caught their face, and Odile saw the thing she'd been watching grow since the gorge: loneliness, wearing the mask of caution, and losing the argument.

"We're low on supplies," Sebastian said. "And if whoever's here knows the river, they might know more than we do."

"The river knows them," Odile said. "That's what worries me."

But Sebastian was already steering for the walkway.

* * *

The man who came down the steps was silver-haired and straight-backed, dressed in a long coat too fine for the wilderness and boots too muddy for the city. He moved like someone who had never once been told to hurry.

He stopped three steps above the landing, and Odile watched him look at Sebastian the way a man looks at a portrait he's only ever seen in miniature.

"Sebastian." His voice was warm gravel. "You have your mother's way of arriving unannounced."

Odile saw Sebastian go still. Not frightened-still. Something worse. Stillness like a struck bell, vibrating somewhere no one could see.

"You know my name."

"I gave it to you." The man smiled, and it was a good smile, worn smooth by use. "Cassian Dravenmoor. Your mother's brother. Which makes me your uncle, though I suspect you've had little use for the word."

Behind her, Odile heard Bramble shift his weight, slow and heavy, the way he did when he was calculating how many men it would take to hold a door. Pip had gone quiet in the way that meant she was memorizing exits.

"You knew my mother," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. It was a wound being pressed gently, experimentally, to see if it still bled.

"I did. Better than anyone left living." Cassian descended the last step and extended his hand. "Come inside. There's soup. Actual soup. I know what the river does to people, and I'd rather talk to family across a table than across a gunwale."

* * *

Inside, the station's great hall had been restored just enough to be comfortable and not enough to be honest. Maps covered one wall—railway surveys, some of them decades old. A fire burned in a hearth the size of a coffin. And the soup was real: venison and root vegetables, hot enough to hurt going down, and Odile hated herself a little for how good it tasted.

Cassian talked while they ate. He talked about Sebastian's mother—her laugh, her stubbornness, the summer she tried to reroute a creek behind the estate because it offended her sense of symmetry. He talked about the family that had cast her out, and his voice carried something that might have been regret or might have been very old, very well-preserved performance.

"They never forgave her for choosing the railways over the name," he said. "And they never forgave me for staying. So understand, Sebastian, when I say I know what it is to be disowned in every way that matters."

Sebastian sat forward. Odile watched it happen—the lean of a starving person toward a full plate—and her stomach tightened around the soup.

"The source," Sebastian said. "Do you know what's upriver?"

"I know what my family has done with it." Cassian set down his spoon. For a moment the warmth dropped away and something older showed underneath, something tired. "They've been throttling it. Choking the spring so the valley stays thirsty and dependent, so every closed line stays closed, so the fortune keeps compounding on scarcity. I've spent twenty years inside that machine pretending to oil it. I'm done pretending."

"And you came here to help us?" Odile asked.

She kept her voice level. She kept her eyes on his hands, which never fidgeted, which rested on the table like they owned it.

Cassian turned the smile on her, and it fit just as well. "And you are?"

"Odile. Lockkeeper's daughter. I read water the way you read ledgers."

"A useful skill." He inclined his head, gracious as a host. "Yes, Miss Lockkeeper's Daughter. I came to help. My men are downstream holding off Vane's hired knives as best they can without revealing whose side they're on. Every day you gain is a day I bought you. Ask yourself why I'd bother, if not for blood."

*Blood.* Odile watched the word land on Sebastian like a coat laid over shaking shoulders. She watched Sebastian's eyes go bright and wet and furious about being bright and wet.

That night, when the others slept, she found Sebastian on the platform, staring upriver at the dark.

"You believe him," she said.

"I want to." Sebastian's laugh was short and cracked. "God help me, Odile, he knew her favorite season. He knew why she named me. Nobody else alive knows those things. Do you know what it's like to be the last page of a book everyone else threw away?"

"Yes," Odile said. "My father died when I was nine. Everyone who remembered him kindly is gone. I'd have believed any stranger who brought him back to life for an evening." She sat down beside Sebastian on the wet planks. "That's exactly why you can't. That's exactly what he's counting on."

Sebastian was quiet a long time. The river ran below them, black and patient.

"He cried," Sebastian said finally. "When he talked about her. You can't fake that."

"You can't fake it," Odile agreed. "But you can rehearse it."

* * *

Odile didn't sleep. She told herself it was habit—a lockkeeper's daughter learns to wake at changes in the current—but the truth was simpler: she didn't trust the house, so she wouldn't trust the bed.

Near midnight, she heard the floorboards.

Not Cassian's tread—he announced himself with every step, a man confident in his own house. This was lighter. Two sets of feet, moving toward the dry store room where they'd stowed their gear, where the map lay rolled inside Sebastian's spare coat.

Odile rose without a sound and followed.

Through the door's gap she saw firelight and two of Cassian's men—one holding a shuttered lantern, the other holding the map, already unrolled, already reading it with the fluency of a man who'd seen it before. The second man fed a corner of it toward the lantern's slit.

Paper caught. Flame crawled up the margin, eating the careful inked lines, the closed rails, the small patient symbols—

Odile moved.

She came through the door low and fast, took the lantern-man's wrist and drove it into the wall. The lantern burst; burning oil spattered; the man screamed and swung blind. The other one dropped the map and stamped at the flames crawling across it, and Odile understood in one cold flash: *not destroy it—control the burn*. Burn it just enough. Make it look like accident or carelessness. Leave the crew with a ruined map and a choice between turning back or trusting Cassian's knowledge of the route.

Trust. That was the whole architecture of it. Burn the map, offer the guide.

Bramble filled the doorway behind her like a landslide, and it ended quickly after that.

* * *

They woke Cassian at gunpoint—Bramble's gun, steady as bedrock. Cassian came out in his dressing gown with his hands visible and his face arranged into sorrow.

"This wasn't my order," he said.

"Your men," Odile said. "Your house. Your fire."

"My men have families too, and my family has ways of reaching them." He shook his head, magnificent in his grief. "I should have known they'd report to the estate behind my back. Sebastian, I swear to you—"

"Don't." Sebastian's voice was quiet, and Odile heard what it cost. "Don't swear. Just answer one question. The symbol on the map, at this station. The one marking it safe. Who put it there?"

Silence. One breath. Two.

Cassian's face did not change, but everything behind it did—Odile watched it happen, watched the warmth drain out through some hidden seam, leaving the machinery bare.

"You found that," he said, "and you still came to my table."

"I wanted it to be you," Sebastian said. "I wanted it so badly I ignored the river." They glanced at Odile, and something passed between them—not forgiveness yet, but acknowledgment. "We leave at first light. All of us. Together."

Cassian straightened his dressing gown. When he spoke again, the warmth was back, but now Odile could see the hinges in it, the places where it folded shut.

"Then you'll want to know what's waiting at the gorge crossing," he said pleasantly. "Because my family's ways of reaching people reach much further than my men do."

Outside, far downriver, the gulls began to scream again.
