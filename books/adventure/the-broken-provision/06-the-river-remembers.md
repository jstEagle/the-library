## 6. The River Remembers

Pip had learned to count in a house where counting was survival. Count the loaves before the baker woke. Count the coins in the till when the shopkeeper turned his back. Count the days since anyone had said her name out loud instead of *girl* or *thief* or *you there*. So she counted now, crouched at the prow of the boat as it slid through the gorge, because counting kept her hands from shaking.

Three ropes on the crossing. Two crates of food left after the last lock. One compass in her coat pocket that did not belong to her and never had.

The gorge swallowed sound. The walls rose sheer and wet on either side, streaked with mineral stains like old tears, and the river ran beneath them with a low continuous mutter, as though it were reciting something it had memorized centuries ago and could not forget. Mist drifted down from ledges high above, fine as flour. Everything smelled of cold stone and iron and the green rot of things drowned long ago.

"Keep her centered," Odile called from the stern, one hand on the sweep oar, her eyes never leaving the water. "There's a shelf rock below the surface here. It's why nobody crosses by boat. We should have portaged."

"We should have done a great many things," Bramble muttered. He sat amidships with his arms wrapped around his knees, a man built like a door who had spent three days trying to make himself small. His knuckles were still scabbed from the gate they'd forced in the abandoned tunnel, and every time he flexed his fingers he winced and pretended he hadn't.

Sebastian stood at the bow beside Pip, map open against their forearm despite the damp curling its edges. The closed lines ran up this gorge like veins drawn in faded ink, converging somewhere ahead, somewhere upstream, toward whatever the dead man in the cave had died trying to reach. Sebastian hadn't slept much. Pip knew because she hadn't either, and because she'd watched them in the firelight the night before, turning the compass over and over, not reading it—just holding it, the way you'd hold a hand.

"The crossing is ahead," Sebastian said. "Odile, do we run it or rope across?"

"Rope." Odile spat over the side, a habit Pip suspected was pure theater. "The river narrows past the bend and drops. Even if we lived, we'd lose everything we're carrying."

"And what are we carrying, exactly?" Bramble asked. "Half a wheel of cheese and my wounded pride?"

"Two crates," Pip said automatically, because she'd counted, and then wished she hadn't, because Sebastian glanced at her with that look—not suspicion exactly, but attention, which was worse. Attention meant someone was watching. Being watched meant being known. Being known meant—

She shut the thought away and counted the mist-beads forming on her sleeve instead.

* * *

The crossing was a ruin of engineering: two stone pylons rising from opposite banks, the ghost of a bridge between them, cables rotted to fraying threads that trailed in the current like drowned hair. The old rail line had crossed here once. Pip could see the iron stumps of ties still mortared into the pylons, rusted to lace. Above them, carved into the cliff face in letters gone soft with lichen, read a single word: **DRAYFELL**. She didn't know what it meant. Nobody offered to explain.

They roped the boat across hand over hand, crate by crate, person by person. It went wrong the way things go wrong: all at once, and quietly first.

Pip felt it before she heard it—a change in the river's voice, the mutter dropping to a hiss. Then the water downstream of the pylons began to fall away, sliding backward like a rug pulled from under furniture, and the whole channel shuddered.

"Undertow!" Odile's voice cracked like a whip. "Off the rope! Get off the—"

The first cable snapped with a sound like a bone breaking in a large animal. The second held for three heartbeats, long enough for Pip to see Sebastian reach for her, long enough for her to reach back, and then the third cable parted and the world tilted.

She went into the water. It was colder than any well she'd ever fallen down, cold enough to stop her thoughts mid-word. The river took her under and rolled her, and through the roar she heard the boat strike stone, heard wood splinter with a deep unhappy groan, heard Bramble bellow something that might have been a name or might have been a prayer. Her shoulder hit a pylon. Her head hit nothing she could identify. She grabbed iron—a rusted tie-end jutting from the stone—and held.

When the river finally spat her onto the gravel shoal below the crossing, she came up coughing, alive, furious, and clutching one thing to her chest without having decided to.

The compass.

* * *

They lost the boat entire. They lost one crate to the water and most of the other to the rocks; what remained was a sad arithmetic of soaked biscuit, a tin of lamp oil gone cloudy, Odile's charts ruined to pulp, and Bramble's tool roll, which he laid out on a flat stone piece by piece like a surgeon counting instruments after an amputation.

"Rope's gone too," he said. "All of it."

"Then we climb." Odile wrung out her sleeve with the expression of a woman who had been betrayed personally by water. "The goat track on the north wall. It's steep but it holds."

"It held for goats," Bramble said.

"You are approximately goat-shaped."

Pip sat apart, on a boulder above the wreck-line, and let them argue. Her clothes steamed faintly in the thin light that made it through the gorge. Her hands would not stop trembling, so she folded them around the compass and pressed it against her sternum until the shaking slowed.

Sebastian climbed up and sat beside her. Not close. Just near enough.

"You saved it," they said.

"I didn't mean to."

"That's usually how saving works."

Pip almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was smaller and more dangerous. "It was his. The dead man's. In the cave. You've been carrying it like it was yours, and I—" She stopped. Started again, because stopping was how lies grew. "I stole it. Twice. Once the first night, from your pack while you slept. Once at the last lock. I put it back both times. I don't know why I put it back."

Sebastian said nothing. The river said plenty, muttering past the shoal, dragging the splintered remains of the boat in slow circles like a dog worrying a bone.

"I take things," Pip said. "That's what I am. That's what I'm for. Somebody hands you a job and a name to steal from, and you do it, because the alternative is going back to counting other people's bread." Her voice was climbing and she couldn't make it stop. "I was sent. That's the truth. I wasn't running away from anything—I was sent to follow you. To watch you. To find out where the map leads and send word back."

The silence that followed lasted four heartbeats. Pip counted them.

"Sent by whom?"

And there it was—the question she'd been choking on since the gorge, since the lock, maybe since the fishing village where she'd first picked Sebastian Dravenmoor's pocket and found not a purse but a purpose. She looked downstream, back the way they'd come, toward the village where the wells had gone dry one by one, where Old Marta's garden had browned to straw, where children walked two miles each morning for water that tasted of iron and grief.

"Everyone," she said. "Everyone downstream. The wells are failing. All of them. First the shallow ones, then the deep ones. The river still runs"—she jerked her chin at the water—"but it runs wrong. Too fast and too empty, like something's drinking it from the other end. The elders sent word upriver asking questions and got no answers. So they scraped together coin and found the cheapest thief available, and that was me, and the job was: follow the engineer with the map. Find out what's eating our river." She finally looked at Sebastian. "I was supposed to send word back from the last lock. I didn't. I couldn't decide whose side I was on. I still can't."

"You just told me the truth unprompted," Sebastian said. "That's usually how sides get chosen."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the pylon, a fire of driftwood that smoked sullenly because everything was wet, including, apparently, the wood's feelings. Odile rigged a drying line for their clothes. Bramble sharpened a piton with more force than the task required. And Pip sat closest to the flames because she was smallest and coldest and had decided, in the fierce private way of orphans, that she deserved it.

Sebastian sat down across the fire from her.

"My mother used to say the river remembers everything it carries," they said. "Every drop of rain, every fish, every body. She said that's why people threw secrets in it." A pause. "I think she was talking about herself. She came from up here. From the family that owns all of this." They gestured at the dark walls, the hidden lines, the whole throttled valley. "I spent my life thinking inheritance meant money I'd never see. An estate. A name. Things behind glass." Their eyes caught the firelight. "But if the river's dying downstream—if someone up here is drinking it dry—then maybe what I inherited isn't a fortune. Maybe it's a bill."

"Those are the same thing," Pip said. "Where I'm from, bills are just fortunes you owe somebody else."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment, and then laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, but real. "You're staying with us. Not as a spy. As crew. If you want."

Want. The word landed in Pip's chest like a coal. She had been wanted before—wanted for her fingers, her silence, her willingness to be small. She had never been wanted the way the fire wanted wood: openly, needfully, with warmth on the other side of it.

"I want," she said, and her voice only broke a little.

Bramble grunted from the darkness, which from Bramble was a christening. Odile tossed her a dry-ish blanket, which from Odile was an embrace.

* * *

Pip woke once in the black hours, cold seeping through the blanket, and lay listening to the river. It sounded different tonight. Louder. Faster. As though it were hurrying somewhere, or fleeing something.

She sat up. The others slept. And far upstream, past the bend where the gorge narrowed to a throat, she saw it: a light. Not firelight—too steady, too white, too high on the cliff face, burning where no lamp had any business burning. It pulsed, very slowly, like something breathing.

Below it, faint as memory, came another sound. Not the river. Metal. A deep rhythmic clanking, regular as a heartbeat, as machinery.

As a pump.

Pip's hand found the compass in her pocket. The needle, which had trembled for three days, now pointed straight upstream and held perfectly still—as steady, she thought, as a thing pointing home.

She reached over and shook Sebastian's shoulder.

"Get up," she whispered. "You need to see what your family built."
