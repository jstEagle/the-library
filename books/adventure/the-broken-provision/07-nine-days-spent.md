## 7. Nine Days, Spent

The gorge had no name on any map Sebastian had ever drawn, which was its own kind of warning. They made camp in the lee of a fallen viaduct pier, its stone furred with moss, the river muttering below through a throat of black rock. Odile rigged the tarp. Bramble built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Pip fell asleep mid-sentence, her head on the pack that held the compass, one arm thrown over it like a gatekeeper.

Sebastian sat apart, turning the map over in the firelight, reading for the hundredth time the closed lines they had followed. *Sealed by order. Sealed by order. Sealed by order.* Nine words in a clerk's hand, repeated like a prayer, or a confession.

The fire cracked. Somewhere upstream, water fell a long way and landed without echo.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said, settling beside them without invitation. "Where you look at paper like it owes you money."

"Everything owes me money."

"Then look at something that doesn't." She nodded at the sleeping shape of Pip, at Bramble's broad back as he fed the fire. "That's the only ledger worth keeping out here."

Sebastian almost answered. That was when the dogs started.

Not dogs — no, worse, lantern-light, three points of it swinging down the gorge trail in the patient rhythm of men who had never once needed to hurry. Bramble killed the fire with a sweep of his coat. Pip woke without a sound, which was the most frightening thing about her, and pressed the compass into Sebastian's palm.

"Marlowe Vane," Sebastian said, and heard their own voice come out flat as slate.

"Marlowe Vane," Odile confirmed, and put her hand on the boat-hook.

* * *

He came into their camp the way he had come into the boarding house in Saltmere: without apology, without hurry, without a single wasted motion. He was thinner than Sebastian remembered, or the gorge made him look so, his coat dark with river spray, his boots somehow dry. Two men flanked him with the bored posture of hired muscle, and behind them the lanterns hung in the mist like small captive moons.

"Nine days," Vane said. "You've spent six."

"I've spent nothing. That's rather the point of me."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, never quite. He looked at the crew the way an assayer looks at ore: Odile, coiled; Bramble, immovable; Pip, small and watchful and closer to Vane's throat than anyone had calculated. Then his gaze returned to Sebastian, and something in it had changed. In Saltmere it had been the look of a man holding a leash. Now it was the look of a man holding something he wished were heavier, so he could set it down with a clear conscience.

"Send your people to the boat," Vane said. "What I have to say is for you."

"They're my people," Sebastian said, and heard the word *my* land in the air between them like a struck bell. "Whatever it is, they hear it."

Vane studied them for a long moment. The river filled the silence.

"Very well." He drew a folded paper from his coat, but did not open it. "Do you know who the dead man was? The one in the cave, with my compass in his hand and your map in his pocket."

"Someone who drowned looking for a river's source."

"He didn't drown. He was dying when he went in. The tide was his choice — a place to end where no one would find him until the sea had made him anonymous." Vane's voice stayed level, but it had acquired a grain, a wear in it, like rope that had held too many loads. "His name was Aldous Wren. He was a surveyor. Forty years ago he worked the original line up this valley — and for the last thirty of those years, he worked with your mother. Correspondence, mostly. Plans. She trusted him with the one thing she never trusted her own family."

The gorge seemed to tilt. Sebastian became very aware of the fire's dead smell, of Pip's breath, of the compass's weight in their hand like a small cold heart.

"My mother," Sebastian said carefully, "was disowned before I could walk."

"She was disowned because she found out what the source was for. Because she wouldn't help chain it." Vane unfolded the paper at last, and even in the lantern light Sebastian could see the age of it, the foxed paper, a woman's hand — a hand Sebastian knew without knowing how, the way you know a face from a dream. "Wren carried her letters for thirty years. And when he knew he was dying, he carried them to the only person she'd ever told him about. You. He just got there before I did."

"And you." Sebastian's mouth was dry. "The debt. The ultimatum. Nine days."

"There was never a debt. Not in any way that mattered. Your mother left provision — the *Broken Provision*, the lawyers called it, because the trust was shattered into conditions no one could meet. One condition was that you be brought to Wren's map. Your uncle's people couldn't find you. Wren couldn't reach you in time. But a debt collector can always find a debtor." Vane's eyes were steady and terribly tired. "I invented your ruin, Sebastian. The creditors, the ultimatum — bait, dressed as a bill. I'm sorry. I'm not built for sorry, but I am, this once."

The world did not lurch. That was the strange thing. Sebastian had spent six days being hunted by their own life, and now the hunt stood revealed as a fabrication, and the ground did not so much as shift. Perhaps, some cold clear part of them observed, because the ground had never been under their feet to begin with.

"The provision," Sebastian said. "What does it give me?"

"Everything. The name. The estate at Greywater. The fortune your uncle has been spending thirty years pretending is his." Vane folded the letter away. "And one more thing your mother wrote, which I was instructed to deliver only in person, only to you." He hesitated — the first hesitation Sebastian had ever seen in him. "She wrote: *Tell my child the river is the inheritance. The rest is the debt.*"

* * *

They gave him the fire, because Odile said a man who tells the truth should at least not freeze while doing it, and because Bramble said nothing, which from Bramble was a verdict.

Vane sat with his hands open on his knees, a man deliberately unarmed. "My employers arrive tomorrow," he said. "Not your uncle — he's still two days behind you, drowning a station in his wake. The men coming are the ones who hold the provision's other half. They want the source sealed and the provision void, and they want you to sign it void yourself, because your signature is the one thing that makes it clean. In exchange: the fortune, the name, the estate, and your debts — the real ones and the ones I invented — dissolved forever."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then they take the valley the old way. Men, gates, guns. They've done it before. That's rather their whole trade." Vane looked into the fire. "I'm supposed to deliver you willing. I have until dawn to decide whether I deliver you at all."

Pip spoke from the dark, where she had been listening with the total attention of the small. "You invented Sebastian's whole life falling apart. Why should we believe any of this is true?"

"You shouldn't." Vane did not flinch. "That's the honest answer, girl. I've lied so long I've lost the trick of proving myself. All I can give you is an hour. One honest hour, before my employers' outriders reach this gorge and honesty stops being an option for anyone." He stood. "I'll be at the boat. When the hour is spent, tell me what you've decided — or tell me nothing, and I'll decide for you, badly, the way I've decided everything else."

He walked to the water's edge and sat on the stones like a man waiting for a tide.

* * *

The hour belonged to Sebastian, and they spent it badly, gloriously, walking the gorge with the compass in their fist and their mother's sentence turning over and over like river water. *The river is the inheritance. The rest is the debt.*

They thought of Greywater estate, which they had never seen, and which their body nonetheless ached toward the way a scar aches toward rain. They thought of a name — *Dravenmoor* — spoken without flinching, spoken in rooms where people stood when you entered. They thought of the fortune, and what a fortune could buy, and how it could buy back every year of salt fish and borrowed beds and doors closed by landlords.

And then they looked back at the camp.

Bramble had rebuilt the fire, small and banked, and was mending the boat-hook lashings by its light, working the way he did everything: without complaint, without praise, as if repair were simply what hands were for. Odile stood at the gorge's mouth watching the upstream dark, counting threats the way other people counted stars. And Pip had crept to the edge of sleep again, but not all the way — Sebastian could see it in her, the half-waking of a child who had learned that love could leave in the night, and who kept one eye on the door of it.

*The river is the inheritance. The rest is the debt.*

They had spent six days learning the river. They had spent six days learning these three. And somewhere in those six days — in Bramble's guilt and Odile's suspicion and Pip's twice-stolen compass — Sebastian had stopped being a man fleeing a debt and become something their mother had planned for all along: a keeper. Not of the fortune. Of the water.

They walked back down to the stones where Vane sat.

"One hour," he said, rising.

"Spent." Sebastian held out the compass — Wren's compass, their mother's legacy, the needle steady and true in the dark. "I'm not signing anything. Not the provision, not the seal, not any bargain that trades a valley for a name. And I'm not going with your employers, and I'm not going with you."

Vane looked at the compass and did not take it. "Then you're choosing the hard road."

"I'm choosing the *right* one. There's a difference, and I think you've known it for years, and I think it's been eating you." Sebastian closed Vane's fingers over the compass himself. "You said you'd decide for me, badly, the way you've decided everything else. So decide well, for once. Take me to them. Tell them I refused. And then — " Sebastian held his gaze — "tell them where I'm going, and how fast I'm moving, and let your conscience work out which side of the gate you'd rather be standing on when the river comes down."

For a long moment Marlowe Vane was silent, and the gorge was silent, and somewhere far upstream the water fell a long way and landed without echo.

Then he did something no debt collector had ever done in the history of money. He laughed — a short, rusty, astonished sound, like a gate opening after thirty years shut.

"The boat," he said, "is yours. The outriders will be here within the hour, and they don't share my recent education." He was already moving, waking the others with two clipped words each. "The source is close, isn't it? The map's last line?"

"Close enough to reach by dawn," Odile said, "if the river lets us."

"Then don't let it ask twice." Vane pressed the compass back into Sebastian's hand and folded their fingers over it, and for one moment his grip was not a collector's grip at all. "Your mother wrote one more thing. She said: *when my child finds their crew, they'll know them because they'll choose them over me.*" His voice roughened. "I never understood that sentence. I think I do now. Go."

Lanterns flared at the head of the gorge — the outriders, early, their light spilling down the rock like spilled mercury.

"Boat," Sebastian said. "Now."

They ran for the water together, all five of them — even Vane, who had spent his whole life collecting debts and was, at last, spending one.
