## 8. The Forbidden Source

The mountain had a heartbeat.

Sebastian had felt it first as a tremor in the soles of their boots, half a mile back, where the abandoned line narrowed to a throat of rock and the rails—rails that should have rusted to lace—ran bright and clean as if polished daily by some enormous, patient hand. Bramble had knelt and laid his palm flat against the steel.

"Warm," he'd said, and his voice had gone strange, the way a man's voice goes when he finds a grave he helped dig.

Now, an hour deeper, the warmth had become a presence. It pressed against Sebastian's skin like a living thing. The air tasted of iron and hot stone and something older underneath, something like rain that had never fallen. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, each drop landing with the regularity of a clock, and beneath the dripping Sebastian could hear the mountain breathing—a slow, vast exhalation, a slow, vast intake.

Pip walked close at Sebastian's side, one small hand hooked in the hem of their coat. She had not let go of that hem since the gorge. She had not spoken of it, and Sebastian had not asked her to. Some fears you carried like a compass: always pointing at the thing you could not look at directly.

The tunnel ended.

Not in rock, not in a sealed gate like the others Bramble had stared at with his old guilt written across his face, but in light.

Sebastian stepped out onto a ledge of black basalt and stopped breathing.

The cavern was a cathedral that no architect could have confessed to building. It opened downward and outward into a vastness the lamplight could not reach the far side of, and at its heart, far below, the source burned.

It was not fire. Sebastian had expected fire, had braced for fire, and what they saw instead was worse—more beautiful, more terrible. A spring the size of a village square, boiling up from a fissure in the earth's bones, glowing a deep arterial orange at its center and cooling to gold and then to the pale green of riverwater at its edges, where it spilled over shelves of stone in slow, luminous cascades. Steam rose from it in columns thick as church pillars, coiling up toward a ceiling lost in darkness, and the steam itself caught the light and held it, so that the whole cavern seemed to be filled with slow-turning clouds of amber.

And threaded through it all—through the water, through the rock, through the steam—ran the machinery.

Sebastian's engineer's eye took it in the way a starving man's eye takes in a banquet, helplessly, completely. Pipes thick as train tunnels. Valves the size of wagon wheels, set into the basalt like the eyes of buried gods. Conduits of riveted iron marching off into eight dark throats in the cavern walls—eight, and Sebastian knew without counting the map twice that every one of them matched a closed line. The sealed railways had not been failures. They had been veins. The mountain's heat, the mountain's water, drawn up and channeled and sent humming down those iron throats to drive the pressure engines of the valley's railways, to warm the waterworks, to turn the mills.

Every closed line ended here. Every line had been sealed from this end, by hands that controlled the flow.

"It's beautiful," Pip whispered, and her voice broke the spell just enough for Sebastian to remember how to breathe.

"It's a throttle," Odile said.

She stood at the ledge's edge, river-gray eyes narrowed against the steam-glow, and she did not look awed. She looked like a lockkeeper's daughter reading a sluice. She raised one arm and pointed, down and to the left, where the great spring narrowed into the first of the outflow channels.

"Look at the gates. Look at how far they're shut."

Sebastian looked, and the awe in their chest turned over like a fish and showed its belly.

The gates were shut. Not open, not half-open—shut, down to a gap a man could step over, so that the mighty spring, with all its furious abundance, was being strangled at the throat. The water backed up behind the gates in a shimmering, pressurized lake, and only a thin, rationed thread of it spilled through to feed the conduits. Enough to run the railways. Enough to keep the mills turning. Enough to make the valley's dependence absolute.

Not enough to fill the wells downstream. Not enough for the villages. Not enough for anything but the ledger of a man who had learned that scarcity was worth more than water.

"Cassian," Sebastian said. The name came out flat, and tasting of copper.

"He's been turning the tap for years," Bramble said. His voice was very quiet. "The droughts. The failing wells. The lines 'failing' one by one, each closure blamed on the rock, on the water, on the war, on anything—" He stopped. Sebastian heard him swallow. "I sealed two of those gates myself. I signed the orders. I thought I was closing dead tunnels."

"You were closing live ones," Odile said, "for a dead man's fortune."

"He's not dead yet," said a voice from behind them.

* * *

Sebastian turned, and the cavern's heat seemed suddenly the least of what pressed on their skin.

Cassian Dravenmoor stood at the far end of the basalt ledge, flanked by six men, and he was not dressed for a mountain. He wore a coat the color of good claret and a waistcoat that had never known a day's labor, and he looked at the burning spring the way a man looks at a portrait of his father—with possession, and resentment, and a hunger that possession had never once satisfied.

"How," Sebastian began.

"The drowned station. You were three days behind me the whole way, cousin. I let you walk ahead because I needed you to see it." Cassian spread his hands, and the amber light climbed his rings. "I needed you to understand what you'd inherit. Not a map. Not a name. *This.* The one lever in the valley that moves everything."

"I'm not your cousin."

"You're my brother's child, and my brother's widow bled this family's secrets into the river before she died, and now here you stand on my ledge looking at my spring." Cassian smiled. It was a well-made smile, and it reached nothing above his cheekbones. "Blood is a debt, Sebastian. You of all people should understand debt."

Pip's hand tightened in Sebastian's coat hem. Odile had gone very still, the stillness of water before it freezes, and Sebastian saw her eyes moving—not to Cassian, but to his men, counting them, measuring the ledge, measuring the distance to the gates below.

"You've been starving them," Sebastian said. "The villages. The farms. Children are drinking from ditches because you wanted the railways to come begging to your door."

"I wanted the valley to *need* me," Cassian said, without shame, without heat, as if reciting a lesson learned long ago and well. "Need is the only loyalty that doesn't rot. My father hoarded gold and died poor in everything that mattered. I hoard the one thing gold can't buy when the wells run dry." He stepped closer, and his men stepped with him, and the steam-light made his shadow enormous on the cavern wall. "And now, cousin, we come to the part of the evening where you choose."

* * *

It happened fast, after that, the way disasters and miracles both do.

Odile moved first. She did not shout, did not warn—she simply stepped backward off the ledge onto the iron stair that wound down the cavern wall toward the gates, a maintenance stair, rust-eaten and narrow, and her hand found Sebastian's sleeve and *pulled*, and Sebastian understood in a single white-hot instant what she meant: the mechanism. The master valve. Somewhere below, at the throat of the spring, there was a wheel or a lever or a fuse that governed all the rest, and it did not matter what Cassian's men did on the ledge if two people reached that wheel first.

"Hold the stair!" Bramble bellowed, and then he was past them, a mountain of a man gone soft in the middle but never in the shoulders, planting himself on the top step like a gatepost given flesh. The first of Cassian's men came at him and learned, briefly and painfully, what a tunnel foreman's hands were for.

Sebastian took the stairs two at a time, Pip's weight suddenly on their back—she had leapt, no hesitation, no permission asked—and the stair groaned under three bodies and the heat rose up to meet them and the spring blazed below, close enough now that spray struck Sebastian's face, scalding and sweet, and the roar of it filled their ears like the mountain shouting its one word over and over: *free, free, free.*

The mechanism stood on a basalt platform above the first gate: a wheel of black iron tall as Pip, banded in brass, its spokes worn bright by decades of a single hand turning it, closing it, closing it, year upon year upon year.

Cassian's voice came down the stair, no longer well-made, no longer smiling. "Sebastian! Turn that wheel and you turn away everything. The name. The estate. The fortune that should have been your mother's—"

Sebastian gripped the wheel. The iron was warm as a living thing.

"—and I will have you hunted to the last county of the map—"

The wheel was stiff with years of closing. Sebastian set their feet, set their shoulder, set their whole ruined, indebted, disowned life against it.

"—you'll have *nothing*—"

"I'll have them," Sebastian said, and turned.

The wheel gave with a groan like a ship coming about, and above them, through the steam, Sebastian heard Bramble roar and the crack of iron on iron, and heard Pip shout a warning in a voice too big for her body, and the gates below began, inch by singing inch, to open—

—and the mountain's heartbeat, for the first time in Sebastian's memory, began to quicken.
