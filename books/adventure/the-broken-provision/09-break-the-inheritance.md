## 9. Break the Inheritance

Odile had grown up reading water the way other people read faces. Her father had taught her at the lock: *the river never lies, little bird. It only tells you what it's been told to do.* All her life she had watched the Greywether run—sometimes fat with spring melt, sometimes thin as a beggar's wrist—and she had always assumed its moods were the moods of weather and mountain. Standing now on the iron catwalk above the source cavern, she understood how wrong she had been.

The spring lay below them like a sleeping animal in a cage of brass and stone. Steam rose off it in slow columns, gilding everything in a wet amber light, and around it coiled the machinery of a century's theft: great geared wheels the size of millstones, sluice-gates thick as church doors, pipes running off into the dark like the roots of an iron tree. Every closed line on the dead man's map ended here. Every starved lock, every failing well, every village downstream learning to ration its rain—all of it traced back to this room, where the Dravenmoor family had taught the river to obey.

And Cassian Dravenmoor stood at the center of it, arms spread like a man showing off his garden.

"You see now," he called up to them, his voice carrying sweetly over the hiss of steam. "You see what they never understood. Not power over the valley—*stewardship*. Someone must hold the reins, or the whole region drowns in its own abundance."

Bramble spat over the railing. Odile put a hand on his arm. Below, at the base of the stair, Sebastian stood very still, water dripping from their coat, Pip pressed close against their side. Between them and the floodgates stretched forty feet of slick catwalk and three of Cassian's men, and behind those men, Bramble, holding the last gate alone, buying them minutes with his shoulders and his guilt.

"Sebastian." Cassian's voice softened into something almost tender, and that was the worst thing about him, Odile thought—the way he could make cruelty sound like kindness worn smooth by use. "Look at you. Soaked through, half-starved, hunted by creditors. Your mother's stubbornness in your face, God help us both. And I am offering to end all of it. The name. The estate at Harrowgate. The fortune your grandfather built and your mother threw away. Everything that should have been yours, if only—" he gestured at the caged spring, at the throttled river, at the whole groaning apparatus of control "—if only you'll stand here beside me and keep it chained."

Odile watched Sebastian's face. She had known them for less than two weeks and already she could read them better than any gauge on any lock: the flicker in the eyes when the word *family* was said aloud, the hunger they tried to hide behind engineering talk and dry humor. She knew what Cassian was doing. He was offering not money but belonging—a mother's name restored, a place at a table that had been set for generations.

For one long moment, Sebastian looked like they might take it.

Then Pip's small hand found theirs, and something in Sebastian's face settled, the way silt settles when the current finally slows.

"The wells are dying downstream," Sebastian said quietly. "Children are carrying water four miles from the springs at Marrow Fen. You did that. With this." They lifted the map—the dead man's map, their mother's ally's map—water-stained and soft as cloth. "He came down from this mountain to tell someone. He died in a cave waiting for the tide to let him out. That's your stewardship, Uncle. A man dead in the dark with the truth in his hand."

"He was a thief of proprietary information—"

"He was *family*," Sebastian said, "in every way that matters, and you let him drown in a hole rather than hear him out." They began to climb the stairs. "I spent my whole life thinking my mother was disgraced because she walked away from this. She wasn't disgraced. She was *escaped*. And I'm going to finish her escape for her."

Cassian's men moved. What followed was ugly and brief—Bramble roaring up from the gate he'd been holding, a wrench swinging, Odile herself putting a lockpick knife somewhere it made a man reconsider his career—but Odile barely registered it. She was watching Sebastian walk past all of it, straight to the master sluice-wheel at the heart of the machine, and set both hands upon it.

"Sebastian!" Cassian's composure cracked at last, and beneath it Odile saw the true face of the family: fear. "That mechanism is under a hundred years of pressure. Open it and you destroy everything—the lines, the locks, the fortune, *your inheritance*. There will be nothing left. Nothing!"

"There was never anything left," Sebastian said. "That's the whole trick, isn't it? You kept the valley thirsty so everyone would stay grateful for drops. Well." Their hands tightened on the wheel. "The river keeps no ledger. Time somebody told it so."

They turned the wheel.

* * *

Odile would remember the sound for the rest of her life. Not an explosion—nothing so simple. It was a groan first, deep and mineral, the sound of the mountain clearing its throat after a century of being gagged. Then the great gates shuddered, rust raining down like red snow, and the spring beneath them seemed to *rise*, steam billowing white across the catwalks, and the water came.

It came the way rivers come home: without malice and without mercy.

Cassian's men fled for the high tunnel, dropping their weapons, and Odile hauled Pip toward the ladder while the lower galleries filled with thunder. But Cassian did not flee. He stood in the rising water among his beautiful machines, watching the gears seize and shear, watching a century of careful throttling tear itself apart, and he screamed—not words, just rage given voice, a man drowning in the vault of his own hoard.

"Sebastian!" he cried as the water took his knees. "This is *madness*! You could have had everything!"

"I have everything," Sebastian called back, and Odile heard it clearly over the roar, and she believed it. "I have a crew. I have a river. Go home, Uncle—if there's still a home above the waterline."

But there was no home left for Cassian Dravenmoor. The main vault door, swollen in its frame, jammed as the pressure surged, and the water that had waited a hundred years behind his gates took back its own with compound interest. Odile saw him go under once, twice, clawing at a wheel that no longer answered anyone. Sebastian lunged for the rail—she saw it, the instinct, the blood calling to blood even now—and then stopped, hands gripping iron, and did not jump.

It was Pip who looked away first. It was Bramble who said, gruff and low, "Some men build their own tombs and call them estates," and turned his back.

The last Odile saw of Cassian Dravenmoor was his hand, pale in the churning amber water, reaching upward toward a fortune that no longer existed.

* * *

They climbed. The mountain shook around them, shedding a century of machinery, and the crew went up the maintenance shaft hand over hand—Pip first, light as a wren; then Odile; then Sebastian, who paused once at a landing where an old brass plaque read *DRavenmoor Hydraulic Works, Est. 18—*, and pried it loose and left it to fall into the dark.

Outside, night air hit them like a blessing. They came out onto the mountainside above the works, and turned, and watched the valley learn to breathe.

The Greywether, freed, ran silver under the moon—running full and loud and reckless, overflowing its old channels, finding forgotten beds, singing through country that had forgotten the sound. Downstream, Odile knew, the locks would be surging, the wells refilling, the fen springs rising to meet the demand of villages that had learned to hope small. The sealed rail lines would flood and fail and be forgotten entirely, and good riddance; some inheritances deserved to be drowned.

Sebastian stood at the edge of it all, soaked and shaking, holding the compass in one open palm. The needle swung wild near the works, useless among all that iron, but pointing now, faintly, steadily, downstream. Toward home.

Odile came to stand beside them. For a long time neither spoke. The grief was there—she could see it, sitting on Sebastian's shoulders like a wet coat, heavy and familiar. Grief for the mother who had died before telling the whole story. Grief for the name that had been offered and refused. Grief for the family that never was, mourned properly at last, buried under moving water.

"You gave up a fortune," Odile said at last.

"I gave up a *chain*," Sebastian said. "There's a difference. My mother knew it. Took me twenty-nine years and a dead man's map to learn it too." They closed their fist around the compass and managed something that was almost a smile. "Besides. I'm told the crew gets a share of whatever we find next."

"Next?" Pip's head popped up from behind a boulder, eyes enormous in the moonlight. "There's a *next*?"

Sebastian looked at the three of them—the lockkeeper's daughter, the disgraced foreman, the orphan thief—and Odile watched the last of the Dravenmoor heir fall away from their face, replaced by something better.

"The river's running free," Sebastian said. "Somebody ought to follow it and see where it goes."

Far below, the floodwaters reached the sea, and the tide, far out along the coast, began—very slowly—to turn.
