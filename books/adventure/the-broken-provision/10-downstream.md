## 10. Downstream

The river ran loud for the first time in years.

Sebastian felt it through the hull before they saw anything at all—a deep, muscular current pushing them along like a hand at the small of the back, the water no longer trickling apologetically past the shallows but *moving*, whole and unbroken, as though some long-held breath had finally been released. The boat rode high. The spray came up cold and clean-smelling, and when Odile laughed from the tiller—actually laughed, a sound Sebastian could count on one hand the occasions of hearing—it was because the river was doing half their steering for them.

"Look at her go," Bramble said from the bow, his bandaged arm cradled against his chest, his face still gray with exhaustion from holding that gate in the flooded dark. But his eyes were bright. "She remembers what she's for."

Pip stood on the middle thwart with the wind whipping her hair into a black tangle, arms spread wide like she meant to take flight off the prow. She'd barely slept since the source. None of them had. But where the others wore their exhaustion like wet wool, Pip wore hers like a banner.

"Race you downstream!" she shouted at the water, at the hills, at nobody, at everybody.

Sebastian sat near the stern with the compass open in their palm, watching the needle swing lazily, uselessly, its purpose spent. Behind them the mountain fell away in green shoulders. Ahead, the valley opened like a hand unclenching.

They passed the first farm by midmorning. Sebastian knew something had changed before anyone spoke—the farmer standing in his turnip rows wasn't staring at the sky or cursing the drought. He was simply looking at the river, hat in his hands, the way a man looks at a thing he'd stopped believing in. As the boat slid past, he raised the hat. Not to them. To the water.

By the second bend, children were running the banks alongside them, shrieking, keeping pace until the current outstripped their legs. By noon they could smell woodsmoke from the village chimneys, and beneath it—the detail that made Sebastian's throat tighten—the green smell of water thrown on dry stone. Someone was washing. Someone had water enough to wash with.

* * *

The village came out to meet them at the old landing, the same rotten planks Sebastian had slipped away from in the dark all those weeks ago, now crowded shoulder to shoulder with people. Fishermen whose boats had been beached for want of a living river. Grandmothers who'd been hauling buckets from a spring two miles distant. Farmers with soil still under their nails, come straight from fields they'd already begun to hope over.

And in the front row, a knot of villagers Sebastian recognized—the ones who had sent Pip upstream with a stolen compass and a child's desperate errand. Their faces were doing several things at once: shame at having sent an orphan to do a dangerous man's work, relief that she'd come back breathing, and something rawer underneath, something that looked like awe.

Pip leapt from the boat before it even touched the pilings, landing light as a gull, and the crowd broke around her like water around a stone. For a moment Sebastian lost sight of her entirely—swallowed by coats and skirts and reaching hands—and felt a spike of old fear, the fear of losing people, before the crowd opened again and there she was, hoisted onto a fisherman's shoulders, laughing so hard she couldn't speak, being carried up the lane like a returning general.

"She did it," Odile said quietly, coming to stand at Sebastian's shoulder. "The little thief. She told them the truth about the river, and they sent her, and she didn't turn back. Not once. Not even at the gorge."

"Not even when we were drowning," Bramble added, wading ashore with the dignity of a man who intended to find food immediately. "Though I notice nobody's carrying *me* anywhere."

"You're too heavy," Pip called down from her perch, without missing a beat. "You eat like three foremen."

"I *was* three foremen," Bramble said. "Before the disgrace."

The laughter that went around the landing was easy and unguarded, and Sebastian stood in the middle of it feeling like a man listening to music in a language he almost remembered. This was belonging, they supposed. It didn't announce itself. It just arrived, muddy boots and all, and started arguing about dinner.

* * *

Marlowe Vane found them at dusk, at the long table outside the Salt & Anchor where the village had insisted on feeding them whether they wanted feeding or not. He arrived on foot, coat brushed, ledger under his arm, looking exactly as he always looked: like a man who had never once been surprised by weather.

The table went quiet. Pip's fork froze halfway to her mouth. Bramble shifted his weight in a way that suggested he was calculating how many chairs he could throw.

Vane set the ledger down, opened it to a page Sebastian knew by heart—the page with their name on it, the figures, the interest compounding like rot—and regarded them across the candlelight.

"Nine days," Vane said. "That was the term."

"It's been eleven," Sebastian said.

"It has." Vane turned the ledger toward them. "Which means, by the strict reading, I should be taking everything you own. Your tools. Your papers. That coat, which I have long admired." He paused. "Fortunately for you, my employers are currently underwater."

A ripple went around the table. Cassian Dravenmoor's name hung in the air between them, unsaid and enormous.

"The contract specified retrieval of the source," Vane continued. "For the family. Sealed, throttled, held. Instead—" he glanced at Pip, at Odile, at Bramble's bandage, at the mud dried on every boot at the table— "you destroyed it. Freed it. Drowned the vault and the fortune and the heir's ambitions in one act of spectacular insolvency." He closed the ledger. "I am given to understand that this constitutes a breach."

"It does," Sebastian said.

"Mm." Vane produced a pen. "However. I have spent twenty-two years collecting debts, Mr. Dravenmoor, and in that time I have learned that paper is only ever worth what stands behind it. Coin backs paper. Land backs coin. And stories—" he tapped the ledger once— "stories back everything else. There is not a house in this valley that will forget what happened on that mountain. There is not a bank, a magistrate, or a creditor who will extend credit against a name that freed a river, because every soul who might collect on that name is drinking from it tonight."

He uncapped the pen.

"So I am calling the debt paid. In full. By story instead of coin." And he drew a single line through the figures—through Sebastian's name, through the interest, through the whole miserable architecture of numbers that had chased them into a sea cave in the first place—then tore the page out, folded it once, and set it on the table like a man laying down a winning card.

Then, because he was still Marlowe Vane, he added: "The meal, however, is your own expense. I don't do charity."

Bramble laughed so hard he nearly reopened his stitches.

Vane gathered his ledger, inclined his head to the table—to Odile last, and longest, which Sebastian filed away for later examination—and walked back into the dark the way he'd come. At the edge of the lamplight he paused, half-turned, and said, without looking back:

"For what it's worth, the dead man would have approved. He always said the river shouldn't belong to anyone." A beat. "He said it should belong to everyone downstream."

Then he was gone, and the debt was gone, and Sebastian sat very still with their hands flat on the table, feeling the strange weightlessness of a person who owes nothing to anybody.

* * *

They stayed three days, because the village wouldn't hear of less, and then a fourth, because Pip made eyes at them across the breakfast table until Sebastian surrendered. Wells that had run to mud filled clear overnight; Sebastian watched an old woman draw the first full bucket and hold it up to the light like a lantern. Farmers walked their fields poking at soil that had gone soft and dark again. Somewhere upvalley, men were already talking about reopening the lower lock—not to hoard the river, but to work with it, freight and fish and flood-season all in their proper turns. Bramble had been asked to consult. He pretended to be reluctant. He had already sharpened his pencils.

On the fifth morning, Sebastian stood alone at the landing with the compass in their hand.

It was a good instrument. Brass case worn smooth, glass scratched, the needle true. Their mother's ally had carried it through closed tunnels and drowned stations to bring it here, to bring *them* here, and it had done its work. It pointed north. It pointed home, if a person had one. It pointed, always, somewhere.

Odile found them there, as Odile always seemed to find them, and leaned on the rail without asking questions.

"They'll want you to stay," she said. "The village. They'll build you a house if you let them. Name a lock after you."

"I know."

"And?"

Sebastian turned the compass over. The sun caught the brass, threw a little coin of light against the pilings.

"My mother's family kept everything," they said. "Estate, fortune, name, river—all of it locked behind gates, and it made them small enough to drown in their own cellar. I keep thinking about that. About how much a person can hold before the holding becomes the whole of them." They looked up at Odile, at the river behind her, running bright and free toward the sea. "I don't want to hold things. I want to *go* places. With people worth going with."

Odile studied them for a long moment, then smiled the rare smile, the real one. "Bramble will complain about the boat."

"Bramble always complains about the boat."

"Pip will steal the compass."

"Twice," Sebastian agreed. "Minimum."

Down the lane, they could hear Pip's voice rising over the market noise, negotiating something outrageous with a fishmonger, and Bramble's rumbling counteroffer, and the ordinary beautiful racket of a town with water enough to waste on argument. The river slid past the landing, strong and steady, going where it pleased.

Sebastian closed the compass and put it in their pocket—not as an inheritance, not as a claim on anything, but as a promise. The needle inside would point wherever they pointed it next.

"North?" Odile asked.

Sebastian grinned, and stepped off the landing, and answered the way the river would have.

"Upstream. There's always another source."
