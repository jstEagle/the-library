## 1. The Weight of Staying

The river at Callow's Bend ran green in the early light, the color of old bottle glass, and Dominic Brightwater watched it from the deck of the *Maren Hale* while the salvage crew's winch groaned through its first pull of the morning. Somewhere below the surface, tangled in the drowned pilings of a ferry landing that had collapsed forty years ago, lay a cargo cradle full of machine parts nobody wanted badly enough to dive for themselves. That was why they'd hired her. Nobody on this crew knew her name. Not really.

"Line's taut," called Boz from the rail, a thick-armed man with a beard he trimmed with fishing line. "You sure about that third piling? Water reads eight meters there."

"It reads eight meters where the water's honest." Dominic coiled her safety tether, checked the clasp twice, then a third time out of habit rather than doubt. "Past the bend it silts up. I'll go down along the upstream face."

Boz grunted, which was his way of agreeing, and threw the tag line into the current.

She went over the side before the sun cleared the bluffs.

The cold hit her like a hand pressed flat against her chest—the river here came down off the high snowfields, and even in late summer it kept winter's temper in its bones. Dominic let the chill do its work, sharpening her, narrowing the world to pressure and breath and the pale geometry of the pilings rising out of the murk. She had been diving since she was fourteen. Her father taught her, back when teaching her was still something he did with his whole attention, before the maps took him. She could hold a thought underwater longer than most people could hold it on land, and the thought she held now was simple: three more days. Three days of wages, a share of the cargo fee, and then north on the morning barge with a new name stitched into her jacket lining and no forwarding address anywhere.

Ten years she'd been doing this. Ten years of crews and border towns and rivers with different names but the same green glass color, the same cold hands. She was good at leaving. It was the one inheritance she'd never resented.

Her lamp found the cargo cradle wedged between two pilings, silted to its axles. She worked the first shackle loose with a short pry bar, feeling the metal complain through her gloves, and for a few minutes there was nothing in the world but the work—clean, mechanical, honest. This was the other thing she loved about diving: it asked nothing of her past. The river didn't care whose daughter she was. Down here she was just a pair of hands and a lungful of air, and hands were all any salvage crew had ever wanted from her.

Then the second shackle shifted, and the cradle groaned, and a sheet of silt lifted off something beneath it—something that wasn't machinery.

A case. Leather, brass-cornered, the kind surveyors used to carry instruments in. It had been down here long enough to grow a skin of black river-fur, but the clasps were still bright, still seated, still doing their job after four decades of patience. Dominic hung in the green water and looked at it, and felt the particular cold that had nothing to do with temperature move up through her chest.

She should leave it. Anything that old, that well-made, sitting under a collapsed landing in a border town nobody surveyed—that belonged to somebody's grief, or somebody's crime, and both kinds of ownership ended the same way when she got involved. She'd learned that lesson twice, once with money and once worse.

But her hands were already moving. That was the trouble with hands like hers; they'd been trained by people who believed every buried thing deserved an accounting, and training outlived belief.

She hooked the case to her lift line and kicked for the surface.

* * *

They opened it on the deck in the noon heat, four of them crouched around it like men around a card game, and what was inside turned out to be worth almost nothing and cost almost everything.

Maps. A dozen sheets of drafting linen, rolled tight, water-stained at the edges but sound at their cores. Survey maps of the canyon country upriver—the Broken Rivergate country, though nobody called it anything but "the bad narrows" here, the stretch where the Callow sank underground and came back three days later wearing a different name. Elevation lines. Soundings. Notes in a cramped engineer's hand.

And across the top margin of the finest sheet, in handwriting Dominic would have known blindfolded, by touch, by the shape of its capital letters alone:

*Meridian Institute — Concession Survey — Final Revision.*

Her mother's initials sat in the approval block. R.M.B. Rowan Meridian Brightwater. The woman whose name had opened doors for everyone in the world except her daughter.

"Well, hell," said Boz, whistling low. "That's Institute paper. That's ten-year paper, maybe older. There's collectors who'd pay for that."

"There's lawyers who'd say we stole it," said the crew chief, a narrow woman named Sefa who owned the *Maren Hale* and trusted nothing she hadn't weighed herself. She looked at Dominic. "Your call, diver. You brought it up."

Dominic kept her face still. It was a skill she'd practiced as long as the diving—keeping her face still while her blood tried to climb out through her skin. *Final Revision.* She knew what those words meant. She'd grown up hearing about the last Rivergate expedition the way other children heard about shipwrecks: funded by the Meridian Institute, led by engineers who came back without half their number, closed by the Institute within the season, sealed in paperwork so dense that even the newspapers gave up on it. Her father had gone down with that expedition. Officially. Unofficially, he had walked into the canyon with her mother and eleven others, and only the papers had come home.

"I say we sell it to the collectors," someone said. "Paper don't drown anybody."

"I say we burn it," said Sefa. "Institute paper's like Institute debt. It finds you."

They argued. Dominic let them, turning the top map toward the light with fingers she made herself keep steady. The drafting was beautiful. That was the thing nobody understood about her mother unless they'd seen her work—Rowan Brightwater drew lines the way other people prayed, with total conviction, and every contour on this sheet was a sentence in a language Dominic had spent ten years refusing to read. The canyon walls rose in careful hatched strokes. The river vanished underground at a point marked with a small, precise symbol, and beside the symbol, in her mother's hand, three words she had to lean close to make out:

*Gate holds. Trust.*

"What does that mean?" Boz asked, reading over her shoulder. "Trust what?"

"Nothing," Dominic said. "It means nothing. Old survey shorthand."

It wasn't. She knew it wasn't. But she folded the map along its original creases with the care owed to something explosive, and she heard herself say, in the flat voice she used for decisions she'd already made, "We split it. Paper goes to the collectors in Harrow's Ferry—they pay fair and they don't ask names. Fee comes out of my share. I'll take it up myself."

Sefa studied her for a long moment. "You know somebody in Harrow's Ferry?"

"I know everybody nowhere," Dominic said. "That's the job."

* * *

She meant to leave that night.

She had the fare counted out in a oilcloth roll under her bunk, had the jacket with the false name already brushed and folded, had the barge schedule memorized the way she memorized everything—once, completely, forever. Leaving was a discipline like diving. You checked your gear, you checked your exits, you didn't linger at depth. Every town had a bottom, and the trick was to surface before you found yours.

So of course she spent her last evening walking.

Callow's Bend at dusk was lamplight and river-smell, drying nets and frying fish, the low brick hump of the toll house where the road crossed over on the old aqueduct span. It was a town built on top of another town—the way border towns always were, she supposed, stacked on their own drowned history. Kids ran the plank walkways barefoot. An argument about card debts leaked out of the Salt House and dissolved into laughter. Ordinary life, going on without anyone's permission, the way it did everywhere she passed through and never stayed.

She told herself she was walking because the evening was fine. She knew better. She was walking because the map was rolled inside her jacket against her ribs, and because *gate holds, trust* had been repeating under her thoughts all day like water finding a crack, and because somewhere in the last ten years she had developed a superstition: things surfaced for a reason. Salvage law, her father used to call it, grinning. Nothing stays buried that wants finding.

He'd grinned like that the last time she saw him. She'd been nine.

She stopped at the rail of the aqueduct span, where the green river slid underneath, dark now, keeping its own counsel. Upstream, past the last lamps, the canyon country began—a black seam in the world, the place the maps were drawn of, the place her parents' generation had gone into and not all come out of. She had spent her whole adult life arranging herself so that she never had to look at that seam directly. Crews upriver, towns downriver, always the long way around. It was astonishing, really, how much of a life you could build out of avoidance if you were disciplined about it. She'd been very disciplined.

*Three days,* she reminded herself. Sell the paper, take the barge, be nobody again by Thursday.

"You're the diver off the *Hale*."

The voice came from the shadow past the toll house—an old man's voice, dry as reeds. Dominic turned with her weight already balanced, the way you turn when a stranger addresses you at night in a border town.

He stood at the end of the span, leaning on a prospector's staff, and the lamplight reached just far enough to show her a face like weathered rope: deep-cut lines, a white stubble of beard, eyes that had the unsettling steadiness of a man who had stopped being afraid of anything some years ago, including death, including strangers. A pack hung off one shoulder, small and final-looking, the pack of a man traveling light because he wasn't planning to arrive anywhere.

"I'm on the *Maren Hale*, yes," she said. Neutral. Ready to deny everything else.

"Heard you pulled old paper out of the landing today." He came forward a step, unhurried, staff tapping stone. "Survey linen. Institute linen."

Word traveled fast in border towns. Word was the fastest thing in them. "If you're looking to buy—"

"I'm not looking to buy." He stopped an arm's length away, and up close she could see how ill he was—not just old, but hollowed, the way men got when something was eating them from inside, slow and patient. His eyes went to her hands, hanging loose at her sides, and stayed there. "Calluses here," he said, not touching her, tracing the air above her knuckles. "Rope and rock both. And the scar across the right palm—pry bar slipped, cold water, you didn't feel it till you came up. Am I close?"

"You're close to wasting my evening."

"Ede Voss," he said, as if she'd asked. "Prospector. Or I was, before the canyon finished with me." And then, softly, with terrible gentleness, the way you'd say a name at a graveside: "You dig like a Brightwater. I've watched enough of them to know."

The river went on sliding under the span. Somewhere behind her a door banged; laughter spilled and faded. Dominic stood very still and felt the ten carefully built years of her life develop a long, hairline crack, running from her hands all the way up.

"My name's Corren," she said. "Della Corren. And you've got me confused with a dead woman's family."

Ede Voss smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man holding a map he'd drawn himself, of a promise he intended to collect, and of time—very little of it, and none of it hers.

"No," he said. "I haven't."
