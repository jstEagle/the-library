## 2. A Map for a Promise

The rain came in off the flats an hour after sundown, and with it the smell of the river—mud and iron and something older underneath, like a coin left too long in a pocket. Dominic Brightwater heard it change before she saw it: the drumming on the boarding house roof shifting from scattered to steady, the gutters waking up, the whole town of Callow's Bend settling into the wet like a dog circling twice before lying down.

She was packing.

Not obviously. She'd learned long ago that obvious packing got noticed, and noticed things got remembered, and remembered things traveled downriver faster than barges. So she worked by lamplight with the door hooked, folding her spare shirt into the bottom of her kit as if she were merely tidying, rolling her tools in oilcloth one at a time—the short pry bar, the hand auger with the cracked handle she'd never replaced because the crack told her exactly how much torque the wood could take before it lied to her. Ten years of leaving towns had made an art of it. You never packed in order of importance. You packed in order of innocence.

The winch chain went in last, coiled at the top where her hand could find it in the dark. A salvage diver's superstition, and she had others, but that was the one she kept closest. Her father had carried the same kind of chain, she'd been told. She didn't remember him well enough to know if it was true. She remembered his hands, though—or thought she did, which amounted to the same thing after twenty years.

Outside, the street had gone quiet in the way river towns went quiet when the weather turned honest. Somewhere below, Mrs. Ansel's kitchen still clattered; the smell of onions and river fish came up through the floorboards along with the sound of the landlady arguing with her stove. Dominic counted what she owed—three nights' lodging, two meals, a patch of tar for her boot—and set the coins under the chipped blue cup on the washstand, weighted with a washer so they wouldn't look like a bribe or a blessing. Just payment. Payment left no thread for anyone to pull.

Tomorrow she'd be on the early barge down to Marrow's Landing, then overland, then gone. Another name crossed off another map. In three weeks nobody in Callow's Bend would remember more than that there'd been a woman who could hold her breath four minutes and didn't talk much, and in six weeks not even that.

She was reaching for the lamp to trim the wick when someone knocked.

Three knocks. Not urgent, not timid. The knock of a man who had decided how many times he intended to knock and would not be moved from the number.

Dominic went still, hand flat against the wall, listening the way she listened underwater—with her whole skin. Footsteps on the stairs had stopped outside her door, and whoever stood there was breathing in the slow, deliberate rhythm of someone managing pain. She knew that rhythm. She'd heard it from men crushed under capsized hulls and women half-drowned in collapsed shafts. It was the breathing of a body that had made its peace with a budget of strength and was spending it carefully.

"Miss Brightwater." The voice was dry as old rope. "I know you're awake. Your lamp's still burning, and packers don't pack in the dark."

Her blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the rain.

*Packers don't pack in the dark.* Which meant he'd been watching long enough to see her working, and patient enough to wait until she finished, and certain enough of what he knew to say *Brightwater* out loud in a hallway where any boarder's door might open.

"I think you've got the wrong room," she said.

"I think," said the voice, "that you've got the wrong idea about how long a dying man will wait."

* * *

She opened the door because the alternative was letting him say more through it, and doors were thin, and thin things carried.

He was smaller than his voice. That was her first impression—a man shrunk into himself the way driftwood shrinks, dried out and hardened around what remained. Ede Voss. She recognized him from the ferry landing, from the market square, from the corner table at the Salt & Cinder where he sat every evening with one glass of cider he never finished: the old prospector everyone in Callow's Bend had written off years ago, the one they called Half-Mad Ede when they called him anything at all. Grey stubble, a coat that had been good thirty years ago, and eyes that were doing all the work his body no longer could—quick, wet-bright, moving over her face like a man reading water for the place to cross.

"You're not supposed to know that name," she said quietly.

"No," he agreed. "I'm not supposed to know a lot of things. Come here—" He didn't wait for permission. He pushed past her into the room, moving with a lurching economy, and lowered himself onto the single chair by the window as if the journey across twelve feet of floorboards had cost him something he'd have to account for later. His breath came shallow. His right hand, she noticed, was wrapped in a rag stained brown at the knuckles, and the fingers above the wrapping trembled with a fine, constant vibration that wasn't fear. She'd seen that tremor before too. It was what certain kinds of sickness did to the hands near the end.

"Shut the door," he said. "Hook it."

She should have put him out. Every instinct she'd polished over ten years said *put him out, walk away, be on the barge tomorrow and let Callow's Bend keep its madman.* Instead she shut the door and dropped the hook, and stood with her back against it, arms folded, and hated herself a little for how easily the room had become his.

"How?" she asked.

Ede Voss smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had found something at the bottom of a hole and was deciding whether to show you.

"Your hands," he said.

She looked at them without meaning to. They hung at her sides, blunt-knuckled, scarred across the backs in pale seams—the rope burn she'd gotten off the *Kestrel's* capstan, the divot in her left thumb where a chisel had skipped, the fine white lattice across both palms that came from years of hauling on wet line. Working hands. Diving hands. Digging hands.

"There are a hundred women on this river with hands like mine," she said.

"No," said Ede. "There aren't. There are a hundred women with laborer's hands, and I'd know the difference, because I've spent forty years learning to read hands the way you people read charts. Laborers grip. Diggers *set*. Look at your own calluses sometime, girl—they're not where a rope puts them, they're where a pry bar puts them. And this—" he lifted his wrapped hand and tapped the air toward her right palm, "—that scar across the heel of your hand, running diagonal? That's a spool-line cut. You were spooling line into a flooded cut, alone, holding tension with your teeth because you needed both hands. Nobody does that for salvage pay. You do it because the work matters more than the wage. I've only known two other people who held line like that."

The rain filled the silence. Downstairs, Mrs. Ansel's stove banged and went quiet.

"And one of them," Dominic said, and her voice came out flatter than she intended, "was my mother."

"And the other," said Ede Voss, "was your father. Yes."

Something moved through her chest—grief's old muscle memory, the ache that lived in her like a splinter worked too deep to draw. She kept her face still. Ten years of practice.

"My parents died in a rockfall at a survey site eleven years ago," she said. "It was in the Institute's report. Everyone who needs to know it knows it."

"The Institute's report," Ede repeated, tasting the words like a man testing whether milk had turned. He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the planes of his face, hollowed and yellow, and for a moment she saw not the harmless madman of the Salt & Cinder but something older and harder—a man built out of the same country her parents had been built out of, all stone and current. "Sit down, Miss Brightwater."

"I'll stand."

"Dying men don't have time for people who stand." He said it without self-pity, almost without emphasis, the way you'd note the depth of a hole. "But I'll talk standing if you do. I'll talk kneeling. What I won't do is leave this room without what I came for, because I have spent nine years and every coin I ever saved getting to the point where I could make this offer, and I am not going to run out of hours one door short."

"Offer of what?"

Ede reached inside his coat.

Every story she'd ever heard about her parents' deaths stood up in her at once—the rockfall, the flooded survey tunnel, the Meridian Institute's tidy report, the funeral she hadn't been allowed to attend because she was sixteen and the Institute's lawyers had opinions about minors and liability. She watched his hand move inside the coat and thought, absurdly, *he's going to draw a weapon on me,* and found that part of her wouldn't even mind, because a weapon was simple, and simple things could be answered.

What he drew out was a leather tube, cracked with age, tied with waxed cord.

He untied the cord with his trembling fingers, and it took him three tries, and she found herself leaning away from the wall to help him before she caught herself and stopped. He noticed. Of course he noticed. He noticed everything.

Inside the tube was a map.

He unrolled it across her narrow bed, weighting the corners with her oilcloth rolls, and Dominic forgot, for a long moment, everything she had planned to say.

It was drawn in ink and iron-gall and something reddish she didn't want to name, on paper that had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. It showed a canyon. Not just the shape of it—the *behavior* of it. The river ran in along the upper reach and simply ceased, marked with a symbol she knew from her mother's old field notes: a broken line, doubled, meaning water passing beneath stone. Below that, the canyon continued drawn in cross-section, layer under layer, like the anatomy of some enormous sleeping animal. Chambers. Galleries. A stair-step of descending works, each marked in a cramped, precise hand.

And at the center, circled twice, two words in that same hand:

*BROKEN RIVERGATE.*

Dominic's mouth had gone dry. "Where did you get this?"

"Copied it," said Ede. "From the original."

"Whose original?"

He looked up at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him answer honestly, which she suspected was rare for him and cost him something.

"Your mother's."

* * *

She made herself sit down then. Not because her legs gave out—she told herself it was strategy, putting herself lower, closer to the map—but the chair took her weight whether she chose it or not.

"That's impossible," she said. "My mother's field materials were seized. All of them. After the accident. The Institute—"

"—collected every page, every chart, every notebook, yes. I know. I tried to buy copies through three different brokers in the years after. The Institute doesn't sell what it's ashamed of." Ede lowered himself back into the window chair, slower this time, and she saw the effort cost him visibly now, the performance of vigor cracking along its seams. "But paper isn't the only thing that remembers. Your mother copied that map twice. Once for the Institute's files, once for herself. The second copy she hid—not in the dig site, not in her quarters. She gave it to a man she trusted, the summer before the end, and told him to keep it until someone came asking with the right hands."

Dominic stared at him. "You're saying my mother gave you her map."

"I'm saying I was the last person alive that Mara Brightwater trusted, and I have spent eleven years being unworthy of it." He said it plainly, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "I was a surveyor's assistant on the Rivergate concession before your parents ever came out. I drew the first bad maps they corrected. When the Institute pulled funding and sealed the site, I was the one who helped carry the crates out. And afterward, when everyone else signed the settlement and went quiet, I couldn't. I went back. Nine times over nine years. Looking for what really happened in that canyon."

"And?"

"And I found enough to break me, girl, and not enough to prove anything, and the difference between those two things is where men like me go to die." He unfolded his wrapped hand, and she saw now that beneath the rag the fingers were mottled dark, the nails gone grey. Sickness of the blood, or of the bone. The kind you got from years in cold wet stone. "The river rises through that canyon every spring. Nine days from now, maybe ten, the melt comes down from the high country and the lower galleries flood to the ceiling. Whatever's down there that your mother wanted found—or wanted *never* found—it drowns a little deeper every year. I was going to go back this season anyway. My doctor says I have a month. My body says less."

"Ede—"

"Look at the margins." He tapped the map with one ruined finger. "Go on. Look."

She looked. Along the left margin, in that same cramped precise hand, ran columns of figures—depths, flow rates, dates. And beside several of them, in different ink, newer, shakier, annotations. Corrections. Confirmations. *Verified, year nine.* *Matches, third descent.* Little notes in a dying man's writing attesting that the dead woman's numbers had proven true, chamber after chamber, gate after gate.

"She was right," Ede said softly. "About all of it. About the aquifer, about the gates, about why the Institute really pulled the crews out. And nobody living knows it except me, and I'm—" he raised his mottled hand and let it fall— "this."

Dominic stood up and walked to the window. It was a coward's move, turning her back, but she needed her face somewhere he couldn't read it, because it was doing things she hadn't authorized. The rain ran down the glass in beads. Below, Callow's Bend lay dark and dripping, a town she'd already left in her mind, and she gripped the sill and felt the whole careful architecture of her exit creaking.

"What is it you want from me," she said. Not a question. She made it not a question.

"I want you to finish it." No hesitation. He'd rehearsed this; she could hear the rehearsal worn smooth. "I want you to take that map down the Broken Rivergate and find what your mother found, and learn the truth of how she died, and decide—with your own eyes and your own hands—what the world gets to know about it. I'm past deciding anything. I'm past proving anything. But you—" his voice roughened— "you're her hands. I watched you load that winch chain at the dock yesterday and I knew you before I knew your name. You coil line the way she coiled line. Left over right, thumb trapping the tail. Nobody does that anymore. Nobody *ever* did that, except the two of them."

"I'm not my mother."

"No," Ede agreed. "You're not. She stayed. You run. That's the difference, and it may be the thing that saves you, and I've thought about that a great deal, believe me." He rose, unsteadily, and came to stand beside her at the window, and up close he smelled of camphor and cold stone. "Here is my offer, Miss Brightwater, and it is the only one I have. The map is yours. Free. Tonight. No price, no conditions written anywhere. All I ask—all I *ask*—is your word that you'll finish what he started, and what she finished, and what I ran out of life to do. Go down the Rivergate. See it with your own eyes. Then choose what to tell the world. That's all. Choose. But choose knowing, instead of choosing blind, the way you've been choosing for ten years."

"I don't make promises," Dominic said.

"I know," said Ede Voss. "That's why I'm asking for this one."

* * *

They stood there a long time while the rain worked at the roof.

She thought about the barge. She thought about Marrow's Landing, and the coast beyond it, and the clean anonymous years she'd built like a wall, brick by brick, town by town. She thought about the Meridian Institute's tidy report and the lawyers and the sealed file, and about how she had spent half her life making sure no one ever looked at her hands too closely.

And she thought—unbidden, unwelcome—about her mother's hands. Not the memory of them, which had faded to a smear of warmth and rope-burn, but the idea of them. Hands that coiled line left over right. Hands that had drawn this map. Hands that had done something, in the dark under the Broken Rivergate, that the most powerful institution on the river had spent eleven years burying.

"You said nine days," she said finally. "Until the melt floods the galleries."

"Nine. Maybe eight. The high snows went early this year."

"And Halvard's people are already sniffing around this town. I've seen the survey marks." She'd seen them that morning, fresh chalk arrows on the ferry pilings, and had told herself it meant nothing. It had never meant nothing. "If the Institute hears anyone's heading for the Rivergate—"

"Then they'll send people," Ede said simply. "They always do. That's not a reason, girl. That's weather. You don't stay ashore because of weather. You pick your boat."

Dominic turned from the window. On the bed, the map lay glowing faintly in the lamplight, her mother's handwriting running down the margin like water down glass, and she understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that she had lost the argument before it began—that she had perhaps lost it eleven years ago, in a lawyer's office, at sixteen, when they'd taken her parents' papers and given her a settlement check she'd never cashed.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "it's on my terms. My crew, my route, my timing. And if I get down there and find nothing but rock and water, I come back up and we're done. No second expedition. No crusade."

"Done," Ede agreed instantly, too instantly, and she understood that he believed—as she was beginning to believe—that there was no version of the Broken Rivergate that ended at *nothing.*

"And the promise," she said. "You understand what you're asking. You're asking me to stop running. You're asking me to walk straight at the one thing I've spent ten years walking around."

Ede Voss smiled again, and this time it was almost gentle, and almost broke her heart, because for a moment behind the ruin of his face she saw what he must have looked like at thirty, wading a survey trench with mud to his knees, laughing at the rain.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly that. It's the only inheritance worth having, Miss Brightwater. I'd have thought a woman with your hands would know."

She held out her hand. He took it—his mottled fingers closing over her scarred palm with surprising strength—and she felt the tremor pass between them like a current, and heard herself say the words before she could weigh them:

"I'll finish it. I swear it. By the river and the rope, I'll finish what you started."

The old man exhaled, long and shaking, as if he'd been holding that breath for nine years. Then he released her hand, rolled the map with care, and pressed the tube into her grip, folding her fingers closed over it with both of his ruined ones.

"Then you'll need a crew," he said, shuffling toward the door, "and a ferryman who knows the gorge, and powder, and nerve. And you'll want to move quick, because—" he paused at the threshold, hand on the hook, and glanced back at her with those quick bright eyes— "there was a man asking after you at the Salt & Cinder tonight. Polite fellow. Institute pin on his lapel. Asking the landlady whether a Brightwater was lodging here."

The rain hammered down. Dominic stood in the middle of her half-packed life with her mother's map in her fist, and felt the last ten years close quietly behind her like a door.

"He what?" she said.

Ede Voss smiled, hooked the door open, and stepped out into the dark.

"Welcome home, girl," he said. "Try not to let them catch you."
