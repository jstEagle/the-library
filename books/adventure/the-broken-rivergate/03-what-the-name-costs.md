## 3. What the Name Costs

The trouble with small towns was that they remembered.

Dominic had known this for eleven years—had built her whole life around the knowledge—and still she'd made the mistake of staying in Callow's Bend four days past the salvage job. Four days was enough. Four days was a lifetime, in a town where the ferryman knew every face on her crossing and the boarding house cook salted your eggs the way you liked them by the second morning and asked about your mother by the third.

She'd given them nothing to work with. That was the discipline of it, the craft she'd honed across a decade of border towns: no surname on the ledger, just *D. Bright*—a name thin as tissue paper but it held if nobody pulled at it. No stories. No drinks bought for strangers. She kept her hands gloved around town because digging hands were a signature all their own, callused in patterns that surveyors and Institute men could read like print. She paid cash. She left before anyone got curious.

But Ede Voss had died on Tuesday, and dying men talked, and Ede Voss had talked to half the town before the cough took him down for good.

She heard it first at Maret's dry goods, standing at the counter with a coil of rope and a tin of lamp oil she didn't need, listening to two deckhands argue over freight rates while the shopkeeper wrapped her purchase.

"—the old Voss place, they're saying he had papers. Maps. Something worth money upriver."

"Voss never had two coins to rub together."

"He had something better than coins, apparently. Some kind of claim. And get this—" the younger one dropped his voice, which meant everyone in the shop was meant to hear it, "—he didn't leave it to family. He left it to a woman. A salvager staying at the Wren's Rest. They say she's a Brightwater."

The word went through the shop like cold water through a hull. The deckhands stopped arguing. The shopkeeper's hands paused on the wrapping paper. Somewhere near the door, a woman Dominic didn't know turned fully around to look at her, and Dominic kept her eyes on the counter and thought, with a strange detached clarity: *there it is.*

Not *a Brightwater.* Not even the name itself, spoken plain. Just the shape of it, the way people said it—the hush under the words, the tilt of attention. Eleven years since the Rivergate disaster made the broadsheets, and the name still worked on rooms like that. *Brightwater,* people would say, the way you'd say *landslide* or *plague ship.* The expedition that went in with twelve and came out with three. The famous engineer Marta Brightwater, dead in the flooded dark along with her husband and nine others, and the daughter who'd been fourteen and three hundred miles away when the news came, who'd spent every year since being not-her.

"Miss? Miss, that'll be—"

She put the money down without counting it and walked out into the street with her rope and her oil and the whole town's attention crawling up between her shoulder blades like a hand.

* * *

By evening, Callow's Bend had done what small towns do with a secret, which is spend it everywhere at once. She felt it at the Wren's Rest when she came down for supper: the landlady's smile a half-beat too long, the way conversation dipped and resumed an octave higher when she crossed the common room. A card player at the corner table watched her over his fan of cards with open, cheerful curiosity, the look of a man assembling a story to tell his wife. Someone had already decided she must be rich. Someone else had decided she must be cursed. By morning there would be versions of her in a dozen kitchens, and none of them would be a woman who hauled chain for wages and wanted nothing from anybody.

She ate fast, went up, and started packing.

It was the old reflex, and she let it run because fighting it took more energy than obeying it. Boots by the door. Kit rolled. The map—Ede's map, the thing she'd promised a dying man she'd finish—wrapped in oilcloth and pushed deep into the bottom of her pack, because whatever else happened, that wasn't leaving behind. She'd told herself on Tuesday, standing over Ede's bed while his breath rattled like water in a failing pipe, that she'd think about it. That a promise made to a man that far gone was a coin you could decide later whether to spend.

But she hadn't taken the map back to him. She could have. He'd been past reading it. And she hadn't, and now it sat in her pack like a splinter working its way toward the heart, and she hated how much of her wanted to keep walking and how little of her believed she would.

A knock at the door. Two raps, precise, unhurried.

Dominic went still. The Wren's Rest landlady knocked like she was apologizing. Deckhands knocked like they were breaking something. This knock belonged to someone who expected the door to be opened and was simply confirming it existed.

She considered the window. Second floor, easy drop onto the lean-to roof, out through the alley—but the alley ran past the common room windows, and besides, running now would confirm everything. She'd learned that lesson too, somewhere along the years: flight was a confession. Better to stand in the room and be nobody until they lost interest.

She opened the door.

The man in the hall was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, dressed in the particular way that money dressed when it wanted to pass as practical: good boots broken in just enough, a field coat that had never seen real weather, a leather document case worn smooth at the corners from handling. He had a surveyor's build—lean, spare, the posture of a man accustomed to standing on ridgelines looking at things. His face was pleasant the way a well-drafted contract was pleasant. Nothing in it you could object to. Nothing in it you could trust.

"Dominic Brightwater," he said. Not a question.

"Dominic Bright," she said.

"Yes." He almost smiled. "May I come in? I'll only take a few minutes of your evening. My name is Halvard. Corren Halvard. I'm with the Meridian Institute."

There it was. She'd known it would come eventually—had felt it coming for days like weather in her knees—and still hearing it aloud landed differently than anticipating it. The Meridian Institute. Her parents' last funding body. The organization that had paid for the Broken Rivergate dig, published the disaster report, settled quietly with the families, and folded its maps away like a man closing a ledger on a bad debt.

"The Institute's a long way from its offices," she said.

"The Institute goes where its interests are." He glanced past her into the room—at the packed kit, the boots by the door—with the mild thoroughness of a man cataloguing. "You were leaving, I see. Good. Then this is well timed. May I?"

He stepped in before she answered. Of course he did. She closed the door behind him anyway, because letting him stand in the hallway felt worse than having him in the room.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said. "You know my name. I don't know why yours matters."

"It shouldn't. I'm nobody—a surveyor, a functionary. I draw lines on maps and write reports other people sign." He set his document case on the table, unhurried, proprietary, a man establishing that the room's furniture was now shared. "But the work I represent is considerable, and it concerns a place you may know something about. The Broken Rivergate."

"I've heard of it."

"Everyone has. It killed twelve people." He said it flatly, without ceremony, and somehow that was worse than any performance of sympathy. "Or rather—it killed nine. Three survived. Your mother was not among the survivors, but your mother, Ms. Brightwater, was among those who *went in.* Chief engineer of the expedition. The finest hydrological mind the Institute ever employed, though we were slow to say so publicly, and slower to say so after." He opened the case and drew out a folder, but didn't open it. Just rested his hand on it. "I'm here because the Institute is reopening its interest in the Rivergate concession. And because certain items have surfaced in this town that suggest the site is not as closed as our records indicate."

"Certain items," Dominic repeated.

"A map. Hand-drawn. In the possession of one Ede Voss, prospector, deceased as of four days ago." Halvard's eyes came up and settled on her, and for the first time the pleasantness in them sharpened into something with edges. "Witnesses say he gave it to a woman. A salvager. A woman with digging hands and no surname on the boarding house ledger, who flinched—my informant was very specific about this—when the name Brightwater was mentioned in Maret's store this afternoon."

Dominic said nothing. Her heartbeat was slow and hard, a fist knocking at the base of her throat. *Flinched.* She hadn't flinched. Had she? She'd kept her eyes down, kept her breathing level, done everything right—and some dockside gossip had read her anyway, or invented the flinch afterward because a story needed a detail.

"You're mistaken," she said. "About the map, about the woman, about me. I haul chain off sunken barges. I don't know anything about the Rivergate except what every schoolchild knows."

"Every schoolchild knows the names of the dead," Halvard agreed. "Fewer know the engineering. Fewer still could read a flood-control schematic, or identify a pressure weir by its stonework, or—" he tapped the folder once, lightly, "—recognize their own mother's handwriting on a forty-year-old survey annotation."

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. Dominic became aware of the window behind her, the drop, the alley. Aware of the knife in her boot and the absurd, animal calculus of whether it would matter. It wouldn't. Men like Halvard didn't travel alone; men like Halvard were the polite part of something with less polite parts waiting outside towns like this one.

"What exactly do you want?" she asked.

"To make you an offer. But first, honesty—I find honesty buys cooperation faster than threats, and costs about the same." He finally opened the folder and turned it so she could see: a reproduction of an Institute survey plat, the Broken Rivergate canyon rendered in faded ink, contour lines crowding the gorge like fingerprints. Stamped across the lower third, in red: *CONCESSION SUSPENDED — PENDING REVIEW.* The review stamp was dated thirty-nine years ago. There was no newer date anywhere on the sheet.

"When the original expedition was lost," Halvard said, "the Institute suspended the concession. Publicly, we called the site unstable. Privately—we called it expensive. The surveys your mother produced in the months before the disaster described aquifer structures of extraordinary scale and complexity. Water-bearing strata under artesian pressure, mapped across six miles of canyon. The Institute's board looked at those numbers and saw revenue. Your mother, evidently, looked at them and saw something else. What she saw, we never learned. The final field reports were never recovered. Neither were her personal journals. The three survivors signed settlements and said very little, and the river did the rest."

"And now?"

"Now the board has changed, and the arithmetic has changed, and the region needs water the way it always does. The Institute intends to resume development of the Rivergate concession. Survey, assessment, extraction where viable." He closed the folder. "We expect obstacles. Sentiment. Local attachment. Possibly physical hazards—the site has been unmanaged for four decades. What we do not expect, and will not tolerate, is private parties interfering with Institute property based on documents obtained irregularly from a deceased claimant."

"Ede Voss's claim predates your suspension," Dominic said, and then could have bitten her tongue off, because Halvard's expression didn't change at all except to deepen by one degree, the way water deepens over a ledge.

"There it is," he said softly. "You do know about the map."

She'd handed him the thread and watched him take it, and there was nothing to do now but hold still and see how much line he'd take. "I know a dying man pressed papers on me and I didn't have the heart to refuse him. If there's a map in it, I haven't looked. I'm not a cartographer. I'm not an engineer. I'm not my mother."

"No," Halvard said. "You're not. Your mother believed in things. It's what killed her." He rose, smoothing his coat, and retrieved his hat from the chair where he'd set it. "Here is the offer, and I'll leave it with you regardless of your answer tonight. The Institute will pay for the map—generously. We will settle any debts attached to your name, and there are a few, Ms. Brightwater; Callow's Bend is a small town but creditors talk across borders. We will provide passage anywhere you care to go and a letter of introduction that will open doors your surname has spent eleven years closing. In exchange: the document, your silence, and your absence. You walk away richer than you arrived and no one ever connects the name Brightwater to the Rivergate again. You can be Dominic Bright for the rest of your life. Permanently. Officially."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the Institute acquires the map through other channels, and you become a person of interest in a jurisdictional dispute, which is a tedious thing to be. And—" he paused at the door, hat in hand, and gave her the first genuinely honest look he'd offered all evening, and it was the honest ones you had to watch for, "—you should know the seasonal melt is early this year. The upper snowfields are giving up their winter ahead of schedule. Our hydrologists give the canyon nine days, perhaps ten, before the river comes up through the gates in earnest. After that, nothing human goes in or out of the Broken Rivergate until autumn. Whatever Ede Voss wanted finished, the calendar is finishing it for us. Nine days, Ms. Brightwater. I'd think about what's worth nine days of your life."

He set a card on the table—cream stock, the Institute's compass-rose seal embossed in silver—and let himself out. His footsteps went down the stairs evenly, without hurry, a man whose evening was proceeding on schedule.

* * *

She didn't sleep.

She sat on the bed with the oilcloth bundle in her lap and unwrapped it for the hundredth time, and the map lay open under the lamp the way it always did: Ede Voss's careful, trembling linework tracing the canyon from the mouth at the old ferry landing up through the narrows to the place where the river dove under stone. And there, in the margins, annotations that were not Ede's. Smaller. Denser. A draftsman's hand with an engineer's economy, letters slanted forward like a woman leaning into wind.

*M.B.,* Ede had told her, the last time he'd been strong enough to talk. *Found her marks all through the upper works, girl. Forty years old and fresh as paint. She wanted whoever came next to understand. I tried. God knows I tried. I got as far as the second gate and what I saw there—* and then the coughing had taken him, and he'd gripped her wrist with a strength that embarrassed his body and said, *finish it, finish what she started, promise me,* and she had. She'd promised. Because he was dying and because some buried thing in her had risen up and answered before her caution could catch it.

Now she traced the margin notes with one finger, not touching the ink. *Her handwriting.* Halvard had known. Had known before he ever knocked, had walked up those stairs carrying her mother's name like a key, and used it on her, gently, precisely, the way you'd use a blade you respected. *Recognize their own mother's handwriting.* Which meant the Institute had copies of Marta Brightwater's correspondence. Which meant they'd been holding pieces of this for forty years, sitting on a suspended concession, waiting for the board's arithmetic to change.

Nine days.

She looked at the window. Below, Callow's Bend lay quiet, lamplit, harmless-seeming. Somewhere out there Halvard was writing his report: *subject located, subject contacted, offer extended, resistance anticipated.* Somewhere out there were the less polite parts of the machine he represented, and they would arrive on the morning boat or the night coach, and they would not knock twice and wait.

She could take the offer. The thought was honest and it deserved its hearing: sell the map, take the money, become Dominic Bright forever, clean, free, anonymous as rain. Eleven years of running rewarded at last with permission to stop running—on Halvard's terms, in a direction of his choosing, with her father's death and her mother's sacrifice filed permanently under *concession suspended, pending review.*

*Pending review.* Thirty-nine years pending.

She rolled the map, wrapped it, and pushed it deep into her pack. Then she took out a fresh page in her notebook and wrote, in the shorthand she used for cargo manifests, three names she'd been avoiding thinking about: the ferrywoman who'd watched her load chain with a professional eye and a grin; the demolition man who drank at the Ferry Slip and blew stumps for farmers at fair rates; the archivist at the county records office who'd helped her trace a bill of lading and gone pale and reverent when she'd mentioned, stupidly, carelessly, the Rivergate.

She didn't know yet if she was gathering a crew or just making a list. She didn't know yet if the promise was a debt or a trap. She knew the melt was early, the calendar was short, and the Institute had just shown her exactly how much her name was worth to people who wanted it buried.

Below, in the street, a coach rolled in late off the mountain road—too late for passengers, too heavy for mail. She stood at the window and watched it halt in front of the Ferry Slip, and watched figures climb down, six of them, moving with the unloaded ease of men who handled equipment for a living. One of them looked up at the Wren's Rest windows as if checking an address.

Halvard had said *nine days.*

Apparently the Institute had decided not to wait for her answer.
