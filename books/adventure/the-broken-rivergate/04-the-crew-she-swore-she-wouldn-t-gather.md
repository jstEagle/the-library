## 4. The Crew She Swore She Wouldn't Gather

Dominic told herself she'd work alone.

She said it out loud, in fact, standing at the rail of Ilsa Rook's ferry as the flat-bottomed boat groaned across the brown water of the Bend, and Ilsa laughed at her—short, unkind, entirely without apology.

"Alone." Ilsa spat a sunflower seed husk over the side. "Into the Rivergate. Alone."

"I've done worse alone."

"Sure you have. And how many fingers you got left?" She held up her own hand, flexed it. "I ferry salvage crews, treasure idiots, and Institute surveyors across this river six days a week. You know what they all got in common? They come back short something. Fingers. Nerves. Years. The ones who go in alone don't come back at all—they just stop booking crossings."

Dominic watched the far bank slide closer. Three days since Ede Voss had pressed that oilcloth-wrapped map into her hands with fingers like winter roots, three days since she'd promised a dying man she'd finish what he started, and every hour since she had been looking for the door out of it. There wasn't one. A promise made to a man on his deathbed was a debt with no bankruptcy. She'd checked the map twice more by candlelight, and both times the same thing had stopped her cold: the fine annotations threading through Ede's careful survey lines weren't his. The handwriting was smaller, surer, slanted forward like it was always hurrying somewhere. She knew it from the two letters her mother had ever sent her, from the title page of *Hydrology of the Northern Reaches*, from a birthday card when she turned nine that her father had helped her mother write because Mara Brightwater could map an aquifer blindfolded but couldn't compose a sentence for a child without drafting it three times.

Her mother's hand, on a map drawn by a prospector who hadn't arrived in Callow's Bend until four years after the expedition vanished.

That was the hook under her skin now. Not grief—grief was old, worn smooth as river stone. This was newer. Sharper. *How.*

"You're not listening," Ilsa said.

"I'm listening."

"Then say it back."

Dominic exhaled through her nose. "I need a crew."

"You need a crew," Ilsa agreed, "and you need it by tomorrow night, because Halvard's already rented the whole east wing of the Tollhouse and bought up every lamp-oil canister in Ovid's shop, and a man doesn't do that unless he means to go into the dark before you do." She swung the pole, caught the current, and let the ferry drift the last thirty feet to the landing with the lazy precision of someone who had been reading this river since before she could read anything else. "So. You want my boat or not?"

"I want your boat."

"And me with it. I don't rent her out. Ask anyone."

"That's extortion."

"That's business." Ilsa grinned, and there was something underneath the grin Dominic hadn't expected—a hunger, banked but real, the look of a woman who had spent twenty years carrying other people's expeditions to the edge of the canyon and watching them walk in without her. "You're going downriver past the Weir. Nobody goes past the Weir. I've wanted to see what's past the Weir since I was nine years old and my father told me the river runs under stone there and never comes all the way back up." She stuck out a hand, callused palm, rope-burn scars across the heel of it. "Half share. Paid on return. And if we find anything worth finding, first pick of whatever fits in my pockets."

Dominic shook it. It felt like signing something.

* * *

Tam Ovid's shop smelled of black powder, lamp oil, and the particular sourness of a man who had been drinking since noon and intended to continue. He was a squat, wide-handed man with a beard singed short on the left side—an old accident worn like a badge—and when Dominic laid Ede's map on his counter between a crate of fuses and a jar of pickled eggs, he looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"Where'd you get this?" he asked finally.

"Ede Voss."

"Ede Voss is dead."

"Dying when he gave it to me. Close enough."

Tam picked up the map then, held it to the lamplight, and traced the channel markings with one blunt finger. "Ede came in here four years ago wanting blasting caps. Wanted enough to open a mountain. I sold him twelve and told him to come back when he'd learned some patience." His finger stopped on a notation near the inner gorge. "He never did learn any. What's down there, Brightwater?"

The name landed like a dropped tool. She should have expected it—Halvard had seen to it that the whole town knew—but hearing it in Tam's mouth, casual and weighted at once, still tightened something behind her ribs.

"A gate," she said. "Old flood works, maybe older. Something the Institute wants and I intend to reach first."

"The Institute." Tam set the map down. "Man named Halvard came by yesterday. Polite fellow. Bought forty pounds of powder and didn't blink at the price, which tells you either he's rich or he's spending somebody else's money, and with Institute men it's always somebody else's." He leaned on the counter. "He offered me triple rates to guide his crew to the upper canyon trailheads. I told him I don't know the trailheads. That part was true. The rest—" he shrugged, "—I figured I'd see which of you paid better."

"And?"

"And he pays in coin. What are you paying in?"

"A chance to blow a hole in something that's been sealed for thirty years."

Tam was quiet a moment. Then he laughed—not the mean laugh Ilsa had given her, but a real one, rusty, like a hinge remembering its purpose. "Thirty years. You know what I did before I ran this shop? Quarry work, up north. Ten years setting charges in stone that fought back. You know what I miss about it?" He didn't wait for her answer. "Nothing. I miss nothing. But I dream about it. Every damn week. Stone going where you tell it to go, for once in your life something obeying you." He pulled a key from under the counter and unlocked the cabinet behind him, revealing rows of caps, spools of det cord, bricks of powder wrapped in wax paper like loaves. "Half share, paid on return, and I keep whatever tools the old expedition left down there. Deal?"

"How did you know there was an old expedition?"

"Brightwater." He nodded at her hands—the calluses, the old scar across two knuckles where a pry bar had slipped. "Ede had hands like yours. Diggers' hands. And diggers' hands don't buy maps of places nobody's heard of unless somebody famous died there." He began stacking supplies without waiting for her confirmation. "Your mother, right? The hydrologist. Juno Pell's got a whole shelf about her, if you can stomach it."

* * *

Juno Pell worked out of a converted granary behind the meeting hall, and she answered the door with ink on her chin and a look of such immediate, naked recognition that Dominic nearly turned around on the step.

"You're her," Juno said. "You're the daughter."

"I'm a salvager looking to hire—"

"You're Mara Brightwater's daughter." Juno stepped back to let her in, and Dominic went, because refusing would have required explaining why, and she didn't have the words yet. The granary's interior was floor-to-ceiling shelving, crates, document tubes, and maps hung from wires strung across the rafters like laundry. In the corner nearest the window, one shelf stood apart from the others—cleaner, dusted, arranged with a care that bordered on devotion. Spines she recognized. Her mother's monographs. The Institute's field reports from the northern surveys, 309 through 312. A framed photograph she hadn't seen since she was eleven: her parents at the Rivergate trailhead, her father laughing, her mother squinting into the sun with a surveyor's transit at her shoulder.

Dominic looked away from it.

"I need an archivist," she said, too fast. "Someone who can read old survey notation, watermarks, structural markers. Someone local who knows the region's records. It's a two-week contract, possibly less. Hazard pay. You'd be underground most of the time."

"Underground." Juno's eyes were bright and terrible. "In the Rivergate."

"In the canyon system. Yes."

"Where your parents—"

"*Yes.*"

The word came out harder than she meant it, and Juno flinched, and Dominic hated herself for it, and hated more that she couldn't take it back. Silence filled the granary. Somewhere outside, cart wheels ground over gravel.

"I'm sorry," Dominic said, and it cost her. "It's been a long week."

"No." Juno wiped her chin, found the ink, wiped again. "No, you're right to be sharp with me. I've been—people come in here asking about her, you know. About the Brightwater expedition. Every few years someone gets the idea there's treasure in the flooded chambers, and they come to me because I'm the one who keeps the file, and I tell them what I know, which is nothing useful, and they leave disappointed, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I've spent fifteen years being the keeper of a story that has no ending. Everyone in this town thinks your mother was reckless. That she took the whole team into a wet canyon in melt season and drowned them. Even the ones who liked her think it. And I've never believed it. Not once. I've read every report. The timelines don't work. The water tables don't work. She was the best hydrologist the Institute ever funded and she did not misread a seasonal gauge." Juno met her eyes. "So yes. I'll come. Not for hazard pay. I'll come because you're the only person alive who might be able to give that story its ending, and I have wanted that ending since I was nineteen years old."

Dominic should have said no. Some part of her, the part that had spent a decade changing her surname on crew manifests, recognized the danger immediately: this woman didn't want an employee. She wanted a relic. She wanted the daughter of the woman on the clean shelf, and no living person could survive being handed to someone as that.

But she also needed someone who could read her mother's markers, and there was exactly one person in Callow's Bend who could.

"Two conditions," Dominic said. "First—you follow my orders underground. All of them. Even the ones that don't make sense."

"Agreed."

"Second." She hesitated. "You don't ask me about her. Not on the river, not in camp, not anywhere. When I'm ready to talk about Mara Brightwater, I'll start the conversation. Until then, she's a name on a map, same as anyone else."

Something moved behind Juno's eyes—disappointment, maybe, or the recalculation of a long-held hope. But she nodded once, firm. "Deal."

They shook hands. Juno's grip was stronger than it looked.

* * *

The Juno interlude:

Juno Pell sat up that night with the file open on her desk and did not touch it for an hour.

Fifteen years she had kept it. Fifteen years of collecting fragments the way other people collected river glass: the Institute's final incident report, redacted so heavily it read like a ransom note; a supply manifest showing the expedition had carried six months of provisions for a planned eight-week survey; a letter from Ede Voss, written drunk, claiming he'd found "the bright lady's markers" in the lower gorge and that they pointed the wrong way, or the right way, depending on what you thought she'd been doing; a single page of Mara Brightwater's field notes purchased from a Callow's Bend trader who swore he'd gotten it off a survivor, though no survivors were ever officially listed.

The page showed a cross-section sketch of the inner gorge. Water levels marked in three colors. And in the margin, in that small, sure, forward-slanting hand, a single line:

*If they won't listen, the river will have to speak for me.*

Juno had built half a life on that sentence. She had constructed theories the way her mother—her actual mother, dead of fever, barely mourned—might have built a bridge: carefully, load-tested, span by span. Mara Brightwater had found something. The Institute had wanted it. Mara had refused. And the flooding—that wasn't accident, wasn't recklessness, wasn't the town's comfortable tragedy of the brilliant woman undone by hubris. It was a choice. Maybe the hardest kind: the kind that costs everything and buys only time.

And now the daughter was sitting in Juno's granary asking to hire an archivist, with her mother's jaw and her father's stubborn shoulders and hands that had never once touched a book, and she had said *don't ask me about her* as if the name were a wound she was still bleeding from.

Juno understood wounds. She had nursed this one, secondhand, for half her life.

But she had also signaled Halvard.

Not yet—not fully, not knowingly, not in words she'd have defended in daylight. Two days ago, before the daughter ever walked into her granary, the polite Institute man had come to the meeting hall asking after regional records, and Juno, star-struck despite herself by the weight of Meridian funding, by the idea that the great institution her hero had served might finally be coming back to finish its work and clear its name, had mentioned—casually, helpfully—that a Brightwater had been seen at Ilsa Rook's ferry. That was all. A sentence. A kindness to a visiting scholar.

She had watched Halvard write it down, and she had seen, for half a second, what lived underneath the politeness, and she had gone home and been sick.

Now she sat with the file open and the guilt rising in her like groundwater, and she told herself the story she needed: that Halvard would have learned it anyway, that the daughter was walking into danger regardless, that information wasn't betrayal, that when they found whatever Mara had found, the Institute's resources would protect them all, and the truth would come out, and the town would have to rewrite its memory of the bright lady, and Juno Pell would be the one who made that happen.

She almost believed it.

She copied the field-note page anyway—carefully, faithfully, every line—and slid the original into the false bottom of her document case, next to her passport and her grandmother's ring. The things you kept when you couldn't trust anyone, including yourself.

* * *

They gathered at the ferry landing an hour before dawn, and it went wrong immediately, which Dominic supposed was fitting.

Tam arrived first, hauling two crates of explosives on a handcart with a cheerfulness that suggested he'd been awake all night either planning or drinking, possibly both. Ilsa came down the path with her collar up against the river damp and took one look at Tam's crates and said, "If any of that touches my deck wrong, I'll feed you to the current myself." Then Juno appeared out of the grey with a rucksack nearly as large as she was and a document case strapped across her chest like armor, and stopped dead at the sight of the other two.

"You're Tam Ovid," she said. "You sold Ede Voss blasting caps."

"You're Juno Pell," Tam said. "You filed a complaint about my powder storage with the town council."

"It was a legitimate safety concern."

"It was *petty bureaucratic—*"

"Enough." Dominic stepped between them, and the word came out with a force that surprised all three of them, herself included. They quieted. The river moved beneath the landing, brown and patient, carrying last night's rain toward the canyon. Downriver, past the bend, past the Weir, the Broken Rivergate waited with whatever her mother had left inside it—and upriver, at the Tollhouse, she could see lamplight burning in the east wing where Halvard's men were surely loading their own boats, their own powder, their own forty pounds of somebody else's money.

Four of them. One ferry. Nine days, Halvard had told the town, until the melt raised the river.

She looked at her crew: a ferrywoman who wanted to see past the Weir, a demolitions man who dreamed of obedient stone, an archivist carrying fifteen years of someone else's story in a case against her heart. Strangers. Liars, probably, each in their own small way—she was certainly one herself. The last crew her parents had gathered had gone into that canyon and never come out, and here she was doing the same arithmetic with different numbers, telling herself it would balance differently this time.

"Rules," Dominic said. "We move at my pace. We turn back at my word, even if we're fifty feet from whatever you came for. Nobody handles the charges but Tam. Nobody reads the markers but Juno. Nobody argues with Ilsa about the boat." She paused. "And nobody asks me why."

"Why what?" Ilsa asked.

"Anything."

Ilsa studied her for a moment with those river-grey eyes, then shrugged and swung aboard. "Fine by me. Everybody's got a locked room. Mine's just bigger than most."

Tam loaded his crates with exaggerated care. Juno boarded last, and as she passed, her hand brushed Dominic's arm—light, accidental, or not—and she said, quietly, so the others couldn't hear:

"For what it's worth, I think she'd be proud you came back."

Dominic didn't answer. She cast off the line herself, and the ferry slid out into the current, and Callow's Bend fell away behind them in the pre-dawn mist—and ahead, faint on the horizon where the land folded down into the gorge, she could see another light moving along the canyon rim. Lanterns. Several. Traveling fast.

Halvard wasn't waiting for the melt.

He was racing them to the gate.
