## 5. Downriver at First Light

The river took the boat the way it took everything—without asking.

Dominic stood at the stern of Ilsa Rook's flat-bottomed ferry, one hand on the tiller, watching Callow's Bend shrink behind them into a smudge of tin roofs and chimney smoke. First light came down the canyon walls in long copper bars, setting the water ablaze where it broke over the shallows. The current here was deceptively gentle. She'd read the map enough times to know what waited downstream: a gorge that swallowed the river whole, stone throats, drowned chambers. A place her parents had gone and not come back from.

"You're steering like a woman expecting to be shot at," Ilsa said beside her, arms crossed, entirely too comfortable for someone who'd known Dominic less than two weeks.

"I'm steering like a woman who's been shot at."

"Fair." Ilsa spat companionably over the side. "Halvard's boat put in at the bend an hour before we did. He's not even pretending to be subtle anymore."

Dominic had seen it. A gray-hulled survey launch, Institute markings freshly painted over older ones, riding at anchor with the crisp efficiency of money. Halvard had stopped knocking on doors. Now he simply followed, the way debt follows, the way a name follows.

Behind her, Tam Ovid was lashing crates of his demolition gear to the mid-deck rail with the tenderness other men reserved for infants. Juno Pell sat near the bow with Ede Voss's map spread across her knees under a sheet of oilcloth, copying it by hand into a ledger, line by line, as though transcription were a form of prayer.

"Copy it twice," Dominic said. "Once in your book, once from memory."

Juno didn't look up. "I've already memorized it."

"Memorize it again."

The ferryman's daughter—no, the ferrywoman herself now—snorted. "You always this charming before breakfast?"

"I'm worse after."

They ran the first six miles in silence broken only by water and the creak of the sweep oar. Dominic let the rhythm of the tiller settle something in her chest. Ten years she'd kept moving—upriver crews, coastal salvage, border towns where nobody asked surnames—and every departure had felt like winning. This was the first time leaving a town had felt like being pushed.

Because Halvard knew. Because the name was out. Because somewhere behind them a dying old man's promise had weight enough to drag her back toward the one place on earth she'd sworn never to go.

The Broken Rivergate opened ahead of them without warning. One moment the canyon was ordinary—scrub pine, red shale, the river loud and free—and the next the walls drew in like a closing hand and the water slid beneath the stone with a sound like a held breath released. The gorge floor was smooth black rock, polished by ten thousand years of flood, and the river ran *inside* it, visible through fissures, audible everywhere and nowhere.

Nobody spoke. Even Tam stopped fussing with his crates.

"There," Juno said quietly, pointing.

On the left wall, forty feet up, a mark had been chiseled into the stone: a circle bisected by a vertical line, weathered but unmistakable. Below it, smaller, almost hidden in the lichen: a second symbol. A Brightwater surveyor's mark—the same one that appeared in the margins of Ede's map, in her mother's field notes, in the old Institute filings Juno had spent three nights pulling from the Callow's Bend records office.

"That's hers," Juno said. Her voice had gone strange. "That's Mara Brightwater's mark. It predates Ede's copy by—I checked the lichen growth alone, it has to be twenty years minimum."

"I know whose mark it is." Dominic kept her eyes on the channel. The river beneath the stone was rising audibly, a pressure like a train passing under the floor of the world. "Mark it and move on. We make the narrows before noon or we camp in the wet."

"Dominic—"

"Mark it and move on."

Juno marked it. But Dominic felt the archivist's eyes on her back for the next mile, patient and heavy as sediment, and knew the question underneath them wasn't going anywhere.

* *

**Juno**

Juno Pell had spent eleven years loving a dead woman's handwriting.

It started in the university archive, third year, when she'd pulled the Brightwater expedition reports for a thesis she never finished. Mara Brightwater's field notes were famous—or should have been—not just for what they recorded but for how. Precise where precision mattered, lyrical where the land demanded it. *The aquifer sings in D minor today,* one note read, beside a pressure reading. Juno had copied that sentence into three different notebooks over the years, the way other people copied prayers.

Then the expedition failed. Then the Institute's report called it structural collapse, human error, tragedy. Then Mara Brightwater became a cautionary tale instead of a hero, and her daughter became a rumor—a girl who changed towns faster than mail could follow.

And now that daughter was standing at the tiller of Ilsa's ferry refusing to look at her mother's own survey marks carved into living stone, and Juno could not bear it.

She waited until camp.

They made the narrows by midafternoon—a slot where the gorge pinched to thirty feet and the underground river roared close enough beneath them that the rock hummed through their boot soles—and pulled up on a gravel shelf below a leaning cottonwood, the first flat ground in eight miles. Tam got a fire going small and smokeless, the way he did everything: efficiently, without being asked. Ilsa set lines for trout. Halvard's gray launch anchored in the wide water a half mile downstream, its lanterns coming on one by one like a row of closed fists.

Juno sat across the fire from Dominic and opened her ledger.

"I transcribed the letters," she said.

Dominic went very still. It was a particular stillness, Juno thought—not surprise. Recognition. As if she'd been waiting for this conversation since Callow's Bend and had hoped, unreasonably, to outrun it anyway.

"What letters."

"The final ones. Mara wrote four letters in the last week of the expedition. Two to the Institute. One to Ede Voss. One—" Juno turned the page. "One unfinished. Addressed to *D.*"

The fire popped. Downstream, a night bird called across the black water.

"You went through my mother's correspondence." Dominic's voice was level. Too level. "Without asking."

"They're in the public record. Sealed annexes to the Institute inquiry—filed, catalogued, and buried where no one would ever request them. I requested them. That's my job. That's what I *do*." Juno heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, breathe, start again lower. "I'm not apologizing for reading history. I'm asking why you won't."

"Because it's not history. It's mine."

"It's everyone's!" The word came out of her harder than she intended, and Tam looked up from the fire, and Ilsa's hands went quiet on her fishing line. Juno pressed on anyway; she'd come too far to stop now. "Do you know what they teach about your mother? Nothing. Do you know what the record says? *Incompetent route selection. Failure to monitor seasonal load. Loss of seven personnel due to preventable error.* Your mother died stupid, according to the Institute, and you've spent ten years letting that stand because looking closer hurt too much. Meanwhile there are letters in a sealed annex where she describes—*in her own words*—floodgates, and chambers, and something she calls 'the heart,' and a decision she says is 'for everyone's good.' That's not the writing of a woman who died by accident. That's a woman who died *on purpose*, doing something, and everyone who could have told the truth chose not to."

Dominic set down the tin cup she hadn't been drinking from.

"You think I don't know that?" she said softly.

The firelight moved on her face. For the first time since Juno had met her, there was nothing guarded in it at all, and that was somehow worse than the guard.

"I've known since I was nineteen," Dominic said. "Ede showed me one page. One. And I burned the rest of my life running from it, because if I never looked, then she was still just a woman who died, and not a woman who *chose*, and I didn't have to ask what kind of person leaves a nine-year-old daughter and doesn't come home." She picked the cup back up. Her hands were steady. Juno hated how steady. "You want me to open the annexes. Fine. But understand what you're asking. You're not asking for the truth. You're asking me to stand in the exact spot where she stood and do exactly what she did, and I have spent my whole life making sure I would never be the kind of person who does that."

"And are you?"

The question hung between them. Ilsa had stopped pretending to fish. Tam had stopped pretending not to listen.

Dominic looked into the fire for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone quiet and rough, like stone under moving water.

"I don't know yet. Ask me again at the gate."

Juno closed the ledger. It wasn't surrender—it was a truce, and both of them knew it. But as she rolled into her bedroll that night, listening to the buried river thunder beneath the gravel, she found she wasn't angry anymore. She was afraid. Because she had seen the shape of the thing in Mara's letters, and she had seen the same shape forming in the daughter's face, and history, she knew better than anyone, did not repeat itself politely.

It repeated itself exactly.

* *

**Dominic**

Dominic took first watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep.

The canyon at night was a cathedral with a heartbeat. Every few minutes the underground river surged against some deep constraint and the whole gorge shuddered faintly, dust sifting from the high walls, and she found herself counting the intervals the way her mother's notes counted them. *Pressure cycle: eleven minutes at surface gauge. Longer the deeper you go.*

She'd read those notes as a child, sitting outside her father's study door. She'd hated them. She'd memorized them anyway.

Boots on gravel behind her. Ilsa lowered herself onto the rock shelf without invitation, close enough that their shoulders touched, far enough that it meant nothing—or pretended to.

"She's right, you know," Ilsa said.

"Don't start."

"I'm not starting anything. I'm ending my part of it." Ilsa pulled her knees up, chin on her arms, watching the gray launch's lanterns downstream. "You hired us with a promise and half a map and no explanation, and we came anyway, which makes us idiots, which makes it my business. So here's all of mine: whatever's down there, whatever your mother sealed, whatever Halvard wants—when it comes time to decide, decide fast, and tell us the truth while you do it. We can survive bad decisions. We can't survive being lied to."

"That's fair."

"That's all?" Ilsa glanced sideways. "No speech?"

"I'm saving my speeches for people who follow me into flooded canyons."

A huff of laughter in the dark. Their shoulders stayed touching. Neither moved away.

Downstream, one of the launch's lanterns went out, then another—but a third light appeared higher up, on the canyon rim, moving. Not a lantern. A signal lamp, blinking in short deliberate pulses toward the gorge depths. Answering it, faint and far below, something blinked back.

Dominic went cold.

"Ilsa," she said quietly. "How many boats did Halvard bring?"

"One that I counted."

"How many lights are on that ridge?"

Ilsa followed her gaze. The lamp on the rim blinked again—three long, two short—and far beneath them, past the stone, past the buried river, in the direction of the gate they wouldn't reach for two more days, something answered.

Three long. Two short.

Not an echo. An answer.

"He's not following us," Dominic said, standing, the cold settling into her bones where it would stay. "He's meeting someone. Someone's already inside."
