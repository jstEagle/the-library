## 6. The Drowned Canyon

The river vanished at dawn.

Not gradually, not the way rivers thinned in late summer, thinning to a brown ribbon you could wade across with dry boots. It simply stopped—swallowed whole by a seam in the canyon wall where the water bent and poured itself into stone like a coin into a slot. One moment it was loud, all white noise and muscle; the next it was gone, and the silence it left behind was so complete that Ilsa's ferry rope creaking against its cleat sounded like a shout.

"Broken Rivergate," Juno said softly, from the bow of the second boat. She had her mother's field journal open on her knees, though she hadn't looked at it once since they'd launched. "The maps call it a sink. The old surveyors thought the river just died here."

"The river doesn't die," Ilsa said. She stood at the stern of the lead boat with one hand on the tiller, reading the current the way other people read faces. "It goes under. Comes back up six miles on, past the Narrows. My grandfather used to say the canyon was drinking."

Dominic stood in the shallows where the water slid into the rock, boots planted on wet limestone, and felt the pull of it through her soles—a faint, constant tug, like standing on the chest of something breathing. Ten years of salvage work had taught her to feel water through stone: the difference between a seep and a spring, between a flooded hold and a hollow one. This was neither. This was a throat.

She unrolled Ede Voss's map across a flat boulder and weighted the corners with four stones.

The map was drawn in charcoal and berry ink on sailcloth, the lines shaky near the edges where his hand had begun to fail him but sure as a plumb line down the center. The gorge ran beneath the canyon for most of a day's walk, the river overhead invisible, the passage itself a series of galleries and choke points marked in Ede's cramped script: *low ceiling, duck. Rubble fall, climb left. Water waist-deep after second bend—do not trust the third.*

And then there were the other markings.

Dominic had noticed them the night Ede pressed the map into her hands, had told herself she was imagining them, grief doing what grief did—finding her mother's ghost in every smudge and shadow. But here in full daylight, with the gorge mouth yawning black ten yards away, there was no pretending.

Someone else had annotated this map. Someone whose hand she knew better than her own face.

Her mother wrote the way she dug: fast, economical, every stroke load-bearing. A single vertical slash for caution. Two slashes for danger. A small circle with a dot at its center for *safe ground, rest here.* And always, always, tiny numerals penciled along the margins—depths, distances, air readings—recorded in a shorthand she'd invented herself and taught to no one, because she'd assumed her daughter was too young to need it.

Dominic had been nine years old when she'd learned that shorthand, sitting cross-legged on a crate of survey equipment while her mother filled out the day's log by lantern light. *Numbers don't lie, little fish,* she'd said. *People lie. Stone lies. Numbers are the only honest thing down a hole.*

The numerals on Ede's map were in that shorthand. Every one of them.

"She copied from someone," Tam said behind her. He'd come up quiet, the way he did, and was looking over her shoulder at the map with his arms crossed, his demolition kit slung across his back like a sleeping child. "Your prospector. He didn't draw these numbers himself—he traced a master. Look at the pressure marks. Charcoal over pencil grooves."

"I know."

"You knew before we launched." It wasn't an accusation. Tam Ovid never accused; he observed, and let the observation sit there like a charge waiting for a wire. "You've been quiet since Callow's Bend. Quieter than your kind of quiet."

Dominic folded the map along its old creases. "Ede Voss spent thirty years trying to reach the Rivergate. He got close twice. Both times he turned back." She looked up at the canyon walls, red stone going gold where the morning hit it. "You don't turn back twice from something you've wanted that long unless someone convinces you to. And you don't convince a man like Ede Voss of anything except with proof."

"So the proof walked out of this canyon twenty years ago," Ilsa said, "and left its handwriting on his map."

"And he followed it," Juno said, "the way I would have."

No one answered that. Juno hadn't meant it as a confession, exactly, but it landed like one—the archivist admitting what they were all carrying: that they weren't following a map into a gorge. They were following a dead woman home.

* * *

They entered the gorge two hours after first light, boats abandoned above the sink, gear redistributed across five backs. Ilsa led with a lantern and a coil of line. Tam carried the explosives because no one else would touch them. Juno carried the documents—Ede's map, her mother's journal, the letters—in an oilskin wrap strapped to her chest like a child. Dominic carried the rope, the spare lanterns, and the front position, because if the gorge wanted a Brightwater it could have one walking point.

The first gallery was tall enough to stand in and wide enough for three abreast, which fooled you into thinking the gorge would be generous. It wasn't. Forty yards in, the ceiling dropped and the walls leaned together, and they went through the first choke in a crouch, sideways, the river roaring somewhere below their feet through cracks in the floor that breathed cold air against their ankles.

"It's under us," Tam said, hunched, his voice flattened by the stone. "All of it. We're walking on top of a river."

"We're walking inside its throat," Ilsa corrected. "Feel the floor. It's warm."

It was. Not hot—but warmer than the air, warmer than stone had any right to be this deep in shade, and Dominic knelt and put her palm flat against the limestone and understood. The water below wasn't just passing through. It was being held. Some vast weight of it, moving slow, filling channels and chambers the way blood fills a body, and the heat rising through the rock was the heat of that volume, the thermal signature of something enormous and patient.

Ede's map called the next section *the Ribs.* It was accurate. The passage opened into a hall braced by natural columns of flowstone, a dozen of them, pale as bone in the lantern light, and between them the floor dipped into shallow pools that glowed faintly green where some mineral in the water caught the flame. Their footsteps echoed wrong—not the flat slap of enclosed stone but a deeper resonance, as if the hall were a drum and they were walking on its skin.

Juno stopped at the third rib and made a sound low in her throat.

On the column, at shoulder height, someone had carved a mark. A circle with a dot at its center.

*Safe ground.*

Beside it, small and sharp as a bird's track, a numeral in her mother's shorthand: **4.2**. Four-point-two. Air quality, or depth to water, or distance to the next hazard—Dominic couldn't read it without the key, and the key lived in a nine-year-old's memory of lantern light and her mother's voice, and her eyes were burning suddenly, stupidly, and she turned away and pretended to check her bootlaces until it passed.

"Ede didn't carve this," Juno said. Her fingers hovered over the mark without touching it, the way she touched everything in the archive at Callow's Bend—reverent, afraid of leaving prints. "This is old. See the mineral bloom in the cuts? Twenty years of calcite. Maybe more."

"Twenty-two," Dominic said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that. "The expedition went in twenty-two years ago, in the spring. They came out in pieces, in the autumn. What happened in between—" She shrugged. "The Institute's report says a flood. The report is forty pages long and doesn't contain a single number my mother would have written."

They moved on. The Ribs gave way to a crawl, the crawl to a rubble fall they climbed on the left exactly as the map instructed, and twice more they found the marks—two slashes at a shelf of loose stone that shifted alarmingly under Tam's weight until he redistributed and swore; another safe-ground circle at a dry ledge wide enough for camp, where soot ghosts on the ceiling showed that fires had burned here once, long ago.

By midday they reached the second bend, and the map's warning proved true: the passage dropped into water. Waist-deep, black, still—no current, which was worse than current, because stillness underground meant depth. Ilsa tested it with a pole and found bottom at four feet, then five, then nothing, then bottom again three feet farther on. They crossed in a chain, hands on each other's packs, the cold climbing to their ribs, and on the far side Juno shook so hard she had to sit down, and Ilsa sat beside her and put the woman's hands around the lantern housing until the warmth did its work.

It was there, on the far bank, drying by turns, that Dominic saw the third thing.

Not a mark. A message.

The wall above the waterline was smooth flowstone, and across it, written large in the same confident hand, in a substance that had gone dark with age—pitch, maybe, or resin—were five words:

**DO NOT OPEN THE LOWER DOOR**

Below that, smaller: **M. BRIGHTWATER**

The gorge was silent except for the river beneath them, that vast slow pulse, and Dominic stood in her soaked trousers with her hair dripping and read her own name off the wall of a place she'd sworn never to enter, written by a woman who had been dead for twenty-two years, and felt the floor of her life tilt.

"That's fresh," Tam said quietly.

"What?"

"The resin. Pitch weathers gray in five years, black in ten. That's dark but it's not dead." He stepped closer, squinting, and touched the last letter with one fingertip and rubbed it against his thumb. "And look at the baseline. She wrote this kneeling. Waterline's higher now than when she stood here." He looked at Dominic. "She wrote it during the flood. Or right before it. She was standing about where you're standing, in about this much water, writing a warning to whoever came next."

"To me," Dominic said. "She knew I'd come. Eventually. Someday. When I was old enough to be stupid about it."

"Or she wrote it to anyone," Juno offered gently. "To Ede. He must have gotten this far—that's how he learned the shorthand, why his copy carries her numbers. He read this wall and turned back. Twice."

"He turned back," Dominic said, "and spent thirty years drawing maps so someone else wouldn't have to." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And here I am. Following both of them. The dead woman who warned me off and the dying man who begged me on. Between the two of them I never had a chance of staying home, did I?"

Ilsa said nothing. She had come to stand at Dominic's shoulder, close enough that their sleeves touched, and she didn't offer comfort and didn't look away, and somehow that was the only comfort that would have worked. After a while Dominic breathed out, and wiped her face with a wet hand, which was as good an excuse as any, and took out the map.

*Do not trust the third bend.* They were past it. Which meant everything from here was territory even Ede's borrowed knowledge described only in fragments—his notes grew sparser past this point, the charcoal lines thinner, trailing off like a man losing his nerve in real time.

But the numerals kept going. Her mother's numbers marched on down the margin, page after page, steady as a heartbeat, recording depths and distances for passages Ede Voss had never seen. He had copied the whole map from a master he'd acquired somewhere—and the master had been drawn by a woman planning a route she intended someone to follow.

Or a route she herself had walked one last time.

"We camp here," Dominic said. "Dry ledge, high waterline, defensible approach. Tomorrow we push for the lower door."

"The door she told us not to open," Tam said.

"The door she told us not to open *unprepared.*" Dominic rolled the map closed. "Read it again. It's not a warning against opening. It's a warning against opening stupid. There's a difference, and my mother never wasted pitch on a distinction that didn't matter."

* * *

Night in the gorge was a different country. No stars, no wind, no birdsong—only the lantern, the four of them, and the sound of the held river, which changed after dark. Dominic noticed it first as she lay awake on her bedroll: a rhythm in the deep water, slow and vast, a swell and release like breath. Like the aquifer flexing against its walls. Like something alive down there in the dark, turning over in its sleep.

She gave up on sleep and sat by the lantern with the map open on her knees, tracing her mother's numbers with one finger, teaching herself the key again letter by letter, number by number, the way you relearn a language you spoke as a child. Behind her, Juno stirred.

"You knew," Juno said quietly. "When you took Ede's promise. You knew those were her markings."

"I hoped."

"You don't hope, Dominic. You plan. You calculate. You've done it since the day I met you—you plan your exits before you finish your hellos." Juno sat up, hugging her knees, firelight catching the ink stains on her fingers. "So tell me the truth. What did you think you'd find down here?"

Dominic considered lying. It would have been easy; it had been her default for a decade. But the wall with her name on it was fifty yards back down the passage, and the river was breathing underneath them, and lies felt heavier than usual tonight.

"I thought I'd find the accident," she said. "The flood, the collapse, the bad luck. The story the Institute sold. I thought I'd walk through it and see that it was just a hole in the ground that killed two people the way holes kill people, ordinary and meaningless, and I could put it down. Leave it. Stop carrying it." She turned the map over in her lap. "Instead I find her handwriting on a prospector's copy, her warnings on the walls, her route planned out like she expected company. She wasn't caught down here, Juno. She *worked* down here. For months, maybe. Long after the Institute says the expedition ended." She looked at the darkness beyond the lantern, toward the south and whatever waited at the third bend's end. "Which means either the report is wrong, or it's a lie. And I've read enough Institute reports to know which one costs more to write."

Juno was silent a long moment. Then: "My mother believed the official story. Right up until the end. She used to say Meridian buried its dead with honors." Her voice tightened. "I built my whole life on that sentence."

"Honors make good landfill," Dominic said. "I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize yet." Juno lay back down, and her voice drifted up from the edge of sleep, soft and strange. "Wait until we find what she was working on. Wait until we know whether she was sealing a tomb—or building one."

The river breathed beneath them. Dominic sat with the map until the lantern burned low, and in the margin of the last leg, in her mother's hand, she finally decoded the final notation—one she'd taken for a depth reading, a routine figure, all evening.

It wasn't a depth.

It was a date. Spring, twenty-two years ago. Three days after the flood the Institute recorded as the end of everything.

Her mother had written it the way she'd written all the others—calm, precise, load-bearing—a timestamp on a journey that officially had no more traveler.

Three days. Whatever her mother had done in the drowned canyon, she had done it alone, after everyone believed her dead, with three days of light and air and time.

Dominic closed the map and, in the dark, began counting backward from three.
