## 7. Ede's Last Camp

Juno Pell had read a thousand documents in her life—deed ledgers, shipping manifests, the waterlogged minutes of three drowned town councils—and she had never once been afraid of paper. Paper was patient. Paper waited for you. It did not wait for Ede Voss.

She felt that wrongness before they saw the camp itself: a stillness in the canyon air, as though the river beneath their feet had stopped breathing. The gorge narrowed here, walls of black basalt leaning close enough that Ilsa's ferry-lantern threw their shadows up and over them, giants walking on stone. Then Tam, who had gone ahead with his crowbar slung like a rifle, whistled low.

"Fire ring," he said. "Cold. Old."

The camp sat in a shelf of dry rock above the high-water line, tucked against the cliff face where the canyon bent out of sight. A ring of stones, ash long since washed to gray. A collapsed tent frame, its canvas rotted to lace. Two crates stenciled with the Meridian Institute's compass-rose mark, lids pried open long ago and left gaping. And beside them, weighted down under a flat rock with the deliberation of a man setting a table for company he expected but dreaded—a leather journal, swollen with damp, its cover stamped *E. VOSS* in faded block letters.

Nobody moved for a moment. Even the river seemed to hold its breath underfoot, a vast muffled sound through the stone, like a crowd murmuring behind a closed door.

"He knew someone would come," Dominic said quietly.

It wasn't a question, but Juno answered it anyway, because answering questions was what she was for. "Prospectors leave notes. Claims markers, directions home. This isn't that." She crouched by the journal without touching it. "This is a message. You don't weight a journal down unless you want it found in order."

"In order?" Ilsa asked.

"Readers skip," Juno said. "He'd have known that about people like me."

Dominic knelt across from her. In the lantern light her face was all planes and shadow, and Juno thought—not for the first time—that she looked less like her mother every day and more like her father: all edges, all angles, a face built for looking away from things. But her hands, when she reached out, were careful. Digging hands. Ede Voss had recognized them in a boarding house sixty miles from anywhere, and Juno was beginning to understand why.

"Go on," Dominic said. "You're the archivist."

"You're his heir apparent," Juno said. "You made him a promise."

"I made a promise to a dying man." Dominic's jaw tightened. "That doesn't mean I get to decide what he wanted said."

"It kind of does," Tam offered from the fire ring, where he'd been poking the cold ash with a stick. "Sorry. But it kind of does."

They compromised, the way expeditions compromise: Juno would read aloud, and Dominic would sit close enough to stop her. She took the journal into her lap. The leather was cool and damp, and the first page crackled like a leaf when she opened it.

*Day 1. Entered the Rivergate by the south chimney route. Water lower than any season in living memory. If I don't write this down nobody will believe me, and if I do write it down somebody will believe me, and I no longer know which is worse.*

She paused there. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far below them, through forty feet of stone, the river went on with its murmuring.

"That's not an opening line," Tam said. "That's a man talking himself into something."

"Keep reading," Dominic said.

* * *

*Day 3. Found the outer gate today. Not a tomb—I was wrong about that, and glad of it, because tombs mean dead men's gold and dead men's gold means companies. It's a work. Older than anything I've seen in thirty years of prying at this country. Flood-control, if I read the marks right. Somebody built a machine to argue with a river, and then somebody else came along later and taught the machine to lose on purpose.*

*I found tools. Modern tools. Brightwater expedition stamps on the handles. They were arranged in a row, points facing the wall, like men grounded from supper. Nobody arranges tools like that by accident. Somebody wanted the next person to know they'd been here, and to feel watched.*

Juno's voice caught on that last line, and she heard why only after she'd said it: because she had felt watched since the moment they'd entered the gorge. Every marker on Ede's map—the ones in her mother's handwriting, the ones that predated Ede's copy by years—had felt less like guidance than like anticipation. *We knew you'd come.* She looked up and found Dominic watching her with an expression she couldn't name.

"Keep going," Dominic said, and her voice was steady, which meant it was costing her.

*Day 5. Followed the gallery north past the second weir. The Institute's survey stakes are everywhere, newer than the tools, older than me. They were measuring flow rates. Flow rates. You don't measure flow rates in a tomb. You measure them when you want to know how much you can take.*

*Found a camp of my own tonight, this shelf. Good stone. Dry even in flood season, or near enough. I'll make my push for the inner chamber tomorrow.*

There were four blank pages after that entry. Juno turned them carefully, feeling the crew lean in around the lantern like iron filings toward a magnet. On the fifth page, the handwriting changed.

Not degraded—changed. The early entries had been a prospector's hand, quick and slanted, written standing up or by bad light. These new lines were slower. Pressed harder into the paper. As if the man writing them had needed the pen to bite.

*Day 6. I saw it.*

*I'm going to write this plainly because plainness is all I have left. There is a chamber past the inner gate where the aquifer comes together—all the deep water of this whole broken country, gathered in one living place. It isn't a pool. It isn't a reservoir. It moves. It answers. I stood at the rail her expedition left behind and I put my hand in the water and the water was warm, and I am an old man who has drunk from every ditch in three territories, and I have never felt water that felt like being greeted.*

*I laughed. I remember laughing. Then I remembered the survey stakes, and I stopped.*

*They don't want to study it. I've read enough Institute paperwork to know the difference between study and inventory. They want to tap it. Strip the chambers, pipe it north, sell it by the acre-foot until there's nothing left but a dry cathedral and a footnote. And the thing in the water—the living part, whatever it is—it can't run. Water doesn't run from water. It would just keep greeting them, all the way down to nothing.*

Juno stopped reading. Her throat had gone tight, and not from dust.

Ilsa spoke first, softly, the way she steered in shallow water. "Warm water. That far underground?"

"Deep aquifers run warm sometimes," Tam said. "Geothermal. It's—" He stopped. He looked at the journal. "It doesn't answer, though. Water doesn't answer."

"Ede Voss was dying of river fever and half-mad with thirst when I met him," Dominic said. "Maybe—"

"Don't," Juno said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't meant to say it out loud. But it was out now, and she was an archivist, and an archivist's one law was that you didn't flinch from the document in front of you.

"Don't explain it away yet," she said. "Please. Let it be what he wrote. We can doubt it tomorrow."

Dominic held her gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded once, a small precise motion, and Juno understood that she had just been given something rare: permission. Not to read the journal—but to believe it.

She turned the page.

* * *

*Day 7. I found her notes today. Marion Brightwater's own hand, in the margins of the Institute's survey sheets, which someone left behind—which she left behind, I think, deliberately, the way you leave a note on a kitchen table before walking out of a marriage.*

*Her figures don't match theirs. Theirs say the aquifer yields X thousand acre-feet sustainable. Hers say the number is a lie built on a lie: the deep water recharges over centuries, not seasons. Tap it at their rate and you aren't mining a resource, you're killing an organ. Everything downstream goes with it. Every spring, every well, every green thread in this brown country. Callow's Bend first. The Bend drinks from this system. So does everything the Bend ever fed.*

*And in the margin, in pencil, pressed so hard the graphite shone: THEY KNOW. THEY HAVE ALWAYS KNOWN. THE NUMBERS ARE THEIRS.*

Juno read that line twice, silently, before she could make her voice work. Around her the camp had gone very still. Tam had stopped fidgeting entirely, which she suspected was the most alarming thing he'd done all week.

"They funded the dig," Ilsa said slowly. "Meridian funded the Brightwater expedition. That's public record—I've seen the plaques."

"And the plaques don't mention that the Institute's own hydrologists falsified the yield estimates," Juno said. Her voice sounded strange to her, level and cold, like reading from a ledger. "Or that the lead scientist found them out. Or what happened next."

"No," Dominic said. "They don't."

There was something in her voice that made Juno look up. Dominic was staring at the middle distance, at nothing, at some interior wall covered in twenty-year-old newspaper clippings. *Salvage accident. Canyon flood. Body never recovered.* Juno had read those clippings too; she'd kept them, in fact, laminated, in a box under her bed, back when keeping them was devotion instead of evidence.

"The official story is a flash flood," Juno said carefully. "Equipment failure. Tragic. The Institute paid for the funerals."

"The Institute always pays for funerals," Dominic said. "It's cheaper than paying for witnesses."

Tam let out a low breath. "Hell of a thing to carry around for ten years."

"I carried the cheap version," Dominic said. "Accident. Bad luck. My mother was reckless and my father followed her in." Her thumb moved along the journal's spine, back and forth, a small unconscious motion, like soothing an animal. "Turns out I've been grieving the wrong death."

* * *

Juno read on, because that was the job, and because stopping felt more dangerous than continuing.

*Day 8. I went back down today. I told myself it was to check her figures. It wasn't. I needed to see it again. That's the honest truth and I hate that it is.*

*It was waiting. Not hostile—nothing so simple. It was attentive. When I reached the rail, the surface of the water had changed. Patterns in it. Circles, spreading from the center outward, regular as rings from a dropped stone except nothing had dropped. I got the mad idea it was counting my heartbeats. I left. I am not ashamed. I am a little ashamed.*

*Here is what broke me, and I will write it small because I don't want it to be true: I understand the Institute now. Not forgive—understand. Standing at that rail, I wanted things. I wanted to be the man who brought this water to the territories. I wanted my name on the pipes. The wanting came up out of the water like heat off a stove, and it fit me perfectly, and that is the most frightening thing I have ever felt. Because if it fits me, it fits everyone. And if it fits everyone, then whoever controls this place controls everyone, forever, and calls it generosity.*

*Marion saw that. God help me, Marion Brightwater saw it first, and she did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic ended with her opening the sluices.*

Juno's hands were shaking now. She set the journal flat on her knees and pressed them still, and looked at Dominic, because some things you cannot read aloud while looking at the page.

Dominic had gone white. Not dramatically—no gasp, no stagger. Just the blood leaving her face in a slow tide, the way water leaves a channel when the source is cut. "The sluices," she said. "The lower chambers. Chapter ten of her field notes—the flooded sections we haven't reached." Her voice was thin. "She didn't die in a flood."

"She made one," Ilsa said quietly.

"To drown the access galleries," Tam said. He was an explosives man; he understood water the way priests understood sin. "You blow the lower weirs, the river takes the chambers, and nobody taps anything. Ever. Including you, if you're still inside."

*For everyone's good,* the journal said on the next page, and Juno read it aloud because it demanded to be said in a human voice:

*She wrote it on the last survey sheet, in the same shining pencil: FOR EVERYONE'S GOOD. Not a plea. Not a justification. A load-bearing sentence. The kind of sentence you build a wall out of. I have been sitting here all night trying to find the flaw in it and I can't, and that is why I am going home. Some knowledge is a door. Some knowledge is a door you have to nail shut, and the nailing has to be done by someone who loved what was on the other side, or it won't hold.*

*I am not that someone. I met her daughter once, years ago, at a claims office in Harrow. Small girl, mud to the elbows, holding a hammer wrong. I am too old and too frightened and too fond of my own name to finish this. But that girl has digging hands, and if she ever finds this map—and I think she will, I think the map wants her the way the water wants the sea—then maybe she can do what I couldn't.*

*Forgive me, Dominic Brightwater. Forgive me for handing you the hammer.*

Juno closed the journal.

The silence afterward was total. Even the river seemed to have drawn back, a held breath beneath forty feet of stone. The lantern flame stood perfectly straight, as if afraid to move.

It was Ilsa who finally stirred—crossing the shelf to stand at Dominic's shoulder, saying nothing, offering nothing but her presence, the way you stand with someone at a graveside. Tam busied himself with the fire ring, feeding it kindling with exaggerated care, giving the moment somewhere to go that wasn't words.

Dominic sat motionless for a long time. Then she reached out and took the journal from Juno's lap, gently, the way you take something breakable from a child.

"He knew my name," she said. "Before I ever gave it to him. He saw me at a claims office when I was—" She stopped. Recalculated. "When I was eight. Nine. He remembered my hands."

"People remember Brightwaters," Juno said softly. "Your mother made sure of that."

"My mother made sure of a lot of things." Dominic looked down at the journal, and Juno watched something settle in her face—not peace, nothing so kind, but a kind of terrible orientation, like a compass needle finding north after years of spinning. "She sealed it. She died sealing it. And the Institute spent twenty years telling the world she was careless." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. It got quieter, and colder, and more precise. "Halvard's surveyors are three days behind us with Institute funding and Institute instruments, and every one of them thinks they're walking toward an asset."

"What do we do?" Tam asked. The fire cracked and spat. "Push on? Turn back?"

Dominic stood. She picked up the lantern, and its light climbed her face and caught in her eyes, and for one disorienting second Juno saw not the salvage diver from Callow's Bend but the small girl from the claims office, mud to the elbows, holding a hammer wrong.

"We push on," Dominic said. "Ede promised me the inner chamber. My mother left me a message. And I am done letting other people finish her sentences."

They broke camp in the gray beginnings of dawn, and it was Juno, packing the journal last into the waterproof satchel against her chest, who noticed what none of the others had: fresh boot prints in the mud at the edge of the shelf. Narrow heels. Institute issue. Days old at most, pressed over Ede's old tracks like a signature over a signature.

Someone had already read this camp.

Someone was ahead of them.
