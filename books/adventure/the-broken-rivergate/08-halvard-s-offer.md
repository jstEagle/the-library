## 8. Halvard's Offer

They heard him before they saw him—the flat slap of oars against water where no boat should be, coming up the drowned canyon's throat at an hour when even the fish kept quiet.

Dominic stood first in the grey pre-dawn, one hand on the cold stone of the canyon wall, listening. The river beneath them made its low constant sound, like something breathing through a pillow, and over that came the rhythm of a hull she knew: not a ferryman's stroke, not Ilsa's economical pull, but the measured, mechanical cadence of a man who had learned rowing from a manual and never from his arms.

"Boat," she said softly.

Tam was up in half a breath, rifle already in his hands, though he didn't raise it. Ilsa rose slower, rolling her shoulders, her eyes going to the bend downstream where the channel narrowed between two ribs of black rock. Juno didn't wake at all until Ilsa shook her, and then she came up blinking with her notebook still creased under her cheek, a line of Ede Voss's handwriting pressed backwards into her skin.

The boat rounded the bend into the last of the starlight.

It was a survey skiff, Institute-grey, clean-lined, with a small electric motor humming where Tam's eyes went first and stayed. Halvard sat in the stern with his hands folded on his knee like a man waiting for a train, and he had, Dominic noted with something between fury and grudging respect, come alone. No escort boat behind him. No rifles visible. Just the polite, patient menace of him, delivered upstream by machine.

"Miss Brightwater." He let the skiff drift alongside their camp ledge and caught the rock with one gloved hand. "You're up early."

"You're up impossible," she said. "There's no launch point within thirty miles of here that isn't ours or dead water."

"There's the old quarry road above Sallow Bend." He smiled faintly. "I did my homework. Your mother's expedition used it too—did you know that? The Institute keeps excellent records."

Behind her, she felt Juno go very still.

* * *

They let him make coffee. It was Tam's idea, offered with a flat sort of insolence—"no sense shooting a man before he's had a cup"—and it bought them all time to think, which Dominic suspected was exactly what Halvard wanted. A man who comes alone is a man who has decided you won't kill him, and every minute you spend deciding whether he's right is a minute he spends inside your walls.

He sat on a camp stool they hadn't offered, drinking from his own tin cup, and looked around their camp with open, cataloguing interest. The rope coils. The dive gear. Ede's map, weighted flat under a stone at the center of everything, because Dominic had stopped pretending it wasn't the heart of the camp three days ago.

"Nine days," Halvard said, without preamble.

Ilsa looked up from mending a strap. "What?"

"The river." He turned his cup slowly. "The seasonal melt starts early this year. Snowpack in the high country is heavy, and there's a warm front moving in from the coast that my meteorologists have been tracking for two weeks. When it hits, the upper weirs will take more water than they were built to hold—or than anyone's maintained them to hold, in forty years. The lower chambers flood. Everything below the second gate goes under. Permanently." He glanced at Dominic. "Your mother's chambers. Whatever she left down there. Nine days, perhaps fewer if the front accelerates."

"You could have led with that," Tam said.

"I'm leading with it now."

Dominic crouched by the map, keeping her body between Halvard and it, though she knew he'd already seen it, probably photographed it through a lens from two hundred yards while they slept. That was the thing about Halvard that made her skin crawl worse than any thug with a shotgun: he never took anything by force. He simply arranged the world so that force became unnecessary.

"Say what you came to say," she told him.

"All right." He set down the cup. "The Meridian Institute is prepared to offer you a contract. Full funding for the remainder of the expedition—equipment, personnel, salvage rights support. Legal protection: whatever debts are currently constraining you—and I'm given to understand there are several, including a rather creative lien on your diving certification from the Marrow Point crew—we make those disappear. Not pay them off. Make them *disappear*. And protection." His voice softened by a degree, which was somehow worse. "Protection from what happens to people who dig into Rivergate history without institutional cover. Your father's death certificate says rockfall, Miss Brightwater. It took the Institute eleven years and considerable expense to make that document true. I'd hate to see you generate paperwork of your own."

The silence after that had weight. Ilsa's hands had stopped on the strap. Tam's jaw worked once. Juno sat with her notebook closed and both hands flat on top of it, like a schoolgirl keeping herself from opening it.

"My father," Dominic said carefully, "died in a rockfall."

"Of course he did." Halvard met her eyes, and there it was—not cruelty, nothing so simple. Just the terrible patience of a man who had all the time in the world and knew she didn't. "The offer stands until the water rises. After that, the site floods, the concession reverts fully to the Institute, and whatever's down there becomes our problem instead of yours. You walk away whole. That's not a threat. It's arithmetic."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you refuse." He shrugged, gathering his cup, rising with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been thrown out of anywhere. "I'll be behind you either way. At the pace the melt allows."

* * *

She walked him back to his skiff, because she wanted to see the motor, and because she didn't want him walking through their camp again on his way out. The channel at the landing ran dark and quick over submerged steps—old steps, cut by hands centuries dead, which was its own reminder of how deep this place's history went and how little of it belonged to any of them.

"Your mother," Halvard said, as he readied the lines, "was the finest field engineer the Institute ever lost. I want you to know that I mean that as more than rhetoric. I read her reports. All of them. Even the ones that were never filed." He paused, one hand on the gunwale. "She would have taken the offer, you know. In your position. She was pragmatic to the bone. She believed institutions were ugly but necessary—that you changed the world by holding the pen, not by burning the paper."

"She flooded her own dig," Dominic said. "Killed her crew. Sealed the site. That doesn't sound like a woman holding the pen."

"Doesn't it?" For the first time, something moved behind Halvard's composure—something almost like grief, quickly filed away. "Think about what it takes, Miss Brightwater, to do what she did. To look at a discovery worth generations of funding and decide it should stay buried. Either she was a monster who murdered her own team out of madness—or she understood something about that place that made murder the merciful option." He stepped down into the skiff. "I've spent eleven years trying to decide which. I'd hoped you might help me."

The motor hummed. The skiff swung out into the current, and Halvard called back over the water, conversational, almost kind:

"Eight days now. Sleep well."

* * *

Nobody slept.

They sat around the banked fire with the map between them and Halvard's arithmetic hanging in the air like smoke. Tam cleaned his rifle he didn't need to clean. Ilsa stared into the coals. Juno wrote, stopped, tore the page out, and burned it in the fire, which told Dominic more than anything she'd written.

"Debts," Ilsa said finally. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that he knew about them." Dominic fed a stick to the fire. "Which means he's been pulling records since Callow's Bend. Maybe longer. Maybe since Voss first started asking questions in town."

"Full funding," Tam said. "Real equipment. We've been running this expedition on ferry rope and prayer. Half our dive gear is older than Juno." He said it without apology, just inventory. "And the man's right about the melt. I grew up on this river. When the high snow lets go, the lower chambers don't flood—they *detonate*. If your mother left anything down there worth finding, nine days is nothing. Eight is less."

"So we should take it." Dominic said it flatly, watching their faces. "That's the argument. Take the money, take the protection, let the Institute carry us down to the second gate on their own dime, and then—what? Hand them the aquifer? Sign the salvage concession and watch them strip-mine the water table under four towns' drinking supply? That's the trade. That's always been the trade. Comfort now for the thing itself later."

"You don't know that's what they'd do," Juno said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She held their gaze with an effort Dominic could see the cost of.

"I've read the Institute's publications my whole life. Their hydrology papers funded half the modern understanding of aquifer systems. Yes, they strip-mine concessions—but they also *map*, they *preserve*, they built the archive in Callow's Bend that I basically lived in as a girl. Institutions aren't monsters, Dominic. They're machines. What they do depends on who holds the levers." Her voice tightened. "Maybe the lever-holders are different now. Maybe Halvard genuinely wants to study the site, not gut it. You can't know from one conversation."

"No," Dominic agreed. "But I can know from eleven years of a falsified death certificate."

Juno flinched. Tam looked away. The fire popped.

"That's the part that should frighten all of you," Dominic went on, softer now. "Not the offer. The offer's clean—it's money for a map, that's a transaction older than rivers. What should frighten you is that he needed the certificate falsified at all. Something happened down there that the Institute couldn't survive being public. They spent 'considerable expense,' his words, making sure a dead man stayed officially killed by rocks. You don't do that to protect science. You do that to protect yourself." She looked around the fire. "My mother didn't seal that site out of madness. And she didn't seal it out of spite. She sealed it because someone was coming to take it, and the taking would have hurt people. The only question I need answered down there is whether that someone was the Institute then—and whether it's the Institute now."

"And if it's not?" Ilsa asked. "If Halvard's telling the truth and the monsters were only ever the ones your mother locked out?"

Dominic was quiet a long moment.

"Then I burn eleven years of my own certainty," she said, "and I still don't hand him the map. Because a man who offers to erase your debts has already priced what your debts are worth, and a man who warns you the river rises in eight days—" she shook her head slowly, "—isn't warning you. He's setting a clock. Clocks make people stupid. People rush, people take shortcuts, people accept terms they'd laugh at with time to think. He knows that. He's counting on it."

"He also might just be right about the water," Tam said.

"He might." She spread her hands. "That's what makes him good."

* * *

Ilsa found her at the ledge an hour before dawn, where Dominic sat with her legs hanging over the black water, watching the current slide past the drowned steps. Below, somewhere under forty feet of green-dark river, the outer gate waited with her parents' tools arranged behind it like a message nobody had finished reading.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Dominic asked.

"I sleep fine. I just like watching you not sleep." Ilsa settled beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Tam's right, you know. About the gear. About the clock."

"I know."

"And you're still not taking the offer."

"I'm still not taking the offer."

Ilsa nodded slowly. "Because of your father. Or because of the aquifer."

Dominic thought about it honestly, which was more than she usually managed at that hour. "Because of the way he said *your mother's chambers*. Like he'd already inventoried them. Like the site was already his, and the only question was whether I'd be permitted to visit." She turned her hand over on the stone between them, palm up—an old diver's habit, checking the air. "Ede Voss traded me this map for a promise. Nobody's ever asked me for a promise before. Everyone else just asks for the name, or the labor, or the silence. He asked me to finish something. Halvard's asking me to sell something. I keep noticing which one felt like respect."

Ilsa was quiet. Then, in the tone of a woman laying a card face-up on a table: "When this is done—if it's done, if we live—you could take the money anyway. Erase the debts yourself. Walk away clean. Nobody would blame you."

"I know."

"But you won't."

Dominic watched the water. Somewhere far upstream, past the bends and the weirs and the high white country, the warm front was crawling inland over the snowpack, patient as Halvard, inevitable as arithmetic.

"No," she said. "I won't."

Below them, deep under the river's breathing, something shifted—a pressure change too subtle for instruments, felt only in the teeth and the bones, the way the canyon spoke when it spoke at all. Ilsa's head came up. Dominic's hand found hers without looking.

Far downstream, faint but unmistakable in the pre-dawn hush, came the sound none of them had wanted to hear yet: the crack and echo of blasting powder, rolling up the gorge like distant thunder.

Halvard had stopped waiting for answers.

The clock had started early.
