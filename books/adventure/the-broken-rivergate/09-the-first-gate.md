## 9. The First Gate

Tam Ovid had blown open bank vaults in Vessany, a customs house in Marrow Quay, and once—though he never told this story sober—a bridge that deserved what it got. He knew stone the way other men knew faces. He could read grain and fault the way a ferryman read current, could put his palm flat against rock and feel where it wanted to break.

The wall at the end of the dry channel did not want to break.

That was the first thing wrong with it.

They'd come down the last stretch of Ede's map at first light, single file along a ledge no wider than a coffin, the river roaring somewhere below them behind stone instead of under their feet. Dominic led. Ilsa followed with the rope line, then Juno with the lamp case, then Tam with forty pounds of charges he was already thinking about not using. The canyon narrowed as they went, walls leaning in like old men sharing a secret, until the passage simply ended.

And there it was.

Not a cave mouth. Not a rockfall. A *wall*—dressed stone, fitted so close that Tam couldn't have slid a knife blade between the blocks if he'd tried, and he tried, because trying was his job. The blocks were pale grey-green, veined with something darker, and every one of them bore the same shallow carving: a spiral, worn nearly smooth by water that hadn't touched this place in centuries.

"Nobody build a tomb like this," Ilsa said. She ran her hand along the seam. "Tombs are meant to be opened."

"Maybe they changed their minds," Juno said.

Tam didn't answer. He was listening to the wall. He pressed his ear against the stone, cold as riverbed clay, and beneath the distant mutter of the hidden river he heard something else—a hollowness, deep and even, like the note a cistern makes when you knock on its side.

"There's space back there," he said. "A lot of it. And it's not full of sand or silt or dead explorers." He straightened up, worked his jaw. "It's dry."

Dominic turned from where she'd been studying Ede's map by lamplight. Her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding something—went very still, very level, like water before it goes over the edge.

"Dry," she repeated.

"Sealed against water." He tapped the wall twice, low, high. Different notes. "Hear that? Solid below, hollow above. Whoever built this built it to keep the river *out* of whatever's inside. Not to keep people out of the river." He shrugged. "Or maybe both. But the engineering's the same either way. This is a dam that thinks it's a door."

Juno had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was copying something down in her head. "Flood-control works," she said slowly. "Ede's journal called the lower chambers 'the works.' I thought he meant mining works. Machinery."

"Could be both," Tam said. "Old civilizations didn't split hairs the way we do. You build a temple, you build it to hold back a flood, you call it holy and practical in the same breath." He set down his pack and began unbuckling the charge kit, more for something to do with his hands than because he'd decided anything yet. "Question is whether we're knocking politely or rudely."

"Politely," Dominic said.

"That'll take longer."

"We've got time."

They didn't, quite—the river rose in nine days, Halvard had said, and Halvard had been right about everything else so far—but Tam let her have the lie. Some lies were load-bearing.

* * *

It took him most of the day to find the way in without breaking anything, and the finding of it bothered him more than the wall itself.

Because the wall wasn't the whole structure. Once he started tracing seams with a chisel handle, tapping and listening, tapping and listening, he found that the dressed face was only the front of something much larger—a wedge of masonry jammed into the canyon throat like a cork, keyed into the living rock on both sides. Ancient. Older than any of the border kingdoms, older than the Meridian Institute's oldest archive, older probably than the idea of archives. And cut into the keying stones, half-hidden by mineral bloom, were channels. Narrow grooves, running vertical, spaced at intervals too regular to be natural.

"Sluices," he said finally, sitting back on his heels. His knees cracked. Everything about him cracked these days; it was how he knew he was still alive. "Water channels. There's a mechanism in here—counterweights, maybe, or floodgates—and these grooves are how they moved it." He looked up at Dominic. "This isn't a wall somebody built and walked away from. It's a machine somebody built and *maintained*. For a long time, I'd guess. Then stopped."

"When?" Ilsa asked.

He rubbed his thumb over one of the grooves. The bottom was smooth; the top still held tool marks, fine as hair. "Centuries since anyone worked the mechanism. But—" He frowned. "Somebody's been here more recently. Look." He pointed to a block near the base, third course up, where the mineral bloom had been scraped away in a clean rectangle. Underneath, the spiral carving stood sharp-edged, freshly exposed. "That's iron tools. That's within the last fifty years, maybe less."

Dominic came over. She looked at the scraped patch for a long moment, and Tam watched her do the arithmetic he'd already done.

"Ede," she said.

"Or the first expedition," Juno said quietly. "Your parents' expedition. They had iron tools."

"Yes," Dominic said. "They did."

She didn't say anything else. She folded Ede's map closed with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a room you're not ready to enter, and told Tam to find them a way through that didn't bring the canyon down on their heads.

So he did what he did. He spent the afternoon drilling—not blasting holes, just pilot bores, thin as pencils, into the joints where the mortar of centuries had gone to powder. The old builders had used no mortar at all, which told him everything: they'd trusted weight and fit, which meant the whole structure was self-locking, which meant you couldn't blow it without bringing half the canyon with it. But the self-locking also meant there had to be a release somewhere. Machines that lock have keys. He followed the sluice grooves down, down, to where they met the channel floor, and there—behind a curtain of flowstone that had grown over it like a scar—he found a recess the size of a bread oven.

Inside the recess was a bar. Iron, newer than the masonry, older than Tam. A lever, wedged in place with a stone chock.

Someone had left the door locked but kept the key where a working hand could reach it.

"Well," Tam said to himself, alone in the dark with his lamp and his aching shoulders, "that's either very welcoming or a trap, and in my experience it's usually both."

* * *

They gathered at dusk. No fire—they were past the point where fires were safe, or at least past the point where Dominic would allow one—and the lamps made a small island of yellow in all that black stone. Tam showed them the lever. Ilsa checked the channel floor above them for signs of seepage and pronounced it dry as an old bone. Juno copied the spiral carvings into her notebook, page after page, and muttered comparisons under her breath that Tam only half-followed—river cults, weir-priests, some dynasty called the Sarn that nobody had ruled anything in eight hundred years.

"It's not a tomb," Juno said at last, looking up. Her voice had a strange, careful quality, like someone carrying a full cup across a moving deck. "Tombs are sealed from outside. This is sealed from inside. There's a maintenance gallery—I can see the joint lines behind the sluice channels. People lived in there, or worked in there, long enough to leave soot. This is a *building*. Maybe a whole complex. And it's been waiting."

"For what?" Ilsa asked.

Nobody answered that. The river answered for them, a long low pressure-note through the stone, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Dominic stood in front of the lever recess with her arms crossed. Tam had learned to read her silences the way he read stone—by what was absent. She wasn't afraid. Fear made people talk. She was measuring.

"One person pulls it," she said finally. "Everyone else stands clear of the face. If anything comes out of that gap that isn't air, we run, we don't fight, we don't stop for gear." She looked around at each of them. "Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Ilsa.

"Agreed," said Juno.

Tam spat into the dust, which was as close to a signature as he ever gave. "One condition. If it's treasure, I get first pick of the small shiny things. Big things sink boats."

The almost-smile that crossed Dominic's face lasted less than a second, but Tam counted it anyway. He'd been keeping count since Callow's Bend. Four, now, including the one she'd given Ilsa over the card game and the one she hadn't known she'd given Juno when the girl recited river-lore from memory. Small numbers. But growing.

He took the lever's grip in both hands. The iron was cold enough to burn.

"On three," he said. "One. Two—"

He pulled on two, because three gave people time to flinch, and flinching was how you lost fingers.

* * *

The sound was nothing like he expected. He'd braced for grinding, for the shriek of stone on stone, for the groan of a mechanism waking after centuries. Instead there was a soft, deep *whump*, felt more than heard, like the whole wall exhaling. Dust breathed out of the seams in a pale curtain. The lamp flames bent toward the gap.

Then the center section of the wall—six courses wide, four high, a slab that must have weighed more than a loaded barge—swung inward. Slowly. Smoothly. On hinges, or something like hinges, that had been greased within living memory.

Greased. Within living memory. Tam filed that away in the drawer where he kept facts that would keep him up at night.

Beyond the swinging slab: darkness, and cold clean air that smelled of stone and iron and, faintly, impossibly, of lamp oil.

Ilsa went in first, because of course she did, rope around her waist, lamp held high. Tam followed with the second lamp. The passage beyond the gate was tall enough for three men standing on each other's shoulders and floored with fitted stone swept clean—swept *clean*, which meant brooms, which meant hands, which meant—

"Lamp oil," Juno said behind him, and her voice cracked on it. "Tam. Do you smell—"

"I smell it."

The passage opened into a chamber, and the chamber stopped Ilsa dead, and Tam walked into her back because he wasn't watching where he was going anymore either.

Racks lined the walls. Wooden racks, iron-braced, and on them: tools. Picks and pry bars, coiled rope hung in neat hanks, canvas packs, tin lanterns, crates stenciled with faded lettering. A workbench along the far wall with a vice bolted to it and a ledger lying open, weighted down with a hammer to keep the pages flat. A cold-lamp array, the kind survey crews used, mounted on a tripod and pointed at a section of wall covered in chalk marks—measurements, arrows, a spiral drawn large and annotated in a cramped, slanting hand.

Everything arranged. Everything deliberate. Not abandoned—*staged*, the way a man lays out his best clothes before a funeral, the way a woman sets a table for guests she knows are coming.

"Meridian issue," Ilsa said. She lifted a lantern off its hook and turned it over. Stamped on the base: the Institute's compass-rose mark. "These are Institute crates. Institute tools. First-expedition equipment."

"They were funded by the Institute," Juno said. "Of course they—"

"No." Ilsa set the lantern down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something that might go off. "Look at the arrangement. Salvage crews don't rack their gear like this. Armories look like this. *Messages* look like this." She turned to Dominic, who had come through the gate last and now stood just inside the chamber, very still, her lamp lowered. "Someone wanted whoever broke through here to find exactly this. Exactly laid out. Exactly preserved."

"The ledger," Juno whispered.

Dominic crossed to the bench. Her boots sounded loud in all that silence. She reached out and turned the ledger so its pages faced the room, and Tam saw her hands—steady, always steady, those digging hands of hers—and saw, for the first time since he'd known her, that they were trembling.

The open page was dated. Twenty-two years ago. The ink had faded to brown but the letters stood sharp, written fast, written by someone who knew they might not get a second draft:

*If you are reading this, you came the hard way, and you are probably mine.*

Below that, in the same cramped slanting hand Tam had seen on the chalk wall:

*The lower chambers hold the reason. Come prepared to be lied to. Bring nothing you cannot carry swimming.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Juno said the name anyway, barely above breath, the way you say a prayer or an accusation:

"Brightwater."

The word filled the chamber. Tam watched Dominic stand over her mother's handwriting, twenty-two years old and waiting for her like a chair pulled out at a table, and he understood suddenly, completely, why the woman had spent a decade running. You could outrun a name. You could not outrun a room that had been expecting you since before you were grown.

"She knew," Ilsa said softly. "Whoever came next. She knew someone would follow."

"Not someone," Tam said. He nodded at the ledger, at the date, at the woman reading it. "Her. Look at the layout again. Tools on the left for a right-handed climber. Rope lengths cut for someone five-six, maybe five-seven." He shrugged, uncomfortable with the shape of what he was saying but saying it anyway, because uncomfortable truths were the only kind worth hauling. "This room was set for a specific person. Height, handedness, habits. Somebody who'd come the hard way and need everything ready because she wouldn't have time to prepare."

Dominic hadn't moved. In the lamplight her face was unreadable, carved, a wall of her own—but her eyes were wet, and she let them be, and somehow that was worse and better than anything.

"She left me a toolkit," Dominic said at last. Her voice came out rough, scraped clean. "Twenty-two years ago. Before I—" She stopped. Started again. "She packed my gear before I knew I'd ever need it."

The lamps flickered. Somewhere far above, faint as a memory, came a sound none of them wanted to hear: a deep, percussive thud, rolling down through miles of stone.

Tam's head came up. Ilsa's did too. They'd both heard that sound before, in different wars, different canyons. It had only ever meant one thing.

"Somebody's blasting," Ilsa said.

From above. From the upper canyon. From the direction of the Meridian camp.

Dominic closed the ledger gently, took it off the bench, and tucked it inside her jacket, over her heart, like a letter she intended to answer.

"Then we'd better find out what my mother left us," she said, "before Halvard finds a faster way in."
