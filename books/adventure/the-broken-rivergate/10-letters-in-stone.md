## 10. Letters in Stone

The tools in the outer chamber had been arranged like a message. The markers deeper in were arranged like a letter.

Dominic saw the difference within an hour of pushing past the first gate, and it changed something in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet. Her mother had been a surveyor before she was anything else—before wife, before mother, before the name that Dominic had spent ten years scrubbing off every manifest and work contract she'd ever signed. Surveyors thought in marks. In cairns and chisels and paint. And these marks, cut into the limestone walls of the passage beyond the drowned stairwell, were not random. They repeated. They spaced themselves at intervals too regular for erosion and too deliberate for vandalism.

"Stop," she said, and the word came out rougher than she intended.

Tam halted mid-stride, lantern swinging. Behind him, Ilsa's boots splashed to a standstill in the ankle-deep water that had followed them since the second pool. Juno pressed close, her copy of Ede's journal clutched against her chest like a breviary.

"What is it?" Ilsa asked.

Dominic lifted her own lamp to the wall. The stone was pale, veined with mineral ghost-lines, and across it ran a sequence of chisel cuts: three short strokes, a gap, a long diagonal, a gap, a circle bisected by a horizontal bar.

"I've seen this before," she said quietly. "On my father's field kit. On the inside of his trunk lid."

"You're sure?" Tam asked.

"My father taught me to read before he taught me to ride." She traced the air above the strokes without touching them. "It's not a language. It's shorthand. Expedition code—the kind you use when you can't risk leaving anything legible for whoever comes after you. He used it on supply inventories. She used it on—" Dominic stopped. Swallowed. "On everything important."

Juno stepped forward, breath fogging in the cold air of the canyon. "Can you read it?"

"Some of it." Dominic kept her eyes on the wall because looking at Juno meant looking at hope, and hope was a currency she'd learned not to carry. "Three short strokes means *survey point*. The diagonal is *proceed*. The circle with the bar—" Her finger hovered. "That one I know best. It means *do not follow*."

Silence pooled in the chamber like the water around their boots.

"Well," Tam said at last, into the quiet, "that's friendly."

"It's not a warning for us." Dominic lowered her hand. "It's a warning for anyone who came after without knowing why. It's a warning for Meridian."

---

They moved slower after that. Every fifty paces, another marker: *survey point*, *water rising*, *seal intact*, *do not follow*. Dominic read them aloud in a low voice, translating her parents' decade-dead shorthand into plain words while the others listened the way people listen to scripture they half-doubt. The passage descended in long switchbacks, the river audible now beneath their feet—a deep, patient rushing, like something breathing under a floor.

It was Juno who noticed the pattern first, because Juno noticed patterns the way other people noticed weather.

"These aren't waymarkers," she said, stopping at a junction where two passages split around a fallen slab. "Look at the spacing. They get denser as we go down. That's not navigation—that's *narrative*. She's telling a story in order. Start to finish." Her voice cracked slightly on the last word. "We're reading it in the right direction."

Dominic looked at the junction markers. The left-hand passage bore a single stroke: *dry route*. The right-hand passage bore three: *survey point*, *water rising*, *seal intact*.

Her mother was telling them which door mattered.

"The right side," Dominic said.

---

The right-hand passage opened, forty minutes later, into a gallery that stopped even Tam Ovid—who talked through everything, including near-drownings and detonations—dead silent.

It was a natural cavern the size of a chapel, ribbed with flowstone that caught their lamplight and threw it back amber and gold. But it was the walls that held them. Someone had worked this room. Not mined it—*finished* it. Channels had been cut into the floor in branching lines, precise as veins, all of them running toward a central basin of dark water that lay perfectly still despite the river they could hear roaring somewhere below. Along the channels, at intervals, small brass fittings sat green with age, and from the fittings, corroded cables of woven wire ran up into the darkness of the ceiling.

"It's a machine," Ilsa said softly. "Or part of one."

"It's a heart," Juno said.

Dominic didn't answer. She was staring at the far wall, where the markers changed character entirely. No more shorthand. These were full inscriptions, cut deep and clean in careful block letters—in ordinary, readable script, in her mother's unmistakable hand. She knew that hand the way she knew her own reflection. She'd seen it on birthday cards, on grocery lists, on the last postcard that had ever come.

She walked to the wall as if walking into deep water.

The inscription began without preamble, the way her mother had done everything:

> **TO WHOEVER FINDS THIS — AND I PRAY IT IS NO ONE FROM THE INSTITUTE —**
>
> **WE CAME LOOKING FOR STONE AND FOUND A LIVING THING.**
>
> **THE RIVERGATE IS NOT A RUIN. IT IS AN ORGAN.**

Dominic's lamp trembled. She steadied it with both hands.

Below, the inscription continued, block after block, wrapping around the gallery wall like a sentence that had waited twenty years to be finished:

> **THE AQUIFER BENEATH THE RIVERGATE IS NOT DEAD WATER IN DEAD ROCK. IT MOVES. IT RESPONDS. THE CHANNELS YOU ARE STANDING BETWEEN ARE NOT PLUMBING — THEY ARE CIRCULATION. WE WATCHED THE FLOW SHIFT WHEN TAM'S DRILL BIT TOO DEEP ON DAY NINE. THE WHOLE SYSTEM REROUTED AWAY FROM THE WOUND OVER SIX HOURS. STONE DOES NOT DO THAT.**
>
> **MERIDIAN KNOWS. THEIR SURVEY REPORTS CALL IT 'HYDROLOGICAL ANOMALY 7' AND RECOMMEND 'AGGRESSIVE EXTRACTION TRIALS.' AGGRESSIVE EXTRACTION MEANS OPEN CUTS. IT MEANS STRIP-MINING THE UPPER CHAMBERS AND DRAINING THE LOWER ONES TO GET AT THE MINERAL DEPOSITS THE FLOW HAS LAID DOWN OVER TEN THOUSAND YEARS.**
>
> **THEY WILL KILL IT FOR THE SCARS IT LEFT BEHIND.**

Dominic heard Juno make a small sound behind her. She didn't turn around. She couldn't have turned around if the gallery were collapsing.

There was more. Her mother had written smaller here, the letters tighter, as if the stone itself were pressing in:

> **I HAVE ARGUED WITH THEM FOR THREE MONTHS. HALVARD'S PREDECESSOR — A MAN CALLED CREEL — TOLD ME TODAY THAT THE CONCESSION PAPERS WILL BE SIGNED WHETHER I APPROVE OR NOT, AND THAT MY CREW'S CONTRACTS RUN THROUGH MERIDIAN, WHICH MEANS OUR WAGES, OUR INSURANCE, OUR WAY HOME. HE WAS POLITE ABOUT IT. THEY ARE ALWAYS POLITE.**
>
> **SO I HAVE MADE A DECISION THAT ONLY MY HUSBAND UNDERSTANDS, AND HE HAS AGREED TO HELP ME, WHICH IS WHY I AM WRITING THIS ALONE WHILE HE SLEEPS.**
>
> **THE LOWER CHAMBERS CAN BE FLOODED. DELIBERATELY. PERMANENTLY. THE OLD GATES WERE BUILT FOR IT — SOMEONE BEFORE US KNEW WHAT THIS PLACE WAS AND BUILT A WAY TO PUT IT TO SLEEP. IF THE LOWER GALLERIES GO UNDER, THE ACCESS SHAFTS COLLAPSE WITHIN A SEASON, AND THE AQUIFER CLOSES LIKE A FIST. MERIDIAN CAN DIG ALL THEY WANT ABOVE. THEY WILL NEVER REACH THE LIVING PART.**

Dominic's knees went strange. Ilsa moved toward her without a word and stood close—not touching, just there, a warm presence at her shoulder in the cold gold light.

The final blocks were cut shallower. Hurried. As if written by someone running out of time or courage or both:

> **IF YOU ARE READING THIS, THEN EITHER I FAILED OR I SUCCEEDED, AND I WON'T KNOW WHICH. THE FLOODING PROCEDURE REQUIRES A MANUAL OVERRIDE AT THE INNER GATE — SOMEONE HAS TO OPEN THE SLUICES BY HAND AND HOLD THEM UNTIL THE CHAMBERS FILL. THE WATER COMES FAST AND THERE IS NO WAY TO HOLD THE LEVERS AND ALSO LEAVE.**
>
> **I HAVE THOUGHT ABOUT WHO THAT SHOULD BE FOR ELEVEN DAYS. I HAVE RUN EVERY OTHER OPTION AND THERE IS NO OTHER OPTION.**
>
> **DO NOT FOLLOW ME. DO NOT MOURN ME LOUDLY. LET THEM CALL IT AN ACCIDENT. AN ACCIDENT PROTECTS THE WORK; A MARTYR INVITES AN INQUEST, AND AN INQUEST INVITES MERIDIAN'S LAWYERS, AND THEY WILL UNMAKE EVERYTHING I DID AND CALL IT ENGINEERING FAILURE.**
>
> **LET THE RIVER KEEP ITS OWN.**
>
> **— M. BRIGHTWATER**

The silence afterward lasted so long that Dominic could hear her own pulse in her ears, could hear the underground river breathing beneath the floor, could hear Tam set his lantern down very carefully on the stone as if his hands had become unreliable.

"That's not possible," Juno said finally. Her voice was shredded. "That's not—she didn't die in a collapse. Everyone says—every account, every memorial, the Institute's official report—"

"The Institute wrote the report." Dominic heard her own voice as if from a distance. Flat. Clear. Strange. "Of course they called it an accident. She *told* them to call it an accident." She laughed once, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "She spent her last hours carving instructions for how to lie about her own death."

"Dominic." Ilsa's hand found her arm. Warm. Solid. "Breathe."

"She died holding a lever open." Dominic turned and looked at them, and her eyes were dry, which somehow made it worse. "In the dark. Alone. Holding a gate open so the water would come and bury the thing they wanted to steal. And the whole world—the whole *world*—spent twenty years calling it bad luck. Calling *her* careless. Calling the Brightwater expedition the one that got greedy and dug too deep." Her voice rose and broke. "They said my father died because of her ambition. They printed that. They *taught* me that."

Juno had both hands over her mouth. Above them, tears cut clean lines through the dust on her face.

"I built my whole life on that story," Dominic said. "Every town I left. Every name I didn't give. Every crew I quit before they got curious. Ten years of running from a woman who—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum as if something in there needed holding in place. "Who gave everything she had to protect a river. And let me believe she was just... reckless."

* * *

They camped in the gallery. Nobody suggested moving on; nobody could have.

Tam built a small stove-fire in the basin's lee, and Ilsa heated water for tea that no one drank, and Juno sat against the flowstone with Ede Voss's journal open on her knees, cross-referencing with shaking hands.

"He knew," she said at last. "Ede. Listen—this entry, dated four days after your mother's last marker." She read aloud, her voice steadying as it went: "*M.B. came to my camp tonight. Asked me to hold a map and a promise. Said if she didn't come back, someone should finish the sealing properly—check the gates every few years, keep the upper works maintained so the flood levels stay true. I told her she was asking a lot of a stranger. She said she wasn't. She said I was the only man on this river who'd ever refused Meridian money.*"

Dominic stared into the fire. "He took the promise and then he never finished it. He sat on that map for twenty years until he was dying."

"Because finishing it meant coming down here," Tam said quietly. "And he saw what it cost."

"No." Dominic shook her head slowly. "Because finishing it meant becoming her. And he couldn't. That's not a judgment. I couldn't either, for ten years." She looked up. "The promise was never to check the gates. The promise was to understand why."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, the river rushed on, indifferent and alive.

"So Creel signed the concession papers anyway?" Ilsa asked. "After she flooded it?"

"They couldn't reach the aquifer, so the concession became worthless on paper. Meridian wrote it off as a failed survey, buried the real reports, and let the Brightwater name take the blame for the failure." Dominic's jaw tightened. "Clean. Efficient. Exactly what she predicted. An accident protects the work—but it also protects *them*. She bought the aquifer's life with her own death and with mine, in a way. With the name. She spent our name like coin."

"And Halvard?" Juno asked. "Why is he here now? Why reopen a dead concession?"

Dominic was quiet for a moment. Then: "Because dead concessions don't send surveyors. Because he didn't ask about minerals when we spoke—he asked about water levels. Because Meridian doesn't spend money chasing twenty-year-old failures unless someone upstairs has decided the failure isn't one anymore." She fed a stick to the fire. "Either they've found new evidence the aquifer is reachable, or they've found evidence it's still *alive*, and either way they want what my mother drowned."

"Then we're in a race," Tam said.

"We've been in a race since Callow's Bend." Dominic stood. Her legs ached; her eyes burned; and beneath all of it, beneath the grief and the fury, something else had shifted into place with the solid, irreversible click of a lock finding its key. For ten years she had carried a shame that was never hers. Now she carried its replacement, and the replacement weighed less. "But now we know what's at stake. Not ruins. Not treasure. A living thing that her mother—" She corrected herself, and the correction felt like setting down a heavy pack. "*My* mother killed herself to protect. We're not finishing Ede's promise anymore."

Juno looked up. "Then what are we doing?"

Dominic looked at the inscription one last time—at the shallow, hurried letters, at the signature that was also her own name—and felt the strangest sensation, like a hand being placed on her shoulder from twenty years away.

"We're keeping it," she said.

* * *

They slept in shifts, and Dominic took the last watch alone, sitting with her back against the flowstone beneath her mother's words, listening to the river breathe.

Near dawn—or what she guessed was dawn, down where the sun was only a rumor—Ilsa woke and came to sit beside her without a word. They watched the dark together for a while.

"You're different," Ilsa said eventually.

"I'm lighter. Is that terrible? She's dead and I feel *lighter*."

"It's not terrible." Ilsa's shoulder settled against hers. "You were carrying something that was never yours. Putting it down isn't betrayal."

Dominic exhaled slowly. "She planned it. All of it. Even the lie. Even me." Her voice caught. "She knew I'd grow up believing the worst version, and she let it happen, because the truth was worth more than my forgiveness. How do you forgive someone for that?"

"Maybe you don't have to." Ilsa nodded toward the wall, toward the last line. *Let the river keep its own.* "Maybe you just finish the sentence."

Before Dominic could answer, a sound rolled down from the upper passages—distant, muffled, but unmistakable to everyone in that gallery. A deep percussive thud, felt in the floor more than heard, followed by a long settling groan of stone.

Tam was on his feet instantly, lantern raised. "That came from above. From the direction of the first gate."

Another thud. Closer. Dust sifted from the ceiling and drifted through their lamplight like gray snow.

"Blasting," Ilsa said grimly. "Somebody's opening the canyon the fast way."

Dominic rose, and the last of the grief in her chest hardened into something with edges. Nine days, Halvard had promised her. He'd lied—or the melt had beaten them both.

"Nobody blasts their way toward a flooded ruin unless they already know exactly what's down here," she said. "Which means he's not following Ede's map." She looked at each of them in turn. "He's following hers."
