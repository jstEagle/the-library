## 11. Ilsa's Crossing

The river spoke to Ilsa Rook before it did anything else. That was the first thing her grandfather had taught her at the ferry crossing, before she was tall enough to see over the gunwale: *listen with your hands on the water, not your ears.* A river told you what it intended a half-mile before it meant it, if you knew how to read the tremor in the current, the way debris drifted a finger's width off true.

She had been reading rivers for thirty-one years. She should have read this one.

The drowned canyon narrowed that morning into a slot of green stone where the water ran fast and shallow over slick cobbles, loud enough that they had to shout to talk. Dominic moved ahead with Ede's map held open against her chest, Juno behind her calling out measurements, Tam hauling the supply sledge with the grim patience of a man who had once blown a bridge and spent two years apologizing for it. Ilsa took the rear, as she always did, because the rear was where you watched.

And the rear was where she saw it.

Not water. Light. A change in the quality of the light coming down the slot canyon behind them—a yellowish thickening, like the air itself had turned to glass. She had seen that light exactly twice in her life. Once the year the millpond dam let go upcountry, and once in a dream she'd never told anyone about, the one where she drowned holding a rope.

"Dom." Her voice came out flat, the way it did when her body knew before her mind did. "Dom, *run.*"

Dominic turned. Ilsa pointed upstream, back the way they'd come, where the slot walls rose sheer and close and there was nowhere to run to at all.

The sound arrived then. Not a roar—something worse, a deep percussive *shove*, like a giant shoulder against a door. Around the bend above them came the front of it: not a wave so much as a wall of brown water carrying whole trees, boulders the size of carts, a churn so dense it looked almost solid, almost climbable, almost *safe* compared to what it actually was.

There was a shelf. Ilsa saw it in the half-second she had—a ledge four feet up the left wall, maybe six feet deep, worn into the stone by some ancient eddy. She saw Tam reach it, hauling Juno up by the wrist. She saw Dominic turn back for her.

She also saw the sledge.

The sledge held their rope, half their food, the medical kit, Tam's charges, the spare lamp oil. It was tethered to her own harness because she was the strongest swimmer and the rear guard and because three days ago Dominic had asked, *can you manage it?* and Ilsa had said *yes* the way she said yes to everything, because she had spent her whole life being the one who could manage it.

The water hit her like a building falling on her sideways.

* * *

Cold first. Cold so total it wasn't a sensation but an absence, a white nothing where her skin had been. Then the tumbling—cobbles under her back, her shoulder, her skull, the world rotating in brown chaos—and then the sledge's tether snapped taut against her harness and the river tried to take her apart at the waist.

She had a knife on her belt. She knew she had a knife on her belt. Her hands wouldn't find it. The water held her under, rolled her, slammed her against the wall of the slot, and the tether wrenched her spine like a man trying to tear a root from the ground, and somewhere above all of it, impossibly, through all of it, she heard Dominic shouting her name.

*Ilsa.* Not *Rook.* Not *ferrywoman.* Her name, torn thin by the flood, and Ilsa thought, with the strange clarity of a person who has perhaps four seconds to live: *she came back for me. She always said she wouldn't. She came back.*

Her fingers found the knife.

She cut herself free of the sledge and the river took her like a debt collected, spat her downstream through the slot in a series of hard collisions she would feel for weeks, and then—because the river was not done being merciful and not done being cruel—thrust her against that same shelf of stone, and a hand closed on her wrist.

Dominic's hand. Dominic lying flat on the ledge with half her body hanging into the flood, soaked to the bone, hair plastered black across her face, hauling with both arms and all of whatever she was made of, and Ilsa's boots found a toehold and she climbed her own weight up onto stone and lay there gasping like a landed fish while the world roared past six inches from her face.

"You *idiot,*" Ilsa coughed.

"You cut the sledge." Dominic was shaking. Ilsa could feel it through the stone where their shoulders touched. "You cut it and you didn't cut your own harness first. You cut the *supplies* free first."

"Supplies don't drown well."

Dominic made a sound that was half laugh, half something else, and pressed her forehead briefly against Ilsa's shoulder, and then pulled away and became the expedition leader again, all edges and orders, and Ilsa lay on the wet stone a moment longer and let herself feel the shape of what had just passed between them before she folded it up small and put it somewhere she didn't look.

* * *

They lost the sledge. That was the arithmetic of it, tallied by lamplight that evening in a dry chamber Juno found forty yards on, a hollow in the canyon wall above the new flood line, floored with old sand and dry enough to strike a fire if Tam was careful.

Half their food, gone downstream. All the rope beyond what they carried on their backs. The medical kit. Six of Tam's twelve charges. The spare lamp oil, which meant from here on every light was rationed, every dark passage a budget.

"Nine days," Tam said. He was wrapping his hands in strips of shirt, a habit he had when numbers went bad. "Halvard said the river rises in nine days. That was three days ago. And it's already flashing."

"It's melt," Juno said. "Upcountry snow. The heat came early this year." She said it evenly, but Ilsa watched her hands on Ede's journal, the way her thumb kept moving over the cover like a woman touching a scar.

"So we move faster," Dominic said. "Lighter. We were carrying too much anyway."

"We weren't carrying too much," Tam said. "We were carrying *enough.* There's a difference, Dom, and the river just ate the difference."

"I know what the river ate." Dominic's voice had an edge to it, and then, visibly, she took it back. She looked at Ilsa across the fire. "Ilsa. Say it straight. Can you go on?"

Ilsa had been asking herself the same question since they'd climbed out of the slot. Her ribs ached where the tether had wrenched her. Her left shoulder had a deep bruise blooming that she could feel with every breath. She was thirty-one years old and she had spent those years being the strong one, the capable one, the yes, and every instinct she owned said *say yes, say you're fine, don't be the reason anyone turns back.*

"I can go on," she said. "But I want it on the record that the next time the river offers me a shelf, I'm taking it without a sledge attached."

That got a real laugh out of all of them, even Tam, even Juno, and the chamber felt warmer for it.

Later, when the others slept, Ilsa sat up. She always slept light near water; it wasn't a choice, it was an inheritance, same as her shoulders and her grandfather's rope-callused hands. The fire had burned to coals. Dominic was on watch, sitting near the chamber mouth with her back to the stone, and Ilsa got up—ribs protesting—and went and sat beside her.

Neither of them said anything for a while. Below them, the river had dropped again, settling back into its channel, muttering to itself like a man apologizing.

"You went back in," Ilsa said finally. "After the sledge. Tam said you tried to go back in for it. Juno had to hold your pack."

"It was half our food."

"You went back into a flash flood for *food.*"

"For the charges. For the rope. For—" Dominic stopped. The coal-light moved on her face. "It wasn't for the supplies."

Ilsa looked at her. Dominic Brightwater, who had spent ten days being a locked door, who answered every personal question with a route plan, who flinched from her own surname like it was a hot iron—the woman sat there looking at the water with her jaw tight, having just said the most honest thing Ilsa had heard from her since Callow's Bend.

"I know," Ilsa said.

"You don't—"

"Ilsa Rook doesn't need things explained to her, Dom. I run a ferry. I know what it means when somebody ties their line to yours." She kept her eyes on the river. It was easier for both of them that way, looking at the water instead of each other. "My grandfather used to say the crossing doesn't care how strong one rope is. It cares whether two ropes are braided."

"That's a terrible metaphor."

"It's a *ferryman's* metaphor. We're not poets. We're people who keep other people from drowning."

Dominic huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "When I saw you go under. I thought—" She didn't finish it. Her hand was on the stone between them, and Ilsa looked at it, at the rope-burn across the palm, at the digging hands Ede Voss had recognized in a boarding house, and she covered it with her own.

Dominic didn't pull away. That was the whole conversation, right there. That was the entire speech.

"Nine days," Ilsa said softly. "Maybe six now."

"Then we don't waste them." But Dominic turned her hand over, laced her fingers through Ilsa's, and held on, and for a long time neither of them moved, and the river ran on below them, and Ilsa thought: *duty says keep moving. Duty says we're on a clock, we're down to half our food, there's a man with dynamite behind us and a flood ahead. Duty says all of that.*

*Duty can wait until morning.*

She slept then, finally, deeply, with her shoulder against Dominic's shoulder, and for the first time since Callow's Bend she did not dream of drowning.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, and with it came the sound that ended the warmth of the night before: a far-off, rhythmic *crack*, rolling down the canyon like slow thunder.

Juno was already at the chamber mouth when Ilsa woke, journal forgotten in her hands, her face gone the color of the stone.

"That's not the river," she said.

Tam reached them at a half-run, counting under his breath as each crack rolled through. "Blasting. Pattern charges, spaced—someone's opening the upper canyon." He looked at Dominic, and Ilsa watched the whole crew arrive at the same arithmetic at the same moment. "Halvard's not waiting for the river anymore. He's making his own door."

Dominic was already rolling the map open against her knee, her finger moving down Ede's inked line, past the slot canyon, past the drowned stair, to a marking near the heart of it—a small, careful symbol Ilsa had noticed days ago and never asked about, because some things on a map you ask about and some things you wait.

"How far?" Dominic said.

"Half a day," Juno said quietly. "Maybe less, if we don't stop."

"Then we don't stop." Dominic looked up, and Ilsa saw the night's softness folded away somewhere safe, and under it something new—something that looked, for the first time since she'd known her, less like a woman running and more like one arriving. "Eat while we walk. Everything left goes on our backs. And Ilsa—"

"I know." Ilsa was already knotting the last of the rope across her chest. "I read the water. You read the map."

They stepped out of the chamber into the gray morning, four of them with half their supplies and all of their secrets, and somewhere above and behind them the Institute's thunder kept rolling down the canyon, closer with every hour, herding them all toward the place where Ede Voss had drawn that small, careful mark—and where, Ilsa was beginning to suspect, whatever her mother had sealed away was still waiting, patient as water, to be found.
