## 12. The Turncoat

Juno Pell had never been good at lying, which was why she had gotten so much practice.

It started small, the way all corruptions do. A smile held a half-second too long when Dominic asked about the morning's route. A casual mention of the day's mileage that she later realized she'd never been asked for. She told herself these were nothing—social lubricant, the oil of a small crew living in each other's pockets—but at night, in her bedroll against the canyon wall with the river muttering below, she lay awake and catalogued each one like a scholar cataloguing fragments, and the fragments were assembling into a shape she didn't want to read.

*You haven't done anything yet,* she told herself. *Thinking isn't betrayal. Wanting isn't betrayal.*

The wanting, though. The wanting had teeth.

It had teeth because of the letter. The third letter, the one she'd found folded inside her mother's copy of Brightwater's field notes—the notes Dominic carried now, wrapped in oilcloth, touched like scripture. Juno had read that letter forty times in the years since the expedition was declared lost. She knew it the way she knew her own handwriting, because in a sense it was her own handwriting, or the handwriting she'd modeled herself on: the small precise capitals, the way her mother crossed her sevens.

*If the work is buried with me,* the letter read, *then it was never worth the digging. Tell the story or the story tells itself wrong.*

Her mother, Wren Pell, had been the expedition's cartographer and archivist—the same role Juno played now, a symmetry she had built her whole life around. When the Brightwater expedition vanished into the Broken Rivergate eleven years ago, the town of Callow's Bend had mourned, then gossiped, then forgotten. The Meridian Institute had published a tidy casualty report: equipment failure, seasonal flood, regrettable loss. No monument. No archive. Wren Pell's maps were never returned to the family. Her letters were sealed as evidence and then, when Juno's petition finally cracked them open, mostly redacted.

*Tell the story or the story tells itself wrong.*

And here was the story, walking around in boots, refusing to tell it.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow above the river channel, where the canyon widened just enough to hold a fire without advertising it. Tam cooked—some unholy amalgam of dried fish and foraged greens that he insisted was stew—and Ilsa cleaned the ferry winch chain by firelight, link by link, her hands moving with the patience of someone who had spent a life maintaining things against the water's argument.

Dominic sat apart, as she always did, the map spread across her knees, Ede Voss's ink and Wren Pell's ink overlapping in the firelight like two conversations held eleven years apart.

Juno watched her and felt the old ache rise. *She has it all and she hoards it.* The letters. The markers in the stone. The truth of what happened down here. Every day they went deeper, and every day Dominic peeled back one more layer for herself and handed the crew nothing but orders.

"We're two days from the second gate," Dominic said, to the fire more than to anyone. "Ede's map shows a maintenance shaft on the east wall, past the weir. If the markers are right, we can come at the inner chamber from above instead of through the flood doors."

"Assuming the shaft hasn't collapsed in eleven years," Tam said.

"Assuming that."

"And assuming," Ilsa said mildly, not looking up from the chain, "that whoever reads those markers reads them the way you do. You've been translating for us, Brightwater. We're taking your word for where we're walking."

The silence that followed had edges. Juno felt them all looking at Dominic, and Dominic looking at the map, and the map saying nothing.

"I've been wrong before," Dominic said at last. "I'll tell you when I know I'm not."

*She's lying,* Juno thought, with a certainty that frightened her. *Or she's not, and it's worse—she's telling the truth and it's still not the whole truth, and she knows exactly what's down there because her mother told her, and she's deciding, all alone, what we get to know.*

That was the rationalization, polished smooth by now, worn like a river stone: *Dominic Brightwater is going to bury it. All of it. The second gate, the aquifer, whatever her parents found—she's going to collapse it behind her and publish nothing, or publish a pamphlet, a footnote, and Wren Pell will stay a name on a casualty report forever. Halvard is a snake, but snakes at least move toward what they want. He'll excavate it properly. He'll put her mother's maps in a museum with a brass plaque. He'll make it matter.*

She hated how reasonable it sounded. That was the thing about rationalization she'd learned this week, hating it: it always sounded like the voice of a sensible friend.

* * *

The signal was Ilsa's idea originally, which was the cruelest part.

Three nights ago, at the last camp before the gorge, Ilsa had shown them how the ferry crews signaled across the water in weather—lantern shutters, a code of long and short. "Old habit," she'd said, hanging the shuttered lamp from a ridgepole. "Half the crossings on this river still talk to each other this way. You'd be surprised what travels."

Juno had asked her to demonstrate. That was all. She'd told herself that was all—a scholar's interest in riverine signal systems, a note for her field journal.

But the next morning, while the others struck camp, she had stood at the canyon's rim with the lamp in her hands and looked downriver, toward the bend where Halvard's survey team had made its camp—visible on clear days as a smudge of canvas and a thread of smoke—and she had counted the shutter-codes in her head until she was sure she had them right.

She hadn't sent anything. Not yet. She told herself that too.

Tonight, though, the fire was low, Tam was asleep with his boots on, Ilsa was on first watch at the channel mouth, and Dominic had taken the map into the rocks to compare it against the stars, the way she did every night, alone, hoarding.

Juno sat with the lamp unlit in her lap and the shutter in her hand and her heart going like a bird in a box.

*If you do this, you can't undo it.* She thought of her mother's letter again. *Tell the story or the story tells itself wrong.*

But there was a second letter. The one Juno had never shown anyone, the one she'd carried folded against her heart for eleven years like a coal she couldn't put down and couldn't hold. The last one. The one that had arrived two weeks after the casualty report, forwarded through three postal systems, water-stained almost past reading, in her mother's hand:

*Juno my heart. If you're reading this then the Institute's version is the only version, which means we failed. What we found under the Rivergate is bigger than any of us and it is not ours. Mr. Halvard's people were meant to arrive with the relief supplies. They did not arrive. Understand what that means. I have to believe someone will finish this honestly. Be brave, but be careful who you're brave for.*

*Be careful who you're brave for.*

Juno had spent eleven years being brave for a ghost. Halvard had come to her boarding house in Callow's Bend six weeks ago with her mother's sealed personnel file open on the table—*your mother's work was extraordinary, Miss Pell; the Institute wants to honor it*—and Juno had heard the brass plaque in his voice and wanted it so badly her hands shook.

*He'll honor it,* she told herself now, thumbing the shutter. *He'll excavate it. He'll name the chamber. Pell Gallery. Brightwater Gate. The story told at last, told wrong, but told.*

*Or told right,* whispered the other voice, the one that sounded like her mother. *You don't know what he'll do. You know what he didn't do. He didn't come when they called for him.*

The lamp stayed dark. She sat with it until the stars wheeled and her legs went numb, and then, hating herself with a thoroughness that felt almost like relief, she lit it.

Three long. Two short. Three long. The code Ilsa had shown her, pointed downriver, toward the bend, toward the smudge of canvas she could no longer see in the dark but knew was there.

It took eleven seconds. She counted them.

Then she shuttered the lamp, and the canyon went black, and somewhere downriver—she couldn't see it, couldn't prove it, would never be able to prove it—a light answered. One long. Two short. One long.

Acknowledged.

Juno sat in the dark with her hands over her mouth and did not make a sound.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, and with it, work. Nobody looked at her differently, because nobody knew, and the not-knowing sat in Juno's chest like swallowed glass. She transcribed markers. She sketched the weir's stonework. She laughed at Tam's impression of Halvard's survey-team foreman, a man who apparently said "per the concession terms" the way other men said *damn.* She was fine. She was so fine it made her sick.

It was near midday, at the maintenance shaft, that everything tilted.

The shaft was everything Ede's map promised: a hand-cut gallery in the east wall, choked with rubble but passable, sloping down toward the second gate. Dominic went first with the lantern, then Tam, then Ilsa, with Juno last, cataloguing as she climbed down—her job, her shelter, the thing she was good at.

Twenty feet in, the rubble thinned, and Juno's lantern found the wall, and the wall found her.

Markers. Dozens of them. Not the weathered ancient glyphs they'd been following for days—these were fresh by comparison, cut with steel tools, in a cipher her mother had invented and used only in her private field notes. Juno had spent three years of her life learning that cipher from the few unredacted pages the Institute had released. She could read it the way she read her own name.

She read.

*Day 41. The chamber is alive. The water moves with intention—not current, intention. Halvard's advance team reached the lower galleries on day 38. They are not here for the survey. I have seen their drilling equipment. I have seen what they intend to do to the aquifer to make it pay. R. says we seal it. I say we seal it and we tell no one, because everyone we tell will be bought. Forgive us, whoever reads this. Forgive us for choosing the water over the truth.*

*Forgive us for choosing the water over the truth.*

Juno stood in the dark with her lantern shaking and read it four times, and each time the last line rearranged itself, and each time it said the same thing.

Her mother hadn't died hiding the truth from the world. She had died hiding it *from the Institute*—from the brass plaque, the museum, the named chamber, the whole machinery of honor that Juno had been ready to sell the gate to buy. *Everyone we tell will be bought.* Her mother had looked at the world, at the world that included Juno's own hungry wanting, and had chosen the water.

"Juno." Dominic's voice, from below. "Find anything?"

Juno closed her eyes. Opened them. Made her voice work.

"Field notes," she called back. "Her cipher. It's—there's a lot here. It'll take me time."

A pause. Then Dominic's boots on stone, coming back up.

Juno looked at the marker one more time—*forgive us*—and then, with her sleeve, with a motion so quick it seemed to belong to someone else, she smeared wet grit across the last line, blurring the cut letters into the stone's own weathering.

When Dominic's lantern light reached her, Juno was crouched over a different marker, one higher up, pointing at it, talking about water levels and day-counts, her voice steady, her scholarship immaculate, the lie coming easier than it ever had before, which was its own kind of damnation.

* * *

Dominic read for a long time. Juno watched her read—watched the lantern light move across her face, watched her fingers hover over the cuts in the stone the way you'd hover over a wound. She was looking for something in those glyphs. Juno knew what: her mother. Her mother's voice, her mother's reasons, the answer to the question that had chased her across a decade of border towns.

And Juno had just decided, in eleven seconds of lamplight and a smear of grit, what Dominic would be allowed to find.

*You're protecting her,* said the sensible friend. *She'll collapse the gate and spend her life thinking her mother was a coward or a fool. You're giving her time. You're—*

*You're doing exactly what the Institute did,* said her mother. *Redacting. Sealing. Deciding what people can bear to know.*

*They let people die.*

*So are you. You lit a lamp at a man who let people die, because he promised you a plaque.*

Juno pressed her thumbnail into the pad of her finger until it hurt. Below them, Ilsa's voice echoed up the shaft—something about the water rising a hand-span since yesterday, something about the melt—and Tam answered, and the crew went on being a crew, and the lie went on being a lie, growing its second skin, its third.

That evening, at camp, Dominic spread the day's findings by firelight: rubbings of the markers, her own translations, the route for tomorrow. Juno contributed. That was the horror of it—she contributed *well*, her cipher-work genuinely indispensable, her readings genuinely correct. She was the crew's best asset and its worst liability and no one knew but her and the dark.

"We're close," Dominic said, and for a moment—just a moment—she looked at Juno across the fire, and something in her face was unreadable in a way it hadn't been before. "Two days. Maybe less, if the melt keeps up."

"Then we should sleep," Ilsa said.

They slept. Juno lay awake.

Somewhere downriver, Halvard's team had received her eleven seconds of light. She didn't know what they'd make of it—whether they'd read it as a stray ferry signal, a trick of the melt, or exactly what it was: *we are here, we are close, come.* She'd learn soon. The knowledge would arrive wearing boots, the way everything in this canyon did.

She turned her mother's last letter over in her mind one final time, looking for permission, and found none, and understood, finally, with a clarity that felt like falling, that there had never been any to find. Her mother had not asked to be honored. She had asked to be *believed*—and belief was a thing Juno had just traded, eleven seconds at a time, for a story she'd been telling herself since she was nineteen years old.

*I can still take it back,* she thought. *Tomorrow. I can tell Dominic. I can—*

Above the camp, on the canyon rim, a rock clattered.

Everyone was awake instantly—Ilsa's hand on her knife, Tam up with a pry-bar, Dominic dousing the fire with one practiced motion, all of them listening to the dark the way animals do. The sound didn't come again. But far down the channel, past the weir, past the bend, a lantern blinked once in the black.

Three long. Two short. Three long.

Her own code, coming back at her.

And beside Juno in the dark, quiet as a closing door, Dominic Brightwater said: "Ilsa. How many people on this river know the ferry codes?"

The silence that followed had edges, and this time Juno was standing on every one of them.
