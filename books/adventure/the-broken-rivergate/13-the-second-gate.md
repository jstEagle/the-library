## 13. The Second Gate

The passage narrowed until they walked in single file, shoulders brushing stone slick as oiled skin. Dominic went first, one hand trailing the wall, the other holding Ede's map low against her chest where the lamplight wouldn't catch it. Behind her came Ilsa, then Tam with the charges wrapped in oilcloth against his back like a sleeping child, then Juno, whose breathing had gone thin and careful since the last marker.

The water was with them again now. Not the river itself—something older, quieter. It seeped through seams in the rock and ran in channels cut so precisely they might have been milled, feeding down toward whatever waited ahead. Her mother's handwriting had appeared on the last three markers, each one a depth gauge, each one more urgent than the last:

*Third chamber holds. Check the weep-line weekly. If the line rises past the red groove, the heart is failing and there is no second chance.*

The red groove was there, cut into the stone beside the final marker, and the weep-line ran a hand's width below it.

Still holding. After twenty-two years, whatever her mother had built was still holding.

"Talk to me, Brightwater," Ilsa said quietly. "Your shoulders have been climbing toward your ears for an hour."

"There's a chamber ahead. The map calls it the heart."

"And?"

"And my mother built a warning system into the walls." Dominic lifted the lamp so the light ran along the milled channel beside them. "She expected someone to come back. Someone who'd need to know if it was failing."

"Expected," Tam said from the rear, and Dominic could hear him chewing on the word the way she'd been chewing on it herself. "That's the part I keep snagging on. Everything in this canyon's been arranged. The tools at the outer gate. The markers. It's like walking into a house where somebody set the table and then never came home."

"Maybe she meant to come back," Juno said.

"No," Dominic said, and heard her own voice come out flat and certain. "She knew she wasn't. That's why she left instructions."

The passage turned, dropped, turned again. The air changed first—cooler, moving, carrying a sound so low Dominic felt it in her teeth before she heard it. A deep, patient rhythm, like something breathing under the world.

Then the passage opened, and the lamp found the heart of the Broken Rivergate.

* * *

The chamber was a cathedral turned inside out.

Water fell from the ceiling in a hundred silver threads, catching the lamplight and scattering it, and where the threads struck the floor they vanished—not pooling, not running off, but sinking into a floor of pale stone veined with channels, all of them converging toward the center of the chamber like the roots of an inverted tree. And at the center, rising out of that converging stone, stood a formation Dominic had no honest word for: a column of rock the width of a wagon, glowing faintly where the water ran over it, humming with that deep subterranean pulse.

The aquifer heart. The living thing the map had led them to. Not a pool, not a river—an organ. The whole canyon above them, the whole watershed, drinking through this stone.

Nobody spoke. Even the water falling seemed to fall more quietly, as if the chamber demanded a certain reverence and the water had learned to give it.

It was Tam who finally broke it, voice hushed to almost nothing. "This is what they wanted to strip-mine."

"This is what they wanted to strip-mine," Dominic confirmed. Her voice sounded wrong in here, too loud, too human. She walked the chamber's edge, lamp high, reading the stone the way she'd learned to read it as a child at her father's knee—looking for tool marks, for cuts, for the story written in what hands had done to what nature built.

She found the story fast.

The channels in the floor weren't all natural. Some had been deepened, redirected, dammed with fitted stone and sealed with a gray mortar that had gone glassy with age. Her mother's work—she knew it before she found the first marker, knew it in the economy of the cuts, the way no stroke was wasted. The whole floor had been reworked into a system: the water gathered, slowed, fed into the heart at a controlled rate. A regulator. Her mother had taken a wild, living thing and taught it to hold its own breath.

And at the chamber's far side, half-hidden behind a curtain of falling water, stood a low stone vault, mortared shut, with a single marker set into its face.

Dominic crossed to it alone. The others hung back—she felt them giving her the room, and was grateful, because her hands had started to shake and she didn't want witnesses.

The marker was in her mother's hand. Not scratched in haste like the depth gauges. This one had been cut carefully, deliberately, the letters deep enough to outlast the mountain:

*To whoever finds this: the Institute knew. Before we ever broke ground, they knew what the Rivergate was. The survey they gave us was falsified. The cores they showed us were from somewhere else. They sent eleven of us to map a treasure they'd already decided to take, and when we found it was alive—when we told them it couldn't survive the extraction—they stopped answering our signals.*

*I sealed the lower chambers myself. My husband held the weir while the water rose. Neither of us was meant to leave, and neither of us did.*

*They will call it an accident. They will call it a tragedy. They will pay for our funerals and fund a memorial and teach their children our names as a caution against ambition. Let them. But if you are reading this, then someone came back, and someone cared enough to dig past the story they told.*

*The heart is not a resource. It is the reason the valley above is green. Take the water and the valley dies. That was always the arithmetic they refused to do.*

*—M. Brightwater*

Dominic stood there a long time. The water fell. The heart hummed.

She had spent ten years building a story to survive inside: that her parents had been reckless, that the dig had been their hubris, that the canyon had simply taken its toll as canyons did, and her mother's silence in the years before—those short letters, that distracted voice on the rare calls home—had been the guilt of a woman who knew she was in too deep. Dominic had carried that story like a stone in her pack, had shaped her whole drifting life around the weight of it.

It had been a lie the whole time. Not her lie. Theirs.

The Meridian Institute had known. Had falsified the survey. Had stopped answering the radio while eleven people sat in a hole in the world asking for help. And then had written the accident report, and paid for the headstones, and let a nine-year-old girl grow up believing her parents' deaths were the price of their own ambition.

The cold that moved through her then wasn't grief. Grief was an old companion; she knew its shape. This was something cleaner and far more dangerous. This was clarity—the kind that comes when fog lifts off a river and you see, all at once, exactly where the rocks are.

Halvard wasn't a bureaucrat doing an ugly job. He was the current end of a decision that had already killed eleven people once. And he was three days behind them with a crew and explosives, coming to finish it.

"Dominic." Ilsa's voice, low, close. Dominic hadn't heard her cross the chamber. "You've been standing there long enough to grow moss. What does it say?"

Dominic realized she'd been holding the marker's edge, her fingers resting in the cut letters the way you'd hold someone's hand. She let go. It cost something to let go.

"It says my mother wasn't careless." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "It says the Institute knew what this was before the first shovel went in, and they sent them anyway, and when my parents told them the truth, the Institute cut the radio and waited."

Juno made a sound behind her—small, wounded. Dominic turned. The archivist had both hands pressed over her mouth, and her eyes were fixed on the vault, and Dominic understood that Juno had spent her whole life curating a legend, and the legend had just died in front of her and been replaced by something harder and truer.

"The memorial," Juno said through her fingers. "The anniversary plaque in Callow's Bend. I used to clean it. I used to—" She stopped. Started again, steadier. "The Institute funded the memorial. I thought it was grief. I thought it was guilt."

"It was a receipt," Dominic said. "You pay for the funeral so nobody asks about the body."

Tam had come forward too, and he was looking at the vault with his demolitions man's eye, and Dominic watched him arrive at the question she was already dreading. "This mortar's the same work as the dams on the floor. Your mother sealed this chamber shut. What's in the vault that needed sealing?"

"I don't know."

"You want to know."

"Yes," Dominic said. "And I want to know what Halvard knows. Because if the Institute's had a copy of my mother's report for twenty-two years—if they've always known where the heart is and what it does—then they didn't need us to find it. They needed us to open it."

The words landed in the chamber and the water kept falling through them, indifferent, patient, alive.

* * *

They made camp at the chamber's edge, back in the last side passage where the falling water couldn't soak their gear, and Dominic laid out everything they'd gathered like a hand of cards she was finally ready to read honestly.

Ede's map. Her mother's markers, transcribed in Juno's careful shorthand. The journal from Ede's last camp. The tools from the outer gate—her father's hammer among them, the handle worn to the shape of his grip. And now the vault, mortared shut, waiting.

"Ede's journal," she said. "Read it again. The parts about the Institute. All of them."

Juno found the pages by lamplight, her thumb moving down the prospector's cramped, deteriorating hand. "'Third day in the gate. Found the regulator floor. Brightwater's work—clean as a surgeon. Whoever she was, she was better than they deserved.'"

"Keep going."

"'The Institute man came through the upper canyon last month. Not a surveyor. He had the old reports. He had her report, the one they buried, the one with the heart in it. He knew the way in better than I did, and I've been coming here eleven years.'" Juno paused. "There's a line scratched out here. He wrote over it. It says—'He asked me if I'd seen the vault. He knew about the vault.'"

The lamplight hissed. Somewhere in the deep, the heart kept its slow time.

"So Halvard's predecessor came looking," Ilsa said slowly. "Twenty years ago. And Ede—"

"And Ede saw enough of what the man wanted that it broke him," Dominic finished. "He spent the rest of his life drawing maps and hiring strangers to finish a job he couldn't face. He wasn't a prospector who got lucky. He was a witness who couldn't carry it."

"Then what's in the vault?" Tam asked again, and this time nobody pretended not to understand the real question. "If the Institute's known about it for two decades, if they sent a man to find it, if your mother sealed it behind a foot of mortared stone—what's in there that's worth all this?"

Dominic looked at the vault through the falling water. Silver threads over gray stone, and behind them, whatever her mother had judged too dangerous to leave loose and too important to destroy.

"Proof," she said. "The original survey. The real cores. The radio logs from the last eleven days—my mother kept logs, I know she did, she logged everything. If it's in there, it's the Institute's own paper trail, signed and dated. It's the thing that ends them."

"Or it's the thing that ends us," Tam said. "Because if Halvard knows it's in there, then the moment we crack that mortar, we stop being trespassers he can scare off and start being witnesses he can't."

"He's already blasting the upper canyon," Ilsa said. "We crossed that line three days ago."

"No," Dominic said. "We crossed it the day Ede put that map in my hands. Everything since has just been the river catching up."

She stood, and the others stood with her, and she felt it settle over the four of them—not a plan yet, but the shape of one, the way a channel settles before the water commits.

"Here's what we know that Halvard doesn't," she said. "We know the heart is alive, and we know how my mother kept it alive, and we know there's a way to seal this canyon so deep that no amount of Institute money ever digs it out. He thinks he's chasing a map. He's chasing a grave. And in three days, when the melt comes down that upper canyon, he's going to be standing in it."

"And the vault?" Juno asked.

Dominic looked at the mortared stone, at her mother's letters, at the hand that had cut them knowing they'd be read by someone who came after.

"The vault opens," she said. "Tomorrow, at first light. Whatever's in there goes out of this canyon in our hands or not at all—because if Halvard reaches it first, the last true account of what happened here gets filed in a basement in Meridian and dies there, and my parents die with it, twice."

She turned back toward the falling water, and for a moment—just a moment—she let herself imagine her mother standing on this same floor, twenty-two years younger, radio dead, husband at the weir, making the same arithmetic with the water rising. Choosing what to save and what to seal and what to spend.

The heart hummed beneath the floor, patient as it had been for ten thousand years.

And from far above them—muffled, distant, but unmistakable, rolling down through the stone like a knell—came the sound of an explosion.

Tam was on his feet before the echo faded, head cocked, counting. "That's not the melt," he said. "That's a charge. Upper canyon, maybe two miles. He's not waiting three days."

The water in the milled channels at their feet shivered, and ran a little faster.
