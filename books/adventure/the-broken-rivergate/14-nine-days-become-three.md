## 14. Nine Days Become Three

Tam Ovid had a professional relationship with explosions, and the sound coming down the canyon at dawn was not one of his.

He knew the difference the way a mother knows her child's cry in a crowded room. His own charges had a voice—crisp, respectful, a *crack* that said the work had been done right, the stemming packed, the fuse timed to the ounce. This was something else. A dull, arrogant *whump* that rolled up the gorge and came back off the walls three times before it died, sloppy and overfed, the sound of men with powder to burn and no respect for the rock they were burning it in.

He was up before the echo finished, boots in hand, out of his bedroll and onto the shelf of stone where they'd made camp above the drowned river's second gallery.

"Tam." Ilsa was already awake, sitting with her back against the pack wall, her hair still damp from the night crossing. She'd taken first watch. Her face told him she'd been listening to it too. "Third one this hour."

"Fourth." He counted on scarred fingers. "They're walking the line. Testing where the rock wants to give."

Above them, the canyon narrowed toward the upper weir—the great shoulder of limestone that held back the seasonal melt, the same weir Halvard's surveyors had spent two days photographing while pretending they weren't. Now they'd stopped pretending. Now they were drilling.

Dominic came out of the side chamber where the map was kept, Juno a step behind her with the journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a thing that might bolt. Tam had watched Juno carry that journal for six days, and he'd watched her carry something else too, something she thought nobody saw, and he'd said nothing, because Tam Ovid had learned in thirty years of breaking rock that you don't point at a crack until you know whether it's holding or running.

"How far up?" Dominic asked.

Tam tilted his head, listening the way he listened—through his boots, through his back teeth, through the old break in his left femur that ached before any collapse worth fearing. "Half a mile, maybe less. They're not trying to come down the gorge anymore. They're trying to come *through* it." He pointed at the ceiling of the gallery, at the pale seam of quartz that ran overhead like a scar. "That seam runs all the way to the weir face. If they're smart enough to find it—and the tall one with the theodolite is smart enough—they don't need the river gate at all. They drop that seam, the whole upper shelf comes down, and they walk in over the rubble."

"And take the aquifer chamber with it," Ilsa said quietly.

"Or flood it," Tam said. "Depends which way the melt's running. And the melt—" he spat over the edge of the shelf, down into the black water that moved beneath the stone without a single ripple, patient as a debt, "—the melt's not waiting for anybody. You hear the ice up top?"

They had all heard it. For two days there'd been a sound like far-off thunder that never quite resolved into thunder, a long groaning exhalation from the high country where winter still held. This morning it was louder. This morning it had a rhythm.

Ede Voss had written nine days in his journal. That had been four days ago.

* * *

They gathered around the map by lamplight, the five of them and the ghost of a sixth, because Ede's hand-drawn lines covered every inch of the oilcloth and his annotations crowded the margins in a script that got shakier the deeper into the canyon it went.

Dominic stood over it with her sleeves pushed back, and Tam studied her the way he studied a face of rock before setting a charge—looking for the fault lines, the places where pressure would find the weakness. She'd changed since Callow's Bend. He'd noticed it first at Ede's camp, when Juno read the journal aloud and Dominic had stood very still by the fire with her jaw set like a woman listening to a verdict. She'd stopped moving like someone halfway out a door. Now she moved like someone with a door at her back that she'd barred herself.

He wasn't sure yet whether that was better. People who barred doors sometimes went down with the building.

"The melt's early," Dominic said. "Ede's nine days assumed a normal season. We don't have a normal season." She looked up at Tam. "You're the one who'd know. How long does that weir hold?"

Tam crouched over the map. His knees cracked—he was fifty-one years old and had spent most of them kneeling in holes, and his body kept its own honest ledger. He traced the upper canyon with one finger, from the weir to the seam of quartz to the point where the gorge pinched above the second gate.

"That rock up there is good limestone. It'd hold for weeks against water alone." He tapped the seam. "But this—" the quartz, the scar, the vein of weakness running through the canyon's shoulder like a splinter, "—this is a cheat. Somebody's been cutting at that weir for a long time. Old cuts. Pre-Institute. Maybe older than the Institute's grandfathers. The original builders cut that seam on purpose, I think—relief channel, or a way to drop the shelf in a bad year and let the flood through controlled." He looked up. "Your mother's people knew it was there. It's marked on the gate wall—did you see the third symbol? The one that looks like a mountain with a line through it?"

"Break the mountain," Juno said softly. "It's in the journal. Ede translated it. *Break the mountain and the river remembers.*"

"Poetic," Tam said. "Also literal. If Halvard's crew sets enough powder on that seam, they don't get a door. They get an avalanche, and everything under it goes into the water." He sat back on his heels. "Nine days was Ede's number. With the melt running early and those idiots drilling? I give it three. Maybe less if they get impatient, and men with that much powder always get impatient."

The silence after that had weight. Tam had heard it before, in collapsed tunnels and flooded shafts, in the pause after a foreman said the words nobody wanted. It was the sound of every plan dying at once and everyone waiting to see what grew in the grave.

"Three days," Ilsa said. "The inner chamber's a day and a half hard travel from here, with the crossings the way they are now."

"Less, if we don't sleep," Tam said.

"More, if the water rises and we lose the low galleries," Ilsa said. "Which we will. The second crossing was at my hip yesterday. Today it'll be at my chest. By tomorrow it's a dive."

"Then we don't take the low galleries." Dominic's finger moved down Ede's map, past the route they'd planned, to a thinner line—one Ede had drawn and then crossed out, twice, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear the cloth. "What's this?"

Tam leaned in. He hadn't looked closely at that line before. Nobody had. Ede had marked it with a single word in his deteriorating script, and Tam had read it on the first night and decided he didn't like it, and had been quietly hoping nobody would ask.

"*The Throat*," he said.

"It's a chimney," Ilsa said. "I've heard ferrymen talk about it. Old rivermen's story—there's a vertical shaft somewhere in the middle gorge, a place where the water goes down instead of across. They say it breathes. Air out at dawn, air in at dusk, like the canyon's lungs."

"Karst," Tam said. "Dissolution shaft. The water cut it over ten thousand years, and the water's still using it." He met Dominic's eyes. "It'll be a climb. Wet rock, no light except what we carry, and at the bottom of it—assuming the map's right, assuming Ede didn't cross it out because it killed somebody—we come out in the gallery above the second gate. Half a day saved. Maybe a full day."

"And the reason he crossed it out?"

Tam spread his hands. "Could be the climb. Could be the water. Could be that a chimney that breathes is a chimney that can stop breathing, and if the pressure changes while you're in it—" He made a small, final gesture with one hand, the one he used for things that no longer existed. "I've pulled four men out of breathing holes in my life. I've left two where they were."

Dominic looked at the map for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, faint as a headache, came the fifth *whump* of the morning, and a fine rain of dust sifted down through the lamplight, glittering, settling on Ede's crossed-out line like a signature.

"Tam," she said. "How much powder do we have left?"

He told her. It wasn't much. Enough for the inner gate's lockwork, if the lockwork was what her mother's markers said it was—a mechanism, not a wall, something built to be closed by hand and held by stone. Enough for that, and nothing to spare.

"And if we take the Throat," she said, "we save the powder's margin. We save the day. We get to the chamber before Halvard gets through the seam, and we close it while it's still ours to close." She wasn't asking him. She was thinking out loud, and Tam recognized the habit because it was his own—the demolitions man's arithmetic, weighing tons against ounces, always with the same currency: how much of yourself are you willing to put in the hole.

"The Throat breathes at dusk," he said. "Air goes *in* at dusk. That's the safe window—air moving down means the pressure below is stable, means the water at the bottom's not surging. We climb down at dusk, we're in the shaft in the dark for maybe four hours, and we come out above the second gate at dawn." He looked around the lamplight at all of them. Ilsa, whose face said she'd take them down any hole in the world but was already calculating the water. Juno, whose face said nothing at all, which from an archivist who'd spent six days narrating every ruin like a festival was its own kind of alarm bell. Dominic, whose face said what it always said now: *my mother did this, my mother stayed, and I will not leave until I understand why.*

"I can rig the descent," Tam said. "Rope ladder off the lip, fixed lines the rest of the way. I go first. I always go first." That wasn't bravado. That was the job. You never sent anyone down a shaft you hadn't tested yourself, and Tam Ovid had never in his life asked another person to stand on rock he wouldn't stand on first.

"Dusk tomorrow," Dominic said. "Today we rest, we dry what's dry, we eat everything that won't keep, and Tam and I go up to the seam and see what we're really up against. I want to see Halvard's work with my own eyes."

"Too dangerous," Ilsa said flatly.

"Too *unknown*," Dominic said. "Different thing." And there it was again, that door barred at her back. "My mother walked up to the thing that killed her and studied it before she acted. I'm going to do the same. You can come if you want."

"I'll come," Ilsa said, in the tone of a woman who had not been asking permission and had not been offering it either.

* * *

They went up in the gray hour before true dawn, when the canyon was all shadow and the river below them made its one audible sound—a long, deep intake, like the whole gorge drawing breath. Tam led, because Tam always led, moving along the goat track that clung to the north wall with the confidence of a man whose relationship with heights had ended in a fair divorce years ago.

The seam of quartz caught the first light like a vein of ice. They followed it up and up, through a fold in the canyon wall, until the track ended at a shelf below the last bend, and there they stopped, and there they saw it.

Halvard's camp sat on the far rim like a wound. Canvas, cranes, a derrick assembled from parts carried in overland on the Institute's money. Men moved between the tents, small with distance, and at the face of the weir—God, they'd gotten close—the drill rig stood against the pale stone like a lance against a giant's throat. Even from here Tam could read the rock's answer. The drill had found the quartz seam. Of course it had. And around the drill collar, the stone was weeping. A dark stain spread down the weir face, and where it dripped, icicles of mineral hung like teeth.

"They're not drilling to enter," Tam said quietly. "They're drilling to *charge*. Look at the pattern—see the collars? They're setting a ring. That's a demolition pattern. That's *my* pattern." He felt something cold settle in his chest that had nothing to do with the morning air. "Somebody over there knows rock. Knows it well enough to do it ugly and fast, but they know it."

"How long?" Ilsa asked.

Tam counted the collars he could see, estimated the depth per collar, did the arithmetic he'd been doing since he was nineteen years old in a copper mine in the southern hills. "They'll want to fire it all at once. Ring charge, full seam. If they're careful, three days. If the melt spooks them—if they hear what we hear—" he nodded upstream, toward the groaning high country, "—two. Maybe they fire it tomorrow night."

Below them, as if in answer, the river under the stone made a sound none of them had heard before. Not the breathing. Not the patient movement. A *knock*. Deep down, once, twice—like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Ilsa's hand found Tam's arm. "What was that?"

"That," Tam said, "is the mountain deciding how much time we all have." He turned back down the track. "We tell them tonight. Dusk tomorrow, we go down the Throat. And we pray to whatever god looks after fools and powder men that the chimney's still breathing when we get there."

Behind them, far above, the drill rig screamed back to life, and the first pale flakes of the melt came down the canyon on the wind like the season's own white warning.

* * *

He told the others at midday, around the last of the hot food, in the flat voice he used for bad ground reports, because a flat voice kept panic out of the room better than any reassurance. Two days, maybe. The Throat at dusk tomorrow. Everything they owned on their backs by nightfall after that.

Juno Pell sat with the journal in her lap and did not open it, and Tam watched her not open it, and thought: *there's the crack. There's the one I keep not pointing at.*

"Tam," she said, when the others had gone to check the lines and pack the gear. Her voice was careful, the way ground is careful before it isn't. "The Throat. Ede crossed it out. Do you think he crossed it out because of the water?"

"Could be a lot of reasons, miss."

"He wrote in the margin. I didn't read it aloud. It's just one line." Her hands were very still on the oilcloth. "*The Throat took the sound of my voice and gave nothing back. Some doors you knock on and something answers.*"

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Above them, faint and steady, the drill went on eating the mountain, and the melt came down, and somewhere below them the drowned river turned over again in its sleep, and knocked.

"Then we knock soft," Tam said, "and we climb fast, and nobody says a word they don't want the canyon to keep."

He went to check his powder.

It was going to be three days after all. Maybe less. And every hour he could hear the clock now—not in the drill, not in the melt, but in his own chest, ticking down like a fuse he hadn't lit.
