## 15. Confrontation at the Weir

The upper weir announced itself before they saw it—a low, grinding thunder that traveled up through the stone and into their boots, the sound of a river being told where to go. Dominic climbed the last ridge with her palms flat on wet granite, and there it was: a shelf of ancient masonry spanning the canyon's throat, half-swallowed by moss, holding back a lake the color of hammered tin. Water spilled over its crest in a single white curtain, thin as a veil, patient as anything built to outlast empires.

Below it, on the gravel bar, Halvard's camp looked like a wound.

Canvas shelters, a generator humming, cable strung from the cliff face to a drilling rig that hadn't been there two days ago. Men in Institute gray moved between the tents with the unhurried efficiency of people who had never once doubted their right to be anywhere. And at the center, seated on an equipment crate with a tin cup in his hands, sat Halvard himself, watching the water fall as though it were a performance staged for his benefit.

"He'll have lookouts," Tam murmured behind her. "We were seen coming up the ridge, guaranteed."

"I know." Dominic straightened. Her ribs still ached from Ilsa's crossing, three days of bruise she'd stopped cataloguing. "That's why we're not sneaking."

Ilsa stood close enough that their shoulders touched. She'd said almost nothing since they'd left Ede's camp—had only watched Dominic with that ferryman's patience, the way you watch a river rise and wait to learn what it intends. Now she said quietly, "You're sure about this."

"No," Dominic said. "But he is."

She walked down.

* * *

Halvard didn't stand when she entered the camp. That was deliberate; she recognized the choreography from a decade of watching powerful men manage their entrances. He simply turned his head, set down his cup, and folded his hands, a surveyor receiving a landowner who had finally come to sign.

"Miss Brightwater." His voice was mild, almost warm. Up close he looked tired in a way that seemed genuine—the creases around his eyes weren't theatrical. "You've made good time. The melt is ahead of schedule, which means we both have less of it than we'd like."

"You're blasting the upper canyon."

"Survey charges. Controlled." He gestured to the rig. "The Institute prefers not to flood its own investment."

"It's not your investment. It never was. You funded my parents' expedition to strip-mine the aquifer under the Rivergate, and when my mother found out what that would do—when she understood you'd drain the basin and salt every well from Callow's Bend to the border—you let them die covering it. You let *her* die. And then you spent twenty years pretending the canyon was empty so no one would come looking for what she sealed."

She said it plainly, without heat, and watched something flicker across his face—not guilt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a ledger read aloud and finding the arithmetic correct.

"Sit," Halvard said.

"I'll stand."

"Then stand." He rose anyway, moving to the edge of the gravel bar, where the spray off the curtain of falling water hung in the air like breath. "Your mother was the finest field engineer the Institute ever employed. Do you know what she was paid? What her name was worth in grants? We built her career, Miss Brightwater. And yes—we intended to develop the aquifer. It holds enough clean water to supply six provinces for a century. Six provinces. Children who drink arsenic today would drink—" He caught himself, smoothed it. "You've read her markers. You know what she found. So tell me: did she leave you a plan? Something better than a wall and a secret?"

"She left everyone alive downstream," Dominic said. "That was the plan."

"And it cost four lives, including hers, and bought twenty years of nothing." He turned to face her fully. "I'm not here to threaten you. I'm past that, and frankly you don't respond to it. I'm here because you are your mother's daughter, and I think you understand something the rest of your crew doesn't—that secrets rot. That wall she built won't hold forever. Nothing holds forever. The gate will fail someday regardless, catastrophically, with no one who understands it anywhere near. Or we do this properly. Engineered extraction. Managed flow. Funding beyond anything you can imagine, legal protection for your crew, your debts dissolved, your mother's name restored with honors instead of rumors." He spread his hands. "Or you keep running, and in thirty years the mountain does what mountains do, and everything she died for comes down in a single afternoon."

It was a good speech. That was the worst part. Dominic felt it working on her the way water works on stone—not breaking anything, just finding every crack.

"The truth," she said slowly, "in exchange for withdrawal. All of it. The aquifer, the cover-up, the first expedition's records—I publish everything except the location. Your Institute takes the scandal now, on your watch, or you walk away and let us seal the gate and bury it deeper. Those are your options."

Halvard smiled, and for the first time it reached his eyes, and it was worse than any threat.

"You misunderstand your position," he said gently. "Exposure doesn't embarrass the Institute. Exposure *ends* it. There are governments on three boards. There are families whose names are on our founding charter. If the world learns we funded an expedition and then abandoned it to die—if it learns we've spent two decades suppressing a public water source to preserve private leverage—there will be no Institute to fund anything, including whatever noble compromise you're imagining. Men in my position don't get to lose gracefully, Miss Brightwater. We only get to lose last."

"So that's no."

"That's arithmetic." He picked up his cup again, and his voice went quiet, almost kind. "Nine days ago I told you the river rises in nine days. I was wrong about one thing—it's faster than my models. My engineers give the upper weir seventy-two hours before the meltwater overtops it naturally. After that, the lower chambers flood, and everything your mother sealed is underwater for another generation, and I start again with better equipment and someone else's map." He drank. "You could stop me. You could even kill me, and it wouldn't matter; the Institute replaces me the way it replaced the last director who hesitated. The only version of this story where you win is the one where you take my offer before the water takes your choice."

Dominic looked past him, at the white veil of the spill, at the lake behind it lying flat and heavy as a held breath.

Her mother had stood somewhere near this spot. Had done the same arithmetic. Had chosen the wall and the secret and the drowning.

"I need to talk to my crew," Dominic said.

"Of course." Halvard inclined his head, courteous to the end. "Take the ridge path. It's safer after dark than the scree."

* * *

They gathered above the camp, out of earshot, in the blue shadow of the canyon wall. Tam crouched over his satchel, counting fuse by feel. Juno sat apart on a boulder with Ede's journal open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it—Dominic noticed that. Had been noticing things about Juno for two days and telling herself they meant nothing.

"We can't beat him to the inner gate if he keeps the high ground," Tam said. "His rig sits right on the approach. We'd be cutting through his camp."

"Then we don't cut through," Ilsa said. "There's the old maintenance gallery. Ede's map shows it running under the weir—her mother's crew cut it, back when the gate was built. It lets out inside the second gate."

"If it hasn't collapsed in twenty years," Tam said.

"If it has," Dominic said, "we're standing in it when it did."

Silence. The waterfall filled it, that endless grinding patience.

Juno closed the journal. When she spoke, her voice was steady, and that steadiness was the first thing that rang false. "And if we seal it—really seal it, the way you're planning—what happens to the truth? To her? She stays a rumor. A woman who drowned some diggers and vanished. I've spent ten years defending her name in archives full of people who call her a saboteur, and your answer is to make it permanent?"

"My answer is that she chose it," Dominic said. "And I'm choosing to honor the choice."

"But *why*?" Juno stood, and the journal slid from her knees into the gravel, and she didn't pick it up. "Because she left you a note in a cave? Because a dying prospector made you promise? You're about to bury the most important discovery in a century based on a dead woman's handwriting, and none of us got a vote—"

"None of us got a vote when the Institute decided to let them die either," Ilsa said quietly.

"They were going to *give it away*," Juno said, and her voice cracked on it, and Dominic saw—not the ambition she'd expected, but grief, raw and old, the grief of a girl who had loved a hero from afar and needed the hero to have been real. "Water for six provinces. She could have been—"

"She could have been a monument," Dominic said. "She chose to be a wall instead. I'm sorry, Juno. I know what her name means to you. I've spent my whole life wishing it meant nothing to me."

Juno looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—shame, maybe, or its twin—and then it was gone, banked like a covered fire.

"I need to check the survey notes," she said, and walked toward the tents, alone.

* * *

Tam found the signal mirror an hour later.

He came to Dominic without drama, without raising his voice, and put it in her hand: a fragment of polished steel, military issue, wedged in the rocks above Halvard's camp where it could catch the morning sun off the glacier field and throw a flash toward the ridge line. Beside it, chalked small on the stone in Juno's careful archivist's hand: a bearing, a time, and the Institute's survey glyph.

Dominic sat with it in her palm for a long while. The steel was cold. Below, the camp lights burned yellow through canvas, and the waterfall ground on, and somewhere in the dark Juno was awake too—she could feel it, the way you feel weather coming.

Ilsa crouched beside her. "What do you want to do?"

Dominic thought about rage. It came easily, actually—rose in her like floodwater, hot and simple, wanting to march down and drag Juno in front of all of them and strip it bare. Wanting to break something. Ten days of trust, traded for a dead woman's statue.

But underneath the heat, colder and older, she found something else. Understanding. Terrible, familiar understanding—because she knew exactly what it was to love someone you'd never met, to build your whole self around a ghost, to need the ghost to justify the shape of your life. Juno hadn't betrayed the crew. Juno had betrayed herself, years ago, and had just been waiting for someone to hand her the knife.

"She signals at dawn," Dominic said at last. "Every dawn, or Halvard wouldn't have held position—he'd have pushed. Which means he already knows where we're going. The maintenance gallery isn't a surprise anymore."

"So we're walking into his guns."

"We're walking into his guns either way." Dominic closed her fist around the mirror until the edges bit. "But he thinks we don't know. That's the only coin we have left, and I'm not spending it on anger."

Isla studied her face in the dark. "You're going to let her keep doing it."

"I'm going to let her see what it buys." Dominic stood, joints aching, and looked down at the falls—at the white veil, the patient masonry, the lake holding its breath behind her mother's wall. Seventy-two hours, Halvard had said. Maybe less. "When the moment comes, she'll choose. Out loud, in front of all of us. Whatever she chooses then, that's the truth of her, and I can live with the truth. It's the performances I can't stand."

Above them, far up the glacier field, a last light flickered against the stars—dawn practice, or a test, or a promise kept. Dominic watched it fade and felt the countdown settle into her bones like cold water.

Three days. One hidden door. One traitor she couldn't yet bring herself to name.

She turned back toward the fire, and began to plan.
