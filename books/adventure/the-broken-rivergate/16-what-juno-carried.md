## 16. What Juno Carried

Juno Pell had spent eleven years learning to read dead women's handwriting.

It was the archivist's curse and gift: you learned to see a person in the pressure of their pen, in the way an *r* went sharp with anger or soft with exhaustion, in the places where ink pooled because the hand had stopped moving while the mind kept going. She could read Maren Brightwater's letters the way other people read faces. She had read them so many times that she sometimes dreamed in that handwriting—dreamed of being the one the letters were written *to*, instead of the one who found them in a university basement box marked MISCELLANEOUS, UNCATALOGUED, 1890–1912.

So when Dominic Brightwater looked at her across the fire at Ede's Last Camp and said, in a voice stripped down to wire and bone, "The signal mirror. Third cache. It's been moved," Juno did not need anyone to tell her what her face was doing. She could feel it doing it. The heat climbing her throat. The way her hands went flat against her knees like she could press herself into stillness.

She had known this moment would come since the night she'd angled the glass toward Halvard's ridge camp. Three flashes, long-short-long, the old Institute field code from the manuals she'd catalogued in another life. Three flashes, and then she'd sat by the fire shaking until Ilsa asked if she was cold.

"I can explain," Juno said.

Dominic didn't move. The firelight did all the moving for both of them, sliding over her face, catching the scar through her left eyebrow that Juno had once, absurdly, traced in a photograph—the same photograph that had hung above her desk for six years, the one of the first Rivergate expedition, seven figures squinting into the sun, Maren Brightwater third from the left with her hand raised against the glare as if she were already shielding her eyes from whatever was coming.

"Then explain," Dominic said.

* * *

Here is what Juno carried, and had carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't normal to carry:

She had been nineteen when she found the letters. A graduate assistant drowning in uncatalogued donations, and there they were—forty-one letters from Maren Brightwater to a colleague named Sera Ilves, spanning nine years, ending three weeks before the Broken Rivergate expedition went silent. The university had no idea what it had. The Meridian Institute, which had funded the expedition, had filed the disaster under *geological misadventure* and moved on, and nobody had ever thought to look in the boxes the families sent after the funerals.

Juno had read all forty-one letters in one night, sitting on the floor between the shelves with a desk lamp and her heart going like a trapped bird. And what she'd found in them was not a scientist. Not the dry, careful figure from the textbooks, the woman reduced to a footnote about a failed concession survey. What she'd found was *funny*. Fierce. A woman who wrote about the river the way other people wrote about lovers, who complained about her husband's terrible camp coffee in one paragraph and sketched the stratigraphy of a canyon wall in the next, who ended letter thirty-nine with: *"If I'm right about what's under the gate, Sera, then everything the Institute told us about this place is a lie, and I intend to make them choke on it."*

Letter forty was shorter. Letter forty-one was four lines, and the last line was: *"Whatever they say happened, remember that water remembers longer than men do."*

Juno had spent eleven years trying to find out what came after that line. She'd published two papers under a name that wasn't quite her own ambition—careful papers, hedged papers, papers that treated Maren Brightwater as a promising researcher cut short rather than a woman who might have been murdered by a funding body's silence. She'd applied to the Meridian Institute archives twice and been refused twice, politely, in letters that smelled of lawyers. She'd built a whole quiet shrine of a career around a dead woman who deserved better than a footnote.

And then Ede Voss died in a boarding house room that smelled of kerosene and left a map to a girl with digging hands, and the footnote stood up and walked into Callow's Bend, and Juno had looked at her across Tam Ovid's kitchen table and felt something crack open in her chest that she still couldn't name without lying about it.

Because here was the thing she'd never said out loud, not even to herself, not even at three in the morning with the letters spread out around her like a séance:

She hadn't joined this expedition for the truth.

She'd joined it for the *story*. For the redemption arc. For the moment when the world would have to look at Maren Brightwater's name and say it properly, say it with the weight it deserved, say it in headlines and textbooks and the mouths of people who'd dismissed her. Juno had wanted to be the one who handed back the hero. Whole. Public. Vindicated. She had wanted it so badly that when the truth started arriving in its actual shape—not clean, not triumphant, but wet and terrible and heavy as the river itself—she had found she couldn't hold it. The truth said Maren Brightwater had flooded a canyon and died doing it. The truth said the Institute had let her. The truth said the story didn't end with vindication; it ended with a sealed gate and a silence that had held for twenty years precisely because someone had chosen it.

Juno had wanted the story. So when Halvard appeared on the ridge with his polite voice and his jurisdictional paperwork, she had made a trade in the dark of her own skull: a few signals, a little leverage, and maybe—this was the part she'd dressed up in reasonable clothes—maybe she could steer things. Maybe she could keep the discovery alive, keep the archive public, keep the name bright, even if it meant the Institute got its hands on the gate. Someone would publish. Someone always published. Better it be done carefully, with her guiding the narrative, than buried forever under another ton of river stone.

That was the version she'd told herself. That was the version she'd rehearsed every night, polishing it, sanding its edges, until it almost sounded like principle.

And now Dominic Brightwater was looking at her across the fire, waiting, and Juno understood with total clarity that the daughter of Maren Brightwater was going to see straight through it, because Juno had spent eleven years reading handwriting and had somehow forgotten that other people could read hers.

* * *

"I signaled him," Juno said. "Three times. From the ridge above the second cache. Long-short-long." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "I've done it twice more since. He knows our position, our numbers, and roughly how far we are from the inner chamber."

Tam swore softly behind her. Ilsa didn't swear at all; she just went very still, the way she did on the ferry when the current changed under the hull.

"Why?" Dominic asked. Just that. No heat in it. Somehow that was worse than shouting.

"Because I wanted—" Juno started, and stopped, because the rehearsed version rose up in her throat like bile and she refused, she *refused*, to say it. She put her hands flat on the cold stone and started again. "I found your mother's letters eleven years ago. Forty-one of them. Nobody else knew they existed. I've read them so many times I can recite them. And in them she's *alive*, Dominic. She's angry and funny and she's about to blow the lid off the biggest cover-up in the history of the concession surveys, and then the letters stop, and the record says accident, and the Institute says accident, and everyone who ever wrote about her wrote about her like a cautionary tale." She swallowed. "I wanted to give her back. To the world. Properly. With her name on it."

"And Halvard was your route to that."

"Halvard was—" Juno's breath caught. "Halvard was the door I told myself I was walking through. I told myself I could control what he saw. I told myself exposure was inevitable and better managed than buried. I told myself—" She heard it then, all of it, the whole architecture of it, and it collapsed even as she described it, and she watched it collapse in Dominic's eyes too. "I told myself I wanted the truth public. But the truth was already public. You were building it, day by day, in front of me. What I wanted was the *version*. The one where she wins. The one where I get to be the person who gives her back." Her voice finally broke, quietly, like something small giving way. "I wanted my hero back. And I sold your position to a man who works for the people who killed her to try to buy her."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-canyon, faint as a memory, water moved in the dark—a sound they'd all learned to hear the way sailors hear weather.

Dominic was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, and it was worse than rage because it was *understanding*, and understanding meant she'd already done this arithmetic before, probably about herself.

"You know what the worst part is?" Dominic said. "I get it. I've spent ten years running from her name because I couldn't stand what people made of it—the tragedy, the mystery, the Brightwater girl whose parents died in the rocks. They took her and turned her into a story too. A different story, but the same crime. They decided what she was before anyone bothered to find out." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight in her eyes. "You did the same thing, Juno. You loved her so hard you turned her into what you needed. And when the real her showed up—when we found those markers in the stone, when you read Ede's journal out loud and heard what she actually chose—you couldn't bear it. Because the real her doesn't fit the statue."

"No," Juno whispered. "She doesn't."

"She's better than the statue," Dominic said. "That's the thing you can't see yet. The statue is safe. The real woman flooded a canyon to stop a theft, knowing it would kill her, and she did it anyway, and she did it *quietly*, and she let the world call it an accident because the alternative was leading the Institute straight to the aquifer. She chose silence over glory. She chose it the way you choose to breathe." Dominic's jaw tightened. "And you tried to un-choose it for her. For a headline."

Every word landed where it was aimed. Juno sat in the wreckage of it and found, underneath the shame, something unexpected: relief. Terrible, clarifying relief. Eleven years of carrying the letters alone, of curating a dead woman into a shape the living could applaud—and here, at last, was someone telling her to put it down.

"What happens now?" Tam asked into the silence. "We're a day from the inner chamber, Halvard's got our position, and our archivist is working for the other side. I'd like to know if she's walking out or—"

"She's not walking anywhere yet," Dominic said. "Because I'm going to give her a choice. A real one." She turned back to Juno. "Tomorrow we reach the second gate. After that, there's exactly one job left: seal it. Permanently. Behind us. Which means the aquifer stays hidden, the Institute gets nothing, and your mother's discovery dies in the dark with her—which is what *she chose*, Juno. It's what she died for. If you help us do that, then everything you signal Halvard from here on is a lie, and you'll have to live inside that lie with us, and when we come out the other side you'll write the testimony yourself—all of it, including this, including what you did—because clearing her name means telling the ugly parts too, or it's just another statue."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you walk out tomorrow morning. Downriver, alone, before the blasting starts. Halvard will pick you up and you can spend the rest of your life with the Institute, with their archives, with whatever sanitized version of her they let you shelve." Dominic's voice didn't harden; it settled, like stone finding its bed. "But understand what you'd be carrying. Not guilt—I'm not sentencing you. Just knowledge. You'd know what's under the gate, and you'd know what she paid to keep it closed, and you'd know you helped open it. That's not a punishment. That's just the weight. Some inheritances you keep by silence." A ghost of something crossed her face—grief, or its opposite. "I only learned that last week. You can learn it tonight or you can learn it in ten years. Your call."

Juno looked at the fire. She thought about letter thirty-nine. *Everything the Institute told us about this place is a lie, and I intend to make them choke on it.* She had read that line a thousand times as a battle cry. Sitting here, in the canyon itself, with the river muttering under the stone like something patient and enormous, she finally heard it correctly. It wasn't a promise of publication. It was a promise of *confrontation*—and Maren Brightwater had chosen the form of her confrontation alone, in the dark, with water, and had never asked anyone's permission to be believed.

The hero Juno had been waiting to give back had never needed giving back. She had never been lost. She had been *keeping* something. And the only faithful act left was to help her keep it.

"I'll write the testimony," Juno said. Her voice steadied as she said it, and kept steadying, like ground firming underfoot. "All of it. My part included. And I'll signal Halvard one more time—tonight. False position. Northeast channel, past the weir. Let him blast rock for three days while we work." She met Dominic's eyes, and for the first time since Callow's Bend she didn't feel like a woman standing in front of a shrine. She felt like a woman holding a shovel. "Give me the mirror."

Dominic studied her for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat, produced the small steel case, and set it in Juno's palm without ceremony—no absolution offered, none needed, just the weight of the object and the weight of the choice, matched.

"One false signal," Dominic said. "And Juno—if he reads it wrong, if he moves early—"

"Then it's on me," Juno said. "I know. I'll carry it."

Above them, far up the black throat of the canyon, a light flickered that was not a star and not a lantern—brief, rhythmic, patient. Halvard, signaling his own people on the rim. Asking, perhaps, why the archivist's reports had gone quiet.

Juno opened the steel case, tilted the mirror toward the distant light, and began to lie.
