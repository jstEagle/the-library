## 17. The Rising

The sound came first through the stone.

Not a blast—not yet—but a deep, subterranean groan, like the canyon clearing its throat after ten years of silence. Dominic felt it in her boots before she heard it with her ears, a tremor running up through the flagstones of the second gate's antechamber, and every hair on her arms stood at attention.

"That's not us," Tam said. He was kneeling by the charge line they'd spent three hours laying along the inner gate's western pier, his hands still dusted with powder residue, and his face had gone the color of wet ash. "That's *him*. That's Halvard. He's blowing the upper weir."

Dominic pressed her palm flat against the wall. The vibration was faint but steady, growing, a pulse of violence transmitted through half a mile of living rock. Somewhere above them—up past the drowned stairways and the dry galleries and the first gate where her parents' tools still lay arranged like a message no one had been meant to survive to read—the Meridian Institute was tearing open the canyon's throat.

"He's early," Juno whispered. She stood near the entrance with Ede Voss's journal clutched to her chest like a shield. "The melt wasn't supposed to peak for another six days. His own surveyors said—"

"His surveyors told him what he paid them to say." Dominic pulled the map from her jacket, her mother's markings ghosted beneath Ede's careful copies, and stared at the lower chambers one more time. The aquifer heart lay beyond the inner gate: a cathedral of water under pressure, held back by stonework older than any nation, weakened now by a decade of neglect and by the slow rot of the seals her mother had set with her own hands before the river took her.

Collapse the gate. Seal the heart. Let the canyon flood and keep its secret forever.

It had seemed simple when it was a plan. Now it was arithmetic done in terror: they needed four minutes to clear the chamber once the charges were placed, and the water rising behind Halvard's breach would give them perhaps six.

Maybe five.

"Tam." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "How long to wire the last two piers?"

He looked at the gate—the vast, seamless face of it, fitted stones the size of oxen, weeping condensation along every joint—and then at the coil of det cord in his arms, and he did the same math she'd just done, and she watched him decide not to say it out loud.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "If nothing else goes wrong."

* * *

Above them, the upper weir failed the way old things fail: all at once, and then in stages, each stage worse than the last.

Ilsa Rook heard it as a change in the river.

She'd been raised on that sound the way other children were raised on lullabies—the river's voice changing key when snowmelt swelled it, when rain fell in the high country, when some logjam far upstream gave way all at once. She knew its moods better than her own. And the sound coming down the gorge now was a mood she'd only heard twice in her life, both times followed by funerals.

"It's coming," she called up the gallery to where Dominic stood at the lip of the flooded stairwell, lantern held high. "Not tomorrow. Not tonight. Within the hour, maybe less."

"How fast?"

"Faster than a man can run. Faster than a man can *swim*, if he's smart enough to try swimming, which he won't be, because the water won't be water yet—it'll be rock moving downhill wearing water's clothes."

Ilsa had ferried the Broken River for nineteen years. She had pulled bodies from it. She had pulled living people from it who wished afterward she hadn't. She knew exactly what a flash flood did in a confined canyon: it didn't rise, it *arrived*, a vertical wall of churning brown destruction traveling at the speed of a galloping horse, filling every passage, erasing every pocket of air, giving nothing time to think.

And below them, in the heart of the canyon, Tam Ovid was wiring charges to a gate that had to fall *before* the water arrived—or not at all.

"We pull him out," Ilsa said. "We pull everyone out now, and we let the river do the work. It'll smash the gate for us. It wants to."

"No." Dominic's face in the lantern light was carved from something harder than fear. "A flood-smash leaves cracks. Cracks leave seepage. Seepage means the Institute comes back in five years with pumps and drills and lawyers. My mother didn't crack this gate. She *sealed* it. We finish her work or we don't do it at all."

"And if the timer runs out?"

Dominic looked down the dark stairwell, toward the distant glow of Tam's work lamp, and Ilsa saw her swallow whatever answer lived in her throat.

"Then we go get him," she said.

* * *

Tam Ovid had blown bridges, lock gates, a customs house, and once—accidentally, a story he told only when very drunk—a chapel. He understood explosives the way priests understood scripture: reverently, and with full knowledge that they would eventually kill him.

What he did not understand was why his hands were shaking now, when they never shook over a charge.

Because this wasn't stone he was breaking. He kept telling himself that. This was a door, and doors were made to be closed as well as opened, and somewhere on the other side of it was the thing that had broken Ede Voss into a trembling old man trading maps for promises in boarding houses. Somewhere beyond these fitted stones was the reason Maren Brightwater had drowned herself in purpose and water both.

But the stones were beautiful. That was the thing nobody warned you about. Ten thousand years of masons had built this gate to outlast their names, and Tam was about to turn it into gravel, and part of him—the part that loved the clean geometry of a well-set arch—grieved.

"Stop admiring it," he muttered to himself, and fed the det cord into the last drill hole.

The plan was elegant, which is how he knew it would probably fail. Four charges at the load-bearing piers, timed to fire within a tenth of a second of each other so the gate would drop inward as a single mass, plugging the throat of the lower channel rather than shattering it. A plug, not rubble. A door swinging shut forever. The aquifer's own pressure would do the rest, compacting the collapse until the heart sealed itself the way a wound closes.

Elegant. Simple. Dependent on every charge firing true, on the stone behaving as predicted, on the river staying polite for twenty more minutes.

The river, Tam reflected, threading the final splice with numb fingers, had never once been polite in his experience.

From somewhere far above came a new sound—sharper, closer, unmistakable even through half a mile of rock. A second blast. Then a third, quick on its heels, and the floor tilted, just slightly, like a ship taking a swell.

Halvard wasn't breaching the weir anymore. He was descending.

* * *

Juno Pell heard the third explosion and felt it land in her chest like a hand.

*You did this,* she thought. *Your signal brought him here early.*

It wasn't strictly true—she'd signaled his position days ago, and Halvard had been advancing regardless—but guilt has never cared much for strict truth. She stood in the antechamber with the journal against her ribs and did the arithmetic of betrayal: every minute Halvard gained was a minute stolen from Tam's fuse, from Dominic's escape, from Ilsa's crossing. Her one small act of desperate faith—that the Institute could be made to *celebrate* Maren Brightwater instead of burying her—had become this: a countdown measured in water.

"Dominic." Her voice cracked. She tried again. "Dominic, if we don't make it out—"

"Then the story makes it out without us." Dominic was coiling rope with quick, brutal efficiency, not looking at her. "Ede's journal. Your notes. You carry them. Whatever happens, you walk out of this canyon and you tell it."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not hearing me." Dominic turned, and Juno flinched from what was in her face—not anger, worse than anger, a terrible calm. "If the gate falls and the water takes the lower galleries, there is one way out and it's the ferry landing, and someone has to be in the boat telling the truth when the Institute's lawyers arrive asking questions. That's your job. That's the job you've had since Callow's Bend. You're the archive, Juno. Archives survive. That's the whole point of them."

Juno wanted to argue. She wanted to confess—here, now, with the mountain groaning around them—to kneel and say *I signaled him, I thought I was saving her legacy, I thought—*

But the fourth blast came, much nearer, and the lanterns swung on their hooks, and somewhere below them Tam Ovid began to shout, and there was no more time for anything except motion.

*Later,* she promised herself, running. *If we live, later.*

She was already learning how liars talked to themselves.

* * *

The water reached the first gate while they were still hauling gear up from the antechamber.

Dominic heard it before she saw it—a hiss, a rush, a sound like the whole canyon exhaling—and then it came around the bend of the lower gallery in a brown rope of foam, ankle-deep, knee-deep, climbing the walls with an eagerness that made her stomach drop. Not the flood itself. Not yet. Just the vanguard, the river's fingers probing forward through passages it had owned for ten years and intended to repossess.

"Time!" she screamed down the stairwell. "TAM! WE'RE OUT OF TIME!"

His answer floated up, muffled by water and stone: "Five minutes!"

"We don't have five minutes!"

"Then bring me four!" And then, absurdly, horribly, she heard him laugh—the wild, singing laugh of a man entirely committed to a bad idea. "Get to the boat! All of you! I'll fire from here!"

"No," Ilsa said, and it wasn't a discussion; it was bedrock. She was already turning, already uncoiling the rope off her shoulder, already tying the bowline knot she'd tied ten thousand times on ten thousand crossings. "Nobody fires alone. Nobody dies alone. That's the rule on my river."

"The rule on your river is going to get you killed!"

"The rule on my river," Ilsa said, meeting her eyes, "is the only reason either of us is standing here."

And Dominic—who had spent ten years perfecting the art of leaving, who had built her whole life on the principle that staying was the one unforgivable weakness—looked at this woman who refused to abandon anyone, ever, and felt the last wall inside herself go down like a blasted weir.

"Together, then," she said.

They went down the stairs together, into the rising dark.

* * *

The lower gallery was a river already.

Waist-deep and pushing, cold enough to stop breath, lit only by Tam's work lamp burning in its waterproof housing like a drowning star. He stood braced against the inner gate itself, one arm hooked through a drill hole, splicing the firing leads with hands that shook and did not miss. The water tore at them sideways. Somewhere above, another blast—closer, Halvard's crew fighting their way down through the upper galleries, racing them to the same door from opposite sides.

"Leads are spliced!" Tam bellowed over the roar. "Firing box is dry! Give me thirty seconds and get clear!"

"Thirty seconds puts the flood in this room!" Ilsa shouted back.

"Twenty-five!"

"Tam—"

"*Twenty seconds!*"

Dominic got her shoulder under his arm and hauled, and Ilsa took his belt, and between them they peeled him off the gate and into the current, and the three of them went down the gallery like debris—bouncing off walls, swallowing the river, dragged by the building push of water that wanted them downstream and dead. The work lamp swung wild. Behind them the inner gate loomed, black and seamless and waiting, wired with enough explosive to end ten thousand years of patience.

The firing box was in Tam's jacket. The leads ran back along the wall, forty feet of cord, and the water was at their chests now, and the cord was going slack, drifting, catching on rubble—

"The line!" Dominic gasped. "The line's fouled!"

Tam twisted, saw it, saw the splice dragging against a fallen block, saw what one hard tug would do. His face went white. If the lead parted, the charges were dead. If they stopped to free it, the flood took them.

There was one option left, and it was the worst one.

"The manual override," Tam shouted. "Her mother's override—in the gate mechanism—there's a mechanical backup, Brightwater designed it into the plans, I've seen the drawings—it fires the primary charge direct, no cord, no box—you have to be AT the gate to set it—"

"Where?"

"At the gate."

Of course. At the gate. In the teeth of it. Alone in the deepest room of the canyon with the river arriving and Halvard descending and the water climbing her ribs like a tide with intent.

Dominic looked back once—Ilsa's hand locked around her wrist, Tam's face a mask of horror and helpless arithmetic—and she saw, in one lightning flash, the whole shape of her life: the decade of running, the map traded for a promise, the mother whose handwriting marked these walls, the woman holding onto her who would not let go even now, even for this.

*Ilsa.* She pried the fingers loose, gently, one by one, and folded Ilsa's hand shut around the rope. "Hold this," she said. "Hold it like you mean it. I'm coming back to take it again."

And she turned, and dove into the black water, toward the gate, toward her mother's hands waiting in the stone—

Behind her, upstream, the canyon filled with a sound like the world ending: the flood had arrived.
