## 18. Her Mother's Hands

The water came for them the way winter comes to high country—not all at once, but as a sound first, a deep grinding groan from the upper canyon that Tam had once described, in a calmer hour, as "the mountain clearing its throat." Now it was less a throat and more a scream.

Dominic stood at the base of the inner gate with her mother's journal strapped against her chest in oilcloth, and she listened to that scream and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that they had perhaps twenty minutes. Maybe fifteen. The blast Halvard's crew had set above the upper weir hadn't just cracked it—it had unmade whatever careful mathematics her mother had built into this place, and now the river was remembering its old path, and it was coming home through every channel at once.

"Charges are set!" Tam's voice carried over the rising roar, raw and cracked. He was halfway up the scaffolding of ancient stone her mother's expedition had erected thirty years ago—timbers gone black with age but still true, still holding, because Brightwater work held. "All four! But Dominic—the mechanism—"

"I see it."

She did see it. That was the terrible thing. She'd been seeing it since they'd entered the chamber an hour ago, since Juno's lamp had swept across the far wall and found the override: a wheel of bronze and iron set into a recess behind a panel of fitted stone, connected to chains that vanished up into the dark of the gate's spine. Her mother had drawn it in the journal, page after page of diagrams in that precise, patient hand, each one annotated in the margins with notes that were half engineering and half prayer.

*The wheel must be turned by hand. There is no other way. I have made my peace with that.*

Dominic had read those words three times now, and each time they landed differently. The first time, they were history. The second time, they were warning. The third time, standing here with water already sheeting across the chamber floor and climbing past her ankles, they were instructions.

"Dominic." Ilsa's hand closed on her shoulder, firm, warm even through soaked canvas. The ferrywoman's face was streaked with grit and spray, her braid half-undone, and she was shouting to be heard over the flood-noise. "Tell me what you need."

"The wheel takes two people or one person with time," Dominic shouted back. "We don't have either. Get everyone to the high ledge. Tam knows the extraction route—Ilsa, you *know* you do, you've run this crossing your whole life—"

"And you'll be doing what?" Ilsa didn't let go. If anything her grip tightened. "Alone?"

"Someone has to hold the wheel through the full rotation. It locks in stages. If I let go before the last stage, everything we've done is—" Dominic gestured at the charges, at the gate, at the whole drowned cathedral of her mother's sacrifice. "It's nothing. It resets. And then the Institute gets it anyway, next season, next decade, whenever they come back with better blasting powder and worse intentions."

"So we find another way—"

"There isn't one." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Ilsa. Look at me. This is the whole reason I came. Not the map. Not Ede. *This.*"

The water hit their knees. Somewhere above them, Tam was swearing steadily and creatively, feeding the last of the fuse line down from the scaffold. Juno stood on the high ledge with the lamp held high, and even from here Dominic could see the girl's face working through something—fear, guilt, resolve—and settling on resolve, which was more than Dominic had any right to expect after the weirs and the signals and the terrible arithmetic of what Juno had done and why.

"Twenty minutes," Juno called down, voice thin but steady. "That's what Tam said. Dominic, that's not enough time to turn that wheel and get out."

"It is if I don't plan on dawdling."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke." Dominic pulled the journal free of the oilcloth, tore out the final diagram—the override sequence, eight stages, each lock requiring a quarter-turn and a hammer-blow to seat—and pressed it into Ilsa's hands. "If I'm wrong about the timing, this goes to Callow's Bend. To no one else. You burn it before you let the Institute have it. Promise me."

"I'm not promising anything," Ilsa said, "because you're coming back, and you can burn it yourself."

And there it was again—that thing between them that neither of them had named, the thing that had surfaced in the flash flood at Ilsa's Crossing and had been growing ever since, silent and structural, like roots under a road. Ilsa's eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely certain, and Dominic felt something in her chest crack open that ten years of running had kept welded shut.

"Ilsa Rook," she said, "you are the most stubborn woman on this river."

"Everyone says so."

"Get to the ledge."

Ilsa held her gaze for one more second—one long, furious, eloquent second—and then kissed her, hard and brief, tasting of river water and fury, and climbed.

* * *

The wheel was cold. That was the first thing. Thirty years underwater and sealed in stone, and when Dominic's hands closed around the bronze rim, the metal was cold as a grave, and beneath the cold she felt the faint tremor of the mechanism alive in the walls, chains taut, locks waiting. Her mother had touched this same rim. Had stood exactly here, in water probably no deeper than this, with the same groaning overhead and the same arithmetic of minutes, and had turned.

*Why did you stay?*

The question had haunted Dominic since Ede Voss put his dying hand on hers in that boarding house room. Why would a woman with a daughter waiting, with a life elsewhere, with every reason to run—why would she stay at a wheel like this while the canyon filled?

Now Dominic knew.

Because the wheel didn't turn itself. Because someone had to hold it through all eight stages, and the stages took as long as they took, and the water rose as fast as it rose, and there was no version of the mathematics where the person at the wheel walked away clean. Her mother hadn't stayed out of despair. She'd stayed because staying was the job. Because love, real love, isn't the running kind—it's the standing kind. The kind that plants itself in front of the thing that needs doing and refuses to move until it's done.

*Oh, Mama,* Dominic thought, and began to turn.

The first quarter-turn fought her like a living thing. Rust and mineral scale shrieked along the bronze; the chains above clanked and shuddered; somewhere in the gate's spine a counterweight shifted with a boom that she felt in her teeth. Then the first lock caught, and the wheel went rigid, and she swung the hammer—her mother's hammer, recovered from the outer gate, its handle worn to the shape of another woman's grip—with both hands.

*Clang.*

Stage one seated. Water at her thighs.

The second turn was easier, the third harder, the mechanism waking in stages, and Dominic fell into a rhythm that was almost a trance: turn, brace, strike, listen for the deep resonant *chunk* of the lock engaging, breathe, turn again. Spray rained down from the cracks spreading across the ceiling. The roar above had become continuous, a freight-train sound, and through the upper gallery she could see white water beginning to pour—not a trickle now but a falling wall, gaining volume by the heartbeat.

Turn. Brace. Strike.

Her arms burned. Her shoulders screamed. The cold had climbed into her bones and turned them to wire. Stage five stuck—stuck badly, the wheel refusing the quarter-turn, and Dominic hung off the rim with her full weight, boots braced against the stone housing, and thought absurdly of her mother's marginal note: *When it sticks, it is not broken. It is deciding whether to trust you.*

"Trust me," she said aloud, to a wheel, to a dead woman, to herself. "I'm staying."

It gave. The hammer fell. *Clang.*

Water at her waist. Six.

Seven.

At seven, she made the mistake of looking up. Through the falling water she could see the ledge, small faces, Juno holding the lamp so high her arm shook, and beside her Ilsa, who had not gone to safety after all but stood at the ledge's lowest point, rope coiled in her hands, watching Dominic with an expression that contained no fear at all—which meant she'd already moved past fear into decision, and Dominic knew exactly what that decision was.

"Don't," Dominic mouthed.

Ilsa smiled. It was not a comforting smile.

Eight. The final stage required the hardest turn of all—the master lock, the one that would drop the counterweights and bring four tons of engineered stone down into the breach her mother had left open all these years, closing the heart of the aquifer behind a seal designed to outlast empires. Dominic set her boots, wrapped her hands, and turned with everything she had left, which was not much, which was exactly enough.

The wheel came around. The hammer rose.

And the world broke open.

The upper gallery failed all at once—a curtain of white water taller than a house, slamming into the chamber, tearing the scaffold apart, ripping Tam's fuse lines into ribbons. The shockwave hit Dominic like a fist; she lost the hammer, lost her footing, went down and under and came up gasping with the wheel still somehow in her grip, her fingers frozen to the bronze, the last lock *unseated*, the whole mechanism trembling on the edge of reset.

Below her knees there was no longer floor. There was only river.

She struck the lock. Once. Twice. The hammer was gone—she hammered with her fist, with her forearm, flesh on ancient bronze, feeling skin split, feeling nothing, caring about nothing except the sound she needed, the *clang* that meant done—

The third blow caught the lock's edge and drove it home.

Deep in the gate's spine, something enormous let go. Four tons of counterweighted stone descended with a sound like the world's largest door closing, and the trembling in the walls changed character—from strain to rest. The seal was set. Whatever happened now, the aquifer heart was closed, and no wheel, no charge, no Institute would open it cheaply again.

Done. It was done.

Dominic tried to let go of the wheel and discovered she couldn't—her hands had locked around the rim, cold-shocked, cramping, and the current was peeling her body sideways off the housing like a leaf off a branch. The chamber was half-full now and filling fast, debris thundering past, a splintered scaffold beam missing her head by a hand's width. She got one boot wedged, then lost it. The river took her legs. Then her hips.

*I did it,* she thought, with strange calm. *Mama, I did it. I stayed.*

Then the rope hit her.

It wrapped her chest once, twice—Ilsa threw like a ferryman, which is to say perfectly, accounting for current and drift without seeming to think about it—and snapped taut. The jerk nearly dislocated her shoulders. For a moment she was a rag in a tug-of-war between a river and a woman, and then Ilsa began to haul, hand over hand, walking the rope up the ledge with her heels dug into stone, screaming something Dominic couldn't hear over the water and didn't need to. She knew what it was. It was the same thing she'd have said:

*Not yet. Not like this. Come back.*

Dominic's fingers tore free of the wheel—one, then the other, skin left behind on the bronze—and the river had her for three seconds, four, five, tumbling her through the brown roaring dark, and then the rope came tight again and the world tilted and she was moving *up*, out, breaking the surface into lamplight and rain and Juno's hands joining Ilsa's on the rope, both of them hauling, all three of them screaming, until the ledge took her weight and she was lying on wet stone, coughing the river out of her lungs, alive.

Ilsa knelt over her, soaked, shaking, furious, radiant. "You promised," she said, though Dominic had never promised anything of the sort. "You absolute—you *promised*—"

"I finished it," Dominic rasped.

"You finished it *drowning*."

"Yeah." She managed a grin, bloody-mouthed, half-dead, happier than she had ever been in her life. "That part was optional."

Above them, muffled through stone and water, the charges Tam had placed finally found their moment—the inner gate's flanking galleries collapsed inward with a rolling series of booms, sealing the approaches beyond any hope of excavation. The whole canyon shuddered, settled, and began, very slowly, to fill.

"We have to go," Tam shouted from the passage mouth, silhouetted against lamplight, waving them in. "High route, now! When this chamber tops out, the surge'll backflush the whole lower canyon!"

Juno hauled Dominic's arm across her shoulders. Ilsa took the other side. Between them, limping, bleeding, laughing in a hollow exhausted way that none of them tried to explain, Dominic Brightwater looked back once—at the water swallowing the wheel, at the seal her mother had set and she had completed, at the place where she had finally, finally stopped running.

Then the three of them turned together, and ran for the light.
