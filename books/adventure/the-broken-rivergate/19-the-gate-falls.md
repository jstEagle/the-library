## 19. The Gate Falls

Tam Ovid had placed a thousand charges in his life, and he could tell you the exact moment each one stopped being his.

It happened in the half-second after he thumbed the plunger—when the charge became the mountain's, the water's, the gods' if there were any—and all he could do was stand back with his ears ringing and watch what he'd made. Most men who worked powder never learned that. They kept reaching for the plunger like it still belonged to them. Tam had learned it young, in a slate quarry outside Vell's Crossing, when a miscalculation of his had brought down twice the face he'd meant to and buried a man he'd drunk with the night before. The man lived. Tam never touched a charge again without first accepting that it would do what it would do.

So when the water came down the canyon at them—black and roaring, the whole drowned weight of the upper weir let loose through Halvard's cracked seam—Tam did not run. Not at first. He stood on the ledge above the inner gate with the plunger still warm in his fist and watched the flood come, and he made his peace with it, the way you do, the way you must, and only then did he turn and shout the only word that mattered.

"MOVE!"

* * *

They had placed the charges in the dark, by lamplight, with the river already singing through the walls.

It had taken four hours. Four hours of Tam crawling along the gate's ancient flood-channels with wax-sealed charges and fuse-cord, his hands steady only because he refused to let them be anything else. The inner gate was not like anything he'd ever been paid to break. It was not quarried stone and it was not poured concrete—it was grown, somehow, or woven, layers of mineral laid down by the aquifer itself over centuries, the water building its own lock. His mother would have called it God's masonry. Tam called it the hardest job of his life, because every instinct he had said *don't*. Every instinct said the gate was holding something back that wanted holding, and to break it was to break the world a little.

But that was the trick of it, and Dominic had seen it before any of them: the gate didn't need breaking. It needed *finishing*. Her mother's markers had said as much, in that shorthand of hers scratched into the stone—*collapse the throat, let the water do the rest.* The gate was a half-built thing, abandoned mid-construction a century before the first expedition ever saw it. Left as it was, it could be reopened. Finished properly—dropped, tumbled, its own mass folded into the channel it guarded—it would seal for good. The aquifer would do the rest, laying new stone over the grave of its own front door.

"You're sure," Tam had said, kneeling at the first charge, and it wasn't a question about engineering.

"I'm sure," Dominic had said. And then, quieter, with her hand flat against the wet stone: "She was sure. That's enough."

He'd lit the fuses himself. Wax cord, slow burn, seven minutes. Enough time to climb to the ledge above, enough time to gather at the far wall of the chamber with their backs against stone that groaned like a ship in weather. Enough time for Ilsa to say something low to Dominic that Tam pretended not to hear, and for Juno to stand with her arms wrapped around Ede Voss's journal like it was a child she was carrying out of a fire.

The charges went as one. The sound was not a bang—it was a *sigh*, deep and structural, the sound of a mountain shifting its weight. Dust rolled through the chamber. The lamplight turned brown. And then the gate, the great woven throat of it, folded—not shattered, not exploded, but *slumped*, stone sliding into stone, tons upon tons of it pouring into the channel like a man lying down at last after standing watch too long.

The aquifer took it from there.

Tam felt it through his boots before he heard it: a pressure change, a vast inhalation. The water in the lower chambers, the living water, the thing they'd come all this way to protect—it moved. It rose up into the rubble and began, with the patience of something that had been building stone since before there were names for stone, to seal. They could hear it working. A grinding, settling, almost gentle sound. The sound of a door being locked from the inside by the house itself.

"It's holding," Juno breathed. "It's—it's actually—"

That was when the upper canyon came apart.

* * *

Halvard's last blast had been meant to force a way in. What it actually did was crack the seasonal weir—a structure older than the gate, older than the Institute, a natural lip of limestone that had held back the spring melt for as long as anyone had mapped the canyon. The meltwater behind it had been building for weeks. Tam had known the number the way he knew all his numbers: enough water to fill a cathedral every minute. Enough to drown the lower chambers, drown the aquifer heart, drown everything they'd bled to reach—unless the inner gate was already sealed.

It was. That was the one mercy. That was the whole gamble, and it had paid.

But the flood still had to go *somewhere*, and the somewhere was the canyon, and the canyon was where they were.

"High ground!" Dominic's voice cut through the roar. "The ledge—Juno, the map says the ledge runs all the way—GO!"

They went.

Tam ran with the plunger-case strapped to his back and his lungs full of stone dust, and behind him the water came down the canyon like the end of an argument. He didn't look back. Looking back was how men died in floods—they saw the wall of it, and the seeing froze them, and the water didn't care what they saw. He ran on the sound alone: the roar doubling, trebling, the walls of the gorge amplifying it until it wasn't sound anymore but weather, a storm made of one thing.

The ledge was narrow. Six feet wide in places, less in others, slick with spray that had been coming off the river for ten thousand years. Ilsa went first—of course Ilsa went first, Ilsa who read water the way Tam read fuses, Ilsa who had nearly died in this same canyon four days ago and had come back from it with something in her eyes that Tam recognized because he'd carried the same thing since Vell's Crossing: the knowledge that the river gives you back changed or not at all.

She found the bad stretch before any of them saw it.

"STOP!" Her voice, pitched to carry over the flood. "The ledge is gone—forty feet, maybe fifty—there's a seam, we have to jump it!"

Tam skidded to a halt beside Dominic. Below them, where the ledge should have continued, there was nothing—a gap where some ancient collapse had taken the walk of stone entirely, and beneath the gap, the new flood was already arriving, white and furious, filling the gorge floor like a kettle filling past the brim.

"Jump it," Dominic said. "All right. Who's first?"

"I am." Ilsa was already uncoiling the rope from her shoulder. "I anchor on the far side, you all cross the line. Tam, you've got the lightest hands—"

"I've got the heaviest pack," Tam said.

"Then you go last and you go slow, and if you fall I'm coming in after you, and I'll be *furious* about it."

There was no time to argue, which was, Tam thought later, the only reason any of them lived—no time for fear to organize itself into refusal. Ilsa made the jump with the rope coiled at her hip, landed hard on the far ledge, rolled, came up bleeding from one elbow and already driving a piton into the seam with the little hammer she wore like other people wore a knife. The rope went taut. Juno crossed with her eyes shut and her teeth bared, hand over hand, the journal strapped to her chest. Dominic crossed like she'd been born on a rope, which Tam supposed, in a way, she had.

Then it was Tam, and the pack, and the plunger-case, and forty feet of screaming dark.

He went slow. He went slow the way he did everything that mattered—deliberately, counting, his hands moving without his mind's permission because his mind was busy being afraid and his hands had work to do. Halfway across, the flood hit the gap below him. He felt it before he heard it, a pressure like a hand against the soles of his boots, spray rising up past his face, cold as a grave. The rope jumped in his grip. Somewhere behind him, upstream, the last of the weir gave way entirely, and the sound that came down the canyon then was the sound the world would make if it cleared its throat.

"TAM!" Ilsa's voice. "Don't stop! Don't you dare stop!"

"I'm not stopping," he said, to no one, to himself, to the water. "I'm counting."

He counted. Four more pulls. Three. Two. His boot found stone, real stone, and then Ilsa's fist was in his collar and Dominic's hands were under his arms and they hauled him onto the ledge in a tangle of rope and swearing, and behind them the gap where the ledge had been was simply gone—not flooded but *unmade*, the whole gorge below filled wall to wall with moving water, brown and white and roaring, the river taking back every inch of the canyon that had ever been borrowed.

They lay there a moment, the four of them, breathing.

"Well," Tam said, into the stone. "That's the gate done, then."

* * *

They climbed for six hours.

The flood had taken the low route—of course it had, water always takes the low route—and the high ledges held, the old shepherds' paths that Ede Voss had marked on his map in a hand so faint Juno had needed two candles to read it. They climbed through spray and failing light, past places where the canyon walls still bore the marks of the first expedition: a rusted ring-bolt, a weathered cairn, one faded paint arrow in a color that had probably been red. Her mother's arrow, maybe. Her mother's hand. Tam watched Dominic pause at it, touch it with two fingers, and keep climbing, and he understood something then that he'd been circling for the whole journey: she wasn't running anymore. She hadn't been for days. Whatever the running had been for, it was finished, and what was left was a woman climbing out of a canyon she'd chosen to save.

The water followed them the whole way. Not chasing—there was no malice in it, Tam had never believed in malice in water—but *arriving*, filling each chamber and passage as they left it, the canyon becoming what it had been before anyone ever cut a gate into it: a drowned place, a closed place, the river's own house with the door locked and the key thrown away. Twice they heard Halvard's people below—shouts, the clatter of gear, once a long blast on some signal horn, ragged with panic. The Institute's team had been in the lower galleries when the weir went. Tam did not wish them dead. He was an explosives man; he knew exactly how it felt to be downstream of your own mistake. But he did not slow down for them either, and neither did Dominic, and when Juno started to say something about them, Dominic said, "They have the high map. Halvard has the high map. He knew the flood numbers better than we did. They'll make it or they won't, and either way we're not dying in their place."

Juno said nothing more about it.

They reached the canyon mouth at full dark, and came out of the gorge the way you come out of a dream—abruptly, all at once, the walls falling away and the open sky appearing overhead like a held breath released. The river ran past them, high and fast and ordinary, the same river it had always been. Somewhere behind them, deep in the stone, the aquifer was laying its new seal, patient as geology.

Ilsa stood at the water's edge, wringing out her braid, and started to laugh. It was a small laugh at first, then a real one, helpless, the laugh of a woman who had been afraid for nine days straight and had just now been given permission to stop. Juno sat down where she stood, right in the mud, and put her face in her hands, and her shoulders shook, and no one asked her whether it was grief or relief, because it was both, and everyone knew it.

Dominic stood looking back the way they'd come, at the black mouth of the canyon, at the faint sound of the flood still working itself out in the deep places. Tam came and stood beside her. He was the oldest of them by fifteen years and he had learned that sometimes the kindest thing you could do for a person was stand next to them and say nothing.

"Heavy pack," she said at last. "You carried it the whole way."

"Someone had to bring the plunger home," Tam said. "It's a good plunger."

She almost smiled. "Tam. What you did in there. Finishing the gate. I know what it cost you to set those charges. I saw your hands."

Tam looked at his hands. Steady now. They'd earned it.

"My whole life," he said, "I've been the man who breaks things for money. Quarries, foundations, old walls that somebody loved once. You get good at not thinking about it. You get very good." He turned the plunger-case over in his hands. "Today I broke the right thing. First time. You don't forget a day like that. You *build* on a day like that."

Dominic put her hand on his shoulder, brief and hard, and then the moment was over, because moments like that don't keep, and the four of them turned together and looked down the valley toward the lights of Callow's Bend, small and yellow in the dark, impossibly ordinary, impossibly far.

"Two days' walk," Ilsa said. "Three, with Juno's feet."

"My feet are fine," Juno said, from the mud.

They made cold camp above the flood line, too tired for fire, sharing a single biscuit tin and the last of the brandy, which Tam had carried through everything on the principle that a demolitions man is entitled to one luxury. The canyon behind them rumbled once, deep and long, the aquifer settling into its new shape, and then went quiet, and stayed quiet, and would stay quiet, Tam understood, for longer than any of them would live. He found that he liked the sound of that. Some things you build to last. Some things you break to last. He was, he decided, drifting off with his back against a warm stone, both kinds of man, and today he'd finally been the right one.

He was nearly asleep when Juno spoke.

"There's a light," she said. "On the river road."

Tam opened his eyes. Down in the valley, on the road that followed the river toward Callow's Bend, a single lantern was coming toward them—steady, unhurried, wrong for the hour and wrong for the place. Not Halvard's team; they were behind, and scattered, and drowning in their own arithmetic. This was one person, walking alone, walking *toward* the canyon mouth, toward the four of them sitting soaked and diminished and alive in the dark.

Dominic was already on her feet.

"That's the ferryman's lantern," Ilsa said slowly. "That's—nobody's carried that pattern since my father died. That's an old light."

The lantern came on up the switchbacks, closer, closer, and Tam watched Dominic's face in the last of the brandy-light and saw her go very still, the way she'd gone still in front of her mother's markers, the way a woman goes still when the past stops being past and starts climbing the hill toward her.

"Whoever it is," Tam said quietly, reaching for the plunger-case out of pure habit, "they knew where to find us."

The light stopped at the edge of the camp.
