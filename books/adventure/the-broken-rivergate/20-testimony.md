## 20. Testimony

The boarding house room in Callow's Bend had not changed since the night Ede Voss died in it, and Juno Pell suspected it never would. The same water stain spread across the ceiling like a continent on an old map. The same window stuck halfway open, letting in river-smell and the clatter of the morning market below. Someone had stripped the bed and swept the floor, but the room still held him—the ghost of pipe smoke, the shape of a man who had spent his last coin on a promise.

Dominic sat at the small desk under the window, and she had been sitting there for three days.

Juno brought the tea the way she'd brought it every morning: two cups, one for herself, one to go cold at Dominic's elbow while she worked. She set them down without a word. On the desk lay the wreckage of a manuscript—pages crossed out, pages torn, pages weighted flat with a river stone Ilsa had fished from the shallows below the new crossing. At the top of the current page, in Dominic's blunt, digging-hand script, read the words:

*My name is Dominic Brightwater. My mother was Mara Brightwater, and the Meridian Institute has spent eleven years letting you believe she killed her own crew.*

Below that, nothing yet. Just the white space where the truth had to live.

"You've rewritten that opening four times," Juno said.

"Five." Dominic didn't look up. "The first version was a eulogy. The second was an accusation. The third was both, and neither worked."

"And this one?"

Dominic turned the page toward her. "This one's just true. That's harder."

Juo pulled the second chair close and sat, tea forgotten. She had spent her whole adult life around documents—cataloguing them in the Callow's Bend records office, preserving them against damp and mice and the particular rot of paper stored too near a river. She knew what documents could do. She knew what they couldn't. What she was watching Dominic attempt was something rarer than either: a document that would have to survive being read by people who did not want it to be true.

"The Institute will contest every line," Juno said.

"I'm counting on it." Dominic tapped the stone-weighted pages. "Contested means examined. Examined means someone finally reads the survey logs Halvard's own team filed. You can't bury a thing twice without leaving two graves."

Outside, the market noise swelled—a cart, a argument about the price of rope, children running underfoot. Callow's Bend had spent eleven years telling itself a story about the Brightwater expedition: hubris, flood, tragedy, closed file. Juno had grown up on that story. She had loved its heroine so fiercely, so privately, that when the real woman's daughter walked into town smelling of diesel and secrets, Juno had done the worst thing she'd ever done. She had traded the story she loved for the story she wanted.

She still hadn't forgiven herself. She wasn't sure she was supposed to.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and by noon the account had a spine.

Juno insisted on structure—she was an archivist before she was anything else, and testimony without structure was just grief wearing a coat. They built it the way you'd build a shoring wall: load-bearing facts first. The dates of the original expedition, cross-checked against Meridian's own published field reports. The funding trail, which Juno had pulled from the public registry in four hours of dusty work that made her fingers black with old ink. The survey logs from Halvard's advance team, which Tam Ovid had copied off a stolen satchel during the retreat from the canyon and which now constituted the single most dangerous stack of paper in the territory.

Then the hard part. The part they argued about for two days.

"We tell them what she found," Juno said, pacing the narrow room. "A living aquifer system, self-regulating, feeding half the watershed. We tell them she flooded the lower chambers to stop the strip-mining. We tell them she died doing it. That clears her name."

"It also draws a map," Dominic said.

"Not if we don't say where."

"You think Halvard can't find 'where' in a week? He had the concession papers before we launched. He has the original surveys. All he needs is one detail—one chamber number, one elevation—and the account becomes a treasure notice with my mother's signature on it."

Juno stopped pacing. "So we omit the aquifer entirely."

"We omit the aquifer entirely."

"Then people will say you're hiding something. They'll say the daughter is protecting the family myth."

"They'll say that anyway." Dominic's voice was quiet, but it had bedrock under it; Juno had learned to hear that quality in her over the weeks underground, the tone that meant the decision had already been made somewhere deeper than argument could reach. "I'm not writing this to be believed by everyone. I'm writing it to be *checkable*. Every claim in here traces to a document someone else holds. If Meridian wants to dispute it, they have to produce their own records—and their own records convict them. If they stay silent, the silence convicts them slower, but it convicts them. Either way, my mother walks out of that file a person again instead of a cautionary tale."

"And the water?" Juno asked. "The thing itself? It just stays down there, unnamed, unclaimed?"

"For now." Dominic looked at the window, at the river beyond the rooftops, running high and bright with spring melt. "Some inheritances are kept by silence. I'm learning to keep mine."

Juno sat back down. She thought of the inner chamber—the green-lit heart of the canyon, the slow pulse of water through living stone, the sense she'd had standing there that she was inside something not dead but *patient*. She had wanted, all her life, to put that kind of wonder in a display case. She was only now beginning to understand that some things were diminished by being shown.

"All right," she said. "Omission it is. But I want it in the record that the omission exists. A sealed annex. One line saying material has been withheld deliberately, held in trust, available to a future generation that asks better questions than ours do."

Dominic studied her for a long moment. Then, almost smiling: "You really are your profession."

"My profession kept me honest exactly once," Juno said, "and it was too late to matter. Let it keep me honest early this time."

* * *

The draft went through seven hands before it went anywhere else.

Tam Ovid read it aloud in Ilsa Rook's kitchen, standing, because he said testimony should be heard on its feet. He stumbled only once—at the passage describing the first gate, the tools arranged in deliberate rows, the message left by the dead for whoever came after. When he finished, the kitchen was very quiet. Rain ticked against the window. The kettle muttered to itself on the stove.

"She comes off proud," Tam said finally. "Stubborn. Kind in ways she probably didn't advertise. Is that how you want her remembered?"

"That's how she was," Dominic said.

"Then it's right." Tam set the pages down with the care of a man handling charges. "Meridian's going to come at this like a rockslide, you know. Lawyers, retractions, character work. They'll dig up everyone who ever served with her and let them choose between a pension and a principle."

"Some will take the pension," Ilsa said from the doorway, arms crossed, hair still damp from the morning crossing.

"Some," Dominic agreed. "But not all. And the ones who don't will be enough. That's all testimony ever is—not enough to stop the machine, just enough to make the machine's operators flinch when they look in mirrors."

Ilsa crossed the kitchen and stood behind Dominic's chair, and her hand came to rest on her shoulder—light, unhurried, the touch of someone who had already pulled her out of rising water once and intended to make a habit of it. Juno watched it happen and felt the old ache in her chest, the one shaped like everything she'd gotten wrong. But underneath the ache now there was something steadier. She had seen what these people were together. Whatever came next, they would be together for it.

* * *

The Institute's response arrived nine days later, and it arrived exactly as predicted: as silence.

Not total silence—that would have been an admission. Instead, a careful, suffocating quiet, engineered in some Meridian office tower by people whose entire craft was making inconvenient things evaporate. A statement appeared in the territorial gazette, two paragraphs long, expressing the Institute's sorrow at the "tragic loss of life" during an "unauthorized expedition into unstable terrain," noting that "questions regarding historical operations remain subject to ongoing internal review," and thanking the authorities of Callow's Bend for their cooperation.

No denial. No rebuttal. No mention of Mara Brightwater at all—which was, Juno realized, reading it for the fourth time in the records office, the loudest confession the Institute could afford to make. To deny the account, they'd have to engage it. To engage it, they'd have to open files. The files were a mass grave of their own digging.

"They're going to bury it," Juno told Dominic that evening, on the ferry landing, where the two of them had taken to meeting without quite arranging to. "Bury it quietly, pay off whoever needs paying, wait out the news cycle. In a year, most people won't remember there was ever an account at all."

"I know."

"You sound calm about it."

Dominic leaned on the rail, watching the current shoulder past the pilings. "Halvard's superiors buried the scandal, not the truth. There's a difference. The scandal dies because scandals die. But the truth is in the gazette now, and in the territorial archive, and in forty copies Tam mailed to newspapers from here to the coast. Somewhere, someday, somebody's going to be doing exactly what you used to do—sitting in a records office, pulling threads—and they're going to find the seam. And when they pull it, everything we left intact will still be there to testify. My mother's markers. Ede's journal. The logs. The water itself, doing what water does, whether anyone believes in it or not."

"And until then?"

"Until then, the river keeps it." Dominic glanced at her, and there was something in her face Juno hadn't seen before—or rather, something she'd seen only once, in a photograph on her childhood wall: the expression of a Brightwater looking at moving water and seeing not danger but continuity. "You gave me the idea, you know. The sealed annex. Withheld in trust. I've been thinking about who holds the trust."

Juno went very still. "Dominic—"

"You. Who else? You know the material better than anyone alive besides me. You betrayed us, and then you carried charges through a flooding canyon to fix it, and I watched you do both, which means I trust your judgment more than I trust almost anyone's—including mine, some days." She said it plainly, without absolution in her voice, and Juno understood that the lack of absolution was itself the respect. Dominic was not forgiving her. She was *counting* on her, which was heavier and worth more. "One copy of the full account. Locations, elevations, chamber maps, everything. Sealed. Your office, your lock, your conscience. You decide when—if ever—it opens. Not me. Not the Institute. Not even you, unless the question gets good enough."

Juno's throat tightened. Eleven years ago she had pinned a newspaper clipping of Mara Brightwater above her desk and promised herself she'd matter to that story someday. She had imagined medals, monographs, her name in a footnote of greatness. This was nothing like the imagining. This was a box of paper and a weight she would carry for the rest of her life, alone, in a damp office above a hardware store in a town the maps barely bothered with.

It was the most important job she would ever hold.

"I'll need a fireproof cabinet," she managed.

"Tam's already building one." Almost a smile. "He says if he's going to build things that blow up, he should build at least one thing that doesn't."

* * *

On the last day of the month, the account ran in full in the *Callow's Bend Courier*—all four pages, set in type so small the printer apologized twice. Juno bought twelve copies. She sent one to her mother, one to the territorial library, one to a university archivist downriver who owed her a favor, and one she folded carefully and placed in the sealed annex, on top of the chamber maps, the first document in a collection that might outlive them all.

That evening, the town talked. It talked in the tavern and the chandlery and the queue at the bakery, voices dropping when strangers passed, rising again when they didn't. Some people were angry—there had always been families in Callow's Bend who took Meridian wages, and wages buy loyalty cheap. Some were grieving, newly, for a disaster they'd thought was settled history. And some—more than Juno had dared hope—came up to Dominic in the street or on the landing, awkward, hat in hand, and said things like *my brother crewed on that dig* or *I always thought there was more to it*, and shook her hand as if shaking the past itself.

Juno watched from a distance, cataloguing, because that was what she did. And what she catalogued, more than anything, was the moment an old ferryman named Aldous Crane—retired thirty years, half-blind, famous for never admitting he was wrong about anything—made his way down the ramp to where Dominic stood, gripped her forearm with both knotted hands, and said, loud enough for the whole landing to hear:

"Mara Brightwater pulled my boy out of the rapids in '61 and never told a soul. Whatever they wrote about her in the city papers, we knew different here. Good to have it written down proper at last."

Dominic said something back that Juno couldn't catch. But she saw her face when he released her arm—the face of a woman receiving something she had stopped believing was owed to her—and Juno turned away to give her privacy, and blinked hard at the river, and let the wind take the blame for her eyes.

* * *

Late that night, Juno climbed the stairs to the records office one last time before sealing the annex. She lit the lamp, unlocked the cabinet Tam had built—heavy iron, sand-filled walls, a lock worth the name—and looked at what lay inside: the complete account, the maps, Ede's original journal, copies of the Meridian logs, and a single sheet in her own handwriting, the annex statement, the line that would greet whoever opened this box in fifty years or a hundred:

*Material withheld deliberately. Held in trust for those who ask better questions than we did.*

She read it twice. Then she added, beneath it, in smaller letters, a sentence she had been carrying for weeks without knowing where to put it:

*The undersigned failed once, and was given a second chance she did not deserve. Spend yours well.*

She locked the cabinet. She blew out the lamp. Through the office window, far downstream, she could see the lights of the ferry landing—two lanterns burning at the new crossing, where Ilsa and Dominic were working late, fitting the new winch gear by lamplight, their shadows leaning together across the boards.

Juno stood in the dark a moment longer, hand resting on the warm iron of the cabinet door, keeper of a secret that was also a promise.

Downstairs, the street door creaked. Footsteps—unhurried, unfamiliar, the tread of someone taking the measure of the town. Juno frowned, moved to the window, and looked down into the lamplit street.

A stranger stood before the records office, travel-worn, consulting a piece of paper against the numbers on the buildings. And though Juno could not have said how she knew—not yet, not consciously—something in the set of those shoulders, the way the figure looked up the street toward the river with an appetite that had nothing to do with ferries, made her move quietly away from the light and stand listening in the dark.

The stranger knocked once on the office door below, soft and patient, like someone who had all the time in the world and intended to use all of it.
