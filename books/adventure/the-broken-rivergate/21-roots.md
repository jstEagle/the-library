## 21. Roots

The duffel bag sat on the ferry deck where it had sat for three days now, packed and repacked, its buckles worn to a shine by ten years of roads. Dominic looked at it the way she'd once looked at maps—measuring distance, weighing what lay on either side of a line.

Downstream, the river ran wide and brown-green toward the lowlands, toward Callow's Bend and every town beyond it that had never heard her name or had heard it too well. Upstream, past the new pilings Ilsa was driving into the shallows, the water came down clean from the canyon, carrying nothing but silt and spring. The Broken Rivergate was sealed. She'd felt it close behind them like a door in her own chest.

"You're going to wear a hole in those boards," Ilsa said, not looking up from the piling she was bracing. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow, forearms corded, a mallet resting against her hip like it had grown there. "Three days of pacing. The wood's starting to know your footsteps."

"I'm deciding."

"You decided Tuesday." Ilsa straightened, wiped her hands on her thighs, and finally looked at her—that level gray look that had pulled Dominic out of a flooding chamber when every rational part of her had been ready to stay there. "You just haven't told yourself yet."

Dominic huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. The morning was cool enough to see her breath. Mist hung over the crossing in long ribbons, and somewhere upstream a kingfisher rattled off its branch. It was, she thought, an obscene kind of beauty. It kept insisting on itself. It kept asking her to notice.

She crouched beside the duffel and worked the top buckle loose. Just the one. Inside: two shirts, a coil of rope, her father's brass compass with the cracked face, the map Ede Voss had pressed into her hands in a boarding house room that smelled of lamp oil and dying. The map was soft as cloth now from handling. She'd promised a dead man she'd finish his work, and she had—or as near as finishing ever came. Ede's last camp had been folded back into the hillside; his journal went to Juno Pell, who had wept over it for an evening and then begun, methodically, to build the archive that would hold all their truths under seal.

The second buckle. The third.

"What are you doing?" Ilsa asked.

"Unpacking."

Ilsa crossed the deck in four strides and looked down into the open bag like it might bite. "That's not unpacking. That's looking at your things."

"It's a process." Dominic lifted out the compass, turned it over. Her father's initials were scratched inside the lid—*R.B.*—in a hand she'd spent ten years trying not to recognize in mirrors. "I'm keeping the rope," she said. "And this. The rest—" She stopped. The rest was everything she'd need on a road, and nothing she'd need anywhere else. That was the whole problem with a life built for leaving. You could pack it in ninety seconds, and then you had to live with how little it weighed.

Ilsa sat down across from her, cross-legged, patient as driftwood. "Say it out loud. It helps. Tam says so, and Tam's usually right about demolition and occasionally right about people."

"If I say it, it's real."

"That's generally the point of saying things."

Dominic looked downstream again. Somewhere out there was the next crew, the next border town, the next set of hands that would shake hers and never learn her surname. Somewhere out there was the version of her life that required no courage because it required no staying. She had been that woman for a decade. She knew her the way you know a coat you've outgrown—by the places it no longer reached.

"I want to stay," she said. It came out rough, half-swallowed. She said it again, clearer. "I want to stay. Here. With you, if—if that's—" 

"Yes," Ilsa said, before she could finish fumbling it, and grinned, and the grin undid something in Dominic's chest that had been knotted since a boarding house in Callow's Bend. "Took you long enough. I was starting to think I'd have to flood another chamber to get an answer out of you."

"Don't joke about that."

"I'm joking about exactly that, because if we don't laugh about it we'll dream about it, and I've had enough dreams about black water to last me." Ilsa reached over and took the compass from her hand, weighed it, handed it back. "Keep that too. A ferrywoman should know where north is."

* * *

They bought the crossing from old Marta Senn on a Thursday, in her kitchen, over tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. Marta had run the ferry for thirty-one years and had been waiting, she claimed, for someone fool enough to take it off her since year twenty-nine.

"The river takes the crossing some springs," Marta warned, sliding the deed across the table. "Takes the dock sometimes. Took my husband's hat in '09 and he never forgave it. You'll fight it every year and lose most years, and it'll still be the best work you ever do. That's the bargain. Sign it or don't."

Dominic signed. Her hand only shook a little.

The name on the line read *D. Brightwater*. She looked at it for a long moment—the first time in ten years she'd written the whole thing where anyone could see—and found that it fit differently than she remembered. Not smaller. Not heavier, either. Just *hers*, the way a tool fits a hand after long use.

"Marta," she said, as they were leaving, "why'd you wait for us? You could've sold to the Institute's surveyors. They offered, didn't they? After the testimony?"

Marta snorted. "Halvard's people offered twice what you did. But I watched you rebuild my east mooring after the flood took it, and you didn't charge me, and you didn't make a speech about it. Institute men build things that look good until the water tests them. You build things that look ugly and hold." She waved them out with her teacup. "Go on. First fare's at dawn, and dawn doesn't wait for sentiment."

* * *

The weeks that followed had a shape Dominic had never lived inside before: repetition. Ferry at dawn, fares at midmorning, repairs in the afternoon, Ilsa's shoulder against hers on the deck at dusk. Farmers with grain. Surveyors with instruments who tipped badly. A peddler with a cart full of tinware who talked the entire crossing and tipped extravagantly. Children who leaned over the rail to watch fish and had to be hauled back by their collars.

Tam Ovid came by twice a week, ostensibly to inspect the pilings he'd helped drive, actually to eat Ilsa's stew and complain about the quarry work he'd taken in Callow's Bend. "Blasting rock for someone else's road," he said darkly, spooning up his third helping. "No artistry in it. No stakes. You blow a wall knowing it'll fall. Where's the tension?"

"You're a menace," Ilsa said fondly.

"I'm an artist between commissions." He pointed the spoon at Dominic. "And you're a farmer now. A *ferry* farmer. I climbed into a flooded aquifer heart with you. I placed charges in a rising canyon while Halvard's crew shelled the upper weir. And you spend your days poling a flat barge across eighty yards of tame river."

"Tame is underrated," Dominic said. "Ask the river."

But she was smiling, and Tam saw it, and shook his head with theatrical despair, and came back Thursday anyway.

Juno came less often and stayed longer. She'd taken rooms above the Callow's Bend records office, where she was assembling the sealed archive—Ede's journal, her mother's stone markers transcribed and translated, the crew's sworn testimonies, the photographs Tam had taken before the water rose. Everything true. Nothing locatable. The seal was Juno's design: three keys, held separately, not to be joined for fifty years, or until the Institute's charter lapsed, whichever came first.

"It's done," Juno told them one evening, sitting on the ferry deck with her boots dangling over the water, a leather case on her knees. "Forty-one documents. Cross-indexed. Copied twice—one copy in the Bend vault, one with a lawyer in Meridian City who hates the Institute more than I do, which is saying something." She touched the case the way other people touched relics. "Your mother's markers, Dominic. I translated the last one properly this week. The one at the inner gate."

Dominic went still. She'd read it herself, in the flooding chamber, by lamplight, with the water climbing her shins. She'd never said all of it aloud.

"*For everyone's good,*" Juno recited softly. "*I stayed so the river could keep what it holds. If you are reading this, choose what you carry, and carry it well.*" She looked up. "I used to think that meant the work. The expedition. The great discovery. Now I think it just meant—this. Whatever you're carrying. Carry it well."

"I'm carrying a ferry," Dominic said.

"And doing it beautifully." Juno smiled, and it was the old smile from the first days of the expedition, the idolizing one—but changed, tempered, aimed at the person sitting in front of her rather than the ghost behind her. "I wanted my hero back," she said quietly. "Whole and public. I got something better. I got the truth, and a friend, and the knowledge that heroes are just people who stay when it counts." She stood, brushed off her skirt, and handed Dominic the case—not the archive, just a single folder from it. "This one's yours. Unsealed. I thought you should have it outside the vault."

Inside was a photograph, water-stained at one corner: the original expedition, eleven people squinting into the sun at the canyon rim. Her mother front and center, laughing at something out of frame, one hand raised mid-gesture. Her father beside her, compass in hand. And at the edge, half-turned away, a young man with a prospector's pan—Ede Voss, decades younger, already watching the canyon instead of the camera.

Dominic looked at it for a long time.

"They knew," she said finally. "Mom knew what the Institute would do. Dad probably argued for the contract money—they needed it, I've read enough now to know they were broke. And in the end she chose the river over the funding, and he stayed with her, and the Institute let them die for it and called it an accident." She closed the folder. "Ten years I ran from that story because I thought it was a story about shame. It was a story about staying. I had it backwards the whole time."

"You couldn't have known," Juno said.

"No. But I can now." She tucked the photograph inside her jacket, over her heart, where the map used to ride. "That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

* * *

Autumn came down the valley in stages, gilding the cottonwoods along the banks, sharpening the mornings. The ferry crossing settled into its rhythms, and Dominic settled into hers, and discovered—to her genuine surprise—that she loved the work. Loved reading the water each morning like a page that had rewritten itself overnight. Loved the small competence of a pole set true, a load balanced, a nervous horse calmed with a low word. Loved that at the end of the day there was a fire, and Ilsa across from it, and nowhere she needed to be by morning.

One evening in late October, Ilsa found her on the deck repairing a cleat, tools spread around her, lamplight gold on the boards.

"You've got sawdust in your hair," Ilsa said.

"It's a look."

"It's a very domestic look. It suits you terribly." She sat down close, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while they just listened to the river—the sound Dominic had decided was better than silence, better than music, the sound of a thing going on being itself whether anyone watched or not. "Can I ask you something?" Ilsa said eventually. "The road. Do you miss it? Honestly. Not the polite answer."

Dominial considered it honestly, which took a while. "I miss the first hour of a new place," she said at last. "That's all. The part where anything could happen. But I've figured out the trick of it—you can get that hour every morning if you pay attention. The river's never the same river twice. Heraclitus or whoever. Juno quotes him when she's showing off." She set down the file and looked at her hands—her digging hands, Ede had called them, the hands that had recognized her across a boarding house room. They were calloused differently now. Ferryman's hands. Her mother's hands, come to think of it; there'd been a ferry in the old photographs too, in the years before the expeditions, before the Institute. Maybe it had always been waiting for her here, this exact stretch of water, patient as inheritance.

"I don't miss it," she said. "I keep waiting to feel trapped and I just feel—rooted. Like the word finally means something good."

Ilsa leaned her head against Dominic's shoulder. "Good. Because I ordered lumber for the winter dock extension, and it arrives next week, and you're not allowed to leave before it does."

"Were you worried I would?"

"Every day," Ilsa said cheerfully. "Right up until about six weeks ago. Then I stopped. You unpacked your bag, Brightwater. Nobody unpacks and runs. It's against the laws of nature."

* * *

Winter was mild. The archive grew. Tam blew a stump for Marta Senn as a favor and was banned from the property, which he declared the finest review of his career. Juno published the testimony—just the testimony, names and cover-up and all—and the scandal moved through the Institute's upper floors like water through a cracked weir. Halvard resigned in March; the papers said *personal reasons*. Dominic read the account twice and felt nothing she'd expected to feel. No triumph. Just a door closing quietly, far away, in a house she no longer lived in.

Spring came early that year, and warm, and the melt came down from the high country in earnest.

Dominic stood on the deck at first light and watched the river rise—watched it come down brown and muscular from the canyon country, carrying ice chunks the size of tables, filling its channel the way it had filled it for ten thousand years. The new crossing held. Ilsa's pilings held. The rebuilt moorings held. Water that would once have torn through the lower chambers of the Rivergate, that would once have been fought and dammed and strip-mined and stolen, simply passed—harmless, unhurried, *kept*—on its way to the sea.

Her mother's work, holding. Her own hands, holding.

"Fare!" Ilsa called from the landing, and there was a traveler on the near bank: a lone figure with a heavy pack and a surveyor's case, standing at the edge of the dock and studying the far shore with the particular hunger of a person looking for something.

Dominic picked up her pole and pushed off into the current, into the first hour of a brand-new day, and turned the ferry toward the stranger, and the stranger raised a hand, and the river carried them both.
