## 1. The River Keeps No Records

The leak announced itself the way leaks always did—not with drama, but with a small, patient sound, like someone licking a stamp somewhere below the deck plates. Casper Okafor heard it through the roar of the engine and the slap of brown water against the hull, and she did not stop what she was doing. Stopping was for people whose boats didn't leak. She finished cinching the last strap on the cargo net, checked the knot twice out of habit rather than doubt, and only then crouched at the stern hatch and lifted it.

Water in the bilge. Two inches, maybe three. Enough to matter by evening if she ignored it, not enough to matter now.

"Tomás," she called over her shoulder, and the word traveled forward along the deck to where Tomás Reyes sat cross-legged beside the outboard like a man attending his own wedding, hands moving over the carburetor with a tenderness he had never once shown a living woman. "We're taking on again. Starboard seam, aft of the fuel box."

"I told you," he said without looking up. "I told you last week. The patch is a bandage on a wound that needs surgery."

"The boat is a bandage on a wound that needs surgery," Casper said. "We're all bandages. Re-patch it."

He muttered something in Spanish that she chose not to translate, because translating it would have meant agreeing with it, and there was no room in her morning for agreement. The river took up all the space there was—the Shyok running high and milk-brown with glacier flour, wide as a road here in the braided flats, narrow as a throat three kilometers downstream where the canyon pinched and every crossing became a negotiation with physics. She ran passengers across twice a day when the traffic came: traders with burlap sacks of apricots, soldiers off leave who paid double and asked no questions, an occasional foreign trekker who thought the word *ferry* meant something official existed somewhere upstream with a stamp on it. Nothing official existed anywhere within four days' walk of this bend of river. That was the whole point of it, and the whole danger, and most days the two felt like the same thing viewed from different banks.

She wiped her hands on her thighs—her trousers had gone past dirty into geology some weeks ago—and climbed to the tiller as the first passengers of the day picked their way down the mud bank. A family of six from the valley settlements, going to market with two goats and a crate of chickens that complained like creditors. An old man with a walking stick who boarded last and slowest and looked at Casper with the particular expression of someone who remembered when this crossing had been free, or at least cheaper, or at least run by somebody else.

"Hold," she said, which was all the safety briefing anyone got, and pushed off.

The current took them sideways immediately, the way it always did, and she corrected with a touch of tiller and more throttle than she liked, the engine coughing once before it caught clean. Below her feet, faintly, the leak licked its stamp. Above her, the sky was doing something she didn't love: a high gray ceiling sliding in from the southwest, too early in the season, too organized to be nothing. Monsoon weather arrived like gossip—it started at the edges, among people who weren't sure yet, and by the time everyone agreed on what was happening, it was already raining.

She'd worry about the sky later. The sky kept its own schedule. Her schedule was shorter and meaner.

* * *

The ledger lived in a plastic bag inside a tin box inside a dry bag, because paper was the one cargo Casper never trusted to the river. She opened it at midday, moored in the lee of a gravel bar while the family unloaded goats and the old man pressed coins into her palm one at a time, counting them out loud as though she might dispute the arithmetic. She wouldn't have. She never disputed arithmetic. Arithmetic was the only honest thing left in her life.

The numbers were bad, but she had known they would be bad; knowing in advance was a small mercy, like being told the name of the disease. Fuel costs up since the checkpoint at Skardu had doubled its informal toll. One passenger short of breaking even on Tuesday's runs. And at the bottom of the page, underlined twice in her own handwriting from three weeks ago, the figure that made the rest of the page decorative: **4,800**. Dollars, though the currency hardly mattered anymore; debt had become its own universal language, fluent everywhere.

Four thousand eight hundred dollars, owed to a man named Gurchen who collected debts in the Karakoram the way rivers collected silt—slowly, everywhere, without malice and without mercy. The money had bought her this boat, or rather had bought her the right to keep running this boat after the first season's earnings vanished into repairs and bribes and the simple friction of existing illegally in a country that tolerated her the way the river tolerated a boulder: something to flow around, until the flood moved it.

And under the 4,800, a newer line, added just yesterday when the letter had come up from Skardu with a trader who also carried mail for a fee: tuition. Meera's spring term. Due in sixty days, or the school would release her place, and there was a waiting list, and the waiting list did not care about floods or blacklists or mothers who had become geography.

Casper closed the ledger and sat with her hand flat on its cover for a moment, feeling the river shiver the hull beneath her.

Meera was eleven now. Eleven, and living with Casper's sister outside Kathmandu, in a house with a garden and a gate that locked and a school where the other girls' fathers were doctors and aid workers and men whose names appeared in newspapers for good reasons. Meera wrote letters that were getting longer and more careful, the way children's letters do when they sense, without being told, that the adult on the other end is fragile. *Are you safe? Is the water cold? Priya Aunty says you eat too little.* Casper answered every letter within a week, always, because reliability was the one inheritance she could still deliver. But she had not been able to answer the last one fully, because the honest answer to *when are you coming home* was *when I am no longer a woman two countries have erased*, and that answer would break something in her daughter that Casper had sworn, on the worst night of her life, standing over a body bag in a helicopter's rotor wash, never to break.

So: sixty days. Tuition plus interest on Gurchen's principal, if she could get him to roll it, which he would, for a price, because everything rolled downhill toward Gurchen eventually.

"You're doing the face," said Priya, dropping onto the gravel beside her with two chipped enamel mugs of tea. Priya Nair, medic, cook, conscience, the only member of the crew who had followed Casper across two borders and one disgrace without ever asking for the story. "The one where you look calm but your jaw is doing the work of a hydraulic press."

"I don't have faces. I have operations."

"You have exactly one face and it's currently lying." Priya handed her a mug. The tea was over-steeped and slightly gritty with river dust, and it was the best thing Casper had tasted in a month, which said less about the tea than about the month. "How bad?"

"Sixty days," Casper said. "All of it. Tuition and Gurchen both, or I start next season owing more and seeing her less."

Priya was quiet. Downstream, Ferro—who was nineteen and built like a question mark and worshipped Casper with the pure, unhelpful devotion of a dog watching a door—was showing the old man how the engine worked, narrating loudly and mostly wrongly. He'd named the boat *La Paciente*, which Tomás found hilarious and Casper found uncomfortably accurate: a vessel held together by treatment, never cured.

"Ferro could take more shifts," Priya offered. "He'd work for free. He'd pay you to let him work."

"He works for free now. That's the problem." Casper drank. "None of us are making it through another season like this, Priya. You know it. Tomás knows it. Ferro doesn't know it because he's nineteen, but his knees will inform him eventually. I can't keep paying people in scenery."

"So we find money."

"There is no money. There's the river, and the river keeps no records, and neither does anyone who pays me in eggs."

Priya smiled at that despite herself, then let the smile go, which was her way of saying the serious thing without saying it: *you could go back, you could appeal, you could tell them what really happened on Nanda Kot.* Casper heard it anyway. She always heard it. And she always gave the same answer she gave the river when it asked her to trust the far channel in flood: *not that one.*

Because the record of what happened on Nanda Kot existed, technically—in reports, in a tribunal summary, in the memory of a client's family who had needed someone to blame and been handed Casper's name like a receipt. But records could be read two ways, and the second reading required evidence she didn't have and influence she couldn't afford, and so the official version stood, and she had learned to live downstream of it. Mostly.

* * *

They saw the patrol boat at four in the afternoon, a gray smudge against the gray water two bends down, coming upstream with the arrogant ease of something that had never once worried about its bilge.

"Border security," Tomás said, binoculars up, voice flat. "Not customs. Security. New paint."

"New paint means new budget," Casper said, already moving. "New budget means quotas. Kill the stove smoke, get the tarp over the cargo. Ferro—"

"Under the netting," Ferro said, already folding himself into the gap between the fuel drums and the spare propeller, where a boy of his size could vanish if he kept still, which he could, when it mattered. It was the only discipline he'd ever shown reliably.

The trick was never to hide. Hiding was confession. The trick was to be boring—to be a licensed-looking local ferry doing licensed-looking local things, piloted by a woman with the right kind of weathered face and a laminated card, purchased long ago from a clerk in Gilgit, that said *River Transport — Inland Waters* in Urdu and English and a third language Casper suspected the clerk had invented. The card had survived three inspections. It would survive this one if the inspector was bored, and inspectors were usually bored, because boredom was the occupational disease of patrolling water where nothing worth seizing ever floated.

She turned *La Paciente* downstream at an angle that said *routine*, throttled to a speed that said *unremarkable*, and waited.

The patrol boat grew. It was bigger than the last one, twin engines, a mounted light even though the sun was still up, and two men on deck in flak vests that looked new enough to squeak. Casper kept her eyes on her own wake and her hands loose on the tiller and performed, internally, the calculation she hated most: not whether she could outrun them, but whether today was a day she'd have to find out. Outrunning meant the canyon, meant the narrows, meant betting the boat and everyone aboard on her memory of water she'd last seen at half this volume. The river would forgive nothing. The river forgave nothing as a matter of policy.

The patrol drew abreast, close enough that she could see the younger officer's chin, the way it lifted as he read her hull, read her face, read the laminated card she was already holding up before he asked.

"Ferry?" he called, in Urdu.

"Inland transport," she answered, matching his flatness. "Market run. You want to inspect?"

A pause. The older officer said something to the younger one, low. Then, waving them on with the magnanimity of a man exercising power he hadn't decided how to use yet: "Papers are fine. Keep to the near bank. Water's rising—there's weather coming out of the south."

"Thank you," Casper said, meaning none of it, and held her course until the gray smudge became a gray memory, and only then did she discover that her hands had gone white on the tiller, and only then did she make them let go.

"That," said Ferro, emerging from the netting with straw in his hair and delight all over his face, "was incredible. You didn't even blink."

"I blinked plenty. You were hiding."

"I was *strategically positioned*."

Casper almost smiled. Almost. What stopped her was the thought arriving uninvited, the way the worst thoughts did, in the quiet after adrenaline: *sixty days*. Sixty days of inspections and tolls and leaks and monsoon, and at the end of it either Meera stayed in school or Casper finally, formally, became the kind of woman who failed her child in writing.

* * *

The letter itself she read alone, after dark, in the lean-to behind Tomás's workshop shed, by the light of a headlamp hung from a nail. She had read it once already, on the trader's porch, standing up, fast, the way you drink something bitter. Now she read it slowly, letting each sentence do its work.

The school's letterhead. The amount. The date—sixty days, confirmed, no grace period mentioned, because grace periods were for institutions that believed in them. And beneath the school's letter, folded together with it, a note in Gurchen's handwriting, which was beautiful and precise, calligraphy learned in some madrassa long before it learned bookkeeping:

*Mrs. Okafor. I am given to understand your circumstances remain difficult. I am not a cruel man. Sixty days for the tuition, as the school requires. For my principal, I will extend the same term, with the usual adjustment. After that, the boat becomes mine, and I am told the river route is profitable, so perhaps this arrangement need not feel like loss. Respectfully, and with wishes for your health, G.*

He was offering to take the boat. Politely. With wishes for her health. Casper read the paragraph three times, admiring it against her will the way you admire a well-thrown punch.

Without the boat she was nothing. Not poor—nothing. A guide with no mountains, a captain with no vessel, a mother with no remittances. The blacklist had taken Nepal and Peru; Gurchen's arithmetic would take the last country that hadn't bothered to notice her.

She folded the letters along their original creases and put them back in the tin box, and sat in the dark listening to the river move in the dark, carrying everything it carried—silt, ice-melt, the occasional unmoored log, the secrets of two nations' borderlands—toward a sea that would keep none of it. The river keeps no records. That had always been the comfort. Tonight, for the first time, it sounded like the threat: no record of what she'd paid, no record of what she'd lost, no record anywhere that Casper Okafor had once been the best high-altitude guide of her generation, before a mountain ate a client and the world decided she had fed it to him.

Her headlamp flickered. Somewhere beyond the shed, Tomás laughed at something Ferro had said, and Priya's voice rose gently over both of them, and the sound of it—this ragged, unpaid, leaking little family—went through her sharper than any of the day's fears.

She needed four thousand eight hundred dollars and a year of tuition in sixty days. The river paid in eggs.

Which was why, when the stranger walked into the tea house at Skardu three mornings later wearing expedition boots too clean for the road and a smile she recognized from a decade of bad dreams, Casper Okafor was already halfway to hating him before he said a single word.

Devlin Marsh set his pack down, looked around the smoky room as if pricing it, and said, "Hello, Casper. I hear you still read water better than anyone alive."
