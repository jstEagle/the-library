## 2. A Ghost with Good Credit

The tea house at Chilas Bend had no name, which suited Casper Okafor fine. Named places attracted inspectors. Unnamed places attracted only the thirsty, the lost, and the occasional smuggler with a polite smile and cash in small denominations—all of whom she could handle.

She'd been there three days, which was two days too long. The *Sarpan* was beached on the shingle downstream with her stern quarter out of the water and Tomas buried to the shoulders in her engine housing, swearing in Spanish and Urdu with equal fluency. A hairline fracture in the cooling jacket. Nothing fatal, but nothing cheap either, and Casper had done the arithmetic on the napkin three times hoping the answer would change. It hadn't. The answer was that she could afford the part or she could afford diesel, and the river did not run on either.

So she sat at the corner table by the window with a glass of tea going cold in front of her and a ledger open to a page she kept not looking at. Rain came down the valley in grey curtains, stitching itself into the Indus, and the light through the window was the color of old pewter. She liked this light. It didn't flatter anyone, including her.

She was forty-one years old and she had learned, in the long apprenticeship of the last six years, to be nobody. It was a skill, being nobody. It required discipline. You wore the same faded jacket until it became invisible. You spoke just enough Urdu to order food and just enough Shina to joke about the weather, and never a word more. You let the young guides at the crossing assume the scar on your jaw came from a bar fight instead of a crevasse fall, because bar fights were legible and crevasse falls invited questions. You became, in effect, water—present everywhere, remarkable nowhere, impossible to hold.

She had gotten good at it. That was the humiliating part. She'd once been *somebody*—Casper Okafor, IFMGA, first woman to guide the east face of Nanda Kot in winter, her name on expedition permits and in magazine profiles and once, absurdly, on a commemorative patch sold at a Kathmandu gear shop. Now her greatest professional achievement was that the patrol boat on this stretch of river had stopped checking her papers, because the patrol officer had decided she was a fixture, like the tea house itself, like the crooked prayer flags on the roof, like the smell of diesel and cardamom that lived in everything.

The letter had come two days ago, forwarded through three hands and a mule driver to reach her, which meant her sister in Rawalpindi was still willing to act as a post office, which was something. Casper hadn't opened it for a full day. When she finally had, sitting right here at this table while the rain came down, it had been exactly what she expected: a tuition notice for Amara's spring term, and beneath it, in her sister's cramped handwriting, a single line—*They came by the house again. Sixty days, they said. I didn't tell her.*

Sixty days. The debt collector's arithmetic was simple and it was designed to be: the amount was just small enough that a person might believe she could climb out of it, and just large enough that she never would. That was not a loan. That was a leash.

Casper turned the ledger page without reading it and looked out at the river instead. The Indus was high with early snowmelt, brown and muscular, hauling whole trees downstream like a man carrying firewood who has forgotten why. She watched it the way other people watched television. There was a standing wave below the bend that hadn't been there last week, and a new channel opening along the far bank where the gravel bar was cutting through, and she thought, distantly, *that bar will be gone in nine days, and there'll be a hole where the eddy line used to be, and somebody's going to die there if they don't know to look.*

Nobody would pay her for knowing that. Nobody paid for knowing things anymore. They paid for permits, for connections, for being somebody.

She was still watching the water when the jeep pulled up.

* * *

She knew the walk before she saw the face. That was the thing about Devlin Marsh—six years hadn't touched his walk. He came down the tea house steps the way he came up mountains: with the unhurried economy of a man who has never once doubted that the ground would hold him. Long stride, weight centered, eyes already doing inventory of the room. He'd added a beard, iron-grey and well-trimmed, and a scar over his left eyebrow that hadn't been there on Nanda Kot, and a jacket that probably cost more than the *Sarpan*. But the walk was the same. The walk was the same and it went through her like a fishhook.

He saw her immediately. Of course he did. He'd walked in looking for her; the inventory of the room was theater, and they both knew it.

"Casper Okafor," he said, and sat down across from her without being asked, which was also the same. "They told me in Gilgit you were dead. They told me in Skardu you'd gone to Oman. They told me in Chilas you were a myth."

"Chilas was closest."

He smiled. He had a good smile; it had always been his best equipment, better than his climbing, which had been competent, or his judgment, which had been lucky. "You look well."

"You look expensive."

"Money agrees with me." He signaled the boy behind the counter with two raised fingers, an easy fluency in the place that told her he'd been here before, or somewhere like it, recently enough that the gestures were warm. "I heard you run a ferry crossing on the Shyok tributaries. Illegal, I'm told."

"It's a community service."

"It's a felony in three jurisdictions."

"Only two. The third one's still deciding." She turned her glass a quarter-rotation on the table. "Why are you here, Devlin?"

He let the smile go. That, too, was familiar—Marsh could switch off charm like a headlamp, and what remained underneath was always a surprise. Tonight what remained was something tired and sharp, a man who had been awake too long in a high place. "I need a guide."

"You have guides."

"I have guides who read maps. I need one who reads water." He leaned forward, forearms on the table, and for a moment the rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the tin roof like static. "There's a stretch of the Keshar headwaters that doesn't appear on any survey. The gorge system above the old concession. No satellite data worth trusting, no ground truth since the nineties. I have a permit for the Concession—exclusive, legal, stamped in Islamabad—and I need someone to take a boat up water that has never been run, through gorges that eat boats, and get out the other side. I'm told there is exactly one person alive who can do it."

"I'm told she's dead."

"I'm told she's sitting in a tea house losing money at a rate I could hear from the road." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. Marsh never needed cruelty; he had always been able to find the exact weight of a truth and set it down gently, like a load on a scale. "Triple rate. Plus hazard. Plus a bonus on completion that would clear what you owe and then some. I know about the debt, Casper. I'm sorry. I know how that feels."

"You don't know anything about how that feels." Her voice came out flat and quiet, and she was distantly grateful for it, because what was underneath the flatness was not quiet at all.

"No," he said. "Maybe I don't."

The tea arrived. He didn't drink it. He wrapped his hands around the glass like a man warming himself at a fire he didn't trust.

* * *

Nanda Kot came for her the way it always did—not gradually, but all at once, the way a serac lets go.

They were at six thousand three hundred meters and the wind had a sound she had never heard before or since, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Marsh was below her on the fixed lines, three rope-lengths down, and Verner was above her, and Verner was the client, and Verner was dying, and the whole architecture of that day was built out of moments she had spent six years trying not to arrange into a story, because every arrangement ended the same way.

*Turn back, Casper. We can still turn back.*

That was Verner's voice. That was Verner, two hours before, at the shoulder, looking up at the summit ridge with the particular hunger of a man who had paid eighty thousand dollars to stop being afraid of something. *You said weather window. You said three days.*

*It's closing,* she'd told him. *It's closing now.*

And then the mountain had spoken over both of them, and the world went white and sideways, and when the wind died she was hanging in her anchors with blood in her mouth and the rope above her was cut—*cut*, she had told every inquiry, every board, every drunk who asked—cut clean, not abraded, not loaded to failure, *cut*—and Verner was not on the mountain anymore. He was two thousand feet down in the icefall, in a place they could not go, in a place they did not recover him from for four months.

The inquiry had found her judgment wanting. The syndicate that owned the concession—Verner had been their man, she'd learned that later, though she hadn't known it then—had found her useful as a scapegoat. The guild had found her convenient to lose. And Casper had found, in the end, that it didn't matter what she knew to be true, because truth without evidence was just a story, and stories about dead clients told by the guide who survived them were worth exactly nothing.

*You should have turned back earlier.* That was Marsh, at base camp, after. Not cruel. Never cruel. Just precise, setting the weight down gently on the scale. *You should have read the weather. That was your job, Casper. That was the one thing that was your job.*

She had not spoken to him in six years. She had thought, in her more honest moments, that she hated him. Sitting here now, watching him hold his tea, she understood that what she actually felt was worse than hate. It was the feeling of looking at a man who had been on the mountain that day, who had heard the same wind, who had descended alive and rebuilt his name and his credit while hers was being fed to the concession lawyers—and who had never once, in six years, said the word *cut*.

"Casper." He was watching her. He had watched her go somewhere, and he had let her go, because he was patient. "I'm not here to reopen anything."

"Then what are you here for?"

"Water," he said. "Just water. I need the best river reader alive, and the best river reader alive is wasting away in a tea house because the world decided she's a ghost." He reached into his jacket and set something on the table between them. A document, heavy paper, folded once. "Triple rate. Hazard pay. Completion bonus that makes the debt a rounding error. Sixty days of work and you're a free woman."

She didn't touch it. "What's the cargo?"

"Survey instruments. Hydrology. The Concession wants baseline data on the headwaters before the next phase."

"Next phase of what?"

"Of the survey." He smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "You don't need to know more than that. You're a guide. You guide."

The old shame rose in her like floodwater, and with it, unexpectedly, the old rivalry, and she found to her surprise that the rivalry was still load-bearing. Because he was sitting there with his expensive jacket and his stamped permit and his easy fluency, and she was sitting here with a cold tea and a ledger full of red, and the difference between them was one dead man and six years of who got believed.

"No," she said.

He nodded, unsurprised, as though she'd said *yes* in a language he'd have to translate. "I figured you'd say that first."

"There's no *first*. There's just no."

"There's a second, though." He stood, and left the folded paper on the table, squared to the edge, neat as a tent stake. "There's always a second with you, Casper. There was a second on Nanda Kot—you could have turned around and you kept climbing, because you're the kind of animal that finishes things. It's your best quality." He pulled his hood up against the sound of the rain. "It's also why I'm not worried."

"Devlin." He paused at the top of the steps. "If you go up that gorge with a crew of strangers and a permit you flash around like a passport, you're going to drown them all. That water doesn't care what's stamped in Islamabad."

"I know," he said. "That's why I want you." And he went out into the rain, and the jeep took him away down the valley road, taillights dissolving in the wet dark like coals dropped in a river.

* * *

She sat for a long time. The tea house emptied. The boy banked the stove and went home through the rain, and after a while the only light was the pressure lamp over the counter and the grey moving water beyond the window, and Casper Okafor sat alone with a folded document she had not touched and a ledger she could not balance and the sound of a mountain clearing its throat, six years old and still going off in her head like an unexploded shell.

*You should have turned back earlier.*

Maybe. Maybe that was true. She had spent six years paying for maybe.

She picked up the paper. It was heavy, expensive stock, and the permit was stamped in blue ink with an officialdom that looked right, that *felt* right, the way Marsh's walk looked right. Keshar Concession. Hydrological survey. Exclusive access. Her name was already on it, typed into the guide line, which meant he'd written it before he came, which meant the refusal had been a formality he'd budgeted for, the way you budget for a storm day.

She should burn it. She knew that with the same certainty she knew the standing wave below the bend would kill someone in nine days. She should burn it and go down to the *Sarpan* and tell Tomas to patch the jacket with epoxy and prayer, and stay a ghost, because ghosts were safe, ghosts didn't drown crews, ghosts didn't climb past the weather window, ghosts didn't have daughters who were about to lose their school.

The rain came harder. Down in the dark, the Indus kept no records. That was the whole reason she loved it, and the whole reason she'd come to it, and the whole reason it had never once saved her: the river kept no records, and neither, anymore, did she. No guild number. No license. No name on a summit register. Just a woman at a table with a debt and a deadline and a document that smelled, faintly, of money and Islamabad.

Sixty days.

She looked at the permit a long time. Then she folded it once along his crease, put it inside her jacket, over her heart, where a man carries the things he intends to lie about later, and went out into the rain to tell Tomas they'd be running the Keshar headwaters in the morning.

The river said nothing. It never did. But somewhere upstream, in the gorges that ate boats, the water was already rising.
