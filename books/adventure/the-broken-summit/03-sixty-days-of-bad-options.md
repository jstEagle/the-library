## 3. Sixty Days of Bad Options

The ledger didn't lie, but Casper had gotten very good at making it whisper.

She sat in the stern of the *Mendicant* with a headlamp clamped over her brow and the account book open on her knees, and she did what she did every season's end: she counted. Diesel, down to the liter. Rope, rated by wear and frayed past safe on three lines. Food stores that would stretch to October if nobody got sick and nobody got hungry, which were two different bets with the same losing odds. The engine Tomas had rebuilt twice from salvage—once with parts he'd traded off a Chinese dredge crew, once with parts that had no honest provenance at all.

The numbers came out the way they always came out. Not dead. Not alive, either. Suspended, like a body in deep water, neither sinking nor surfacing.

Four hundred and eleven dollars. That was what remained after the fuel for the last run, after the bribes at the checkpoint below Skardu, after the rice and the oil and the cylinder of cooking gas Priya had insisted on because scurvy was a stupid way to die and she'd said so with the flat finality of a woman who'd watched it happen. Four hundred and eleven dollars against a debt of nine thousand, due in sixty days, or the letter went where letters go when they're meant to wound: to her daughter, to the school, to every institution that kept a file on Miriam Okafor's mother and why she wasn't there.

Casper closed the ledger. Opened it again, as though the arithmetic might have softened overnight. It hadn't. Numbers never did. That was the one mercy of them.

Above her, the night sky over the Karakoram was doing its indifferent spectacular work, stars thick as spilled salt, the peaks black cutouts against a sky so clear it looked like a lie. Somewhere downstream, a patrol boat's searchlight had swept the far bank an hour ago—the border police running their seasonal harassment of unlicensed traffic—and Casper had killed her lamp and sat motionless until the beam moved on, an old animal trick, stillness as survival. The river muttered under the hull. It kept no records. It remembered nothing and forgave nothing and asked nothing, and some nights that felt like the only honest relationship left in her life.

She heard Ferro before she saw him, because Ferro hadn't yet learned to move quietly, or perhaps had learned not to bother around her, which amounted to the same thing and was somehow worse.

"You're still up." He dropped onto the gunwale with the boneless ease of nineteen, legs swinging over water black as ink. He smelled of engine grease and the cheap clove cigarettes he thought nobody knew about. "Tomas says you've been out here two hours."

"Tomas should be asleep."

"Everyone should be asleep." He grinned at her, that open, unguarded grin that made her chest hurt in a place she didn't examine. In the dark his face was all angles and optimism, a face that had never been blacklisted from anything, that had never watched a client fall eight hundred meters into a couloir and heard the sound the body made when it stopped. "I ran the numbers too," he said. "On my phone. We're fine till spring if we don't buy diesel."

"We're not buying diesel?"

"I mean—" He caught himself, and she watched the understanding arrive in him like weather, moving across his features in stages: first confusion, then calculation, then the gray dawn of it. "We're not fine," he said quietly.

"No."

"How not fine?"

"Ferro."

"How not fine, Casper?" He used her name deliberately; he was one of maybe four people alive who did, and he knew the weight of it. "I can take it."

She almost laughed. Almost told him the truth—that fine was a country she'd been exiled from along with Nepal and Peru, that not fine was simply the climate now, the weather she dressed for every morning. Instead she said, "Go to bed," and he went, but slowly, looking back once from the bank, and she knew he'd do his own audit tonight, and she knew what he'd find, and she hated herself a little for letting him find it alone.

* * *

Morning brought Priya to the tea house with two cups of salt tea and the expression she wore instead of asking questions.

Casper took the cup. They sat on the bench outside, watching the river run brown and muscular with early snowmelt, and for a while neither spoke, because that was the architecture of their friendship—load-bearing silences, gaps you could trust your weight to.

"Tomas's shoulder," Priya said finally. "It's worse. He won't say so, but I've watched him favor it for three weeks. If we run another season of heavy cargo without proper rest and actual physio, he's looking at surgery he can't afford, in a country where he has no papers." She sipped her tea. "Also, his daughter writes to him. Did you know? Every month. He keeps the letters in the toolbox with the good sockets."

"I didn't know."

"He thinks nobody knows. That's rather the point of Tomas." Priya turned her dark eyes on Casper, unhurried, clinical, merciless in the gentlest possible way. "And Ferro hasn't bought new boots since March. He's been cutting drainage holes in the old ones and telling me they're a design feature. And I have—" she paused, and Casper watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched her finish it anyway, because Priya did not flinch from data, "—about five months of medication left for my mother in Rawalpindi, and the pharmacy there does not extend credit to women whose remittances stop."

The river said nothing. Rivers were good at that.

"So you've audited us," Casper said.

"I audited us in June. You audited us last night. I saw your light." Priya set down her cup. "The question isn't whether we're broke, Casper. We're always broke; broke is our natural state, like altitude sickness. The question is what you're going to do about the man who came into this tea house yesterday offering triple rate, and why you're sitting here drinking tea with me instead of signing his paper."

"You know about that."

"Everyone knows about that. Devlin Marsh walked in here like a monsoon front, everyone in the room pretended not to watch, and you sent him away with his hat in his hand. Ferro nearly wept. Tomas polished an engine part very aggressively." She picked up her cup again. "So. What are you going to do?"

What was she going to do. She thought about the permit lying on Marsh's table yesterday afternoon, the Keshar Concession printed across it in official-looking type, the route sketched in red ink through country she knew better than she wanted to—through country she had sworn off the way a burn victim swears off fire. She thought about Nanda Kot, which she did not let herself think about, which arrived anyway at 3 a.m. most nights: the rope team, the weather window closing, Marcus Webb's voice on the radio saying he could make the summit push if she'd just hold the high camp one more day, and her own voice saying yes, yes, one more day, the decision that had cost a man his life and Casper everything else. The inquiry board had called it guide error. She had called it a hundred other things—weather, ambition, Webb's own insistence—but none of the names had stuck, and the blacklist had, and here she was.

"He wants me to guide him into the Keshar headwaters," she said. "Nobody goes into the Keshar headwaters. There's a reason."

"There's usually money in reasons."

"That's what worries me."

Priya studied her for a long moment. Then she stood, brushed off her trousers, and said, in the tone she used for setting bones: "You'll sign it. We both know you'll sign it. The only question is whether you tell us the truth about why, or whether you tell us it's just money." She gathered the cups. "For the record, I'd prefer the truth. But I'm experienced at working with whatever you give me."

She left Casper alone with the river and the salt taste of being known.

* * *

She found Tomas in the engine bay that evening, elbow-deep in the block, singing something low and tuneless in Spanish—a bolero, half-remembered, the kind of song a man sings when his hands need occupation and his mind needs silence. He worked the way he did everything: patiently, thoroughly, with the resigned competence of someone who had stopped expecting the world to improve and committed instead to keeping his small corner of it running.

Casper handed him a wrench before he asked for it. Twenty years of partnership lived in gestures like that.

"The port injector," he said, without surfacing. "It's eating itself. I can keep it alive another season if I baby it. Two seasons if God intervenes, and God and I haven't spoken since Cartagena." He backed out of the engine, wiped his hands on a rag already more rag than cloth, and looked at her with those patient brown eyes. "You're here about the money."

"I'm here about the injector."

"Casper." He folded the rag with great care, squaring its corners, buying time the way careful men do. "My daughter is fourteen. She wants to study. Real school, in the city, not the village school where I studied until I left to fix boats. The tuition is—" he named a number, and it was not a large number, and it might as well have been a mountain. "Every father says he will give his child a better life. Most of us are lying, or we die trying. I would like to stop lying."

"And if I can't pay you this season?"

He smiled then, slow and sad. "Then I stay anyway. You know this. You know all of it—you did the audit, I saw your light too, we all saw your light, this boat is not big enough for secrets." He put his hand briefly on her shoulder, the one that wasn't bad. "But staying is not the same as surviving. Ask me the real question."

She asked it. "Can you survive another season like this one?"

Tomas looked at the engine, at the beautiful ruined machine he'd kept alive through will and wire and prayer, and said nothing, and the nothing was the answer.

* * *

Midnight found her back in the tea house, which was empty except for the owner sleeping behind the counter and the smell of yesterday's parathas gone cold in the air. The lamp burned low. Outside, the river ran on in the dark, keeping no records, and above it the mountains stood in their ancient indifference, and somewhere south of all of it was a girl doing homework who believed her mother ran legitimate expeditions, and a debt collector who knew better, and sixty days ticking down like water dripping into a cave that would eventually hollow out whatever it touched.

Marsh's contract lay on the table where he'd left it. Triple rate. Sixty days, coincidentally, or perhaps not coincidentally—men like Marsh did nothing coincidentally. The Keshar Concession stamped in official purple. His signature already dry at the bottom, waiting for hers like an open mouth.

She read it through twice, looking for the trap, finding only terms. Payment on completion. Route at guide's discretion. Client's objectives confidential. That last clause she read three times. Confidential from whom?

Just money, she told herself. This is just money. Nine thousand dollars clears the debt, keeps Miriam in her school, buys Tomas's daughter her tuition, refills Priya's shipment, replaces Ferro's boots and the port injector and the three condemned ropes. Just money, moving from one pocket to another, no different from any run she'd ever done, no different from ferrying smugglers' whiskey or survey crews or the German tourists who wanted to photograph glaciers before the glaciers finished leaving. She had never asked what cargo wanted. Cargo was cargo. The river didn't judge its freight.

The pen was on the table. Marsh had even left a pen, the presumptuous bastard, a good pen, heavy brass, the kind of object a man carries when he expects people to sign things.

Casper Okafor, who had read water on three continents, who could look at a rapid and see its whole biography—where it was born, what it had eaten, how it would kill you—who had survived blacklists and floods and the particular drowning that is grief, sat alone at midnight holding a pen over a contract and understood, with total clarity, that she was lying to herself. That it was not just money. That Marsh had come to her specifically, that the route ran through the valley where her life had ended, that a man like Devlin Marsh did not pay triple rate for anything except a reason, and that she was going to sign anyway, because the alternative was the letter arriving at her daughter's school, and because Priya was right, and because Tomas's silence was right, and because Ferro's boots were right, and because a woman can live on pride exactly as long as she can live on four hundred and eleven dollars, which is to say not at all.

Self-deception, she thought, is just commitment that hasn't admitted what it wants yet.

She signed.

The scratch of the nib sounded enormous in the empty tea house, louder than the river, louder than the mountains. When it was done she sat looking at her own name—Casper Okafor, guide of record, Keshar Concession expedition—and felt the strange vertigo of a door closing behind her, the specific loneliness of having chosen a path and hearing it seal itself shut.

Then she folded the contract, tucked it inside her jacket, and stepped out into the cold starlight to wake her crew, because they shipped with the tide, and the tide waited for no one.

Ferro took the news like a boy handed a festival. Tomas nodded once, unsurprised, and went to check the injector. Priya looked at Casper for a long moment—not at the contract, at her—and said only, "Just money?"

"Just money," Casper said, and hated how easily it came, and how much it cost.

Priya held her gaze one second longer than comfortable, then turned to pack the medical kit. "Then we leave tomorrow," she said. "And Casper—if it's just money, why did Marsh ask for you by name?"

Downriver, faint on the wind, Casper thought she heard the sound of an engine that wasn't theirs.
