## 4. The Stranger Who Reads Currents

Ilsa Voss had learned long ago that the best way to enter a place was to look like you belonged there slightly more than anyone else. Not arrogance—arrogance invited challenge. Just the settled air of a woman who had already been expected, whose arrival was a formality rather than an event. She wore it now like a rain shell: light, functional, and not to be seen through.

The river camp at Chilas Bend smelled of diesel, wet rope, and frying onions. Three boats sat pulled up on the shingle, and only one of them looked like it could survive anything worse than a bad mood. That would be Okafor's boat. Marsh had described it in his briefing with something close to reverence—the old hull, the patched transom, the tiller rig that shouldn't have worked but did—and Ilsa had spent the flight from Islamabad turning that description over, looking for the seam in it. Marsh never described anything without wanting something from it.

She stepped off the jeep with her instrument cases stacked around her feet and waited. It was a test she ran on everyone: how long before someone came to move her things, to make her welcome, to make her useful. The answer told her where power lived.

It took eleven minutes. A young man came down from the fire first—all elbows and restless energy, cigarette behind his ear like a pencil—and stopped dead when he saw the cases.

"Those aren't survey cases," he said.

"Good eye." Ilsa crouched and flipped the latches open one by one, letting him see: the acoustic Doppler current profiler with its housing of matte black ceramic; the sediment traps nested in foam like eggs; the sealed water-quality sonde still in its factory wrap. "Most people think hydrology is a bucket and a stopwatch."

"Who are you?"

"Ilsa Voss. Marsh's hydrologist." She stood, brushed grit from her knees, and offered her hand. He took it after a pause, his grip careful, as if she might be rigged to something. "And you're the one who keeps the engines alive. Tomas, yes? Marsh said you could hear a fuel pump lie."

That earned her half a smile. Good. The mechanic was the keel of any river crew; win him and the rest followed.

"And that—" Tomas nodded upriver, toward the woman standing at the waterline with her back to them all, watching the current the way other people watched sleeping animals, "—is the boss. Whatever Marsh told you."

"I know what Marsh told me," Ilsa said. "I'm interested in what's true."

Tomas laughed, short and surprised, and carried two of her cases up to the fire.

* * *

Casper Okafor turned when Ilsa's shadow fell across the shallows, and Ilsa performed the assessment she'd been rehearsing since Islamabad and found it useless inside four seconds. You couldn't assess a woman who was assessing you faster. Okafor's eyes went to the cases, then to Ilsa's hands—reading calluses, reading history—then out to the river, then back. The whole audit took less time than a blink. Her face gave away nothing except competence, which is itself a kind of mask.

"You're the scientist," Okafor said. Not a question. Her voice was low and gravelled, a voice used to being heard over water.

"Hydrologist."

"On this river?" Something moved at the corner of Okafor's mouth—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one that had died young. "The Indus headwaters don't need studying. They need surviving."

"Everything needs studying. Survival just means you studied it too late." Ilsa set her remaining case down on a boulder and opened it with deliberate slowness, letting the instruments catch the light. It was theater, and she knew it, and she suspected Okafor knew it too. But there were languages even suspicious people understood, and expensive equipment was one of them. "Marsh hired me to map flow dynamics through the Keshar gorge system. Discharge rates, sediment load, channel migration. The concession wants baseline data before they break ground on access roads."

"The concession wants a lot of things." Okafor looked back at the water. "You've run whitewater before? Actual whitewater, not a research raft on a class two?"

"Class four in Norway. Class five once, in Chile, by accident."

"By accident." Now the almost-smile again. "That's usually how it goes."

"I'm not here to be a passenger. Tell me what you need me to carry and I'll carry it. Tell me what to bail and I'll bail. But I'll also be taking my measurements, and some of them will slow us down, and I won't apologize for that."

Okafor studied her for a long moment. The wind came down the valley carrying the smell of snowmelt and, faintly, of the mine—something mineral and wrong beneath the clean cold, though Ilsa doubted anyone else in camp could taste it yet. Okafor could. Ilsa watched her nostrils flare slightly, watched her glance upstream without seeming to, and filed it away.

"Questions," Okafor said finally. "You ask a lot of them. Marsh warned me about that."

"What did he tell you to do about it?"

"He didn't. That's what worries me." Okafor pushed off the rock she'd been leaning against. "Dinner's at seven. Boats load at dawn. If your instruments can't get wet, leave them ashore, because everything gets wet."

She walked away, and Ilsa stood in the shallows feeling, for the first time in six years, slightly out of her depth. It was not an entirely unpleasant sensation. It was the sensation of meeting someone who might actually be worth deceiving carefully, rather than casually.

* * *

The questions started at dinner, because of course they did. Ilsa had planned them like an itinerary, each one innocuous on its own, each one a single degree off true north.

To Ferro, the young one, all elbows and hero worship: *How long has she run this stretch? Does she ever talk about Nepal?* — and Ferro, starved for someone new to talk to, gave her everything: the blacklist, the expedition that ended badly, the daughter somewhere south paying tuition on money that didn't come. Ilsa listened with her chin on her hand and let him pour.

To Priya, the medic, quieter and harder to crack: *What does she take for the shoulder? I noticed she favors it in cold water.* Priya's eyes sharpened at that—*you noticed that in one afternoon?*—and Ilsa shrugged and said, *I notice currents. People are currents.* Priya didn't laugh, but she answered, eventually, and the answer was worth the wait: the shoulder was old, the pain was managed, and Okafor had never once let it be an excuse.

To Marsh himself, over the last of the tea while the others washed dishes: nothing at all. With Marsh she asked about permits and logistics and radio frequencies, all the boring scaffolding of the official job, and watched him perform the boring scaffolding flawlessly, and understood that whatever was actually in those dry boxes he'd loaded at Skardu, he intended it to stay a secret until the river made secrecy impossible. Fine. She had secrets of her own, packed in foam, photographed twice.

And to Casper Okafor, alone at the fire afterward, when the others had gone to their tarps and the river had dropped its daytime voice to a deep night mutter—Ilsa asked the question she'd flown four thousand kilometers to ask, dressed up as small talk:

"Why did you say yes to him?"

Okafor didn't pretend not to understand. She fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch. "Money."

"That's the reason you'd give a bank."

"It's the only reason that's true." A pause. The firelight found the lines around her eyes and made them deeper. "Why do you care?"

"Because men like Marsh don't pay triple rate for navigation. They pay triple rate for absolution. Someone convinced him you're worth forgiving, and I'd like to know who sold him that story, because whoever it was wanted you on this river badly enough to spend real money."

For three full seconds Okafor said nothing, and Ilsa felt the silence the way you feel a change in current under a calm surface—the boat hasn't moved yet but something below has decided to move it. Then Okafor laughed, one dry note, and shook her head.

"You interrogate like a woman who's been interrogated."

"Everyone from Oslo has been interrogated. It's called winter."

"Mm." Okafor stood, stretched until her spine cracked, and looked down at Ilsa with an expression that was almost friendly and entirely unreadable. "Ask your questions, Voss. Ask all of them. Just remember the river asks better ones, and it doesn't wait for answers."

She walked to her tarp. Ilsa stayed by the fire a while longer, listening to the water, doing arithmetic in her head: four days to the gorge mouth, if the weather held, and the clouds stacking over the western ridgeline at sunset had not looked like weather that intended to hold.

* * *

She couldn't sleep. This was normal—six years of normal—but tonight the insomnia had a direction to it, so she got up quietly, stepped over Ferro's sprawled sleeping bag, and went down to the boats.

Moonlight lay on the shingle like spilled milk. The river talked to itself in the dark, that endless gravel-and-glass murmur, and Ilsa walked the line of hulls with her hands in her pockets until she reached Okafor's boat and knelt beside it.

She hadn't come to snoop. That was the honest truth, or near enough: she'd come because sleepless nights on expeditions were best survived with your hands busy, and because there was a thing she'd noticed at dinner that wouldn't stop bothering her.

At dinner, when the wind had gusted, the mooring line on Okafor's boat had sung—a particular note, tight and high. And Ilsa, who had spent her twenties measuring rivers and her thirties measuring what rivers did to the things people built near them, had recognized the note. It was the sound of a line attached to a bow cleat that had worked loose a quarter-turn past safe. Not broken. Not dangerous yet. Just wrong, in the way a sentence is wrong when one word is quietly misspelled.

She checked the cleat first. There it was: the backing plate shimmed with a leather washer that had compressed to paper, the whole fitting able to rock a few millimeters under load. On a calm night, nothing. On a flood-night in a narrowing gorge, with the boat snatched sideways by a standing wave—it was exactly the kind of small failure that became a large one at the worst possible moment.

She fixed it. It took twenty minutes by feel and moonlight: re-shimming the backing plate with a strip cut from a spare fuel-gasket, torquing the bolts in sequence, testing with her whole weight. Then, because her hands were warm now and her mind refused to cool, she went over the rest of the rigging the way she'd go over a bridge before crossing it with instruments.

That was when she found the second thing.

The anchor rode—Okafor's spare line, coiled in the bow locker—was tied off with a bowline whose tail had been trimmed too short. In freezing water, in the dark, with numb fingers, that knot would be a fight to untie or, worse, a temptation to cut. And the anchor itself was stowed aft, ten feet from the line, which meant that in an emergency anchorage—say, a flash flood pushing you toward a sieve, say midnight, say monsoon—you'd be crawling the length of a pitching deck to marry line to anchor while the river tried to kill you.

Someone had reorganized this boat. Recently. Someone who wanted the anchor slow.

Ilsa sat back on her heels in the dark and felt the hair rise along her forearms.

Or—no. Or someone had simply stowed it lazily, the way every crew on every river stowed things lazily, and she was six years of paranoia dressing coincidence in intent. That was the other possibility, and it was just as plausible, and she had learned—had paid tuition, blood tuition, to learn—that the difference between the two possibilities was the difference between a survivor and a corpse, and you did not get to choose which possibility was true. You only got to choose which one you prepared for.

She prepared. She re-tied the bowline with a proper tail, doubled it through for a quick-release. She moved the anchor forward, into the bow locker beside the line, and lashed both down where a hand could find them blind. She re-rigged the throw bag on its rail mount so it deployed without snagging. Small work, quiet work, the work of a woman who had once watched a boat full of people become a boat full of names because nobody had spent twenty minutes checking knots.

When she finished, the sky over the eastern ridge had gone the grey of old dishwater. Dawn soon. She should sleep, or at least lie down.

Instead she stood at the stern and looked at the sandbar.

It wasn't visible. That was the point of it. The main channel bent hard against the far bank below the camp, and every eye—even a guide's eye, even *that* guide's eye—would read the smooth water on the near side as the deeper line, the easy line, the line you'd take half-asleep at dawn with tea still in you. But Ilsa had spent yesterday evening walking the bank with her range finder while the others ate, and she'd clocked the tell: a faint riffle-line downstream, a V of disturbed water pointing *upstream*, which meant the current was piling against something submerged. Sand, most likely, deposited by the spring melt, sitting eighteen inches under a surface that lied about it beautifully.

If Okafor took the near channel at dawn, she'd hit it. Probably survivable—a hard grounding, a lurch, maybe a cracked skeg, maybe cargo shifted and soaked. Probably survivable. *Probably* was a word Ilsa had sworn off in a collapsing tunnel six years ago, along with several other words she could no longer pronounce without tasting copper.

So she did the last thing. She took the boat's spare painter, waded shin-deep into the freezing shallows, and drove a driftwood stake into the gravel bar at the exact spot where a wake would show the channel's true edge—visible from the helm at dawn, meaningless to anyone who didn't know to look for it. Then she marked the safe line too, three small stakes stepping the deep channel out from the bank, subtle as prayer flags, obvious as a runway to anyone who read water for a living.

Let Okafor decide what it meant. Let her decide whether it meant a stranger's competence or a stranger's trap. Either way, Ilsa thought, climbing back onto the shingle with her boots soaked and her heart hammering from the cold, either way the boat would live through the morning, and the morning was the only country either of them lived in anymore.

* * *

Casper Okafor was already at the boats when Ilsa came down with her kit bag, and she was standing very still, looking at the stakes in the water.

She looked at them for a long time. Then she looked at Ilsa, and her face did the thing it had done yesterday—gave away nothing except competence—but this time the competence had a different temperature. Colder. More interested.

"You were busy last night," Okafor said.

"Couldn't sleep. Your bow cleat was loose. I tightened it." Ilsa set her bag on the shingle and met her eyes. "Your anchor was stowed ten feet from its line. I moved them together. And there's a sandbar under the near channel that your eyes will lie to you about at dawn, so I staked the true edge. The safe line runs wide right, off the three markers."

Silence. The river filled it, talking its gravel talk. Down the beach, Ferro was loading dry boxes; Tomas was muttering to an outboard; Marsh stood at the far tent pretending to study a map and listening to every word.

"You boarded my boat," Okafor said, "in the night, and changed it."

"Yes."

"If I'd found you aboard, I'd have put you off this trip."

"I know. That's why I finished before you woke up."

Another silence, longer. And then something happened in Okafor's face that Ilsa would think about later, on the water, in the gorge, in all the loud dark days that followed: something unclenched. Not trust—nothing so generous. Recognition, maybe. The look of one locked room acknowledging another.

"The bar," Okafor said slowly. "Eighteen inches down, near channel. I'd have hit it."

"You'd have hit it."

Okafor looked at the stakes a moment more. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something almost private. "Six years ago a man I trusted re-rigged my gear while I slept. It cost a life. So understand me, Voss: I'm not thanking you. I'm watching you. Every day, all the way up that gorge. Whatever you're carrying besides instruments—" she lifted her kit bag and handed it back, handle first, a courtesy shaped like a warning, "—the river will bring it up eventually. It always does."

She turned and waded out to check the transom patch, and Ilsa stood on the shingle with her bag in her hand and her pulse louder than the water, thinking: *she knows. Not what. But she knows there is a what.*

Above the western ridgeline, the clouds that had stacked at sunset had grown overnight into a wall, blue-black and patient, and the first wind of the day came down the valley smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet anywhere, but would.
