## 5. Upstream of Everything

The gorge announced itself before they saw it.

Casper heard it first, two bends below the confluence—a low, grinding hum that lived in the hull of the boat and in the bones behind her ears. Water moving through stone. Not the broad, patient voice of the Indus tributaries she'd been running for three years, all silt and swagger, but something older and less forgiving. The Keshar headwaters. She had known they were coming the way you know a tooth is going to ache.

"Sound's good," Tomas said from the engine well, though he said it the way men say things when they want them to be true. He'd rebuilt the outboard twice since Gilgit with parts cannibalized from a tractor and a prayer, and every hour upstream he listened to it the way a father listens to a sleeping child.

"It's good," Casper agreed, and put her eyes back on the water.

The river narrowed as the walls rose. In the morning light the gorge ran grey-green, deep enough that the current didn't so much flow as *pour*, smooth on the surface and violent underneath, the way a polite man can be violent underneath. She read it the way other people read faces: the dark seam along the left wall where a spring bled cold water into the main channel; the submerged shelf downstream that would fold a boat like paper if you gave it your stern; the eddy fence behind the mid-channel boulder, spinning its slow carousel of foam. Every feature was a sentence in a language she'd been fluent in since she was nineteen years old, and reading it steadied her the way nothing else did anymore.

Behind her, Marsh sat amidships with the concession file open on his knees, pretending to study it. He'd been pretending to study it for an hour. What he was actually doing, Casper suspected, was watching her hands.

She kept them still. That was the discipline now—keeping them still.

* * *

They made the first camp at dusk on a gravel bench the color of old bone, wedged between the river and a cliff face streaked white with raptor droppings. Ferro got the stove going and produced rice and lentils and, through some alchemy Priya refused to explain, actual fresh onions, which he distributed like a man handing out gold teeth. Ilsa set up her instruments on a flat rock at the water's edge—the strange brass-and-glass contraptions Casper still couldn't name—and stood over them with her collar up against the wind, taking readings by headlamp.

"Flow rate's climbing," Ilsa said, not looking up. "Glacier melt plus early season. Tomorrow it'll be maybe fifteen percent higher than today."

"Fifteen percent is nothing," Marsh said.

"Fifteen percent in a gorge this width is a different river." Ilsa closed her notebook. "I'm just telling you what the instruments say."

"And what do the instruments say about tomorrow's route?" Marsh asked, and there it was—the thing he'd been circling all day, the question under the question. He turned to Casper then, and in the firelight his face had the careful blankness of a man about to step onto thin ice. "You've run this section before."

It wasn't a question. Casper fed another stick into the fire and watched the sparks climb.

"I've run everything between here and the headwaters," she said. "That was the point of me."

"The point of you," Marsh agreed softly. "The route I've mapped—it follows the main channel up through the gorge system, but there's a portage section where the canyon narrows past running. We'll line the boats through. And after that—" he paused, and Casper understood, with a cold, slow certainty, exactly what was coming, had perhaps understood it since the tea house in Chilas, since the moment she'd signed her name at midnight and told herself it was only money— "after that we cut up the Sarpang valley."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river, loose rock clattered down a scree slope, the mountain clearing its throat.

"Sarpang," Casper said.

"You know it."

"I know it." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of how level it came out. "That's Nanda Kot country. That's the approach valley. That's where—" She stopped. Started again. "You mapped a route through the one valley in the Karakoram I have sworn off, and you waited until we were two days inside the gorges to tell me."

"I told you because you needed to be committed first," Marsh said. "Because if I'd told you in Chilas, you'd have said no, and if you'd said no, none of us eat this winter. Because I need the best guide on this river, and the best guide on this river happens to be the woman who knows Sarpang better than anyone alive, including the people who live there." He spread his hands. "I'm sorry. I'm not sorry. Both are true."

"That's not an apology, Devlin. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic keeps boats floating."

"Arithmetic got a man killed."

The words left her before she could catch them, and the whole camp went quiet around the hole they left. Ferro froze with the ladle halfway to the pot. Priya looked up from her kit. Even Ilsa, twenty meters away at the waterline, turned her headlamp toward them, a pale eye in the dark.

Marsh held her gaze. To his credit—he had always had this much credit with her—he didn't flinch and didn't perform flinching either. "Nanda Kot wasn't your fault," he said quietly. "Everyone who was there knows it wasn't your fault. You're the only one still serving the sentence."

"Then everyone who was there can go to hell too." She stood, brushed the rice from her trousers, and walked to the edge of the camp, where the gravel met the black silk of the water, and stood listening to the gorge grind its endless stone.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She lay in her bag watching the stars wheel slowly behind the cliff rim and listening to the river, and the river sounded like that night six years ago the way a certain song sounds like a certain person. The roar of the icefall coming off Nanda Kot's shoulder. The radio crackling. Okafor, come in, Okafor, and the silence where Halloran's voice should have been. The way the snow had kept falling afterward, indifferent, beautiful, filling the tracks of the rescue party as fast as they were made, erasing everything, erasing even the evidence of the erasing.

*He fell,* the inquiry had concluded. *Client error. Route conditions exceeded safe margins.* And then, quieter, in the way such conclusions always carry a second sentence inside them: *And the guide should have known.*

She had known the route. She had known the conditions. She had checked the anchors herself, twice, because Halloran was heavy through the shoulders and climbed like a man arguing with the mountain instead of negotiating with it, and she had liked him for it, and she had let herself like him, and somewhere in that liking was the slack in the rope. Or there wasn't. Six years and she still couldn't find the flaw in her own work, which was worse than finding it. A found flaw could be fixed. An absent one just circled forever, a vulture over a summit that never resolved into anything you could bury.

At some point in the dead hours she became aware that someone had come to sit near the fire, and that the someone was Priya, feeding sticks into the coals with the economy of a woman who had spent years making small resources do large work.

"You should sleep," Priya said.

"So should you."

"One of us being awake is enough." Priya looked at her across the flames, and her face in the firelight was older than her thirty-odd years, the way faces get when they've seen the insides of too many people. "Your hands were shaking at the tiller today. Coming through the narrows."

Casper said nothing.

"I wasn't going to say anything in front of the others." Priya turned the stick over. "But tomorrow we go into Sarpang, and I'd rather know now whether it's your hands or your head I need to worry about. Hands I can tape. Heads are harder."

"My hands are fine."

"Casper."

"They're fine, Priya."

Priya nodded slowly, accepting the lie the way a nurse accepts a patient's claim to be fine—with professional courtesy and total disbelief. "Six years ago I worked a clinic outside Kagbeni," she said. "Avalanche year. We had a man come in whose family died above the village, and he was fine, he was completely fine, he carried bodies and dug foundations and smiled at relief workers for eleven months. Then one day he picked up a teacup and couldn't hold it. Just that. Nothing else wrong. His body had decided it was done carrying whatever he wouldn't put down." She set the stick down. "I'm not asking you to put it down. I'm asking you to tell me if the cup starts slipping."

Casper looked at her own hands, pale in the firelight, long-fingered, scarred across the knuckles from rope and rock and one bad winter with a cleaver. Steady. They looked steady. They had looked steady all day.

"They won't slip," she said.

Priya rose, shook the grit from her blanket, and paused at the edge of the firelight. "In Sarpang," she said, "there are people who remember you. The boy's family—the one who asked about his father—they summer on the lower slopes. If we stop at Serkhang for resupply, like Marsh's plan says we will—"

"I know what Marsh's plan says."

"Good." Priya wrapped her blanket around her shoulders. "Then know it walking in. Not finding out halfway."

She went to her bed, and Casper stayed by the fire until the stars began to fade behind the eastern ridge, and when she finally slept, she dreamed of snow falling upward, erasing the mountain from the bottom, and woke with her fists clenched so hard her palms held four crescents of blood.

* * *

Morning came up copper. The gorge had changed overnight—not from the water rising, though Ilsa's instruments confirmed it had, but from the light, which entered late and left early and turned the stone walls into furnace doors. They broke camp in the blue shadow and pushed off into a current that took the boats like it had been waiting for them.

The narrows came at midday, exactly where her memory said they would come, and for forty minutes Casper stopped being a debtor and a disgraced guide and a mother who owed sixty days of money, and became simply a pair of hands and a pair of eyes and a channel of water. She lined the first boat through the slot with Marsh hauling the bow line and Ferro on the stern, the hull screaming along the rock within a hand's width of disaster, and brought it out the far side clean, and did the second boat alone while the others scrambled the portage trail, and brought that one out clean too, and felt—for those forty minutes only—the old, uncomplicated joy of work done well, which was the closest thing to forgiveness the world had ever offered her.

Then the canyon opened, and the Sarpang valley opened beyond it, and the joy went out of her like heat out of a cracked kettle.

She had told herself she wouldn't recognize it. Six years was long enough; rivers move boulders, forests fall, trails migrate. But the valley had been waiting for her with the patience of geography. There was the lateral moraine where they'd staged the loads. There was the moraine crest where the base camp tents had made their five bright dots. There was the hanging glacier on Nanda Kot's north shoulder, smaller now—or she was farther away, or time had shrunk it, or grief had inflated it—but there, unmistakably, the white tongue that had spoken once and never spoken again.

The tiller trembled under her hands.

Not much. A vibration anyone else would have read as the current, the engine, the shallow water over the gravel bar. But she knew the difference between the river's shaking and her own, the way you know your own cough in a crowded room, and she gripped the wood harder and stared straight ahead at the channel and did not look at the mountain, and the trembling traveled anyway—from her wrists to her forearms to the place behind her sternum where she kept the sealed room, and something in the sealed room shifted its weight.

"You're drifting right," Ilsa said.

She was beside Casper at the tiller, crouched low, ostensibly checking the depth sounder. Her voice was quiet, pitched under the engine noise, meant for no one else.

"I'm not drifting."

"You're drifting right. Half a meter and you kiss the gravel bar." Ilsa didn't look at her. Her eyes stayed on the water ahead, reading it, and Casper realized with a small, unwelcome shock that Ilsa was reading *her* too, the same way—currents under surfaces, force under calm. "Whatever that mountain is to you," Ilsa said, "it's also to starboard. Don't give it your attention and don't give it your wake. Look where you want to go."

It was good boat-handling advice. It was also, Casper understood distantly, the most perceptive thing anyone had said to her in six years, and she hated how much it helped.

She corrected the heading. The gravel bar slid past harmlessly. Ahead, the valley widened toward the resupply village on its bench of terraced fields, smoke already rising from morning fires, and Casper knew without being told that some of that smoke came from hearths belonging to people who would know her name, and not kindly.

Priya appeared at her shoulder with tea in a dented tin mug, pressed it into her hands, and folded Casper's fingers around the warmth with both of her own.

"Hold this," Priya said. "Both hands. Doctor's orders."

Casper held it. The tea was terrible and too sweet and exactly right. Ahead, Serkhang grew out of the hillside stone by stone, and the mountain stood over it wearing its glacier like a wound dressing, and Casper steered toward the village with her hands wrapped around the mug and her eyes fixed on the channel, looking where she wanted to go, and not once—not once—letting them drift to starboard.

But that night, moored below the village, she would learn what the valley remembered. And the boy would ask his question.
