## 6. What the Permit Doesn't Say

Ilsa Voss had learned to read documents the way other people read weather: for the pressure behind them, the front that hadn't arrived yet, the thing the sky was holding back. Her father had been a notary in Hamburg, a man who believed a stamp was a promise made of ink, and she had spent her childhood at his elbow watching him tilt papers against the lamp to check for erasures, for the ghost-lines where something had been lifted and replaced. *Paper remembers*, he used to say. *People forget. Paper never does.*

She thought of him now, three days upriver from the last town with a name on any map, sitting cross-legged in the stern of Casper Okafor's patched and stubborn boat while the Karakoram gorge walls rose around them like the inside of some enormous throat. The concession permit lay in its waterproof sleeve across her knees, and under the flat grey light that leaked down between the cliffs, she could see what Marsh had hoped no one would look for.

The stamp was wrong.

Not crudely wrong — whoever had made it had been careful, had matched the font, had aged the paper with tea or smoke or both so that the edges browned convincingly and the creases looked like they'd been folded in a government office rather than a hotel room. But the date read 14 August, and today was the ninth of August, and permits were issued, never pre-issued, because the Keshar Concession window opened only after the hydrological survey cleared — and the survey hadn't happened yet. She knew that because she was supposed to be the survey. That was the fiction of her employment: Dr. Ilsa Voss, hydrologist, retained to assess headwater flows for a legitimate extraction operation. She had signed on because it got her into the valley. She had told herself she would worry about the paperwork later.

Later had arrived, riding down the river on a current of grey meltwater, and the paperwork was a forgery so fresh the ink on the stamp still smelled faintly of solvent if you held it close enough. Which she did, casually, tilting the sleeve toward her face as though checking for water damage, while ten feet away Casper Okafor stood at the tiller reading the water with an attention Ilsa found almost frightening in its intimacy.

*Fourteen August.* Five days from now. The stamp had been pressed onto paper that hadn't yet earned it.

She lowered the permit into her lap and smiled at nothing, and felt the particular cold settle into her chest that she had come to know over six years — the cold of knowing something alone.

* * *

The crew had settled into its rhythms by the third day, and Ilsa had spent those rhythms learning them the way she learned everything: quietly, from the outside, cataloguing.

Tomas the mechanic rose before anyone else and could be heard muttering to the engines in Spanish, a running argument he always seemed to be losing but never conceded. Priya the medic kept her kit organized with a precision that suggested either military training or a personality that treated chaos as a personal insult; she asked Ilsa careful questions about altitude sickness and waterborne pathogens, and Ilsa answered carefully back, each of them circling, neither landing. Ferro was nineteen or twenty, all elbows and appetite, and watched Casper Okafor with the naked devotion of a boy who had decided someone was a story worth living inside.

And Casper. Casper Okafor was a problem Ilsa hadn't finished solving.

She had expected bitterness. She had done her research — everyone who came within a hundred kilometers of Nanda Kot's disaster had done their research, the story was too good and too terrible not to travel — and the woman she'd imagined from the accounts was harder than this one. The guide who "let the mountain eat" her client, the one blacklisted from two countries, the one whose name still surfaced in expedition forums like a bad omen. Ilsa had pictured someone carved down to function, all competence and scar tissue, nothing soft left to find.

What she'd found instead was a woman who checked Ferro's life jacket twice without being asked. Who ate last and least and pretended it was preference. Who stood at the tiller for eleven hours a day and never once complained about her hands, which Ilsa had noticed were wrapped in tape beneath the gloves, and which trembled sometimes when the gorge narrowed and the walls closed in — not from fear of the water, Ilsa didn't think, but from something the water passed through on its way to her.

Complicit or mark? That was the question she'd carried aboard, and three days hadn't answered it. A complicit guide would have asked more questions about the cargo. A mark would have asked fewer. Casper asked exactly the right number and then went silent in a way that could have been professionalism or could have been performance, and Ilsa hated how much she wanted it to be the former.

Because there was this, too, which she admitted only in the privacy of her own skull: watching Casper read the river was like watching someone speak a language Ilsa had studied for years but never dreamed in. On the second day they'd come to a bend where the water lay smooth as poured glass, and Casper had held them hard against the left wall for no reason Ilsa could see, and thirty seconds later a boil of submerged rock had erupted exactly where the right channel would have taken them, violent enough to flip a loaded boat. Casper hadn't even turned her head. She'd just said, "Eddy fence," in the tone of someone noting the time, and moved on.

Ilsa had re-rigged the bow lines that night while the camp slept — not because they needed it, but because she'd found the anchor point set in gravel that would scour out in a flood pulse, and because doing it gave her hands something to do besides hold the knowledge she couldn't put down. Casper had appeared beside her halfway through, said nothing, watched her finish, and then handed her a cup of tea that was somehow exactly sweet enough, and gone back to her bedroll. It wasn't thanks. It was acknowledgment. Ilsa had drunk the tea slowly and understood that she was in trouble of a kind she hadn't planned for.

* * *

On the fourth morning they tied off below a side canyon where a tributary came down in a white rope of snowmelt, and Marsh called a two-hour halt to take flow readings. This was Ilsa's cover work, so she took the instruments ashore and did it properly, because she was a real hydrologist even if the job was a lie, and because the data mattered to her for reasons Marsh couldn't imagine.

The tributary ran milky. Not glacial-milk blue-grey, which was normal, but a flat, opaque, chemical white-green that stained the rocks at its margin with a residue like dried paint. She crouched at the edge and dipped a sample vial, labeled it, and held it up to the light. Fine particulate hung in it, refusing to settle. Downstream, where the tributary met the main channel, the confluence drew a long visible seam — clear water and poisoned water running side by side for fifty meters before reluctantly mixing, as if even the river knew better than to swallow it.

She photographed the seam. She photographed the stain line on the rocks, high above the current level, which meant the contamination wasn't new. She photographed the dead zone downstream of the junction where the insect life should have been working the surface and wasn't — a stretch of moving water so quiet it looked painted.

"You're a long way from your gauges."

Marsh's voice, behind her. She didn't startle; she'd heard his boots on the scree. She capped the vial and turned.

"Confluence chemistry," she said. "Tributary's carrying suspended solids that don't match lithology." She let him chew on that. "Tailings, probably. Something upstream is grinding rock very fine and dumping the fines."

Something moved through Marsh's face — quick, controlled, gone. He was a handsome man in the way of men who had decided to be handsome as a career strategy, silver at the temples arranged rather than grown, and he wore surprise badly because he resented needing it.

"Old mine workings," he said. "Abandoned decades ago."

"Then the abandoned mine is still producing," Ilsa said pleasantly. "That's not abandonment. That's neglect with a budget."

He laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and easy, and she reminded herself that the best liars always laughed well. "This is why I hired you. You see the river like a crime scene."

"Everything's a crime scene if you look long enough," Ilsa said, and watched him carry that away, and wondered which of them he thought he was reassuring.

* * *

It was that evening, in the blue hour after the sun left the gorge but before full dark, that she opened the permit again — really opened it, with the whole document, not just the concession page. She waited until Ferro had gone downriver to wash the dinner pots and Tomas was under the engine cowling swearing at a fuel line and Priya was restocking the med kit with the concentration of a surgeon scrubbing in. Casper sat apart on a boulder at the water's edge, as she always did, facing upstream, a silhouette going to ink against the last light. Watching the water come toward them out of the dark, always. Like a sentry. Like a penance.

Ilsa spread the pages on her sleeping mat inside the fly of her one-person tent, angled her headlamp low, and worked through them the way her father had taught her, page by page, tilting each against the light.

Page one: concession grant, Keshar Valley Integrated Extraction Project, Ministry of Minerals and Northern Development. Stamp dated 14 August. Signature block: a deputy director whose name she looked up in her memory and found plausible, unremarkable, the kind of name that existed precisely to sign things.

Page two: environmental clearance certificate. Stamp dated 2 August. Also future-dated — four days ahead. Two different stamps, two different offices, both pressed with dates that hadn't happened yet.

Page three: the hydrological survey authorization. Her own name on it. Dated 28 July. This one was real — she knew because she'd watched it signed, in a hotel lobby in Skardu, by a man whose hands shook slightly and who wouldn't meet her eyes. Real paper, real signature, attached to a fiction. That was the elegant part. You built the forgery around one true document the way you built a counterfeit bill around one genuine serial number.

Page four stopped her breath.

It was an internal memorandum, misfiled — or filed deliberately, she couldn't decide which was worse — among the annexes. Letterhead she recognized: Meridian Himalaya Resources, the syndicate's public face, the company whose logo she had spent six years learning to hate from newspaper photographs and shareholder reports. The memo was brief. It referenced "the Okafor incident (Nanda Kot, 9 years prior)" and noted that "the route documentation provided by M. has proven durable" and recommended "continued retention of M. for access purposes, given his familiarity with the northern gorges and his standing with the guide community."

Durable. Provided by M. Retained for access purposes.

She read it three times. The headlamp seemed to dim, though she knew it was her vision narrowing, the blood leaving her extremities for somewhere more essential. *M.* There was only one M on this river. Only one man with silver temples and a good laugh and a permit folder full of dates from next week.

Nine years ago. Nanda Kot. The rockfall that killed a client named — she searched her memory, and found it, because she had memorized the names the way she memorized everything connected to the mine — Anand Sherpa? No. The client. The dead client was the client, the foreigner, the one whose family had sued and settled and vanished. But the guides' forum threads always mentioned the local names too, the ones nobody sued for, the ones who were just gone. And the memo said *route documentation provided by M.*, nine years ago, which meant Marsh had sold the approach — sold the route, the timing, whatever piece of it mattered — to the syndicate a year before the mine's expansion phase, and the rockfall had come down on schedule, and a client had died, and a guide had been destroyed for it, and the syndicate had written a memo calling the arrangement *durable*.

Ilsa sat very still inside her tent and listened to the river.

Her brother Jonas had died in a collapse at the Keshar adit six years ago. Officially: geological instability, unforeseeable, regrettable, compensation paid to next of kin, case closed. Unofficially — unofficially was the word she had worn smooth handling it for six years — the collapse had come two days after blasting that the mine's own geologists had flagged, in a section of tunnel that the safety report had marked amber and the production schedule had marked green. She had quit her university post eighteen months later. She had spent her savings on instruments and her grief on purpose, and when Devlin Marsh had approached her in Islamabad with a contract to survey the Keshar headwaters, she had known it was a lie and signed anyway, because lies are doors too, and she had walked through this one on her own legs.

But she had assumed she was the only liar aboard.

She looked at the memo until the words blurred, then photographed it — frame by frame, corner to corner, macro shots of the letterhead, the date, the initial *M.* — and then the stamps, all of them, every page, with a scale reference card beside each for authenticity. Her camera had no wireless. Its card lived in a lead-lined pouch against her ribs, next to the vial of milky water, and no one on this river could take it from her without taking the ribs too.

Then she repacked the folder exactly as she'd found it, down to the angle of the rubber band, and stepped out of her tent into the dark.

* * *

Casper was still on her boulder. Of course she was. In the starlight the river was a black ribbon stitched with white noise, and Casper sat facing it with her forearms on her knees, and Ilsa stood in the shadow of the tent for a long moment, weighing things.

Tell her. The thought arrived with the force of hunger. Tell her about the memo, the stamps, the *durable*. Watch what happens to her face. Complicit or mark — one sentence would sort it, one sentence would tear the answer out of her like a splinter.

But tell her how? *Your rival forged your permit and may have sold your ruin* — to a woman who already flinched at gorge walls, who ate last, whose hands shook at the tiller? If Casper was a mark, the news might break her, and Ilsa needed her whole; the escape routes out of this valley lived in that woman's body the way music lives in a pianist's hands. And if Casper was complicit — if some part of her had taken Marsh's money knowing what it bought — then warning her would only warn Marsh, through her, and everything Ilsa had carried for six years would burn unread.

So she tested instead. That was the discipline. You didn't interrogate people directly; you gave them small true things and watched which way they bent.

She crossed the camp quietly and sat on the adjacent boulder, close enough to talk, far enough to be deniable. For a while neither of them spoke. The river filled the silence the way water fills anything.

"The tributary today," Ilsa said finally. "The white one."

"Mm." Casper didn't turn.

"That's not glacial flour. I sampled it. Suspended tailings fines, and a stain line on the banks a meter above current flow. Whatever's discharging up there has been doing it for years." She paused. "Long enough to reach the villages downstream. Long enough to get into wells."

Casper was quiet for so long that Ilsa began counting riverbeats between heartbeats. Then: "Serkhang's on that water." Flat. Not a question.

"Serkhang and every settlement below it."

Another silence. When Casper spoke again her voice had changed — lower, rougher, like a rope drawn over stone. "I ran supply boats past that junction for two seasons. Never went up the white fork. Told myself it was because the gradient was wrong for the hull." She turned her head then, and in the dark her eyes caught the stars. "You're telling me I was hiding from it. Both of us."

"I'm telling you the water knows," Ilsa said. "Water keeps records. People are the ones who don't."

Something passed across Casper's face then — Ilsa saw it clearly, even in starlight, because it was too large to hide: not guilt exactly, and not surprise, but the terrible recognition of a person hearing their own secret spoken by a stranger. And underneath it, unmistakably, grief. No accomplice looked like that. Accomplices calculated. This woman was bleeding.

*I'm sorry,* Ilsa thought, absurdly, uselessly. *I'm sorry I can't hand it to you yet. I need to know you first. I've been alone with the truth for six years and I can't afford to give it to the wrong hands.*

"Get some sleep," Casper said at last, rising, rolling her shoulders as if shrugging off a weight. "Tomorrow we enter the narrows. Water changes character up there." She hesitated, one foot on the shingle, and added, without looking back: "You re-rigged my anchor lines the second night. Gravel set. Scour risk." A pause. "Nobody else noticed. Good eyes."

Then she was gone toward her bedroll, and Ilsa sat alone between the boulders with the river talking its endless testimony, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: Casper Okafor was not complicit. Whatever else she was — damaged, deceived, dangerous in her own right — she was a mark, and worse, she was a mark who suspected nothing because she had already convicted herself of a different crime entirely.

Second: tomorrow they would enter the narrows carrying forged permits stamped with next week's dates, sealed cases Ilsa now half-expected to contain evidence of murder, and a man whose initial was *M.*, and the monsoon clouds she'd seen stacking over the western ridgeline that afternoon — two weeks early, fat and bruise-colored, waiting — were going to make all of it infinitely worse.

She touched the lead pouch against her ribs, feeling the little hard rectangle of the camera card like a second heartbeat.

*Paper remembers*, her father had said. Soon, she promised the dark, soon she would make it speak. But tonight the knowledge stayed hers alone, heavy as ballast, and the river carried it onward into the gorge without asking her permission.
