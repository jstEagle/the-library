## 7. Monsoon Warning

The clouds came two weeks early, and Casper saw them before anyone else did.

She'd been awake since four, lying in her tent with the fly open, watching the ridgeline east of the Keshar headwaters go from black to bruise-colored. By dawn the sky above the pass had stacked itself into towers—cumulonimbus building in ranks, white at their crowns, blue-black at their roots, the way clouds look when they've made a decision about you. She'd spent twenty-two years reading water, but water was only weather that had already arrived. The sky was water still deciding.

She stood on the gravel bar with her coffee going cold and counted the towers. Seven distinct cells, aligned along the crest like teeth. Behind them, higher up, the cirrus had gone to mare's tails overnight, which meant wind aloft, which meant the system wasn't local. It was coming over the top of everything, dragging the whole monsoon trough with it, and it was not supposed to be here for another fourteen days.

"Ferro," she said, without turning around. The kid was already up—he was always already up, hovering at the edge of her mornings like a dog hoping to be useful. "Get the barometer out of the dry box. The brass one."

"You think it's coming?"

"I think it's already decided." She handed him her cup. "Coffee's yours. I don't want it."

He took both instructions with the same eager gravity, and she almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked back at the ridge and stopped wanting to smile at all.

By seven the barometer had fallen eleven millibars since midnight and was still falling, the needle drifting down in small reluctant steps, like a man walking toward something he'd rather avoid. Casper taped a strip of paper beside it and marked the readings hourly, because numbers were better than dread—they gave dread a shape you could argue with. Tomas came to look at the gauge twice before breakfast and said nothing either time, which from Tomas was a paragraph. Priya checked the medical kit inventory without being asked. Ilsa stood at the river's edge with her instruments, taking readings Casper didn't understand, and when Casper walked over, the hydrologist didn't even glance up.

"Flow rate's up nine percent since yesterday," Ilsa said. "Glacier melt plus pressure gradient. That's normal."

"And tomorrow?"

Ilsa finally looked at her. She had that quality Casper had noticed on the first night—the stillness of someone who'd learned to keep her face like a locked room. "Tomorrow depends on what that cloud does," she said. "And what that cloud does depends on Marsh."

* * *

Marsh held his morning briefing the way he held everything: loosely, theatrically, as if the world were an audience he was doing a favor by performing for. He spread the concession map on a camp table weighted with stones, tapped the route with one finger, and talked about schedule.

"We're twelve kilometers below the first gorge gate," he said. "If we make good time today and tomorrow, we're through the lower canyon before the week's out, and the upper section is a formality. I've run this window three times. It holds."

"The window doesn't exist anymore," Casper said.

It came out flat, not loud, and that was worse—she could feel the whole crew recalibrating around the change in temperature. Marsh looked up from the map with the expression he'd worn across thirty years of rivalry: patient, amused, faintly wounded, a man being inconvenienced by history.

"Casper."

"Look at the ridge." She pointed east, where the towers had thickened and merged into a single wall, its base already dissolving into gray precipitation-veils that hadn't reached them yet but would. "That's the monsoon. Two weeks early. That's not a shower, Devlin, that's the engine turning over. When that breaks over the Karakoram, every tributary in this system comes up between eight and fifteen meters inside forty-eight hours. The gorges become pipes. Full pipes. There's no eddies, no recovery water, no portage ledges—there's just velocity and walls. You don't run that. You get *run*."

"I've seen forecasts wrong before."

"I'm not reading a forecast. I'm reading the sky." She kept her voice level. Somewhere behind her she heard Ferro shift his weight, and Priya go very still, the particular stillness of a medic who knows an argument is about to produce casualties. "Barometer's dropped eleven millibars in six hours. River's up nine percent on melt alone. Add rain-on-glacier event—that's the scenario where warm rain falls on ice and you get runoff that has nothing to do with anything you've ever planned for—and your three-times-run window becomes a coffin with a schedule."

For a moment nobody moved. The river filled the silence, that low constant conversation gravel makes under current.

Then Marsh smiled, and Casper felt the old heat rise in her chest, the specific anger only Devlin Marsh had ever been able to reliably manufacture in her.

"You're calculating fear," he said gently. "I understand. Given your history with—" he gestured vaguely upstream, toward the valley he knew full well skirted the place her career had died, and Casper understood that the gesture was not vague at all, "—with this geography. Nobody would blame you for wanting to turn around. But we have a contract, and the contract has dates, and the dates have consequences. We push on."

"The contract isn't worth the paper if we're dead."

"That's dramatic."

"That's *arithmetic*." She stepped to the table and put her own finger on the map, hard enough to crease it. "Four days. That's what we have. Maybe five if the front stalls on the divide, and it won't stall, because there's jet-stream wind pushing it. Four days to clear the lower gorge system. After that, the gorges flood and stay flooded until September, and every option downstream closes except the ones that kill us. So we have a choice. We run hard for four days, light load, no stops, and we're through—or we stop now, cache the cargo, and wait out the season like sane people. What we do not do is what you're proposing, which is continue at survey pace and hope the mountain respects our itinerary."

"Hope," said Ilsa quietly, from the edge of the group, "is not a hydrological variable."

Casper didn't look at her, but something in her chest registered the sentence and filed it away.

Marsh straightened. He was taller than her by half a head and had always used it, unfolding himself slowly, letting the shadow arrive before the argument. "This is my expedition."

"This is my boat."

"And the boat is under charter—to me." He let that sit. Around the table, the crew had arranged themselves into a geometry Casper read instantly: Ferro behind her left shoulder, loyal and useless; Tomas with his arms crossed, watching her hands; Priya between the two camps, which was where Priya always lived; Ilsa apart from everyone, which was where Ilsa lived. "I hired you to guide, Casper. Not to commandeer."

"No," she said. "You hired me because I can read water no one else can. Fine. Here's the reading." She turned so she was addressing all of them, because this argument had never really been with Marsh—it was with the five people who would drown or not drown based on who they believed. "In four days, that gorge gate becomes a death trap. In four days after that, this entire valley becomes unrecognizable. If we're caught in the gorges when the flood pulse arrives, there will be no rescue, because there is nowhere for rescue to stand. I have watched a river take people I—" Her voice snagged, one syllable, barely. She hated herself for it. She pushed through. "I know exactly what these rivers cost. I am not asking you to trust my judgment. I'm asking you to check my math."

Silence. The clouds had eaten the sun while they argued; the light on the gravel bar had gone the color of old pewter, and the first fat drops hit the map table—one, two, a third—like knuckles rapping a door.

Tomas spoke first. "Boat can make four days hard running," he said. "If we strip weight. Spare frame stays. Half the fuel stays. I'll re-rig tonight."

Priya: "Medical reality—if anyone swims in a flooded gorge, I can't do anything. Hypothermia plus trauma plus no landing zones. I vote we either run fast or not at all."

Ferro: "I vote whatever Casper votes." He said it too loudly, with the naked devotion that made her want to shake him, and she saw Marsh file that away too—*the boy follows her, not me*—and she understood she'd just paid a price for it.

All eyes went to Ilsa. She considered the river for a long moment, instruments forgotten in her hands.

"The flow data supports her timeline," Ilsa said. "Four days. Five at the outside." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "I'll note that I flagged the pressure gradient last night. To Mr. Marsh. Directly."

Something crossed Marsh's face then—fast, quickly buried, but Casper had spent decades learning to read him, and she caught it. Not surprise. *Recalculation.* As if Ilsa's public defection were a number he'd been expecting and had already priced in.

"Well," he said lightly, rolling the map closed with a snap. "Democracy on the river. How modern." He looked at Casper, and beneath the theatrical disappointment was something harder and more interested, the way a gambler looks at a card he didn't expect but can still use. "Four days, then. Hard running, stripped load. You have your window, Okafor. But hear me clearly—" and now he dropped the performance entirely, and for one raw second she saw the man underneath, the one from Nanda Kot, the one who had signed things, "—we are not turning back. Whatever happens. We are not turning back."

"That," Casper said, "is exactly what worries me."

* * *

They stripped the boat through the afternoon in a rain that came and went in sulky intervals, testing them. Tomas worked like a man performing surgery on a family member, muttering to the hull, redistributing kilograms with a mechanic's fanaticism. Casper carried dry boxes until her shoulders burned and found Ilsa re-sealing the sample cases—not the fuel, she noticed, not the food, the *cases*—lashing them into the most protected position in the load plan with a thoroughness that bordered on devotion.

"Those matter more than the fuel?" Casper asked.

Ilsa didn't stop working. "Everything matters less than those cases."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have right now." She cinched a strap, tested it, and looked up. Rain beaded on her hood. For a moment the locked-room face opened a crack, and what was behind it looked less like secrecy than exhaustion. "Ask me again when the river lets us both survive the next four days."

Casper should have pressed. Six years ago she would have pressed. Instead she heard herself say, "Fine," and went back to the rigging, and understood, distantly, that she was accumulating debts of ignorance the way the barometer accumulated millibars—steadily, measurably, toward some reading she didn't want to see.

At dusk the rain stopped as if switched off, and the clouds parted long enough to show the ridge, and what she saw there emptied her mouth of spit. The wall of weather had grown all day, and now it filled the eastern horizon bank to bank, lit from within by continuous silent lightning, a country-sized thunderhead grinding slowly westward like a machine that had all the time in the world.

Ferro appeared beside her with tea, having learned that she wouldn't eat but might drink. "Is it really four days?"

"Maybe." She took the cup. Below them the river ran high and brown and getting browner, carrying the first pale load of fresh glacier flour from altitudes where rain was already falling as rain instead of snow. "Kid, listen to me. Tomorrow we enter the gorge gates at first light, and we don't stop until dark, and we do the same the day after, and the day after that. No resupply stop at Serkhang—we'll cache what we need on the bank and pick it up on the way down. Nothing slows us. You hear me? Nothing."

"Even Marsh?"

She watched the lightning flicker inside the cloud, silent at this distance, like artillery in another war.

"*Especially* Marsh," she said.

But that night, checking the barometer one last time before sleep, she found the needle had fallen another six millibars, and the paper strip of readings curved downward like the profile of a wave just before it breaks—and she lay awake doing the arithmetic again, honestly this time, without the confidence she'd sold the crew at noon, and got three days, maybe less, and understood that she had lied to them by rounding up.

Above the tents, the first real rain began to fall on the Keshar watershed, soft and steady and patient, and somewhere upstream in the dark, the river was already listening.
