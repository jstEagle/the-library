## 8. The Village That Remembers Her Name

The river widened below the gorge and forgot its anger all at once, spreading into braided channels the color of gunmetal, and Casper felt the boat settle beneath her hands the way a horse settles when it smells stable. Serkhang sat on the west bank where the valley opened like a palm—forty stone houses, a whitewashed chorten streaked black with rain-rot, terraces of barley climbing the slope in green stitches. Smoke stood straight up from the morning fires. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and somewhere else a woman was singing to herself while she winnowed grain, the song carrying across the water in fragments.

Casper had been here eleven years ago. She had come through with a full expedition and a full heart, bought butter tea in the house with the blue door, and paid too much for it on purpose because the old man who ran the tea house had a laugh like a landslide. She had been somebody then. Not famous—guides were never famous—but somebody whose name opened doors instead of closing them.

She brought the boat in slow, reading the shallows by the color of the water, and called the landing to Tomas in the flat voice she used for everything now. Ferro leapt onto the shingle before the hull touched, rope in hand, eager as a puppy, and Casper didn't have the heart to tell him that eagerness was the first thing a river took from you.

"Two hours," she said to the crew, climbing out. "Fuel from the co-op, food from whoever will sell it to us. Nobody wanders. Nobody photographs the chorten like a tourist. We are passing through, not arriving."

"And you?" Ilsa asked. She stood at the gunwale with her instruments strapped to her back, watching the village with an expression Casper couldn't read—something between recognition and arithmetic.

"I'm going to buy tea," Casper said. "Like a person."

* * *

She wasn't halfway up the bank when she understood that the river was the only thing here that had forgotten her.

It started with silence. The singing stopped. The dog stopped barking. A man stacking firewood straightened with a log still in his hands and looked at her the way men look at weather they don't trust. Then an old woman coming down the path with a basket of dung cakes saw her face, and stopped, and the basket swung from her arm like a pendulum winding down.

Casper knew her. Dolkar. She had been younger eleven years ago, quicker, her hair still black. Now she was all silver and angles, and her eyes went over Casper's face with a terrible unhurried attention, the way you'd examine a wound.

"You," Dolkar said. Not a question. A verdict.

"Dolkar-ji." Casper's voice came out lower than she intended. "I'm just resupplying. We'll be gone by—"

The woman spat. It landed in the dust an inch from Casper's boot, precise as a surveyor's mark, and the whole village seemed to lean in around the sound.

"We burned juniper for them," Dolkar said, in the Karakoram trade tongue Casper still dreamed in. "Eleven years ago. When your expedition came through, we burned juniper so the mountain would know you were friends. My husband carried your loads to base camp on his own back, and he would not take money, because he said a mountain shared is a mountain made lighter." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it. It stayed level as a lake. "And when the mountain took one of yours, you left. You left and you never came back, and no one ever told us what we were supposed to do with all that burned juniper."

"Dolkar-ji, I—"

"Go buy your tea." The old woman lifted her basket again. "The tea house is still there. The blue door. The old man is dead, but his daughter makes it the same. Everything here is the same. That is what happens to a village when the ones who leave never come back. It stays."

She walked on. Casper stood in the dust with the spit drying an inch from her boot and felt the eleven years close over her head like water.

* * *

The tea house had a new roof but the same blue door, flaking now, and the daughter—Tsering, she had been a girl of fifteen then, chasing goats—was a broad, tired woman who looked at Casper across the hearth and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of sentence. She poured the tea. Casper paid. Tsering took the money without looking at it.

"The whole crew?" Tsering asked finally, nodding toward the door. "The tall one with the instruments. The boy. The mechanic."

"Four of us. Plus Marsh, the expedition lead. He's arranging the fuel."

"Marsh." Tsering said the name like she was tasting something to see if it had spoiled. "The loud one? He came through before too. Years ago. With men in company jackets."

Casper's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "Company jackets?"

"Grey. With a mark like a mountain inside a circle." Tsering shrugged, a big slow shrug that moved her whole body. "They asked questions about the upper valley. About the glacier streams. Whether the water had changed. Whether the fish had gone. My father told them the truth and they wrote it down and paid him and never came back, and the next spring the fish were gone anyway." She refilled Casper's cup without being asked. "You want food for how many?"

"Six. For four days." Casper set the cup down. "Tsering. The man who died. On Nanda Kot. The client—"

"Herr Vogel." The name came out instantly, unhesitating, and Casper realized the whole village could have said it the same way. "The German. Yes."

"You knew him?"

"He stayed in this house." Tsering's hands kept working, grinding the barley she'd been grinding when Casper came in, but her voice changed—went quieter, went somewhere else. "He was not like the others who come to climb. He was not interested in the top. He was interested in the water. He had bottles, small glass bottles, and he would fill them from every stream. He had a machine he held up to the rock and it clicked. My father thought he was a little mad. He laughed a lot, though. He taught my son to whistle with a blade of grass." Her hands stopped. "That son is the one who asks about you, when strangers come through. He was six when the mountain took Herr Vogel. He is seventeen now, and he still whistles, and he still asks."

Casper's throat had gone tight. "Asks what?"

But she got her answer before Tsering could give it, because the door opened and the boy came through it.

He had his grandfather's shoulders and a face still deciding what it would be, and he stopped dead when he saw her, and Casper watched the recognition move across him like a shadow crossing a field. He didn't spit. He didn't shout. He just stood there with a bundle of kindling in his arms and looked at her, and then he set the kindling down very carefully, the way you set down something you might need your hands free to throw.

"You're her," he said. In English, accented, careful. "The guide. The one who let the mountain eat my father."

"I didn't—" Casper started, and the sentence collapsed, because what was the end of it? *I didn't let it? I didn't mean it? I didn't know?* Every ending was a door slamming on something true behind it.

"He talked about you," the boy said. "Tsering told me. He said the guide was the best water-reader in the range. He said if the guide said a slope was safe, it was safe." His voice didn't break. He had practiced this, Casper realized. He had said these words to himself so many times they had worn smooth, like stones in a riverbed. "So either you were wrong, or you lied. Which one?"

"Namgyal." Tsering's voice cracked across the room like a whip.

"No. Let her answer." The boy's eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "Everyone says *let it go, he was a foreigner, it was the mountain's will.* But he whistled. He taught me to whistle. Mountains don't eat people who whistle. Somebody made a mistake, and everybody says it was her, and she never came back to say if it was."

Casper stood in the smoke of the hearth with the whole weight of eleven years pressing down on her chest, and she gave him the only honest thing she had.

"I don't know," she said. "I have spent eleven years not knowing. I have gone over that day ten thousand times and I cannot find my mistake, but I cannot prove I didn't make one, and I know that's not good enough. It wasn't good enough then and it isn't now." She held his gaze. "But I'm going back up that valley. In the next few days. And if there's anything up there that explains what happened to him—anything at all—I'm going to find it. That's not an answer. It's a promise I might not be able to keep. But it's what I have."

The boy stared at her for a long moment. Then he picked up his kindling, and at the door he stopped, without turning around.

"The water changed," he said. "The year after he died. The streams ran grey in the mornings. The animals stopped drinking from the lower pools. My grandfather said the mountain was sick, and the lama said the mountain was angry, and the men in the grey jackets said it was nothing." He looked back at her, just his eyes, half-turned. "He was collecting the water because he thought it was something. Maybe if you're going up there, you should look at the water too."

He left. The kindling smell of pine hung where he'd stood.

Casper sat back down on the bench and found her hands were shaking, and she put them flat on her knees and watched them shake and did not try to stop them. Tsering put a fresh cup of tea in front of her without a word, and for a while the only sound was the grind of the millstone and the wind in the chimney.

"He was hired by someone," Casper said at last. "Vogel. Clients don't fund their own expeditions to sample stream water in the Karakoram. Someone sent him."

Tsering's grinding slowed. "He had letters. He showed my father once—paper with that same mark. The mountain inside the circle. He laughed and said he was going to be very expensive to silence." She looked up, and her face was old in a way it hadn't been five minutes ago. "Eleven days later he was dead on your mountain, and the men in grey jackets came through asking about the water, and my father said to me: *Tsering, mountains don't get angry in the right places at the right times.*" She resumed grinding, slow and even. "My father is buried behind the chorten. He died still wondering."

* * *

Casper walked back down to the boat through a village that watched her go, door by door, face by face, and the watching had changed. It wasn't the blank hostility of the landing anymore. It was something worse and better: expectation. She had made a promise in a tea house and in a village like Serkhang, promises traveled faster than rivers.

Ilsa was waiting at the waterline, crouched over one of her instruments, but her eyes came up when Casper's boots hit the shingle. "You're pale," she said. "And you walk like someone carrying something heavier than supplies."

"I talked to people."

"People here don't talk to you."

"They didn't." Casper looked up the valley, where the river came down out of the gorge system in its long grey braid, toward Nanda Kot's hidden shoulders, toward the mine she had never let herself think about because thinking about it meant thinking about the water, and the water meant the rockfall, and the rockfall meant the day her life had split into before and after. "Ilsa. What do you know about the upper watershed? The streams feeding the Keshar. Have your instruments shown anything on the approach?"

Something flickered across Ilsa's face—there and gone, fast, but Casper had spent a lifetime reading water and people were just water with better disguises. Hesitation. Calculation. A current pulling somewhere she couldn't see.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because a dead man was sampling these streams eleven years ago," Casper said. "And a year later the water ran grey and the fish died and the animals left, and nobody ever said why. And I'm leading an expedition up that valley for a man who may or may not be telling me the truth, and I just made a promise to a seventeen-year-old boy that I'd find out what ate his father." She held Ilsa's gaze. "So I'm asking you what you know. And I'm asking you to notice that I asked you first, before Marsh, because I don't think Marsh tells the truth when the truth costs him anything."

The wind came down the valley, cold off the glacier, and flattened the barley on the terraces in long silver waves. Ilsa looked at her for a long moment, and Casper watched the war happen behind her eyes—whatever she was carrying, whatever she had boarded the boat with in those anonymous instrument cases, it was straining against the lock.

"Your client," Ilsa said finally. Carefully. "Vogel. What company sponsored him?"

"I never saw the paperwork. Marsh handled the contracts."

"Of course he did." Ilsa stood, shouldering her instrument case, and for a moment she looked out at the river with an expression that was almost—almost—pity. "Ask him about the grey jackets, Casper. Ask him about the mountain inside the circle. And watch his hands when he answers."

She walked up the bank toward the co-op, leaving Casper standing on the shingle with the tea gone cold in her stomach and the valley waiting above her like a held breath.

Behind her, from the direction of the fuel depot, she heard Marsh's voice—loud, expansive, doing what it did best—and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, a boy whistling with a blade of grass.
