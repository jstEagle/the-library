## 9. Cargo in the Dry Boxes

Ferro had been thinking about cigarettes for three days.

Not smoking them—he'd quit twice and failed twice, which in his private accounting meant he was still a smoker, just a deprived one—but thinking about them the way a thirsty man thinks about wells. The thought lived behind his teeth. And the thought had led him, on the fourth night above the confluence, to the dry boxes.

Everyone knew you didn't open Marsh's dry boxes. That was the first rule of the expedition, delivered by Marsh himself at the put-in with a smile that didn't reach his eyes: *the equipment cases stay sealed until Keshar*. Casper hadn't contradicted him. Priya hadn't either. Tomas had shrugged the way he shrugged at everything that wasn't an engine. So Ferro had filed it away with all the other things about this trip that didn't add up—the hydrologist who asked questions like a customs officer, the route that bent around valleys no map required them to avoid, the way Devlin Marsh checked his satellite phone face-down against his thigh like a man hiding a hand of cards.

But cigarettes. Priya kept her medical stores locked, but she kept her personal kit in the green duffel, and Ferro was ninety percent sure he'd seen a crumpled pack of Gold Leafs tucked into the side pocket two weeks ago, back when they were still speaking easily to each other, back before the river turned brown and everyone's tempers turned with it.

The green duffel was not in the dry boxes. He knew that. He told himself that as he unzipped the tent flap and crossed the camp in the blue hour after dinner, when Casper was down at the waterline checking the tie-offs for the third time—she always checked three times—and Marsh was inside his own tent with the sat phone pressed to his ear, speaking low in a language Ferro couldn't place. Urdu, maybe. Or money.

The gear raft was beached above the high-water line, lashed to a granite fin that stuck out of the bank like a shark's tooth. The dry boxes were stacked amidships under a tarp, six of them, olive-drab, each one stenciled with a serial number and a weight rating and nothing else. Ferro crouched beside them and listened to the camp behind him: the hiss of the stove Priya was banking, the mutter of the river, the small metallic complaints of cooling metal. Nobody shouted. Nobody came.

He lifted the tarp.

The boxes were padlocked—all except the last one, the smallest, whose hasp hung open because someone had been careless or hurried or both. Ferro stared at it for a long moment, telling himself the story he'd come to tell: *just looking for cigarettes, just checking whether anyone stashed a pack in with the gear, perfectly normal, everybody does it.* It was a good story. He almost believed it. Then he opened the box anyway.

No cigarettes.

Core samples.

Six of them, packed upright in foam like wine bottles, each sealed in clear plastic, each labeled in neat block capitals: **KESHAR CONCESSION — BOREHOLE 7 — DEPTH 84M**. The rock inside was gray shot through with veins of something pale and wrong-looking, a color Ferro had no name for until he did: tailings color, waste-rock color, the color of the slag heaps he'd grown up seeing outside the smelter town where his uncle worked, the color that got into everything and never washed out. Below the samples, wrapped in plastic sheeting, lay a brick of documents—borehole logs, chain-of-custody forms, water assay reports, their pages fanned just enough that he could read the letterhead on the top sheet.

He knew the logo. Everyone in these valleys knew the logo: a stylized mountain peak inside a droplet, silver on white. Himalaya Consolidated Minerals. The company that leased half the upper watershed. The company whose trucks he'd hitched rides on as a kid, whose payroll office had rejected his job application when he was seventeen because his left hand was crushed from a childhood accident and they said he couldn't safely hold a drill casing. The company that owned the road, the clinic, the police post in Skardu, and—as far as anyone could prove—nothing else, because everything else was done through intermediaries with clean names.

Ferro stood very still in the dark with the box open in front of him and felt the whole expedition tilt beneath him like a raft hitting a hole he hadn't seen.

*Surveying*, Marsh had said. Hydrological survey, sediment load, feasibility data for a bridge project. That was the story. That was what Casper had signed on for—that was what *they'd all* signed on for, the triple rate explained by the difficulty of the water, the secrecy explained by commercial sensitivity. Ferro had believed it right up until the moment his fingers touched the plastic over borehole seven.

You didn't smuggle core samples out of a concession you were surveying *for*. You smuggled them out of a concession you were surveying *against*.

He photographed the label with his phone, hands not quite steady, the flash off, the screen brightness turned all the way down. Then he closed the box, rebuckled the hasp, pulled the tarp straight, and walked back across camp with his heart going like a two-stroke engine and no cigarettes at all.

* * *

Priya was scraping the last of the dal out of the pot when he sat down across from her at the fire. She glanced up, registered his face, and set the ladle down.

"Whatever it is," she said quietly, "don't say it loud."

"I need to show you something. Not here."

She looked at him for a long second. Priya had spent nine years running trauma bays in Srinagar before she'd washed up running river medic rotations in the Karakoram, and she had a way of looking at people that triaged them—airway, breathing, circulation, *lie*. Whatever she saw in Ferro's face made her nod once and stand up.

They walked upstream along the bank until the firelight fell away and the only light was the moon coming up fat and orange behind the western ridge, turning the river into a long seam of hammered tin. Ferro stopped where the gravel bar widened, well out of earshot, and showed her the photo.

Priya held the phone close to her eyes even though her vision was fine. She zoomed in on the letterhead. She zoomed in on the borehole label. She did not say anything for so long that Ferro started talking just to fill the silence.

"It's not a survey," he said. "It can't be. You don't run evidence out under seal unless—"

"I know what it is." Her voice was flat, clinical, the voice she used for bad news delivered in front of patients. "I've been watching Ilsa log water samples since Gilgit. She doesn't record sediment loads. She records *chemistry*. pH, conductivity, heavy-metal screening with reagents I assumed were for drinking-water safety." She lowered the phone. "I told myself it was thoroughness. Good hydrologists are thorough."

"And now?"

"Now I think we've been carrying somebody's lawsuit up this river in dry boxes, and nobody thought to tell the people pulling the oars." She handed the phone back. "Who else knows?"

"Just you. I found it twenty minutes ago."

"Casper?"

Ferro shook his head, then stopped halfway, uncertain. "She checks those tie-offs like her life depends on it. She reads Marsh's moods like weather. But I don't—" He ran a hand through his hair. "I don't know anymore. She took this job for money. People who take jobs for money don't ask questions. Maybe she asked and he paid her not to."

"You don't believe that."

"No," Ferro admitted. "But I believed the survey story too, and look how that went."

Priya gazed downstream toward the black shape of the camp, where Marsh's tent glowed faintly from a lantern inside, and beyond it the gorge walls rose into darkness on either side like the covers of a book closing. When she spoke again her voice had lost its clinical edge and gained something rawer underneath.

"My brother works a lease operation near Chilas," she said. "Contract labor, no benefits, dust suppression that consists of hoping for rain. Last spring three men from his crew came down with what the company doctor called 'seasonal respiratory illness.' One of them was twenty-four." She turned to look at Ferro, and the moonlight caught the lines around her eyes that the fire usually hid. "So don't tell me what this cargo means. I already know what it means. What I want to know is why Ilsa Reiner is hauling it through flood country in a boat patched with bicycle inner tubes instead of handing it to a lawyer in Islamabad like a normal person."

"Then let's ask her."

"Ferro—"

"She's awake. I saw her headlamp down at the instruments ten minutes ago." He was already moving. "And if we wait until morning, Marsh will have us pushing into the next gorge by seven, and there won't be another quiet bank for two days."

Priya swore softly in Hindi, caught up with him in three strides, and grabbed his sleeve. "We do this my way. No accusations. We ask what the cargo is, and we watch her face when she answers. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Ferro." She held his arm a moment longer. "Whatever she says—we don't decide anything tonight. We're four days' hard water from anywhere, with a mechanic who sleeps like the dead and a guide who may or may not already know. We are not in a position to burn the boat we're sitting in."

"That's exactly what scares me," Ferro said. "That we're not in a position to do anything at all."

* * *

Ilsa's instrument station was a flat shelf of rock above an eddy, sheltered by a leaning slab of granite, and she worked it the way Ferro imagined a surgeon worked—a headlamp throwing a hard white circle, gloved hands moving with unhurried precision, everything in its place. She had vials racked in a foam block, a portable spectrometer the size of a lunchbox humming to itself, and a waterproof notebook in which she wrote in a small, dense, entirely legible hand. She heard them coming—of course she did; she heard everything—and she didn't turn around.

"The turbidity meter is giving me garbage again," she said. "If you've come to help, hold this light steady. If you've come for anything else, say it plainly. I'm tired."

Ferro looked at Priya. Priya gave him a fractional nod.

"We opened the dry box," he said.

Ilsa's hands paused over the rack of vials—only for a beat, then resumed. She capped a sample, slotted it, reached for the next. "Which one?"

"The small one. The unlocked one."

"The unlocked one was deliberate." Now she turned, and the headlamp swung with her, catching Priya and Ferro full in the eyes so they both flinched and stepped sideways out of the beam. "I loosened the hasp yesterday morning. I've been waiting to see who'd take the bait."

"You *wanted* someone to find it?" Ferro's voice cracked upward. "You let us sit on top of that stuff for two weeks and you wanted—"

"I wanted to know who on this crew still had a spine," Ilsa said. "Congratulations. It's the deckhand and the medic. I'd honestly stopped expecting better." She pulled her gloves off finger by finger. In the spill of lamplight her face looked older than it had on the first day at the put-in, the competent stranger's face worn down to something more human and more exhausted. "Sit down. Both of you. This takes five minutes, and you're going to want to be sitting."

Nobody sat. She shrugged and talked anyway.

"The core samples are from boreholes inside the Keshar Concession. Seven sites, drilled between fourteen months ago and last March. The assays show heavy-metal contamination in the groundwater table at every site—arsenic, lead, cadmium at concentrations that would get a mine shut down in any country with functioning regulators. The leachate is entering the watershed. Which means it's entering the Indus system. Which means it's entering the drinking water of approximately eleven million people downstream, though you'd need another year of sampling to prove the full pathway, and the syndicate's lease renewal comes up in eight months, and by then the boreholes will be capped and the data will be gone." She said it all evenly, like reading a shipping manifest, and somehow that made it worse. "Devlin isn't doing a survey. Devlin is extracting evidence. The survey story was cover for the permits, and the permits are cover for the extraction, and the extraction is the whole point of this expedition."

The river filled the silence behind her, patient and enormous.

"Why you?" Priya asked finally. Her arms were crossed tight. "Why not lawyers, journalists, the EPA—why hire a disgraced river guide and a stolen boat and a crew of nobodies to run it through monsoon season?"

"Because every official channel is bought, and every journalist who's tried has been bought or buried, and because the evidence has to leave the country physically—it can't be transmitted, the files are watermarked and traceable, and the syndicate monitors every data link out of the valley." Ilsa folded her gloves and tucked them into her pocket with the same maddening precision. "Because a river is the one road they don't control. And because Casper Okafor is the only guide alive who can read the gorges above the confluence at flood stage, and Devlin Marsh is the only man venal enough to have figured that out and ruthless enough to act on it."

"You're saying she doesn't know," Ferro said.

"I'm saying I don't know what she knows." For the first time, something moved behind Ilsa's composure—not softness exactly, but the admission of a limit. "She read the permit for thirty seconds at the tea house and signed. Either she didn't look closely, or she looked closely and decided sixty days of debt was worth not asking questions. I've spent two weeks trying to work out which, and I genuinely cannot tell you. That woman hides things better than I do, and I've been hiding things professionally for six years."

"What things?" Ferro asked. "Whose evidence is this? Who's paying for the spectrometer and the permits and Marsh's cut? Because I've met greedy men before, and Marsh doesn't spend his own money on other people's lawsuits."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Down at the eddy, the water turned over a submerged boulder with a sound like slow breathing. When she answered, her voice had dropped its briefing-room evenness, and what was underneath was quieter and much heavier.

"There's no organization. There's no NGO. There's me, a dead man's savings account, and a forensic lab in Kathmandu that owes me a favor." She reached into her jacket and drew out a photograph, creased soft with handling, and held it where the headlamp lit it: a young man in a mine supervisor's jacket, squinting into the sun, one arm slung around a laughing woman who was unmistakably Ilsa twenty years younger and thirty kilos lighter. "My brother managed environmental compliance at the Keshar operation. On paper. In practice he collected samples like these for three years and sent them to me, because he believed the paperwork would eventually matter. In April, six years ago, he reported an unstable tailings dam to the district office. Two weeks later the dam failed—officially due to 'unprecedented precipitation'—and the debris flow took out the lower camp. Eleven dead. My brother was listed among them. His sample archive was listed among the losses." She put the photograph away. "It wasn't lost. He mailed the duplicates to me the week before he died. I've spent six years finding a way to get them somewhere they'd count, and every road I tried ended in a man like Devlin Marsh holding out his hand."

Priya had gone very still. "Casper's client," she said slowly. "On Nanda Kot. The man who died."

"Rafiq Anwar." Ilsa said the name like setting something down carefully. "Environmental consultant. He was on that mountain taking ice-core and meltwater samples for a client he never named to the expedition. The rockfall that killed him happened three hours after he radioed that he'd found contamination signatures in the glacier fed by the Keshar headwaters." She met Ferro's eyes, and hers were dry and terrible. "I've read the incident report forty times. I don't believe in coincidences that convenient. Neither, I suspect, does your guide—though she's spent seven years believing the alternative, because the alternative was her fault, and guilt is easier to carry than suspicion."

Ferro felt cold despite the down jacket, cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude. He thought of Casper at the tiller, day after day, reading water no one else could see, hands steady, jaw set, carrying something none of them had been allowed to weigh. He thought of Marsh checking his phone face-down against his thigh. He thought of the boy in Serkhang—he'd heard the story secondhand from Tomas—asking if she was the one who let the mountain eat his father.

"So what happens now," he said. "Now that we know. Do we confront Marsh? Do we tell Casper? Do we—"

"We tell no one, and we change nothing, and we watch," Priya said, but her voice lacked conviction, and Ilsa was already shaking her head.

"No. You're half right. We don't confront Marsh—confronted men make mistakes with other people's lives, and we are standing in his mistake. But Casper finds out tomorrow." Ilsa began packing the spectrometer into its case, movements brisk again, decision made. "Not because it's strategically ideal. Because she's going to find out anyway—Marsh will tell her some version of it when he needs her scared enough to run his escape route, and a version Marsh tells her will be a version built to keep her obedient. She deserves the truth from someone who isn't selling her something." She snapped the case shut and looked up at them, and the headlamp threw her shadow huge against the granite behind her. "First light. Before he's awake. You two found the box—you two stand there with me. If she decides to put us off the raft at the next eddy, that's her right, and I'll swim for it gladly. But she hears it from us."

Above the western ridge, the moon had cleared the rock and laid a white road down the center of the river, and Ferro thought it looked less like moonlight than like a seam splitting open—like something long stitched shut finally starting to come apart.

He was halfway back to camp, Priya silent beside him, when he saw the light.

Not a headlamp. Not the stove glow. A thin blade of lantern light lying along the ground at the mouth of Marsh's tent, and Marsh himself standing outside it in the dark, motionless, facing upstream. Facing the instrument shelf. Facing them.

He didn't call out. He didn't move. He simply stood there in the cold with the light at his feet, and even at fifty meters Ferro could feel him listening, the way you feel a storm building behind a ridge line—nothing yet, no wind, no sound, only the pressure of something enormous deciding which way to break.
