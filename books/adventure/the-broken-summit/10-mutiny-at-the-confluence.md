## 10. Mutiny at the Confluence

The confluence announced itself before they saw it—a deep, grinding roar where the Keshar's grey meltwater slammed into the brown flood of the main channel, and the whole river seemed to stand up on end. Casper killed the engine and let the current carry them in sideways, reading the seam of calmer water that curled along the true left bank like a sentence written just for her. Behind them, Tomas's boat followed her line exactly. He'd learned. They'd all learned.

She nosed into the eddy beneath a wall of black schist and threw the painter around a boulder the size of a bus. The water here was only two feet down but moving like it had somewhere to be, tugging at the hull, impatient. Above the junction, the gorge narrowed to a throat. Below it, the river widened into a braided sprawl of channels that would all be underwater in four days if the clouds stacking over the western ridgeline kept doing what they were doing.

"Camp here," she said. "We eat, we sleep, we decide."

"Decide what?" Ferro asked.

Casper didn't answer. She was watching Devlin Marsh.

He stood at the bow with his hands jammed in his pockets, looking upriver toward the throat of the gorge with an expression she recognized from twenty years ago, from base camps and briefing tents and the long evenings when they'd argued route lines over tea gone cold. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want anyone else to see. And behind him, Ilsa stood with her arms crossed, her instruments stowed, her face giving away nothing—which, Casper had learned over ten days on the water, was itself a kind of information.

Priya climbed out first and went straight to the dry boxes without being asked. She'd been doing that for two days—checking seals, counting cases, running her thumb along the seams. Tomas killed his engine and drifted in beside them, saying nothing, which for Tomas was a speech. Ferro tied off the second boat and then just stood there on the rocks, not unloading, not asking for orders, his young face tight with something he'd been carrying since the night he and Priya had opened the wrong dry box looking for cigarettes.

That had been three days ago. Three days of silence that wasn't peace. Three days of Priya watching Ilsa. Three days of Marsh being very charming at dinner and very quiet after. Three days of Casper feeling the crew's attention slide toward her and away again, testing, waiting to see if she'd ask the question herself or keep pretending there was no question to ask.

She'd been pretending. She could admit that now, standing on the shingle with the confluence roaring at her back. She'd signed a contract she hadn't read closely enough, told herself it was money, told herself the sealed cases were survey equipment, told herself that whatever Marsh was really doing upstream of everything, it wasn't hers to know until he made it hers. That was the oldest lie she owned: *not my problem until it touches my boat.*

Well. It touched her boat now.

"Everyone ashore," she said. "Now."

They gathered on the flat shelf of rock above the high-water line, the six of them, while the wind came down the gorge smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Casper built no fire. Fires meant comfort, and comfort meant this would take longer. She set the fuel drum upright as a table and put her palm flat on it.

"I'm going to say this once," she said. "Three days ago Ferro opened a case that wasn't food or fuel. Since then nobody has said anything out loud, and I've let that go because I wanted to be wrong." She looked at Marsh. "I'm done wanting to be wrong. What's in the boxes?"

Marsh didn't flinch. That was the thing about Devlin Marsh that had always made him dangerous—he never flinched, even when flinching was the correct human response. He looked around the circle once, cataloguing faces the way he catalogued rapids, and then he crouched, popped the seal on the nearest dry box, and lifted out a core sample tube as casually as a man showing off wine.

"You want to see," he said. "Fine."

He laid the tubes out on the rock in a row. Grey-green stone, sliced and numbered. Then documents—sheaves of them, plastic-sleeved, stamped with a logo Casper knew the way you know a scar. The Meridian Karakoram Mining Consortium. The same logo that had been on the trucks she'd watched roll up the valley road eleven years ago, the summer everything ended.

"These are core samples from the Keshar headwaters," Marsh said. "Taken illegally, over eighteen months, by people Meridian believes are their own contractors. Water tables, tailings seepage, chemical assays of the outflow streams." He tapped one sleeve. "And this is the internal correspondence that proves they knew. Not suspected. *Knew.* For nine years."

"Knew what?" Ferro asked. His voice cracked on it.

"That the mine was poisoning the watershed," Ilsa said quietly. She stepped forward, and Casper noticed her hands weren't steady. "Arsenic, heavy metals, processing chemicals in every stream draining the east face. In the villages' drinking water. In the grazing land. And—" she stopped, looked at Casper, and something passed across her face that might have been apology, "—and in the rock itself."

"What does that mean, *in the rock*?" Casper asked. Her voice came out flat. Too flat. She heard it and hated it.

"The tailings ponds sit above a fractured zone," Ilsa said. "Meridian's own geologists flagged it in year two. The drainage they cut for the ponds channeled water directly into the fracture system. Freeze-thaw cycles, lubricated shear planes, destabilized slope." She took a breath. "Eleven years ago, in the Nanda Kot approach valley, a section of hillside the size of a village let go onto the glacier moraine below it. Everyone called it an act of God."

The world went very quiet for Casper, the way it did sometimes—the confluence still roaring, the wind still moving, but all of it behind glass. She was aware of Priya stepping closer to her. Aware of Ferro staring. Aware of her own hands, which she folded deliberately so no one could see whether they were shaking.

"My client was on that moraine," she said. "Daniel Osei. He was roped fifty feet ahead of me when the slope came down. I was certified, I was careful, I ran the route clean. They took my license anyway. Nepal blacklisted me. Peru followed. Eleven years I've been running illegal ferries on rivers nobody official cares about, and you're telling me—"

"I'm telling you it wasn't an act of God," Marsh said. "It was an act of accounting. The remediation would have cost more than the concession was worth, so they buried the report and kept mining." He paused, and for the first time she saw something under the composure—not guilt exactly, but its expensive cousin. "There's more. You're not going to like it."

"Devlin." His name came out of her like a blade leaving a body. "Say it."

He held her gaze. "Osei wasn't a tourist. He was an investigator. Environmental compliance, working for a firm hired by the villagers of Serkhang to document what the mine was doing to them. He hired you because a famous guide's expedition was the perfect cover to get eyes on the upper valley." He spread his hands. "He found what he needed. The rockfall happened three days later. Conveniently."

The glass around the world shattered. Casper moved before she decided to move—two steps, her fist half-cocked—and it was Priya's hand on her arm and Tomas's body simply appearing between them that stopped her, the big mechanic saying nothing, just standing there, immovable as ballast.

"They let me carry it," she said. Her voice was shaking now and she let it shake. "Eleven years. Every border crossing, every interview, every night—I carried *their* rockfall. I thought it was mine. I thought I'd misread the mountain. I built my whole life on top of their crime like it was my grave."

"I know," Marsh said.

"You don't know anything. You sat on this. How long have you had these samples? How long have you known what they did to me?"

"Eighteen months for the samples. Longer for the rest." He didn't look away. "And yes, Casper. I knew what it would mean to you. That's why I needed you."

"There it is." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "There's the real contract. Triple rate for a guide who can read water nobody else can. You didn't hire my hands, Devlin. You hired my nightmare."

"Yes," he said simply. "Because the escape route out of the Keshar headwaters doesn't follow the concession road—it follows the old survey line through the gorge system, the one you mapped the year before Nanda Kot. The one nobody else has ever run. The syndicate owns the roads, the checkpoints, the officials. The river is the only way this evidence leaves the valley alive. And you are the only person alive who knows that river."

Silence, except the water. Ferro sat down slowly on a rock like his legs had been unplugged. Priya's hand was still on Casper's arm, but lighter now, less restraint than anchor.

"So the mutiny," Casper said at last, turning to face her crew, because this was theirs too, whatever Marsh thought. "You've all been sitting on this for three days. Say your piece."

Tomas spoke first, which surprised everyone including, probably, Tomas. "I fixed engines for a mine company once. In Bolivia. I know what the trucks look like when they leave." He shrugged his enormous shoulders. "I'm in. But I pick the camp sites from now on."

Priya's voice was steady. "I've known since the dry box. Ilsa told us the truth that night, and I've spent three days deciding whether you were part of it or another mark, Casper. I've decided mark." A ghost of a smile. "But I want it said plainly: we are smuggling evidence past armed security with forged permits through monsoon floods. Someone should write that down so our families know how we died."

"We don't die," Casper said. "That's the job description."

Ferro looked up. "My father worked a concession in Sindh. Same logo, different valley. He coughed for six years before it took him." He stood. "I'm in. I've been in since the box."

Which left Ilsa, who had been watching Casper the entire time with an expression Casper couldn't file—something between assessment and appeal. When she finally spoke, it was quiet enough that the river nearly ate it.

"I lost someone to that mine too," she said. "Not in the rockfall. Slower. I've spent six years getting close to this evidence, and eight months inside Meridian's contractor network getting it out. I re-rigged your boat the first night because I needed to know if you were good enough to get us all out alive." She held Casper's gaze. "You are. So. Are you in? Because the samples leave this valley in the next six days or they leave in a Meridian incinerator, along with everyone who touched them."

Casper looked at the row of core samples gleaming on the wet rock. At the logo. At Daniel Osei's name living suddenly in her mouth again after eleven years of exile. At the five people standing on a shingle bar at the edge of the map, waiting for a woman who had sworn off mountains, rivers, responsibility, and hope in roughly that order.

The gorge above the confluence roared. Four days, maybe five, before the monsoon turned those waters into something even she couldn't read.

She picked up the topmost sample tube and weighed it in her hand. Lighter than a client's rope. Heavier than a license.

"Here's how it goes," she said. "Nobody lies to me again—not you, Devlin, not you, Ilsa, not the river. We run the old survey line, my way, my calls, and if any of you second-guess me on the water, you swim home. And when this is over—" she pointed the tube at Marsh like a compass needle finding north, "—you and I are going to have a much longer conversation about Nanda Kot, and what you knew, and when. Because I don't believe for one second a man this careful found out about Osei by accident."

Something flickered across Marsh's face—there and gone, fast as a fish turning in current.

"Fair," he said.

"Break camp in forty minutes," Casper said. "We run the throat of the gorge tonight, ahead of the weather. Eat while you can."

The crew scattered to the boats. Casper stayed on the shingle a moment longer, the sample tube still in her fist, watching the two rivers grind against each other at the confluence—grey against brown, neither yielding, both going the same direction whether they liked it or not.

Behind her, boots on stone. Ilsa, coming back for the last case, stopping beside her.

"You felt it too," Ilsa said. "When he said *conveniently.*"

Casper turned. "Felt what?"

"The flicker. When you mentioned Nanda Kot." Ilsa's voice dropped. "Casper—Marsh didn't just find out about Osei recently. I've read his field notes. There's a page dated the week before the rockfall. He was in that valley. He knew Osei. And he's never once mentioned it."

Above them, the first thunder of the monsoon rolled down the gorge like a door closing.
