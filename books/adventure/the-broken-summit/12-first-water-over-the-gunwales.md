## 12. First Water Over the Gunwales

Ilsa woke to the smell of wet stone.

Not rain—not yet—but the mineral exhalation of a gorge that had been holding its breath for two weeks and was finally about to exhale. She lay in her sleeping bag under the tarp Tomas had rigged between two boulders and listened to the river below their camp, and she heard something in its voice that had not been there yesterday. A thickness. A lower register, like an instrument being tuned down toward its breaking note.

She was out of her bag before she'd finished deciding to be.

The sky over the eastern ridgeline was wrong. Not dark exactly—the sun hadn't risen yet—but bruised, a color like old blood diluted in milk, stacked in layers that had no business being that organized at four in the morning. Clouds don't stack themselves into battlements unless something is driving them, and what drives them is pressure, and what pressure means here, in this season, in this gorge, is water.

She did the arithmetic standing up, boots half-laced. The gauge she'd planted at yesterday's confluence would be reading thirty centimeters higher than it had at dusk. By noon, sixty. By evening—

"Okafor," she said, and then louder, crossing the camp in six strides, "Casper."

The older woman was already awake. Of course she was. Ilsa had learned that about Casper Okafor in the first week: she slept like a sentry, one ear above the surface of sleep at all times, surfacing instantly and completely, no groggy interzone, no negotiation. She came out of her shelter with her hair still braided from the night before and her eyes already on the sky, and for one unguarded second Ilsa saw something move across her face that wasn't calculation at all. It looked almost like relief.

"Early," Casper said.

"Eleven days early."

"I said early." Casper was moving now, kicking the toe of her boot against Ferro's shelter, then Priya's, then shouting down the bank where Marsh and the security men—no. Not the security men anymore. The two who'd survived the capsized pursuit boat, Rennick and the quiet one whose name Ilsa still hadn't caught, were camped apart with their own fire, prisoners of circumstance more than of anyone's decision. Casper didn't even glance at them. "Tomas! Get the dry boxes lashed. Double lashings. Everything you love in this world goes inside a box or inside a barrel in the next ten minutes."

The camp detonated into motion. This was the thing Ilsa had been studying for weeks now, the thing she'd come aboard intending to measure and had instead found herself unable to stop watching: how Casper Okafor turned panic into choreography. Nobody screamed. Nobody froze. Ferro was hauling the fuel drums before he was fully awake; Priya was striking the medical shelter with the economy of someone who'd done field triage in places with worse weather than this; even Marsh, gray-faced and still wearing the yellowing bruise from his negotiation with the syndicate men, moved when her voice moved him, like a man remembering he had once known how to take orders.

And through all of it, Casper walked the boat.

Ilsa followed her. The *Kali Ma*—Tomas had named her, nobody else would have—sat on the shingle where they'd beached her the night before, and in the pre-dawn murk Ilsa watched Casper run her hands along the hull the way a doctor palpates a patient, pressing, listening, reading the boat's body through her palms. At the port-side seam just aft of midships she stopped. Pressed twice. Looked up at the sky as if consulting it.

"Tomas," she called. "The patch on the port chine. How many rapids do you give it?"

Tomas came at a jog, wiping his hands on a rag that had long ago stopped being a rag and become a geological formation. He crouched, pressed the same spot, and made a sound in his cheek that Ilsa had learned to translate as *it depends on whether God is feeling generous*.

"One," he said. "Maybe two if we're gentle."

"We're not going to be gentle." Casper stood. "We're going to be running flood-stage water in a boat held together by prayer and bicycle inner tubes. Gentle left us three days ago."

Ilsa felt the words land somewhere under her sternum. Three days ago. The night in the sea cave. The conversation that had not been a negotiation so much as two women setting down heavy things between them and discovering neither intended to pick hers back up. Since then something had changed in the geometry of the crew—Ilsa couldn't have named it, wouldn't have wanted to—but she noticed she now stood next to Casper without needing a reason, and that Casper had stopped saying *your instruments* and started saying *our readings*, and that these small grammatical shifts felt, absurdly, like the most significant events of her adult life.

Focus. She made herself focus.

"The headwater gauges," she said. "If the storm cell sat on the upper catchment overnight—and those clouds say it did—we're not looking at a rising river. We're looking at a wall. It'll come down the gorge in a pulse. First hour could jump a meter and a half."

"How long do we have?"

Ilsa checked her watch, then the sky, doing the math she'd already done and hating it twice. "If we launch in twenty minutes, we're ahead of the pulse. Barely. If we wait for full daylight, the pulse catches us in the worst section of the gorge with nowhere to eddy out."

"There's nowhere to eddy out anyway," Ferro said, arriving with a coil of rope over his shoulder and the particular brightness in his eyes that meant fear had arrived and been converted directly into readiness. "You've seen the maps. There's no eddies. There's barely any *riverbank*."

"That's why we launch in twenty minutes," Casper said.

* * *

They launched in seventeen.

The first hour was almost kind, which Ilsa distrusted immediately. The river ran high and fast but coherent, big brown water moving in long predictable tongues, and Casper read it aloud from the tiller in that low continuous narration she did—*tongue left, hole right of center, stay off the rock garden, ferry hard*—and the boat answered her the way a good horse answers a rider it trusts. Ilsa knelt amidships with the sample cases strapped around her wrist by a short tether, checking seals, checking straps, checking the little foam-collared cases that contained six years of her brother's posthumous testimony. The core samples. The water assays. The internal memos with the syndicate's letterhead that Marsh had carried across three borders sewn into his jacket lining. All of it heavier than gold and worth less than nothing if the river took it.

The sky opened at 0940.

Not gradually. The monsoon doesn't arrive; it ambushes. One moment the gorge walls were visible in their terrible vertical mile, green and dripping; the next the world went white and roaring, rain falling not in drops but in columns, hitting the river so hard the surface seemed to boil. Visibility dropped to fifty meters. Sound collapsed into a single frequency. Ilsa pulled her hood down and shouted the depth readings to Casper by rote, and Casper shouted back the line, and between the two of them the boat went on threading the needle while the world dissolved.

It was the third rapid where the river changed its mind about them.

Ilsa heard it before she saw it—a new sound underneath the rain, a deep subterranean grinding, like the gorge clearing its throat—and then the water ahead of them simply stood up. A wave, but not a wave; a standing feature taller than the boat, folding back on itself, fed by the pulse coming down from the headwaters exactly as she'd predicted, except that predictions are cold comfort when the prediction is currently trying to eat you.

"Hard left!" Casper's voice, cutting through everything. "LEFT, Tomas, pull—"

The boat hit the wave's shoulder rather than its face, which was the only reason they lived. But the impact lifted the starboard bow clean out of the water, hung them there for a horrible suspended instant—Ilsa would remember afterward the strange weightlessness, the sample case floating against its tether, Ferro's outstretched hand not quite touching anything—and then slammed them down and sideways into the trough, and the cold came in over the gunwales like a hand reaching into the boat.

Water. Everywhere. Knee-deep in seconds.

"Bail!" Priya was already moving, snatching the cut-down jerrican they used as a bailer. "Ferro, bail!"

Ferro bailed. Ilsa bailed. The rain kept filling what they emptied, and the river kept pushing them downstream toward the next rapid, and through all of it Casper stood at the tiller with water swirling around her calves, steering a partially submerged boat through flood-stage whitewater, and her voice never changed register. Never cracked. *Ferry angle. Hold. Now punch it.* As if the boat weren't drowning. As if drowning were merely a condition to be managed.

Then Tomas screamed.

Ilsa turned. He was flat against the port side, both hands pressed to the seam below the waterline, and water was coming through his fingers in a bright arterial jet. The patch. The patch that was good for one rapid, maybe two. The pulse had found the weak point the way water always finds the weak point, because finding weak points is the only job water has ever had.

"It's opening!" Tomas shouted. "I can't—it's peeling—"

"Caspar—" Ilsa started, and didn't know what she was asking for. There was nothing to ask for. There was no eddy, no bank, no stopping this boat until the gorge let go of it, and the gorge had no intention of letting go.

Casper looked at the jet of water. Looked at the river ahead—one more rapid, a big one, visible even through the rain as a horizon of white chaos. Looked at the fuel drums lashed midships, forty liters of petrol sitting in six inches of bilge water, and Ilsa watched her do the same math everyone else was doing: fuel drums float. Fuel drums are buoyant. Fuel drums, if the boat went down, might actually keep someone alive.

And the sample cases were strapped to Ilsa's wrist.

"Cases outrank fuel," Casper said. It wasn't a question. It wasn't even an order, really. It was a statement of physics, of priority, of the whole expedition's meaning compressed into five words spoken while water rose around everyone's knees.

But it was Ilsa's choice. Casper made it hers deliberately—turned her head, caught Ilsa's eyes through the rain, and gave her the decision like handing someone a knife handle-first. Because it was Ilsa's brother in those boxes. Ilsa's six years. Ilsa's grief, sealed in waterproof cases with syndicate letterhead.

Ilsa looked at the fuel drums. Forty liters. Their reserve power—the outboard's backup tank, the stove fuel, the means of running the pump that might keep a swamped boat alive. Without it, if they survived today, they were paddling. Human-powered, muscle-powered, all the way to Serkhang, through whatever came next.

She looked at the cases on her wrist.

"Overboard," she said. Her voice surprised her by being steady. "Both drums. Cut them loose."

"You sure?" Ferro, wide-eyed, knife already in his hand.

"They'll float. Someone might find them." A lie—they were in the middle of a monsoon gorge; no one would find anything—but it was a kind lie, and kindness was also cargo. "Cut them."

Ferro cut them. The drums went over the gunwales one after the other, bobbed once in the boiling brown water, and were gone behind them in seconds, swallowed by the roar. Ilsa felt the loss like an amputation—the boat suddenly lighter and infinitely poorer, their future suddenly manual—and then there was no time to feel anything, because the last rapid stood up in front of them.

* * *

Afterward, Ilsa could never reconstruct those ninety seconds in order. They existed in her memory only as sensation: the boat surfing sideways down a face of water that should have flipped them; Tomas with his whole arm through the failing seam, his flesh itself becoming the patch; Priya singing—actually singing, some Punjabi lullaby pitched loud over the chaos, and later Ilsa understood it was for Ferro's benefit, a medic's trick for keeping panic out of a young man's spine; and Casper, always Casper, making micro-corrections no sane person could perceive, reading the chaos and finding within it a thread, and holding that thread with both hands.

They came out the bottom of the rapid upright. Swamped to the benches, engine drowned, patch failing, but upright, drifting in the relative calm of a widening pool below the rapid, surrounded by walls of rain.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Tomas pulled his arm out of the seam, looked at the water still seeping through, and started laughing—a low, disbelieving, exhausted laugh that spread through the boat like the warmth from a shared fire.

"Held," he said. "By meters. By *centimeters*. That patch owed me money, I swear."

Priya was already moving among them, checking hands, checking eyes, pressing a thermos of something hot into each pair of shaking fingers. Ferro sat very still, staring at the place where the fuel drums had been, and Ilsa reached over and put her hand briefly on his shoulder, and he covered it with his own and didn't look at her, and that was its own kind of conversation.

Casper stepped out of the water pooled in the bottom of the boat and sat down heavily on the thwart beside Ilsa. Up close, Ilsa could see what the tiller distance had hidden: the fine tremor in her jaw, the exhaustion underneath the command, the way her hands—those legendary hands, the ones that read water—were shaking, not from cold.

"You gave up our power," Casper said quietly. Rain ran off the brim of her hood between them.

"You told me to."

"I told you the cases outranked the fuel. Choosing it was yours." She turned her head. Her eyes were the gray of wet slate, and in them Ilsa found none of the assessment she'd braced for, none of the weighing and measuring this woman had subjected her to since the day she boarded. Just recognition. One professional acknowledging another's work under load. "Six years," Casper said. "You chose six years over forty liters. I needed to see you make that call, because the next one will be worse, and I need to know what you do when it counts."

"And now you know."

"Now I know." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the geological evidence that smiles occurred here. "Paddle, then. Both of us. Everyone. Unless you'd rather drift."

Ilsa looked downstream. The gorge bent away into the rain, and somewhere beyond that bend lay the checkpoint, the syndicate agents, the impossible route Casper had begun describing in fragments over the last two nights—the route from her nightmares, the one she'd sworn off, the one that was now, apparently, the plan. Their reserve power was gone. Their boat was wounded. The monsoon had eleven more days of ambition.

She picked up her paddle.

"No," she said. "I'd rather finish it."

Casper nodded once, and put her own blade in the water, and the *Kali Ma*—swamped, stripped, patched with hope and bicycle tubes—began to move again under human power alone, nine small hearts driving her down the throat of the storm.

It was Ferro, an hour later, who spotted it: far back upstream, at the lip of the last rapid, a shape in the rain that was not a rock. A boat. Running their wake.

Someone had found the fuel drums—or hadn't needed them—and was following.
