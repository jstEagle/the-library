## 13. Two Women, One Bilge Pump

The cave found them the way mercy usually did—late, and by accident.

Ilsa saw it first from her station at the bow: a black seam in the cliff wall where the river had been chewing at limestone for ten thousand years, a mouth maybe four meters wide, half-drowned at this water level but dry inside. She didn't say anything clever. She just put her hand flat against the gunwale twice—the signal Casper had taught her on the second day, back when they still spoke to each other like strangers negotiating a border—and Casper's head came up, read her line of sight, and swung the tiller without a word.

The eddy behind the rock fin was ugly, recirculating, full of the debris the flood had stripped off the hillsides. Casper took them into it anyway, backward, which was the only way in, ferrying on the eddy line with that economy of motion Ilsa had stopped trying to describe to herself because every description came out sounding like something other than what it was. The boat kissed the cave's threshold. Ferro and Tomas were over the side before it settled, hauling the bowline up onto a shelf of dry stone, and then they were inside, and the roar of the gorge cut down to something almost conversational, and the rain became a sound happening somewhere else, to someone else.

"Everyone out," Casper said. "Everything out. If the river rises tonight, we lose the boat, not the cargo."

They worked by headlamp. The sample cases went up first—of course they did; Ilsa carried them herself, both hands, refusing help she didn't need—and then the fuel, the medical kit, the food barrels, the tarp, the stove. Priya got Marsh settled against the far wall, where his bandaged ribs made him breathe in short, careful installments. He hadn't spoken since the portage. Whatever he'd said to Casper out there among the broken rock, he seemed to have said everything he had left.

Tomas built a fire from driftwood that burned green and smoky, and nobody complained, because smoke meant warmth and warmth meant the day was finally over. It was the ninth day since the monsoon arrived early. It was the fourth day since the second boat had appeared behind them. It was the second day since Marsh had come back bleeding from his negotiation, and since Ferro and Priya had stood in front of Ilsa with a pry bar and an open dry box between them, and since she had told them the truth because there was no longer any version of lying that survived contact with core samples stamped with the syndicate's mark.

It was the first night, though, that there was no one else awake to overhear.

Ilsa checked the cases one more time—seals intact, desiccant holding, the foam lining doing its dumb patient work—and then she looked across the fire and found Casper looking at her.

Not staring. Casper didn't stare. But she was sitting with her back against the limestone, boots off for the first time Ilsa had seen since the confluence, turning a cup of tea she hadn't drunk, and her eyes were on Ilsa with the same attention she gave to water. Reading. Waiting for the current to show her what was underneath.

"You re-rigged my boat," Casper said. "Night before the gorges. Before you'd told anyone anything."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ilsa set down the case lid. She had rehearsed this conversation for six years, in three languages, in hospital corridors and mining offices and the passenger seat of trucks crossing four borders. She had rehearsed it so thoroughly that she knew all its versions by heart—the angry version, the diplomatic version, the version where she cried, the version where she never mentioned her brother at all and simply presented the evidence like a lawyer laying cards on a table. And now, in a sea cave in the Karakoram, with green woodsmoke stinging her eyes and a bilge pump drying on a stone beside her, none of those versions would come. What came instead was just the fact itself, plain as gravel.

"Because I've watched you run water for two weeks," she said, "and I couldn't stand to watch you die for someone else's lie."

Casper's cup stopped turning.

"The sandbar below the second bend," Ilsa went on. "It wasn't on any chart because it moved after the first flood pulse. Your chart said deep channel, left side. Left side was six inches of water over hardpack. You'd have grounded at speed, in the narrows, with the whole flood behind you." She shrugged, and hated how small the shrug sounded. "So I moved your anchor points while everyone slept. And then I lay awake wondering if you'd notice, and whether noticing would make you throw me off the boat."

"I noticed."

"I know you noticed."

"And you did it anyway. Before I'd earned it. Before—" Casper gestured vaguely, the gesture taking in the cases, the forged permit folded in Marsh's jacket, the whole architecture of secrets they were all living inside. "You didn't know me."

"No," Ilsa said. "But I knew what happens to boats that hit things they can't see. My brother was on one."

The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the cave, water dripped onto stone with a patience that made the silence feel geological. Downstream, muffled by the rock, the gorge kept up its argument with itself, and Ilsa let it talk for a moment because it was easier than she was.

"His name was Anders," she said. "He was a hydrologist too. Younger than me by four years and better at it, which he never let me forget and I never admitted. We grew up in a valley in Norway where the river ran so clean you could drink it straight from the source, and he used to say that was the whole job, really—all of it, everywhere—just keeping water the way it starts out." Her voice held. She'd had practice holding it. "He took a contract here. Water quality survey, upstream of a new mine site, very legitimate, very well-funded. He sent me samples every month. Then the month came when the samples stopped, and the letter that came instead said there had been an accident. A slope failure above the adit. Nine men dead. They buried him in a village churchyard because flying him home cost more than the compensation payment, and the compensation payment was less than his last invoice."

Casper had gone very still. Not the stillness of shock—Ilsa had learned the difference on this river. This was the stillness of a woman listening to a set of rapids she'd been hearing from a long way off, waiting for the drop to show itself.

"They called it a natural event," Ilsa said. "Act of God. Slope destabilization due to seasonal saturation. There was even a report. Forty pages, peer-reviewed, immaculate. I read it eleven times. And then I got hold of the raw seismic data from the monitoring stations—data the report didn't use—and the failure didn't look like saturation. It looked like loading. Weeks of controlled blasting in a pit that was supposed to be a hundred meters further east, undercutting a slope the geology report had flagged as unstable. They knew. They had the memo. They mined anyway, because the ore body was richer on that side, and when the mountain came down on nine men they wrote a beautiful document explaining why it was nobody's fault, and the men who signed the memo signed the report too, and everyone kept their jobs."

"Anders's samples," Casper said quietly. "The ones he was sending you."

"Heavy metals. Orders of magnitude over every limit. He'd found the tailings seep in month two and started documenting it properly, which is the kind of thing that gets a hydrologist invited to meetings and then not invited to meetings." Ilsa fed a piece of driftwood to the fire and watched it catch. "Six years. That's how long I've been following this. Contracts, consultancies, cover jobs—I surveyed irrigation channels in Tajikistan for a year because it put me in the right watershed. When Marsh approached me with the Keshar concession, I knew the permits were wrong before I finished reading them. I came anyway. Because the only thing worse than working for a liar is standing outside the cave for six years while the evidence sits in the dark."

She looked up. Casper was watching her with an expression Ilsa couldn't file under anything—not pity, not admiration, nothing with a clean name. Something rawer. Recognition, maybe, of the specific loneliness of carrying knowledge you couldn't yet share, which was a country they had both been living in for years without knowing they shared a border.

"Nanda Kot," Ilsa said. Not a question. "Marsh told us the route skirts the valley. He didn't tell us why your hands shake at the tiller when we're near it. But I've been reading currents a long time, Casper Okafor, and I'm telling you I know what a hidden hazard looks like from the outside. So if you want to tell me what actually happened up there, I'll listen. And if you don't, I'll sit here and drink this terrible tea and not ask again."

For a long moment Casper didn't move. Then she laughed—a short, airless sound with no humor in it, the laugh of someone who has just been shown the exit from a room she'd forgotten she was locked in.

"You want to know what happened on Nanda Kot."

"I want to know what you've been carrying."

"Everyone wants to know what happened on Nanda Kot. The inquiry wanted to know. The press wanted to know. My ex-wife wanted to know, in the way that means *give me a reason to believe you*, and I couldn't, because I didn't have one. I had weather data and a route plan and a client who walked into a rockfall zone forty minutes ahead of schedule, and none of it added up to anything except that I was the guide, and guides are responsible, and responsibility is the story people prefer because it has a villain and the villain is conveniently already standing there." She turned the cup. Drank, finally, grimacing at the temperature. "I've spent seven years building a life around the shape of that guilt. River work, border work, work that doesn't ask questions. I got very good at not thinking about it. And then Marsh puts a map on a table in a tea house and draws a line through the exact valley, and I sign his contract anyway, because my daughter's tuition is due, and I tell myself it's just money."

"It isn't just money."

"No." Casper set the cup down. "It isn't. Here's what I haven't said out loud, to anyone, ever: I suspected. From the beginning. The rockfall zone was flagged on my own route notes—flagged by me, red ink, my handwriting—and the client's itinerary had him passing through it at the wrong hour. Someone had adjusted the timing. I assumed it was a scheduling error. I was tired, I was managing a difficult client, I was flattered by the size of the fee, and I let myself believe the error was innocent. And when he died, and the inquiry asked why I hadn't double-checked the timing, I had no answer except the true one, which is that part of me didn't want to look too closely at a client who was paying that much and asking questions about the watershed." Her jaw tightened. "He was investigating the mine, Ilsa. Upstream. Same as your brother. And I half-knew it, and I ran, and running is the only crime I've ever committed that no court has ever charged me with."

The drips in the dark counted time. Ilsa realized her hands had gone cold around the case handle and made herself let go.

"So we match," she said.

"We match."

"Two women who each spent years being told the disaster was their fault, or their grief was unreasonable, or their questions were disloyal. Two sets of evidence sitting in sealed boxes because the systems that were supposed to want them don't." She moved around the fire and sat down on the stone beside Casper—not close, but within the radius where words could stay low and still land. "Marsh needs us because we're the only ones who can get this out. Fine. But that's not why I'm telling you this, and it's not why I'm asking what I'm about to ask."

"Ask it."

"When we get through the last gorge—if we get through it—Marsh will want to sell this to journalists, or lawyers, or whoever pays best and asks fewest questions. That's his nature. I've watched him negotiate with a river and try to haggle." Ilsa turned to face her fully. "I'm not giving this evidence to a system. Six years taught me the systems are where evidence goes to be embalmed. I'm taking it to Serkhang. To the villages. To the people whose fathers are in that churchyard and whose children are drinking that water. Read aloud, in their language, in their square, where it can't be filed or settled or bought. And I want to know—before the water rises, before the checkpoint, before any of it—whether you're with me on that, or whether you're just hired hands until the money clears."

Casper was silent for a long time. Ilsa let her be silent. She had learned on this river that Casper's silences weren't absence; they were depth soundings, taken seriously, reported honestly.

"My daughter," Casper said at last. "If I do this illegally—if I deliver stolen evidence to villages with my name already blacklisted—they'll bury me. Not the license. Me. Every concession, every border, every legal river on three continents. And the debt collector doesn't care about justice; he cares about sixty days." She looked into the fire. "And if I don't do it, I spend the rest of my life running tourists past valleys where the water is poison and calling it adventure."

"Yes."

"That's not a choice. That's a fork in a flooded river where both channels have holes in the bottom."

"Yes," Ilsa said again. "I've been in that channel for six years. You stop choosing the lesser hole and start choosing who's bailing beside you."

Casper turned her head. In the firelight her face was all planes and exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion something was moving—something Ilsa recognized from mirrors, from the moment on her brother's last birthday when she'd understood that grief was not going to pass through her but settle in her, and that the only question left was what she would build on top of the ruin.

"Serkhang," Casper said. "Read aloud. Their square, their language." A breath. "All right. Together."

No handshake. No oath. Casper picked up the bilge pump from the stone between them, wiped it down with a rag, and handed it to Ilsa handle-first—an ordinary object, dented, smelling of bilgewater and diesel—and Ilsa took it, and that was the whole ceremony. Two women, one pump, and a river outside that forgave nothing and owed nothing and did not care what they had decided in the dark.

But when Ilsa lay down later on the tarp with the sample cases stacked against the wall above the high-water line, she found she could sleep. Not easily—the gorge saw to that—but without the particular weight she'd carried since the confluence, the weight of being the only one awake inside a secret. Across the dying fire, Casper's breathing had gone slow and even, and once, near morning, Ilsa heard her murmur a name in her sleep—*Meera*, soft, the daughter's name, the debt's name—and Ilsa lay listening to it and thought: *we'll get you back to her. Not redeemed. Back.*

Outside, the rain kept falling on a world that kept its records in water. Inside, two liars and two mourners and two hydrologists of the same poisoned watershed had become, without ceremony and without permission from anyone, the same crew.

Tomorrow they would run the flooded narrows. Tomorrow the checkpoint, the agents, the arithmetic of days. Tonight there was only the fire, the drip of the cave, and the pump between them, dry and ready, holding nothing yet, waiting for whatever the morning brought up from below.
