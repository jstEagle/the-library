## 14. Marsh's Second Boat

Ferro saw the boat before anyone else did, and for once in his life he didn't say anything right away.

This was new behavior for him. Nineteen years old and built out of elbows and appetite, Ferro had spent his whole time on Casper's crew being the one who said things too early — spotted the crocodile wake too late to matter but shouted anyway, smelled the fuel leak an hour after Tomas had already fixed it, announced his opinions on everything from Priya's rationing to the shape of clouds. He was the youngest by a decade and he knew it, knew it the way you know a stone in your boot: constantly, painfully, and mostly when you're trying to walk somewhere fast.

But this morning, poling the *Laxmi* through the long green slackwater below the confluence while the others slept off their watches, he saw the glint upstream and held his tongue.

It was just a flash at first. Sunlight off something metal, way back where the river bent around the drowned forest. He told himself it was a bird. Then it came again, steady, rhythmic, like a signal lamp, and birds don't blink in rhythm, and Ferro's stomach did the thing it did when he stood too close to rapids — that drop, that cold hand around the spine.

He counted. Old habit from his uncle, who'd run cargo on these rivers back when the cargo had been legal. You count the seconds between flashes and you know how far away they are, and if the interval stays the same while your own boat moves, then whatever's flashing is moving too.

Moving with them.

* * *

The morning fog hadn't burned off yet. It lay on the water in ropes and rags, and the gorge walls rose up on either side so steep and black-wet that the sky was only a rumor of gray overhead. The *Laxmi* moved through it all like something half-dreamed — Tomas's patched hull holding, barely, the engine muttering its one good cylinder's complaint, the sample cases lashed down amidships under tarps that nobody looked at anymore because everybody knew what was under them now.

Ferro watched the glint for twenty minutes. It stayed. It kept pace. When the river curved left, the glint cut the curve tighter than any boat should, taking the inside line like it knew the channel, like it had a chart, like somebody aboard had run these waters before or — worse — had instruments that made running them unnecessary.

He thought about waking Casper. Her bunk was the canvas sling aft, and she slept like a folded knife even now, even after everything, her boots on, always, since Serkhang. But waking Casper meant saying what he'd seen, and saying what he'd seen meant admitting he'd been watching the rear water instead of minding his station, and there was a smaller, stupider part of him that still wanted her to think he was good at this. That wanted it more than he wanted almost anything.

Then the fog shifted, and he saw the boat itself.

Not a patrol skiff. Not a fisherman's longtail. It was gray, low, twin-hulled, with a cabin block that sat wrong for these waters — too heavy, too armored-looking, the kind of craft you'd see on a military canal in another country entirely. No flag. No registration numbers painted anywhere he could see. Just a dark rectangle of glass at the bow where a man stood with binoculars, and even at that distance, even through fog, Ferro felt the look land on him like a hand on the back of his neck.

He went aft and woke Casper.

She came up out of sleep the way she did everything — no wasted motion, already listening before her eyes opened. He told it plain: second boat, gray, keeping pace, maybe eight hundred meters back, inside line on the bends. She listened without interrupting, which scared him more than shouting would have. Then she stood, took the tiller from him without asking, and looked back downriver for a long time.

"That's not Marsh's tender," she said finally. "Marsh's tender is a rubber duck with a bad knee."

"Whose is it?"

"I don't know." And then, quieter, in the voice she used for truths she didn't want to be true: "I know whose it isn't."

* * *

By midmorning the whole crew knew. There was no hiding it; the gray boat had stopped bothering with subtlety, showing itself openly whenever the fog thinned, hanging back at exactly the distance where it could watch and not be boarded. Twice Ferro caught the sun-flash of glass again — someone studying them through optics, unhurried, patient as a fisherman over a hole in the ice.

They rafted up in an eddy behind a gravel bar to argue about it, engines idling low, and the argument happened the way arguments happened on the *Laxmi*: everyone talking at once until Casper's silence got loud enough to fill the space.

"It's security," Ilsa said. She stood at the gunwale with her instruments case hugged to her chest like a child, and her face had gone very still and very pale, which Ferro had learned meant she was thinking fastest. "When the syndicate detected the forged permit — and they would have, weeks ago, at the first checkpoint database cross-check — standard procedure is asset interdiction. They hire local security contractors to shadow the vessel and recover the cargo."

"Standard procedure," Tomas repeated flatly. He was crouched by the engine housing with grease to his elbows, and he didn't look up. "You say that like you've read the manual."

"I have read the manual." Ilsa's voice didn't waver. "That's rather the point of me."

Priya had her medic bag open on her knees, though nobody was hurt yet, and Ferro understood suddenly that she'd opened it the way other people crossed themselves. "How many men does a thing like that carry?"

"Eight. Ten. Enough."

"And us?" Ferro heard his own voice crack on it and hated himself. "We're five. Six counting Marsh, and Marsh counts for half."

Nobody laughed. That was the worst part. On the *Laxmi*, jokes were load-bearing; take them away and the whole structure groaned.

Casper had been looking downriver the entire time, at the place where the gray boat would appear when the next bend gave it to them. Now she turned around, and Ferro saw her do the thing he'd seen her do twice before — once before the flood surge at Keshar Gorge, once the night she signed Marsh's contract — the thing where she stopped being a person deciding and became a person who had decided, some private border crossed behind her eyes.

"Marsh," she said. "Get up here."

* * *

Devlin Marsh came forward from the bow where he'd been sitting apart from everyone since the confluence, since the confession, since Ferro and Priya had dragged the truth out of the dry boxes and the whole expedition had turned into something else. He looked older than he had at the tea house, Ferro thought. The charm was still there, laminated over him like varnish, but underneath it his face had started to go soft and gray the way fruit goes.

"You knew," Casper said. It wasn't a question.

"I suspected—"

"You *knew*. The forgery gets flagged, the syndicate hires dogs, and you knew the dogs would come because you've worked with these people for fifteen years. You knew, and you let us float down this river like bait on a hook."

"Casper." Marsh spread his hands, and Ferro noticed they were shaking, just slightly, the fine tremor of a man running on caffeine and fear. "If I'd told you at the tea house — 'oh, incidentally, there may be armed contractors following us' — would you have signed?"

"No."

"There it is, then." He said it lightly, almost breezily, and for one second Ferro actually admired the sheer engineering of the man's self-justification, the way a bridge is admirable right up until it drops you into the gorge.

"They're not going to board us," Casper said. "Not in open water, not with witnesses. They'll wait for the narrows, or the night, or the portage — someplace quiet. So we're going to give them a conversation instead. One conversation, on my terms, before they pick theirs."

"And how," Priya asked, "do you propose to have a conversation with ten armed men?"

"We don't. He does." Casper looked at Marsh. "You sold us this route. You can sell them a reason to let us keep floating it."

Something moved across Marsh's face — relief and terror braided together, and under both of them, briefly, something that might have been shame. "Alone? They'll want to know why the crew isn't present."

"Because the crew doesn't trust you," Casper said. "Which happens to be true, which makes it the easiest lie you'll ever tell."

* * *

They put him ashore at the old ferry landing below the cliffs — a rotted platform on pilings, half-swallowed by the river, a place where people had been crossing water longer than any of the countries nearby had existed. Marsh stepped off onto the boards in his ruined leather jacket, and Ferro watched him straighten his collar before he walked, and thought: *he's going to meet them like it's a business lunch.* Some men couldn't help dressing the inside of a disaster.

The *Laxmi* backed off downstream into the fog and idled, engines low, everyone watching the landing through the murk. Minutes passed. The fog breathed. Somewhere upslope a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"He'll sell us out," Ferro whispered to Priya. "Again. That's the whole pattern. He sold the Nanda Kot route, he sold the permit story, he'll—"

"Maybe." Priya didn't look away from the landing. Her hands were busy with nothing, re-rolling a bandage that was already rolled. "But here's the thing about Devlin Marsh that took me three weeks to see. He's a coward about himself and not about the job. He'll lie to our faces all day. But he wants this evidence delivered more than he wants to breathe. Whatever else he is, he's not going to hand it back to the syndicate."

"Why not? What's he get out of it?"

Priya was quiet a moment. "I think," she said slowly, "it's the only thing he's ever done that isn't for sale. I think that frightens him worse than the guns do."

On the landing, a shape detached itself from the cliff shadow. Then another. Then four more, moving with the loose coordinated economy Ferro recognized from the border towns, from the men who guarded convoys — professionals, spread out in an arc, weapons slung low and ready. Marsh stopped walking. Raised his hands, palms out, the universal grammar of *not a threat, let's talk.*

The fog closed. Sound carried strangely; Ferro caught fragments — Marsh's voice rising and falling in its salesman's cadence, another voice answering, deeper, unhurried, and the terrible thing about the deep voice was how calm it was. Angry men shout. This voice never needed to.

Then the fragments stopped altogether, and the waiting got very long.

Tomas killed the engine so they could hear better. Ilsa stood at the bow with her fists white on the rail. Casper had the tiller in one hand and, Ferro noticed for the first time, a boat hook in the other, and he understood that she had already decided what she'd do if Marsh screamed, and it wasn't anything negotiable.

The deep voice said something short.

Marsh's voice answered, higher now, faster, the cadence cracking, losing its polish — and Ferro heard the exact moment the negotiation stopped being a negotiation. It was a sound he knew from schoolyards and street markets and every hierarchy he'd ever lived inside: the sound of a man realizing the price has changed and he can't afford it.

A scuffle. Boards banging on pilings. A single sharp cry that cut off wrong.

"Go," Casper said, and the *Laxmi* surged for the landing.

* * *

They found him face-down at the edge of the platform, one arm trailing in the water, and for two full seconds Ferro's heart simply stopped, because the arm wasn't moving and the water around it was blooming pink.

Then Marsh coughed, and the world restarted.

They hauled him up over the rotten boards — Casper gripping his jacket, Priya already kneeling, gloves on, scissors out — and Ferro knelt on his other side and held his head off the wet wood and saw the damage up close. Split brow, blood sheeting into his eye. Lip opened against teeth. And when Marsh tried to push himself up, he made a sound like tearing cloth and curled around his ribs, and Priya's hands went there next, quick and clinical, peeling back the shirt.

"Two, maybe three cracked," she said. "Bruised deep. They were careful — nothing ruptured, I think. They *wanted* him walking."

"Message," Ilsa said quietly. She had come ashore last and stood at the edge of the platform, looking out at the fog where the gray boat had been, and her voice had a flatness Ferro had never heard from her, a floor-of-the-ocean flatness. "Beaten, not killed. Left alive at a landmark we couldn't miss. That's not punishment. That's punctuation."

"What did they say?" Casper asked Marsh. "Before. What did they tell you?"

Marsh laughed, and it turned into a wet cough, and he laughed again anyway, blood on his teeth, and Ferro thought: *there it is, the varnish, still trying.* "They said—" he wheezed, "they said I could have my contract fee. Full payment. If I brought them the cases and the crew's names. All five names, written down, and they'd let me walk away rich."

The platform was silent except for the river working at the pilings underneath.

"And you said?" Priya asked softly.

"I said—" Marsh got a hand under himself, sat up against a piling, and looked at each of them in turn, and whatever Ferro had seen in his face before, the charm and the fear and the shame, none of it was there now. What was there was exhausted, and strange, and clean, like a man who'd set down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying. "I told them the crew's names weren't mine to sell. I told them they'd missed their window by about three weeks." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at the smear of red and laughed again, quieter. "First honest sentence I've spoken in years. Cost me three ribs."

"The names," Casper said. "They wanted our names because we're not contractors anymore. Is that it? That's what this is?"

Ilsa answered before Marsh could, and her ocean-floor voice made it worse somehow. "Witnesses. Once the forgery was flagged, everyone on this boat stopped being hired labor and became evidence. People who've seen the samples, the documents, the route. The syndicate doesn't pay witnesses, Casper. It erases them. Or it owns them — names on paper, leverage forever, the same trick they used on you at Nanda Kot."

Ferro felt it land in his chest like cold water. *Like you at Nanda Kot.* He looked at Casper and saw her take it too, saw the old wound and the new knowledge grind together behind her face.

"So that's it," Tomas said from the boat, where he'd kept the engine ticking over the whole time, faithful as a heartbeat. "There's no job left. No contract, no fee, no client. We're not getting paid, we're not going home the way we came, and there are ten men on that boat who need us to disappear." He spat over the side. "Somebody say the thing out loud, because I'm tired of everyone thinking it alone."

Casper stood. Blood on her hands from Marsh's jacket, fog beading in her hair, and Ferro watched her look downriver toward the gorges they still had to run and then back at her crew, one face at a time, and he braced for the speech, the order, the captain's voice.

What she said was quiet. "Anyone who wants off, I'll put you ashore at the next village myself, and I'll never think less of you. Not for one second. That's not a test. It's the truth."

Nobody moved.

Ferro realized his mouth was open and shut it. He thought about the debt collectors and the tuition letter and the sixty days, about the money that had gotten him onto this boat, and found — genuinely, to his own surprise — that he couldn't remember why it had ever mattered. There was a gray boat full of men who wanted his name, and a woman with blood on her hands offering him a door out, and nineteen years of being the kid who talked too early, and none of it added up to wanting to leave.

"I'm in," he said, and his voice didn't crack this time. "Obviously."

"One for one," Priya said. "All the way to Serkhang."

Tomas grunted, which from Tomas was a signature on a contract. Ilsa just nodded once, and looked downriver, and her hands finally loosened on the instrument case.

Marsh struggled upright against the piling, gray-faced, breathing in careful shallow sips. "For what it's worth," he said, "the man with the deep voice — Karim. He runs their river security. He told me one more thing before his friends explained the situation to my ribs." He paused, and Ferro watched him decide to say it, watched the last of the varnish come off. "He said the checkpoint at the head of the final gorge is manned as of yesterday. Syndicate agents, not concession staff. They're not chasing us anymore, Casper. They're ahead of us. Waiting where the river gets narrow enough that ten men with rifles only need to hold one bank."

The fog was lifting off the water in long silver sheets. Downstream, around the bend, the gorge walls waited, black and close, the river sliding between them like a wire being drawn thin.

Casper looked at the water for a long moment. Then she stepped back aboard, took the tiller, and pushed them out from the landing into the current.

"Hold on to something," she said. "We're going to need a route nobody's ever run."
