## 15. The Portage of Broken Things

The channel ended where the mountain had decided it should.

Casper saw it before anyone else did—the way she always saw things first, a guide's curse, her eyes reading the river the way other people read faces. A quarter mile downstream, the gorge pinched to nothing, and beyond that pinch the water was wrong. Not fast-wrong, not rapids-wrong. *Dead* wrong. The surface moved in sheets instead of lines, smooth as poured glass over something enormous and buried, and the color had gone from glacier-teal to a brown so thick it looked solid.

Landslide dam. Recent. The lake behind it would be filling by the hour, and when it went—and it would go, in monsoon water like this—it would take everything downstream with it, including them, including the boat, including whatever was left of Marsh's evidence and Marsh's plan and Marsh's face, which was already starting to look less like a negotiator's and more like a man who'd been introduced to a rock at speed.

She killed the engine and let the current carry them the last hundred meters, drifting toward a gravel fan on the true left where an old slide scar came down off the ridge like a scar across a throat—raw rock and shattered timber, a wound the mountain had opened years ago and never bothered to close.

"Everyone out," she said. "We're done with the river for a while."

Ferro looked at the scar, then at Casper, then back at the scar, doing the arithmetic of a nineteen-year-old who'd learned this season that arithmetic could kill you. "That's not a trail."

"It is now."

* * *

They spent two hours getting the boat out of the water, and Casper hated every minute of it, because hauling a loaded raft up a gravel bank while watching the sky stack grey over the ridgeline was the kind of work that made time feel like a debt collector. Tomas rigged a haul line to a birch trunk and they walked the boat up in stages, Ilsa directing from the bow with her hands flat on the gunwales like she could feel the current through the hull, and maybe she could; Casper had stopped underestimating her somewhere around the second rapid of the morning, when Ilsa had called a hidden hole three seconds before it opened under them and saved Priya's ribs in the process.

By noon the boat sat above the high-water line, lashed to trees that might themselves be underwater in four days, and Casper stood at the base of the slide scar with her hands on her hips and let her body do something her mind had been refusing to do since Nanda Kot.

It remembered.

Not the route—not exactly. Routes were maps, and maps were lies told slowly. What her body remembered was older and stranger: the way her weight wanted to shift onto her left foot on broken ground, testing each block before trusting it; the angle at which to place a hand on wet granite so the fingers found friction instead of hope; the rhythm of moving through wreckage, three points of contact, never hurrying, because hurry was how mountains collected their interest. She'd learned all of it young, on her father's shoulder, then alone, then teaching clients, and she'd spent six years trying to forget it, and none of the forgetting had taken.

"You've run this scar before," Ilsa said beside her. Not a question.

"I ran this valley before." Casper didn't look at her. "Different year. Different reason."

"The syndicate's survey maps show this slope as stable."

"The syndicate's survey maps are why we're carrying their sins uphill." She tested the first boulder with her boot. It held. "Stay behind me. Step where I step. If the ground moves, don't run—drop flat and hold. Rock doesn't chase you. It just falls on whoever's standing in the wrong decade."

She started up.

* * *

The portage took what remained of the day and gave back nothing but bruises.

The scar was three hundred meters of vertical chaos—a landslide's leavings, house-sized blocks tumbled among splintered pine and slurry mud that hadn't dried since the year it slid. Casper led them through it the way water finds its way through it: patiently, laterally, always reading two moves ahead. She heard her own breathing change into the old expedition cadence, slow and deliberate, and she heard Ferro behind her copying that cadence without knowing he was doing it, and something about that—something about passing the rhythm down—hurt worse than the scrape on her shin or the weight of the sample cases distributed across six backs.

Twice the ground shifted under them, small settlements of stone settling deeper into the slope, and twice she called the halt and made everyone freeze until the mountain finished its small argument with itself. The second time, Priya's hand found Ferro's collar and held him still, and Casper thought: *this is what a crew is.* Not a contract. Not a permit. Just people agreeing to stand very quietly together while the world rearranges itself.

At the top of the scar, where the slope gentled into scrub meadow below the ridge, they cached the heavy cases under a cairn and dropped the empty packs, and Casper allowed them twenty minutes of sitting, which was eleven more than she wanted to give.

Marsh sat apart, the way he'd been sitting apart since he came back from his negotiation with the second boat bleeding through his shirt. He'd gone grey around the mouth. Priya had cleaned the gash along his ribs that morning and re-dressed it at the midday halt, and each time she touched him he flinched—not from the pain, Casper thought, but from being seen.

Now she watched Priya cross the meadow to him anyway, kit in hand, because Priya was the kind of person who treated wounds whether or not she respected the body wearing them.

Casper should have looked away. She didn't.

* * *

"This is going to hurt," Priya said.

"It already hurts."

"That's infection talking. Sit still."

Marsh sat still. Up close, in the flat afternoon light, he looked older than his rivalry had ever let him look—all those years of competing with Casper across briefing rooms and permit offices, and she'd never once noticed he was going grey at the temples. Or maybe she'd never let herself notice. Rivalry was easier when the other person stayed a silhouette.

Priya peeled the dressing back and studied the gash with a medic's impersonal attention. "You need stitches. Real ones. This is field-closure territory and I'm working with fishing line and vodka."

"Do it."

"I will. But first—" she threaded the needle, unhurried, terrible in her calmness, "—you're going to tell her. Because I've been sewing men shut for twelve years, Devlin, and the ones who won't talk always tear open again."

Marsh looked past her, across the meadow, and found Casper looking back. There was nowhere for either of them to go. That was the thing about portages: they stripped away the river, the engine, the noise, all the machinery of forward motion, and left you sitting on a hillside with the truth and a needle.

He laughed once, without humor. "She's right. You know she's right."

"I know she has a sharp object," Casper said, and came closer, and sat down on a rock opposite him, and waited.

For a long moment Marsh watched his own hands. They were shaking. She'd never seen them shake, not on the worst briefing, not on the Nanda Kot summit push, not ever, and seeing it now did something complicated to her chest that she refused to name.

"Nanda Kot," he said. "You want to know what actually happened. Six years you've wanted to know, and I let you believe it was your call, your route, your error. I let the whole industry believe it." He exhaled. "I sold the route, Casper. Two seasons before. To a fixer working for the mining consortium—Keshar's parent, the same one whose logo is stenciled on our dry boxes. They paid for a detailed route description. Timing, camps, the icefall window, the serac schedule above Camp Two. Everything. I told myself it was just paper. Route beta. Everyone sells beta."

Priya's needle paused. Started again.

"But it wasn't beta, was it," Casper said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Underneath the levelness was something vast and quiet, like the water above the landslide dam.

"No. It was a schedule. They needed to know when the mountain would be full of witnesses and when it wouldn't. Your client—Reyes—he wasn't there to climb. You knew that much. What you didn't know was that he'd been hired too, by a different set of people, to document what the consortium was doing upstream. The tailings, the outflow, the watershed. And when they realized he was climbing *your* route, on *my* sold schedule—" Marsh's hands flattened against his knees, pressing down as if to keep them honest, "—they triggered the rockfall. Timed charges, probably. Small ones, in the release zone above Camp One. Enough to bring down what was already loose. And every report afterward said what reports always say: natural instability, tragic season, guide error."

The word landed where it had always lived. *Guide error.* Six years of it. Blacklist letters using it. Debt collectors implying it. Her daughter growing up inside its shadow.

"You knew," Casper said. "How long?"

"Since the season after. A fixer got drunk in Kathmandu and bragged sideways about 'the Keshar job.' I put it together within a month." He met her eyes finally, and there was no rivalry left in his face at all, just a tired man on a hillside holding out the worst thing he owned. "And I said nothing. For five years. Because saying something meant admitting I'd sold the route, meant my licenses, my name, my company. I told myself the timing was never confirmed. I told myself a lot of things." He shook his head. "This expedition—the evidence, all of it—it started as another deal, I won't lie to you. It started as leverage. But somewhere on this river it stopped being that, and I'm not going to insult you by pretending I planned the conversion."

"So why tell me now? On a hillside. With a needle in your ribs."

"Because you turned back today." He said it simply. "Against the flood window, against the schedule, against everything profitable—you saw a drowning man and you turned the boat. I've watched you for twenty years, Casper, and I have never once seen you do anything that stupid or anything decent, and today you did both at once, and I thought—if she can still be that person after what I did to her name, then the least I owe her is the truth while she can still use it."

The meadow was very quiet. Somewhere below, the blocked river pressed against its dam, patient as a creditor.

Casper found that she was angry in a way that had no heat in it—an old anger, structural, load-bearing, the anger that had held up her exile like a roof beam. Take it out and she didn't know what the building would do.

"You don't get forgiveness," she said at last. "Not today. Maybe not ever. What you get is this: you're going to carry the heaviest case over the next pass, and you're going to be the last one off every slope, and if we make it out of these gorges, you're going to stand in front of Serkhang's assembly and say what you just said to me, to the people whose fathers are dead. And then we'll see what's left of you, and what's left of us."

Marsh nodded slowly, like a man accepting a sentence he'd been drafting for himself for years. "That's fair."

"It's not fair. Fair would be Reyes coming home." She stood. "Get stitched. We move in ten minutes."

* * *

They crossed the pass in the last light, single file, Casper leading on ground her feet knew and her mind had stopped arguing with. Behind her: Ilsa, steady as ballast; Ferro, breathing in her old rhythm; Tomas with the fuel; Priya with her kit; and Marsh at the rear, exactly where she'd put him, the heaviest case on his back and his blood seeping slower now under Priya's careful line-work.

From the col, Casper looked back once. Down in the gorge, the landslide lake had begun to spill over its dam—a thin white thread across the brown, widening even as she watched, the mountain beginning to settle its accounts.

And below the spill, far down the drowned channel they'd abandoned, a light moved. Small. Deliberate. Following their wake.

"They found the cache site," Ilsa said quietly, beside her. "Or they found where it used to be."

Casper turned north, toward Serkhang, toward the last checkpoint and the nightmare route waiting beyond it, and adjusted the strap of her pack.

"Then we stop leaving a wake," she said. "We move at night now. All of us. Starting immediately."
