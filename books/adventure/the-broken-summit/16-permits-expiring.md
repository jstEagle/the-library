## 16. Permits Expiring

The radio found them at dawn, the way bad news always did—sliding into camp before the tea was hot, before anyone had finished arguing with the cold.

Casper was awake anyway. She had been awake since the small hours, lying in her bag listening to the river change its voice as the flood built behind them, and when the handheld crackled to life on the supply crate beside her tent she was already reaching for it, already knowing, in the marrow-deep way she knew weather, that nothing good traveled that frequency at that hour.

"Base to Okafor. Base to Okafor. Come in."

She knew the voice. Yusuf Karim had run the permit office in Gilgit back when her paperwork had still been worth the paper, and he had been the one—she had never forgotten, never let herself forget—to stamp the suspension that ended her. Not out of malice. Out of procedure. That was the thing about the machinery that had chewed her up: nobody in it had been cruel. They had all just been doing their jobs, and the job had been her destruction.

"Okafor here." Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in a small and bitter way. "You're a long way from your desk, Yusuf."

"Retired." A pause, static filling it like water filling a boot. "Mostly. Casper, I'm calling because someone has to, and I'd rather it was someone who—" He stopped. Started again. "The Keshar Concession window closes in seventy-two hours. Midnight, third day. After that the entire drainage reverts to closed status under emergency mineral-security provisions, and anything inside the boundary is—"

"Illegal," Casper said. "I know the word, Yusuf. I've been living in it for six years."

"That's not the worst of it."

She had known that too. She sat down slowly on the supply crate, the radio's coiled antenna dragging across her knees, and watched the light come up gray over the water. Behind her she could hear the camp stirring—Ferro's cough, the clank of Tomas rebuilding the bilge pump for the fourth time, Priya's low voice reading aloud to Ilsa from some battered paperback, the two of them having reached some unspoken truce since the night the dry boxes were opened. Her crew. Her people. She listened to them the way she listened to a river, cataloguing every sound, and she braced.

"The syndicate's legal team filed yesterday," Yusuf said. "Petition to the regional magistrate, copied to the tourism authority, the mountaineering federation, and the border police. They're asking for a criminal declaration against the entire expedition party. Unlicensed entry into a closed concession, theft of proprietary geological material, conspiracy. They've named seven people, Casper." He read them out slowly, and she heard each name land like a stone dropped down a well: Marsh. Tomas. Priya. Ferro. Ilsa Voss. Devlin's engineer, a man called Okonkwo. And last, with a kind of terrible inevitability, as if the lawyers had simply reached into the old drawer where her file had been gathering dust and pulled it out again—Okafor, Casper M.

"They cited your prior suspension," Yusuf said quietly. "As evidence of a pattern. Of you as—what was the phrase—*a habitual offender against the regulatory order of the high mountains.*"

Casper laughed. It came out wrong, a dry cracked thing with no humor in it, and she pressed her fist against her mouth until it stopped.

"Habitual offender against the regulatory order of the high mountains," she repeated. "That's poetry, Yusuf. Somebody in that firm gets paid by the adjective."

"Casper." His voice hardened, and underneath the hardness she heard something she hadn't heard from him in six years, something that might have been shame. "I'm not calling to joke. If that declaration goes through, every one of those people becomes what you are. Barred. Warranted. Unhireable in four countries. The boy—Ferro, is it? He's what, nineteen? Twenty? They will take his entire life before he's old enough to rent a vehicle in his own name."

She looked down at her hands. They were steady now. They had stopped shaking somewhere in the portage, on the landslide scar, when her body had remembered routes her mind had sworn off, and she had understood—standing on rock that still bore the marks of the rockfall, six years old and never repaired, never even investigated—that the shaking had never been fear. It had been the truth trying to get out.

"I hear you," she said.

"Do you? Because I don't think you do." Yusuf exhaled, and the static breathed with him. "There's a way out. That's why I'm calling. The syndicate's lawyers left the door open—deliberately, of course, they always do. Any member of the party who turns over the disputed materials and signs a statement disavowing the expedition's purpose gets named as a coerced party instead of a conspirator. Immunity. Clean record. They're offering it especially to you, Casper. *Okafor's cooperation would be viewed favorably in light of her history.*" He paused. "They want you to be the one who breaks. You're the famous one. You're the story. If you fold, the whole expedition becomes *disgraced guide leads gullible crew into crime*, and nobody asks any more questions about what's in those boxes."

The gray light had turned to silver while he talked. Down at the water's edge, Ilsa stood with her instruments, taking readings she no longer pretended were about hydrology, her silhouette thin and straight against the brown flood. Six years hunting her brother's death. Casper thought about what those boxes weighed—not in kilograms, in everything else.

"And if nobody folds?" she asked. "If we run the evidence out before midnight, third day?"

"Then you're smuggling," Yusuf said. "It doesn't matter that it's true. It doesn't matter that it would save the valley. You'll have moved proprietary material across a closed boundary without a valid permit, and the permit is the only language the law speaks. You'll be guilty of the exact crime they say you are. The truth will come out eventually—maybe years, maybe never—but *you* will be finished. All of you. Permanently." He let that sit. "There's no version of this where you win, Casper. There's only versions where you decide what losing costs."

The radio hissed. Somewhere behind her, Ferro laughed at something Tomas said, a bright unguarded sound, and it went through her like a fishhook.

"Seventy-two hours," she said.

"Seventy-two. And Casper—" A long pause. When he spoke again his voice was lower, stripped of officialdom. "I stamped your suspension because the file was complete and my job was stamps. I've had six years to wonder what was in the parts of the file that weren't. If you come out of there with what I think you're carrying—" He didn't finish it. "Base out."

The frequency dissolved into static, and Casper sat holding the dead radio against her chest for a long time, watching the river run.

* * *

She called the crew together at the fire, because they deserved to hear it from her and not from the panic that would otherwise travel camp to camp like a spark in dry grass.

She told them all of it. The window. The declaration. The seven names. The offer of immunity, and the shape of the trap underneath it. She watched their faces as she spoke, and it was Priya she watched hardest—the medic who had stitched Marsh's back on the portage, who had held the crew's morale in her two capable hands through flood and betrayal and the long gray grind of the gorge. Priya had a daughter too. Priya had a clinic in Skardu that ran on her expedition wages. Casper knew what a criminal declaration would do to a medic's license, and she knew Priya knew it, and she made herself say the names plainly, all seven, including the girl's.

When she finished, nobody spoke for a while. The fire cracked. The river roared its one long syllable.

"So," Tomas said at last. He was turning a wrench over in his hands, not using it, just holding it the way a man holds something familiar when the unfamiliar has gotten too big. "If we quit. Sign their paper, hand over the boxes. We're clean. We go home."

"You go home clean," Casper said. "That's the deal."

"And the water stays poisoned." He said it without drama, almost mildly, the way he diagnosed an engine fault. "And the village downstream keeps drinking it. And the next expedition that comes asking questions gets the same treatment we got—only next time the syndicate will know to check the dry boxes on day one." He set the wrench down. "I've got a brother in the valley below the mine, Casper. I signed on for money. I'm not saying I didn't. But I've been listening to Ilsa for two weeks, and I've done the arithmetic, and I can't unknow it."

"I didn't ask you to—"

"No," Tomas said. "You didn't. That's the point." He looked around at the others. "She never asked any of us to stay. She's telling us the door's open. I'm saying I'm not walking through it."

Ferro spoke up fast, too fast, the way the young did when they were terrified and determined to be brave about it. "I'm in. Obviously. I mean—my whole life I wanted to be on a crew that mattered. This is—" He caught Priya's eye and faltered. "I'm in," he said again, quieter.

Priya said nothing for a long moment. She was looking at the fire, and Casper saw her doing the calculation she herself had done at midnight six years ago and again three weeks ago and again every single night since: the ledger of what safety cost, written in other people's blood.

"If I sign," Priya said slowly, "I keep my license. I keep the clinic. I keep my daughter's school." She looked up. "And every morning for the rest of my life I pour water for my patients and I know where it comes from, upstream of me, and what's in it, and that I could have—" She stopped. Shook her head. "No. I'm sorry. I can't. I've been the person who kept her hands clean my whole career. I'm done with it."

Ilsa hadn't spoken. She sat slightly apart, as she always did, her back against a boulder, and when Casper's eyes found her she was already looking at Casper with that still, level attention that had unnerved her from the first day and that she had come, without ever deciding to, to lean on like a handrail in the dark.

"They gave me the same offer six years ago," Ilsa said. "After the collapse. Sign the settlement, accept the compensation, agree the incident was geological. My parents signed. I understand why—they had nothing left, and it was that or nothing." Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed a sentence for six years and was only now saying it aloud. "I didn't sign. I became a hydrologist instead. Do you know what it's like to spend six years becoming the one person in the room who can read the water, so that when the day came, nobody could tell you the water lied?" She looked at the dry boxes, lashed under the tarp. "That day is in seventy-two hours. I'm not signing anything."

Casper nodded. Her throat was too tight for words, so she didn't try. She looked around the fire—Tomas with his brother in the valley, Ferro with his whole unwritten life, Priya with her clinic and her clean hands and her daughter, Ilsa with her six years—and she felt something swell in her chest that was terrible and warm at once, because they had all just chosen to fall, and they had chosen it looking at her.

"Understand what you're choosing," she said. "All of you. I've lived it. The blacklist isn't a metaphor. It's a door closing in every office on four mountain ranges. It's my daughter growing up on the other side of it. There is no ceremony at the end where someone hands you back your name. You don't get the state's forgiveness for doing the right thing. The state isn't in the forgiveness business. It's in the permit business, and our permit expires at midnight in three days, and everything after that is us, alone, against the paper."

"We know," Priya said gently.

"You don't. You can't, until you're in it. That's not a criticism. It's just—" Casper stopped, and heard her own voice change, the guide's command voice giving way to something rawer. "I spent six years thinking the worst thing that could happen to me was what they did to my name. Then I found out there was something worse. It was knowing the truth and not saying it. It was running." She looked at the fire. "I'm not running twice."

* * *

Night came down over the camp, and with it the argument she had been dreading—the one with herself, conducted in the old familiar courtroom behind her ribs where she was plaintiff, defendant, and the entire exhausted jury.

She climbed above the camp to the shelf of rock where she did her best thinking, and she sat with her knees drawn up and looked at the river, and she laid the trap out piece by piece the way Tomas would lay out an engine's parts on a tarp, because a trap you can see whole is a trap you might survive.

The first truth: if they delivered the evidence legally, there would have to be a legal channel, and every legal channel belonged to the syndicate. That was the elegance of it, the real architecture of the thing. The permit system wasn't a wall around the mountain. It was a filter, and the syndicate owned the filter. Evidence that arrived through proper channels arrived through *their* channels—logged, delayed, reviewed by their consultants, contested by their lawyers, buried under appeals until the samples degraded and the witnesses scattered and the villages drank another decade of poison. Legality was not the opposite of the trap. Legality *was* the trap.

The second truth: if they delivered it illegally, the evidence might still be dismissed—tainted, stolen, inadmissible. Except. Except that evidence didn't only live in courtrooms anymore. Except that Ilsa had photographed every page, every stamp, every impossible date, and there were copies in three places Casper knew of and probably four she didn't. Except that the villages were downstream, and villages had voices now, and the syndicate's lawyers could declare seven people criminals but they could not declare four hundred herders and farmers and schoolchildren criminals for reading a document aloud in their own assembly hall. She thought of Serkhang. She thought of the old woman who had spat at her feet, and the boy with his father's question, and she thought: *that's where it goes. Not to the magistrate. To them.*

The third truth was the one she turned over longest, the one with the hook in it. Her daughter. Amara, fourteen now, in a good school in Kathmandu, paid for by the debt Casper had been bleeding to service—the debt the collector wanted settled in sixty days, the debt that had put her on Marsh's boat in the first place. A criminal declaration wouldn't just bar her from the mountains. It would follow her into every bank account, every contract, every border crossing. It would make the debt unpayable and the tuition impossible and the distance between her and her daughter permanent, geological, a rift valley.

*Deliver it illegally and stay blacklisted forever. Or quit and let it burn.*

Yusuf had said it like a choice. But sitting on the cold rock with the flood roaring below her, Casper finally saw it clearly, and seeing it made her almost laugh at the audacity of it: it wasn't a choice at all. It had never been a choice. The syndicate had built the two doors themselves—the shameful door and the doomed door—and they had built them because as long as she believed those were the only two doors, she would stand in the hallway forever, paralyzed, and paralyzed people signed things. Paralyzed people took immunity. Paralyzed people handed over boxes and went home and let the water stay poisoned and told themselves it had been the sensible thing.

But there was a third door. There always had been. It was the one she had walked through six years ago without knowing it—the door that led outside the system entirely, into the country of the blacklisted, where she had been living all along. And the thing about that country, she realized, the thing nobody told you, was that it had a population. Tomas's brother. Priya's patients. Ilsa's dead brother and the parents who had signed. The old woman in Serkhang. The boy. Every person the permit system had been built to filter out. It wasn't exile. It had never been exile.

It was a constituency.

Casper sat with that word, turning it over, feeling its weight. She had spent six years thinking of herself as a woman who had lost everything—license, name, daughter, mountains. But she had a boat, and a crew that had just chosen to fall with her, and evidence that could not be un-photographed, and a route in her body that no syndicate lawyer could revoke, and seventy-two hours.

The clouds over the western ridgeline had cleared at sunset, and the stars came out hard and cold, the way they did at altitude, close enough to hurt. Somewhere below, Ilsa was standing the first watch. Somewhere downstream, the syndicate's second boat was out there in the dark, and agents at the checkpoint, and lawyers filing papers in offices that smelled of carpet. Let them file. Paper was their whole world—paper, and stamps, and the filter. But paper, Casper thought, remembering the forged dates on Marsh's beautiful concession, remembering how recently and easily the syndicate itself had made paper lie, was only as strong as the truth it was standing on. And theirs was standing on poisoned water.

She got up. Her knees cracked. She was forty-one years old and about to become, officially, a habitual offender against the regulatory order of the high mountains, and she found—to her own surprise, a deep and quiet surprise—that she was not afraid.

She climbed down to camp, and found Ilsa at the water's edge with the radio in her lap, and Ilsa looked up and read her face the way she read currents, all at once and completely.

"You've decided," Ilsa said.

"We're going to Serkhang," Casper said. "Not the checkpoint. Not the magistrate. The assembly hall. We deliver it to the people it happened to, and we let them do what they want with it, and if the syndicate wants to declare four hundred villagers criminals, they can stand in that square and try." She paused. "There's one problem."

Ilsa waited.

"The only route to Serkhang that beats the flood and the second boat and the checkpoint," Casper said, "is the gorge. The one from my nightmares. The one nobody has ever run twice." She looked upstream, where the water was already climbing the rocks like something waking up hungry. "And we'd have to run it tomorrow. At peak flood. With a boat Tomas patched four times."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Then she stood, and slung the radio over her shoulder, and there was something in her face that Casper had seen only once before—on the portage, at the top of the scar, when the impossible route had turned out to be possible.

"Then we'd better wake Tomas," Ilsa said.
