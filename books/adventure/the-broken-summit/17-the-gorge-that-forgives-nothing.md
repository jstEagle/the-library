## 17. The Gorge That Forgives Nothing

Ilsa had learned to read water the way other people learned to read faces—slowly, at first out of necessity, then out of obsession. Her brother had been the same. Arun could stand on a bridge over any river in the Karakoram and tell you what it had eaten that morning, what it was thinking about eating next. She'd inherited half his gift and all of his stubbornness, and standing now at the bow of Casper Okafor's patched and scarred boat, watching the entrance to the final gorge come apart in the rain, she understood for the first time why the two of them had never met before this year.

The water would not have let them meet. The water kept them apart the way it kept everything—silently, patiently, until it didn't.

The gorge mouth was a black seam in the cliff wall, maybe sixty meters wide where it should have been two hundred. Peak flood. Every tributary above them had been feeding it for three days of monsoon, and the Indus's forgotten cousin ran brown and muscular through the gap, folding over itself in standing waves taller than Tomas, the foam blowing off their crests sideways in the wind. It did not look like water anymore. It looked like something being poured.

"Two hundred meters to the entry line," Ferro called from midship, one hand locked on the cargo rail, the other shielding his eyes against the rain. "Maybe less. Current's pushing us faster than the gauge."

"I can see the line," Casper said.

Her voice was flat. Not calm—Ilsa knew her well enough now to know the difference—but flattened, pressed down under weight, the way snowpack sounds when you step on it just before it holds or doesn't. Casper stood at the tiller with her whole body, boots braced wide, rain sheeting off her hood, and she was looking at the gorge the way Ilsa imagined she looked at everything that had ever hurt her: directly, without blinking, with an accounting running behind her eyes.

Behind them, four hundred meters back and closing, came the syndicate's boat.

* * *

They had named it the Hyena among themselves, though its real name was painted on the hull in clean corporate lettering: *Meridian Resource Logistics, Unit 7*. Forty feet of aluminum and outboard horsepower, six men aboard, radios crackling with lawyers' instructions translated into threats. It had found them at dawn yesterday when Marsh's forgery finally tripped whatever tripwire the syndicate kept in the permit offices, and it had been shadowing them since—not attacking, not yet, because the gorge ahead was as lethal to them as to anyone, but herding, the way hyenas herd, waiting for the moment exhaustion or fear drove the prey somewhere convenient.

Marsh stood amidships with his arms wrapped around the sample cases, all eleven of them, roped into a pyramid under tarps. His face had healed badly from the beating Unit 7's men had given him two nights ago—the eye still swollen to a slit, the lip split in a way that made him whistle faintly when he talked—but he hadn't complained once since, and Ilsa had begun, cautiously, against every instinct of six years, to revise her file on him.

"They'll run it," Marsh said. "You know they'll run it. They've got a pilot who's done this gorge twice."

"Then they're idiots," Priya said, cinching a strap across the medical kit. "Nobody's done this gorge at this level. Nobody's *seen* this gorge at this level."

"Casper has."

Everyone went quiet at that. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

Casper didn't turn around. "Once," she said. "Eleven years ago. At half this flow. And I swore on a dead man's name I'd never run it again." She adjusted the tiller a degree, reading some shift in the current that Ilsa couldn't see yet but would learn to see in the next ninety seconds if she wanted to live. "Swearing things is how I got here. So we'll see."

* * *

The truth about Ilsa Vik—that is, the truth beneath the hydrologist's credentials and the forged survey contract and the careful, unremarkable way she dressed—was that she had spent six years becoming an instrument. When Arun died in the Keshar collapse, buried along with forty-one others under a mountainside that the mine's own internal reports had predicted, she had grieved like anyone grieves, badly and privately, and then she had done what he would have done: she had started taking measurements.

Six years of measurements. Water tables. Core samples. Tailings seepage rates. The slow poison map of an entire watershed, drawn in her own hand, hidden in dry boxes, carried now in a leaking boat toward the only people stubborn enough to get it out alive. She had hired herself to Devlin Marsh because he had permits, real-seeming ones; she had tested Casper Okafor for weeks because she needed to know whether the famous guide was a collaborator, a victim, or something else. She had photographed the forged dates on the concession paperwork alone, in the dark of the equipment tent, and told no one, because knowledge shared too early dies young.

She had told Casper about Arun in a sea cave while the monsoon tried to drown them both, and Casper had listened without flinching and then said, *Then we finish it,* as simply as if she were agreeing on where to pitch a tarp. Ilsa had loved people before. She had never before been *chosen by* someone, deliberately, in full knowledge of what choosing cost. She was still learning how to carry that.

Now she clipped herself to the bow rail and watched the gorge eat the light.

* * *

"Entry line," Ferro shouted. "Thirty meters!"

What happened next happened in pieces, and Ilsa would spend the rest of her life reassembling them.

Casper brought the boat around—not straight at the seam, which was suicide, but at an angle upstream of it, into the eddy fence that curled off the gorge's left shoulder. The current grabbed them like a hand closing. The world became noise: the roar of the pour-over, the crack of the hull flexing, Tomas screaming engine RPMs that made no difference because no engine made a difference here, only angle and commitment and the water's terrible arithmetic.

They went in.

The first wave took the bow to the sky. Ilsa saw the whole gorge open beneath her for one suspended second—a brown canyon of chaos, holes and haystacks and a mid-channel rock shaped like a broken tooth with the river detonating against it—and then the wave set them down so hard her teeth cracked together and the cold hit her like a wall of fists. Spray turned to rain turned to spray. The boat surfed, yawed, and Casper's hands moved on the tiller in small, vicious corrections, each one a sentence in a language only she spoke.

"Tooth!" Ferro bellowed, pointing.

"I see it," Casper said, and threaded them past it with perhaps two meters on either side, close enough that Ilsa could have touched the wet black stone, could see the veins of quartz in it flashing as they passed.

And behind them—she heard it before she understood it—a sound like the end of the world clearing its throat.

* * *

Unit 7 had followed them in.

Later, everyone agreed the syndicate pilot must have believed the gorge was runnable because Casper had run it, must have mistaken skill for safety, must have thought the woman they'd been hunting was showing them a door instead of a cliff. Whatever he believed lasted eleven seconds. Ilsa, twisted around in the stern with the sample cases lashed at her back, saw the big aluminum boat hit the second wave train broadside, saw it stand on its side like a door swinging open, saw six men and forty feet of corporate metal become debris.

The hull cartwheeled. Once. The sound it made hitting the tooth rock was one she would hear in dreams for years—a deep, structural boom, a whale's death knell—and then the river had it, and the river did what peak-flood rivers do to anything that isn't water: it pulled it down and chewed.

Men came up. That was the thing Ilsa hadn't been prepared for, in all her years of studying disasters on paper. The river didn't kill cleanly. It spat them back up—two of them, three, figures in orange drysuits popping to the surface like corks, arms windmilling, heads barely clearing the standing waves, swept downstream in the same current that carried Casper's boat, fifty meters back and falling further behind with every second.

"One's got a PFD," Ferro shouted. "Other one's—other one's swimming, he's not—" 

"I see them," Casper said.

And then Ilsa watched her do the thing that rearranged everything Ilsa believed about her.

Casper looked downstream. Ilsa saw her look—saw her head turn, saw her eyes make the calculation every guide on every river makes a thousand times: distance, drift, eddy options, exit lines, the odds. And Ilsa saw the exact moment the calculation returned its answer, because Casper's jaw tightened and her shoulders dropped half an inch and something in her face closed like a book.

The answer was no. There was no eddy within reach. Turning back meant giving up the momentum that was keeping them alive, means surfing back upstream into the worst water in the gorge, with the evidence of six years and five deaths lashed to their deck, with the whole expedition balanced on a knife-edge of speed. Every instinct Casper Okafor had—the instincts that had kept her alive through Nanda Kot and exile and eleven years of rivers nobody else would run—every instinct said *keep going*. The dead men were the men who had beaten Marsh bloody, who had chased them like animals, who worked for the company that killed Arun. The river was offering her a gift.

"No," Casper said aloud.

It wasn't clear, in that roar, whether she was saying it to the river or to herself.

Then she put the tiller over and turned the boat around.

* * *

"Tomas! Hard reverse on my mark—we ride the tail eddy behind the tooth, ferry back to the right wall, there's a pocket behind the split boulder—" She was shouting the route as she built it, inventing it in real time, and Ilsa realized with a strange, floating clarity that this was what Casper actually was. Not the blacklisted guide. Not the woman who let the mountain eat a father. This: a person who, at the moment of maximum pressure, with every reason and every instinct pointing one way, turned the other way because turning away was not who she had decided to be. Eleven years ago she might have frozen. Eleven years ago, on Nanda Kot, maybe she had—that old shame, that old paralysis. But the woman at the tiller now had been carved by those years into something the blacklist had never managed to name.

"You'll lose the line," Marsh shouted. "We'll never get it back—we'll flush through the lower gorge blind—"

"Then we go through blind," Casper said. "Ferro, throw bag on the bow. Priya, board ready. Ilsa—"

She looked at Ilsa, and the rain ran down between them, and for one second, in the middle of the loudest place on earth, there was silence.

"Ilsa. Hold the cases. Whatever happens, hold the cases."

"I know," Ilsa said. "Go get them."

* * *

The maneuver was impossible and she almost made it look routine. Tomas reversed the engine to its screaming limit; the boat stalled in the boil behind the tooth rock, slewing, shipping water over the gunwales in green buckets; and Casper ferried them across the current's shoulder with the finesse of a surgeon working inside a hurricane. The first swimmer—the one with the life vest—came down the wave train face-first, and Ferro's throw bag uncoiled across the water like something alive, and the man caught it, and for ten endless seconds the whole expedition hung on a rope and a nineteen-year-old's grip while the river tried to take him back.

Priya hauled him over the gunwales. He was maybe thirty, gray-faced, vomiting river water, and the first thing he did when he could speak was say, "Why," and nobody answered him, because there wasn't time.

The second man had no vest. He surfaced twice more, thirty meters down, arms slapping, going under longer each time, and the pocket eddy behind the split boulder was above him now, unreachable, and Ilsa saw Casper see it—saw the mathematics fail, saw the thing enter her face that enters the faces of people who have guided long enough to know that you cannot save everyone, that the river forgives nothing, that this is the toll and always has been.

Casper tied a line around her own waist.

"No," Priya said. "Absolutely not—"

"He's twenty meters out. I'm a stronger swimmer than any rope we've got." She was already clipping in, already kicking off her boots. "Tomas, hold us in the pocket. If I go past the boulder, cut me loose and don't be stupid about following."

"Casper—"

"That's an order. I'm still, technically, the guide."

She went over the side before anyone could argue, into water that could crumble a cliff, and Ilsa held the sample cases and watched the woman she loved swim into the brown chaos after a man who had come to destroy them, and understood, finally and completely, who Casper Okafor was. Not the sum of her guilt. Not her record, her licenses, her blacklist, her ledger of debts. A person is what they do when the doing costs everything and no one would ever know if they chose otherwise. The river would know. Ilsa would know. That was all.

The rope paid out. Ten meters. Fifteen. The boat strained against the eddy's grip like a hooked fish. Then the line went taut, jerked once, twice—dragging weight—and Ferro and Tomas hauled, and Priya hauled, and Marsh hauled with his ruined eye streaming, and they brought them both in: Casper first, coughing, alive, furious with cold; then the security man, blue-lipped, breathing, alive, delivered onto the deck of the boat he'd been hired to stop like a piece of salvage the river had decided, today, to give back.

The fifth man they never found. The river kept him. The river keeps no records, but it keeps everything else.

* * *

They flushed the lower gorge blind, exactly as Marsh had predicted, and survived it—barely, wildly, on Casper's shouted readings and Tomas's engine and a hull that took the rocks like punches and kept its shape. Two kilometers down, where the gorge opened its throat and the water spread and gentled into long gray rollers, they finally drifted in exhausted silence, bailing, shivering, six strangers and two enemies sharing one small floating country.

One of the rescued men sat wrapped in Priya's emergency blanket, staring at Casper with an expression Ilsa recognized because she'd worn it herself, weeks ago, in a tea house by the river: the expression of a man whose file on another human being had just burned down to ash and blank paper.

"You turned around," he said hoarsely. "For us. You turned *around*."

Casper wrung out her sleeve and didn't look at him. "Don't make it a story," she said. "It was just the fastest way to keep my crew from having to watch a man drown."

But Ilsa caught her eye across the boat, and held it, and smiled—small, soaked, certain—and Casper, after a moment, almost smiled back.

Then Ferro, scanning the banks with Ferro's luck, went rigid and pointed downstream, to where the river bent around a bluff of pine.

Smoke was rising from the checkpoint at the gorge's mouth. Three vehicles. Men deploying along the bank with rifles slung, setting up barriers across the only landing beach for twenty kilometers.

The permit window closed in seventy-two hours, and the syndicate had finally stopped chasing them.

They had arrived first.
