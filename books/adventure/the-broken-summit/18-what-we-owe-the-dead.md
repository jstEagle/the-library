## 18. What We Owe the Dead

The two security men sat on the shingle like drowned men who hadn't yet been told they'd died. They had stripped off their outer layers and hung them on rocks to steam in the thin sun, and they huddled in their thermals with their hands cupped around a fire that Tomas had built without being asked—built for them, which was the part that seemed to confuse them most.

The taller one was called Raza. Casper knew this because Ferro had demanded names before anyone was allowed near the food stores, and the man had given it up with a kind of exhausted honesty, the way you surrender a weapon when your arms are too numb to hold it anymore. The shorter one hadn't spoken at all since Casper hauled him over the gunwale by his collar, coughing out half the gorge. He just stared at the river as if it might come back for him.

They were syndicate men. Their boat was syndicate money. Two days ago they had been shadowing the crew with orders nobody had said aloud, and Marsh had gone to negotiate alone and come back bleeding from a split lip and something worse underneath, a bruise blooming along his ribs like ink dropped in water. And now here they all were, sharing a fire on a gravel bar below the last gorge, because the river did not care whose payroll you were on when it decided to eat you.

Casper stood apart from the circle, arms crossed, watching the water. It ran high and brown-green with snowmelt and rain, churning through the boulder garden downstream with a sound like continuous applause from an audience that wanted someone hurt.

"You could have let us drown," Raza said. He said it to the fire more than to her.

"Yes," Casper said.

He waited for her to say more. She didn't. Priya moved past him with the tea kettle and poured two tin cups without ceremony, pressing one into each man's hands. Her face held nothing readable—she was very good at that—but Casper saw how carefully she kept her distance, the way she'd keep it from a snake that had been defanged but not declawed.

"The company doesn't know we're alive," Raza tried again. "When we don't report in—"

"They'll assume we killed you," Casper finished. "Or the river did. Either way, convenient."

His jaw worked. The silent one finally spoke, voice scraped raw: "We're not going back."

Casper turned and looked at him properly for the first time. He was maybe thirty, with the soft hands of a man hired for intimidation rather than labor, and there was something in his face she recognized—the look of a person who has just understood, all at once, what he is actually working for. She'd seen it on clients at altitude. She'd seen it in mirrors.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Danil."

"Danil. You can sit at our fire tonight. Tomorrow you can help carry. That's the whole arrangement." She let that land. "You're not crew. You're not forgiven. You're people who needed pulling out of the water, and I pulled you out, and now you owe the water a debt, not me. Understood?"

Neither of them answered, but neither of them argued, and in the Karakoram that was as good as a signature.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn and walked the boats upriver through the shallows where the channel braided into threads too thin to run. By midmorning the valley narrowed and changed character. Casper felt it before she could name it—a wrongness in the banks, a slickness to the stones, a smell beneath the wet-rock mineral smell of the gorge. Sulfur, faintly. Something metallic and old, like blood in a closed room.

Ilsa came alongside her, carrying her instrument case the way other people carried infants. "Half a kilometer," she said quietly. "Maybe less."

The mine's outflow announced itself before they saw it. A side channel entered the main river from a cut in the eastern wall, and where the two waters met there was a seam as sharp as if drawn with a ruler: on one side the living green-brown of the glacier-fed Indus tributary, on the other a pale, opaque gray-white, thick as milk of magnesia, running sluggish and strange. Where the seam dissolved downstream, the combined current carried a scum of fine sediment that clung to the rocks in layers, like the rings of a tree that recorded only sickness.

Nobody spoke. Even Ferro, who filled silence the way other men filled pockets, went quiet.

They grounded the boats and climbed the bank toward the outflow cut, Marsh leading despite his ribs, moving with the careful stiffness of a man who had learned overnight exactly how much pain a body could carry and still walk. The path switchbacked up through scrub willow, and then the trees simply stopped—not thinned, stopped—and the ground turned to a gray paste that sucked at their boots. Above them, terraced into the mountainside, the mine opened its wounds.

Casper had seen mines before. She'd seen open pits in Peru so vast they swallowed weather, seen shafts in Nepal that went into the dark like throats. This was smaller than those, meaner—a scar of tailings terraces, settling ponds the color of old teeth, a processing shed with its roof half-collapsed under snowload no one had bothered to clear. But it was the ponds that made Priya make a small sound behind her hand. They were not gray. They were blue. An impossible, beautiful, poisonous blue, like glacial meltwater photographed in a magazine, and the color came from chemistry, not depth.

"That's the watershed," Ilsa said. Her voice was flat in the way Casper had learned meant the opposite of calm. "That's the headwater. Everything in those ponds seeps. Through the tailings, through the fracture zones—it's karstic limestone up there, it's Swiss cheese—and every drop comes back out somewhere. Springs. Seeps. The village channels above Serkhang. The grazing slopes." She lifted her instrument case. "Six years I've been modeling this from samples bought on the black market, from satellite spectra, from dead men's notes. And it's exactly—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "It's worse than I modeled."

Marsh stood a little apart, looking at the processing shed, and Casper watched him do arithmetic with his face. "The rockfall," he said. "At Nanda Kot's lower flank. The report blamed freeze-thaw. Natural cycle."

"Your report," Casper said.

"My report." He didn't flinch from it. "Commissioned by the concession holders. I sampled where they told me to sample. I wrote what the data allowed and left out what it didn't." He turned to face her fully, and up here in the poisoned light his age showed in every line of him. "The blasting runs day and night during the season. You've heard it—don't tell me you haven't, these last two days, that low knocking under the wind. Shock waves travel through saturated rock differently than dry. The flank above the route was drainage-cut, Casper. Water everywhere, rock fractured everywhere, and charges going off three times a night. What came down on your rope team wasn't weather. It was a schedule."

The words went into her like cold water into a crack, finding every fissure she'd spent six years pretending was solid. She thought of the sound the mountain had made—that deep, wrong exhalation, the slope letting go—and how she had replayed it ten thousand times searching for her own error, her own sin, and found only weather, only chance, only the mountain being a mountain. She had built her whole exile on that foundation. *It was my fault. I read the route wrong. I led them into it.* And the foundation had been poured by men with a production quota.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger, and then she looked away from them, up the valley, toward the mine, and the shaking slowed.

* * *

They ate a cold lunch on the rocks above the confluence because no one would drink the filtered water once Tomas tested it downstream of the seam and the test strip came back the color of a bruise. They drank tea boiled from snow carried down from the col instead, and the absurdity of it—surrounded by rivers, dying of thirst—made Ferro laugh once, high and unhappy, before he caught himself.

It was Priya who said it first, quietly, to her tea: "My grandmother's village lost its well to a tannery when I was small. Everyone got sick for a year. Two children died. And the company paid a fine and the well stayed dead and everyone pretended it was fate." She looked up. "Fate is very profitable for some people."

Casper set down her cup. She looked at her crew—Tomas with his grease-blackened hands folded still, which was how he prayed; Priya with her grandmother's ghosts; Ferro trying to be a man about it and mostly managing; Ilsa with her brother's name locked behind her teeth for six years; Marsh, broken open and not yet sure whether that was ruin or relief; Danil and Raza sitting at the edge of the circle like guests at a funeral they'd been paid to disrupt and now couldn't leave.

And she heard herself say: "Say their names."

The circle went still.

"When I was guiding," Casper said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, "we had a ritual. Before a summit push. You said the names of everyone climbing with you, out loud, so that if something happened, the mountain would know they weren't anonymous. That they were loved by specific people with specific faces." She stood. Her legs felt unsteady and she let them. "The syndicate counts on anonymity. Dead villagers are statistics. Dead clients are regrettable accidents. Dead hydrologists are security incidents. As long as nobody says the names, the ledger stays clean." She looked at the gray water running its poisoned seam into the living river. "So we say the names."

She started with her own dead, because someone had to go first and it was hers to carry.

"Marcus Adeyemi," she said. "Forty-one years old. Client. Father of two girls. He climbed strong and laughed at bad jokes and he trusted me with his life, and the mountain took him on the lower flank of Nanda Kot in a rockfall that was not weather and not fate and not my error, whatever I have spent six years telling myself at three in the morning. Marcus Adeyemi." Her throat closed and she forced the next word through it. "Remembered."

"Remembered," Priya said softly, and then Tomas, and then Ferro, and then Ilsa, steady and clear, and even Marsh, hoarse, and after a moment Danil and Raza too, uncertain but present, the word passing over the gravel like a stone skipping water.

Ilsa stood next. She opened her instrument case and took out—not an instrument—a photograph, creased white at the folds, of a young man with a serious face and a hydrologist's field vest, standing in front of a range of peaks.

"Mikael Voss," she said. "My younger brother. Twenty-nine years old. He came to this valley to test water because he believed data was a form of care—that measuring something honestly was how you loved it. He sent me his last samples six weeks before he died in the collapse at the adit. The company called it subsidence. Unrelated to operations." Her voice did not break, but it went somewhere very quiet. "He was right about everything. He just wasn't loud enough. Mikael Voss. Remembered."

"Remembered," said the circle, and this time no one hesitated.

Then Casper turned toward the east, toward Serkhang, invisible beyond the ridge but present in every grain of this valley. "The fathers of Serkhang," she said. "Dorje Nurbo, who grazed the high pasture and whose goats began bearing dead kids four seasons ago. Tashi Wangchuk, who walked to the provincial office nine times with jars of his spring water and was told nine times that the water met standards. Old Amne Dolker's husband, whose name I never learned, who died of what the clinic called stomach illness, twice, in the same house, in the same year." Her voice steadied as she went, gathering force like water finding its channel. "The men and women of this valley who drank what flowed down from that blue and were told it was clean, who buried their animals and then their parents and were told it was luck, who are being told right now, today, that the mountain is sick the way mountains get sick—naturally, unfortunately, no one's fault."

She looked at the seam in the river, the gray thread poisoning the green, and something in her chest that had been grief for six years turned over and became something else. Not lighter. Denser. Usable.

"We owe them," she said. "Not guilt—I'm done paying interest on guilt. We owe them the truth, carried all the way home. Every sample, every page, every photograph in those dry boxes goes to Serkhang and gets read out loud where the whole valley can hear it, in their language, before their own assembly, before any court or ministry or journalist gets to translate it into something safe." She looked around the circle, meeting each pair of eyes. "That's the job now. Not Marsh's job. Not the contract. Ours."

Marsh opened his mouth—to object, to plan, Casper didn't know—but Ilsa spoke first.

"I've been carrying this alone for six years," she said, "because I thought finishing it alone was the only way to honor him. I was wrong. It takes all of you." She put the photograph back in the case with both hands, gently, the way you close a coffin. "All of us."

Tomas nodded slowly, the way he approved engine repairs. Priya reached over and squeezed Ferro's shoulder before the boy could say something he'd regret—or something true, which amounted to the same thing at his age. Even Danil shifted forward slightly, and Raza said, quietly, "We know the checkpoint rotations. If you're doing this, you'll need that."

Casper almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

They struck camp as the afternoon light went long and copper, and the valley's wrongness followed them down to the boats. But as Casper took the tiller and the current took the hull, she felt different in her own body—lighter in one place, heavier in another, the way a climber feels after leaving behind gear that was never going to save them anyway.

Ahead, the river bent north toward the last checkpoint, and somewhere beyond it, the syndicate's lawyers were already drafting the paperwork that would make them all criminals. Somewhere behind, the blue ponds gleamed in the dusk, patient as poison.

Ilsa crouched beside her at the stern, checking the seal on the dry boxes one more time, and said, without looking up:

"Serkhang's assembly meets at moonrise. If we make the upper trail by tomorrow night, we'll arrive while they're gathered."

Casper calculated the water, the portage, the hours. Fourteen, maybe fifteen, if nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong, she thought. That was the joke she'd been telling herself since Nanda Kot.

"Then we row," she said, and put the tiller over.
