## 19. Deliverance, Illegally

The permit expired at dawn in three days, and the syndicate knew it.

Casper stood on the shingle below the sea cave and watched the checkpoint through Ilsa's field glasses: a steel-hulled patrol boat riding at anchor where the gorge narrowed to a throat, two more men on the cliffside trail above it with binoculars of their own. They weren't hiding. That was the message. *We see you. We know what you carry. Come to us or don't come at all.*

Behind her, the camp smelled of wet canvas and Tomas's soldering iron. The patched boat sat half in the water like an old dog that had learned not to trust its legs. Inside the dry boxes, wrapped in oilcloth and Priya's spare gauze, lay six years of dead men's evidence—core samples laced with arsenic, ledgers with dates that hadn't happened yet, photographs of a watershed turning brown from the source down.

"They'll search everything," Ferro said beside her. He'd stopped asking for cigarettes weeks ago; now he asked questions instead, which was worse. "Every box. Every pocket."

"They won't search the river," Casper said.

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But once it was out, it hung there in the monsoon air, fat and inevitable as the clouds stacking over the ridgeline, and she felt the old geography of her nightmares unfold beneath her feet like a map she'd burned and memorized anyway.

*The Khumbak Gorge.* Eleven kilometers of water that dropped four hundred meters through rock so narrow the sun reached the bottom for twenty minutes a day. She'd run it once, seven years ago, with a client named Daniel Osei who had laughed at the wrong moment and died at the right one—the rockfall that wasn't her fault, the rockfall that was never investigated because the mine upstream paid for the silence. She'd sworn on his grave, on the shingle where they'd buried what the river gave back, that she would never run it again.

The river had kept no records. She had kept all of them.

* * *

She laid it out that night on the floor of the cave, using river stones for boats and a wet boot print for the gorge. Marsh sat propped against the wall, his ribs strapped where the syndicate's negotiators had worked him over, listening the way a man listens when he knows he's run out of lies worth telling.

"Two routes," she said. "One is real. One is bait."

Ilsa crouched at the edge of the map, her instruments packed, her face doing what it always did—which was nothing, until you learned to read the small weather of her eyes. "You're thinking of Khumbak."

"I'm thinking of what they're thinking." Casper moved a stone downstream, past the checkpoint throat. "They've got the main channel locked. They expect us to try a portage around the cliffs—they know about the landslide scar, their own surveyors flagged it three years ago. So we let them expect it. We make noise up there. Torches, gear noise, Tomas hammering on empty boxes. Everything we own goes overland, loud as a wedding procession."

"And the real cargo?" Priya asked quietly.

"Also overland." Casper looked at Ilsa. "You. Copies of everything, split across your jacket and my old med kit, on foot through the high pasture route to Serkhang. Two days' hard walking. You know the way—Ilsa drew it for me the first week, back when she still pretended she was just a hydrologist."

"I could go," Ferro said, too fast.

"You could," Casper said, "and you'd be caught, because they've got a man watching every trailhead who isn't a cliff face. Ilsa reads country the way I read water. Nobody follows her twice." She turned back before he could argue. "And me? I take the boat down the main channel alone, running straight at the checkpoint like an idiot, with enough decoy weight aboard to look like the whole job. They commit to me, Ilsa walks free, and if I'm very lucky I don't die in the first kilometer."

The silence afterward had texture. Rain on the cave mouth. The river muttering in the dark.

"That's the nightmare gorge," Marsh said at last. Not the Khumbak—the other one. The one even the maps hedged about. "Casper. You swore."

"I swore a lot of things." She heard her own voice come out flat and steady and wondered whose it was. "I swore I'd never guide again after Nanda Kot. I swore I'd pay off the debt and go home to my daughter. I swore I didn't kill Daniel Osei." She folded the map into her pocket, stones scattering. "Turns out the mountain keeps better records than the river does. It's time somebody read them out loud."

* * *

They worked through the night, and the work was a kind of grace.

Tomas stripped the boat to essentials and then built the lie back into her—weighted crates filled with river rocks and scrap, sealed and stenciled with the syndicate's own shipping codes that Ilsa copied from the forged permits. *Let them open them,* Tomas said, welding the last seam. *Let them find a hundred kilos of their own mountain.* Priya repacked the real evidence into parcels small enough to disappear against a body, taping each one herself, checking seals twice, saying nothing, which was how Priya prayed. Ferro sat sharpening the portage axes over and over until Marsh took the whetstone away from him gently, like taking a knife from a child, and told him the truest thing anyone said that night: "Courage is mostly logistics, boy. Do the logistics."

Casper found Ilsa at the cave mouth at three in the morning, checking the pasture route by headlamp against a hand-drawn map gone soft with sweat.

"If they take you," Casper said, "the evidence dies with you."

"If they take me, the evidence dies with me," Ilsa agreed, "and my brother stays a statistic in a company filing cabinet, and your client stays a drunk tourist who fell off a mountain, and Serkhang keeps drinking poison for another decade." She rolled the map. "So I won't be taken."

"There's a bend at the second pasture fence where the trail drops out of sight for forty meters. If anyone's watching, that's where."

"I know." Ilsa looked at her then, fully, and Casper felt it like current under calm water—that thing between them that neither of them had named because naming it would have required stopping long enough to drown in it. "Six years I've carried this alone. Three weeks ago I was rigging your boat at night because I couldn't sleep and couldn't trust you. Now I can't imagine handing this to anyone else." A pause. "Come back down the gorge, Casper. Don't make me have carried it for nothing."

"I'll meet you in Serkhang," Casper said. "I'll bring the boat."

They shook on it, formally, absurdly, like two merchants closing a deal in a market that sold only futures. Then Ilsa did something she had never done: she put her hand briefly on the back of Casper's neck, the way you steady someone on a rope, and left it there for exactly as long as a breath takes.

* * *

Dawn came gray and boiling off the water.

They staged the deception at first light with the discipline of people who had already said their goodbyes and were now just executing them. Tomas and Ferro fired up the outboard and began the loud, clumsy business of hauling crates up toward the landslide scar, cursing theatrically, leaving torch stubs and dropped rope everywhere. Through the field glasses Casper watched the men on the cliffside trail shift their weight, lean into their radios. Watching the wrong thing. Believing the easy story.

Marsh insisted on going with the overland party—"nobody trusts my face on a boat anymore, including me"—and when he shook Casper's hand at the treeline, his grip said the apology he'd made at the portage all over again, and something new besides: *this time I mean it.*

Priya hugged her without words. Ferro tried to salute and turned it into fixing his pack strap, and Casper let him, because some mercies are quiet.

Then it was just her and the boat and eleven kilometers of nightmare.

She pushed off into the main channel with the sun not yet over the ridge, the weighted crates riding low and honest-looking behind her, and the gorge walls rising on either side like the pages of a book closing. Her hands were shaking on the tiller. She noticed them the way you notice weather—as fact, not judgment—and let them shake. Fear was just the body reading water ahead of the mind. Her body had always been faster than her mind. That was the whole trouble with her life.

The first rapid came up grinning.

She ran it clean, mostly, the patchwork hull complaining but holding, and the second, and the third, and by the fourth she had stopped narrating her own death and started doing the only thing that had ever saved her: reading the water, trusting the water, being—for these few kilometers, illegally, gloriously—exactly the guide the blacklist said no longer existed.

The checkpoint throat appeared around the bend at midmorning, steel hull gleaming, men on the rocks with rifles slung casual as camera straps. A voice crackled across the water through a megaphone, ordering her to heave to.

Casper Okafor cut the engine, stood up straight in the boat so they could see her face, and shouted back the only answer that mattered:

"This is a licensed concession delivery! Permit number K-114! You want to explain to your lawyers why you boarded your own cargo?"

It was a beautiful lie. It was also, in every way that counted, the truth.

The megaphone went silent. Somewhere on the cliffside, a radio hissed. And in the pause—in that held breath of a moment where the syndicate's whole machine hesitated, weighing money against certainty—Casper saw, far above on the eastern rim, a single figure with a red pack moving fast along the skyline, heading for Serkhang, carrying six years of the dead in a med kit on her back.

Ilsa was clear. The evidence was moving. Whatever happened next happened next.

The megaphone crackled again, angrier now, and the patrol boat's engine turned over with a sound like a decision being made.

"Hold position," the voice ordered. "We're coming alongside."

Casper put her hand on the throttle and thought of her daughter's letter, unsent, folded in her breast pocket. Thought of Daniel Osei laughing at the wrong moment. Thought of a village drinking brown water and calling it fate.

"Not today," she said—to the men, to the river, to the ghost with good credit she'd been hauling around for seven years—and opened the throttle full, driving the boat straight down the throat of the gorge while the first rifle shot cracked off the rock above her head and the nightmare, at last, woke up.
