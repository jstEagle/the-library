## 20. The Broken Summit

The gorge had a name that no map carried and every boatman in the valley refused to say aloud. Casper had heard it once, years ago, from an old ferryman with river-blind eyes who told her it was called the Throat of the Mountain, and then he'd crossed himself in the way of a man who didn't believe in God but believed in water.

She stood at the put-in at first light, alone, and looked at what she'd sworn never to run again.

The monsoon had swollen the Indus tributary into something barely recognizable as the river she'd known. It ran brown and muscular between walls of black schist, compressed to half its dry-season width, and where the gorge narrowed—the place they called the Gate, though Casper thought of it by another name, the name she'd given it in her nightmares for six years—the water stacked itself into a standing wave taller than their mast. Below the Gate came the Squeeze, then the Ledges, then a mile of churning chaos that hydrologists would have called Class VI and Casper called the long dark, because that was all you saw of it: dark water, dark rock, and whatever light your own courage could carry down there with you.

This was the route from her nightmares. Not metaphorically. She had dreamed it so many times that she knew the sound of it asleep—the hollow boom of the wave collapsing, the hiss of water through rock—and waking to that sound had ruined more mornings than she could count.

"You don't have to do this."

Ilsa's voice behind her. Casper didn't turn. Her hands were busy checking the throw bag on her belt, the knife, the whistle she probably wouldn't live long enough to use. Everything on her body had a purpose except her fear, which just hung there, useless and enormous.

"Yes, I do," Casper said. "That's the whole design."

"They'll follow you because they think you have the cases. That's the bait. But if they catch you—"

"Then Ferro and Ilsa and Priya are already over the pass with the real evidence," Casper said, "and I'm one guide in a river full of guides." She turned then and found Ilsa watching her with those flat grey eyes that read currents the way other people read faces. "You taught me that, actually. Water doesn't remember who it drowns. Neither do syndicate men. They want the cargo. Let them chase a woman who looks like she's carrying it."

"The decoy cases weigh forty kilos."

"I know. I packed them." Casper allowed herself a thin smile. "Rocks and old survey paper. Heavy enough to matter, worthless enough not to grieve."

Ilsa stepped closer. In the grey dawn light her face was stripped of everything she usually wore over it—all the careful neutrality, the hydrologist's professional distance—and what remained was simpler and harder to look at. "Six years I've been hunting this," she said quietly. "Six years, and I keep thinking about how it ends. In my version, nobody dies at the end. I need you to be in my version, Casper."

Casper looked back at the river. The Throat boomed somewhere below, patient as geology.

"In mine," she said, "somebody always does. I've just been trying to make sure it isn't anyone I love." She shouldered her dry bag. "Get up the pass, Ilsa. Read the water for Priya. And when this is over—" she paused, because the sentence she wanted to say next wasn't safe yet, wasn't survivable yet, so she said the smaller true thing instead: "When this is over, there's a tea house two hundred kilometers downstream that makes terrible coffee. I intend to complain about it in person."

Then she pushed off.

* * *

The first kilometer was almost kind, which frightened her more than violence would have. Kind water meant the river was saving its strength, and she knew this stretch the way she knew the scar tissue on her own palms: the eddy behind the split boulder, the tongue of green current threading between the submerged rocks, the point past the second bend where the gradient doubled and the whole world tilted.

She rode it standing, one hand on the tiller, the decoy cases lashed amidships under a tarp that bulged convincingly like instruments. Behind her, upstream, she heard it start: engines. Two of them. The syndicate's rigid inflatables, heavy with armed men, coming down the same water she'd chosen because only a madwoman or a native would choose it.

Only a madwoman or a native. Marsh had said that to her once, back at the confluence, half apology and half accusation. *That's why I needed you.* And he'd been right, and being right about her had been the cruelest thing he ever did.

The Gate appeared around the bend like a held breath finally released.

It was worse than her memory. Memory had softened it, sanded the edges, made it survivable in retrospect. The reality was a wall of brown water folding over itself, and at its base the recirculation—a hydraulic, boatmen called it, a keeper, the river chewing its own tail—wide enough to swallow their whole expedition and keep it. Somewhere in that foam, six years ago, a different river in a different valley had done the same thing to a different boat, and a man named Aldous Wren had gone in and not come out, and the world had decided it was her fault because the world needed someone to blame and she was the one still breathing.

"Casper Okafor," she said aloud, to herself, to the water, to the dead man whose ghost had ridden every river since. "Reading the water."

She drove the boat straight at the left side of the wave, where a seam of cleaner water climbed the face like a rope thrown down from heaven. The bow rose. Rose. Rose until the sky was all there was and the engine screamed at nothing—

—and then the drop, and the world went white.

* * *

There is a moment in every capsize that lasts longer than it does. Casper lived inside that moment now: the hull hitting sideways, the cold detonating through her clothes, the tarp tearing free and the decoy cases spinning away like coins dropped down a well—good, let them chase rocks—and then the hydraulic took her.

The keeper held her the way it holds everything: gently, endlessly, turning her over and over in the aerated wash where nothing floats and nothing sinks and swimming is a word without meaning. She counted rotations the way her instructors had taught her, twenty years and a lifetime ago—*one, relax; two, feel for the surface; three, wait*—but the counts dissolved. Something struck her shoulder. Rock, probably. Or the boat. Her boot caught on the line that had saved her life a hundred times and now tried to end it, the painter wrapped around her ankle, towing her down into the green-black heart of the recirculation where the river kept its records after all.

*The river keeps no records,* she had told herself in chapter one of her life, in the first days of her exile, patching leaks and dodging patrols. She'd believed it. It had been a comfort.

It was a lie. The river kept everything. It kept Aldous Wren. It kept the shape of her hands on a tiller the morning everything ended. It kept the exact temperature of the water that had taken him, and it gave that water back to her now, degree for degree, as if to say: *you never left here. You have been drowning in this place for six years and calling it a career.*

Her lungs began to burn. Her vision narrowed to a coin of grey light far above, wobbling, unreachable.

And in that coin of light, absurdly, she saw the summit.

Not a mountain—she was underground of the sky, inside the throat—but the idea of one. Every summit she'd ever chased, every client she'd hauled to a top so they could stand on it and feel larger than their lives. Nanda Kot. The Karakoram peaks. The whole industry of conquest she'd served, the belief that the high places existed to be beaten, that a mountain was a problem with a solution, that reaching the top of anything meant you'd won.

She had spent her whole life climbing toward summits. And here, at the bottom of the water, dying in the dark, she finally understood what the title of her own ruin had been trying to tell her all along: the summit was never the point. The summit was the thing that broke you open. Every one of them. Nanda Kot hadn't broken her career; it had broken the lie she'd built her career on—that competence was safety, that skill was a contract the world honored. The mountain had broken that open and shown her the truth underneath: that we are all of us downstream of forces we don't control, and the only choice we ever really get is what we hold onto while the water takes us.

So she stopped fighting the rotation. She reached down, calm as a surgeon, and cut the painter off her boot.

The river, offended or satisfied, spat her out.

* * *

She surfaced gasping in the tail-out below the keeper, one arm hooked over the upside-down hull of her boat, which had decided, against all odds and all physics, to stay with her. The decoy cases were gone. The engine was drowned. The second inflatable was nowhere in sight—yet—and the roar of the gorge ahead told her she had perhaps ninety seconds before the next rapid made her situation academic.

"Ninety seconds," she said to the boat, to the sky, to whoever was keeping score. "Fine. Ninety seconds is enough to drown in."

She got a knee over the hull and started kicking for the right bank, where a gravel fan spilled out of a side canyon—the one exit, the one she'd mapped in her head at midnight while the others slept, the route nobody swore off twice because nobody but her had ever survived it once.

She was fifty meters from shore when the first inflatable came around the bend above.

They'd run the Gate. Of course they had—they were professionals, syndicate security, men who ran rivers the way accountants ran spreadsheets, with equipment and contempt for consequences. The boat knifed through the foam and the men aboard raised rifles, and Casper, clinging to her dead hull in the middle of a flood, understood with perfect clarity that she had miscalculated exactly one thing: they didn't need to catch her. They just needed her to stop moving.

The muzzle flashes were small and bright against the grey morning. The water around her stitched itself with holes.

She let go of the boat and dove.

* * *

The bank, when she reached it, cost her skin she couldn't spare. Gravel scoured her forearms, the current tried to peel her off the shallows and hand her back to the guns, and she crawled the last meters on her belly like something born again and immediately regretting it. Above her, the inflatable circled, looking for a landing. There wasn't one—not on this bank, not in this flow—and she heard the men shouting to each other in Urdu and Pashto, arguing, recalculating. They would go downstream and intercept at the confluence. They had time. They had numbers.

What they didn't have was her.

Casper rolled onto her back on the gravel and laughed, a raw, ugly sound with blood-tinged spit in it, because she was alive, actually alive, and the decoy cases were at the bottom of the keeper where the syndicate would spend a day and a fortune dredging for them, and Ilsa and Priya and Ferro were already three hours up the pass with the real evidence strapped to real backs.

Except.

Except the second inflatable. She hadn't heard it since the Gate. And the plan—the beautiful, arrogant, Casper-shaped plan—had assumed both boats would follow the bait.

She got to her feet, dripping, and looked downstream, and her stomach dropped through the gravel into the center of the earth.

The second inflatable was already at the confluence. And it wasn't chasing her.

It was chasing Marsh.

* * *

He'd come down the portage trail with the reserve fuel and the radio gear, alone, because Marsh had never in his life trusted anyone else to carry the important things, and the syndicate boat had cut him off at the junction with the patience of men who had been paid to wait. Casper watched from two hundred meters of impossible terrain as the scene resolved through the rain-haze: Marsh standing in his own small boat, hands visible, voice carrying thin across the water—she couldn't hear the words but she knew their shape, because she'd heard Marsh negotiate his whole life, smooth and reasonable, offering terms.

The terms had changed. She could see that even from here. The men in the inflatable weren't contractors anymore; the forgery had been detected weeks ago, and witnesses had become liabilities somewhere around the confluence mutiny, and everyone on this river knew it now, including Marsh.

Including Marsh, she realized, watching him raise one hand in a gesture that was unmistakably a salute—or a farewell—and then open his throttle and turn his boat downstream, away from her, away from the checkpoint, straight into the flooded lower gorge where no rescue would ever look and no body would ever conveniently surface.

Drawing them off. All of them. The whole armed weight of the syndicate's attention, pulled after Devlin Marsh like a lure behind a boat, so that the checkpoint upstream would sit empty for exactly as long as a battered crew needed to walk through it with forty kilos of truth.

"No," Casper said. Out loud. To a man who couldn't hear her. "Marsh—"

But he was already gone, his boat shrinking down the brown corridor of the flood, the inflatable swinging after him with its engines wide open, and the last thing she saw of Devlin Marsh was his hand raised high out of the spray—not surrender, not triumph, just acknowledgment, the oldest signal on the water: *I see you. Go on.*

Then the gorge bent, and the river closed over him like a page.

* * *

She didn't remember deciding to move. Her body simply did what bodies trained by twenty years of mountains do: it climbed. Up the gravel fan, into the side canyon, along the goat track her feet knew better than her mind, toward the saddle where the checkpoint sat and the others would be coming down to meet her. Rain hammered the schist. The Throat boomed below like a heart too big for the world.

Halfway up, she heard voices below and behind—shouting, not hostile, urgent—and turned to see figures on the gravel bar she'd crawled from. Three of them. Big men in soaked tactical vests, one limping badly, all of them waving their arms over their heads in the universal grammar of *don't shoot, help.*

The reformed security men. The ones she'd pulled from the capsized boat in the final gorge, the ones Priya had stitched back together at the sea cave, the ones who had spent two days silent and ashamed and useful. They must have slipped their handlers when the boats went after Marsh. Now they were stranded on the wrong side of the flood, and the river was rising, and the gravel bar would be gone within the hour.

Casper stood on the track in the rain and weighed it, the way she weighed everything now: quickly, honestly, with the summit broken open inside her.

Then she uncoiled her rope and started back down.

* * *

They got the last man across as the bar vanished. He was the limping one, a heavyset Pashtun with a stitched scalp and eyes that had seen the bottom of the river and hadn't liked it. He gripped her forearm at the top of the track, water pouring off both of them, and said something in Pashto that she didn't understand, and then, seeing her face, repeated it in careful English:

"We saw the old man turn downstream. We know what he bought for you." His hand tightened. "Whatever happens at the checkpoint—we are not your enemies today, guide. Today we are your witnesses."

"Witnesses," Casper said. The word tasted strange, like water from a new source.

"That is what I said." He almost smiled, a crack in the professional mask. "The mountain does not care who wins. But people watch. People remember. You pulled us out when every reason said leave us. So today, we watch. We remember."

Above them, at the saddle, a figure appeared against the grey sky—small, wiry, waving both arms, and even at four hundred meters Casper knew the silhouette the way she knew her own hands. Ferro. Running down toward her, stumbling on the wet rock, twenty years old and crying and laughing at the same time, and behind him the rest of them, Ilsa and Priya, packs heavy with the truth, coming down out of the pass into the last hour of the permit window.

Ferro reached her, grabbed her shoulders, looked her over with the frantic triage gaze Priya had drilled into all of them. "We saw the boats go after Marsh, we saw the guns at the Gate, we thought—I thought—" His voice cracked clean through. "You were supposed to be bait, not—"

"Bait usually survives," Casper said. "It's the hook that gets eaten." She looked past him, up at the saddle, at the checkpoint road beyond it where the syndicate's agents were waiting and the clock was bleeding out its final minutes. Then downstream, where the river swallowed everything, including, possibly, a man who had sold her route to devils six years ago and had just paid for it with the only coin he had left.

"He drew them off," Ferro said quietly. "All of them. There's nobody at the checkpoint but the lawyers and two guards. Casper—it's open. The road is *open.*"

The rain eased, just slightly, just enough. Casper Okafor stood on the flank of the mountain that had broken her, holding the rope that had saved three men, and felt something in her chest break open too—not painfully, not like before, but the way ice breaks on a river in spring: suddenly, all at once, releasing everything it had held frozen.

"Then we walk," she said. "Right now. While it's open. And Ferro—" she hoisted the pack Ilsa handed her, forty kilos of other people's crimes, light as a promise, "—when this is over, remind me to tell you what your name means to me. There isn't time now."

They climbed. Behind them the Throat of the Mountain roared on, keeping its records, holding its dead, forgiving nothing—and for the first time in six years, Casper didn't ask it to.
