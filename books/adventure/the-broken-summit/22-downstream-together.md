## 22. Downstream, Together

The river had gone quiet in the way it only did at the end of the monsoon, when the water dropped back into its banks and remembered it was a river and not a rolling brown argument with the sky. Casper Okafor stood at the tiller of the *Mended Heart*—Tomas's joke of a name, painted on the hull in letters that leaned like they'd had a long night—and watched three tourists from Manchester take photographs of cliffs that had never tried to kill anyone.

"Mind the left channel," she called, not because it mattered, but because saying it felt like a prayer she'd gotten good at.

The left channel was fine. Everything, this morning, was fine. That was the strange part—the part she still hadn't learned to trust. Six weeks since the gorge. Six weeks since she'd come up out of the water coughing her lungs onto the rocks while Ferro hauled her by the collar and the two syndicate security men, the ones she'd turned back for, held her arms and shouted her name like it was a word they'd just learned and wanted to keep. Six weeks since Marsh's boat went downstream empty and never came back.

She'd stopped waiting for it to come back somewhere around week three. Not because she believed he was dead—Devlin Marsh was too mean and too lucky to die in water, even that water—but because she'd understood, finally, that his absence was the last gift he had in him. He'd drawn them off. He'd walked into the checkpoint at Serkhang Gorge with his forged permit held up like a torch and his voice pitched to carry, and while every agent in the valley turned to look at him, Ilsa had gone over the pass with a dry bag full of the truth.

The tourists laughed at something. One of them, a round man with a sunburned neck, pointed at a Himalayan griffon turning circles on the thermals and asked Ferro what it was.

"That's a vulture," Ferro said, with the gravity of a man announcing a head of state. "He's the local. Been here longer than any of us."

Ferro had filled out since the gorge. Filled out in the shoulders, in the voice, in the way he stood at the bow now like the boat was his to protect. He'd stopped asking Casper to sign things he could sign himself. That was how she knew he'd grown: he'd learned that competence wasn't permission to stop being careful.

Priya sat amidships with the Manchester woman who'd gotten seasick in what Casper generously refused to call rapids, holding her hair back and telling her about electrolytes in the tone of a woman who had said these exact words four hundred times and still meant them. Priya's clinic fund was full now. Full enough that she'd started treating villagers from Serkhang for free on Sundays, driving up the new track the mining company had been forced to grade as part of its settlement—its *partial* settlement, the lawyers said, but Priya said the word *settlement* like it was a bone she'd finally gotten to set.

And Ilsa—

Casper found her the way she always did, without looking. Ilsa was at the stern, one hand on the rail, watching the water with the instruments of her attention: the way the current braided, the way the light came off it, the way a river told you everything if you were willing to be told. She'd stayed. That was the fact Casper turned over every morning like a stone she couldn't stop picking up. Ilsa Reiner, who had a brother buried in the tailings above Serkhang and a six-year hunt finished and a dozen reasons to go home to Zürich and a life with walls in it—she had stayed.

"Depth's good," Ilsa said, coming forward, her voice pitched just for Casper. "Sandbar's moved again, though. It's building on the inside of the bend."

"I saw it."

"You didn't see it."

"I saw it *spiritually*."

Ilsa's mouth did the thing it did—half a smile, quickly filed away, like a document she wasn't ready to release. Six weeks, and Casper had catalogued maybe nine full smiles. She was in no hurry. Some things you didn't want to run at summit speed.

* * *

They moored at the crossing by midafternoon, the one that had started all of this—the illegal ferry run, the leaky hull, the patrol boat Casper had once outrun with a hold full of strangers and a heart full of arithmetic. The patrol boat was moored there now too, permanent, but its crew waved. One of them, a young officer named Dawa, had taken to bringing them tea on Sundays, and last month he'd looked at Casper across the gunwale and said, quietly, "My cousin was at the assembly in Serkhang. When they read the names." He hadn't said anything else. He hadn't needed to.

The Manchester tourists disembarked, tipped extravagantly, and stood on the bank taking one last photograph of the boat. The round man shook Casper's hand with both of his.

"They said you were the best guide on this stretch of the Indus," he said. "They said you used to do the big mountains."

"I did a lot of things," Casper said. "This is the one I'm good at."

She watched them go up the track toward the road, and she felt the old shape of the want—the summits, the permits, the official redemption, the laminated card with her name that would let her walk back into Nepal or Peru with her head up—and she examined it the way you examine an old injury. It still ached. But it ached the way a healed bone aches before rain. It ached because it had been broken, not because it was breaking.

The blacklist stood. She'd learned that in week two, when a lawyer in Islamabad—paid out of the syndicate settlement, the first money Casper had ever taken that she hadn't flinched to touch—had called to tell her, with genuine regret in his voice, that no court in three countries was prepared to overturn her blacklisting. The evidence had cleared her *in the valley*. The villages had held an assembly, had read the core sample reports aloud in Urdu and Balti and English, had heard Ilsa stand up and say *my brother's name is Anselm Reiner and this water killed him*, and the valley had spoken her name back to her clean.

But the ministries had their files, and the files had their stamps, and the stamps did not care what a village assembly believed.

She'd hung up the phone and stood in the tea house—the same tea house where Marsh had left the permit on the table like a dare—and she'd waited for the grief to come. It hadn't. What had come instead was Ferro, banging through the door with a fish he claimed was a record and Tomas behind him claiming it wasn't, and the moment had passed, and she'd understood that the grief had been quietly replaced by something with better posture.

* * *

The letter took three drafts.

Casper wrote it in the evenings now, at the table in the boat shed, under the one bulb that Tomas had wired to a solar panel he'd salvaged from the syndicate's abandoned security post. The panel was stolen goods, technically. Tomas had argued, at length, that the syndicate had no moral standing to press charges. Casper had let him win. There were worse legal theories.

The first draft had been all apology. She'd burned it in the stove.

The second draft had been all explanation—the mine, the rockfall, the sabotage, the years of running, the reason her mother had been a ghost with good credit and bad timing. She'd read it back and heard a woman building a case, and Amara had spent her whole childhood listening to adults build cases. She'd burned that one too.

The third draft started: *Your tuition is paid. It's paid for four years. Before you ask how—*

She told her. All of it. The debt collector and the sixty days and the contract she'd signed at midnight telling herself it was just money. The forged permit. The core samples in the dry boxes. The valley where her career had died and the truth of how it had died—Marsh's route sold to the syndicate years before, the rockfall not an act of the mountain but an act of men, her client Daniel Okonkwo not a victim of her error but a witness they had decided to erase, with her name as the eraser.

*I didn't kill him, Amara. I need you to read that twice, because I have only just now managed to believe it myself.*

She wrote about Ilsa's brother. She wrote about the old woman in Serkhang who had spat at her feet, and about going back to that woman's door in the fourth week with the evidence in her hands, and about the silence that had passed between them—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but a recognition: *we were both lied to by the same people, and we both stayed anyway.*

She wrote about the boat. She wrote about the tourists from Manchester. She wrote, *I run safe water now. I know how that sounds, from me. But it turns out there is a whole kind of courage in taking people somewhere beautiful and bringing them back, and I was too proud to see it when I was young, and I am not too proud now, because pride is heavy and I have put down a lot of things lately.*

The last paragraph she wrote slowly, the way you cross a scree slope, testing each hold.

*I am not coming back the person you were promised. I am not licensed, and I am not clean in the eyes of anyone who works in an office, and I have chosen people over institutions because the institutions had their chance. But the debt is paid—not the money, Amara, the other one, the one I've been carrying since you were nine. I'm sending this with the truth enclosed because you deserve a mother who doesn't edit the river. Come see it sometime. It forgives nothing. I've stopped asking it to. That's the freest I've ever been.*

She read it four times. Then she put it in the envelope, wrote the address in Lisbon, and did not seal it, because sealing it was the kind of thing you did at dawn, with a steady hand, and her hand was not steady and the dawn was nine hours away.

Ilsa found her there, the way she always did.

* * *

They walked the bank after dark, the way they'd started doing without ever deciding to. The river talked its night talk, low and constant. The stars were the aggressive, unfiltered kind you only got this far from cities.

"Four years," Ilsa said. "Tuition."

"Settlement money. The syndicate's, technically. The lawyer called it restitution." Casper kicked a stone into the shallows. "I called it blood money for about a week. Then Priya pointed out that Amara's tuition was going to come from somewhere, and it was either that or another season of running patrol-boat gauntlets, and one of those options has dental."

"You could go to her. Lisbon's not the end of the world."

"I know where Lisbon is. I've been reading about it." Casper looked at the water. "I'm going to. When the season ends. I'm going to stand in her doorway with the letter in my hand and let her decide what she wants to do with me." A breath. "But I'm coming back."

Ilsa said nothing. She was good at nothing; it was one of the things Casper had learned to read as fluently as water. The nothing meant *say the rest*.

"I spent six years thinking exile was a punishment," Casper said. "That the mountains had taken my license and the ministries had taken my name and what was left was a woman running an illegal ferry because she had nowhere legal to be. And then it turns out the ferry was the whole time. The crew was the whole time. Tomas and his stolen solar panel and Priya's Sunday clinic and Ferro learning to read a sandbar and—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "You could have gone home, Ilsa. Six years of hunting, finished. And you're here, re-rigging my boat in the dark because you don't trust my knots."

"Your knots are a war crime."

"You *stayed*."

Ilsa looked at the river for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness she used when something mattered too much to decorate. "My brother died because people looked at a mountain and saw something to take. I spent six years thinking if I could prove it, I'd be done. Finished. Free." She turned. "Then we ran the gorge, and you turned that boat around for two men who'd been paid to drown us, and I watched you do it, and I understood that I wasn't finished. I was just—done with the wrong part. The hunting part." Her shoulder came against Casper's, deliberate, an anchor set. "The found part starts here."

The river kept talking. It forgave nothing, and it never would, and that was its gift: it was the one honest thing left, the one witness that couldn't be bought or forged or filed away. Casper stood in its sound with Ilsa's shoulder against hers and felt the last of the old arithmetic—*sixty days, triple rate, one more job*—finally, completely, settle to zero.

"Dawn tomorrow," she said. "We've got the German family at seven. They want the bird cliffs."

"I'll check the bend."

"You'll check the bend."

They walked back along the bank toward the light in the boat shed, where Tomas was arguing with Ferro about the fish and Priya was winning an argument nobody else knew was happening, and on the table inside, under the one honest bulb, the letter to Lisbon waited for morning—sealed, addressed, and carrying, for the first time in six years, nothing but the truth.

Casper Okafor slept that night like a woman with no debts, in a country with no records, on a river that kept none.
