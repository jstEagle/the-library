## 1. Miles, Not Years

The trick to surviving the interior is to want nothing from it. The river doesn't care about your plans. The weather doesn't care about your schedule. And the people in the scattered settlements along the water — good people, mostly, generous with tea and terrible with goodbyes — will happily take ten years of your life if you let them.

So I don't let them.

I've been flying medicine runs out of Kestrel Bend for a decade now, and in that time I've learned exactly three things worth knowing: how to read a river from two thousand feet, how to land a Cessna on a strip of gravel that would make a goat nervous, and how to leave before anyone starts expecting me back. Everything else is decoration.

Tuesday's run was routine, which in my business means only moderately likely to kill me. Insulin and antibiotics for Ferrow Landing, vaccines for the mission at Two Sisters, a crate of antimalarials for the clinic at Drenna Post. Four stops, six hundred miles of river country, home by dark if the sky cooperated.

The sky did not cooperate. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

I took off at first light, when the mist was still peeling off the water like gauze off a wound. From up there the interior looks almost kind — a green sprawl stitched through with silver, the Vaskar River and its hundred daughters braiding and rebraiding across the lowlands. Pilots who don't know it call it empty country. There's nothing empty about it. It's full, just not of people. Or rather: full of people, just not where the maps say they are.

That's the thing nobody tells you about this part of the world. The maps are lies. Polite ones, mostly — cartographers rounding off bends, guessing at tributaries, naming things after surveyors who never got within fifty miles of them. But some of the lies aren't polite at all. Some of them are deliberate. Whole villages inked out. Rivers renamed so thoroughly that even the old people who remember the old names have started using the new ones, the way you'd wear someone else's coat because yours was taken.

I know the river country the way other pilots know their instrument panels. Ten years of flying supply runs will do that. I know that the channel below Ferrow Landing shifts every spring flood and the charts are three years stale. I know the fishing camps at the mouth of the Serra are abandoned by August. I know which bends hold fog until noon and which ridgelines funnel wind like a throat. I know all of it the way you know anything you use daily and love never: completely, and without ceremony.

* * *

Ferrow Landing went fine. Old Marta met me at the strip with her usual complaint — that the insulin always arrives either frozen or about to be, and why can't I time it better — and her usual gift, which was a jar of plum preserves pressed into my hands before I could refuse. I said what I always say. *Marta, I can't take this.* She said what she always says. *Then throw it out the window, pilot, but you're taking it.*

Two Sisters was fine too, if you count a crosswind gusting thirty knots as fine, which after ten years I do. The mission nurse, a wiry woman named Okonkwo who has been trying to learn my first name since 2019, waved me off with a salute and a shout about coming to dinner sometime. *Sometime,* I called back, which is the word I use instead of *never.* It sounds kinder. It isn't.

Drenna Post was where the day turned.

The clinic there serves maybe four hundred people spread along forty miles of shoreline, and the man who runs it, Dr. Ilves, is the kind of tired that coffee can't fix. He helped me unload without talking, which I appreciated, and then he talked anyway, which I expected.

"Storm cell building over the high country," he said, nodding west. "You'll want to beat it downriver."

"I'll beat it."

"You always say that."

"And I'm always here to say it again." I zipped the cargo net. "That's the system working."

He gave me a look — the look people give me when they've decided something about me and are too polite to say it. I get that look a lot. It usually means one of two things: *you're running from something,* or *you're running toward nothing.* People assume those are different.

I lifted off at half past two with the western sky already bruising. The storm sat over the high country like something brooding, all purple underbelly and slow rotation, and between me and Kestrel Bend lay ninety miles of river valley that funnels weather the way a chimney funnels smoke.

* * *

Here's what they don't put in the flight manuals: sometimes the right decision is the embarrassing one. I could have pushed for home. Ten years ago I would have pushed for home, because ten years ago I still believed that wanting to get somewhere was the same as getting there. Instead I did the math — headwind, cell movement, fuel, daylight — and the math said *land, idiot.*

The problem was that the math didn't say *where.*

There are perhaps six usable strips between Drenna Post and Kestrel Bend, and the nearest one, a logging cut at Harrow Bend, was forty minutes behind me and directly under the storm's leading edge. That left the old mining strip at Callow Flats, which hadn't seen regular use since the mine closed, which meant the surface was a rumor. Gravel, weeds, probably drainage ditches where drainage ditches shouldn't be.

Or there was the third option. The one that wasn't on any chart I owned.

I'd flown over it a dozen times without landing — a shelf of flat ground above the river, maybe sixty miles short of home, where an old map in my hangar showed a village called Selvane. Except Selvane wasn't there anymore. I knew that the way I knew most things about this country: from altitude, at a glance, filed away without comment. The village had drowned. Some dam project upstream, decades back, or a flood, or the river simply deciding it wanted the ground more than the people did. The roofs had come off years ago. What remained were foundations showing through the shallows like knuckles through skin, and a stone church tower standing alone in the water, its bell long gone, its windows open to the weather.

Pilots call places like that *the drowned marks.* Every river country has them. We use them as landmarks and think nothing of it. People lived there; now the water does. The river keeps what it keeps.

I put down on the shelf above the village with a bump and a prayer, killed the engine, and listened to the storm arrive.

It arrived the way storms do in the interior — not gradually but *at once*, as if it had been waiting for permission. Rain came sideways. Lightning walked the ridgeline on white legs. I tied the plane down triple, crawled into the back with a sleeping bag and half a tin of cold beans, and told myself this was fine. This was the job. This was freedom, actually — no schedule, no obligations, nobody waiting up, nobody worried. Just me and the weather and nine hundred pounds of medicine that didn't care if I lived or died.

That last thought was supposed to be comforting. I noticed, somewhere around the third hour of rain hammering the fuselage, that it wasn't.

* * *

Morning came gray and rinsed-clean, the way mornings do after a storm wrings the sky out. The river had risen overnight — brown now, muscular, carrying whole trees downstream like it was late for something. I walked down to the shoreline while the engine oil warmed, coffee thermos in hand, telling myself I was checking the water level for takeoff calculations.

I was not checking the water level.

Selvane sat in the shallows below me, and in the morning light it looked less drowned than *paused.* The church tower stood hip-deep. Roofless walls made a grid beneath the clear water, streets you could trace with your eye, doorways opening onto nothing. A fence line ran into the flood and kept going, posts marching down into the green dark like a sentence that gets cut off mid—

I stood there longer than I meant to. Long enough for the coffee to go cold. Long enough to notice details I'd never given myself permission to notice from the air: a child's swing frame rusting in the shallows, still hanging from its branch, though the branch was dead and the seat had rotted to a ring. A rowboat pulled up onto higher ground and left there, upside down, waiting for owners who had understood, apparently, that they weren't coming back.

And carved into the stone lip of the old well — the one dry thing in the whole drowned grid, standing on a rise the water hadn't reached — a mark I didn't recognize. A crest of some kind. A bird, maybe, wings folded, inside a border of interlocking lines. Weathered nearly smooth. Older than the drowning, I'd have guessed, though I'm no historian. Historians need footnotes. I just needed to get home.

But I didn't get home. Not yet. I stood at the shoreline of a village that no longer existed on paper, in a country that had erased it so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased, and I felt something crack. Small. Hairline. The kind of fracture you don't hear, just later notice — the way you notice a mug handle loose only when it comes off in your hand.

It was loneliness, I think. Mine, meeting theirs. Ten years I'd told myself that solitude was a choice, that the horizon was a companion, that a man — a person, sorry, Marta corrects me every time — that a person could live entirely in transit and call it free. Standing there, looking at everything Selvane had lost, I understood for the first time that the water hadn't just taken their homes. It had taken their *belonging*, and that I had spent a decade treating mine like ballast. Something to jettison to fly lighter.

The river moved past, brown and indifferent, carrying its trees.

I turned to go, and that's when I saw it — snagged in the drowned fence line twenty yards out, half-submerged, turning slowly in the current. Not driftwood. Not debris. A chest. Iron-banded, tar-black with age, and on its lid, catching the first real sunlight of the morning, a crest.

A bird, wings folded, inside a border of interlocking lines.

The same mark as the well.

I stood very still, the way you do when the country you thought you knew looks at you back.
