## 2. What the River Keeps

The chest turned in the current like something patient.

I've spent ten years learning what the river keeps and what it gives back, and the arithmetic is simple: it keeps almost everything. It gave back a fence post once, forty years after the village drowned. It gave back a church bell that rang for three days in a barn before anyone admitted where it came from. It does not give back chests with crests on them. Crests belong to people, and people who own crests get looked for.

I should have walked away. I want that on the record, somewhere, in case this account is ever read by a court or a coroner: Noor Galloway saw the thing, assessed it correctly as trouble, and began walking away from it at a brisk professional pace.

I got maybe thirty yards.

The problem was the well. The problem was that mark, cut into stone by somebody's grandfather, worn smooth by a hundred years of buckets, sitting in a drowned field under eleven feet of green water — and here was its twin, riding the current like it had an appointment to keep. Two points make a line. I fly by instruments; I think in lines. And this line pointed somewhere I didn't want to look but couldn't stop looking at.

Also — and I'll be honest about this because honesty costs nothing when nobody's listening — I hadn't touched anything of anybody else's in ten years. Not a hand, not a debt, not a promise. A chest is not a hand. But it was close enough that my hands wanted it.

* * *

Getting it out took most of the morning and all of my dignity.

The fence line held it just above the mud shelf where the old road used to run, which meant I could wade out at low water if I didn't mind the cold, and I did mind, enormously, and went anyway. The river in that stretch runs over clay the color of a bruise, and it sucked at my boots like it had opinions about me leaving. The chest came free on the third pull with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle, and then it was mine, all hundred-odd pounds of it, and I dragged it up the bank through the reeds while a heron watched me with the flat contempt herons reserve for men doing stupid things alone.

I got it into the shade of the willows and sat there dripping, breathing hard, feeling older than my years and younger than my judgment.

Up close it was worse. Better. Worse. Iron bands gone to orange lace, tar-black wood that had kept its grain like a secret kept too long starts keeping you. And the crest — I wiped the silt off with my sleeve and there it was, sharp as the day some engraver cut it: the folded-wing bird inside the interlocking border. Vaskaria. Except nobody said Vaskaria anymore, the way nobody says the name of a man at his own funeral. The treaty called it "the former northern territories." The maps called it nothing at all. My grandmother, who was alive for the last year of it and never talked about anything else, used to call it the country that got filed under mountains.

Sealed, though. That was the word my hands kept coming back to. Sealed — lead poured across the hasp, stamped with the same bird, unbroken. Somebody had closed this chest intending it to stay closed until it reached wherever it was going, and the river had taken the courier instead. Which meant either the courier was still down there, or the chest had been traveling for a very long time.

I sat back against a willow and let the sun start drying me and told myself the sensible things. Salvage law in the interior is mostly rumor and rifle. Unclaimed goods on public water revert to whoever pulls them out, minus whatever the district office decides to remember it's owed. A sealed chest could hold anything: trade ledgers, someone's grandmother's dishes, ammunition gone soft with rust. Ninety-five percent of river salvage is disappointment with hinges.

But I'd seen the well. And the other five percent was sitting in front of me humming like a live wire, and I found I wasn't reaching for the pry bar I keep in the plane's survival kit. Partly because breaking a seal felt like a kind of violence I hadn't agreed to. Mostly, if I'm honest, because once it was open it would just be objects, and right now it was a question, and I have built ten good years on questions I don't answer.

So I did the next best thing to nothing. I covered it with brush, marked the spot against a split boulder I've used as a landmark since before I knew why, and walked back to the strip to file a flight plan I had no intention of flying, because a man standing around a chest all day looks like exactly what he is.

* * *

She came up the bank at four in the afternoon, when the light goes long and everyone's story gets harder to verify.

I heard her before I saw her — no engine, just gravel under boots, deliberate, the walk of somebody who wants you to know they're coming. I was doing aircraft checks I'd already done, which is the bush pilot's version of pacing, and I turned around and there she was: dark-haired, river-tanned, mid-thirties maybe, wearing the particular tired competence of a guide at the end of a season. She stopped at a conversational distance and looked at the plane, then at me, in that order, and I understood immediately that the plane was the real subject.

"That's a Kestrel 6," she said. "Haven't seen one flying in years."

"She flies fine."

"Must cost you."

"Everything costs." I set down the cowling rag. "Can I help you?"

"You're Galloway. The medicine runs." Not a question. "You landed here yesterday ahead of the storm. Nobody lands here ahead of a storm unless they know the water." A pause, mild as milk. "And this morning you were at the drowned village."

The temperature of the conversation dropped ten degrees and I hadn't even opened my mouth yet. I gave it my best level voice. "I go where the work is. You have salvage concerns?"

"I have questions." She came a step closer, and I caught woodsmoke and river mud and, underneath it, something clean like snowmelt. "About rights. There's a family two bends downstream that fishes that water. Three generations. If something comes out of their reach, they'll want to know who pulled it."

"Then they can ask me directly."

"They sent me." Her eyes were steady and entirely too interested. "They also said a stranger waded out at dawn and hauled something heavy through the reeds, and they wanted to know what, and whether anyone else has seen it."

"And what did you tell them?"

"I told them I'd find out." She glanced past me, up the bank, toward the willows, and I watched her do the geometry — the drag marks, the flattened reeds, the direction — without moving her feet. Good. Very good. "Sefa," she said, offering nothing else. No surname. Guides out here drop their surnames the way pilots drop altitude before weather.

"Noor." I didn't offer mine either, which she noticed, and something moved at the corner of her mouth that might have been approval.

"So, Noor. What did you pull out of the river?"

"A chest."

The word left me before I'd decided to spend it, and I watched her take the hit — a small stillness, a recalculation behind the eyes, the way a compass needle jumps when you bring iron near. She'd been expecting driftwood. She'd been expecting anything but that.

"What kind of chest?"

"Iron-banded. Sealed. Old."

"Sealed how?"

"Lead. Stamped." I let that sit. "With a bird."

For a moment neither of us breathed. Somewhere upriver a kingfisher hit the water and the ordinary world went on without us. When Sefa spoke again her voice had changed — lower, flatter, the voice of someone setting down something fragile.

"Who else has seen it?"

"Nobody. I covered it. I haven't opened it."

"Don't." It came out fast, hard, and then she caught herself, and I watched her rebuild the calm brick by brick, and that frightened me more than the shout would have. People who are careful about being afraid have usually been afraid before, recently, for reasons. "Open seals get reported. District office logs every manifest. A sealed chest is salvage. An opened one is evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"That's not my question to answer." But her face answered it anyway, briefly, before she shut the door — and what crossed it wasn't curiosity. It was grief. Quick and total, like a bird hitting glass.

We stood there in the long light, two strangers holding half a secret each, and I realized we were circling each other exactly like weather fronts — hers coming off the mountains cold and heavy, mine off ten empty years, and somewhere in the middle of us a pressure dropping fast.

"The family," I said carefully. "Do they fish that bend often?"

"Not anymore." She looked away, upriver, toward the canyon country where the water comes from. "Not since the flood took the low fields. They keep the nets in out of habit more than hope." A beat. "Habit's a kind of keeping, out here."

"Is that what you're doing? Keeping?"

Her eyes came back to me, and for a second the whole careful structure slipped and I saw underneath: exhausted, watchful, and lonely in a way I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. Then it was gone, shuttered, professional.

"I'm doing my job," she said. "Which is finding out what strangers pull out of dead villages." She stuck out her hand — calloused, rope-scarred, steady. "Come by the landing tomorrow. Bring the chest or bring a lie about the chest. Either way, we should talk before someone less polite asks you the same questions."

I shook it. Her grip was dry and strong and brief, and I registered — with the distant irritation of a man watching his own instruments drift off their calibration — that I wouldn't mind shaking it again.

"And if I just fly out?" I asked. "Medicine run waits for no one."

"Then you'll be carrying it with you," she said, "and everything that comes with it, into whatever sky you're hiding in." She turned and started down the bank, then paused, not looking back. "It was sealed for a reason, Galloway. Rivers don't lose things. They deliver them."

I stood there a long time after she was gone, watching the water move brown and indifferent past the willows, thinking about delivery routes.

Then I walked up to the brush pile, knelt down, and put my ear against the lid — which is insane, which is the behavior of a man who has been alone too long — and listened, as if the chest might tell me whose side of the weather front it was on.

From the strip, that night, I could see the landing lights of the downstream town, and beyond them, faint, the running lights of a boat working upstream against the current, slow and methodical, sweeping the banks with a searchlight.

Somebody was already looking.

And the seal was still unbroken, and so, barely, was I — but I lay awake in the Kestrel's cargo bay with the river talking to itself in the dark, and I understood that both of those conditions had an expiration date, and that the woman with the rope-scarred hands had known it before I did.
