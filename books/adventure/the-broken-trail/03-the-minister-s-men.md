## 3. The Minister's Men

I knew the government had come to Kestrel's Landing before I saw a single official face. You can always tell. The river changes.

Not the water itself — the water doesn't care who's in power — but the way people stand near it. On the morning the ministerial launch nosed up against our dock, the fishermen who'd spent forty years pretending the government didn't exist were suddenly very interested in their nets, and the nets were suddenly very interesting back. Old Petra closed her tea stall an hour early, which she only does for funerals and tax collectors, and this was neither.

The launch was white and too clean, the kind of clean that says *we have never once been rained on*, which in this country is either a miracle or a lie. It flew no flag. It didn't need to.

I'd been expecting them since Noor Galloway hauled that chest out of the shallows three days ago. Not because I'm psychic — because I know what rivers do with secrets, and I know what governments do when secrets surface. My family spent two generations learning that lesson the expensive way. The tuition was paid in full.

So when the man in the pale linen suit stepped onto our dock like he was stepping into his own garden, I did what my mother taught me: I became furniture. I picked up a coil of rope and started coiling it, badly, with tremendous concentration.

He was handsome in the way that makes you check your pockets afterward. Silver at the temples, arranged rather than earned. A smile calibrated to the millimeter — warm enough to open doors, cool enough to close them behind you. He shook hands with Dockmaster Ilves, who came away from the handshake looking like a man who'd agreed to something without knowing what.

"Corven Hale," he said, loud enough for the whole dock. "Deputy Ministry of Interior Affairs. Just passing through on an inspection tour."

Nobody believed him. He knew nobody believed him. That was part of the performance — the charm of a man confident enough not to bother lying well.

* * *

I watched him work the town for two hours before he got to Noor, and it was like watching weather move. He bought drinks at the Broken Oar and remembered everyone's names by the second round. He complimented the fishing, the bridge repair, a child's missing tooth. By noon, half of Kestrel's Landing would have lent him money, and the other half was already rehearsing how they'd tell the story later.

The other half included me. I made myself scarce the way you do when your family name has a file attached to it in some ministry basement. Sefa. Keeper family. Deceased. All accounts closed. Except accounts like ours don't close; they just stop being updated.

But then Hale walked down the dock toward Noor's hangar, and I discovered that my legs had opinions my brain hadn't approved. I followed. Furniture doesn't follow people, but nobody was checking.

Noor was under the plane's cowling when we arrived, elbow-deep in engine, wearing the expression they wear around other human beings — polite, distant, already halfway gone. They looked up at Hale the way they look at a storm front on the horizon: assessing distance, estimating arrival time.

"Deputy Minister," Noor said. Not a question. Bush pilots hear about government launches faster than anyone; half the interior reports to them via radio gossip.

"Pilot Galloway." Hale spread his hands like a man presenting a gift. "I'll be honest with you, because I respect honesty in professionals."

That was the first lie, delivered beautifully.

"There's been a salvage recovery on this stretch of river. An item of historical interest. The ministry is prepared to offer — generously — for its return." He named a figure. I won't repeat it here because numbers like that shouldn't be spoken aloud in towns where a good winter is one where nobody starves. It was enough to buy every plane on this dock, the dock itself, and the dock owner's forgiveness.

Noor wiped their hands slowly on a rag. "What kind of historical interest?"

"A chest. Sealed. Bearing a crest you may have seen on old coins or older gravestones." Hale smiled. "Vaskaria. A charming footnote. The ministry collects footnotes."

"And if I haven't seen any such chest?"

Hale's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a shutter, a recalculation. "Then I've wasted a lovely morning, and I'll buy you dinner anyway as an apology."

"No thank you."

"Suit yourself." He turned to go, then paused, artfully, the way actors pause. "You know, Pilot Galloway, Vaskaria wasn't erased by treaty because it was inconvenient. It was erased because it was dangerous. Do you know how many people died making sure that country stayed buried? I do. I've read the files." He let that sit in the air between them like a dropped knife. "History is heavy, friend. Best to let the river carry it."

He walked off toward the launch, pausing to wave at Petra's empty tea stall as though it had waved first.

Noor stood very still under the cowling. I watched their knuckles whiten on the rag. When they finally spoke, they didn't look at me, which meant they'd known I was there the whole time.

"You heard that."

"I hear most things."

"How many people died, Sefa?" Their voice was flat, but flatness in bush pilots is a load-bearing wall. Take it out and everything comes down.

"More than the files say," I said. "Files are written by survivors, and the survivors were all on the same side."

They set down the rag with exaggerated care, the way you put down something that might go off. Which, I suppose, is exactly what they were doing.

* * *

That evening I found Noor at the Broken Oar, in the corner seat with sightlines to both doors, drinking exactly one beer at the pace of a man defusing it. I sat across from them uninvited. They didn't object, which from Noor is practically a marriage proposal.

"He knows," Noor said quietly.

"He suspects. There's a difference, and right now it's the width of a hair."

"The fisherman who saw me pull it up. Tam. He talks."

"Tam talks about fish. This is bigger than fish." I leaned in. "Listen to me. Hale didn't come out here for a footnote. Footnotes get handled by clerks with letters. A Deputy Minister personally travels four days upriver for one thing only — because whatever's in that chest can hurt someone important enough to matter. Maybe him."

Noor stared into their beer. "You knew what that crest was. First day, on the dock. You asked who else had seen it before you even asked what it was."

"I asked because I know what happens to people who are seen with Vaskarian things." My voice came out lower than I intended. "My family kept Vaskarian records for two hundred years. Papers, photographs, names. We passed them hand to hand so no single person could be caught holding the whole truth. And then the treaty came, and the men with lists came, and my parents put me on a boat downstream with a satchel I wasn't allowed to open, and told me to forget the word Vaskaria." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I was nine. I've been forgetting it professionally ever since."

Noor looked at me for a long moment. In the lamplight their face did something complicated — the look of a person recalculating a map they thought they'd memorized.

"You never said."

"You never asked. That's the arrangement out here. Nobody asks."

"That's not an arrangement," Noor said. "That's just loneliness with better manners."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I drank my beer, and we sat in the noise of the bar like two people standing in the eye of something.

"Here's what I can't stop doing," Noor said finally. "Math. If I give it to Hale, I'm rich and safe and I never think about it again. If I burn it, I'm poor and safe and I never think about it again. Both options end with *never thinking about it again.* That used to be the whole point of my life."

"And now?"

"Now there's a third column in the equation and I hate it." They drained the beer. "Because I opened it, Sefa. Last night. And it isn't history in there. It's arithmetic. Names. Living names."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the pilings, into the cold river. "You opened it without me?"

"I was going to—"

"Don't." I stood up. Around us the bar went on laughing, and I felt the old family dread settle over my shoulders like a coat that still fit. "Tomorrow. First light. We open it properly, together, and then you and I are going to have a conversation about what you're willing to lose. Because Hale offered you a fortune today, Noor, and men like Corven Hale don't offer fortunes for things they plan to leave alive."

* * *

I want it noted, for whatever record survives me, that I slept badly for reasons entirely unrelated to premonition. Premonition implies mystery. There was no mystery. I lay in my bunk above the boathouse listening to the river and doing my own arithmetic: one chest, one pilot, one deputy minister, one keeper's kid who should have stayed downstream.

I was still awake at two in the morning when I heard the hangar.

Not a sound, exactly. Hangars don't make sounds at night. It was the absence of one — the dog that didn't bark, the generator hum that stuttered for three seconds and resumed. I was out my window and down the ladder before my boots finished deciding, moving along the tree line the way my father taught me, weight on the outside of the feet, breath through the nose.

Two figures inside, working by penlight. Methodical. Professional. One held open a canvas bag while the other went through Noor's charts, toolboxes, the cot, the floor seams. Looking for the chest. Finding nothing, because Noor — cautious, paranoid, instrument-trusting Noor — had moved it somewhere even I couldn't guess, and I suddenly loved them a little for it.

The taller one spoke, barely a breath. "Not here. He'll want the pilot questioned."

"Questioned," the other repeated, in a tone that made the word sound like a tool with a sharp edge.

They slipped out the back toward the water, and I pressed into the shadow of a fuel drum and let them pass within arm's reach. Close enough to smell cigarette smoke and gun oil. Close enough to see the ministry crest embossed discreetly on the canvas bag.

I stood in the dark a long time after they were gone, heart going like a floatplane at takeoff.

Then I crossed the yard, knocked on Noor's door in the pattern we'd agreed on that afternoon — two, pause, three — and when they opened it, sleepless, dressed, a crowbar in one hand and the fear finally visible in their eyes, I said the only true thing left:

"They didn't find it. But they'll be back before dawn, and next time they won't be looking."

Noor looked past me at the black river, and I watched ten years of careful distance collapse into a single decision.

"Get your bag," they said. "We're opening that chest tonight."
