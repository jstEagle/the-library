## 4. Paper Kingdoms

The chest smelled like a church that had drowned.

That was my first thought, kneeling on the floor of Sefa's boathouse at midnight with the padlock finally giving up its argument to a cold chisel and my worse patience. Brine and cedar and something older underneath — candle smoke, maybe, or paper that had been waiting so long it had started praying.

"You could have let me do that," Sefa said. They were sitting cross-legged across from me with a hurricane lamp between us, arms folded, wearing the expression of someone watching a surgeon operate with a chainsaw.

"And dull my one talent."

"Locksmithing isn't a talent."

"It is when you're bad at everything else." I set the chisel down. My hands weren't steady, and I was glad of the dark to hide it in. "You want to do the honors?"

Sefa didn't move. In the lamplight their face had gone careful, the way water goes still before it freezes. "Once we open it," they said, "we can't un-open it."

"I've heard that about oysters too."

"I'm serious, Galloway."

"So am I. Mostly." I wiped my palms on my jeans. The truth was I'd been circling this chest for two days the way I circled weather — reading it, respecting it, refusing to commit. A man doesn't survive ten years in the interior by opening boxes that other people are willing to kill over. A man survives by flying above them. But there's a difference between caution and cowardice, and somewhere around hour forty of not sleeping, I'd stopped being able to tell which one I was practicing.

"Fine," Sefa said, and reached past me, and lifted the lid.

* * *

Inside, on top, was a child's shoe.

Leather, small enough to fit in my palm, stitched with a pattern of interlocking waves that I recognized without knowing why — some emblem from a textbook, half-remembered, the way you remember the shape of a country that no longer exists. Under the shoe: paper. Dozens of ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft as string cheese. Photographs in a tin that had kept them dry through whatever voyage this chest had taken, downriver, under bridges, past towns that pretended not to see it float.

Sefa unwrapped the first ledger with hands that shook only a little, which was more than mine were doing.

"Census," they said quietly. "Vaskarian census. Look at the dates." They turned it toward me. Columns of names, ages, occupations, villages — a whole nation filed out in neat ink. *Mirela Vasko, weaver. Tomas Vasko, ferryman. Ilse Vasko, age four.* The handwriting changed every few pages, different clerks, different years, the same patient record-keeping going back decades. Somebody had believed, right up until the end, that writing things down would keep them safe.

"That's the crest on the cover," I said. "Same as the seal on the chest."

"The double wave." Sefa traced it with one finger. "River meeting mountain. My grandmother used to sing a song about it. I thought it was a lullaby." They laughed, short and wrong. "It wasn't a lullaby."

I pulled the tin of photographs toward me and pried the lid. Faces looked up out of the lamplight. Families on doorsteps. Boys holding fish bigger than their legs. A wedding party squinting into a sun that stopped shining eighty years ago. And one photograph near the bottom that made my chest go tight: a whole town photographed from the hillside above it, rooftops and steeples and a bridge over white water, and written on the border in faded pencil, *Selva-on-the-Fork, spring.*

I knew that bend in the river. I'd flown over it four hundred times. There was nothing there now but a lake the color of bottle glass and, on clear days, if you knew where to look, the ghost of a steeple under thirty feet of green water.

"They flooded it," I said. My voice came out flat, which was how I knew I was angry, because when I'm actually calm I sound interested. "The government. They put a dam on it and called it hydroelectric and drowned an entire—"

"Read the letter," Sefa said.

It was at the very bottom, folded into eighths, protected by wax that cracked like eggshell. The handwriting was old-fashioned, formal, the kind taught in schools that no longer existed. Sefa read it aloud because I asked them to; my eyes had gone unreliable.

*To whoever the river chooses —*

*If you are reading this, then the water has done what we asked of it, and carried our testimony past the men who burned the archives and renamed our valleys and told the world we were never here. They will call us rebels, bandits, a fiction. We are none of these. We are the people of the double wave, and we are not all dead.*

*The heirs live. Mirela's line did not end at Selva-on-the-Fork. What remains of us keeps to the high country where the water is born, beyond the canyon, past the maps' last honesty. If your heart is true, carry this word upstream. If your heart is afraid — and it will be, because theirs is a long arm — burn this letter and grieve us quietly, as the world has agreed to do.*

*But know what was done. Know who signed the order. Know that the Ministry of Interior Affairs did not merely permit the drowning of Vaskaria. It planned it, funded it, and buried the census so no one could count the missing.*

*We were counted anyway.*

There was a signature, and beneath the signature, a list. Names of ministers. Dates of payments. Requisitions for explosives marked *agricultural survey.* One of the names, three lines from the bottom, in ink darker than the rest, added later by a different hand:

*Corven Hale, Deputy Minister — continuation of policy, present day.*

I sat back on my heels. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the river kept doing what rivers do, which is everything, always, whether anyone watches or not.

"Well," I said. "That's a grenade with a ribbon on it."

* * *

We didn't talk for a while after that. There's a particular silence that falls between two people who have just learned the same terrible thing, and it isn't comfortable, but it isn't empty either. It's load-bearing.

Sefa broke it first. "My family kept records too. Smaller ones. Births, marriages, the old words." They were repacking the photographs with exaggerated care, as if tidiness could hold anything together. "They died keeping them. All three. House fire. Official cause: faulty wiring." Their mouth twisted. "In a house with no electricity."

"Sefa—"

"Don't." They closed the tin. "Don't do the face. I've had ten years of people doing the face."

So I didn't. Instead I said the thing I actually thought, which is a habit I usually manage to suppress. "Hale offered me forty thousand for this chest. He said salvage rights. He said historical artifacts, museum quality, no questions." I picked up the letter, read the list again. "He wasn't buying history. He was buying the only copy of his own confession."

"Now you understand."

"I understood before. I just hoped I was wrong. Hoping is cheaper than knowing." I stood, and my knees cracked, and the sound was absurdly loud in the little boathouse. "Here's what I know how to do, Sefa. I know how to file flight plans. I know how to read cloud formations and fuel gauges and the exact angle at which a river bends too sharply to trust. That's the whole inventory. I don't know anything about heirs or hidden valleys or bringing down ministries."

"No," Sefa said. "But you know where the water is born."

I looked at them. They were lit from below by the lamp, and behind them the chest sat open like a mouth, and outside the night pressed against the boathouse windows the way it always does up here — total, indifferent, full of room to disappear into. Ten years I'd treated that darkness as a friend. Room to vanish. Altitude as a personality.

And now some clerk-dead weaver named Mirela Vasko was asking me to fly straight into the one place on the map where disappearing wouldn't save anybody, least of all me.

"The canyon's unnavigable past Kettle Bend," I said. "Everyone knows that."

"Everyone official knows that."

"There's no strip. Nowhere to land. I'd be leaving the plane."

"Yes."

I turned the letter over in my hands. *If your heart is afraid — and it will be.* Whoever wrote that had known strangers pretty well.

"Give me until dawn," I said. "I think better with instruments. Numbers. Distance measured honestly." I almost smiled. "And I think better angry, and I'm getting there."

Sefa blew out the lamp. In the sudden black, their voice came close and quiet. "For what it's worth, Galloway — I hope you decide to come. But I'm going either way. Alone, if I have to."

"I know," I said. "That's the part that scares me."

Not the ministry. Not Hale's long arm, not the canyon, not the drowning of a nation. That. The fact that for the first time in ten years, somebody's leaving mattered to me — and that I already knew, lying awake in the dark of that boathouse listening to the river run uphill toward its source, exactly which way dawn was going to find me pointed.

Upstream.

God help me. Upstream.
