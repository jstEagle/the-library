## 5. Fuel, Lies, and Departure

There are three kinds of lies a river guide tells in a lifetime: the ones that keep you alive, the ones that keep other people alive, and the ones you tell yourself at two in the morning while the water talks to itself outside your window. I'd spent most of mine on the first kind. The second kind had gotten my family killed. It was the third kind I was about to become fluent in.

The fuel clerk at Meridian Strip was named Oskar, and Oskar had exactly two interests in life: aviation fuel and the belief that everyone else was lying to him. This made him exhausting and, under normal circumstances, useless. But it also made him cheap to bribe, because a man who assumes everyone lies will happily accept money to do it himself. It confirms his worldview. People pay good money for that.

"Two drums," Noor said, sliding the chit across the counter. "Survey work. Upper tributary, past Kessel Bend."

Oskar looked at the chit. He looked at Noor. He looked at me, standing by the door doing my best impression of a woman with no opinions, which is harder than it sounds when your whole body is screaming them.

"Nobody surveys past Kessel Bend," Oskar said. "Kessel Bend's where surveys go to die."

"New ministry contract," Noor said, with the flat boredom of someone reading a cereal box. "Hydrographic. You want to call the ministry and check?"

I watched Okar weigh this. Calling the ministry meant talking to the ministry, and men like Oskar would rather drink fuel than do that. He stamped the chit. "You crash up there, I never sold you anything."

"We were never here," Noor agreed, and for one absurd moment they almost smiled at each other, two liars recognizing craft.

Outside, the morning was doing that thing the interior does in late summer — hanging its heat over everything like a wet blanket, making the air feel borrowed. Noor loaded the drums in silence, and I stood there holding the weather report I'd paid forty dollars and one very awkward favor for, feeling the weight of it like a stone in my pocket.

* * *

The weather station at Vell Hill was run by a man named Dushko who had once, according to local legend, correctly predicted a storm three days out and never let anyone forget it. He was also, according to less flattering legend, the kind of man who could be bought with a bottle of plum brandy and a sympathetic ear. I brought both.

"The front coming down from the pass," I said, after the brandy had done its opening work. "How bad?"

Dushko squinted at his own instruments like they owed him money. "Bad enough. Why? Nobody flies into that."

"Somebody might."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I saw him do the arithmetic — the guide, the pilot, the fuel drums, the direction nobody goes. Dushko wasn't smart, but he was old, and old men in this country know what upstream means. His family had been on the river as long as mine. Maybe longer. Maybe his grandmother had sung the songs mine did, back before singing them became a way to die.

"You're going to the water," he said quietly. Not a question. Not quite an accusation either. Something in between, the way grief is always somewhere between those two things.

"I'm going flying," I said.

He refilled my glass. "Storm hits the pass tomorrow night. Day after, visibility drops to nothing for a week." He tapped the logbook on his desk. "Tomorrow morning, I record clear conditions until noon. After noon, I record whatever keeps questions away." He pushed the brandy bottle toward me. "For the flight. And Sefa—" he said my name carefully, the way you handle something that might still be sharp, "—if you find what your mother was looking for, don't bring it back down. Bring yourself back down. That's all she'd ask."

I hadn't mentioned my mother. I didn't have to. That's the thing about river towns: we're all carrying pieces of the same wreck, and sometimes somebody recognizes the splinter in your hand because they've got the matching scar.

I took the bottle. My hands were steadier than I expected them to be.

* * *

Noor's plane — the *Wren*, they called her, though officially she was some string of numbers only the registry loved — sat in the hangar like a patient animal that knows something's wrong. Noor checked her over twice, three times, running their hands along the struts the way you'd touch a sleeping child's hair. I pretended to pack supplies and watched them instead.

"You could stay," I said. It came out before I could stop it, which is how the truest things usually arrive. "Fly home. Give Hale the chest back, tell him you found it empty. He'd believe you. You're not the type he worries about."

"And you are?" Noor didn't look up from the fuel gauge.

"My family was the type he worries about. It's genetic."

That landed. I saw it land. Noor closed the fuel cap with more force than it needed and finally turned around, and there was something raw in their face that ten years of flying hadn't managed to sand off.

"I've spent a decade making sure there's nothing in my life that can be taken from me," they said. "Do you understand what that means? It means nothing gets kept. Nothing gets planted. You don't leave things anywhere, because leaving things is how you end up with a life you can lose." They gestured at the plane, the hangar, the whole tidy architecture of their solitude. "And now I'm standing here calculating how much fuel it takes to fly toward the people who burned a country off the map, and I can't make the math come out to sensible. So don't ask me if I could stay. Ask me why I'm not."

I didn't have an answer that wouldn't have been a confession, so I zipped the bag shut instead. Some silences are agreements. We were getting fluent in those too.

* * *

We heard about Tomas's boat before we saw it.

Tomas fished the lower shallows and sold information the way other men sold fish — small portions, fresh daily. He'd tipped us off about Hale's men renting rooms at the landing inn, about the satellite phone they carried, about the deputy minister's habit of walking the docks at dawn like he owned the tide. Tomas was careful. Tomas had survived twenty years of being careful.

Hale's men found his boat tied at the north dock at dusk and set it alight with him standing close enough to watch. They didn't touch Tomas. That was the message. A boat burns in minutes; a reputation burns slower, and they wanted him to live inside the smoke for a while.

We got there after the crowd had thinned, after the fire crew had given up pretending and gone home. The hull was a black ribcage against the dark water, and the smell of char hung over the dock like a held breath. Tomas sat on a crate, wrapped in someone's coat, staring at the water where his boat used to be reflected.

"They wanted me to see it," he said when we stopped beside him. His voice was steady, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "Forty years that boat kept me fed. Forty years, and it took them four minutes." He looked up at us, and his eyes were dry and terribly clear. "They know someone talked. They don't know who. Yet."

"Sefa," Noor started, low, "we should—"

"No," I said. "Don't say it. Don't say we should wait, or reconsider, or any of the reasonable words. Because tomorrow morning there won't be a reason left that isn't fear, and fear is exactly what they're selling." I crouched next to Tomas. "Did they take anything?"

"Nothing worth taking." He almost smiled. "Everything worth taking, I moved last week. Old habits." He pressed something into my palm — a brass river-token, the kind keepers used to trade between stations, back when the stations existed. My mother had worn one just like it around her neck until the day men came who didn't care about tokens or songs or anything except silence. "For luck," Tomas said. "Or spite. Whichever works."

* * *

We flew at dawn, under Dushko's clean blue lie, with the drums full and the chest wrapped in oilcloth in the cargo hold and my heart doing something complicated against my ribs. Noor flew the way they did everything — precise, economical, like a person rationing themselves. Below us the river unspooled silver through the green, going the wrong way, going up, going home to wherever it was born.

Past Kessel Bend the valley narrowed. Past the old survey markers it narrowed again. By early afternoon the canyon walls had come together overhead like a book closing, and Noor set the *Wren* down on the last strip of gravel that could hold her — a shelf of rock above black water, barely long enough, hemmed in by stone that went up and up until the sky was just a bright seam.

Noor cut the engine and the silence that followed was enormous. Canyon silence. The kind of quiet that has weight.

We unloaded in the gold light: packs, rope, the wrapped chest, food for eight days, medicine for worse. Then Noor stood beside the plane for a long moment, one hand flat on her flank, and I understood I was watching a funeral. Ten years of distance, parked on a gravel shelf in the middle of nowhere, and every hour we walked upstream made it harder to walk back.

"She'll be fine," I said. "Nobody comes here."

"That's what I'm counting on." Noor shouldered their pack. Their face was pale in the canyon light, but their voice was level. "Last chance to turn around, Sefa."

"There's no turning around. There hasn't been since the chest came out of the water, and you know it. Everything since has just been packing."

Noor looked at me for a long moment — measuring, maybe, or memorizing, the way pilots memorize a runway before committing. Then they turned upstream, toward the seam of light between the walls, and started walking, and I followed, because following them had apparently become the truest thing I knew how to do.

Behind us the *Wren* shrank to a speck, then to a memory. Ahead, somewhere past the rapids and the rockfall and whatever else the canyon was keeping, the water was being born — and so, I suspected, were we.

We'd made perhaps two miles when Noor stopped so abruptly I walked into their shoulder. They were staring at the canyon wall ahead, at a mark painted high on the stone, half-erased by decades of weather but unmistakable to anyone raised on keeper stories.

A spiral. Blue and white. The sign that said *someone is watching this place*, and that the someone was not the government.

It was fresh.
