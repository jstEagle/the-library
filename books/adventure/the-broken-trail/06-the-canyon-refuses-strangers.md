## 6. The Canyon Refuses Strangers

The canyon announced itself the way bad news does — quietly at first, then all at once.

We'd left the plane at a strip of gravel Sefa called an airstrip and I called optimism with grass growing through it. From there it was the river or nothing: two days by raft through country so vertical the sun only visited for an hour around noon, like a landlord checking on tenants he regretted renting to.

"Name of this stretch?" I asked, pushing off from the bank.

Sefa didn't look up from the oars. "The Canyon Refuses Strangers."

"That's not a name, that's a warning."

"It's both. Vaskarians named things honestly." They shipped the oars once, twice, testing the current's mood. "Everything past here has a name that doubles as a eulogy."

I'd flown over this country for ten years. From three thousand feet it was beautiful in the abstract way — blue shadows, white water stitched thin as thread. Up close it was loud. The river didn't run here; it fell, in stages, furious about each one, and the walls closed in until I could have spat across the gap and hit rock on both sides.

I am, by profession and temperament, a person who trusts instruments. Airspeed, altitude, heading — numbers don't lie to you, they just tell you when you're about to die, which is more than most friends offer. Rivers have no instruments. A river is all instinct and grudge, and we were asking it to carry us and a waterproof duffel full of the most dangerous paper in the country through its own throat.

* * *

The first rapids nearly took us before lunch.

Sefa read the water like I read weather — three seconds ahead, always adjusting — but reading isn't controlling, and the river had opinions. We went through a standing wave sideways, took most of it into the boat, and came out the other side spinning while Sefa swore in Vaskarian, a language that apparently was invented specifically for cursing geology.

"You could row," they said, wringing out their shirt.

"I'm ballast. Ballast doesn't row."

"Ballast can bail." They shoved a dented pot at me. I bailed. It felt honest, somehow — scooping the river back out of our lives one potful at a time, knowing it would keep coming in.

That was the thing about Sefa I kept failing to file away properly. Ten years I'd built a life with no attachments heavier than fuel receipts, and here was this person, soaked to the ribs, laughing at a wave that had almost killed us, and something in my chest kept doing a maneuver I hadn't cleared with myself.

Portage number two was worse than the rapid itself. Half a mile of carrying a raft and gear over talus slopes while the canyon narrowed around us like a closing fist. My shoulders were screaming by the end. Sefa carried the duffel with the documents strapped to their chest, refusing every offer to share the weight.

"It stays on my body," they said. "That was the arrangement."

"The arrangement was made before I knew what was in it."

"So was yours," they said, and I didn't have an answer, because they were right. I'd agreed to fly upstream before I'd opened the chest. Whatever I'd been agreeing to, I'd agreed blind, which for a pilot is either faith or suicide depending on how the landing goes.

* * *

The rockslide came on day two, and it did not come quietly.

We heard it first — a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, deep and grinding, somewhere above the rim. Then dust boiled down the far wall, and rocks the size of trucks came with it, and the river stood up.

I've been in turbulence that threw me against my harness. I've landed in crosswinds that bent the rules of what wings are for. None of it prepared me for water moving sideways because the earth told it to. The wave caught the raft and lifted it, and I remember thinking, with the strange bookkeeping clarity of disaster: *this is where the ledger closes.*

Then the world flipped.

Cold. Total, absolute cold, the kind that erases thought. The river had me and I was cargo now, tumbling, and my boots found nothing, and my hands found nothing, and somewhere above the surface there was air and sunlight and a person I'd known four days, and none of those things were reaching me.

What reached me was an oar.

It came out of the chaos like a lifeline thrown by someone who'd already decided I was worth the throw. I grabbed it and Sefa hauled — I felt the whole force of them through the wood, leaning back against the current with their heels dug into the raft frame — and then hands, arms, and I was over the gunwale and coughing up half the canyon onto the floorboards while Sefa held the raft straight through the tail of the rapid with the other hand.

We eddied out below a bend, shaking. Or I shook. Sefa just sat there breathing hard, staring at me with an expression I couldn't parse — fury wearing fear's coat, or the other way around.

"You let go of the line," they said finally.

"There wasn't a line."

"There's always a line. You tie yourself in on this water. Any guide who's worked a season knows that. You—" Their voice cracked, which startled me more than the wave had. "You were under for eleven seconds. I counted. I was counting and I couldn't reach you and the river was deciding whether to keep you."

"Sefa—"

"Don't." They turned away, hands still white-knuckled on the oar. "Don't say thank you. Don't say it was fine. I watched the water take you and I thought, *there it is, again, the river taking someone who knew too much,* and I couldn't tell if I was grieving you or grieving the documents, and I hated myself for not knowing."

* * *

We camped that night on a gravel shelf barely wider than the raft, under a sky so crowded with stars it looked like the aftermath of something. I built a fire small enough to hide. Sefa sat with their knees drawn up, and the silence between us had weight, the kind you can stack things on.

So I said the stupid thing. "Tell me about your family."

They looked at me like I'd asked them to open a vein. Then, maybe because we'd both nearly died twelve hours ago and the usual defenses seemed beside the point, they talked.

"My grandmother was a keeper. That was the word — *dëshmorë*, the ones who remembered. When Vaskaria was erased, the treaty didn't just redraw maps. It unmade records. Birth registries burned. Names struck from census rolls. An entire nation edited out of existence, and the ministry called it administrative cleanup." Their voice was flat, recitation-flat, the tone of a story told so many times it's worn smooth. "My family kept what the fires missed. Documents, photographs, songs. Passed down like contraband, because that's what it was. My father carried papers through these exact canyons. He died doing it — less than what's in that duffel. A letter. One letter naming one family. They drowned him for it and called the inquest an accident."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, water fell over rock, patient as arithmetic.

"And you kept going," I said. "After that."

"What else was there? Grief needs a job or it eats the house." They glanced at me. "You'd know."

I would know. Ten years of flying medicine and fuel into places no road bothered with, because a cockpit is the only room where nobody asks why you're leaving. I'd told myself it was freedom. Altitude as armor. But freedom that requires constant motion is just grief with a propeller, and I'd known that for years without letting the knowledge land.

"They died for less," Sefa said. "My whole family, for less than what you're carrying. And you treat it like freight."

"What would you have me treat it like?"

"Like it matters! Like people bled for every page in that bag and you're just — ferrying it. The way you ferry everything. Detached. Professional. Like if you don't care, it can't cost you." They were on their feet now, firelight jumping on their face. "Well, news, Noor: it costs. It cost me everyone. It will cost you too, the moment you admit any of it matters, and you're so terrified of that bill you'd rather drown polite."

"That's not fair."

"No. It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. Hale's men burned a man's boat last week for talking to us. Mirela's people have lived in a hole in the mountains for twenty-five years. Fairness is downstream, if it exists at all." They dragged a hand through their wet hair. "So yes, I'm angry. I'm angry that I care whether you live, because caring about you is one more thing this country can take from me."

And there it was. Laid out on the gravel between us, sideways, in the middle of a fight neither of us wanted to win — because winning meant ending it, and ending it meant being alone on opposite sides of a fire in a canyon that refused strangers.

I should have said something careful. I said what was true instead.

"When I went under," I said, "the last thing I saw before the water closed was you reaching. Not the sky. Not the light. You. And some part of me that doesn't do instruments, doesn't do calculations, thought — *oh. So that's what the rope is for.*"

Sefa went very still.

"I spent ten years learning to need nothing," I said. "You just made it a wasted decade."

For a long moment neither of us moved. The river ran. The stars did their slow indifferent work overhead. Then Sefa crossed the gravel and sat down next to me, shoulder against shoulder, close enough that I could feel them shaking — not from cold, I realized. From the effort of having said everything they'd said and survived saying it.

"We argue like this again," they murmured, "and the canyon will hear us."

"Let it. It refuses strangers. We're not strangers anymore."

They huffed something that was almost a laugh, and leaned their head against mine, and for a while the fight stayed won by neither of us, which felt like the only honest outcome available.

* * *

Near dawn, Sefa woke me with a hand on my arm and a look I'd learned to dread in four short days.

"Smoke," they said. "Downriver. Woodsmoke and engine oil."

I crawled to the edge of the gravel and looked. Far below, where the canyon widened toward the valley mouth, a thread of gray rose against the paling sky. Campfires. More than one. And beneath the smell of burning wood, faint but unmistakable to anyone who'd spent a decade around bush strips, the smell of two-stroke fuel.

Outboard motors. This far upstream, where the canyon supposedly refused everything with an engine.

"Somebody's coming up the river," Sefa said quietly. "Somebody with money behind them."

I looked at the duffel, at the fire I'd have to kick apart, at the person beside me whom I'd known four days and intended to know considerably longer, and understood that whatever the canyon had refused so far, it hadn't yet met Corven Hale.

"We beat them to the source," I said. "Or we die trying. Those are the options now."

Sefa was already breaking camp.
