## 7. Where the Water Is Born

The canyon ended the way a held breath ends — not gradually, but all at once, the walls falling open and the river spilling into a valley so green it looked like an apology.

I killed the outboard and let us drift. Ten years of flying over this country had taught me every trick of light it owned, and I knew what I was seeing was impossible: terraced gardens climbing slopes that no survey map admitted existed, smoke rising thin and domestic from stone chimneys, a mill wheel turning on a side channel with the lazy confidence of something that had never once been bombed.

"Sefa," I said. My voice came out strange, like it belonged to someone else.

"I see it." Sefa stood in the bow, one hand on the gunwale, and for the first time since I'd met them — through suspicion, through the chest, through the rockslide that nearly ate us both two days ago — they looked afraid. Not of the valley. Of being seen by it.

"Your family were keepers," I said. "You knew."

"I knew there was a place past the last rapid. Knowing and seeing are different animals." They turned to me. "Whatever happens in the next hour, Noor — don't lie to them. They'll know. They always know."

"That's comforting."

"It's not meant to be."

We drifted toward a landing of flat stones where three figures waited. Two were young, armed with the kind of patience that doesn't need rifles, though they had those too, slung casual as fishing rods. The third was old enough that her spine had opinions about standing straight, but she stood straight anyway, white hair braided back from a face like weathered birch. Her eyes found the chest between my feet before they found my face.

"You brought them home," she said. Not to me. To the chest.

"Mirela," Sefa said, and their voice cracked on the name like ice on a spring river.

The old woman's expression didn't change, but something behind it did. "Little Sefa. You have your mother's way of arriving at the worst possible moment." She extended a hand, palm up, to me. "And you are the pilot. The one who pulled our dead out of the water."

"They weren't dead," I said. "They were waiting."

Mirela studied me for a long moment, the way you'd study an instrument reading that contradicted everything else on the panel. Then she laughed — one dry note, surprised out of her. "Come up, then. Both of you. And bring the children."

* * *

They fed us first. That's the Vaskarian way, Sefa had told me once — grief, negotiation, war, all of it comes after bread. We sat at a long table under a roof of hand-split shakes while the whole valley seemed to drift through in ones and twos, pretending not to stare at the strangers and failing completely. Children peeked around doorframes. An old man touched Sefa's shoulder as he passed and said nothing, which somehow said everything.

There were maybe two hundred of them. A generation born and raised inside a lie the size of a mountain range, in a valley the government's own maps marked as unrunnable canyon. Because it nearly was. Because the people who'd built the terraces and the mill and the schoolhouse with its slate roof had decided, thirty years ago, that being erased once was quite enough.

Mirela sat across from me and watched me eat like a scientist watching a control group.

"You fly," she said.

"Badly, according to my mechanic."

"You've flown this country for ten years. Alone. No partner, no home strip you stay longer than a season." It wasn't a question. Sefa had talked. Or Mirela simply read people the way I read cloud formations — instantly, and with a professional's contempt for wishful thinking.

"Home strips rust," I said. "Planes don't care where you sleep."

"No," Mirela agreed. "But people do." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "You opened the chest."

"With Sefa. Yes."

"And you read what's in it. All of it."

"Census rolls. Photographs. A letter naming heirs alive and hidden 'where the water is born.'" I shrugged, or tried to; it came out stiff, like a wing icing over. "And ministry correspondence proving Corven Hale's office didn't just bury Vaskaria after the treaty. They ran the operation. The drownings, the resettlements, the records burn. His signature's on the requisitions for the boats that never came back."

The table went very quiet. Somewhere outside, the mill wheel kept its patient turning.

"Then you understand," Mirela said, "why the documents cannot leave this valley."

* * *

The argument happened that evening in a stone room that served as council hall, archive, and — judging by the scorch marks — occasional kitchen. Mirela laid the documents out on the table with hands that trembled only slightly, and I realized she wasn't trembling from age. She was trembling the way you tremble holding something you've spent your whole life believing you'd never touch again.

"My grandmother is in this photograph," she said quietly. "Third row. She carried this census forty kilometers through snow with soldiers behind her, and she made it here, and she died here, and every year we say her name so the treaty can't finish its work." She looked up at me. "You want to carry these papers downstream to journalists, to courts, to a world that signed its name to our disappearance and called it peace."

"Not me specifically. Sefa—"

"Sefa grew up outside. Sefa knows what the outside does with truths it finds inconvenient." Mirela's voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "You show these to the world, and the world has two choices. Admit a crime enormous enough to shake every institution that profited from it, or make the evidence — and the witnesses — disappear. Institutions are cowards, pilot. Cowards choose the second option nine times out of ten. And the tenth time, they take so long deciding that Hale's men find this valley first."

"People survived Hale finding things before," I said.

"People *hid*. There's a difference, and the difference is measured in graves." She pressed both palms flat against the table, over the photographs, over the faces. "These pages are the only proof we exist. If they leave, we gamble everything. If they stay, we remain a rumor. A ghost story pilots tell each other over fuel pumps." Her eyes were wet and furious about it. "I will not spend my grandchildren's lives buying back my grandmother's name."

I should have argued harder. That's what I tell myself now, anyway. But the truth is I looked around that room — at the young scouts who'd never seen a city, at the schoolteacher who taught arithmetic from memory because books were dangerous, at Sefa standing by the door with their jaw set like a man watching his own funeral procession — and I understood something that ten years of altitude hadn't taught me.

Some things you can't outrun. Some things you have to land inside of.

"The chest came out of the river," I said finally. "Rivers go both directions. That's sort of their whole personality."

Mirela almost smiled. "Meaning?"

"Meaning I'm not proposing anything tonight. But I don't leave tomorrow either. Give me a few days. Let me think like a pilot instead of a courier." I tapped the table. "There's more than one way to move paper down a canyon. And there's more than one kind of proof. You're guarding the documents like they're the treasure. They're not. They're the *map*. And maps can be copied, memorized, sung. You can't shoot a song out of the sky."

Sefa made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Mirela looked at me for a long time. "You sound like a woman I knew," she said at last. "She also believed nothing was impossible if you refused to do the math properly." She gathered the documents back into the chest, gently, the way you'd tuck in a child. "Stay. Think. The valley has room, and the valley has questions, and you'll find we ask better ones than the government does."

* * *

That night they gave me a guest room in the mill house, and I lay awake listening to water going over the wheel, over and over, patient as breathing.

Ten years I'd told myself the sky was freedom. Ten years of wheels-up at dawn and nobody waiting at any strip, and I'd called that liberty because the alternative word was too heavy to lift. But lying there in a house full of sleeping strangers who shared a grandmother and a grievance and a recipe for bread, I felt something open in my chest that I didn't have instruments to read.

Belonging. That was the word. The heavy one.

And underneath it, quieter, the fine print I should have read before signing: belonging costs. It cost Mirela her grandmother. It cost Sefa their family. It would cost whoever carried the truth downstream — possibly everything, if Hale's expedition was already moving, and I had no reason to believe it wasn't. Satellite phones don't care how good your canyon is.

I got up and went to the window. Below, a lamp still burned in the council hall, and Mirela's silhouette moved behind it, awake too, keeping her vigil over paper and ash.

A shape detached itself from the shadows near the landing stones and started up the path toward the hall — quick, familiar, wrong. Sefa, at midnight, moving like someone with an errand they hadn't announced.

I was down the stairs and out the door before I'd decided to be, following the path along the channel, the mill wheel churning beside me, toward whatever Sefa intended to do that they couldn't do in daylight.

Because I'd seen that walk before. On my own face, in hangar mirrors, ten years ago, the night before I flew away from everything.

And some instincts you don't argue with.
