## 8. The Long Way Down

The valley woke us the way it always did: with water. Not rain — sound. The source falls in three threads off the glacier wall above the terraces, and by morning they'd braided themselves into a single voice that got into your chest and stayed there. I had grown up two hundred miles downstream and I still wasn't used to it. Noor slept through it like a stone, which told me everything about how tired they were, and how far from home.

I was on the eastern lookout with Danel's daughter when I saw the glint.

Not a bird. Birds don't hold still. This held still — a pinprick of silver hanging over the southern ridge, patient as a debt collector.

"Satellite," I said.

The girl — her name was Ilva and she could name every plant on the terraces but had never seen a traffic light — squinted at my finger. "What's a satellite?"

"A star that works for Corven Hale."

She laughed, because she was eleven and hadn't yet learned which jokes were true.

* * *

By noon the whole valley knew. Mirela convened the council under the great beam-roofed hall where the documents had been read aloud two nights before — census pages, photographs, the letter naming heirs where the water is born. The same hall where an old woman named Branka had stood up afterward and said, quietly, "My grandmother's recipes are in there. My grandmother is in there." The same hall where someone had then gone out into the dark and done what traitors do.

We knew because the satellite knew. Because the fishing party at the lower weir had found one of their cache barrels emptied and resealed with fresh pine pitch, badly. Because Tamás, who had taught half the valley's children to swim, had stopped meeting anyone's eyes.

Nobody said his name in the hall. They didn't have to. Mirela stood at the head of the long table with the chest open in front of her, papers fanned out like a hand of cards nobody wanted to play, and she looked older than she had four days ago. Older than the mountains, almost.

"Two roads," she said. Her Vaskarian was the old dialect, all gravel and vowels; Sefa-translation came automatic to me now, after a week of it. "We burn them. Everything. The census, the photographs, the letter. We go back to being a rumor, a story grandmothers tell. We live. Our children live. And the people who drowned our country keep their clean hands until they die of old age."

Silence. Somewhere outside, the water kept talking.

"Or we send them downriver," Mirela said, "and we are a nation again for approximately as long as it takes Corven Hale to get helicopters up this canyon. Which, given the weather window, is nine days."

An old man near the door — Ferenc, who mended the fish nets — said what everyone was thinking: "Then why are we choosing? Burn them. It isn't even a choice."

"It is always a choice," Mirela said. "That is what being alive means. The dead don't choose anything anymore." She put her palm flat on the table, next to a photograph of a family posed stiffly in front of a stone church that no longer existed except in that paper. "I will not decide this alone. But I will not pretend one road is free."

* * *

Noor didn't say much during the council. That should have warned me. Noor quiet is Noor calculating — I'd learned that over rapids and portages and one memorable argument about whether canned peaches constituted food. When the hall emptied, they stayed behind, turning a census page over slowly, like it might be hot.

"You're doing the thing," I said.

"What thing?"

"The face you made before you lied to the fuel clerk about our cargo weight."

"I didn't lie. I *reclassified*." They set the page down. "Sefa. How many boats does Hale have on the river?"

"Three that we know. Maybe six."

"And he knows the valley exists now. He doesn't know exactly where — Tamás gave him the canyon mouth, not the source. There's still a gap between what he knows and where we are." Noor tapped the map pinned to the beam, the one with the river drawn in Mirela's own hand, blue ink fading to ghost. "Gaps close. So we give him something to fill it with."

I felt it coming the way you feel weather in a bad knee. "Noor."

"Forged papers. Mirela has scribes who've been copying the real documents for a generation — practice copies, training material. Age them, damage them, make them convincing enough at arm's length. Then somebody carries them downstream, loud, visible, slow. Hale's whole expedition turns to chase the courier, because that's what ministers do — they chase the thing they can put in a report. While they're chasing—" 

"No."

"—you take the real ones out the other way. Up and over the pass with the scouts, down to the rail line, to the journalist network. Two weeks—"

"*No.*" It came out louder than I meant. A boy stacking firewood outside the hall turned to look. I lowered my voice and hated how it shook. "You are not volunteering to be bait. That's not — that's not how this goes."

"How does it go, then?" Noor asked, and their voice was gentle, which was worse. Gentle Noor was dangerous Noor. "Somebody from the valley does it? Ferenc? He's seventy. Ilva's father? He has a kid. You?" They let that sit. "You're the only person alive who can navigate the lower gorge blind, and if the decoy dies in the first set of rapids, the whole plan dies with them. It has to be someone who can actually survive the run. That's me or you, and I'm faster on water than you'll admit."

"Because I taught you," I said. "Everything you know about that river, I put there. I am not going to be the reason it kills you."

Noor was quiet for a moment. The fire cracked. Outside, the source fell and fell and fell, indifferent.

"Sefa," they said finally. "Ten years ago I decided the safest way to live was to belong to nothing. No town, no crew, no one waiting anywhere. It worked. It worked *great*. And then a chest came out of a river and ruined everything, because now there's something worth belonging to and it's full of people who will die if nobody acts, and I have spent ten years running from exactly this kind of math." They picked up the forged-copy plan like it weighed nothing, which was the lie of the year. "I'm not brave. I'm just done pretending distance is a virtue."

"That's the most words you've said since the rockslide."

"Don't change the subject."

"I'm not. I'm stalling, because if I stop stalling I have to agree with you, and I hate agreeing with you, it sets a precedent."

Noor smiled — small, crooked, the one I'd have followed anywhere, which was precisely the problem. "So you agree."

"I agree," I said, "and I'm going to spend every hour between now and your departure making sure the forgery is good enough that Hale believes it, and the route is safe enough that you come back, and if you die I'm going to be so insufferable about it that they'll carve it on your marker: *Here lies Noor Galloway, survived by Sefa, who told them so.*"

"There it is," Noor said softly. "Romance."

* * *

We worked three days. Mirela's scribes aged paper with tea and ash and careful tearing; I coached Noor on the courier's script — where to hesitate, when to look afraid, how to let a boat be seen without seeming to want to be seen. The scouts mapped the high pass for the real documents, a route that would take Noor's place in the story only after the decoy had pulled every eye south.

On the third night, Tamás came to the hall.

He stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, an old man made entirely of apology, and the room went cold around him. Mirela looked at him for a long time. I watched her decide something — watched grief and fury and something older than both move across her face like weather.

"They have my grandson," Tamás said simply. "They took him off the school boat in June. I have had one photograph since. You would have done the same."

Mirela crossed the room. Everyone tensed. She took the hat gently out of his hands and set it on the table, and she said, "Yes. I would have. That is why we do not kill you, and why you will now tell us everything you told them, so that we may use it better than they did."

Tamás wept like a child. From his information we learned Hale's timetable moved up — his lead boats would reach the lower canyon in two days, not five. The plan held, barely. The margin thinned to paper.

* * *

Departure was before dawn, because departures should be. Mist on the water thick enough to drink. Noor stood at the bow of the scout boat in borrowed river clothes, the forged chest lashed down, looking — I'll say this once and never again — smaller than I'd ever seen them.

"The pass party leaves tonight," I said, for the fourth time. "Real documents. If your radio dies, we assume success and proceed."

"If my radio dies, assume I'm being dramatic and wait a day."

"Sefa protocol. Understood."

They looked at me then, really looked, and whatever Noor Galloway had been before a chest floated into their life — pilot, ghost, professional stranger — it wasn't standing on that deck. What was standing there was someone with a home they'd chosen and a job they'd volunteered for and a person they hadn't managed to leave, despite a decade of expert practice.

"Hey," Noor said. "When this is over—"

"When this is over, you're unpacking. All of it. I've seen the duffel. There are socks in there from 2019."

"That's not what I was going to say."

"I know what you were going to say." I stepped close and pressed my forehead to theirs, the old way, the way my mother used to part from my father before he went downriver on nights the ministry's boats ran dark. "Say it on the water. Words carry better downstream."

Noor laughed, wet-eyed, pushed off, and the mist took the boat the way rivers take everything — gradually, then completely.

I stood on the bank until the sound of the engine faded into the sound of the falls, which is to say I stood there long past sensible. Then I turned uphill, toward the pass, toward the real papers and the long climb, and I made the only vow that mattered:

Come back, or I'm coming down that river after you, and I am *worse* than you are.

Somewhere below, in the fog, Corven Hale's boats were already moving north.

And so, God help us, was Noor.
