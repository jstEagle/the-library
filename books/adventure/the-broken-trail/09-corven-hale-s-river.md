## 9. Corven Hale's River

Fog on a river is a liar with excellent credentials. It tells you the water is empty, the banks are safe, and the world ends twenty meters from your nose. I've flown through enough weather to know better than to believe anything that smooth, but that morning, poling the decoy barge down the Vaskara's upper reaches with a crate of forged papers under my feet and a radio transmitter in my jacket that I was supposed to use only in extremis, I believed it anyway. I wanted to. Fog meant Hale's boats couldn't see me, which meant I was clever, which meant the plan was working, which meant Sefa was safe in the valley and I was the one holding the grenade with the pin pulled.

You can build a whole identity out of a sentence like that. I had, for about four hours.

The barge was a joke and we all knew it. Mirela's people had dressed it up as a medicine run — my medicine run, actually, the one legitimate crate I'd ever hauled, now riding shotgun with the forgeries stacked beneath it in a false bottom Sefa's cousin had built in a single feverish night. The theory was elegant: Hale's satellite people had watched us come upstream, so they'd be watching for us going down. Give them a target. Give them *me*. Let them take the papers, let them parade them to the ministry as proof the valley was a myth, and let the real documents travel the slow way — by river network, by fishing skiff, by the hands of people whose families had been keeping Vaskarian secrets since before Corven Hale learned to tie his own tie.

Elegant. Right up until the fog lifted.

* * *

It went all at once, the way fog does — one moment the river was a held breath, the next it was a corridor of gray-green water with walls of pine and granite, and I could see everything, including the two black-hulled boats sitting in the narrows a kilometer downstream like punctuation at the end of a sentence I did not want to read.

I did what any sensible pilot does when the weather closes: I turned. The barge turned with all the grace of a floating barn, which is to say eventually, and by then the boats had turned too, and one of them was fast in a way that government money buys and river guides don't bother faking.

I want it on the record that I did not panic. I *inventoried*. That's the pilot's version of panic — a rapid, cold-blooded accounting of assets. One barge, slow. One river, narrowing. One crate of fake documents, worthless except as a prop. One radio transmitter I'd been told to use only if things went truly wrong, and a growing suspicion that this was the moment it had been built for, except that using it would tell Sefa I was caught, and Sefa would do something heroic, and heroic was not in the plan.

The plan. God. Somewhere in that hidden valley, an eighty-year-old woman had looked at me — a person whose longest relationship was with an altimeter — and I had volunteered. Volunteered. I'd stood there in the firelight with the smell of woodsmoke and bread and forty people's whole lives, and I'd said *I'll carry the fakes down* as if I were offering to watch someone's dog for the weekend.

The fast boat closed the gap in the time it took me to have that thought.

* * *

They didn't shoot. That was the first thing I noticed, and the second thing I understood: dead couriers don't get interrogated, and Hale wanted to know what I knew. They put a hood over my head anyway, which struck me as theatrical, and zip-tied my wrists, which struck me as personal. Then came a long, cold, engine-thrummed ride downstream, during which I did what I always do when I can't fly: I navigated. Counted the bends. Estimated the current by the sound of it against the hull. Kept a running fix on where I was, because a position is a kind of hope, and I have never in my life traveled anywhere without one.

They took the hood off in a boathouse — a government boathouse, concrete and fluorescent, with the particular smell of diesel and bureaucracy that I recognized from a decade of fuel clerks and customs sheds. And there he was.

Corven Hale was smaller than his reputation. That's always the trick of men like him; the reputation does the work of three people, and the man himself only has to stand there looking reasonable. He was in his fifties, silver at the temples, wearing a rain jacket that probably cost more than my first airplane, and he had the smile of a man who had never once been surprised by bad news because he'd caused all of it personally.

"Pilot Galloway," he said, reading my name off a folder like we were old friends catching up. "Ten years in the interior. No fixed address. No family. No debts. You know what that made you, in our files?"

"Efficient?"

"Invisible." He said it warmly, like a compliment. "Do you know how many people in this country are truly invisible? Almost none. Everyone is a node. A daughter, a debtor, a taxpayer, a patient. But you —" he tapped the folder — "you were a gap in the map. I've always liked gaps in the map. They're where the interesting things hide."

"Is this the part where you offer me a fortune? Because I hear you're good at that."

Something flickered behind the smile. "You've been talking to my former associates."

"I've been talking to a river guide. Same thing, apparently."

He laughed, and it was a good laugh, and I hated how good it was. He gestured, and a man brought the crate — my crate, the false bottom already sprung open, the forgeries spread across a steel table like a tarot reading. Hale picked up one of the forged census pages and held it to the light with the tenderness of a jeweler.

"Excellent work," he said. "Genuinely. The paper stock is right, the ink's been aged, the hand matches the period. Whoever made these has a gift." He set it down. "Which is why I know they're fake."

I kept my face doing nothing. Ten years of solo flying teaches you a face like that.

"The real documents," Hale continued, "are in a valley that does not exist, in the custody of people who do not exist, and you were carrying these downstream so that I would find them, celebrate, and stop looking." He folded his hands. "The problem, Pilot Galloway, is that I have already found the valley. Satellite passes. A thermal anomaly in a canyon that's supposed to be uninhabited. A supply trail worn into the scree. You people flew a medicine crate in and out of a dead canyon and thought the twentieth century had ended." He sighed, and for one unguarded second he looked genuinely tired. "I was hoping you'd tell me I was wrong."

"And if you're right?"

"Then forty-one people are a rounding error." He said it the way a man reads a serial number. No cruelty in it. That was the thing I'd remember later, the thing that kept me up: there was no cruelty in it at all. "The treaty that dissolved Vaskaria has a maintenance clause. Every government since has renewed it quietly, every decade, because the alternative is admitting what the original signatories did to earn it. My career, Pilot Galloway, is the latest coat of paint on a wall built out of — " he paused, choosing the word with care — "logistics."

"Is that what they called it. The massacre."

"The *resettlement complication*," he corrected, mildly, "in the documents I have read and you have not." And then, because he was not a stupid man, he watched my face while he said it, and he saw whatever he was looking for. "Ah. So you've read them. The real ones. The letter. The photographs." He leaned forward. "Tell me something. When you looked at those pictures of the drowned town — and I know there are photographs, they inventoried everything, it's how I learned the trade — did you think about the people who took them? The census clerks? They were following orders too. Good clerks. Family men. And every one of them went home to dinner afterward, and every one of them slept, because that is what institutions are *for*, Pilot Galloway. They are machines for sleeping well."

The fluorescent lights buzzed. Somewhere out on the river, water went over rocks the way it always had, the way it had when the valley was full, the way it would after.

"You're stalling," Hale said pleasantly.

"I'm thinking."

"Don't. Thinking is how people like you get hurt. People like you should be in airplanes." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and I understood with a lurch that the interview was over and the next phase had already begun, had maybe begun before I arrived. "Here's what happens now. My boats go back upriver tonight. The thermal anomaly becomes a training exercise becomes an unfortunate fire. The documents you carried become evidence of a forgery ring, and you become the forger, because a pilot with no family and no address is exactly the kind of person who vanishes into a prison without anyone asking why. And in ten years, when some other pilot finds some other chest, I will be a minister, and this conversation will be a file in an archive, and the archive will be sealed."

He was almost at the door when I heard myself speak.

"The boats aren't going anywhere."

He turned. "No?"

"Check your radios."

He didn't, of course. Not right away. But the man by the door murmured into his collar, and then murmured again, and then a third time with a different tone, and I watched the temperature of the room change the way I watch weather change — not the event itself but the pressure before it.

Because here's what I'd done, in the boathouse dark with a hood over my head, counting bends: nothing. That was the whole trick. I'd done *nothing*, and Sefa had done everything. While Hale's fast boat chased my slow barge through the fog, Sefa and the valley scouts — people whose grandmothers had paddled these waters before the maps were burned — had gone up the other fork, quiet as silt, and put themselves between Hale's remaining boats and the canyon. Not to fight. To *disable*. A fuel line here. A seized prop there. The river network he'd never bothered to understand, doing the one thing it does better than any ministry: refusing strangers.

The man by the door said something about engines, and something about the upstream detachment not answering, and Corven Hale stood very still in his expensive rain jacket, and I watched a man arrive at the exact coordinates of his own life.

"They were your clerks," I said. "The census people. Your career is a coat of paint on their wall. You said it yourself — logistics. Forty-one people in that valley, plus a river guide, plus a network of fishermen who have been passing your secrets downstream since before you were born, plus a journalist in the capital who is going to open a package tomorrow morning that you cannot burn, because you don't know where it is. You can escalate. You're going to escalate, I can see it, it's the only tool you've ever been handed. But the wall's already down, Minister. You're just standing in the rubble insisting it's load-bearing."

He looked at me for a long moment. And I want to be honest, because the whole point of this is honesty: I was terrified. My hands were shaking inside the zip ties, my mouth was dry as a summer gravel bar, and every instinct I'd polished for ten years was screaming *fly, climb, run, there's still a gap in the map, go be invisible.* That was the old arithmetic. Distance equals safety. Altitude equals peace.

But I'd stood in a valley where the water is born. I'd eaten bread that a stranger baked for me without asking what I was worth. I'd watched Mirela hold a photograph of her drowned city the way you hold a sleeping child. There was no gap in the map anymore. There was just the map, and me in it, standing on the ground.

"Untie them," Hale said quietly to the man at the door, and it was the most frightening thing he'd said yet, because it wasn't mercy. It was a man reordering his assets. "Pilot Galloway is going to fly me up that canyon," he went on, turning back, and the charm was gone now, and what was underneath it was worse, "because the river is closed to me, but the sky, as I understand it, is not. And you're going to show me the thermal anomaly, or we're going to revisit the question of what happens to invisible people."

He smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Get her a coat," he said. "It's cold at altitude."

* * *

They walked me out onto the dock, and the river ran gray and patient under the last of the fog, and far upstream — beyond the narrows, beyond the bend, up where the water is born — I knew Sefa was watching the current and counting my absence in heartbeats, the way I once counted miles.

Hale's men were loading the boats. All of them. Every hull they had left.

He wasn't going up by river after all.

He was going to make me fly him straight over the valley — and everyone in it.
