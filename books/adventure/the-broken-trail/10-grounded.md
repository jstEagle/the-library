## 10. Grounded

*Noor*

The plane sold in nine days.

I'd expected it to take longer — the *Kestrel* was old enough to vote and ugly enough to scare off casual buyers — but a mining survey outfit from downriver paid cash and didn't haggle, which told me they needed her more than I did. I watched them fly her out on a gray Tuesday morning, wheels leaving the strip I'd landed on four hundred times, and I felt... nothing dramatic. No grief. No relief. Just the particular quiet of a room after a piece of furniture you'd stopped noticing is finally gone.

Sefa stood beside me with their hands in their pockets. "You okay?"

"I'm standing still," I said. "Ask me again in an hour."

Because that was the thing nobody tells you about running: stopping is its own kind of turbulence. For ten years I'd measured my life in fuel loads and river bends, in the distance between me and whatever I didn't want to feel. The interior had been my co-conspirator. Miles, not years — that had been the whole arrangement. And now here I was, years arriving all at once like weather.

The medicine runs stayed. One crate a month, hauled up to the valley by the river network Sefa's people had been quietly building for longer than anyone guessed — ferry pilots, net fishermen, a woman who ran a floating chapel and never asked what was under the tarp. Hale had underestimated them because they were poor and scattered and spoke in dialects his ministry had officially declared extinct. That was his mistake, and it was a beautiful one.

I kept the crate because Mirela asked me to. "You have a pilot's hands," she said, "and we have children who get fevers." It wasn't sentiment. It was logistics. I could live with logistics.

What I couldn't quite live with, yet, was the house.

* * *

*Sefa*

My grandmother used to say that Vaskaria didn't drown. It just went quiet, the way a person goes quiet when they're listening at a door.

For twenty-six years I thought she meant the country was listening for rescue. Standing in the valley square the morning the journalist's story broke — Mirela reading it aloud off a battered radio-printout while three hundred people went absolutely still — I understood she'd meant something else. We were listening for each other.

The story ran under a headline I won't forget if I live to be a hundred: THE KINGDOM THAT WASN'T SUPPOSED TO EXIST. Nine thousand words. Census pages reproduced in full. Photographs of the 1971 evacuation orders, signed by a junior minister whose name had since become a street — Corven Hale Avenue, in the capital, no less. The journalist, a sharp-faced woman named Odile Ferran whom the river network had smuggled in through three checkpoints and one very confused customs post, had done what governments always fear most: she'd made it boring. Documents. Dates. Cross-references. Massacre rendered as paperwork, undeniable as arithmetic.

Hale resigned within the week. Not arrested — not yet — but the word *inquiry* was suddenly attached to his name in every broadcast, and ministers who'd spent decades pretending not to know him were suddenly very interested in knowing exactly how much he'd known.

"They'll protect themselves before they protect us," Mirela warned, and she was right to. But protection cuts both ways when the whole world is watching. You can't quietly erase a valley that's on the front page. Outrage, it turns out, is a kind of wall. Ugly, loud, temporary — but a wall.

That night there was music in the square. Real music, the kind that hadn't been played openly in a generation, and an old man danced badly and wept into his sleeve, and children who'd never been taught the words sang them anyway, from somewhere deeper than teaching.

Noor stood at the edge of it, as usual. Watching. I went and stood next to them.

"You could go in," I said. "They'd let you."

"I'm letting it let me in," Noor said. "It's slower, but it holds."

* * *

*Noor*

Unpacking, it turns out, is a skill. Like landing in crosswinds. You can know the theory your whole life and still freeze up the first time you actually have to do it.

The house was Sefa's idea — a former ranger station two miles downstream of the valley, close enough to walk in for supper, far enough that Mirela's council could pretend we weren't living inside their security perimeter. It had a stove that smoked, a roof that leaked over the bed specifically, out of what I can only assume was spite, and a view of the river that I initially found unbearable.

Because the river was the road. Every current in it pointed away.

The first week, I caught myself doing it — standing at the window at dawn, mentally filing flight plans. South to Kessel Flats. West over the divide. Anywhere with a horizon I hadn't memorized. It wasn't wanting to leave, exactly. It was more like a phantom limb: ten years of escape routes don't vanish just because you've stopped needing them.

Sefa found me there one morning, third week in, coffee going cold in my hand.

"Bad weather?" they asked, meaning: *what are you running from today?*

"No weather at all," I said. "That's the problem."

They didn't try to fix it. They never did — that was the thing about Sefa, the thing I'd circled suspiciously back in chapter one of this mess and only later understood was love: they let weather be weather. They just put their mug down next to mine on the sill and stood there with me, looking at the same empty sky, until the itch passed. Which, eventually, it did.

Then I unpacked.

Not the survival kit — that stays packed, some habits are load-bearing. I mean the rest. The duffel I'd lived out of for a decade, the one whose contents I could list blindfolded, the one that had crossed more borders than most diplomats. I dumped it on the floor of the bedroom and put things in drawers. Books on shelves. A photograph — the chest's photograph, the Vaskarian family, 1970, everyone smiling like the water wasn't coming — went up over the desk where Mirela could see it when she visited, which she did, often, ostensibly to inspect our roof repair and actually to drink Sefa's terrible tea and complain about committees.

The last thing in the duffel was a spare ignition key for the *Kestrel*. I held it a long moment.

"Keep it," Sefa said from the doorway. "Some things you unpack by keeping."

I put it in the drawer anyway. But I knew where it was. That's different from carrying it.

* * *

*Sefa*

Here is what nobody tells you about the end of a war — even a cold one, even one fought with paper instead of bullets: it doesn't end. It just changes who has to be brave.

Mirela's council meets Thursdays now, in the open, with observers from the capital and everything. Odile Ferran comes maybe once a month, chasing follow-ups, sleeping in the guest hut and eating us out of dried fish. There are lawyers. Actual lawyers, arguing about restitution, about the treaty, about whether the drowned villages' land titles still legally exist. (They do. Vaskarian law never lapsed; it just went quiet, listening at the door.)

And there are still men like Hale's, scattered through the ministries, the ones who haven't read the newspapers or pretend they haven't. Last month someone burned a fuel cache at the lower landing. Nobody hurt. A message, though — the old kind, the kind I grew up learning to read between the lines of drowned letters.

Noor flew down to look at it. Borrowed the network's mail plane, went alone, came back at dusk with soot on their sleeves and a report nobody wanted but everybody needed.

"You didn't have to go," I said.

"Yeah, I did." They washed their hands at the basin, unhurried. "Turns out staying isn't the same as hiding. Took me ten years to learn the first one. Only took me a season to learn the second."

That's Noor. Ten years of distance distilled into one sentence delivered while drying their hands on a dish towel. I married — well. We haven't gotten around to the word yet. The person I love looked at a burned fuel cache and chose to be the one who goes and looks, and came home, and will come home tomorrow, and the day after. That's the whole miracle. That's the entire treaty.

* * *

*Noor*

Spring came up the valley late, the way it always does when you're surrounded by mountains that hoard their snow out of stubbornness.

On the first warm morning, Sefa and I walked down to the shore below the house — the same kind of shoreline, though not the same one, where I'd once lingered too long beside a drowned village and felt the first crack open in a decade of armor. The water was high and brown with melt, carrying winter's debris seaward. Somewhere upstream, where the water is born, Mirela's people were planting.

"I got a letter from the survey outfit," I said. "The ones who bought the *Kestrel*. She's flying fine. They named her."

Sefa raised an eyebrow. "Named her what?"

"*Second Chance.*" I grimaced. "Mining engineers. No poetry whatsoever."

But I laughed, and Sefa laughed, and the river kept moving the way rivers do — taking things downstream, bringing things back, keeping what it keeps. Down there, past the rapids that had nearly killed us, past the burned boats and the fog chases and the minister who chose escalation over truth, the water was carrying this year's stories to Odile Ferran and the inquiry and whoever else was listening. Upstream, the source ran quiet and clear, guarded now not by secrecy but by the louder, sturdier thing that had replaced it: three hundred people whose existence the world had finally been forced to acknowledge, and a public that had decided, loudly, that erasing them again would cost too much.

Love against duty. I'd spent a winter thinking those were opposite banks of some impossible crossing.

Standing there on the shore with Sefa's shoulder against mine, watching the current take the spring downstream, I finally saw it plain: they were the same water. You stay for the people. You fight for the people. You unpack the duffel for the people. There was never a choice between them. There was only ever the question of whether you'd stop running long enough to notice you were already home.

I had. Notice, I mean. Home took a little longer — it's still leaking over the bed.

But the roofers come Thursday.
