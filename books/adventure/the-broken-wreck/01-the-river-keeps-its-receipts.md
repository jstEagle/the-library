## 1. The River Keeps Its Receipts

The Vell ran green that morning, the color of old bottle glass, and Emrys Thackeray trusted it about as far as he could throw his own boat.

"Green means glacier melt," he told the wheel, because after thirty years of talking to the Kindly Hour he'd stopped seeing any reason to stop. "Glacier melt means the narrows are running fast, which means every fool with a debt ledger in Sallow Bend will be watching the docks instead of the water."

The Kindly Hour said nothing, being a boat, but she answered in her own way — a shudder through the deck plates, a wheeze from the port boiler that sounded suspiciously like a laugh. She was forty-one years old, older than Emrys by a decade, patched in six places with metal that had been other boats first, and she handled the river the way an old woman handles gossip: slowly, knowingly, and with a great deal of opinion.

He came around Harker's Bend at half past dawn and there she was below him — Sallow Bend itself, all crooked rooftops and crookeder promises, smoke rising off the fish-curing sheds in gray ribbons. And there, exactly where he knew they'd be, were the two men on the dock who did not look like fishermen.

Fishermen lean. These two stood square, arms crossed, watching the water like it owed them money. Which, in fairness, it was about to deliver him directly into their view.

Emrys sighed and rang down to the engine room twice — slow ahead, then slower — and began doing arithmetic he didn't like. Osgar Pell held paper on the Kindly Hour to the tune of four hundred crowns, give or take the interest Pell calculated with a pencil sharpened on both ends. The collectors had been waiting three mornings now. They were patient men. Patience was cheaper than muscle, and Pell never spent a coin he didn't have to.

But patience had a weakness, and its name was tide.

"Right," Emrys murmured, and took her in wide, past the mooring buoys, aiming for the mudflat shallows east of the curing sheds where no collector worth his boots would follow. "We'll do this the dignified way."

The dignified way involved grounding a riverboat on a mudflat, which Emrys acknowledged was a generous use of the word *dignified*. But the Vell dropped two feet with the ebb, and if you beached her just right — bow up-slope, stern in the channel — she'd sit like a hen on a nest until the water came back and lifted her off, free and clear, while the collectors stood on the dock growing older.

It worked for eleven minutes.

For eleven glorious minutes Emrys sat in his pilothouse with a cup of chicory, feet on the rail, watching the two collectors squint across the flats and argue about whether wading was in their job description. It was not. Pell paid for patience, not laundry bills.

Then the dredge snagged something heavy, and the morning stopped being funny.

* * *

The dredge was a small affair, a chain of buckets off the stern that Emrys used to pull silt from his own intake and, when times were thin, whatever the river chose to give up. Bottles mostly. Buttons. Once, memorably, a wedding dress that Widow Ash had cleaned, pressed, and worn to the Sallow Bend harvest dance purely to watch people try not to say anything.

The chain jerked, groaned, and went taut as a fiddle string.

Emrys set down his cup. A snag was ordinary. A snag that made the whole stern list two degrees starboard was a conversation, and the river was saying something.

He threw the buckets out of gear before they tore themselves apart and went aft, leaning over the rail. Down in the tea-green water, maybe eight feet down on the falling tide, something pale caught the light. Not a rock. Rocks don't have corners.

By noon the tide had dropped enough that he could see all of it: a chest, iron-banded, oak-ribbed, sitting in the muck like it had been set there on purpose by somebody with very poor judgment about furniture placement. The dredge chain had hooked one of the bands. He sent down the gaff hook, worked it under the lid's hasp, and hauled, cursing, sweating, nearly going over the rail twice — and the second time only saved himself because Finch, the mute boy who sometimes sculled odd jobs for passage, appeared from nowhere and took a fistful of his collar with the calm of someone catching a dropped plate.

"Thank you," Emrys gasped. "Yes. I know. I'm an idiot. Help me pull."

Between the two of them, grunting, they got it over the rail and onto the deck with a boom that rattled the pilothouse glass. It was bigger than a breadbox and smaller than a coffin, which Emrys felt was the exact size of trouble. Green slime clung to it in ropes. Barnacles had claimed the lower band. But stamped into the lid, deep and clean beneath forty years of river, was a crest: a mountain split by a sword.

Emrys went cold in the warm sun.

Every child on the Vell knew that crest the way they knew their own names. Karvenia. The drowned kingdom. The nation that had been, once, rich enough to buy the mountains and proud enough to think the river couldn't touch them — until the last treasury ship broke apart in the glacier narrows forty years back, and everything a country had ever stolen or saved went to the bottom in a single night. Salvors had hunted that wreck for a generation. Half the fortune-hunters between here and the coast had gone broke, mad, or dead looking for it.

And here it was, sitting on his deck, dripping.

"Finch," Emrys said carefully, "you saw nothing."

Finch looked at him with the flat, unimpressed expression of a boy who read water better than any pilot alive and had already decided Emrys was going to do something stupid. Then he shrugged, which from Finch was practically a signed affidavit, and went forward to mind the lines.

Emrys dragged the chest into the hold, threw a tarpaulin over it, stacked two crates of salt cod on top, and sat down on the pile to think.

* * *

Widow Ash found him there at dusk, still sitting, still thinking, having accomplished neither.

She was a broad woman with forearms like mooring line and gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fish fork. She had cooked aboard the Kindly Hour for nine years, since the flood took her house and, she said, finally gave her an excuse to leave a husband she'd been meaning to leave anyway. She looked at Emrys, then at the tarpaulin, then at Emrys again.

"You've found something," she said.

"What makes you say that?"

"You've got the face of a man standing in a doorway deciding whether the noise upstairs is a burglar or a blessing." She folded her arms. "Also you skipped supper. You never skip supper. Last time you skipped supper we lost the rudder pin."

Emrys weighed his options. There was one option. He pulled back the tarp.

Widow Ash looked at the crest for a long moment. She didn't gasp. She was not a gasping woman. What she did was sit down heavily on a crate, which for her was the same thing.

"Well," she said. "Karvenia."

"You recognize it."

"I buried a husband who spent eleven years and our entire savings hunting that wreck. I recognize it the way you'd recognize the man who robbed you." She reached out and touched the iron band, gently, almost kindly. "Open it."

"It might be trapped."

"It's been underwater forty years. If it were trapped, it would've sprung by now. Open it."

The lock was long dead; the hasp gave to a pry bar and a prayer. Inside, packed in wool gone to gray felt: coins. Not many — a layer, maybe two hundred, gold and silver both, minted with the split-mountain crest. A compass rose, brass, broken clean off its card, lying in the corner like a fallen flower. And beneath the coins, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically —

— a book.

Half a book, rather. The ledger was thick, leather-bound, and had been torn violently down the spine, half its pages gone. But the surviving half was dense with writing: columns of names, amounts, dates. *Taken from.* *Owed to.* Families. Dozens of families. Whole villages listed with the sums the old regime had stripped from them — taxes, seizures, "levies," each entry neat as a hymnal.

Emrys turned pages with hands that had steered through ice floes without shaking and were shaking now.

Then he stopped.

Three-quarters down the left-hand page, in ink faded to brown:

*Thackeray, Aldous — ferryman, Millreach Crossing. Taken: vessel, license, bond of 60 crowns. Owed to heirs.*

His father. His father's ferry, taken the year before the collapse, the year before Aldous Thackeray drank himself quiet on the wrong side of the river he'd been born working. Emrys had grown up on stories about bad luck and worse government. He had never once seen either one written down in a fair hand, itemized, *owed.*

"They kept receipts," he said quietly. "Forty years at the bottom of the Vell, and the river kept their receipts."

Widow Ash watched him. "That's your family's name."

"That's my family's name."

"And the other half?"

"At the wreck, I'd guess. Wherever this came from." He closed the ledger carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Which is at the bottom of a glacier narrows nobody can reach except three weeks a year, guarded by ice that eats ships."

"Mm," said Widow Ash.

"And there's a fleet director named Corvane Hale who's spent years hunting this wreck, and men like Hale don't hunt things they intend to share."

"Mm," said Widow Ash again, and got up to fetch the lamp, because dusk had come down and some conversations deserve proper light.

They sat a while in the yellow circle of it, the chest open between them, coins glinting like a handful of caught stars.

"We could sell it," Emrys said at last. "Coins alone. Quietly. Pay Pell, refit the boiler, never speak of it."

"Yes," said Widow Ash. "And every time you looked at your father's name torn in half, you'd know you sold the other half of it to the first man with coin."

"I hate when you're right. You're always right. It's exhausting."

"Then open the other crates of cod and help me salt them, and tell me what you're actually going to do."

Emrys looked at the ledger. Somewhere upstream, past the narrows, past the high country, a ship full of stolen lives lay under the Serpent's Tooth icefall, holding the rest of the names. Somewhere behind him, in Sallow Bend, two patient men were reporting to a patient man with a double-sharpened pencil. And somewhere out in the dark water, luck had finally come calling — arriving, as luck always did on the Vell, with teeth.

"I'm going to sleep on it," he said.

"You're going to stare at the ceiling until midnight and then decide to run the narrows."

"...I'm going to sleep on it."

From the darkness near the hold door came a small sound. Finch, who was supposed to be asleep, who heard everything and said nothing, looked in at the open chest, at the broken compass rose, at Emrys's face — and nodded once, slow, like a pilot confirming a heading.

Emrys had the distinct, sinking feeling that the crew of the Kindly Hour had just doubled without his permission.
