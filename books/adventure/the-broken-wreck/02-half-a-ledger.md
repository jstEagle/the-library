## 2. Half a Ledger

The chest sat on the Kindly Hour's deck all afternoon, and Emrys Thackeray did not open it.

This was not cowardice, he told himself. It was strategy. A man who opens a mysterious chest in daylight is a man who can be seen opening a mysterious chest, and Emrys had spent fifty-one years perfecting the art of being unremarkable. He'd scrubbed the deck twice. He'd re-caulked a seam that didn't need it. He'd even argued with the mooring post about the proper angle of a line, loudly, so that anyone watching would conclude the captain of the Kindly Hour was a harmless fool who talked to rope.

Widow Ash brought him tea at dusk and set it down beside him without comment, which was her way of saying *I know you're stalling and I find it entertaining.*

"You could just look," she said.

"I'm letting it season."

"Chests don't season."

"This one does. Karvenian chests season like cheese." He wrapped both hands around the tin cup. The river slid past, brown-gold in the last light, carrying its usual cargo of silt and secrets. "You know what happens to men who find treasure on the Vell, Widow?"

"They retire."

"The lucky ones drown first. It's quicker."

She snorted and went back to her galley, and Emrys sat with his tea going cold, listening to the water slap the hull, and finally — when the lamps along Sallow Bend's quay had guttered down to embers and no honest soul was awake to see — he knelt on the deck with a pry bar and opened the thing that was going to ruin his life.

* * *

The lock surrendered without a fight, which somehow made it worse. Forty years in the shallows and the mechanism turned like it had been oiled yesterday. Karvenian workmanship. They'd built everything to last: ships, locks, lies.

Inside, under a skin of river silt, lay coins. Not many — two dozen, maybe three dozen, gold, stamped with the double-headed swan of the old crown. Enough to pay his debts at Sallow Bend twice over. Enough to stop running from collectors, to fix the Kindly Hour's rotten stern planking, to eat something other than Widow Ash's barley soup for a month. (He loved the soup. He also dreamed of onions.)

Beneath the coins, a broken compass rose — brass, palm-sized, snapped clean across its face, half the cardinal points sheared away as if someone had taken offense at north. And beneath that, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job stubbornly well, a book.

A ledger. Half of one, anyway. Torn down the spine, ragged-edged, the remaining half thick as a brick. Emrys carried it into the galley where Widow Ash's lamp burned low, and she came to stand at his shoulder, drying her hands on her apron, and together they looked at the columns of names.

Not names alone. Names, holdings, sums. *Vellmark ferry rights, seized. Two hundred acres, orchard land, confiscated. Household goods, one lot.* Page after page after page, in a clerk's neat hand, the tidy arithmetic of theft. The Karvenian regime hadn't looted in passion; they'd looted in ink. Every family along the upper Vell who'd ever owned anything worth owning, catalogued, assessed, and taken — and then the nation itself had drowned, and the ledger had gone into the river with everything else, and forty years of water had turned robbery into legend.

"Half a ledger," Widow Ash murmured. "Where's the rest?"

"At the wreck, I'd wager. Or at the bottom of it." Emrys turned a page. "Half a receipt is still a receipt."

"Whose name are you looking for?"

"I'm not looking for—"

"Emrys."

He stopped turning pages. Because there it was, halfway down a column, in that patient clerk's hand:

**Thackeray, Aldous. Ferryman. Boat, one. House, one. Debt to the Crown, invented.**

*Debt to the Crown, invented.*

His father had been a ferryman too. Had poled passengers across the narrows for thirty years, laughing, whistling off-key, giving free rides to children and widows and once, famously, a goat. And when the regime wanted his stretch of the river for their own tolls, they had invented a debt — written it down in this very book, in this very hand, *invented*, the clerk not even bothering to disguise it — and taken the boat and the house, and Aldous Thackeray had died four years later in a rented room above a tannery, coughing, poor, telling his son that the river keeps its receipts, boy, the river keeps its receipts, and young Emrys had thought it was just something old men said.

Emrys set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Well," said Widow Ash.

"Well," said Emrys.

"Your da's in here."

"My da's in here." He laughed, one short bark with nothing behind it. "Forty years I thought we were poor because we were unlucky. Turns out we were poor because somebody wrote it down."

Widow Ash put her hand flat on the ledger, over the swan stamped into the cover. "What are you going to do?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The sensible answer was: sell it. Find a buyer, take the coins, take whatever a collector would pay for Karvenian paper, buy new planking, buy onions, forget the whole business. The sensible answer had kept Emrys alive through five decades of dodging creditors and sandbars and men with collections in their voices.

But his father's name sat on the page looking up at him, and it did not look sensible. It looked patient. It looked like it could wait another forty years if it had to, but it would very much like to be paid.

"I'm going to sleep on it," Emrys said, which was what he always said, and for once he meant it as more than a stalling tactic.

* * *

The stranger came aboard two nights later, which meant whoever had watched Emrys dredge up the chest had been patient, thorough, and close enough to count the coins before Emrys himself had.

It was near midnight, fog lying on the Vell like a wet blanket, when Emrys heard footsteps on the quay — measured, unhurried, the walk of a man who had never once been chased by a debt collector — and then a voice at the rail.

"Captain Thackeray."

Emrys had the boathook in his hand before he'd fully decided to reach for it. A figure stood at the top of the gangplank: tall, gray-cloaked, face shadowed under a hood that was either fashionable or practical, and with this sort of man it was always both.

"You have the advantage of me."

"By design." The man descended the plank without asking permission, which told Emrys plenty. "Let's not waste each other's night. You pulled a chest from the shallows below the bend. Karvenian stamp. I'd like to buy it."

"Saw that from the fog, did you?"

"I make it my business to know what comes out of the Vell." The hood tilted toward the galley, where lamplight leaked through the shutters. "Name your price. For the chest, contents included, sight unseen. I don't need to inspect it. I only need it gone."

Every instinct Emrys owned stood up and started shouting. A buyer who doesn't inspect isn't buying treasure; he's buying silence. Sight unseen meant *whatever's in there frightens me*, and things frightened powerful men were worth more than gold — they were worth trouble, the kind that arrived in multiples.

"Curious merchandise," Emrys said slowly, "selling sight unseen. What if it's empty? What if I fished up an empty chest and I've been sleeping next to disappointment all week?"

"Then I'll have bought an empty chest, and we'll both have learned something about the river." The voice stayed pleasant, which was somehow worse than anger. "Captain. I'll offer you this once, generously, and then I'll offer it less generously elsewhere, and you'll wish you'd dealt with me while I was still polite. Name a figure."

Emrys looked past the man, out at the fog rolling off the water, at the dark line of the current sliding toward the narrows, toward the high country, toward wherever the rest of that ledger lay sleeping under forty years of ice. His father had ferried people across that river for nothing half the time. *Debt to the Crown, invented.*

"No sale," Emrys said.

The hood went very still.

"I beg your pardon?"

"It's not for sale. Not tonight, not tomorrow, not at any figure you care to name. You see, I opened it." He smiled the smile he usually saved for customs inspectors. "Sight seen. And it turns out I know some of the folks in there."

For a long moment the only sound was the river, slapping the hull, keeping its receipts.

"That," said the stranger quietly, "was the wrong answer, Captain." He stepped back onto the quay, unhurried even now. "Corvane Hale sends his regards. You'll hear from the Meridian Fleet again — sooner than you'd like, and in a form you won't enjoy."

Then the fog took him, footstep by footstep, and Emrys stood at the rail holding a boathook against nobody, heart hammering, while behind him in the galley the lamp flickered and the ledger lay open to a page with his father's name on it, patient as the river, waiting to be paid.

"Widow Ash," Emrys called softly, "how attached are you to Sallow Bend?"

From the galley: "Never much liked it. Why?"

"Because I think we're leaving on the morning tide."
