## 3. Corvane Hale's Long Shadow

The stranger came back on the third morning, and this time he brought a friend.

Emrys saw them from the wheelhouse of the *Kindly Hour* before they'd crossed half the dock — two men walking in step the way soldiers do, or men who'd once been soldiers and hadn't found anything better to be since. The first was the same soft-spoken gentleman from the tavern, still wearing that coat too fine for Sallow Bend, still smiling like a man who'd been told his whole life that his smile was disarming and had never once checked whether it was true. The second was wider. Much wider. He walked like a door that had learned to move.

"Captain Thackeray." The fine coat stopped at the gangplank without asking to come aboard, which Emrys noted and appreciated. "May I?"

"You may stand there," Emrys said. "It's good footing. I've tested it personally."

The smile didn't waver. "My name is Vess. I represent an interested party. I believe we spoke briefly at the Drowned Gull."

"We spoke. You talked. I drank." Emrys leaned on the rail. "I remember it fondly."

"My employer has heard you recovered something from the shallows below the narrows. A chest, perhaps. Karvenian work, by the description." Vess produced a small leather folio and opened it with one finger, the way a man shows you a knife without showing you the knife. "He's prepared to offer four hundred crowns. Sight unseen. The chest, its contents, and your discretion going forward — which, frankly, is the more valuable part of the arrangement."

Emrys looked at him for a long moment. Downriver, a gull was screaming at nothing, which struck Emrys as about right.

"Four hundred," he said.

"Four hundred. And my employer's gratitude, which in certain circles is worth considerably more."

"And who's the employer, then? Some collector? Some rich fool who likes old boxes?"

Vess's smile tightened by a degree, the way ice tightens before it lets go of the bank. "Corvane Hale. Director of the Meridian Salvage Fleet."

* * *

Emrys had never met Corvane Hale, but like everyone who worked the Vell, he knew the shape of him the way you know the shape of a storm cloud on the horizon — not the details, just the size of it, and the certainty it was coming your way eventually. Meridian Salvage ran forty hulls from the delta to the high lakes. They dredged, they dove, they claimed wrecks by paper and held them by muscle. Fishermen told stories about Hale's survey barges arriving at a site days after word of a find, always with documents signed by someone important, always with men who didn't smile.

"He's welcome to make his offer himself," Emrys said. "I hear he owns boats. Boats go places."

"Mr. Hale doesn't travel for small transactions."

"Then he shouldn't send a man who calls four hundred crowns small. That's a year of honest dredging, friend. It's insulting precisely because it isn't."

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes — recalculation, maybe, or disappointment. "Five hundred."

"No."

"Six hundred, and we never trouble you again."

"No." Emrys came down off the wheelhouse steps slowly, hands visible, the way you do when there's a wide man standing at your gangplank. "Here's my trouble, Mr. Vess. You want this chest so badly you're offering real money for it, sight unseen, which means either you don't know what's inside or you do. If you don't, you're a fool, and fools don't carry six hundred crowns. If you do—" He shrugged. "Then what's inside matters to somebody powerful enough to pay for silence. And things that matter that much have a way of mattering to me too."

The wide man shifted his weight. Just slightly. Just enough.

"I'd advise against whatever you're considering," Emrys told him pleasantly. "I'm sixty-one years old, I've been brawling on this river since before your mother met your father, and I cheat. Ask anyone."

"Captain." Vess closed the folio. "My employer is a patient man, but patience is a budget like anything else. Consider the offer. I'll call again tomorrow." He turned, then paused, and looked back with something almost like kindness. "Whatever you think you've found — it was never lost. It was put away. There's a difference, and the difference belongs to Mr. Hale."

They walked away down the dock, in step, the gull still screaming its opinion.

Widow Ash appeared at the galley hatch with a wooden spoon in her fist like a marshal's baton. "Well?"

"Well what?"

"Are we rich, or are we dead?"

"Ask me tomorrow," Emrys said, and went below to look at the ledger again.

* * *

He sat up most of that night with the chest open on his table and the lamp turned low, though nobody could see through the *Kindly Hour*'s salt-fogged glass but the moths. Half a ledger. That was the thing he kept circling back to, turning the book over in his hands: it was half. The binding had been cut clean — sheared, not torn — and the surviving pages ended mid-entry, a family called Verren cut off between one line and the next, their confiscated holdings listed down to the spoons.

Forty years ago, the Karvenian treasury ship *Glorious Return* had gone down in the glacier narrows with everything the old regime had stolen from everyone it had robbed. Everyone knew the story. Nobody knew where exactly she'd broken, because the river had spent four decades moving her grave. But if half a ledger had washed downstream into Emrys's dredge lines—

Then the other half hadn't gone far.

His father's name sat on the fourth page. *Thackeray, Aldous — ferryman, Vell Crossing. Seized: vessel, license, savings of 212 crowns.* His father had rowed people across the river for thirty years and died poling a barge for another man's profit, and somewhere in a government office a clerk had written it all down neat as a hymnal, and now here it was in Emrys's hands, smelling of river silt and forty years of dark water.

"They wrote it down," he said aloud to Widow Ash, who had come down without being asked and sat across from him peeling potatoes, because Widow Ash believed no conversation should happen without potatoes nearby. "Every theft. Every family. They kept receipts."

"Governments keep receipts," she said. "It's how they know what they're owed."

"It's how *we'll* know what they owe *us*." He tapped the page. "Half the names are in this book. The other half is lying under the Serpent's Tooth with the rest of the treasury, and Corvane Hale wants this half badly enough to burn money on it. Which tells me he knows exactly what the other half says."

Ash peeled her potato in one long unbroken ribbon, which she considered a minor art. "And what does it say?"

"That the wreck wasn't an accident." Emrys heard himself say it and felt the truth of it settle in his chest like ballast. "Think, Ash. A treasury ship, heavy as sin, running the narrows in calving season? No pilot alive would take her through. Somebody ordered her through anyway. Or—" he turned the thought over, "—somebody wanted her at the bottom, and the ledger with her. Paper burns, Ash. Water keeps."

The potato ribbon dropped into the bowl. Ash looked at him over the lamplight, and for once she didn't have a dry remark ready, which frightened Emrys more than the wide man at the gangplank had.

* * *

The warehouse burned at two in the morning.

It was Ollie Brack's warehouse, down on the mudflats — Ollie who bought salvage off small boats without asking questions, Ollie who had stored Emrys's dredge gear every winter for a decade, Ollie who had once, briefly and unwisely, mentioned to a stranger in the Drowned Gull that Captain Thackeray had pulled up something interesting that autumn. Emrys stood on the *Kindly Hour*'s deck and watched it burn, orange light rolling across the black water, and felt the heat on his face from two hundred yards off.

Nobody was hurt. Of course nobody was hurt. That was the message. Fire that kills is rage; fire that doesn't is arithmetic.

By dawn the whole bend of the river knew, and by noon they knew whose arithmetic it was. Vess didn't come back. He didn't need to. Instead a boy delivered a note to the dockmaster, unsigned, in beautiful handwriting:

*Last offer. Six hundred crowns stands until the next tide. After that, Mr. Hale acquires the item by other means, and acquires the holder with it.*

Emrys read it twice, fed it to the stove, and stood watching it curl.

He should sell it. Any sensible man would sell it. Take the six hundred, run downriver ahead of winter, buy a new boat with a bottom that didn't leak, grow old stupid and comfortable. That was the sensible ledger entry. *Thackeray, Emrys — seized: one conscience, value nil.*

But he kept seeing his father's name in that clerk's neat hand. And he kept thinking about the other families — the ones on pages that were currently lying under forty feet of green glacier water, waiting for Corvane Hale's divers to bring them up so they could be burned properly this time. Hale wasn't hunting treasure. Treasure he could buy. Hale was hunting *names*, and you only hunt names if the names can hurt you.

Which meant the wreck wasn't just a grave. It was a witness. And witnesses, Emrys thought, deserved better than to be silenced twice.

He went topside. Widow Ash was at the rail with Finch — the mute boy who haunted the docks and read the water like scripture — and both of them were looking upriver, toward the narrows, toward the white line of the high country where the Vell came down out of the ice.

"The tide turns at dusk," Emrys said. "We'd have maybe six tides before calving season opens the high route, and maybe six more before it closes. After that, nobody goes up until spring — and neither does Hale."

Ash looked at him. "You're talking about running the Serpent's Tooth reach in the *Kindly Hour*. That boat leaks sitting still."

"She leaks with dignity." Emrys gripped the rail and looked upriver, and found — to his mild surprise — that his hands had stopped shaking somewhere around the time the warehouse caught. Fear was still there; fear would always be there. But underneath it, something older and harder had woken up, something that had been asleep since his father died poor. "Hale thinks I'm holding something worth six hundred crowns. He's wrong. I'm holding something worth forty years, and I'm not selling it to the man who lit the match the first time."

Finch tugged his sleeve. The boy pointed upriver, then made a quick chopping motion with both hands — the icefall, coming down — then pointed at Emrys, then at the sky, and grinned like a jackdaw.

"Yes," Emrys said. "That's about the size of it. We're going to need a diver."
