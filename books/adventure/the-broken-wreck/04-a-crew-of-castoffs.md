## 4. A Crew of Castoffs

Emrys found Widow Ash where he always found her when the world needed putting right: in the galley of the Kindly Hour, threatening a pot of stew with a wooden spoon as though it owed her money.

"You've been standing at that rail all morning," she said without turning around. "Which means you opened the chest again. Which means you're about to ask me something foolish."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You will." She tapped the spoon against the pot's rim, twice, like a judge bringing down a gavel. "Sit. Eat. Then tell me why you look like a man who's found religion and lost his nerve in the same week."

He sat. The stew was venison and river onions and something she refused to name, and it was better than anything served in Sallow Bend for triple the price. He told her about the ledger — the names, the columns of seized lands and drowned fortunes, and the one entry that had stopped his breath: *Thackeray, Aldous. Riverhold farm, forty acres, taken under writ of the Crown Provisional.* His father's farm. Taken the year before his father took to drink, and not the other way round, the way Emrys had believed his whole life.

Widow Ash listened the way she did everything — completely, without hurry, her hands never stopping their work.

"So," she said at last. "Half a book of stolen names, a burned warehouse, and a salvage director who kills people over paper. And you're going upstream after the other half."

"I hadn't said—"

"You were going to say it alone, which is worse." She finally turned, and her eyes were the gray of the Vell in November. "Emrys Thackeray, I have cooked on this boat eleven years. You think I don't know what you are? A man who pays his debts and everyone else's too, and calls it pride so he doesn't have to call it loneliness." She pointed the spoon at him. "You'll need a diver. You'll need someone who can read ice. And you'll need me, because you'd starve inside three days without anyone telling you when to eat."

"That's true," Emrys admitted.

"Then stop looking surprised and go find your diver."

* * *

Tam Oriel was drinking in the Drowned Gull with the particular stillness of a man who has decided not to be anywhere else. He was broad through the shoulders and narrow through the prospects, with rope-scarred hands and the pale, pressure-creased skin of someone who had spent years below the surface of things.

"I know who you are," Tam said before Emrys sat down. "And I know what you fished out of the shallows. Half this port knows. The other half works for Hale."

"Then you understand my problem."

"I understand you're a dead man walking who wants company." Tam turned his glass in a slow circle. "I dove for Meridian six years. Wrecks, recoveries, one job up north I don't talk about. Hale pays well and forgets faster. When a diver stops being useful, he stops being careful." He looked up. "Why me?"

"Because you said 'up north' like it meant something. Because you didn't pretend not to know what chest I mean. And because a man who drinks alone in the afternoon is either hiding from something or getting ready to do something brave." Emrys set a coin on the table — not payment, just an anchor. "I'm running the high narrows before calving season. Six tides, maybe seven. I need a diver who isn't afraid of black water under ice."

Tam was quiet long enough that the coin could have melted. "The narrows in calving season," he said. "You want to die with witnesses."

"I want to live with them. There's a difference, and it's mostly timing."

Something moved across Tam's face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would build if a man remembered how. "I'll dive for you, Captain. One condition. If we find what's in that wreck, nobody sells it back to Hale. Not for pardon, not for gold, not for anything." He extended a hand scarred like old rigging. "I've done enough work for men who buy the past. I'd like to drown one job of theirs instead."

They shook on it, and neither man let go quickly, because both understood they had just become each other's problem for life.

* * *

Nessa Quill kept her maps in a room above a chandler's shop, pinned to every wall, layered on the floor, curling from damp. She was small and quick-eyed, with ink stains to the second knuckle and the defensive posture of someone who had been laughed at by institutions.

"The Survey revoked my license," she said, before Emrys finished explaining why he'd come. "Do you know what they revoked it *for*?"

"Tell me."

"I corrected them." Her chin came up. "Their chart of the upper Vell placed the Serpent's Tooth falls half a mile wrong. Half a *mile*. I said so. Loudly. In front of the Director of Cartography, whose grandfather drew the original error, and whose family honor apparently outweighs the lives of every pilot who trusts his map." She swept a hand at the walls. "So now I draw maps no one buys, for rivers no one asks about, and I am *extremely* bitter about it, thank you for noticing."

"I'm asking about the high narrows," Emrys said. "Specifically, whether a flat-bottomed riverboat of shallow draft can run them ahead of the calving window."

Nessa went still in the way of people whose obsession has just been handed a purpose. She crossed to a wall map — hand-drawn, obsessive, beautiful — and traced the Vell from Sallow Bend up into the glacier country with one ink-stained finger.

"Everyone runs the low route because the high route is suicide nine months of the year," she said. "But the narrows open early. The melt comes first to the high country — my grandfather's charts show it, and my grandfather was the only surveyor honest enough to measure twice. You have a window between the thaw opening the channel and the first big calving closing it. It's narrow. It shifts. Every pilot who's tried it guessed, and guessing kills."

"And you wouldn't guess."

"I would *know*." She turned, and there was fire in her now, banked for years and suddenly given air. "I've redrawn those narrows four times from every account, logbook, and drunk fisherman's memory I could buy. I know where the channel bends, where the ice shelves, where the water lies about its depth." She paused, and some of the fire cooled into something more careful. "It's still dangerous. People could die. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is what ruined me."

"People could die staying here, too," Emrys said. "Hale's made sure of it. The only question is whether we die running or waiting."

Nessa looked at him for a long moment. "You talk like a man reading from a ledger," she said. "Everything balanced. Fine. I'm in. But I keep the charts, I call the lines, and if I say beach the boat, you beach the boat, even if it costs us the prize. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Good." She was already rolling maps into a waterproof oilskin sleeve. "One more thing. There's a boy. Finch. He's mute — throat damage from when he was small, nobody knows the whole story. He can't speak, but he reads water better than any pilot on the Vell. Currents, eddies, sandbars moving overnight — he sees it the way I see lines on paper. The harbor pilots use him and pay him in bread and pretend he's furniture. I won't work without him."

"A boy? How old?"

"Fourteen, maybe. He doesn't know himself." Nessa slung the map case over her shoulder. "And before you ask — yes, I know what I'm proposing. Taking a child into the narrows. But Emrys, that boy sleeps under the chandler's stairs in winter. Whatever we're running toward, he's already running from something worse. At least on your boat he'd be running fed."

* * *

Finch was on the breakwater, as Nessa said he always was, sitting cross-legged above the water with his chin on his fists, watching the tide come in. He was thin and dark-eyed, with a coil of fishing line over one shoulder and a heron's patience.

Emrys crouched beside him and said nothing for a while. It seemed polite. The boy was clearly having a conversation; it simply wasn't in words.

Finally Finch held up one finger, pointed downstream, then made a slow rolling motion with his hand — the current, coming in strong off the ebb. He pointed again to a spot near the breakwater's foot where the water looked perfectly calm, brown and smooth as tea, and shook his head sharply.

"Don't moor there," Nessa translated. "Sandbar's shifted since yesterday. It looks deep. It isn't."

"How does he know?"

Finch answered by pointing at a faint ripple line, a seam in the water's surface no wider than a scar, invisible until you knew to see it. Then he looked up at Emrys with an expression of grave, patient expectation, as though to say: *well? Are you going to be dense about this too, like everyone else?*

"He wants to know if you can see it," Nessa said.

Emrys squinted. Honest answer: no. He'd have put the Kindly Hour's bow exactly there and spent a tide digging her off.

"No," he said. "But I'm hoping you'll come aboard and teach me."

Finch studied him for a long moment — the way he'd study a strange current, testing it for what lay underneath. Then he reached into his shirt and produced a piece of chalk, and on the flat stone of the breakwater he wrote three words in a small, careful hand:

**FEED ME FIRST**

"Widow Ash's cooking," Emrys said solemnly. "You've heard."

Finch nodded once, gravely, and began gathering his line and his few belongings, which fit in a flour sack. It occurred to Emrys, watching him, that the boy had agreed somewhere in the last minute and had simply been waiting for the adults to catch up.

* * *

That evening the Kindly Hour rode low with provisions and high with argument. Widow Ash claimed the galley and annexed Finch immediately, feeding him three bowls while interrogating him entirely through raised eyebrows, to which he replied fluently. Tam coiled line on deck and pretended not to be listening to Nessa, who had spread her charts across the pilothouse floor and was lecturing the wheel on the subject of the narrows as though the wheel might sit an examination.

Emrys stood at the rail and watched the lights of Sallow Bend come on behind them, one by one, like debts being counted.

Six tides. Maybe seven. Ahead of them: black water under ice, a wreck beneath a falling glacier, and Corvane Hale, who had already shown them what his messages looked like when written in fire.

Behind them: nothing worth the name.

He felt a presence at his elbow — Finch, silent, offering him a heel of bread from his own ration, which by the boy's arithmetic was roughly half his fortune. Emrys took it, because refusing would have been an insult, and broke it in two, and handed half back.

Finch considered this transaction, nodded as though terms had been satisfactorily concluded, and pointed upriver, into the dark, where the water ran cold and fast toward the mountains.

*Yes,* Emrys agreed silently. *Up.*

At dawn they cast off. By noon they had passed the last pilot buoy. And by dusk, when the first far-off boom rolled down the valley like a door slamming shut — the glacier, talking in its sleep — Nessa stood beside him at the rail with her charts clutched to her chest and said, very quietly:

"Captain. That was early. That was *days* early."

Emrys looked upriver, where the mountains had begun to glow white in the failing light, and understood that six tides had just become five.
