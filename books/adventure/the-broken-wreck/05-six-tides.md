## 5. Six Tides

Tam Oriel had counted tides for a living once, down in the Meridian yards, where the foremen chalked the numbers on slate and you learned to read the river the way a debt collector reads a man's face. Six tides. That was what Emrys had said at the dock at Sallow Bend, spitting into the current like a man laying a wager with God. Six tides before the calving season opened the high narrows, and six tides before Corvane Hale's iron barge came grinding up from the delta like a sermon nobody wanted to hear.

They were on tide two, and Tam's hands wouldn't stop shaking.

Not fear, exactly. He'd been afraid plenty of times in his life and had learned to tell it apart from its cheaper cousins. This was something older — the particular tremor of a man carrying a debt he hadn't confessed yet, walking around with it like a stone in his boot.

"You're doing it again," Finch signed at him from the rail, quick-fingered and merciless.

"Doing what?"

"The face." Finch made an expression of profound suffering, eyebrows knotted, mouth turned down like a wet rope. "The one Nessa says means your conscience is constipated."

"I don't have a conscience. I have experience."

Finch grinned and went back to reading the water, which was what he did best. The boy couldn't speak, but he could look at a stretch of brown water and tell you what it was thinking. He'd point left when the channel bent left before the bend existed, and twice now he'd saved them from snags that would've peeled the Kindly Hour open like a tin of sardines.

Tam went aft, where Widow Ash had rigged a pot of coffee strong enough to strip varnish. She poured him a cup without being asked, which was either kindness or surveillance.

"You look like a man rehearsing a speech," she said.

"I'm not."

"Mm." She stirred her own cup. "I buried three husbands, Tam Oriel. I know what rehearsal looks like."

He drank the coffee and didn't answer, and she let him be, which was her other great talent. Behind them, low and patient as a headache, the smoke of Hale's barge sat on the horizon. Iron hull, steam screw, a crane on her deck that could lift a locomotive. Tam knew her name without asking: *Meridian's Mercy*. He'd helped fit her crane bearings four years ago, back when he still took Hale's coin and told himself it was honest work because salvage was salvage, wasn't it, no matter whose secrets got dragged up out of the dark.

* * *

Tide three found them in the Gullet, where the Vell pinched between shale cliffs and ran fast enough to make the wheelhouse timbers groan. Emrys stood at the wheel with his collar open and his jaw set, steering by some private argument he was having with the river.

Nessa Quill was up in the bow with her grandfather's charts spread across a hatch cover, weighting the corners with coins from the chest, which everyone agreed was either blasphemy or poetry. She looked up as Tam passed.

"You've been circling," she said. Nessa had a cartographer's habit of noticing movement and naming it precisely. "All morning. Like a gull over a fish kill."

"Gulls are honest about it."

"So are you going to be?"

He looked at her. Freckles, ink-stained fingers, eyes that had already mapped every exit from this conversation. "After the sandbar," he said. "There's a thing I need to do first, and if I say it now, somebody might talk me out of it."

"That's usually how you know it's the right thing," Nessa said, and went back to her charts, leaving him holding that sentence like a hot coal.

The sandbar was Emrys's idea, or rather Emrys's memory, which amounted to the same thing. There was a reach above the Gullet called the Long Shallows, where the river braided itself into a dozen channels over gravel flats, and every year the spring floods rearranged the channels like a card sharp shuffling. No chart survived it. No pilot trusted it. But Emrys Thackeray had run the Long Shallows forty times in twenty years, and he claimed — with the serene confidence of a man who had never once been right about anything except boats — that the river kept habits.

"Rivers are lazy," he told them that evening, drawing in spilled coffee on the deck planks. "Water takes the deepest line and keeps taking it until the flood makes it take another. And floods come in patterns, same as everything. There's a bar here—" he tapped a spot, "—that sits just under the surface, soft as pudding, and every boat bigger than us that follows the main channel hits it and sticks. Hale's barge draws nine feet. We draw four."

"And you want to lead him onto it," Widow Ash said.

"I want to *invite* him onto it. Politely. Then keep sailing while he explains himself to the mud."

Finch clapped his hands, silent applause, and even Tam felt his mouth twitch. It was a good plan. It was also a plan that required someone to go ashore at the narrows below the bar and move a beacon lantern — the old navigation light on Snagtooth Point, which every pilot on the Vell trusted to mark the deep channel. Move it thirty yards upstream, and the barge's pilot would steer straight across the bar's soft heart.

"Somebody has to wade out there in the dark," Emrys said, and then his eyes did the terrible kind thing and skipped right past Finch and settled on Tam, because Tam was the only one aboard who'd worked lights and lines and night salvage, and everybody knew it, including Tam.

"I'll do it," Tam said.

And there it was. The coal in his hand, glowing.

* * *

Snagtooth Point at midnight was all wind and gravel and the smell of cold water. Tam rowed the skiff in alone, lantern oil in one pocket, Emrys's spare signal lamp in the other, and the whole time his heart kept time with a sentence he'd been avoiding for three days:

*I used to work for Corvane Hale.*

Not casually. Not for a season. Four years, Meridian Fleet, diver and rigger, and in the last year of it he'd stood in Hale's chart room and seen the search grids for the Karvenian wreck laid out in red ink, and he'd understood that this wasn't salvage. It was erasure. Hale wasn't hunting treasure; he was hunting paper. Names. And Tam had stayed anyway, because the pay was good and the alternative was poverty, and he'd told himself the oldest lie on the river: *not my business*.

Until the day he'd refused a dive order — a sealed compartment on a sunken packet boat, no questions allowed — and Hale had looked at him with those flat gray eyes and said, "You're a useful man, Oriel. Don't stop being useful." And Tam had walked off the fleet that night with nothing but his kit and a debt he'd been paying interest on ever since.

The beacon came easier than the confession. He waded the shallows, unshipped the lamp from its post, carried it thirty yards up the gravel spit, and set it burning where the bar lay waiting under its skin of silver water. Then he stood there in the wind, looking back at the Kindly Hour's riding lights downstream, and knew he couldn't row back aboard and keep the stone in his boot one more hour. They were going to trust him with their lives tomorrow. Finch had taught him the sign for *brother* last week, laughing, half a joke, and Tam had felt it land somewhere he hadn't armored.

He rowed back.

Emrys was awake in the wheelhouse, of course. The man slept like a cat on a clothesline. Tam climbed aboard and stood dripping on the deck, and Emrys looked at him and said, "Beacon's moved?" and Tam said, "Beacon's moved. And there's something else, and I'll say it once, and then you can put me off at the next landing if you want."

So he said it. All of it. The four years, the red-inked charts, the dive he refused, the flat gray eyes. His voice held steady until the end, and then it didn't quite.

Widow Ash appeared at the wheelhouse door with her arms folded. Nessa came up from below with a chart roll still under her arm. Finch stood in the shadows by the boiler, watching Tam's hands instead of his face, because Finch read hands the way other people read faces.

"Hale knows my name," Tam finished. "Knows my diving record, my debts, my mother's grave in Sallow Bend. If I wanted to sell you out, I'd have done it before the Gullet. Instead I just spent my midnight moving a lantern so we can wreck his barge." He swallowed. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to let me earn it. Starting tomorrow."

Nobody spoke. The river talked instead, slapping the hull, patient as ever.

It was Finch who moved first. The boy crossed the deck, took Tam's shaking right hand in both of his, and signed against it, slow, so there could be no mistake: *Debts get paid. That's all a family is.*

Then he looked at Emrys.

Emrys rubbed his jaw for a long moment, staring out at the black water. "I fished a drowned nation's chest out of the shallows," he said finally, "and the only honest thing anyone's told me since is standing on my deck dripping river water and confessing to crimes I didn't commit." A dry sound that was almost a laugh. "Get below, Oriel. You're on the skiff at dawn. And Tam —"

"Yeah?"

"When this is over, you're telling me everything else. Every last bit. Over Widow Ash's coffee, which will be punishment enough."

Behind him, Widow Ash said, "It certainly will," and for the first time in four years, Tam Oriel laughed out loud.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold, and with it, far downstream, came the *Meridian's Mercy*.

She came up the Long Shallows at full steam, smokestack rolling, bow wave white and confident, following the beacon light on Snagtooth Point exactly as her pilot had done a hundred safe times before. From the Kindly Hour's stern, hidden in the willow shadow of the far bank, they watched her swing toward the light, toward the deep channel that wasn't there anymore.

"She's going to hit," Finch signed, bouncing on his toes.

"She's going to *stick*," Emrys corrected, and there was a boyish gleam in the old captain's eye that Tam suspected had gotten him thrown out of better ports than Sallow Bend.

The barge's bow touched the bar with a shudder they could feel through a mile of water. Her screws churned. Mud boiled up around her flanks, brown and triumphant, and the great iron hull settled by the head, stuck fast, nine feet of draft married to four feet of pudding. Through the morning air, faint and furious, came the sound of Corvane Hale's crew learning new vocabulary.

"A night's lead," Nessa said softly, marking it on her chart. "Maybe more, if they have to lighten ship."

Emrys rang the engine bell twice, and the Kindly Hour swung upstream toward the high country, toward the glacier, toward the wreck beneath the Serpent's Tooth.

Tam stood at the rail and watched Hale's barge shrink behind them, stuck in the river like a splinter, and felt the stone finally leave his boot — replaced, he noticed, by something heavier and warmer that he decided not to examine until after the next tide.

Because far ahead, past the last farms, the mountains had come into view. And crowning them, white and blue-green and ancient, hung the icefall they'd been racing toward.

Even from here, it was calving.
