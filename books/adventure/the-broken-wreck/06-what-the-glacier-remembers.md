## 6. What the Glacier Remembers

Nessa Quill had spent eleven years drawing maps for men who used them to move armies, and she had learned one thing above all others: a map never lies, but it will absolutely let you lie to yourself.

She was lying to herself now, and she knew it, because she had told the crew that her grandfather's charts would make everything plain, and here in the frozen high country they were making nothing of the kind. The paper was older than she was, soft as moth wings, and her grandfather Aldous had drawn in a hand that could only be described as deliberately difficult. He had been the royal cartographer of Karvenia in its last year, which meant he'd been charting a country while it drowned, and perhaps that was why every contour line seemed to carry a small private grudge.

"Your grandfather," Tam Oriel said, leaning over her shoulder in the cabin of the Kindly Hour, which was no longer floating so much as being held prisoner by the river ice, "wrote his numbers in mirror script."

"He did. He said it kept casual thieves honest."

"Did it keep you honest?"

"It kept me up all night with a hand mirror and a headache," Nessa said. "Which is a form of honesty, I suppose."

Outside, the cold had a texture. Downriver, cold had been a wet thing, a thing that got into your boots. Up here, at the top of the world where the Vell was born, the cold was dry and enormous and it pressed on the cabin roof like a patient animal that had decided, for now, not to eat them.

Widow Ash set down three cups of something that was technically tea and spiritually an act of aggression. Finch sat cross-legged on the floor with the charts spread around him, and he studied them the way he studied water — silently, completely, with his whole body tilted toward the problem. He couldn't speak, but he had a way of tapping a finger twice when something was wrong and once when something was right, and Nessa had learned in three days that this made him a better proofreader than any clerk she'd ever employed.

She turned the mirror-script page and her stomach dropped.

"There," she said. "The Serpent's Tooth."

The icefall was drawn as a jagged column of hatched lines, and beneath it, in Aldous's cramped mirror hand, a notation she translated twice to be sure: *The treasury lies under the tooth's root. It breathes only when the mountain exhales. Six days, no more. Come after, and the door is stone.*

"Breathes when the mountain exhales," Tam repeated. "That's calving."

"That's calving," Nessa agreed. "The icefall sheds its face in the warm weeks, and for a few days there's a gap under the Serpent's Tooth — a tunnel of open water where the wreck sits. My grandfather charted it the year before the collapse. He was on the last survey ship." She paused. "The one that didn't come home."

The cabin went quiet. Even the stove seemed to burn more politely.

Emrys stood in the doorway with river ice in his beard, and Nessa watched him take in the chart, the mirror script, the six-day window, and do the arithmetic every captain does — distance divided by time, minus everything that goes wrong.

"Then we're not racing Hale anymore," Emrys said. "We're racing the mountain. And the mountain doesn't take bribes."

"No," Nessa said. "But it does take idiots. My grandfather's notes are very clear on that point as well."

* * *

They made the high country in two days of hauling and cursing, the Kindly Hour left moored in a side channel where Finch had found a pocket of deep water that would hold her through the freeze. Above the treeline the world turned white and blue and utterly indifferent to human plans, and Nessa, who had drawn mountains for a living, discovered that drawing them and climbing them were entirely different professions.

It was Finch who found the approach.

They had lost the trail under a fresh snowfall, and Nessa had her grandfather's chart open against her knee, matching contour lines to a slope that refused to match anything, when Finch walked out ahead, stopped, and tapped the ice twice.

Wrong.

He led them left, along a shelf Nessa's chart showed as a crevasse field — except the crevasses were bridged with old snow, and Finch read the bridges the way he read water, finding the solid crossings by some language of light and hollow sound that Nessa could not begin to learn. Twice he stopped them with a raised hand and made them walk single file, wide apart, and the second time, Tam probed behind them with an ice axe and found the bridge they'd just crossed was thin as a hymnal.

"He's not reading the map," Tam said quietly, to Nessa. "He's reading the mountain. Your grandfather drew it. This boy is *asking* it."

Nessa looked at the mute boy out ahead of them, small against all that white, patient as a heron, and felt something in her chest that eleven years of disgraced cartography had no symbol for.

* * *

The avalanche came on the third afternoon, and it came without ceremony.

They were traversing below the Serpent's Tooth itself by then — close enough that Nessa could see the icefall's face hanging over them like a wave that had forgotten to fall — when Finch, out ahead as always, stopped and tapped twice and then tapped twice again, fast, which was a language none of them had heard from him before.

The mountain answered.

It began as a sound Nessa felt in her teeth before she heard it — a deep, tearing groan, like the world's largest door opening on the world's largest hinge — and then the slope above them moved. Not all of it. Just a shelf of wind-loaded snow the size of a chapel, and it came down the chute directly toward the small dark figure of a boy who had turned, very calmly, to look at them, as if to say *I did tell you twice.*

"FINCH!" Emrys was moving before the sound finished, and Nessa would remember for the rest of her life that the captain did not run away from the slide but *across* it, angling uphill, into the path, arms wide like a man wading into surf. The snow took Finch at the knees, then the waist, and Emrys hit the edge of the flow at a dead run and went down with him, and then the mountain had them both and the world was nothing but white roar.

Then it stopped. Avalanches are like that — a lifetime of noise and then a silence so complete your own heartbeat sounds like an intruder.

They dug with hands and axe-blades and Widow Ash's iron ladle, which she had carried up a mountain for reasons she declined to explain, and they found Emrys first, facedown, one arm locked forward in the snow like a swimmer's stroke, still gripping the back of Finch's collar. Finch came out blue-lipped and coughing, and when Tam pulled the boy's scarf from his mouth, Finch looked at Emrys — snow-crusted, sixty years old, wheezing like a bellows — and tapped the captain's shoulder once.

Right.

Emrys laughed, or something near it, and lay back in the crater they'd dug, and said, to the sky, to all of them, "Boy, next time you tap twice, we *leave*."

Finch tapped twice more, solemnly, on Emrys's own chest, and Nessa understood he was saying: *the mountain tapped first. I only translated.*

* * *

That night they made fire in the lee of a stone overhang, and the cold came down so hard the stars seemed to ring like struck glass. Widow Ash produced food from a pot that Nessa privately suspected was bottomless, and for a while nobody talked, because the body wants quiet after it has nearly been erased.

It was Tam who broke it, in the end, because Tam always broke things.

"I keep waiting for one of you to ask me," he said, to the fire.

"Ask you what?" Widow Ash said, in the tone of a woman who already knew and was deciding whether to be kind.

"Whether I'm going to sell you all to Hale. Whether that's still in me." He turned his hands toward the flame. "I worked salvage fleets eleven years. You don't leave that clean. There's a version of me that Hale would say he recognizes, and some nights I hear that version clearing his throat."

Nobody spoke. Nessa watched Emrys, and Emrys watched the fire, and then the captain reached over and took one of the flat stones from the fire's edge — the ones they'd been using to warm their boots — and set it in front of Tam.

"Stone's hot," Emrys said. "Hold it as long as you like. Put it down when you want. Nobody's going to make you carry it, and nobody's going to make you drop it. That's the whole arrangement."

Tam held the stone. His face did something complicated. Then he laughed — a short, cracked sound — and set it down between them all, and Widow Ash immediately used the freed-up space to hand him more stew.

"Your grandfather," Tam said to Nessa, nodding at the charts spread on the sledge, "drew the Vell delta when he was what, seventy?"

"Seventy-three. He said his hands shook too much for fine work, so he started drawing *true* instead of *neat*. It's all in his margins — every correction, every doubt. *Here the channel lied to me. Here I trusted the wrong star.*" She smiled despite herself. "I used to think the margins were the failure of the map. Now I think they're the map."

"That's the crew, that is," Widow Ash said, ladle raised like a scepter. "A lot of margins. Very little neat."

Finch, wrapped in every blanket they owned, drew in the snow with one finger. He drew the Serpent's Tooth, and beneath it a shape like a ship, and then — after a pause — he drew six more figures around a fire, small and lopsided and unmistakably them. He tapped the drawing once.

Right.

Nessa looked at the little snow family and at the people around the fire — a captain who ran toward avalanches, a cook with an infinite pot, a diver holding warm stones and old ghosts, a boy who spoke mountain — and she thought of her grandfather's last margin note, the one she'd found only that morning, translated at dawn by mirror-light:

*The country is lost. The names are not. Whoever carries this chart: the treasure is the list. The gold is only the paperweight.*

She had not told them yet. She looked at the fire, and at Emrys, and decided she would tell them at the Tooth itself, when it mattered.

Above them, the Serpent's Tooth stood black against the stars, and somewhere in its frozen face, a door waited to breathe open — six days, no more — and far downriver, Nessa would have sworn she could feel it, like a cold draft under a door: the steady, patient grind of an iron barge, coming on.
