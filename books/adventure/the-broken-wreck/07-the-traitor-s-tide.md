## 7. The Traitor's Tide

Tam Oriel had spent eleven years learning to read the moods of iron barges, and the one grinding up the narrows behind them had murder in its engine.

He heard it before anyone else did — a low, patient thrum beneath the river's chatter, the sound of something that did not need to hurry because it knew it would arrive. He was on deck with a mug of Widow Ash's chicory when the fog ahead of them thinned and he saw the shape of the Serpent's Tooth narrows opening like a jaw, and behind them, half a mile back and closing, the black silhouette of Hale's barge wearing its crane like a gallows.

"Emrys," Tam said quietly. "Company."

Emrys Thackeray came out of the wheelhouse wiping his hands on a rag that had seen better decades. He looked back once, the way a man looks at weather he'd been hoping to outrun.

"How long have you known?"

"Since midnight. I didn't want to say until I was sure." Tam set down his mug. "I'm sure."

The rest of the crew gathered the way small crews do — Finch first, silent as ever, already at the rail reading the water; then Nessa with her grandfather's charts rolled under her arm; then Widow Ash last, carrying a ladle as though it were a sidearm.

"He's gaining," Nessa said.

"He's always gaining," Emrys said. "That's the whole trick of him."

* * *

They ran the first bend hard, the Kindly Hour leaning into the current like an old woman hurrying to catch a door before it shut. The narrows ahead were a corridor of granite and white water, and somewhere in that corridor was the channel Emrys knew by heart and Hale knew only from charts bought secondhand.

But Hale had something better than charts. He had a voice.

It came across the water on the speaking-horn, amplified and pleasant, the way a knife is pleasant right up until it isn't.

"Captain Thackeray. A pleasure, as always. Might I have a word with Mr. Oriel?"

Every head on deck turned toward Tam.

Tam's hands went cold around the rail. Eleven years ago he had stood on a barge very much like that one, in a diving suit very much like the ones stowed below, and taken Hale's coin for work he still couldn't say aloud. He had told himself the confession on the sandbar night had settled it — that leading Hale's search boats into the wrong fork of the Vell had paid the debt. But debts like that don't get paid. They get refinanced.

"He can't have a word with you," Emrys said. "You're busy."

"I can hear him fine," Tam said.

"Tam—"

"He's going to talk whether I listen or not. Better I listen than Finch." Tam stepped to the stern rail. "Go on, Director. I'm listening."

"You were the best diver I ever employed," Hale's voice said, warm as bathwater. "I want you to know that whatever happens today, that hasn't changed. So here is my offer, and I'll make it only once. Tell me where Thackeray means to run, tell me which channel, and when this is over you walk away clean. Full pardon. My lawyers are very good, and the things you did for me are the sort of things that go away entirely when a man cooperates. You could dive again, Oriel. Properly. Your own boat, if you wanted it. You're what, forty-one? Forty-two? You have twenty good years left in your shoulders. Do you want to spend them rotting on this floating kitchen?"

Behind Tam, Widow Ash made a small offended noise about her kitchen.

Tam looked at the water. It was green-gray and moving fast, and in it he could see, if he let himself, the faces of the Karvenian survivors he'd helped pull from the cold forty years ago — no. Not pulled. That was the lie he'd carried. He hadn't pulled anyone. He'd been nineteen and new to Hale's fleet, and what he'd actually done was worse than anything the pardon could cover, and Hale knew it, and that was why the offer was so generous.

"I need a minute," Tam called back.

"Take two," said Hale. "The river's wide, but not that wide."

* * *

They found him at the bow, alone, which lasted four seconds before Emrys sat down beside him on the hatch cover, and then three more before Finch appeared at his elbow without appearing to walk there, and then Widow Ash simply arrived, ladle and all, declaring that nobody made life decisions on an empty stomach and pressing a heel of bread into his hands.

"I'm going to tell you all something," Tam said. "And then I'm going to do something, and I'd take it kindly if you'd let me do both before anybody hits me."

"Nobody's hitting anybody," Emrys said. "Probably."

Tam turned the bread over in his hands. Forty years of holding a thing inside makes a man clumsy when he finally sets it down.

"The story I've told you — that I worked salvage for Hale, that I quit when I saw what he was — it's true as far as it goes. But it doesn't go far enough. When the Karvenia broke on the narrows, there were survivors. Three longboats got off the wreck before she went down. Twenty-two people. They were making for the south bank, for the villages. And Hale—" Tam's voice caught, and Finch put a small hand on his forearm, and that steadied him enough to finish. "Hale ordered us to run them down. Said witnesses were worth more dead than the rescue was worth alive. I was in the water that night. I was in the water, and I swam for the longboats, and I reached the second one, and I held a child's hand for maybe ten seconds before the barge came through. Ten seconds. Then nothing. I've counted those ten seconds every day for forty years."

Nobody spoke. The river spoke for them, loud in the narrows.

"Hale sank the survivor boats to hide the theft," Tam said. "The ledger names the families he robbed. But the other thing — the thing that would hang him in any court that still respects rope — is those boats. And I'm the only living soul who knows it besides him. That's why the pardon. That's why it's generous. He's not buying a channel, Emrys. He's buying the last witness."

Widow Ash sat down heavily on the other side of him. "Eat your bread," she said, which in her language meant several things, none of them about bread.

"And now?" Emrys asked. "What's the doing part?"

"Now I answer him." Tam stood. "And I'm going to lie to him so beautifully he'll weep."

* * *

He went to the stern rail and waited until the speaking-horn crackled again, impatient now, the warmth gone out of it.

"Well, Oriel?"

"East fork," Tam said. "Thackeray's taking the east fork. There's a shelf of gravel under the third bend — the Kindly Hour draws too much to clear it loaded, but your barge won't even feel it. He thinks I don't know he knows I know the west fork's a death trap, so he's committed east. You can be ahead of him in twenty minutes if you push your engines."

A pause. Tam could hear Hale thinking, could hear the arithmetic of forty years of knowing each other running behind the silence.

"You understand," Hale said at last, softly, "that if this is false, our arrangement dissolves. And arrangements dissolving have consequences."

"If it's false," Tam said, "you'll have plenty of time to punish me, because I'll be standing on this deck when you come alongside. That's how sure I am."

Another silence. Longer.

"East fork," Hale repeated. "Third bend."

"Third bend. God keep you, Director."

The horn clicked off. On the barge, engines changed pitch — deepened, quickened, swung wide toward the eastern channel. The black shape slid behind a spur of granite and was gone.

Tam stood at the rail until his legs remembered how to hold him.

"West fork," Emrys said beside him. It wasn't a question.

"West fork. The one that nearly killed us both in 'sixty-one, and the one he'll never take, because I just told him it's a death trap, and the worst part—" Tam laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle, "—the worst part is it mostly is one. We're going to die in a channel I recommended against."

"We nearly died in six channels getting here," Nessa called from the wheelhouse. "One more is practically tradition."

Finch tugged Tam's sleeve. The boy looked up at him, and then, deliberately, slowly, pressed his own fist against his chest — once, twice — the little gesture he used instead of words when words weren't big enough. Tam had seen him use it for the fire after the avalanche. For Emrys pulling him off the ice.

He had not expected it to be aimed at him.

"Don't," Tam said thickly. "Don't you do that. I ran those boats down, boy. Whatever you think I am—"

Finch pressed the fist again. Twice. Unmoved. Then he pointed at the western fork, where the water ran white and furious between walls of black stone, and grinned the gap-toothed grin of someone who reads rivers the way priests read scripture, and made it unmistakably clear that he thought the death trap looked like fun.

"Widow Ash," Emrys said, taking the wheel, "batten everything. Nessa, charts. Tam—" he glanced back, and there was no judgment in his face, only the tired, stubborn warmth of a man who had decided a long time ago who his people were, "—you're on the lead line. You know these waters better than any of us now. All of them. Both sides of your life."

Tam took the lead line in hands that wouldn't quite stop shaking.

"Aye, Captain," he said. "Both sides."

Ahead, the west fork opened its gray throat, and behind them, faint but growing, came the first ugly crunch of iron meeting gravel in the east — Hale's barge finding the third bend, finding the shelf, finding the beautiful lie — and Tam Oriel, traitor to one captain and finally faithful to another, swung the lead and began to call the depths.
