## 8. Serpent's Tooth

The calving window opened the way Widow Ash said it would — not with a roar but with a kind of held breath, the glacier going quiet as a congregation before the sermon. For three days the Serpent's Tooth icefall had groaned and spat at the lake below it, shedding shards the size of church bells. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, it simply stopped.

"Window's open," Tam said, standing at the rail of the Kindly Hour with his diving helmet under one arm like a hat he'd rather not wear. "Three tides, maybe four. After that she'll start talking again, and we don't want to be in her mouth when she does."

Emrys looked up at the icefall. It was a terrible beautiful thing, a wall of blue-green ice two hundred feet high, veined with grit and old stone, hanging over the water like a jaw deciding whether to bite. Forty years ago a Karvenian treasury ship had come down the narrows beneath that jaw and never come out. The river had kept its receipts. Now, for a few tides only, it was willing to show them.

"Nessa," Emrys called down the hatch. "How close can I bring her?"

Nessa Quill emerged with her grandfather's charts rolled under her arm and squinted at the icefall the way she squinted at everything — as if it were lying to her personally. "Two hundred yards off the face. No closer. The chart says the wreck sits in forty feet of water on a gravel shelf, just past where the melt current curls left." She tapped a smudged X drawn in faded iron-gall ink. "My grandfather marked it *the tooth's shadow*. He was very pleased with himself about the name."

"He sounds like a man after my own heart," Emrys said.

"He sounds like a man who got three expeditions killed," Nessa said, but there was no edge in it. Grief, maybe. The gentle kind that lives next door to love.

They anchored in the tooth's shadow as the morning fog burned off. Finch perched in the rigging — he'd hardly been out of it since the avalanche, as though the water had betrayed him once and he intended to keep an eye on it forever after — and made his silent reading of the surface. Then he raised one hand, flat: calm. Two fingers walking: current, moving left, just as Nessa said. Finally he pointed down, twice, and looked at Emrys with an expression that on a talking boy would have been *there it is*.

"You're sure?" Emrys asked him.

Finch nodded. Then, after a moment, he climbed down and pressed something into Emrys's palm: the broken compass rose they'd fished from the chest at Sallow Bend, the brass half-rose with its missing eastern points. He'd carried it every day since, turning it over, studying it. He pointed at it now, then at the water, then at his own chest.

"You want to dive for its other half," Emrys said slowly. "You think it's down there."

Finch nodded again, fierce.

Tam snorted. "Boy's got salvage fever. I've seen it take grown men. They start believing objects call to them across the water." He paused. "Course, in my experience the objects usually do."

"That's not comforting, Tam."

"It wasn't meant to be."

* * *

They went down in pairs because Tam insisted, and Tam knew diving the way Emrys knew the Vell's sandbars — in his bones, with all the scars bones collect. Tam went first, into water so cold it felt less like liquid than like argument. Emrys followed down the shot line, the world narrowing from gray sky to gray-green murk to a darkness lit only by their lamps, and then —

There it was.

The Karvenian ship lay on her side on the gravel shelf, masts snapped, hull furred with forty years of silt and pale cold-loving growth, but whole. Whole in a way that made Emrys's throat tighten behind the mouthpiece. She hadn't sunk in a storm. She'd been *put* here. He could see it even through the murk — the neat scuttle holes cut low along her flank, too regular for battle damage, too deliberate for accident. Somebody had opened her veins and let the river finish the work.

Tam swam the length of the hull, lamp sweeping, and stopped at the stern where a name was still legible under the silt: *Vow of Karven*. He turned to Emrys and made the gesture they'd agreed on — fist to chest, then open palm. *Confirmed.*

The hold was a cathedral of silt. They worked by feel mostly, Tam's hands reading the dark the way Finch read water. Coins first — hundreds of them, fused into bricks, stamped with the double-headed eagle of a nation that no longer existed. And beneath the coin bricks, lashed into an iron frame that some careful dead officer had bolted to the deck beams, a second chest. Smaller than the first. Sealed with wax that had gone the color of old teeth.

Emrys worked the frame loose while Tam held the lamp steady. When the chest came free it came light — too light — and Emrys understood before he opened it what he'd find. Not gold. Paper. Half a ledger's worth of paper, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as a sermon. The names of every family the old regime had robbed, written in a clerk's patient hand. And tucked against the ledger's spine, folded twice, a dispatch bearing the seal of the Meridian Salvage Fleet — dated thirty-nine years ago, signed in a young man's confident script: *C. Hale, Salvage Master.* The orders were brief. *No survivors. No witnesses. The treasury is lost; see that it stays lost.*

Emrys read it twice by lamplight, forty feet under a glacier, and felt something in his chest go very quiet and very hard.

Hale hadn't spent forty years hunting the wreck. Hale had spent forty years hunting the *proof*. The survivor boats hadn't gone down in the calving surge, whatever the official record said. They'd been sunk by a salvage master with a claim to protect and a conscience he'd apparently misplaced somewhere around his twenties.

Tam was watching him. Through the faceplate Emrys couldn't see his eyes well, but he could see the set of his jaw, and he could imagine what Tam was thinking — that Tam had been a young diver in those years, that Tam might have been on a Meridian crew, that the water Tam loved had been used for murder by the man whose bread Tam had once eaten.

Emrys put a hand on his shoulder plate. Later, the gesture said. Above water, with words.

They rose slow, the way Tam demanded, the chest slung between them, and broke the surface into sunlight so bright it seemed rude.

* * *

Widow Ash met them at the rail with blankets and a flask of something that smelled like it could strip barnacles. Nessa took one look at the dispatch in Emrys's hand and sat down right there on the deck planks, charts and all.

"That's his seal," she whispered. "That's Hale's seal, from before — before anyone knew who he was." Her grandfather had charted these waters for Meridian once, long ago. Her grandfather had also died poor and discredited, his maps called forgeries, his reputation picked clean. "Emrys. This isn't just the families' proof. This is *his* proof. It clears my grandfather's name."

"It does more than that," Emrys said. "It hangs him."

"Then let's hang it high," Tam said, and for once nobody laughed at the grimness, because it deserved the room.

It was Finch who spoiled the moment, which was perhaps fitting, since Finch had earned the right. He was standing at the rail, staring down at the water, both hands gripping the wood, making the flat-palm sign — calm — over and over, except his hands weren't calm at all. They were shaking.

Then he spun and pointed up.

At the Tooth.

The icefall had begun to talk again. Not the deep groans of the past week — something sharper, quicker, a crackling like green wood in a hot fire, coming faster and faster. The window was closing early. Glaciers, Emrys thought wildly, kept worse hours than debt collectors.

"The chest," Tam said. "We have what we came for. We weigh anchor *now*."

But Finch wasn't looking at the icefall anymore. He was looking at the water, and then at Emrys, and then he did something he had never done in all the weeks they'd known him. He made a sound — small, urgent, wordless, but a sound — and dove off the rail.

"FINCH!" Nessa was on her feet. "What is he —"

Emrys knew. God help him, he knew. The compass rose. The boy had been carrying that broken brass rose for weeks like a talisman, and somewhere in that boy's water-reading soul lived the certainty that its other half lay on the wreck, and the window was closing, and Finch did not believe in windows.

Emrys was moving before he decided to move. Boots off, coat off, the cold of the air nothing next to what waited below. Tam was already stripping his gear back on, swearing steadily in three languages. "Forty feet! In closing water! The boy's mad!"

"He's one of ours," Emrys said, and went over the side.

The cold hit like a fist. Down the shot line, lamp beam swinging, lungs already complaining. On the shelf the Kindly Hour's anchor light filtered down faint and green, and beside it a smaller light moved — Finch, in nothing but his clothes, kicking for the wreck's stern with the terrible efficient grace of an otter. Emrys caught him at the hull and grabbed his collar, and Finch fought him — actually fought him, twisting toward a glint in the silt near the rudder post. There, wedged in the wreckage of the stern lantern, brass catching lamplight: the missing half of the compass rose, points east, exactly where a falling lantern would have pinned it forty years ago.

Finch wrenched it free. And the glacier, as if it had been waiting for precisely that, spoke.

Not cracked this time. *Boomed.* The whole icefall shifted, a soundless visible shudder running up the blue wall, and the water around them trembled, and from far above came the first white thunder of a face letting go.

They kicked for the line together, Finch clutching the brass half-rose to his chest, Emrys hauling him by the collar when his kicks faltered, the pressure wave arriving behind them like a hand shoving them upward. They broke the surface into chaos — Tam's arm hooking Emrys, Widow Ash hauling Finch over the rail, Nessa screaming the anchor loose, the Kindly Hour already leaning into the current as the first house-sized slab of the Serpent's Tooth calved off and hit the lake with a boom that Emrys felt in his teeth, in his ribs, in the soles of his feet.

The wave lifted the boat like a toy and set her down hard, shipping half a ton of freezing water, and then they were through it, running downstream with the flood, the icefall roaring behind them like a door slamming on forty years.

* * *

An hour later, wrapped in every blanket aboard, Finch sat on the deck prying the two halves of the compass rose together. They fit. Of course they fit. Brass remembers its shape. He held the whole rose up to Emrys, and his eyes were wet, and he pointed north — no. He pointed *upriver*, back toward the narrows, toward the outlet where Hale's barge would be waiting, because Hale always waited, Hale had forty years of waiting in him.

Emrys took the rose and turned it in his hands. East points restored. A drowned nation's needle, pointing home.

"We've got the proof," Nessa said softly, the ledger and the dispatch safe in oilcloth against her chest. "We've got everything."

Emrys looked upriver, where the narrows narrowed and the water ran quick and dark, and felt the triumph sit in his chest like swallowed river-stone.

"No," he said. "Now we've got everything *to lose*."
