## 9. The Scuttling

The outlet of the Serpent's Tooth narrows was a throat of gray water barely wide enough for two boats abreast, and Corvane Hale's barge filled one side of it like a cork in a bottle.

Emrys stood at the wheel of the *Kindly Hour* and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since dawn. The barge — iron-hulled, steam-driven, its crane arm folded flat like a mantis at prayer — sat squarely across the channel with maybe forty feet of open water to port. Forty feet. The *Kindly Hour* drew six. He could thread it, if the current behaved, if his hull held, if Finch's reading of the water was right.

Three ifs too many.

"He's signaling," Nessa said from the rail, spyglass up. "Flags. It says... 'Parley.'"

"It would," Emrys said. "He doesn't want us sunk. Sunk men can't hand over ledgers."

Tam came up the companionway with the particular heaviness of a man carrying news he'd already rehearsed and hated. "Current's turning. We've got maybe an hour before the tide slacks off, and after that—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Everyone on deck had heard the glacier talking all morning, that deep groaning under the wind, like an old man shifting in bed before deciding whether to get up.

When it decided to get up, the calving season would begin in earnest, and the route behind them — and ahead of them — would close for another year.

"Emrys." Widow Ash appeared at his elbow with coffee she'd doctored until it qualified as soup. She pressed the mug into his hands and studied him with the frank, unimpressed gaze of a woman who had buried a husband, outlived three captains, and stopped being fooled by any of them somewhere around her fortieth year. "You've got your thinking face on."

"Do I?"

"You look like you're about to apologize to somebody."

He almost smiled. "Might be."

"To who?"

He looked at the boat. His boat. Twenty-two years of river mud and patched planking and a wheel he could steer blindfolded through Sallow Bend's worst fog. The *Kindly Hour* had been his father's before it was his, and before that it had been nobody's, and it had never once let him down except in the ways boats let everyone down eventually, which is to say financially.

"I'll tell you when I know myself," he said.

Hale's launch put off from the barge at midmorning — a sleek black thing with four men aboard and Hale himself standing at the bow like a monument to somebody else's vanity. He came up the ladder uninvited, brushed past Tam without acknowledgment, and surveyed the deck of the *Kindly Hour* with the expression of a man inspecting a stable he intended to burn.

"Captain Thackeray." Hale was smaller than his reputation, which somehow made him worse. Neat gray beard, neat gray coat, eyes like coins left too long in water. "I'll make this brief, because neither of us enjoys the other's company."

"That's the first honest thing you've said."

"The ledger." Hale extended one gloved hand, palm up, as though Emrys might simply set forty years of stolen names into it like change. "In exchange: full pardon. Your debts cleared. Your name cleared. Passage home on my own dime. You'll never see me again."

Behind him, Emrys heard Finch shift at the rail — the boy couldn't speak but he made a sound sometimes, low and urgent, when water told him something. Right now he was looking at the channel, then at the sky, then back at the channel, and his hands were moving fast.

Nessa translated quietly. "The icefall's going early. Hours, not days. And the current..." She paused. "Finch says the current's about to make a decision for us."

Emrys set down the coffee. He thought about his father's name in that ledger, written in a clerk's careful hand among hundreds of others — families robbed so thoroughly that even their grief had been confiscated. He thought about the pardon, warm and clean, like a bed in a house he'd never been able to afford. He thought about how easy it would be. That was the terrible part. How easy.

"Here's my counteroffer," Emrys said. "You turn your barge around, go back downriver, and spend what's left of your life being a smaller man than you already are."

Hale's face didn't move. "You'd trade everything for paper."

"Not paper." Emrys touched his breast pocket, where the ledger rode against his ribs like a second heartbeat. "Names. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered behind Hale's coin-colored eyes. Not anger. Recognition. The look of a man hearing, very faintly, a bell he'd spent forty years pretending wasn't ringing.

Then it closed over, and he smiled. "I have salvage rights signed by three governments, Captain. Whatever you think you're doing—"

"I'm doing arithmetic," Emrys said. "Get off my boat."

* * *

They got him off — barely, and not politely — and the launch went back across the channel, and then there was nothing to do but stand at the wheel while the hour burned down around them.

It was Widow Ash who said it first, because Widow Ash always said the unsayable things first, usually while stirring something. "You're going to sink her, aren't you."

Not a question. Emrys's hands tightened on the wheel.

"The channel's narrow enough," he said. "If she goes down broadside, right at the throat of the outlet, nothing bigger than a dinghy gets through. Not his barge. Not anything. The glacier does the rest — when it calves, the ice seals the upper route for the season. He'd be trapped above the wreck with no way to move his salvage gear, no court that could reach him up here, and no ledger." He swallowed. "And no *Kindly Hour*."

Silence on the deck. The kind of silence that has weight.

"She's the last thing I had of his," Emrys said, quieter. "My father's. Everything else got taken. The debt collectors took the house, the papers, the nameplate off the door. This boat is the only receipt I ever kept." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "And now I'm supposed to give her up to buy justice for a hundred families I never met."

"No," Widow Ash said. "You're not *supposed* to. That's the point. Nobody's making you." She took the coffee mug out of his hands, which was her way of holding them. "You get to choose. That's what makes it worth something."

Tam spoke from the stern, arms crossed, voice rough. "I worked for Hale eleven years. I watched him buy judges, drown witnesses, forge claim papers with breakfast still on the table. If that man gets downriver with those rights intact, he'll strip every wreck on the Vell and bury every name attached to them." A pause. "Sink her, Captain. I'll say the eulogy. I'm good at funerals."

Finch tugged Emrys's sleeve and pointed — at the channel, at the barge, at the water sliding past in long green ropes. Then he made his low sound and drew one finger across his throat, then spread both hands wide, palms up, like a man releasing a bird.

Nessa's voice caught slightly. "He says the river will take care of her. He says boats don't belong to people. They belong to the water; we just borrow them."

Emrys looked at his crew. His castoffs. His family, though none of them had ever said it aloud, because saying it aloud seemed like the sort of thing that jinxed boats.

"All right," he said. "All right. But we do it right. Ash, get everyone and everything onto the skiff. Provisions, the charts, the ledgers — both halves. Tam, we'll need her scuttled broadside, which means opening her seams midship and putting her over hard. Nessa—" he hesitated, "—you'll want to mark the position properly. For the record. Somebody should know where she lies."

"Somebody should know *why*," Nessa said, and went below for her instruments.

* * *

They ran it at slack tide, which Finch insisted was the only honest time to lie to a river.

The plan was simple in the way that terrible plans are simple. They'd bring the *Kindly Hour* in slow, dead slow, angled across the throat of the outlet. At the last moment Emrys would swing her broadside, Tam would blow the seams with the black powder they'd carried since the narrows, and they'd take to the skiff as she settled. Forty feet of old riverboat, sunk square across a forty-foot gap. A lock made of grief.

Hale saw them coming, of course. The barge's whistle sounded — one long blast, then another, the signal for *heave to or be fired upon*, and Emrys noted with distant interest that the barge had mounted a swivel gun he hadn't seen before. But Hale wouldn't fire. Not yet. Firing meant wreckage scattered across the channel instead of a clean blockade, and besides, Hale still believed the ledger could be bought, because men like Hale believed everything could.

The *Kindly Hour* slid into the throat of the outlet. The barge loomed off the starboard bow, close enough now that Emrys could see the men along her rail, and Hale at the center of them, and the claim papers in Hale's fist — actual paper documents, fluttering, as though governments were weather and he'd caught some in a jar.

Through the wheelhouse glass, Emrys looked at his own reflection ghosted over the channel, and beyond it at the wheel worn smooth by his father's hands and then his own.

"Thank you," he said to the boat. "For twenty-two years. For the fog runs and the sandbars and the night we outran the flood at Greywater Bend. You were better to me than I deserved."

Then he rang the engine bell twice — *full astern, then hard over* — and swung her.

The old girl answered like she knew. She came around broadside to the current with a groan of timber and a shudder Emrys felt in his teeth, and Tam lit the fuse, and the world went white and loud and then suddenly, terribly quiet.

She settled fast. Riverboats do; they're built to sit in water, not fight it. Emrys stood in the skiff fifty yards downstream and watched his father's boat take the green water over her gunwales, roll once, gently, like a tired animal lying down, and go under with her decks level and her nameboard facing the sky.

The channel sealed behind her like a wound closing.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, and Emrys only ever assembled it afterward from pieces.

Hale's barge tried to force the gap anyway — of course it did; pride is a rudderless thing. The iron hull struck the submerged deck of the *Kindly Hour* with a boom that rolled up the valley walls, backed off, and tried again, and the second time the old riverboat's mast, snapped and tilted, tore a gash in the barge's plating below the waterline that no amount of pumping would answer.

And then the glacier made its decision.

The sound came first — not a crack but a *report*, like the mountain clearing its throat — and then the face of the Serpent's Tooth let go. Ice the size of churches calved into the upper channel, and the wave it raised came down the outlet like the hand of something with opinions.

The barge, holed and heavy, didn't stand a chance. Emrys saw men go over the rail, saw a launch get away with three of them clinging, saw Hale — Hale, who had reached the bridge wing with his claim papers still clutched in one fist, as though the documents might argue with the water — go down with his vessel when she rolled.

The papers floated. Emrys watched them go, little white ghosts on the brown flood, dissolving into pulp, the signatures of three governments returning to ink and then to nothing.

The wave lifted the skiff like a leaf and set it down, spinning, in the calmer water below the outlet, and when Emrys got his bearings the crew was counting heads — Ash, Tam, Nessa, Finch, himself — five heads, five heartbeats, one ledger dry inside its oilskin.

Above them, the glacier kept calving, ice thundering into the channel, sealing the high route behind them for another season. Sealing the wreck. Sealing the *Kindly Hour*. Sealing Corvane Hale and his claims beneath a hundred fathoms of cold ambition.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Then Finch leaned over the gunwale of the skiff, looked down into the dark water where the channel bent away toward the low country, and made his low sound — soft this time, almost tender.

"What's he saying?" Emrys asked.

Nessa wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "He says the river accepted her." A breath. "And that she's keeping the channel company until spring."

Emrys nodded and took the tiller. Downstream, the Vell widened toward the villages — toward the hundred names in his breast pocket, waiting to be given back.

"Come on, then," he said, and his voice broke only a little. "We've got deliveries to make."
