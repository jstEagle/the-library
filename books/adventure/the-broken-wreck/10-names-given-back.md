## 10. Names Given Back

The Vell gave us back to itself a mile below the narrows, spitting us out into the shallows like a cat depositing something damp and mildly disappointing on a doorstep.

We had, between the five of us: one dory, half a ledger wrapped in oilskin, two oars, Tam's dive knife, Nessa's charts (ruined for navigation, priceless for everything else), Finch, and my hat. The Kindly Hour was behind us, resting on the riverbed across the channel with her boilers split and her name — my father's joke, my father's boat — serving as a headstone for Corvane Hale and his claim papers both.

I did not cry, because captains don't. I did sit down in the shallows and let the river soak me to the waist for a long while, which is different, and no one laughed at me, which is how I knew we were a family now.

"Well," said Widow Ash, wringing out her apron with the brisk violence of a woman who has buried a husband and outlived a war and does not intend to be defeated by a wet hem. "Anyone hungry?"

We were always hungry. She fed us anyway, from a dented pot, on the bank of a river that had taken everything else.

* * *

The first village was Ferrow's Landing, forty miles downstream, and we walked most of it, because the dory was better suited to carrying the ledger than carrying us. Nessa carried it in turns, strapped to her chest like a child, and I don't think any of us ever joked about it, though Finch once tried to feed it a biscuit, which was as close as we came.

Ferrow's Landing was a net-mending town, all salt-bleached wood and suspicious dogs. The name we carried for it was Vessic. *Vessic, ferry rights revoked, holdings seized, year of the Collapse.* Nessa read it aloud in the square because I asked her to — my voice had gone strange in my throat, and hers was a cartographer's voice, made for saying places and things plainly.

An old woman came forward with rope-burned hands and said, "My grandmother was a Vessic. We always thought we'd just been poor."

"You weren't poor," Nessa said gently. "You were robbed. There's a difference, and it has a name, and here it is."

The old woman held the ledger page like it might hatch. Around her, the square had gone very quiet, the particular quiet of a town rewriting forty years of its own story.

They gave us supper, beds, and — this is the part I'll tell my grandchildren, if the river ever lets me have any — they gave us thanks. I had spent so long thinking of the ledger as my burden, my evidence, my pardon waiting to be bought. It had never once occurred to me that it was a gift. I had been carrying a present for forty miles of mud and complaining about the weight.

* * *

We went village to village down the Vell all that autumn. Marlow's Reach. The Two Sisters. A hamlet so small it didn't have a name, just a family named Duvall who turned out to be Duvall *and* Karven, which made the widow of the house weep into her bread dough and then laugh at herself for it.

Word traveled faster than we did, as word does. By the fourth village, people were waiting for us on the docks. By the sixth, there was a boy running ahead of us between towns — a professional, Finch decided, studying him with the flat appraisal of one expert recognizing another — and by the eighth, someone had started calling us the Ledger-Walkers, which Nessa loved and Tam pretended to hate and I privately thought was a bit grand for five damp people and a dory.

Tam, for his part, spent the whole walk doing penance in the only way he knew how: working. He fixed nets, patched roofs, pulled a stuck ferry off a bar with nothing but muscle and swearing. In Marlow's Reach a man spat at his feet when he learned Tam had once dived for Hale's fleet. Tam took it. He stood there and took it, and then he helped the man's sons haul crab pots until dark, and by the end of it the man was drinking with him and pretending he'd never spat at all. That's the Vell for you. It remembers, but it also thaws.

Finch, meanwhile, discovered that mute boys who read water are oracles in river towns. Old pilots came to him with charts and squinted at his fingers moving across them. He'd tap a line, shake his head, tap again. They'd argue, test it, come back sheepish. Nessa started keeping a list of his corrections under the heading *FINCH IS RIGHT, STOP ASKING*, which she eventually had printed, at her own expense, in a small edition that river pilots still carry.

And Widow Ash — well. Widow Ash found that half the villages we passed through had a widow in them, or a widow-adjacent woman, or a lonely old cook with nobody to scold, and by the time we reached the lower Vell she had a network. A sisterhood. A conspiracy of stockpots. If you'd told me a year ago that the most formidable intelligence-gathering operation on the river would be run out of soup kettles, I'd have called you a liar, and I'd have been wrong.

* * *

My father's name was on page eleven.

I'd known it was there since the first night, of course — *Thackeray, pilotage and toll rights, seized, year of the Collapse* — but knowing a thing and doing a thing are different rivers entirely. Every village had its ceremony, its weeping, its bread. I kept finding reasons to postpone my own.

It was Nessa who called it, in the way she called everything, with a pencil behind her ear and no patience for cowards. "Emrys. We're at Sallow Bend. You grew up here. The riverbank is right there. What exactly are you waiting for — a better river?"

So it was a gray morning in late autumn, frost on the reeds, that I walked down to the bank below Sallow Bend with a page of a dead nation's ledger and a trowel Widow Ash had requisitioned from a gardener who hadn't dared refuse her.

My father had piloted boats on this stretch for thirty years. He'd been cheated of his toll rights by a regime that collapsed before he could even hate it properly, and he'd died poor and funny about it, and he'd named a riverboat the *Kindly Hour* because he said the river was kindest at dusk, when it stopped showing off.

I dug a small hole in the bank, above the flood line, where the reeds grow thick. I put the page in. I didn't say anything grand. I said, "They know now, Da. All of them know. It's yours again — or it's the river's, which amounts to the same thing."

Then Finch, who had followed me down without my noticing — the boy moves like water, it's unnerving — crouched beside the little mound and pressed a smooth gray stone into the dirt. A marker. He looked up at me, and his face did the thing it did instead of words, and I understood him perfectly: *There. Now it's a place.*

I put my hand on his head, and we stood there a while, the two of us, orphans of different floods, watching the Vell go by with all its receipts kept and all its debts paid.

* * *

The hull was Tam's find, because of course it was.

We'd come back to Sallow Bend for the winter, having run out of villages and excuses, and there she was, half-buried in the mud below the old shipyard: a Karvenian river skiff, sixty feet if she was an inch, sunk and forgotten since before any of us were born. Karvenian lines — that high stern, those flared bows — a boat from the drowned nation, lying in the mud of the river that drowned it.

"She's sound under the rot," Tam said, up to his thighs in muck, tapping the timbers. "Karvenian oak. They built for glaciers. Somebody sank her on purpose — soft landing, good mud. She's been *waiting*, Emrys."

"Everything on this river's been waiting," I said. "That's rather the theme."

We bought her for almost nothing from a shipyard owner who was delighted to have the hole in his yard filled by idiots. And then — this is the part I love, the part I'd keep even if the ledger burned — we fixed her. All winter. All five of us, and half the town, because Widow Ash's soup network had opinions and strong arms and nothing but time.

Nessa redrew her lines from memory and her grandfather's charts, and when the shipwrights told her she was wrong, she produced documents until they stopped. Tam rebuilt the hull with his own hands and taught Finch to caulk, and Finch turned out to have the steadiest hands on the river, because of course he did. Widow Ash provisioned her, organized her, and installed a galley so efficient it frightened visiting sailors.

And I — I did what my father did. I steered. We took her out on a bright cold morning in early spring, all five of us, no cargo, no ledger, no debts, and ran her down the Vell just to feel her move, and she moved like the river had been holding a shape in the water all these years and we'd finally arrived to fill it.

We needed a name. There was a long debate. Tam wanted something fierce. Nessa wanted something accurate. Finch tapped out a rhythm on the rail that nobody understood for two days until Widow Ash suddenly laughed into her tea and said, "The boy's a poet, and he's right."

We painted it on the stern in Karvenian blue: the *Kindly Hour*. The second one. Because boats aren't wood, my father used to say. Boats are what the wood remembers.

* * *

It was on that first shakedown run, coming back upriver at dusk with the water gone gold and kind, that Finch tugged my sleeve and pointed.

A barge was coming down the Vell — not Hale's; Hale's was rusting on a sandbar behind a wall of new ice, and its director was feeding the fish. This was a survey barge, official-looking, with a government pennant and a man on the foredeck holding a brass instrument and looking at our boat with great interest.

Nessa shaded her eyes. "They're sounding the narrows," she said slowly. "Above the icefall. Emrys — if they're surveying the high route, someone in the capital knows the wreck is reachable now. Someone knows what we did."

The survey man lifted his hand — not a wave. A summons. A question. A beginning.

Widow Ash came up beside me and said, mildly, the way she said everything, "Well, Captain. Shall we go and be famous, or shall we go and be gone?"

I looked at my crew. My family. The castoffs, the ledger-walkers, the people the river had given me instead of a pardon.

I put the helm over.

"Neither," I said. "We're going to go and be *Thackerays*. It's a long story. They'll want soup first."

Finch grinned. The *Kindly Hour* turned upstream, into the gold water, toward whatever the river was keeping for us next.
