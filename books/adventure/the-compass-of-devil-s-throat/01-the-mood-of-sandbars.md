## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river never forgave you for sleeping in.

Astrid Vasquez had learned that lesson the way she'd learned most things — with her hands, in cold water, before dawn. By the time the first gray light bled through the mist over the Kettle River's lower reaches, she was already at the wheel of the *Marrow*, her boots planted on a deck plate worn smooth by eleven years of standing in exactly that spot, one hand on the throttle lever, the other on the brass rail that had gone green with age and salt from her palms.

The sandbars had moved again.

She could feel it before she could see it — a wrongness in the way the current shouldered against the hull, a hesitation in the wake, like a sentence spoken by someone who'd forgotten halfway through what they meant to say. The *Marrow* drew four feet when she was empty, six when she was loaded, and somewhere down there in the tea-colored water was a bar of silt that hadn't existed last Tuesday, waiting with the patience of all geological things to open her belly.

Astrid eased the throttle back and let the boat drift, listening. The river talked. It always talked. A broad, flat hiss where the water ran shallow over clean gravel — safe enough. A low, muscular gurgle off the port bow where the channel bent around the drowned willows — deeper there, but the eddy would grab a slow boat and swing it sideways into the cutbank. And beneath everything, almost too quiet to hear over the diesel's idle, a sound like someone dragging their thumb across wet silk. That was the new bar. That was the river rearranging its furniture in the dark, the way it did every spring when the snowmelt came down hard off the high country and the whole watershed shrugged and started over.

She rang the engine back up and threaded the *Marrow* left, then right, then left again, reading the surface the way other people read letters. A V of ripples meant rock. A long smooth tongue of water sliding between two boils meant depth. A patch of water that looked *tired* — flat, dull, uninterested — meant silt settling out, meant shallow, meant trouble wearing a disguise.

Her father had taught her that. He'd stood her on this same rail when she was seven years old, her bare feet gripping the brass, and told her: *The river is honest, mija. It only lies about where it's been.* She had hated him a little for saying things like that, even then, even before. Now the words lived in her skull like tenants who paid no rent, and she couldn't evict them no matter how many miles of water she put between herself and the man who'd said them.

Eleven years since she'd answered to the name Vasquez. Eleven years since the family's river rights had gone to the Corvane Salvage Syndicate in a courthouse so corrupt the judge kept a syndicate pen in his breast pocket, and the Vasquez clan — boatbuilders, pilots, dredgers, three generations of people who belonged to the water more than to any piece of land — had scattered like seed on wind. Her mother south to the coast towns. Her cousins west. Mateo—

Mateo she didn't think about. Mateo was a door she kept painted shut.

Astrid had taken a new name the way you take a coat off a dead man: quickly, without ceremony, because you need it and he doesn't. *Captain Ishikawa* — no. Just Astrid. Just the woman on the *Marrow*. The boat itself carried a name that wasn't hers either, a name she'd won in a card game from a man who'd cried when he signed it over, and she'd felt bad about that for exactly as long as it took to realize the crying had been theater and the man had been syndicate-adjacent, trying to move a debt-ridden hull onto somebody else's books. Nothing on this river was clean. You just chose which dirt you were willing to stand in.

The rule was simple, and she kept it the way monks keep vows: **never stop moving**. Stillness was where they found you. Debt collectors, syndicate men, the past in all its patient uniforms — they didn't chase, not really. They just waited at the docks, at the landings, at the fuel barges, because they knew that sooner or later everyone stopped. Everyone moored. Everyone slept. Money owed was a hook, and stillness was the hand that set it.

So the *Marrow* moved. Cargo upriver — pump parts, salt pork, roofing tin, whatever wouldn't ask questions. Cargo downriver — furs, pelts, ore concentrates, once an entire disassembled chapel that smelled of incense for a month. Never passengers. Or rather: passengers only under the old rules, posted on a card tacked by the galley door in her own blunt handwriting:

NO QUESTIONS ABOUT THE CAPTAIN.
NO QUESTIONS ABOUT THE ROUTE.
NO WEAPONS UNLESS DECLARED.
PAY IN ADVANCE.
THE RIVER DECIDES THE REST.

People signed the card or they stayed on the dock. In eleven years she'd thrown exactly two passengers off mid-river — both of them into waist-deep water near a landing, which she considered generous — and both times it had been because they'd asked questions. Not dangerous questions. Just *questions*, the ordinary human itch of wanting to know who was steering them, and where they'd come from, and why a woman alone ran a boat this far north of anywhere. Curiosity was fine in its place. Its place was not aboard her vessel.

Because here was the thing Astrid knew and most people didn't: you couldn't outrun your history, but you could make it expensive to follow. Every mile was a toll. Every anonymous cargo run, every night anchored in some unnamed slough with the engine ticking as it cooled, bought another day in which nobody knew where Captain Nobody was. The dread never left — it lived in her chest like a second heart, smaller and colder — but motion starved it. Motion kept it small.

Mostly.

* * *

Kettle Landing materialized out of the mist at half past eight, a straggle of stilt houses and fish racks and one long warehouse whose roof had been patched with so many different colors of tin it looked like a quilt made by a drunk. The smell arrived before the sight did: woodsmoke, fish guts, tar, and under it all the sweet rot of the season's first berries going soft in somebody's neglected crate.

Astrid brought the *Marrow* in against the current, easy, the way she did everything with the boat — no wasted motion, no drama. She'd watched young pilots come in hot, all engine and swagger, and she'd watched the river humble them inside a season. There was no swagger on this water. There was only competence, and its absence, and the absence got people killed.

She tossed the stern line to a kid on the dock — Toma, maybe fourteen, all elbows and ambition, who fancied himself a deckhand and whom Astrid tolerated because he never asked questions, only answers, which was different — and stepped ashore to settle her business before the mist burned off.

Three items of business. Fuel from the barge. Salt pork and lamp oil from the warehouse. And the mail packet, which she always collected personally, because the mail was how the world tried to sneak up on you.

The fuel went fine. The warehouse went fine. The mail was where the day turned.

There was a letter waiting. Not addressed to Astrid — addressed to *the captain of the Marrow*, which was worse, because it meant whoever sent it knew the boat but not the name, or knew the name and was being polite about it, and politeness on this river was almost always a blade wrapped in cloth. The paper was good stock, city stock, and the seal was gray wax pressed with a symbol she recognized the way you recognize a snake's head: a coiled cable around a hook. Corvane Salvage Syndicate.

She stood very still on the dock boards, the letter in her hand, and let the feeling move through her — that cold drop from throat to stomach, the old familiar vertigo of being *found*. Eleven years of motion, and here it was anyway, delivered to a landing she'd chosen precisely because it was nowhere.

Stillness is where they find you. She hadn't even stopped, and they'd found her.

"Captain."

The voice came from behind her, low and unhurried, and Astrid turned with the letter still in her hand because running would have been worse than anything the voice could do.

He was a small man, neat, dressed in a way that pretended not to be expensive — river-gray wool, scuffed boots, a hat that had been good five years ago. His face was forgettable in the professional way of faces designed to be forgotten. He held a leather folio under one arm the way a priest holds scripture.

"You'd be the captain," he said. Not a question. "Name's Ostrer. I represent certain financial interests." He smiled without engaging any part of his face above the mouth. "I'll keep it brief, because I know how you people feel about standing still."

"I don't owe anyone money," Astrid said.

"No." Ostrer opened the folio, unhurried, and consulted a page as though checking the weather. "You don't. That's actually the interesting part. The *Marrow* carries a lien — inherited with the hull, back when you won her in that card game upriver. Old debt, rolled over twice, sold twice. You've been paying it down through the cargo brokers without knowing, I suspect, or maybe knowing and hoping it would die of old age." He tapped the page. "It hasn't died. Debts on this river don't die, Captain. They migrate. And yours has migrated to us."

"How much?"

"More than the boat's worth. Less than you'd imagine." He closed the folio. "Which is, of course, the design. We don't want the boat. Boats are work. What we want is arrangement. Regular runs. Certain cargoes, certain landings, no manifests filed, no questions asked—" he paused, and the smile came back, thinner, "—and I'm told you're already philosophically aligned on that last point."

The dock swayed under Astrid's feet, or seemed to. Around them Kettle Landing went about its morning — Toma coiling line, a woman gutting whitefish two slips down, the mist lifting off the water in ragged sheets — all of it indifferent, all of it continuing, and none of it real to her. There was only the folio and the gray wax and the coiled cable around the hook, and underneath that, eleven years deep, the courthouse and the pen in the judge's pocket and her father's face when the rights were read aloud and the word *Corvane* filled the room like floodwater.

They had taken everything once by paper. Now the paper had drifted downstream and found her again, and it wanted not her boat but her obedience.

"I'll consider it," Astrid said.

"Consider quickly." Ostrer glanced up at the sky, at the heavy, bruised-looking clouds stacking over the western ridgeline, clouds that had no business forming this early in the season. "Monsoon's coming early this year. The brokers say weeks. I say less. Rivers close, Captain. When they close, debts compound. When they reopen—" he settled the folio back under his arm, "—I'll be wherever you're moored. And you will be moored. Everyone is, eventually. That's not a threat. It's just hydrology."

He touched the brim of his hat, a gesture of courtesy so precise it felt rehearsed, and walked away down the dock with the unhurried gait of a man who owned the ground he stood on whether or not anyone had told him so.

Astrid stood a moment longer with the sealed letter in her fist. Then she walked to the end of the dock, and she dropped it into the river, and she watched the gray wax take water and sink, the coiled cable turning once, twice, like something rolling over in its sleep, before the current took it.

It changed nothing. She knew that. Letters had copies; debts had twins; men like Ostrer were hydra-headed. But some things you did because the alternative was standing still, and standing still was the one thing she had sworn off the way other people swear off drink.

* * *

By noon the *Marrow* was loaded and pointed upriver, because upriver was away, and away was the only compass heading Astrid trusted anymore. The clouds in the west had thickened. The barometer in the wheelhouse — an instrument older than she was, its glass face yellowed, its needle prone to sulking — had begun a slow, stubborn slide downward, and every hour it fell another notch Astrid's chest tightened another degree. Early monsoon. Ostrer hadn't been bluffing about that part; the river itself was confirming it, running higher than it should for the season, carrying more silt, moving its bars with a restlessness that felt almost nervous.

Toma waved from the dock until he was a smudge in the haze. Astrid did not wave back. She never did. Sentiment was ballast, and she kept her ballast trimmed.

The first day's run took her through the long meander country below the bluffs, where the river swung in great lazy arcs and the channels changed overnight and the pilot's job was less navigation than negotiation — constant, silent, unwinnable negotiation with a counterparty that never slept and never repeated itself. She ran until the light failed, then tucked the *Marrow* into a slough she'd used before, a narrow drowned forest behind a willow point where the current couldn't reach her and the mosquitoes could, and she sat in the wheelhouse after dark with a cup of coffee gone bitter and cold, watching lightning walk along the horizon like something pacing a fence.

Never stop moving. She'd broken the rule for the night, as she broke it every night, and every night the breaking cost her sleep. She lay in her bunk below and listened to the rain start — fat, scattered drops at first, then a steady whisper on the cabin roof — and she thought about Ostrer's parting words. *Everyone is moored eventually. That's not a threat. It's just hydrology.*

He was wrong, she told herself. People weren't boats. People could keep moving forever if they wanted it badly enough.

But the rain kept falling, and the barometer kept falling, and somewhere upriver — though she couldn't have said how she knew, only that she'd known it since morning, the way animals know weather — something was waiting for her. A bargain, or a body, or a letter that couldn't be sunk. The river was bringing it down to her at walking speed, patient as silt.

In the morning she raised steam and took the *Marrow* back out into the current, and the current leaned against her hull like a hand on her chest, and she pushed through it, because pushing through it was the only prayer she knew.

Two days later, at a fuel stop called Gravel Bar Landing, a stranger was waiting on the dock. An old man, propped against a piling like something the river had deposited, coughing in a wet, tearing way that Astrid heard from fifty yards and understood from a hundred. He watched the *Marrow* come in with eyes that had the fixed, feverish brightness of a man spending his last coin, and when the gangplank went down he straightened himself with visible effort and started toward her, and Astrid felt the cold second heart in her chest turn over and begin, against all her rules and all her sense, to beat faster.

He was carrying a map.
