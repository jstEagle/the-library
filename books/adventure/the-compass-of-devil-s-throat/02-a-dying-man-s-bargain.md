## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

He reached the gangplank before she could decide whether to stop him.

That was the thing about dying men — they moved with a terrible economy, as if they'd already spent everything except the last few steps and meant to spend those well. Elias Thorn came up the plank one hand over the other, the map rolled in his fist like a baton he intended to carry across some finish line only he could see, and when he stepped onto her deck the *Marrow* rocked under his weight and settled, and Astrid thought: even my boat is too polite to tell him no.

"Captain." His voice was gravel dragged through water. "Vasquez."

The name landed on the deck between them like something dropped from a height.

"No Vasquez on this boat," she said. "You've got me confused with somebody worth talking to."

"I don't confuse easily." He coughed then, that wet tearing sound up close, and turned aside with the practiced courtesy of a man who had been doing this for months, angling his shoulders so the blood-flecked spittle went over the rail instead of onto her deck. When he straightened, there was a thread of red at the corner of his mouth and his eyes were clearer than ever, fever-bright, fixed on her face like he was reading a chart of it. "I've been asking after you at every landing from Coppermouth down. They say you run cargo and you don't ask questions and you don't answer them either."

"They say right."

"They say you go upriver." He said it carefully, the way a man sets down something fragile. "All the way up. Even past Kettle Landing. Even into the country where the maps go thin."

Astrid leaned against the rail and let the silence do its work. It was an old trick and it usually worked; people hated a quiet more than they hated a refusal. Behind Thorn, Gravel Bar Landing went about its business — two boys hauling ice blocks in burlap slings, a woman arguing fuel prices with a man who kept shaking his head, the brown river sliding past all of it, indifferent, going where it was going whether anyone aboard agreed or not.

Thorn didn't fill the silence. That was the first thing that made her uneasy. Most men talked to cover their fear. This one stood inside his like it was a room he'd furnished long ago.

"I need passage," he said finally. "Upriver. To the mouth of Devil's Throat."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"There's nothing to hear." She pushed off the rail. "I don't carry passengers. I especially don't carry passengers who want to go to Devil's Throat in the season when the river starts eating boats. You want to die, old man, the landing's got a perfectly good ditch behind the fuel shed. Cheaper."

Something moved through his face — not hurt. Recognition, almost. As if she'd confirmed something he'd already written down about her in some ledger of his own.

"That's twice," he said.

"What?"

"Twice I'll have asked." He held up one finger, gnarled as driftwood. "I'm giving you the courtesy of counting. Most men don't get a count."

He turned and started back down the gangplank, and Astrid discovered that watching him go was harder than it should have been. Not because she wanted what he carried — she didn't know yet what he carried, not truly — but because of the way he walked. Like a man leaving a room he knew he wouldn't be invited back into. Like her father used to walk out of arguments he'd lost, spine straight, dignity intact, carrying the loss home in his hands where nobody could see it.

She looked away. The river helped. The river always helped.

* * *

She gave him an hour.

It wasn't mercy. She told herself it wasn't mercy. It was arithmetic: a man that sick would either die on the dock, which made trouble with the landing's harried magistrate, or he'd find another boat, and any boat fool enough to take him upriver in monsoon season would founder, and foundered boats made the whole river harder for everyone. Her refusal was practically civic duty. She repeated this to herself while she checked the bilge pumps, while she argued with the fuel man down to a fair price, while she ate a bowl of fish stew she barely tasted at the counter of the landing's single cookhouse, where the woman who ran it asked, too casually, if the old prospector had found her yet.

"Found me for what?"

"He wouldn't say. Just sat there all morning watching the channel like he was waiting for a specific boat." The woman wiped the counter in slow circles. "Had a look about him. Like a man who'd made peace with something bad."

"We've all got a look. It's called a face."

But she left the cookhouse faster than she'd meant to, and she took the long way back to the dock, along the shingle where the ice-slings dripped and the meltwater ran silver over stones, and she told herself the detour was for exercise, for the legs, for nothing.

Thorn was still on the dock. He hadn't moved, really — he'd just arranged himself differently against the piling, sitting now, his back to the wood, his hat tipped forward, and for one lurching moment she thought he'd died there in the hour she'd given him, died waiting for a boat that had already said no.

Then he lifted the hat brim with one finger and looked at her, and she saw he wasn't dead, only patient, and somehow that was worse.

"You're still here," she said.

"Where would I go?" He smiled, and it transformed his face briefly into something younger, something that had once charmed women in cookhouses and talked his way past magistrates. "Captain, do you know how much time I have? Neither do I. Could be a month. Could be till Thursday. That's a strange way to live, but it does simplify things. I can't afford to be proud anymore. Pride costs days."

"Flattery costs more."

"That wasn't flattery. That was inventory." He fumbled inside his coat — she watched his hand shake, watched him master it — and brought out two things. The map, oilcloth-wrapped, soft-cornered with handling. And a letter, sealed with plain wax, addressed in a careful hand: *For Sera Thorn, to be delivered by hand only.*

"My daughter," he said. "Sera. She lives up past the Throat, on the east fork. There's a homestead — the maps will show it, or close enough. I'm asking you to carry these to her. That's all. Not me. Me, you can leave on the dock to rot; I've made my arrangements here, such as they are. But the map and the letter go to her before the rains come, or—" He stopped. Coughed, smaller this time. "Or they never arrive, and everything I did was for nothing."

"What's on the map?"

"A route."

"To what?"

"Captain." He closed his eyes as if her suspicion physically tired him. "You're doing the thing where you ask questions. I was told you don't do that."

"Who told you?"

"People at landings. People who clearly admire you very much and fear you slightly." He opened his eyes again. "Here are my terms, since you like terms. Triple fare. Paid half now, half on delivery. And one condition of mine: you don't read the letter, and you don't turn aside. Straight upriver, before the monsoon closes the high water. If the weather turns, you put in wherever you must and wait it out, but you deliver. Sworn on whatever you swear on. I noticed the little saint by your wheel — the chipped one. River Mary, my mother called her. Swear on her, if she's listening. Someone ought to."

Astrid looked at the chipped saint in her memory — glued back together twice, eyes worn smooth by her own thumb on bad nights — and felt anger rise in her throat like heat lightning. How dare a dying stranger know her talismans. How dare he stand on her dock and speak of mothers.

"No," she said. Second refusal. She counted it herself, silently, and wondered why.

* * *

He didn't argue. That was the worst part.

He simply wrapped the map back in its oilcloth with fingers that trembled and didn't quite obey him, tucked both items away, and sat back against the piling as though settling in for a long wait he fully expected to lose. Astrid walked away. She got as far as the *Marrow*'s gangplank before her feet stopped, without consulting her, and she stood there with one hand on the rope rail looking at the brown water sliding past the pilings, and the river looked back at her the way it always did — patient, complicit, keeping everyone's secrets including hers.

Her father had been a river man. That was the shape of the whole wound, when you boiled it down. Tomás Vasquez had loved the water the way saints love God, absolutely and ruinously, and the river had taken his boats, his rights, his name off every ledger he'd ever signed, and finally — in the indirect, deniable way rivers do such things — taken him. Astrid had grown up on a deck like this one, learning channels from a man whose handwriting she still knew better than her own. The looping V's. The way he crossed his sevens. The little hook he put on the ends of his numerals, like each number was trying to swim away.

She hadn't seen that handwriting in eleven years. She'd made sure of it. Burned what she couldn't leave, left what she couldn't burn.

"Captain." Thorn's voice, closer than she'd expected. He'd come down the dock after her, slow, one hand on the rail. "One more thing, and then I'll let you go. I promise it's the last coin I'll spend."

"You've got no coins left. You said so."

"I've got one." He drew out the map again, unwrapped it right there on the open dock, in the wind, in front of the ice boys and the fuel man and God and the brown indifferent river, and held it out flat between them.

And Astrid looked down, because curiosity is a current and she had spent eleven years swimming against it and her arms were tired, and she saw the survey lines, the soundings, the careful marginal notes in faded iron-gall ink — and she saw the handwriting.

The looping V's. The crossed sevens. The little hooks on the numbers, swimming.

The dock seemed to tilt. Or she did. The river noise fell away the way it does sometimes, all that brown murmur compressing into a single thin tone in her ears, and she heard her own breath going in and out, and somewhere far off the ice boys were shouting and none of it mattered because her father's hand was lying on a stranger's map, drawing channels for a stranger's purpose, in a place her father had sworn — had *sworn*, drunk, weeping, the last night she ever saw him — never to go again.

"Where," she said, and her voice came out sanded down to the grain, "did you get this."

Thorn watched her with that terrible clarity. "Now that," he said gently, "is a question I'll answer aboard. If we're sailing."

"This is my father's writing."

"I know whose writing it is, Captain." He began, slowly, wincingly, to roll the map closed, keeping it just out of her reach with the instinct of a man who had bargained his whole life and knew exactly when the other party's eyes changed. "I've known for thirty years. Longer than you've known it, maybe. It's why I waited at this landing instead of any other. It's why I asked for you by name — your real name, the one you pretend isn't yours." He tucked the map inside his coat, over his heart, absurdly, formally, like a man arming himself. "Triple fare. Half now. Deliver the letter to my daughter before the rains. And in exchange, I'll tell you what Tomás Vasquez was doing in Devil's Throat the year you were born — and what he buried there."

The wind came off the water and pushed her hair across her face and she didn't move it. Downriver, dark clouds were stacking themselves against the mountains with the unhurried confidence of something that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more, and the first smell of rain — cold stone, green rot, iron — was already threading through the river air, weeks early, the season closing around them like a hand.

Astrid looked at the dying man. She looked at the river. She put her thumb, without deciding to, against the smooth-worn spot on her belt where the chipped saint hung beneath her shirt, and felt the second heart in her chest beating fast and traitorous and alive.

"Half now," she said. "And you sleep in the hold, and if you bleed on my deck you scrub it yourself."

Thorn smiled his young man's smile, and paid her.
