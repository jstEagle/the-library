## 3. What the River Owes

The lamp on Thorn's chest rose and fell with his breathing, and Astrid counted the rise the way she counted anything she didn't trust — twice, then again.

He had not moved from the deck chair in an hour. The blood he'd coughed into his handkerchief had gone the color of rusted chain, folded small and hidden in his fist like a card he was ashamed to play. Outside, the river muttered against the hull, that low conversation water makes with wood when it has all night and no hurry. *Kestrel* rocked at her moorings with the patience of an old dog waiting to be told to run.

Astrid stood at the wheel even though they weren't going anywhere. It was a habit from her father, who used to say a captain should hold the wheel at least once every watch, even tied up, so the boat would know whose hands it belonged to. She'd kept the habit after everything else about him had been taken or sold or scattered. Her palms knew the spokes better than they knew their own lines.

"You're still deciding," Thorn said. His voice came out sanded down, half of it already spent.

"I'm not deciding anything." She adjusted a spoke she didn't need to adjust. "I told you no."

"Twice."

"Then you've been counting too."

He smiled, and the smile cost him something; she watched him pay for it in a slow tightening around the eyes. "I'm a prospector, Captain. Counting's the whole trade. You count color in the pan, count the seasons, count what's left." He lifted the map tube slightly, an offering, an accusation. "I've got maybe one season left. Less."

"Then spend it somewhere else."

"Where?" He said it without self-pity, which was worse. "I've been spending my whole life getting here. To this dock. This boat. Your name on the stern."

Her name wasn't on the stern. That was the point of the faded nameplate, the letters worn to ghosts by sun and sandblast, repainted once a decade ago with nothing but weatherproof gray. A boat with no name drew fewer questions. A boat with no name could pass under the syndicate's black hulls like a fish under ice.

"Vasquez," Thorn said, because he was dying and therefore entitled to cruelty. "You're a Vasquez."

The word went through her like cold water through a boot. Not pain exactly. Recognition. The river had been saying it to her all night in its muttering way, and now a stranger had said it aloud, and there was no pretending anymore that the sound meant nothing.

"Wrong family," she said. "Common name around here."

"Is it." He coughed, turned his head, did it properly into the handkerchief this time, and when he brought it away she made herself look, because a captain looks at things. Bright red now, not rust. The season was shortening.

* * *

Her father had owned eleven miles of the Verraza before the syndicate came, and Astrid had been nine years old when she learned what a piece of paper could do to a family.

She remembered it as weather. That was how memory stored it — not as men in gray suits with documents, but as a change in the air. Her father had come back from the land office in Corrientes with his hat in his hands instead of on his head, and her mother had looked at the paper he carried and then out the window at the river, and neither of them had spoken, and the silence had lasted through supper and into the next week and never entirely ended.

Eleven miles of river. The rights had been in the family since before the maps were good — her great-grandmother had held the first claim, a woman who'd run cargo through Devil's Throat when everyone else called it a graveyard, and the paper proving it was older than the province itself, written in ink that had gone brown as old blood. Her father used to take it out once a year on the anniversary of the first crossing and read it aloud at the table like scripture, and the children would groan, and he would say, *You laugh, but paper is the only ghost that can hurt you.*

The syndicate's paper was newer. Newer and thicker and stamped with seals, and it said, in language designed to be unreadable, that the Vasquez rights had been collateralized, transferred, consolidated — words that sounded like machinery, and worked like machinery, and ground eleven miles of river out of one family's hands and into a ledger in a vault nobody could find.

Her father fought it for three years. Astrid remembered the fight as a smell: ink and lamp oil and the inside of lawyers' offices, a smell of dust and desperation. He sold the second boat first. Then the warehouse. Then, in the last spring, quietly, without telling anyone until it was done, the eleven miles themselves — sold cheap, sold broken, sold to a buyer whose name on the deed was a company and whose company was a shadow — because the alternative was watching the syndicate take them for nothing, and a man will sell his own hands before he lets someone else amputate them.

After that the family came apart the way rivers come apart in flood season — not all at once but channel by channel. An uncle went north to work the logging runs. Two cousins took berths on coastal freighters and were heard from twice a year, then once, then not. Her mother went inland to her sister's and took the youngest ones, and the house by the river was sold to a man who tore out the old pilings and put up a fence, as if a fence could hold a river.

And Mateo — Mateo had stayed. Mateo was eighteen and furious and said the word *syndicate* like a man spitting teeth, and then one day he wasn't saying it anymore, and one day after that he was wearing a gray coat with the syndicate's mark on the collar, and her father had looked at him across the table the way you look at a channel that has shifted overnight, where the water you trusted yesterday will run you aground today.

Astrid had left the same month. Seventeen years old, a deckhand's pay, and one rule installed in herself like a rivet: *never stop moving.* Stillness was where the paper found you. Stillness was where the debt collectors sat, patient as herons, outside the places you slept. If you kept moving — if the channels changed under you faster than the ledgers could follow — then no seal, no stamp, no man in a gray coat could pin you to a place long enough to take it.

It wasn't freedom. She'd stopped calling it freedom years ago. It was motion, and motion was cheaper than grief, and grief was the one cargo she refused to carry because it didn't fit in any hold and it sank boats.

* * *

"Captain." Thorn's voice pulled her back to the deck, to the lamp, to the rust-colored handkerchief. "You've gone somewhere. I can always tell. Pilots get a look like they're reading water that isn't there."

"I'm reading the water that is there," she said. "It says go south, resupply, and forget I ever saw you."

"It also says," Thorn said, "that you haven't asked me what's on the map."

Because asking was the hook. She knew the shape of the trick — she'd grown up in a family of bargainers, people who could smell a negotiation forming like weather. He wanted her curious. Curiosity was the toll gate; once you paid it, you paid everything after.

So she didn't ask. She checked the bilge line instead, though she'd checked it an hour ago, and coiled a rope that was already coiled, and let the silence do its work. The river muttered. Somewhere downstream a night bird made its one note and quit.

Thorn waited her out. Dying men are patient; they've settled all their other accounts.

Finally she said, "Why my father's handwriting?"

There. Paid. She felt the toll click behind her ribs.

Thorn closed his eyes briefly, as if the question had cost him more than the coughing. When he opened them, they were wet, whether from illness or something else she couldn't tell and didn't want to.

"Because your father gave me the map," he said. "Twenty-two years ago. Before the syndicate finished with your family. Before the paper came. He was trying to document the old routes — the true channels through Devil's Throat, the ones from before the surveys. He thought if the routes were mapped, the rights couldn't be forged. Proof on paper. Paper against paper." A breath that whistled faintly. "He hired me to survey. I was young. I had instruments and no sense. And I took his money, and I mapped his channels, and then—" The handkerchief moved toward his mouth and stopped halfway, discipline winning. "—and then I sold the survey. Both copies. Sold his routes to the very men who were building the case to strip him. They needed to know the river to value it, Captain. I taught them what your family was worth. Then I left the country for twenty years and told myself distance was the same thing as innocence."

The lamp hissed. Astrid discovered she was gripping the wheel with both hands, hard enough that the wood had printed its pattern into her palms, and she made herself let go one finger at a time.

"My father," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her, "died believing the river itself had betrayed him. That the channels shifted. That's what he told my mother. That the Throat ate his proof."

"The Throat didn't eat it." Thorn pressed the map tube flat against his chest. "I did. And the original — the fair copy, in his hand, with his annotations — I kept. Twenty-two years I kept it, because selling it twice seemed like the one sin even I couldn't commit. It's the only honest paper left from your family's claim. Maybe the only honest paper in the whole valley."

"And the letter?"

"For my daughter. Different envelope. Different errand." His eyes held hers, and in the lamplight they were the color of shallow water over gravel — clear and bottomless at once. "Two deliveries, Captain. One to a grave I owe, one to a girl I owe. Take me upriver before monsoon and I'll see both delivered myself, God willing. If God declines—" the handkerchief again, the rust and the red, "—then it's just you and the paper. Paper against paper. Your father's words, more or less."

Astrid looked at the river. It ran dark and indifferent past the pilings, carrying its silt and its secrets south, owing nothing to anyone, which was the only honest creature on the water.

She thought: *this is a delivery job.* Cargo is cargo. A sealed tube and a sealed letter, freight rates doubled for hazardous sentiment, nothing aboard but paper, and paper can't sink a boat unless you let it below decks. She thought: *you're doing it for the fare,* because the fare was real and the engine needed rings and the hull would want caulking before monsoon, and a woman has to eat. She thought: *you'll drop the tube at whatever dock the daughter lives near and never open it and never look back,* because looking back is how rivers catch you.

She thought all of this in the space of one breath, the way she'd been thinking it for ten years, the tidy arithmetic of a woman who had converted every grief she owned into ballast and ballast into speed.

Then she heard herself say: "Upriver takes six days if the channels hold. Seven if they don't. Monsoon's early this year — the barometer's been falling since Kettle Landing like it owes someone money."

"That sounds like a yes," Thorn said carefully.

"It sounds like freight terms." She crossed the deck and held out her hand, and he took it, and his grip was lighter than a bird's, all the weight of him already spent. "One condition. You don't say my surname again on my boat. Not that one. Not any version of it. As far as this vessel is concerned, I'm Captain Ishikawa's problem now, and Ishikawa keeps her own books."

Something moved across Thorn's face — regret, or relief, or the particular sorrow of a man watching someone build the same wall he'd built. "Whatever you say, Captain."

"And the map stays sealed. You don't show me what's on it, I don't ask. It's cargo."

"It's cargo," he agreed, and they both knew it was a lie, and they both let it stand, because some lies are load-bearing.

* * *

She cast off before dawn, because staying tied up past sunrise felt like standing still, and standing still was the one thing the rule forbade.

The river took *Kestrel* the way it always did, gently at first, then with intent. Astrid stood at the wheel with the mist coming off the water in ropes and the engine knocking its familiar complaint two octaves below her thoughts, and she told herself the story she'd prepared, the delivery-job story, the freight-and-fare story, and it almost held. It held the way a patched hull holds — fine in calm water, untested in the Throat.

Behind her, in the deck cabin, Thorn coughed, and slept, and coughed. In the chart drawer beneath her elbow lay a sealed tube bearing her father's handwriting, and she had not opened it, and she would not open it, and she caught herself, four times between the first bend and the second, calculating exactly how much force the seal would take.

Paper against paper, her father used to say. But he'd never said what happens when the paper wins.

By midmorning the mist burned off and she could see far upriver, to where the valley narrowed and the first gray shoulders of the high country stood against the sky, and beyond them, invisible from here but present anyway, the way a bruise is present, the gorge they called Devil's Throat.

At the same hour, thirty miles downstream, a black-hulled patrol boat with the syndicate's mark on its stack was reading a telegram, and the telegram contained a name, and the name was Vasquez.

But Astrid didn't know that yet. She only knew the river was running higher than it should for the season, and the barometer was still falling, and somewhere ahead of her a daughter waited for a letter, and somewhere behind her a debt collector was counting days.

She opened the throttle. The rule held: never stop moving.

For now, moving was enough.
