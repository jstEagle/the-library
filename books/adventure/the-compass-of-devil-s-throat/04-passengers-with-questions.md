## 4. Passengers with Questions

Wren had learned to read a boat the way other people read faces, and the *Marrow* had a face like a held breath.

She sat low in the water at Kettle Landing, her hull streaked with old rust weeping down from the rivets like dried tears, her wheelhouse glass fogged and salt-hazed. Everything about her said *don't look too long*. Wren appreciated that in a vessel. It was how they preferred to be regarded themselves: briefly, incuriously, forgotten by the time the rope was cast off.

The landing itself was hardly worth the name — three pilings gone soft as bread, a plank walkway that flexed underfoot like something alive, a fuel drum converted to a rain barrel catching the first spit of the season. Kettle Landing existed because the river bent there, slow and deep, and because the men who ran cargo up to the high camps needed somewhere to lie about their tonnage. The air smelled of creosote and wet rope and, underneath it all, the green iron stink of water that had come a long way down from the ice.

Wren paid for passage the way you pay for anything worth having: in cash, counted twice, and without meeting anyone's eyes longer than necessary.

"Triple fare," they said, laying the notes on the barrel head. "And I don't eat much."

The woman behind the ledger didn't look up right away. She had a pen moving across the page in small, economical strokes, and she finished her line before she gave Wren the courtesy of her attention. When she did look up, Wren got the full force of it — dark eyes set deep under a sun-browned brow, hair cut short and practical, a jaw that looked like it had been drawn with a straightedge. Captain Astrid Vasquez, though the ledger said nothing of the kind. The ledger said *A. Marlowe*, which was a lie so thin Wren could see through it like oilcloth.

"Triple fare doesn't buy questions," the captain said.

"I haven't asked any."

"You will." She took the money, folded it once, and slid it into her breast pocket without counting it. That told Wren something. A captain who counted was careful; a captain who didn't count was either rich or desperate, and no one running a boat this tired was rich. "Deckhand's berth, aft. You'll work if I need hands. You'll stay out of the wheelhouse. And when we make stops, you're cargo until I say otherwise."

"Fair terms."

"They're not terms. They're conditions." She closed the ledger. "Boarding's now. We leave on the hour whether your kit is aboard or swimming."

* * *

The *Marrow* carried four souls besides Wren: the captain; a cook-engineer named Doss, built like a stove and about as talkative; a young deckhand called Pell who chewed tobacco and lost arguments with knots; and, shut up in the forward cabin like contraband, a dying man whose coughing carried through the bulkheads at night like someone chopping ice.

Wren heard him before they saw him. On the second morning out of Kettle Landing, wedged between the engine's rhythm and the slap of water against the hull, came a sound Wren knew from salvage camps and fever tents — the wet, rattling effort of lungs filling with something that wasn't air. Then a voice, cracked and far away, saying words Wren couldn't catch, over and over, like a prayer worn smooth from handling.

They found the source that afternoon. The dying man had dragged himself onto the aft deck, wrapped in two blankets despite the heat, and sat propped against the rail watching the banks slide past. Up close he was mostly architecture — cheekbones and knuckles and a beard gone to wire — with eyes still burning bright in the wreckage of his face. He looked at Wren the way an old prospector looks at new ground: assessing, weighing what might be under the surface.

"You're the one who paid triple," he rasped.

"And you're the one who coughs like a landslide."

That earned a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't rusted out of him years ago. "Elias Thorn." He didn't offer a hand. His hands were busy holding himself together. "You going up to the Throat?"

The question landed exactly where Wren didn't want it to land. They kept their face still — a skill bought dear, in rooms where showing interest cost fingers — and shrugged. "Going where the boat goes."

"Boats go where captains take them." Thorn's eyes glittered. "And captains go where they're paid, or where they're haunted. Usually both." He pulled a blanket tighter. "She won't tell you where we're bound. Won't tell me why she agreed to carry me, neither, though I suspect she asks herself that every night. You want to know a person, watch what they do with a secret that isn't theirs."

Wren crouched down to his level, forearms on knees, close enough to smell the sickness on him — sweetish, wrong, like fruit rotting in a locked room. "What makes you think I want to know anyone?"

"Because you boarded a stranger's boat paying triple fare and asked nothing, and nobody does that who isn't full to the brim with questions they can't afford to ask out loud." He smiled with too many teeth. "I was full of those once. Now I'm just full of river water and regret. Cheaper, in the end."

He turned back to the banks then, conversation over, and Wren walked away with the distinct and unwelcome feeling of having been read like a chart.

* * *

The trouble with watching someone is that eventually they notice.

Wren made a study of the captain anyway. It was habit, and habit kept people alive. She rose before dawn and stood at the wheel reading the water the way priests read scripture — lips moving slightly, eyes tracking seams and boils and the pale ribbons where current fought current. She knew every sandbar on this stretch by its temperament, called them under her breath like old enemies' names: *the Widow, the Split Tooth, Old Grudge*. She ate standing up. She slept, when she slept, in the wheelhouse chair with her boots on. And twice — only twice, but twice was enough — Wren caught her looking at them with an expression that wasn't suspicion exactly. Suspicion would have been simpler. It was closer to recognition, the look you give a face you half-remember from a bad dream.

On the fourth day, the river narrowed and the banks grew teeth. The channel threaded between drowned timber and rock shelves, and the captain worked the wheel without pause while Doss called depths from the bow in a flat chant. Wren stood at the rail, ostensibly idle, actually memorizing.

"Devil's Throat starts forty miles up," the captain said, without turning her head. Her voice was conversational in the way a knife is conversational. "Since you were wondering."

"I wasn't."

"Everyone wonders. It's the one thing on this river everyone wonders about, and the one thing nobody who's been through will describe. So they wonder harder." She eased the wheel a spoke left, and the *Marrow* leaned obediently around a snag. "You paid triple fare. Triple buys a destination. So here's mine: I'll carry you as far as the mouth of the Throat and not one fathom further. If you want inside, you hire a different boat. There aren't any. But you're welcome to keep asking."

"I told you—"

"You told me nothing. That's what I'm holding you to." Now she glanced at them, one dark eye sliding sideways, and there it was again — that flicker of recognition, or maybe just curiosity wearing recognition's coat. "I've hauled smugglers, surveyors, a tax collector once, and a woman who turned out to be somebody's wife running from somebody's gun. Every one of them asked about the Throat inside a day. You lasted four days. Either you're patient or you already know what's in there, and I haven't decided which worries me more."

Wren held her gaze. It would have been easy to give her something — a cover story, a dead father upriver, a mining claim, any of the dozen lies they kept oiled and ready. Instead they heard themselves say, "Which answer gets me further upriver?"

Something moved at the corner of the captain's mouth. Not quite a smile. The place where a smile might have been installed and later removed. "Honesty," she said. "But I'd have to verify it, and verification takes time, and time is the one cargo I don't carry."

* * *

That night the rain began — not monsoon yet, not the real thing, but a preliminary drumming on the wheelhouse roof that sent Pell scrambling to batten the hatches and made the whole boat feel smaller, a walnut shell on a rising sea. Wren sat under the aft awning with their back against the rail, listening to the water and doing what they did instead of sleeping: inventorying exits.

The dying man joined them. Of course he did. Thorn moved like a man who had decided sleep was a luxury for the living, and lowered himself onto the bench with a grunt, blankets and all, close enough that the sweetness of his breath reached Wren through the rain-smell.

"She lied to you today," he said.

"She lied to everyone today. It seemed democratic."

"Mm." He watched the rain stitch silver threads across the black water. "You know what I admire about a good liar? Economy. No wasted motion. Ask her about the Throat, she gives you a rule. Ask her about herself, she gives you a rate card. Ask her where she's from—" he tapped his temple, "—and she gives you weather. Beautiful. Twenty years I watched men lie for a living, and none of them were half so tidy." He paused. "Course, the best liars are lying about the biggest things. Tidy lies are load-bearing. Pull one and the whole house comes down."

"Is that a warning or a wager?"

"It's an observation from a man with nothing left to lose but his observations." Thorn shifted, and the cough took him then, deep and terrible, folding him nearly double until he spat something dark into the scuppers and wiped his mouth with the back of a hand that shook like a compass needle near iron. When he could speak again, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "There's a letter in my kit. Sealed. When I'm done, it goes where the map goes, and nowhere else. You understand? Whatever you're carrying, whatever you're after — that letter matters more than everything else on this boat combined. More than the boat."

Wren went very still. Rain ticked on the canvas overhead. Somewhere below, Doss's engine turned over in its sleep.

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you watch her the way I used to watch ledgers." His eyes found Wren's in the dark, bright and awful. "Looking for the entry that doesn't balance. Well. Nobody balances. Not her, not you, not me least of all. The question is what you do when you find the line item with your own name on it." He pushed himself up, swaying with the boat's motion, and gathered his blankets. "Sleep if you can. The river gets honest after midnight. That's when it tells you what it's taken."

He shuffled toward the forward cabin, and Wren sat alone under the awning a long while, watching the rain write and rewrite its endless gray ledger on the water.

* * *

It was near midnight when Wren gave up on rest and walked the length of the deck, boots soft on wet planking, and found the wheelhouse lit.

The captain stood at the wheel though the river ran wide and easy here, straight as a canal, needing nothing from her. She was looking at something in her hands — a fold of paper, old and soft-edged, opened just enough to show a corner of inked lines. A map. Even from the doorway Wren could see the way she held it: not like cargo, not like a tool. Like a letter from someone she hated.

She felt Wren's presence the way cats feel weather. She didn't turn, but her shoulders changed, weight shifting to the balls of her feet.

"Wheelhouse is off limits."

"I know. I knocked with my boots."

That got a breath of almost-laughter from her. She folded the paper away into her coat, quick and practiced, and finally turned. In the lamplight her face was stripped of its dockside armor, and what was underneath was younger and more exhausted than Wren had budgeted for.

"Can't sleep either?" she said.

"I sleep fine. I just don't do it much."

"Hm." She looked past them, out the fogged glass at the rain. "Thorn talked to you."

"He talks to everyone. Talking's the last thing he owns."

"What did he say?" And there it was — the crack in the tidy masonry, the question asked too fast, wanting the answer too much. She heard it herself and closed her jaw, but the damage was done. Wren had spent years learning to find those cracks, prying them gently, seeing what the pressure inside was made of.

"He said the best liars are lying about the biggest things."

The captain's eyes came back to Wren's, and for a moment neither of them blinked, and the rain filled the silence like water filling a hold.

"Your turn," she said quietly. "One truth, since we're trading. Something verifiable. Because I've been watching you too, and I'll tell you what I see: someone who moves like salvage crew but boards like a passenger. Someone who knows boats but pretends not to. Someone who paid triple fare to go nowhere in particular, which means wherever you're going, it isn't a place." She stepped closer, and the lamplight carved her shadow long across the deck plates. "So. One truth. Make it count."

Wren weighed it. Every instinct said deflect, charm, retreat. But there was something in the wheelhouse air — the dying man's letter, the map held like a wound, the rain coming down like a countdown — that made the oiled lies feel suddenly heavy, like diving gear in deep water.

"My family had a name on a document once," Wren said. "River rights. Bottomland, a millrace, the whole inheritance. Gone before I could walk. Sold, signed away, or stolen — depends which relative tells it." They watched her face as they said it, and saw the blood leave her cheeks by degrees, saw her hand drift toward her coat pocket where the map lived. "I'm not going anywhere in particular, Captain. I'm going toward paperwork."

For a long moment the only sound was rain and engine and the river muttering to itself in the dark.

"A. Marlowe," Wren added softly. "That's not your name either, is it?"

The captain looked at them the way you look at a sandbar that has shifted overnight — recalculating everything, the whole channel redrawn. When she answered, her voice was steady, but Wren had learned her steadiness by now, and knew it for what it was: a dam holding.

"We make the mouth of the Throat in two days," she said. "After that, we'll see who's telling the truth about what."

She turned back to the wheel. Wren took the dismissal and walked out into the rain, heart hammering with something that wasn't fear, or wasn't only fear — and behind them, through the fogged glass, they saw the captain draw the map out again and hold it to the lamp, staring at the handwriting on it like a man staring at a grave.

Neither of them slept. Neither of them mentioned it. And forty miles upstream, where the gorge waited with its throat of white water, something in the dark was already closing around them both.
