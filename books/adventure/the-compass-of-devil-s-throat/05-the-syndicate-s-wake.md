## 5. The Syndicate's Wake

The black hull appeared at the bend below Mile Twenty-Six, and Astrid knew it before she knew it — the way you know a storm by the taste of copper on your tongue, the way you know a debt collector's knock by the particular quality of silence that precedes it.

She had been watching the water all morning, not for snags but for wakes. A river teaches you to read its surface like a ledger: every ripple a transaction, every eddy an entry in some account older than any of the people floating on it. And this wake was wrong. Too straight. Too steady. The pleasure boats out of Kettle Landing wandered like drunks; loggers' scows wallowed; even the mail steamer cut a lazy, self-satisfied line. But this vessel came upriver with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry because it had never once needed to be.

Black hull. Twin smokestacks venting thin gray ribbons. No name painted on the bow, which on these waters meant one thing only: a name so well known it didn't need painting. The Meridian Salvage & Navigation Company didn't put its name on its patrol boats for the same reason a knife doesn't label itself.

Astrid's hand found the wheel spoke and stayed there.

"Captain?" Ruiz called from the engine housing, where he'd been coaxing another half-knot out of machinery that had been dying since before she bought her. He'd seen her face change. He always saw.

"Company boat," she said. "Coming up fast."

Behind her, under the tarp amidships, she heard the wet rattle of Elias Thorn's breathing pause — then resume, quicker, like a man dreaming of running.

* * *

They had made forty miles since dawn, threading the braided channels above Kettle Landing where the river split and rejoined like argumentative siblings. It was good water for losing things — cargo manifests, debts, histories. Astrid had spent ten years learning every shifting throat of it, every sandbar that migrated with the seasons, every channel that existed in spring and lied about itself by August. Her rule had kept her alive this long: never stop moving, never take passengers who ask questions. Thorn had broken the second rule by dying too slowly, and now his map rode against her hip inside her coat, folded into a square no bigger than a hymnal, warm from her body heat, heavy as a sin.

She had read it again last night by lamplight while Thorn slept off his laudanum. Not the route itself — she'd memorized that in the first hour — but the margins. Her father's handwriting. She knew it the way you know a scar: the cramped leftward slant of the numerals, the way he crossed his sevens with two strokes instead of one, as if one stroke weren't enough to hold them down. *Bear north of the split willow. Soundings in fathoms, my hand.* Twenty years dead, and Tomás Vasquez was still giving orders from the grave, still drawing lines on paper, still — apparently — keeping company with prospectors who coughed blood onto strangers' decks.

She hadn't decided yet what that meant. She only knew that when the black hull rounded the bend, her first thought was not *I'm carrying contraband* but *they can't have what he wrote.* The distinction frightened her more than the patrol boat did.

"Ruiz," she said. "Get the bilge hatch open. Quietly."

Ruiz's eyebrows climbed toward his sweatband. "We got oil back there, spare line, my second-best boots—"

"And in twenty seconds it's going to have a map in it. Go."

He went. Astrid brought the *Marta Cielo* around a sandbar's shoulder with a touch of reverse current off the point, buying herself ninety seconds, maybe two minutes — enough time to do what needed doing without witnesses. She knelt at the open hatch. The bilge breathed up at her, cold and mineral, smelling of lake-bottom and old iron and the particular sourness of water that has been somewhere. She wrapped the map in oilcloth, weighted it with a shackle pin, and lowered it into the dark between the stringers, wedging it beneath the limber boards where the pump wouldn't find it and no inspector would want to kneel.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere beneath the steadiness, fear was doing its work — not the hot panic of the young but the slow crystallization she'd learned on the night the syndicate's men came to the Vasquez landing with papers and pistols and her father's signature already forged on the transfer documents. Fear, she had discovered, was like river ice in late winter: given time and pressure, it stopped being soft and became something you could walk on. Something that could hold your weight if you moved carefully and never, ever stood still.

She closed the hatch. Rose. Wiped her palms on her trousers.

"Marta Cielo," she said to the wheelhouse glass, to the reflection of her own face ghosted over the water. "You're hauling timber scrap and a sick old man. That's all you've ever been."

The lie settled around her shoulders like a coat.

* * *

The patrol boat came alongside with a courtesy that was worse than aggression. No horn blast, no shouted demands — just a slow matching of speed, the black hull sliding parallel thirty feet off her starboard side, close enough that Astrid could see the rivet lines, the mounted searchlight with its canvas cover, the man at the rail holding a megaphone he hadn't bothered to raise.

Then the loudspeaker crackled anyway, from somewhere above the wheelhouse, a voice flattened by electronics into pure bureaucracy:

"*Motor vessel Marta Cielo. This is Meridian patrol vessel Four-Seven. Reduce speed and prepare for routine inspection. Do not alter course.*"

"Routine," Ruiz muttered, appearing at her elbow with a rag he was using to wipe hands that were already clean. "Since when does Meridian do routine anything above Mile Twenty?"

"Since they started looking for something." Or someone. Or some piece of paper. Astrid eased the throttle back and felt the old boat sag obediently into the patrol's shadow. Up close, the black hull drank the daylight. Even the water along its sides seemed darker, as though the vessel were drinking that too.

She counted six crew visible. Two at the rail with carbines slung casually, muzzle-down, which was its own kind of message. A wiry man working a deck winch that didn't need working. And at the forward rail, alone, a figure in a gray officer's coat with brass at the collar and a face like a closed ledger: middle-aged, sun-cured, eyes the color of nothing in particular.

The gangway came across before Astrid finished tying off. Of course it did. Meridian didn't wait for invitations; invitations were for people who mattered.

"Captain." The officer stepped aboard without asking her name, which told her he already had it. His boots were dry. His voice was mild. Everything about him was mild, the way a trap is mild until it isn't. "Lieutenant Ansel Roque, Meridian Salvage & Navigation, Upper Basin Division. We'll be quick. Papers, manifest, and a look below decks. Standard for this stretch — there's been unlicensed salvage activity reported."

"There's been unlicensed everything reported on this stretch," Astrid said. "Half the river's been salvaged twice and burned once." She handed over her papers — registration, cargo declaration, her pilot's license issued under the name A. Mercer, ten years clean, ten years boring. She watched him read them. She watched him not react, which was its own performance, and she understood suddenly that whatever he knew, he'd known before he stepped aboard.

"Mercer," Roque said, tasting it.

"Family name."

"Mm." He handed the papers back with two fingers, the way you return something you don't quite trust yourself to hold. "And your passengers?"

"One. Old prospector, sick, going up to his people at Silver Bend. My deckhand Ruiz. That's the whole census."

"I'll want to see them both."

"The sick man's sleeping."

"He can be seen sleeping."

There it was. Astrid held his gaze and let three full seconds pass — long enough to be insolent, short enough to be deniable — then shrugged and led him aft. Behind her she heard the carbine men drop onto her deck, heard their boots take inventory of her planking, and made herself think about the bilge, about the oilcloth bundle sleeping under the limber boards in eight inches of cold black water, and about how a man like Roque would rather burn a boat than get his coat wet.

Thorn lay in the sheltered corner behind the wheelhouse where they'd rigged the tarp awning. He looked, Astrid thought with a grim flicker of gratitude, exactly like what he was: a dying man. Gray as ash, lips cracked, the blanket rising and falling in that shallow, snagging rhythm. His eyes opened when their shadows fell across him — fever-bright, unfocused, then focusing with terrible suddenness on the brass at Roque's collar.

"No," Thorn said. It came out as a whisper, then again, louder, cracking: "*No.*" His hand scrabbled at the blanket. "Not the company. Captain — captain, don't let them—"

"Easy." Astrid crouched, took his wrist, felt the pulse fluttering like a bird in a sack. "It's an inspection, old man. Routine. Nobody's taking anything." She said it loudly enough for Roque to hear, and watched the word *routine* land on him like a small, precise insult.

Roque studied Thorn with the unhurried attention of a man reading a document. "Name?"

"Elias Thorn," Astrid answered for him. "Silver Bend district. You can check the settlement rolls."

"Oh, we know the name, Captain Mercer." Something moved behind Roque's flat eyes — not recognition exactly. Appetite. "Mr. Thorn has been of... intermittent interest to the company. Claims staked in the upper basin. Some correspondence. Nothing recent." He glanced at Astrid, and the mildness in his face thinned like ice over moving water. "Tell me, how does a timber-scrap hauler out of Kettle Landing come to be ferrying Elias Thorn upriver? He's not a poor man. He could have hired a proper packet."

"Proper packets don't run in monsoon season. I do. Triple fare. I'm not sentimental, Lieutenant, I'm solvent. There's a difference, but it's a narrow one."

For a moment — one breath, maybe two — she thought he might smile. Instead he turned away and gestured, and the inspection began in earnest: hatches lifted, holds sounded with a boathook, crates pried and repacked with a carelessness that was itself a message. Astrid stood at the rail and watched them work and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the black hull first appeared. They were looking for something specific. Not contraband generally — a thing. A paper, most likely, or a person attached to a paper. Thorn's *intermittent interest*. His claims. His correspondence. His map, riding in the bilge like ballast, like a stone at the bottom of a well.

And Roque had said *Mercer* the way a man says a word he's checking against a list.

* * *

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find, and because Ruiz — bless his greasy, faithful heart — had spent the inspection kneeling in the engine room being loudly incompetent, offering to show them the bilge himself, apologizing for the smell, standing in the way with such magnificent obliviousness that the inspector finally waved him off with a grimace. The bilge hatch stayed shut. The oilcloth bundle slept on.

Roque returned to the rail where Astrid waited, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Below them the river ran brown and patient, carrying its silt, its secrets, its drowned branches, indifferent as arithmetic.

"You run this water often, Captain?"

"Every season. Every channel."

"Then you'll know the Throat is closing early this year. Glacier's calving ahead of schedule. Company advisory went out this morning — upper basin restricted to licensed traffic only." He paused, delicately. "Your license covers the lower runs. Not the Throat."

"My license covers wherever my hull floats."

"See that it doesn't float anywhere expensive." He straightened his coat, and as he turned to step back onto the gangway, he said it — quietly, almost kindly, pitched for her ears alone:

"Give my regards to the family name, Captain. Whichever one you're wearing this decade."

Astrid did not move. Did not blink. Inside her chest, something that had been water for ten years went hard and clear as ice.

"I don't know what you mean, Lieutenant."

"No," Roque agreed pleasantly. "Nobody ever does. That's what makes this river such a profitable place to work." He descended. The gangway came in. The black hull slid away from her side with that same unbearable patience, its wake rolling under the *Marta Cielo* and setting her timbers groaning, and then it was fifty yards off, a hundred, a smudge of smoke standing up against the rain-colored sky, heading upriver toward the Throat — toward the very water Astrid intended to run.

Ruiz came and stood beside her. He didn't say anything for a long moment. Then: "He knew."

"He guessed. There's a difference, but it's a narrow one." She watched the smoke. "They're not hunting Thorn, Ruiz. Thorn's dying; dead men are cheap. They're hunting what he's carrying. Which means somebody upstream already knows what it is."

"Then we turn him around. Drop him at the next landing, let his trouble be somebody else's weather."

Astrid was quiet. Below decks, faintly, she could hear Thorn muttering in his fever — fragments, numbers, soundings, a woman's name repeated like a prayer worn smooth. *Ines. Ines. Where the ice keeps time.*

Ten years ago, men with forged signatures had taken the Vasquez rights to sixty miles of the best water in the basin, and her family had scattered like seed on wind, and she had renamed herself and sworn she'd never carry anything for anyone, never owe anyone, never stop moving long enough for the past to catch her hull. Now the past was riding in her bilge in her father's handwriting, and a man with brass on his collar had just smiled at her alias like it was a joke they were both in on.

"They know the name," she said finally. "Which means the name's already in their books. Running doesn't take it out. It never has." She put her hand on the throttle and felt the old engine answer before she gave the order, faithful, stubborn, hers. "So we stop running sideways. We go through."

Ruiz stared at her. "Through the Throat? In monsoon? Captain—"

Ahead, far upriver where the valley walls drew together, thunder rolled — or something that wasn't thunder, a deep grinding report from the high country, like a door being forced. The glacier, talking in its sleep. Telling time.

Astrid opened the throttle, and the *Marta Cielo* leaned into the current, running straight at the weather.
