## 6. Monsoon Warning

The radio woke before anyone did.

It came through the *Kestrel's* cabin wall at four in the morning, a thin crackle like ice giving way, and then the voice of the Kettle Landing weather station, flat as a ledger line: *"All river traffic, all river traffic. Monsoon front has shifted north by two hundred miles. Expect sustained rainfall within seventy-two hours. Upper gorge routes will close. Repeat: upper gorge routes will close."*

Astrid Vasquez lay in her bunk listening to it twice more, once for the words and once for what lived underneath them — that low administrative calm the weather men used when they were telling people to run. She had heard that voice before, three years ago, from a different mouth, over a different river, and she had not run then either. It had cost her a boat and nearly a hand.

She got up, dressed in the dark without lighting the lamp, and went out onto the aft deck where the rain had not yet begun but the air already tasted of it — wet iron, wet stone, the green rot-smell of the lowland forests breathing out ahead of the storm. The river ran black under a sky the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges. Somewhere upstream, past the last sandbar, past Mile Forty where the channel threaded between drowned timber, Devil's Throat waited with its glacier and its secrets.

Behind her, in the forward hold, Elias Thorn coughed until the boat itself seemed to shudder.

*Seventy-two hours,* she thought. *Maybe less.*

She turned the wheel over to the current and let the *Kestrel* run.

* * *

Wren heard the radio from the cargo deck, where they'd been sleeping rough since Kettle Landing, and lay still in their bedroll doing arithmetic.

Three days to the Throat's lower mouth if the river cooperated. Two days if it didn't. And somewhere behind them — Wren had checked twice from the pilothouse window, casual as a man watching birds — the black hull of a syndicate patrol boat, holding its distance the way a held breath holds distance.

They sat up. The tarp overhead drummed once, twice, with the first exploratory drops, then went quiet, saving itself.

The map was aboard. The letter was aboard. Wren had been sent to make sure both reached syndicate hands, and every hour the *Kestrel* pushed deeper into the narrowing country made that job easier and made something else in Wren's chest harder. They kept coming back to the letter. Thorn had sealed it, but wax cracks, and paper whispers, and Wren had read enough of it through the fold to know it wasn't coordinates for gold. It was names. A list of names, and beside each name a number, and beside some of the numbers a word Wren knew from childhood dinner-table arguments spoken in furious whispers after the bailiffs left: *stolen.*

Wren's family name had been on that kind of list once. Not this one. But lists like it traveled in packs.

Rain tapped the tarp again, indecisive. Wren pulled the bedroll tighter and watched the shore slide past — dark pines, dark water, dark hills folding themselves around the river like hands closing.

*Which of us is running faster?* they wondered, and could not answer, and slept badly.

* * *

Odile Marchand smelled the monsoon before the radio confirmed it, because Odile had grown up on a delta where the storms came like visiting relatives — announced, inevitable, expensive. She stood in the galley at dawn frying the last of the good coffee grounds and listening to Thorn rave in the hold, his voice rising and falling with a rhythm she recognized from her grandmother's deathbed: the body burning through its last fuel in bright, useless flares.

*"Where the ice keeps time,"* he was saying. *"You understand? Not where the ice IS. Where it KEEPS time. There's a difference, there's a — God, there's a difference—"*

Bram, the engineer, came in wiping grease off his knuckles and poured himself coffee he didn't drink. He was sixty, built like a bollard, and he had worked the upper river long enough to have opinions he considered facts.

"We turn back," he said.

"Captain decides," Odile said.

"The captain knows I'm right. That hull's older than both of us put together. You take a boat like this into monsoon water on a flooded river, you're not running rapids, you're running a coffin." He set the cup down untouched. "And we've got a dying man aboard who wants us to go *up*, which is the wrong direction for everything except regret."

"His money cleared."

"His money'll clear the same whether we're going up or down."

Odile looked at him. Bram had lost a brother to the syndicate's dredging operations — everyone on the upper river had lost something to them, which was why nobody talked about it, the way you don't touch a burn. She said, gently, "You want to turn back because of the hull?"

Bram's jaw worked. "I want to turn back because turning back is the only thing left that's ours to decide. Once we're in the Throat, nothing's ours."

Outside, the first real rain arrived — not the shy tapping of the night but a sudden flat roar across the whole canopy, as if the sky had simply opened a valve. Odile felt it in her teeth. Seventy-two hours, the radio had said. Listening to it now, she gave it fifty.

* * *

Astrid called the crew together at midmorning in the pilothouse, rain hammering the glass so hard the river beyond was less a view than a rumor. Thorn lay below, sedated with the last of Odile's willow bark and brandy, muttering about ice and ledgers and a daughter whose name changed every time he said it.

"Here's the truth," Astrid said. "Front's early. We have maybe two days of running water before the river turns against us. The Throat's lower entrance is thirty-six hours at this speed, if the channel holds. If we turn back now, we lose the fare, we lose the delivery, and we spend monsoon sitting in some landing town with a patrol boat asking questions about our cargo." She let that sit. "Or we push on, make the lower mouth before the flood crest, and shelter in the side canyon until it passes. Old trapper cache there. Solid rock. We wait out the worst of it dry, and finish the run when the water drops."

"And if the water doesn't drop?" Bram asked. "Monsoon can run six weeks."

"Then we wait six weeks."

"With him dying in the hold."

"With him dying wherever we are," Astrid said. "That doesn't change."

"It changes everything," Bram said, "and you know it. Dead men don't pay fares, Captain, and dead men's errands don't pay double."

The wheel spoke under Astrid's hands, small corrections, the conversation of an old boat with old water. She hadn't told them about the handwriting on the map. She hadn't told them about the patrol boat. There was a version of honesty that was just cowardice wearing a lantern, and she wasn't ready for it.

"Vote, then," she said. "Odile?"

Odile shrugged. "I didn't sign on to be comfortable. Onward."

"Bram?"

The engineer stared out at the gray violence of the rain for a long moment. When he turned back, his face had settled into the grim architecture of a man who has decided to be angry rather than afraid, because anger at least points somewhere.

"Onward," he said. "But I'm logging my objection. In writing. So when we're wrapped around a rock at Mile Forty, my ghost gets to say I told you."

"Noted," Astrid said, and put the wheel over, and the *Kestrel* leaned into the current like a tired animal asked for one more mile.

From the doorway, where he'd been standing silent long enough that they'd forgotten him, Wren said: "There's a boat behind us. Black hull. It's been matching our speed since yesterday evening."

The pilothouse went very quiet except for the rain.

"I know," Astrid said.

"You knew?"

"I know most things about my river." She met Wren's eyes and found, not for the first time, that looking at them was like looking into moving water — the surface told you nothing reliable about the depth. "What I don't know is why they're following *you*."

Wren's face did something small and controlled. "Maybe they're following the dying man."

"Maybe," Astrid agreed. "Keep watching."

* * *

Thorn surfaced from fever near dusk, and for an hour the fever gave him back to himself — clear-eyed, terrible, lucid the way the dying are lucid, having burned away everything that wasn't essential.

Astrid sat with him in the hold because someone had to, and because part of her — the part that had recognized her father's handwriting on his map and had not yet forgiven herself for recognizing it — needed to hear what he would say when the drugs loosened him.

"You knew my father," she said.

Thorn's eyes found her. They were yellow now, the whites gone the color of old ivory. "I knew a lot of men," he said. "Your father was one of the few worth knowing. Worth betraying, too, apparently. Men are efficient that way."

"What did you do?"

He laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing became a bright thread of blood at the corner of his mouth that he wiped away with the indifference of long practice. "I kept books," he said. "For men who don't keep books. For men who prefer their thefts unwritten — except, of course, that everything must be written. Everything must be *counted*. That's the secret nobody tells you about power, Captain Vasquez. It's not fists. It's arithmetic."

"My father's claim—"

"Is in a book." His hand crawled across the blanket and caught her wrist with shocking strength. "In a vault. In a place where the ice keeps time — where the cold holds everything exactly as it was, year after year, nothing rotting, nothing changing, nothing *forgiven*. Do you understand? They never destroy the ledgers. Destroying them would end the claims. They *keep* them. Frozen. Perfect. Every stolen inheritance in the valley, filed like specimens." His grip tightened. "Yours is in there. Your father's river rights, your mother's name, the whole Vasquez water — page after page. I wrote some of those pages myself."

The rain filled the silence, vast and indifferent.

"Why tell me this?"

"Because I'm paying," he said simply. "Late. Badly. But paying." His eyes drifted toward the bow, toward the black upstream dark. "The map shows the way in. The letter tells her — tells whoever — what's inside. You deliver it, Captain. You deliver it if the river has to carry the boat there in pieces."

"That's not how I run a boat."

"No," Thorn said, and smiled with bloody teeth, and the smile was almost young. "That's why I chose you."

The fever took him back an hour later. By midnight he was raving again, shouting bearings to no one, shouting a woman's name — *Mara, Mara, the ice keeps time, Mara* — while Odile sat with him and did not sleep.

* * *

Wren took the midnight watch in the pilothouse and spent it counting things.

Counting the flashes of lightning upstream, which came faster now, walking toward them across the hills like something with intent. Counting the seconds between flash and thunder, which shrank each hour: ten seconds, eight, six. Counting the times they glanced astern and found the black hull's running light still there, patient as a debt.

At two in the morning the river rose visibly — you could see it happen, brown water climbing the banks, swallowing the exposed roots of trees, and Wren understood with a cold professional clarity that the flood was coming down from the high country already, ahead of schedule, that the seventy-two hours had quietly become forty-eight or fewer, and that every calculation they'd all made today was already obsolete.

Below them, the *Kestrel*'s old hull worked in the swollen current, timbers groaning in a language Bram understood and no one else wanted to learn.

Wren thought about turning the boat. They were alone at the wheel; the thought was physically available, thirty seconds of work, and no one would ever know whether the *Kestrel* had turned back on its own or been turned. They thought about the syndicate boat behind them, and the report they owed, and the sentence waiting for failure. They thought about a dinner table twenty years ago and a father saying *they keep records, kid, that's how you fight people who keep records — you get the records.*

Thunder walked closer. Five seconds now. Four.

Wren put the wheel over and held the *Kestrel* dead on the channel centerline, upstream, into the throat of the coming storm, and told themselves it was because turning back would look suspicious.

At dawn, the rain doubled. At Mile Thirty-Nine, the river lifted a submerged log out of the dark like a fist.

And the *Kestrel* began, very quietly, to die.
