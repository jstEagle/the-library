## 7. Leaking at Mile Forty

The rain came in the way the old pilots had always said it would — not all at once but in stages, like a debt collector working his way up a street. First a mist that silvered the water and made the far banks soft as charcoal sketches. Then a harder drizzle that ticked against the wheelhouse glass and turned the river the color of gunmetal. And by midmorning, when we were somewhere past Mile Thirty-Five, the real thing: fat, warm drops that hit the deck with a sound like small applause, then heavier, until the applause became a roar and the whole world was noise.

I'd been running this stretch of the Verrazano for eleven years, and I knew its moods the way I knew my own hands. The channel at Mile Forty braided wide and shallow, split by a long gravel bar that shifted every spring flood, and the safe line ran close to the left bank where the current ate at the roots of the alders. In dry years you could see every snag. In wet years — in monsoon years, which this was shaping up to be three weeks early — the water rose over everything and hid its teeth.

"Keep her on the left," I told Odile, who was standing at the rail under an oilskin slicker, watching the banks slide past. She'd been with me four seasons now, quiet as ballast, good with the engine if not with people.

"I see the line," she said.

"See it and trust it aren't the same thing."

She didn't answer. That was one reason I kept her.

Wren was on deck too, aft near the cargo hatch, and I caught myself watching them in the wheelhouse mirror more than I liked. They stood the way sailors stand — knees loose, weight riding the boat's motion instead of fighting it — but they hadn't said a word about salvage or syndicates since Kettle Landing, and they paid their fare without haggling, and they asked questions only when they thought I wasn't listening to the answers they got from Odile. Which meant they were patient. Patience on a riverboat is either a virtue or a profession.

Thorn lay below in the narrow bunk behind the engine room, wrapped in blankets and coughing blood into a tin cup he refused to let anyone else empty. He'd slept maybe two hours all night, and when he did sleep he talked — names I didn't know, numbers, once, clearly, *where the ice keeps time* — and then woke ashamed of himself and apologized to the bulkhead as if it were a person he'd offended.

The map rode in the bilge, wrapped in oilcloth inside a flour tin, wedged between the limber boards where no inspector would think to look and no leak would reach it. I'd checked it twice before dawn. I told myself I checked it because I'd given Thorn my word. I told myself a lot of things before dawn.

At Mile Forty-One the rain thickened until the alders on the left bank dissolved into gray smears, and the sound on the roof went from roar to something like standing inside a drum. Visibility dropped to perhaps sixty meters. I throttled back to half and felt the boat's response come sluggish through the wheel — the *Marta Cielo* had never been quick, but today she felt like she was wading.

"Debris," Odile called, pointing.

I saw it a heartbeat later: a whole tree coming down the current, root ball first, turning slowly like something asleep. Big enough to stove in a hull if it caught us broadside. I put the wheel over, eased right, gave it room, watched it wallow past ten meters off our starboard bow, trailing branches like drowned hair.

And that was the mistake. Not the maneuver — the maneuver was correct. The mistake was the river itself, which had spent the last day lifting things off the bottom and setting them drifting where no chart and no memory could find them. Somewhere under us, just beneath the surface where the brown water swallowed all light, lay a log the flood had torn loose from some bank upstream, waterlogged, riding low, its broken end pointed at us like a spear.

I heard it before I felt it — a deep wooden knock, once, polite as a knock at a door.

Then the *Marta Cielo* shuddered along her whole length, the wheel went strange and light in my hands, and Odile shouted something I didn't hear over the rain because the engine was suddenly screaming at a pitch no engine should scream, and the deck tilted, and cold water came up through the floorboards around my boots.

* * *

We grounded her fast. That part I'll say for myself: eleven years of never stopping moving, and when moving stopped being possible, I put her aground in ninety seconds flat, easing her bow onto the gravel shelf below the alder bank so she'd lie steady while we found out how badly she was hurt. A boat that takes water in open current is a coffin with opinions about its schedule. A boat on gravel is a problem. I can live with problems.

I got below with a lantern while Odile killed the engine, and the bilge told me everything before I reached it. Water sliding back and forth across the sole, six centimeters deep and rising, carrying pine needles and a cork float and one of Thorn's tin cups. Forward, under the cargo plates, I found the wound: a splintered gash maybe forty centimeters long where the log's butt-end had driven planks apart rather than breaking them, the way a shoulder opens a door. Not fatal. Not trivial. The kind of hole that kills you slowly, in the rain, forty miles from the nearest yard.

"We're holed," I said, coming up the ladder. "Forward bilge. We ground here, patch it, pump her out, and run for Harker's Slough before dark."

Odile looked at the sky, which had gone the color of wet slate, and said nothing, which from Odile was a full paragraph of doubt.

It was Wren who came forward, already stripping off their coat, already looking at the hatch to the forepeak with the particular expression of someone doing arithmetic. "You have canvas?" they said. "Tar, pitch, anything? And a hammer, and short nails, and something flat to back the patch."

"You've done this before," I said.

"Once or twice." They were already moving. "Canvas and tar. Go."

I went. It wasn't lost on me, going because a stranger told me to, and I noted it the way you note a current pulling at your rudder — not fighting it yet, but feeling it.

* * *

Here is what I learned about Wren in the next three hours, kneeling in six inches of bilgewater by lantern light, holding things, passing things, getting soaked to the skin in water that smelled of diesel and river mud:

They worked like a shipwright. Not like a passenger, not like someone who'd read about repairs, but like someone whose hands had done this exact work so many times the motions lived below thought. They found the gash's true shape by feel, mapped it aloud — "splinters driven inward, we push them back out, we don't cut them flush or we widen the gap" — and when I handed them the hammer they took it without looking, the way you take bread at your own table.

They backed the worst of the split with a piece of the broken log itself, sawed flat, driven tight against the planking from outside while I held position in chest-deep water against the hull — I drew the short straw by owning the boat — and nailed through into sound wood with nails I keep for exactly this prayer. Then canvas, three layers, painted thick with the tar I use on the deck seams, pressed on hot from a bucket heated over the galley stove and passed hand to hand down the ladder. Then a patch plate over all of it, bolted, caulked, tarred again.

It was ugly. It would hold. Those are usually the same thing on a river.

"Salvage," I said, at some point, when the tar fumes had made the lantern light swim. "That's where you learned this."

Wren didn't look up from driving the last bolt. "Something like that."

"On the Verrazano?"

"Up and down it. Other rivers too." They seated the bolt, tested it with a wrench, tested it again. "There's always work raising what other people sink. Boats, barges, cargo. Sometimes things that were sunk on purpose."

The rain drummed on the deck above us. The lantern hissed. Somewhere aft, Thorn coughed, a long tearing sound, and went quiet again.

"Sunk on purpose," I repeated.

"Insurance fraud. Debt collection. Disposal." Wren wiped their hands on their ruined trousers and finally looked at me, and their face in the lantern light was very still, the way water goes still over something deep. "When a syndicate lends money against a boat and the borrower can't pay, sometimes the boat has an accident. And sometimes the syndicate hires people to make sure the accident happens clean, and other people to raise whatever's worth raising after. I was the second kind. Mostly."

"And the first kind?"

A pause long enough for three waves of bilgewater to slosh against the new patch and stay, blessedly, outside it.

"Once," Wren said. "Once I was the first kind. Early. Before I understood what 'clean' meant to them." They took the lantern and held it up to inspect the patch, and their voice stayed level, but their knuckles were white around the bail. "I've been the second kind ever since. Exclusively. If that matters."

"It might," I said. "Depends what you're doing on my boat."

"That," Wren said quietly, "is a longer story than a leaking bilge."

* * *

We pumped her out through the afternoon, Odile and me taking turns on the hand pump while Wren bailed the pockets the pump couldn't reach and Thorn, against all sense, crawled out of his bunk to sit on the engine-room step and watch, wrapped in his blanket like a heron in a bad mood.

"You're good," he rasped, at Wren. "Where'd you learn?"

"Told you. Salvage."

"Syndicate salvage." Thorn's laugh turned into coughing. He spat into the bilge, unapologetic. "Boy or girl, they train you the same. Make you grateful. That's the trick of it. They give you a berth and a wage and a name to wear, and after five years you can't tell which parts of you are yours." His eyes found mine, and there was something in them I didn't want — recognition, apology, a debt being tallied. "You know how that works, captain."

"I know how boats work," I said. "Pump's yours, Odile."

But Thorn kept talking, low, to the bilge water as much as to us. "They had a ledger man on the upper river, when I was young. Kept accounts on families the way a factor keeps accounts on fur. Who owned what. Who owed what. What could be taken and what had to be waited for." His voice dropped. "Ice keeps time better than paper does. Paper burns, rots, gets eaten by mice. Ice just waits."

"Thorn." I crouched to his level. "Save it. Save your breath and save the rest for the girl. We're delivering, remember? That's the whole job. Delivery."

He looked at me with those fever-bright eyes and almost smiled. "Yes, captain. Delivery." But he said it the way a man says *yes* to a priest, meaning something entirely different underneath, and I stood up and worked the pump until my shoulders burned and I couldn't hear him anymore.

* * *

By late afternoon the bilge was down to a sheen, the patch was holding, and the rain had settled into a steady gray curtain that looked, horribly, like it might be the weather now — not a storm passing through but a season arriving early, three weeks ahead of any chart. I stood at the wheelhouse window and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Harker's Slough was six miles downstream. Any yard beyond that was two days' hard run. The radio that morning had carried the first official monsoon advisory, hedged and bureaucratic, talking about *elevated precipitation probabilities* for the upper basin, and everyone on the river knew what that translation meant: get high, get covered, or get out. Devil's Throat sat at the top of the navigable water, and above the Throat the canyon narrowed until the river came down like a dropped rope. When the monsoon hit the high country, the Throat didn't rise. It detonated.

And Thorn's daughter — Thorn's coordinates, the homestead marked on that map in handwriting I knew better than my own — lay upriver. Above Mile Sixty. Above the narrows. Possibly above sense.

Odile came into the wheelhouse and stood beside me, following my eyes upriver.

"You're thinking about it," she said.

"I'm thinking the patch holds in calm water and shakes loose in rapids."

"I'm thinking about the radio."

"I'm thinking about that too."

Behind us, on the deck, Wren appeared in the doorway with two mugs of the terrible coffee Odile brewed, and handed one to me without asking, which was either presumption or peace offering, and I honestly couldn't tell which, and neither, I suspected, could they.

"The patch will hold," Wren said. "If you don't hammer her through standing waves. Back her down anything worse than Grade Three."

"You've run the Throat?"

"No one runs the Throat in monsoon."

"That's not what I asked."

Wren drank their coffee and looked out at the rain, at the river running high and brown and fast past the alder roots, carrying whole trees now, whole worlds of debris, the flood beginning somewhere far above us and working its way down like a rumor.

"No," they said at last. "But I've read the surveys. And I've raised enough boats out of the lower gorge to know what the river does to hulls that argue with it." A pause. "Including syndicate hulls. Especially syndicate hulls. They run it heavy and fast because they can afford to lose one now and then."

From below came a crash — Thorn's tin cup, knocked from the bunk rail, rolling — and then his voice, thin but carrying, raving again at the bulkhead, at the river, at whoever his fever had summoned: *"—the ice keeps time, it keeps ALL of it, every name, every claim, you dig where the blue ice shows the rings, you'll SEE—"*

Odile and I exchanged a look.

Wren set down the mug. "Captain," they said, and for the first time since Kettle Landing there was nothing smooth in their voice at all, nothing performed, just a raw and careful question, "what exactly are we delivering?"

I looked upriver, where the rain erased the world past the first bend, and where somewhere in that erased distance a black-hulled patrol boat might already be running our wake, and where a dead man's map lay wrapped in a flour tin in my bilge, drawn in my father's hand.

"Cargo," I said. "Start the engine, Odile. We make Harker's Slough before dark, and then I decide."

But even as I said it, my hand was already on the throttle, and the *Marta Cielo* was already swinging her patched bow upriver, against the current, toward the Throat — and I told myself it was to buy time, to think, to wait out the patrol boat's search pattern downstream.

The river, which knows liars better than anyone, picked up speed beneath my keel as if to carry me there faster.
