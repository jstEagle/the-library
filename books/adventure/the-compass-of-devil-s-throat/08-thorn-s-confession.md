## 8. Thorn's Confession

The coughing woke him before the rain did.

Elias Thorn lay in the narrow bunk beneath the wheelhouse, listening to his own chest rattle like gravel in a sluice box. Each breath came in wet and went out thinner, as though something inside him were being winched away a little at a time. He had watched men die of the lung-rot before — half the camps upriver smelled of it, sweet and terrible, like flowers rotting in standing water — and he knew the sound of it now living in him. He was forty-nine years old and he sounded like seventy.

Outside, the river spoke against the hull. That low, patient conversation water makes with wood when it has decided to take its time with you.

He pulled himself upright by degrees, one hand braced on the bulkhead, and waited for the world to stop tilting. The *Marta's Promise* — that was what the boat was called, though the nameplate had been painted over so many times it read like a scar more than a word — swayed gently at her mooring off Kettle Landing's lower dock. Rain ticked against the deck above him like fingers counting.

He should have died in the mountains. That had been the plan, if a man could be said to plan such a thing: walk until his legs gave out, sit down under a spruce, and let the cold do the bookkeeping. Clean. No witnesses. But the fever had other ideas, and his legs had betrayed him down to the landing, and there she'd been — Astrid Vasquez, arms crossed on her own deck, looking at him the way a woman looks at weather she intends to outlast.

He hadn't known her face. He wanted that understood, even if only by the dark. He hadn't known. Not until the map was already out of his pack and into her hands, not until he saw her eyes move across his father's handwriting — no, not his father's, *his own*, the letters he'd copied for the syndicate for eleven years, the careful surveyor's script that had unmade families — and something behind her expression shifted like ice going out in spring.

*Vasquez*, the manifest had said. He'd read the name three times before he believed it.

Now he sat in the dark of the bunk with his lungs full of knives and did the only thing left to a dying man with an honest hour: he counted.

Eleven years of ledgers. Forty-one families. He could still recite them, some nights, in order, the way other men recited psalms. The Aldous claim on the Copperline. The Marchetti orchard rights. The Okonkwo ferry charter. The Vasquez river rights — nine miles of the best channel on the upper reach, taken in a single season through forged surveys and a judge whose daughter needed a dowry. Elias had drawn the maps himself. His hand. His ink. His beautiful, precise, damning lines.

They had paid him well. That was the joke of it, the joke he would carry into whatever came after: they paid him *well*. Enough to buy a cabin, a mule, a reputation as an eccentric who wandered the high country drawing pretty pictures. And every night for eleven years he had told himself the same story — that the families would have lost their claims anyway, that the syndicate was a river and he was only a stone in its current, that a man could not be guilty of geography.

Then the Marchettis drowned themselves in the irrigation ditch rather than be evicted, and the story stopped working.

* * *

Footsteps on the deck above. Slow ones, deliberate, the tread of someone checking lines in bad weather. Then the hatch opened and lamplight spilled down the ladder, and Astrid Vasquez descended into the hold with a mug in one hand and a bottle of something amber in the other.

"You're awake," she said. "I could hear you rattling from the wheelhouse."

"I'm always awake now," Thorn said. "Sleeping's become a negotiation I keep losing."

She set the mug near his elbow — willow bark and ginger, by the smell, the river remedy every boat woman swore by — but kept the bottle herself, settling onto the crate across from him with the ease of someone who had spent years making herself comfortable in small hard places. Up close, in the lamplight, he could see the fatigue carved under her eyes, and beneath that something older and harder to name. A watchfulness that had calcified into a way of seeing.

"Your fever's worse," she said.

"My fever's been worse since 1889."

"That so." She turned the bottle in her hands but didn't drink. "You want to tell me about the letter now? Or are we saving all your secrets for the eulogy?"

Thorn looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the deck planks, running in bright threads down the porthole glass. Somewhere forward, the crew moved quietly — he'd heard the young one, Wren, come aboard two days past, triple fare and too many questions, and he'd heard Astrid lie to them with the smoothness of long practice. Liars recognized each other. It was almost a comfort.

"The letter's not for you," he said. "It's for my daughter."

"Who lives at coordinates."

"Who lives where I say she lives." He coughed then, deep and ruinous, and pressed his fist to his mouth, and when he brought it away there was blood on his knuckles, black in the lamplight. He wiped it on the blanket without apology. She didn't flinch. He liked that about her, or would have, in another life.

"Astrid Vasquez," he said.

Her chin lifted a fraction. Just that. But her whole body had gone still, the stillness of a animal that has caught a scent on the wind.

"You know my name."

"I've known it since you signed the manifest at Kettle Landing." He drew a breath, and it cost him, and he spent it anyway. "I've known the name longer than that. Eleven years longer."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the hatch. Neither of them moved.

"There's a ledger," Thorn said. "Not the one the syndicate keeps in their counting houses. The original. Every claim they ever took — every forged survey, every bought judge, every family dispossessed on paper before they were dispossessed on land. Names, dates, signatures. Proof enough to break them in any court that isn't already bought, and proof enough to hang the men who can't be broken." He paused. "Including yours. Nine miles of the Vasquez channel. Your father fought them for six years. Did you know that?"

Something moved behind her eyes — grief, fury, both wearing each other's clothes.

"He died fighting it," she said quietly.

"He died fighting a forgery," Thorn said. "I drew the forgery."

* * *

The rain came harder after midnight, and the river rose a hand's width against the pilings, and Thorn told her everything, because there was nothing left in him but the telling.

He told her how he'd come to the syndicate young and hungry, a surveyor's son with a gift for lines and no land of his own, hired first to chart honest channels and then — so gradually he couldn't mark the day it changed — to chart dishonest ones. How the work arrived with clean names attached: *reassessments*, *boundary corrections*, *title consolidations*. How the money grew and the questions shrank. How a man can draw a line on a map and never once think of the kitchen table that line runs through, until one day he stands in the doorway of a farmhouse watching a family carry out their grandmother's coffin because the deed says the graveyard belongs to someone else, and the deed bears his handwriting, and the handwriting is beautiful.

"The worst part," he said, and his voice cracked on it, "isn't the lying. Lying's easy. You get good at it the way you get good at anything. The worst part is that I was *good*. Every line true north, every measurement exact. I took pride in it, Astrid. Pride. In the instrument of it." He laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing bent him double over the mug of cold willow bark. "A compass doesn't choose what it points at. That's what I told myself. A compass just points."

"And now?" she asked.

"Now I know the compass is a liar too. It points wherever the man holding it wants to go." He straightened, slowly, wiping his mouth. "Three years ago I started copying. Everything. Every ledger entry, every signature, every bribe recorded in their own tidy columns — I copied it into a second set of books, and I hid the second set where no auditor would ever look, because no auditor looks where the ice keeps time."

"The glacier," she said. Not a question.

"The cave below the calving shelf, up the Throat. The syndicate uses it for cold storage — seal fat, powder, things that spoil. They think of it as theirs. They don't know their own sins are sleeping forty feet above their whiskey." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, that smile, yellowed and bloodless and utterly without joy. "I put them there myself. Carried them up in survey cases over four summers. Nobody searches a dying man's maps."

"Why?" The word came out of her flat and hard. "Why hide them instead of burning them? Why not just destroy the whole rotten record?"

"Because destruction is mercy," Thorn said, "and I don't deserve mercy. Burning them frees the families, yes — but it also frees *them*. No proof, no reckoning. The syndicate keeps everything it stole and answers for none of it. No." He shook his head, slow. "The ledgers stay. They're the only honest thing I ever made. Let them testify."

"And the map?"

"The map is the penance." He reached beneath the blanket, into the oilcloth wrapping he'd slept with for three years like a child with a talisman, and drew out the weathered sheet — his own fine lines, the false bearing he'd given her at the landing, the true bearing hidden in the margin flourishes where only a surveyor would think to look. "I gave you a dead man's errand because I couldn't ask a living one. A daughter who isn't there. A delivery that ends at the glacier whether you follow the letter or the lines. I built the whole route as a trap for my own conscience." He met her eyes. "You were supposed to be angry. You were supposed to throw me off this boat at the first sandbar. Instead you're sitting here listening to a dying forger confess, and I don't know what that makes either of us."

For a long moment Astrid said nothing. The rain filled the silence, and the river filled the rain, and somewhere far upstream thunder rolled along the gorge like a door closing.

"My brother," she said finally. "Mateo. He stayed when I ran. He stayed and fought and lost, and I've hated him for it — hated him for staying, hated him for losing, hated him for making me the one who fled." Her jaw tightened. "If your ledgers say what you say they say—"

"They say the Vasquez claim was stolen twice," Thorn said. "Once by the syndicate, and once by the son who signed it over to save his own share. I'm sorry. I drew those papers too."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, the grief was still there, but it had been folded away somewhere, filed under later, and what remained was the pilot's face — the one that reads water.

"Dawn," she said. "We make for the Throat at dawn."

* * *

He did not sleep. Sleep had stopped negotiating days ago; now it simply refused, and Thorn lay in his bunk listening to the ship's timbers work and composing, in the manner of dying men, the speech he would never give.

He thought about his actual daughter — not the phantom on the letter, but the real one, Marta, grown now, living under a different name in a coastal town he'd never visited, who had every right to spit on his grave and probably would. He had written her eleven letters and sent none of them. Cowardice, he had learned, was not a single decision but a practice, renewed daily, like prayer.

He thought about the Vasquez girl sitting her wheel in the storm above him, hands steady on spokes that answered to a river that had eaten her family's livelihood, and he marveled at the arithmetic of guilt — how one man's penance required another woman's courage, how his redemption was to be purchased with her risk. There was no fair division of it. There never is. The ledger balances itself however it likes.

Near the coldest hour, footsteps again, and Wren came down the ladder — quiet as a cat, moving like someone who had learned to move in places where noise cost money. The stranger crouched by the little stove, fed it a stick, and looked at Thorn with those unreadable eyes.

"You're dying," Wren said. Not cruelly. As a fact, the way one notes weather.

"Imminently."

"Wanted to say—" Wren stopped. Started again. "The salvage crews. The ones who worked the wreck lines. We used to find papers in the hulls. Deeds, mostly. Families' papers, soaked and ruined, floating out of boats the syndicate had scuttled for insurance. I carried them to shore and burned them, because wet paper's worthless and dead men don't need deeds." A pause. "I've been thinking, lately, that maybe I should have dried them out."

Thorn studied the stranger for a long moment. "Everyone in this story has ink on their hands," he said. "Mine's just the oldest."

Wren nodded once, fed the stove another stick, and climbed back into the rain.

Dawn came gray and slow, the light rising out of the river itself, and Thorn felt it coming the way you feel a sandbar shift under a keel — a change in the pressure of things, a shallowing. He made himself sit up. He made himself dress. There was a last thing, and he meant to be upright for it.

He got as far as the deck.

The rain had thinned to mist. The river ran wide and brown and patient, and up ahead, faint through the haze, the first black walls of Devil's Throat stood out of the morning like the edge of a held breath. Astrid stood at the wheel. Wren worked the bow lines. The engine turned over with its old arthritic knock, and the *Marta's Promise* leaned into the current, and Elias Thorn gripped the rail with both hands and felt, for the first time in eleven years, that he was pointed at something true.

"Astrid," he said.

She glanced back. He raised one hand — toward the gorge, toward the glacier, toward the forty-one families sleeping in the ice — and the words he had rehearsed all night dissolved on his tongue, leaving only the simplest one.

"Forgive me. Or don't. But finish it."

Her eyes held his across the deck, and she nodded once, captain to crew, and turned back to the wheel.

Thorn slid down against the rail, and the mist came off the water cool on his face, and the last thing he saw was the gorge opening ahead of them, dark and enormous, taking the boat in.

The river, at least, had never needed his forgiveness.
