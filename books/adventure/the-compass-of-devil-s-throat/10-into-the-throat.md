## 10. Into the Throat

The rain came in the night like a debt collector.

Astrid felt it before she heard it — a change in the air pressure that made her teeth ache and the old boat's timbers tick and groan, the way they always did when the sky decided to lean on them. She was awake in her bunk with the letter open on her chest, reading it for the hundredth time by the light of a guttering oil lamp. *Not a daughter. A bearing.* The words had not changed since the homestead, since the grave with its crooked marker and its handful of river stones. Elias Thorn had not sent her upriver to deliver anything. He had sent her to *find* something, and he had used his dying breath and her own decency as the rope to haul her there.

She should have been furious. Some part of her was — a low banked coal of it, sitting behind her sternum. But underneath the fury was something worse, something she refused to look at directly: relief. Because now she didn't have to pretend anymore. Now the map wasn't a favor owed to a dead man. It was a key, and it fit a lock she'd been circling her whole life without knowing the door existed.

*Where the ice keeps time.*

The first fat drops hit the deck like thrown gravel, and then the sky simply opened.

* * *

By dawn the river had changed its mind about everything.

Astrid stood at the wheel with her collar turned up and her hands bare on wood gone slick and dark, watching the water rise around the pilings of the last fuel dock before the gorge. The current had picked up an edge overnight — that muscular, impatient shove that meant snowmelt and rainwater were conspiring somewhere upstream, filling channels that had been dry for years, drowning sandbars that had been landmarks for decades. Her charts were useless now. Half the soundings marked on them belonged to a different river.

"You're not seriously considering this."

Marta, her engineer, stood at the base of the wheelhouse stairs with a mug of coffee she hadn't drunk and a face like a closed ledger. Marta had been with her six years, through two groundings and one near-sinking, and she had earned the right to say things like *you're not seriously considering this* in exactly that tone.

"The window's closing," Astrid said. "Thorn said the calving opens the upper route. If we wait out the monsoon at the landing, the syndicate will be waiting when it clears. You saw their boat at Mile Forty. They know what we're carrying."

"They know we're carrying *something*. That's not the same as—"

"It's the same." Astrid turned back to the glass. Through the gray curtain of rain she could see Wren on the foredeck, coiling lines with quick, economical movements, pausing every few minutes to study the upstream horizon the way a gambler studies a dealer's hands. "They'll have men at every landing between here and the confluence. We can't outrun them on open water. But nobody patrols the Throat. Nobody sane goes into the Throat during monsoon."

"Because people die in the Throat during monsoon."

"Some people," Astrid said. "I don't plan on being some people."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Rain drummed on the wheelhouse roof, a sound like applause from a very large and unfriendly audience. "You've got that look," Marta said finally. "The one you get when you've already decided and you're just letting me talk so you can feel like you consulted somebody."

"I value your consultation."

"I hate you." Marta drained the coffee in one swallow and set the mug down hard. "I'll check the bilge pumps. If we're going to die in a gorge, I want to die with dry feet."

* * *

Devil's Throat announced itself long before they reached it.

First there was the sound — a deep, continuous rumble that Astrid felt in the soles of her boots and in the fillings of her teeth, the voice of ten thousand tons of water being forced through a gap that hadn't asked for it. Then the walls: black basalt rising on either side of the channel, sheer and wet and streaming with runoff, so close together in places that the river between them seemed less like a river than a held breath. Mist hung in the gorge at head height, torn into rags by the wind that funneled through, and above the mist the rain fell sideways, needling, relentless.

Astrid brought the boat in slow, engine at half, feeling for the current the way you'd feel for a stair in the dark.

Ten years ago she had run the Throat with her father, in the old days, before the syndicate took the licenses and the rights and the family along with them. She had been nineteen and immortal, standing where Wren stood now, and her father had taught her the only rule that mattered: *the river doesn't want to kill you, mija. It wants to go downhill. Your whole job is to want the same thing at the same time.*

Her father had wanted a lot of things at the wrong time. That was how they'd lost everything else. But this lesson he'd gotten right, and it lived in her hands now, independent of him, independent of grief.

"Talk to me," she called down to Wren.

Wren appeared at the wheelhouse door, soaked through, hair plastered flat, eyes bright with something that might have been fear or might have been its close cousin. "Left wall's undercut about two hundred meters ahead. I can see the boil line. There's a rock garden past it — I count five, maybe six heads showing, but the rain's lying to me about distance."

"Lying how?"

"Everything looks closer than it is. Or farther. I honestly can't tell which."

"That's the Throat," Astrid said. "It doesn't show you the river. It shows you what you're afraid the river is." She eased the throttle forward a notch. "Stay on the bow. Call the water, not the rocks. Rocks I can't dodge anyway — I need to know where the current wants us."

Wren nodded once and went back out into the rain, and Astrid watched them take up position at the rail with one hand locked on the lifeline and the other pointing, calling out the water in a clear steady voice that didn't waver even when the boat did.

*Boil line on the left. Eddy tongue center-right. Something white under the surface, mid-channel, moving fast.*

Astrid read the surface the way other people read faces. The smooth green tongue of water threading between the boils was the river telling her where it was willing to carry her; the fractured white water was it telling her where it would break her if she argued. She put the bow on the tongue and let the current take hold, and the boat surged forward like a dog slipping its leash, and the walls closed in, and the sound rose until it swallowed everything — the engines, the rain, her own heartbeat, all of it gone under the roar of the gorge.

This was the part she never told anyone. This was the part that shamed her a little, in the quiet afterward: that she loved it. That there was a version of herself that only existed here, in the narrow places, where there was no room for the past because the present took all of it — every scrap of attention, every muscle, every instinct — and left nothing over for regret. On open water she was Astrid Vasquez, or whatever was left of that name, hauling cargo and dodging debts. In the Throat she was just hands and eyes and the next fifty meters of river, and the next fifty meters after that, and that was all a person needed to be.

The first rock garden hit them like a fist.

* * *

"Hard left! LEFT!"

Astrid threw the wheel over. The hull struck something glancing — a submerged shoulder of granite wearing two inches of water for camouflage — and the whole boat shuddered, spun a quarter turn, and for one endless second lay broadside to the current while the river considered what to do with them.

Then it decided to carry them, and they shot through the gap between two rocks close enough that Astrid heard stone scrape paint off the hull, and they were through, wallowing, shipping water over the rails, Wren clinging to the bow cleat with both hands and laughing — actually laughing, a wild whoop torn away by the wind.

"Don't do that!" Astrid shouted, though she couldn't have said whether she meant the laughing or the nearly dying.

"Do what?" Wren shouted back, and laughed again.

Ahead the channel bent hard right around a spur of black rock, and beyond the bend Astrid could see the mist thickening, could hear the pitch of the roar climbing into a register she remembered from ten years ago and had hoped to forget. The Drop. Every gorge had one, if it was honest about itself — the place where the river stopped negotiating.

And behind them, faint under everything, an engine note that didn't belong.

She heard it the way you hear your own name across a crowded room. She cut the throttle to idle — reckless, insane, but she needed three seconds of quiet — and there it was again, riding under the rain: the deep diesel thrum of a heavy patrol hull, still distant, but closer than it had been at dawn. The syndicate boat. Of course. They wouldn't follow her into the Throat — no captain they hired would risk it — but they didn't have to. The Throat ran twenty miles and spat out at the lower confluence, and the confluence was theirs. Mateo's. She still didn't know why she was sure of that, only that she had been sure since the patrol captain at Mile Forty had said her surname like a man handling something hot.

They were running the gauntlet toward the mouth of the gun.

"All right," she said quietly, to the wheel, to the river, to whatever was listening. "You want to go downhill. So do I. Let's not fight about it."

She opened the throttle all the way.

* * *

The Drop took four minutes and cost them the port railing, one of the two spare propellers lashed on deck, and most of Astrid's certainty that she was going to survive it.

She ran it by memory and by faith. Memory gave her the line — right of center, then hard left at the seam where the two currents braided, then whatever came after, because no one remembered what came after, the Drop was too loud and too fast for remembering, it was pure animal now, hands and water and prayer. Faith gave her the boat. The *Compass* was thirty-one years old, patched twice, cursed regularly, and built by a shipwright named Okonkwo who had believed that a hull should be able to take a beating meant for a larger vessel. She blessed him anew with every impact.

Water came aboard in tons. The bow plunged into a trough and stayed down long enough that Astrid's stomach floated free of her body, then clawed up and over the next crest with a groan of timber that she felt in her spine. Somewhere below, Marta was fighting the bilge pumps — she could tell because the pumps were running, ragged and furious, and because Marta's voice carried up through the deck plates in a stream of profanity so creative it functioned as navigation aid.

And through all of it, Wren on the foredeck, calling the water. Never stopping. When a wave took them abeam and the world went white, Wren's voice came through it anyway — *seam's gone, new line left of the hole, TRUST ME* — and Astrid trusted them, because there was nothing else to trust, and because Wren had proven at Mile Forty with wet hands and a patch kit that they knew boats, knew salvage, knew how to keep a dying thing alive with whatever was at hand.

The last wave lifted them and set them down gently, almost courteously, into the long green run below the Drop, and the roar fell away behind them like a door closing, and Astrid discovered she had been holding her breath for approximately half a mile.

She breathed. Her hands ached. She realized she had been gripping the wheel so hard that her fingers had to be pried loose one at a time, and she did this deliberately, watching them tremble, letting them tremble, because trembling was honest and honesty was a luxury she could afford for exactly ten seconds.

* * *

Below the Drop the gorge widened a little — enough for the mist to gather instead of tear, enough for the rain to fall mostly downward again. The river ran fast but conversational, and Astrid used the respite the way she used all respites: checking damage and counting enemies.

The damage: port railing gone aft of the winch. Both bilge pumps working but the starboard one laboring, coughing at intervals, which meant debris in the intake or worse. The spare propeller was somewhere in the Throat now, keeping the rocks company. And there was a new sound from the hull, a faint flexing creak forward of the engine room that Marta would deny and Astrid would lie awake listening to.

The enemies: one heavy patrol hull, closing. One brother, waiting at the end of the water. One dead man's map, sealed in oilcloth inside her coat, warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Wren came aft and leaned in the wheelhouse doorway, dripping, breathing hard, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The rain filled the silence with static.

"You knew that line," Wren said at last. "At the Drop. Nobody runs that line blind. You had it before you saw it."

"I ran it ten years ago. With my father."

"And it's still in your hands."

"Rivers don't change as much as rivers' daughters do." Astrid wiped her face with her sleeve, which accomplished nothing, the rain being committed to its work. "How are you with a caulking iron? That creak forward of the engine room is going to want attention before the confluence."

"I'm good with seams." Wren said it lightly, but something moved behind their eyes — that flicker Astrid had learned to watch for, the one that surfaced whenever salvage came up, whenever the question of *where did you learn that* got within arm's reach. "I'll take a look."

"Wren."

They paused at the rail.

"Whoever taught you — whoever you worked for — they're going to be at that confluence. Aren't they."

Wren was quiet long enough that the rain got loud again. "Yes," they said finally. "Probably."

"Then say it now. Whatever it is. Because in an hour we're going to be too busy staying alive to have this conversation, and I'm done finding out the important things about the people on my boat after the water's already coming aboard."

Wren looked at her, and Astrid watched them weigh it — watched the confession climb toward the surface and get pulled back down, twice, three times, a swimmer testing cold water. "It's not a short story," they said.

"We've got twenty miles of gorge."

But before Wren could answer, the sound came again from behind them — closer now, unmistakable, the diesel growl bouncing off the canyon walls so that it seemed to come from everywhere at once — and beneath it, thin and distorted by echo, a voice amplified through a loudhailer, saying words that the wind shredded but whose shape Astrid recognized the way you recognize a knife coming.

She caught only fragments. *...last warning... ...Vasquez... ...the ledger...*

The ledger. Not the map. The ledger.

So they knew. Thorn had told more people than her, or the syndicate had known all along what the old man carried, and everything since the homestead — the grave, the letter, the bearing — had happened inside a net she'd been swimming into with her eyes open and her mouth full of river.

Astrid put her hand flat against the oilcloth packet over her heart and felt the map's edges through the fabric, sharp as a promise.

"They're not going to follow us in," she said. "They don't have to. They're herding us. Every mile of this gorge ends at their dock." She looked upstream at the black walls, at the rain stitching water to water, and felt the terror in her chest do the thing it always did when she fed it enough focus — turn over, change state, become something with an edge. "So we don't go where the gorge ends. There's a side channel at Mile Sixteen. Old survey maps call it the Weeping Stair. My father said it connects to the high country above the glacier."

"The one where the ice keeps time?"

"The very same." She pushed the throttle forward and the *Compass* answered, tired but game, leaning into the current like an old horse that had decided, against all evidence, to trust the rider. "Hold on to something, Wren. And start talking. You've got sixteen miles to make your story worth the fare."

Behind them the loudhailer boomed again, and the gorge ate the words, and the river carried them all — the hunted and the hunters, the guilty and the grieving — downhill together, toward the ice.
