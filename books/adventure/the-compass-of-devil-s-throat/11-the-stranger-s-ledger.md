## 11. The Stranger's Ledger

The rain had found a rhythm by the time Wren gave up pretending to sleep.

It came off the gorge walls in sheets, drumming the deck planks like fingers on a coffin lid, and beneath it the river spoke in its new monsoon voice—deeper than it had been two days ago, hungrier, full of gravel and drowned trees grinding against each other in the dark. Wren lay in the narrow bunk aft of the wheelhouse, listening to the *Vasquez* boat's hull work against the current. The old girl groaned at every seam. She had been leaking since Mile Forty, and she would leak until someone with more time and drier timber could do something about it, and every hour the water rose another finger's width in the bilge.

Wren had patched her once already. Knees deep in cold riverwater, hands numb past feeling, pressing tarred canvas into a split that wanted to swallow them whole. Astrid had worked beside them without a word for three hours, and when it was done she had looked at Wren's hands—cut, blackened, shaking—and said only, "You've done this before."

Not a question. That was the trouble with Astrid Vasquez. She never asked questions, which meant she was always answering them herself.

Wren turned onto their side and watched rain crawl down the porthole glass. Somewhere forward, Thorn's body lay wrapped in sailcloth on the cargo deck, weighted with chain, waiting for a stretch of slow water deep enough to give him back to the river. He had died at dawn as if he'd been holding out for the light, and Wren had watched Astrid stand over him for a long time afterward, saying nothing, doing nothing, just breathing like a woman counting what she owed.

The letter was still in Wren's coat.

They had taken it from Thorn's effects after the burial preparations—or told themselves they had taken it, though their hands had moved toward it before any decision was made—and read it twice by matchlight while Astrid slept at the wheel. It was supposed to be reconnaissance. It was supposed to be the job: find out what the old man had passed along, confirm the map's bearing, report back. That was all. Wren had done a hundred jobs like it, small mercies of information, and had never once lost sleep over them.

But the letter wasn't about gold.

Wren closed their eyes and saw the words again anyway, the way you see a face in firelight after the fire is out. *To whoever carries this,* it began, because Thorn had known his daughter was a grave and had written to a stranger instead. And then the old man had laid it all out in a dying hand that wobbled across the page like a drunk walking a plank—the syndicate, the ledgers, the vault where the ice kept time, and the names. Dozens of names. Families who had come up this river with deeds and rights and inheritances in their pockets, and been relieved of them by men with ledgers instead of guns, paper thefts so clean the victims often never knew they'd been robbed until they tried to sell or pass on what was theirs and found it already belonged to someone else.

*Ask the bearer whose name is written in my book of debts,* the letter said near the end. *I stole from everyone I loved. I only ever repaid one debt, and I paid it with a map.*

Wren had gone through the letter looking for the daughter's name and found instead, in Thorn's careful enumeration of the ledger's contents—a list he seemed to need to make, a confession in inventory form—a name that stopped their breath.

Halloway.

Not their name now. They had shed it years ago along with everything else, the way you shed wet clothes you can't bear to wear another minute. But ink doesn't care what you call yourself. Ink keeps what it's given. *Halloway & Sons, ferry rights, lower Kettle Landing, acquired 18—, consideration: none recorded.*

Consideration: none recorded. Four words that meant stolen. Four words that meant there was a page in a frozen vault with Wren's father's name on it, and their grandfather's, and the ferry landing where Wren had learned to swim and to lie and to want things, all of it filed away like a specimen in ice, owned now by men who had never seen the water.

Wren had sat with the letter until the matches burned down to their fingers.

That had been last night. Tonight the guilt had caught up, the way guilt does on rivers—it travels faster than boats.

* * *

Astrid found them on the foredeck at first light, or what passed for first light under the monsoon—a gray dimness like the inside of a shell, rain silvering down through it, the gorge walls rising on either side so sheer and black that the sky was just a rumor between them.

She didn't say anything at first. She stood at the rail a few feet away, watching the water, and Wren understood that this too was a kind of question. The silence had weight. It pressed.

"You went through his things," she said finally. Not accusing. Worse—flat, factual, the voice of a woman reading a channel ahead.

"I did."

"The letter's missing."

"It's not missing." Wren pulled it from inside their coat, dry inside an oilcloth wrap, and held it out. "I read it. I'm giving it back."

Astrid took it but didn't look at it. She looked at Wren instead, and her eyes in the gray light were the color of the river under storm—brown shot through with moving green, impossible to read a bottom in. "You were sent," she said. "Someone sent you."

There was no point denying it. Wren had watched her talk her way past a syndicate patrol captain with a smile and a lie smooth as river stone; they had watched her hide a map in a bilge full of filthy water without flinching. She missed nothing. Denial would only insult her, and insulting her felt suddenly like a thing Wren couldn't afford.

"Yes."

"Who?"

"The Meridian Salvage Combine." Saying it aloud felt like opening a vein. "Their recovery division. I was contracted to intercept the map before it reached anyone downstream. Confirm its bearing. Report." Wren heard their own voice go quiet. "I boarded at Kettle Landing because that's where the job said you'd stop."

Astrid nodded slowly, and Wren braced for the fury—for the rail, for the river, for being put off in the rain a hundred miles from anywhere with nothing but the clothes they'd lied in.

Instead she said, "Triple fare."

"What?"

"You paid triple fare. Nobody running an errand pays triple fare." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I knew. From the second night. You asked about Devil's Throat like a man asking directions to a place he'd already been."

Wren's face went hot, then cold. "Then why keep me aboard?"

"Because you patched my hull like your life depended on it. Because you didn't run when the patrol boat came. And because—" she stopped, jaw working, and looked out at the water. "Because I know what it is to be sent somewhere by people who own a piece of you. I wanted to see what you'd do when you got there."

The rain filled the silence between them, patient as interest accruing.

"There's something else," Wren said.

"I assumed."

Wren told her. All of it, standing at the rail with the letter between them like a body on a table—the job, the reading, the list of names, and then the name. Their voice stayed level until it didn't. When they said *Halloway*, their throat closed around it like a fist, and they had to start again, and the second time it came out cracked and young and sounding exactly like the kid who had stood on the lower landing at fourteen years old and watched strangers measure the dock their family had built, writing numbers in a book, telling them it was all perfectly legal, perfectly settled, nothing personal.

"Consideration: none recorded," Wren finished. "That's what it says. That's what my family's whole life was worth to them. Nothing recorded. We weren't even worth the ink of a price."

Astrid was silent a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of her hat and dripped between them.

"My father's handwriting is on that map," she said at last. "Thorn carried it eleven years. I've spent ten years making sure no one ever wrote my name down anywhere—not on manifests, not on registries, nowhere. I named my boat nothing. I let people think Ishikawa was mine because a borrowed name leaves no trail." She folded the letter along its worn creases, precise as a surgeon. "And you've spent however many years letting a name be taken off you. We're not so different, you and I. Except you went to work for them."

The words landed like a hook behind the sternum. Wren didn't flinch away from them—that felt important, somehow, to stand in the line of it.

"Yes," they said. "I did. I told myself it was just information. Paper moves, paper gets found, nobody bleeds. But paper is how they bleed you. Every page in that vault is somebody's landing, somebody's orchard, somebody's right to fish water their grandmother fished. I helped them guard the vault. I just didn't know I was guarding it from the inside of my own name."

"And now?"

"Now—" Wren looked upriver, where the gorge narrowed toward Devil's Throat, walls closing like a book deciding whether to open. The rain was heavier there, or maybe it only looked that way. "Now I want to see the page with my father's name on it. I want to hold it in my hands. And then I want to decide what happens to it. Not them. Not the Combine. Not even the dead man's ghost. Me."

Astrid studied them for a long time, and whatever she was reading in Wren's face, she must have found the channel sound, because she finally tucked the letter inside her coat, next to the map, next to whatever arithmetic she'd been running since Kettle Landing.

"The syndicate will have figured out by now that their courier went quiet," she said. "When they do, they won't send questions. They'll send the black boat, and it won't ask to inspect anything." She turned toward the wheelhouse, then paused. "You could still go back. There'll be a landing below the Throat. You could walk away clean, tell them the map was destroyed, collect whatever they pay for failure that comes with a story attached."

"No," Wren said.

It came out fast and certain, surprising them both.

"No," they said again, slower, tasting it. "I'm done being sent. Whatever's up that river, I'm choosing it."

Astrid looked at them a moment longer, and then she did smile—small, tired, real, the first one Wren had seen from her that wasn't deployed for someone else's benefit.

"Then get below and bail," she said. "We take the Throat on tomorrow's light, if the rain gives us any. And Wren—" she paused at the wheelhouse door, half-turned, rain streaming off her, "—when we find that vault, you're the one who reads the names. All of them. Out loud. Somebody should hear them besides the ice."

She went inside. Wren stood at the rail a while longer, watching the gray water slide past, carrying silt from country they'd never see, going where the river decided.

Then they went below to bail, hands steady for the first time since Kettle Landing, and above them the gorge walls leaned closer together, and far upstream, where the ice kept time, something in the dark was waiting to be read.
