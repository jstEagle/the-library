## 12. Brother at the Confluence

The rain had found a rhythm by the time they saw the fires.

Astrid stood at the wheel with her hands wrapped in wet cloth, knuckles screaming from six hours of fighting the current, and watched the lights bloom out of the dark like a fever breaking on skin. The confluence camp sat where the Graywater met the Vane — two rivers arguing themselves into one — and its fires burned orange through the curtain of monsoon, throwing long shadows across the water that looked, from this distance, like hands reaching for the boat.

"Camp's bigger than I remember," Callum said from behind her. He'd been bailing since Mile Forty, off and on, and his voice had gone hoarse with exhaustion. "Last survey put it at a dozen men."

"Surveys are old." Astrid eased the throttle down. "Syndicate money isn't."

Wren came up beside her, close enough that she could feel the heat of them through their soaked jackets. They hadn't spoken since the confession below decks — since Wren had said *my family's name is in those ledgers* with a voice like something torn open — but their shoulder pressed hers now, brief and deliberate, an anchor dropped without ceremony.

"They'll have heard us coming," Wren said. "Engines carry up the gorge."

"I know."

"And you're still going to dock?"

Astrid watched the fires. Somewhere under all that light was a ledger vault packed in ice, and somewhere behind all that ice was a man who had signed her family's river rights away with a flourish of ink she could still see when she closed her eyes. She had spent ten years telling herself he was dead to her, which was easier than admitting he was alive and she missed him.

"I'm going to dock," she said, "because the alternative is running the Throat backward in the dark, and I'd rather face the devil I know than the rapids I don't."

* * *

They tied off at the lower pilings just past midnight. The camp resolved itself slowly as they approached: stilted barracks built from salvaged timber, a crane arm reaching over the water like a question, tarps strung between poles so the whole settlement looked draped in funeral cloth. Men moved between the fires with the loose-shouldered walk of people who were paid enough not to care and not enough to leave.

Astrid came down the gangplank first. She'd left the map sewn into her coat lining and the letter against her ribs, and she walked with her weight forward, the way you do when you expect the ground to shift.

The man who met her at the fire line was broad through the shoulders, gray coming in early at his temples, wearing a syndicate oilskin that fit him too well. He carried no weapon that she could see, which meant he didn't need one. Behind him, half a dozen men arranged themselves with the casual geometry of violence.

"You're a long way past your draft mark, Captain," he said. "This stretch of river's closed for salvage operations."

"Closed rivers belong to someone," Astrid said. "I'd like to know who."

He smiled then, and the firelight caught the scar through his left eyebrow — the one he'd gotten falling out of the persimmon tree when he was nine, the one she'd cleaned with river water and her own shirt because their mother was already gone by then and there was no one else to do it.

The world went very quiet, the way it does before a hull gives way.

"Mateo," she said.

His smile held, but something moved behind it — something old and drowning. "Hello, Astrid."

* * *

They gave her tea she didn't drink, in a barracks office that smelled of diesel and wet canvas. Mateo dismissed his men, and then it was just the two of them and the rain hammering the tarp overhead like a crowd that wouldn't stop applauding something terrible.

"You look good," he said.

"Don't."

"Astrid—"

"I said *don't*." Her voice came out flat and cold, and she was grateful for it, because underneath the flatness was everything else — ten years of it, pressing up her throat like floodwater. "You don't get to tell me how I look. You don't get to say my name like we're sitting on the porch at Vasquez Bend watching the fog come off the water. That porch is gone. That bend is gone. You signed it away."

"I was nineteen."

"I was seventeen, and I managed not to sign."

"You weren't there." For the first time his composure cracked, just at the edges, like ice before the calving. "You weren't in that room, Astrid. You didn't see what they showed me. Dad's debts, the liens, the photographs of what happens to families who fight them. They gave me a pen and told me I could either be a partner or a cautionary tale. Nineteen years old, and I chose to keep us breathing."

"Keep *us* breathing?" She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Where were you when Mama died? Where were you when I buried her? Where were you when I took this boat and swore I'd never stop moving because stopping meant thinking about the brother who sold our name?"

"I was here." He said it quietly. "Enforcing the same thefts that emptied us. Because if I didn't do it, someone crueller would, and I could at least slow the paperwork. I could lose files. I could misfile a claim here, delay an eviction there. I told myself it mattered."

"And did it?"

The silence stretched. Rain filled it.

"I don't know anymore," he said finally, and it was the most honest thing he'd said in ten years, and it broke her heart more cleanly than any lie would have.

* * *

She should have left then. She knew it even as she stayed — knew it the way you know a sandbar is shifting under your keel before the depth gauge confirms it. But he poured himself a drink, and pushed the bottle toward her, and said, "How did you find the Throat, Astrid? Really."

And she heard the trap in it, soft as a snare-wire under leaves.

"Elias Thorn," she said, because lying to him had never once worked, even when they were children stealing plums off the Hendricks' tree. "Dying man. Paid me to deliver a map to his daughter."

Something crossed Mateo's face — recognition, and beneath it, dread.

"Thorn," he repeated. "Old prospector. Worked the upper claims back in the day."

"That's what he said."

Mateo set down his glass very carefully, the way a man does when his hands want to shake and he won't permit it. "What was on the map?"

"Rapids. Bearings. A route through the Throat."

"Astrid." He leaned forward, and the firelight from the stove made him look suddenly older, suddenly tired, suddenly like the boy who used to fall out of trees and come home bleeding and laughing. "Whatever he paid you, whatever he promised you — give it to me. The map, the letter, anything he gave you. There are men upstream who will burn your boat with you in it for less than what you're carrying. I'm trying to save your life."

"You're trying to save your ledger."

"It's the same thing!" It burst out of him, loud enough that someone outside shifted their weight on the boards. He lowered his voice, and it was worse for being quiet. "Don't you understand? If Thorn's map leads where I think it leads, and if anyone finds what's there — the ledgers, the vault, all of it — then everything I've done for ten years means nothing. Every compromise, every dirty job, every night I couldn't sleep — it was all building toward a pension, Astrid. A buyout. A way out. And that map is the difference between walking away clean and disappearing into the river like everyone else who crossed them."

"So that's what we are now." She stood. Her legs were steady, which surprised her; everything else felt like standing on a deck in a storm. "Collateral in your exit strategy."

"Astrid—"

"No." She picked up her hat from the table. "You asked how I found the Throat. Here's how: a dying man asked me to keep a promise, and I said yes, because keeping promises is the only inheritance our family has left. You had the same inheritance, Mateo. You just spent yours."

At the door she stopped. She didn't turn around. She looked at the rain streaming off the eave and said, quietly, "Mama kept a plate for you until the year she died. Set it at every supper. Do you know what I did with it after?"

She heard him stand, heard the boards creak under his weight, heard him stop halfway across the room as if the distance between them had become a river too wide to ford.

"What did you do with it?" His voice was wrecked.

"I let the river take it." She pulled her hat brim low. "It seemed fitting."

* * *

Outside, the rain had thickened into something vertical and total. Wren fell into step beside her without a word, matching her stride down the boardwalk toward the boat, and only when they were past the last fire did they speak.

"Well?" Wren asked. "Do we run tonight?"

"He'll have guards on the water by dawn. Maybe sooner." Astrid's mind was already working the problem — currents, fuel, the leak they'd patched at Mile Forty holding or failing. "We go at first light, before the patrol rotation changes. We take the eastern channel where the sonar's useless."

"And the vault?"

Astrid stopped at the head of the gangplank. Below her, the river churned black and silver, carrying ice down from the glacier like a fleet of small white boats, all of them headed the same direction, all of them obedient to the same pull.

"The letter named coordinates inside the Throat," she said. "Thorn raved about a place *where the ice keeps time*. If the vault's real, it's real, and it's upstream of us and downstream of nothing. We're the only ones who know where to look."

"Except Mateo suspects."

"Mateo *knows*." She said it and felt the truth of it settle over her like ballast. "He knew Thorn's name. He knew what the map might lead to. Which means the syndicate knows it exists, and they've been looking for it longer than we have."

Wren was quiet a moment. "Then we're racing them."

"We were always racing them." Astrid looked back once, up the boardwalk, toward the barracks where her brother stood in a lit window with a glass in his hand, watching her the way you watch a boat leave harbor — knowing you can't call it back, knowing you never tried hard enough to make it stay. "Get some sleep, Wren. Both watches at first light. We run the eastern channel and we don't stop for anything."

"And if he comes after us himself?"

Astrid stepped down onto the deck of her boat, her boat, the one thing on this river that had never lied about its name.

"Then I hope he remembers," she said, "that I learned everything I know about piloting from watching him read water."

She went below to check the bilge, and above her the rain kept falling, patient as a debt collector, certain as a tide.
