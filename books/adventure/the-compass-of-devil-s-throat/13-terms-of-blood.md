## 13. Terms of Blood

Mateo Vasquez had learned long ago that power smelled like nothing at all.

That was the first thing they taught you in the syndicate's service, though no one said it aloud. Fear had a smell — sweat and river mud and the sour breath of men who hadn't slept. Grief had a smell. But authority, real authority, was odorless. It was a clean canvas tarp over the confluence camp's stores, a kettle of coffee that someone else had boiled, a chair that no one asked you to give up. Mateo sat in that chair now, beneath a stretched tarp strung between two cottonwoods, and watched the rain come down the gorge in gray curtains, and felt nothing where his soul was supposed to be.

The camp at the confluence was built the way all syndicate camps were built: temporary-looking, permanent as teeth. Canvas and timber that could be struck in an hour, but the pilings driven ten feet into stone. A radio mast disguised as a surveyor's pole. Crates stenciled with the names of mining concerns that existed only on paper. Forty men on the payroll, half of them boys from villages whose names had been erased from the shipping charts twenty years ago — villages like the one he and Astrid had been born beside, back when the river still carried their family's name on its manifests.

He turned the tin cup in his hands. The coffee was good. That was the thing no one warned you about, either. The coffee was always good.

* * *

She had come up the switchback trail an hour after dawn, under guard, hands loose at her sides, chin level. He'd watched her through the field glasses from the ridge before she ever reached the camp — watched her count the sentries without appearing to, watch her mark the boat landing, the fuel cache, the sight lines. Ten years gone and she still walked like a woman taking the measure of a room she intended to leave by her own door.

His sister. Astrid.

The last time he had seen her face this close, it had been smeared with coal dust at their father's funeral fire, and she had been seventeen, and she had told him she would never forgive him for what he was about to do. She had been right, which was the cruelest part. She'd had the prophecy of it, the way she'd always had the river — reading water three bends ahead while everyone else was still watching the bank.

Now she stood at the edge of the tarp's shelter, rain running off her oilskin in bright threads, and looked at him the way you look at a sandbar that has shifted since your last charting. Not hostile. Recalculating.

"You've put on weight," she said.

"You haven't." He gestured to the second chair. It had been placed there that morning, angled just so, by a man who understood staging the way other men understood rigging. "Sit down, Astrid. You're dripping on my ledger."

Something flickered across her face at the word *ledger*. Good. She knew what he knew. Thorn's map had reached her; the syndicate's own runners had confirmed the old man had boarded her boat at Kettle Landing with a sealed case and a cough that sounded like gravel sliding downhill. And somewhere between there and here, the terms of everything had changed.

She sat. She did not drip on the ledger, because there was no ledger on the table — only the coffee pot, two cups, and a flat lacquered box that he kept his hand resting on, lightly, the way a man rests his hand on a dog that might bite.

"Where's Thorn?" she asked.

"Dying or dead. He was dying when he boarded your boat, so I'd wager dead." Mateo poured her coffee himself, which the guards at the treeline would find remarkable. Mateo Vasquez poured coffee for no one. "You buried him?"

"We put him in the river above the homestead. His way."

"The daughter's grave too, then." He said it gently, and watched the gentleness land like a hook. Her jaw tightened. "Yes. We know about the homestead. We've known about that clearing for eleven years. Thorn bought his silence with geography — we let him live out there because he never came back east of the narrows, and because a dying man's guilt is a leash shorter than any rope."

"He wasn't leashed," Astrid said. "He was carrying something to you. Or away from you. I haven't decided which story is true."

"They're both true. That's usually how it goes with penance." He leaned back. Rain drummed the tarp overhead, a sound like fingers on a coffin lid. "I'm going to make you an offer, Astrid. Before I do, I want you to understand something. I want you to understand that I know exactly what this will look like to you, and I'm making it anyway, because it's the only honest thing left in my inventory."

"That should be reassuring," she said dryly. "It isn't."

"It shouldn't be."

* * *

Here was what Mateo remembered, in the long middle watches of nights when the camp slept and the river spoke in its sleep below the bluff:

He remembered his father standing in the shallows at dawn, hip-deep, teaching them both to read the water. *The river doesn't owe you anything,* Tomás Vasquez used to say, *but it keeps its books honestly. Learn the entries.* He had been a licensed pilot then, with rights to run cargo on forty miles of the upper channel — rights signed and stamped and paid for by a grandfather who had bought them off a fur company with gold dust and stubbornness. The Vasquez name had been on those waters the way a name is carved into a prow. Not decoration. Title.

Then the syndicate had come upriver the way syndicates do — not with guns first but with paper. An audit. A lien. A court sitting three hundred miles downstream in a town where the judge owned shares in a dredging concern through his brother-in-law. Their father fought it for two years and lost everything but the houseboat, and then lost the houseboat to a fire that the insurance investigators called suspicious and everyone else called instructive. Tomás Vasquez drank himself into the current within the year. Their mother went inland to cousins who had no room. And Mateo, nineteen years old and furious, had done the arithmetic that Astrid had refused to do: the river kept its books honestly, but the men who held the ledgers did not, and the only way to survive inside a rigged game was to become the kind of player the riggers needed.

He had told himself he would climb high enough to reach the levers. That was the promise. Every man in the syndicate's service made some version of that promise to himself, and every one of them discovered, sooner or later, that the ladder went up only as far as the handholds were useful. Above a certain height there were no rungs, only hands reaching down to use yours.

He had enforced the seizure of eleven families' rights in nine years. He could recite them like a litany, and sometimes, in the bad hours, he did. The Okonkwos' ferry charter. The Lindqvist dredge claim. The Reyes moorage. On and on, each one a small honest enterprise converted into a syndicate tollbooth, each one signed away by a man like him holding out a pen and a version of mercy.

And the Vasquez claim itself — the original, the rootstock — had been the twelfth. Reassigned to him personally, as a test. Sign away your own blood, and we'll know you're ours. He had signed. The ink had been blue. He remembered that. Blue ink, on a day of hard frost, and his hand steady, and something in his chest going quiet in a way that had never come back.

Power smelled like nothing. That was what remained where the quiet had spread.

* * *

"I'll say it plainly," he said now, "because you'll trust plainness more than kindness, and you'd be right to."

He lifted the lacquered box, opened it, and turned it toward her. Inside, on green felt, lay a single sheet of heavy paper under a thin pane of glass. The syndicate's seal in red wax. The text dense and formal. And there, in the third paragraph, the words *Vasquez* and *in perpetuity* and *restored*, and at the bottom a signature line already bearing the initials of two directors.

Astrid looked at it for a long time. She didn't touch it. He noted that — the discipline of it, the way her hands stayed wrapped around the tin cup as if the cup were the only true thing on the table.

"Restored rights," he said. "Full title. Upper channel, forty miles, the same grant our grandfather held. Countersigned before the monsoon ends. In exchange: Thorn's map, whatever papers he gave you, and your silence. Not just your silence — your crew's, your stranger's, everyone aboard your leaking tub. You take the grant, you go back to running honest cargo, and the Throat stays closed. Nobody burns anything. Nobody talks. The world keeps its present shape, and you get back the only thing I know you've ever wanted."

"And the ledgers," Astrid said quietly. "The ones in the ice. They stay where they are."

"They stay where they are."

"Bound. Every family in them stays bound."

Mateo held her gaze. This was the moment he had rehearsed on the ridge at dawn, and rehearsing had been useless, because there was no way to hold this moment that didn't feel like holding a knife by the blade.

"Yes," he said. "They stay bound."

* * *

She set down the cup. Carefully, precisely, the way she did everything — he remembered that from childhood, the terrible competence of her, eight years old and coiling lines better than deckhands twice her age.

"Say her name," Astrid said.

"What?"

"Lena Okonkwo. She ran the ferry crossing at Mile Twelve when we were children. Papa traded eggs with her husband. Say her name and tell me what she got for her charter."

"Astrid—"

"Say it."

He looked at the rain. "She got a settlement of sixty dollars and a warning not to appeal."

"She got sixty dollars," Astrid repeated, "and a warning. And you delivered it. Didn't you. You were the one at her table with the pen."

There was no answer that wasn't confession, so he gave none. Outside, a sentry called the half-hour to another sentry, voices flattened by rain. Somewhere down at the landing, the black patrol boat's engine idled low, patient as a shark's heart.

"You look well," Astrid said at last, and her voice had gone strange and soft, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "That's the thing I couldn't have imagined, all these years. I thought they'd at least leave marks. I thought I'd see what it cost you written on you somewhere. But you look fed. You look rested. You look like a man who sleeps at night, and I keep trying to find the place where they hollowed you out, and I can't see it, and that's worse. Do you understand? If you were broken, I could grieve you. If you were cruel, I could hate you. But you're just—" she searched, and found it, and said it without flinching, "—comfortable. They gave you a chair, Mateo. And you sat in it for nine years while Lena Okonkwo ferried passengers on a boat she rented from the men who took hers."

The words went in the way cold water goes into a boot — slowly, and then all at once, and then everywhere. He did not move. He had learned not to move. That was the whole curriculum of power: learn not to move.

"You think I don't know their names?" he said finally. His voice came out lower than he intended. "You think I sleep the way you think I sleep? I can recite all twelve seizures, Astrid. Dates, signatures, settlements. I keep the list myself. In my own hand. Because if I don't keep it, no one does, and if no one does, then it was all just weather."

"Then you know what the list is worth."

"It's worth my life."

"No," she said. "Your life is worth the list. That's the difference you never learned. You thought climbing meant the river would finally owe you something. But the river doesn't owe anybody anything — Papa told us that on the same morning he told us it keeps honest books. You just stopped believing the second half. The ledgers are the dishonest books, Mateo. All of it — the syndicate, the grants, this box, your chair — it's all one long falsified entry, and burning it doesn't destroy anything true. It just stops the lie from compounding."

He laughed. It surprised them both — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing his own funeral sermon preached accurately.

"And your share?" he said. "Our grandfather's forty miles? Your name back on the water? You'd burn that too? Out of principle? You'd hand up everything and walk away with nothing but — what, being right?"

"I'd walk away with the river," Astrid said. "Which is more than you own, sitting on top of all of it."

* * *

Later — much later, after she had risen and declined the escort back down to the landing, after she had paused at the edge of the tarp and looked back at him once with an expression he would spend the rest of the night failing to name — Mateo sat alone with the lacquered box open in front of him.

The rain had thickened. Down at the landing, lanterns swung on their hooks, and the patrol boat's searchlight swept the confluence in slow white arcs, checking, always checking. His radio operator would be expecting orders. His superiors, downstream, would be expecting results. There was a man named Corvus arriving on the morning boat — Mateo had read the wire twice — a man with a director's pin and a reputation for closing accounts, and the wire had used the phrase *final resolution regarding the Thorn materials*, and Mateo had understood, with the clarity that comes too late to be useful, what *final resolution* meant when it appeared in a sentence containing his own name.

He looked at the grant in its glass pane. *Vasquez. In perpetuity. Restored.*

It was beautiful. That was the trap of it, and he could see the trap plainly now, sitting in his chair in the smell-less dark, and seeing it changed nothing. It was beautiful the way a lure is beautiful — engineered to be, by people who studied hunger the way engineers study load. Our grandfather's forty miles. His father's dawn shallows. Astrid's name and his own, back on the water, in perpetuity, as if perpetuity were a thing anyone downriver had ever honored.

He thought about the list he kept in his own hand. Twelve entries. He had told himself for nine years that keeping it was a form of resistance, a candle in the vault. Sitting here, he could finally admit what it actually was: a record of his own signature, kept by the only witness who would never testify.

From the treeline, one of the night sentries moved along his round, boots sucking in wet duff, and Mateo heard — faintly, carried down the bluff on the rain-wind — the sound of the riverboat's engine turning over at the landing. Low, cautious, testing. Getting up steam in the dark.

They were going to run for it. Of course they were. His sister had sat in his chair, drunk his coffee, looked at the most beautiful lie the syndicate had ever printed, and thanked him for the directions to the vault by refusing them.

Mateo closed the lacquered box. Then he opened it again. Then he took out the grant — the heavy paper, the red seal, the two directors' initials — and held it over the table lamp's flame, and did not let go, and watched the corner catch and curl and blacken, and felt, for the first time in nine years, something move in the hollow where his chest used to be. Not warmth. Not yet. Just motion. The feeling of a frozen river, deep under the ice, beginning — very slightly, in the dark, where no one could see — to shift.

By morning, Corvus would arrive asking for the Thorn materials, and Mateo Vasquez would have to decide, before the sun cleared the eastern rim of the Throat, which side of his own ledger he intended to die on.

Down at the landing, the engine note climbed. The riverboat was moving.
