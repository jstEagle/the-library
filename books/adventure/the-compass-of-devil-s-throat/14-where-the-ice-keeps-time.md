## 14. Where the Ice Keeps Time

**Astrid**

The map had lied to them twice before it told the truth.

The first lie was the daughter — a grave behind an abandoned homestead, a wooden marker softened by ten winters of meltwater, and a letter that named no child at all but a bearing: *two hundred and six degrees from the split stone, past the weeping wall, where the ice keeps time.* The second lie was the river itself, which had spent three days pretending the upper route was closed, then opened it all at once with a crack like the sky splitting its knuckles.

Now the glacier calved in the distance — a sound Astrid felt in her back teeth more than heard, a deep tectonic groan rolling down the gorge like a god clearing his throat. Chunks of ice the size of chapel roofs sheared off the face and plunged into the flood-swollen water, and the wave that followed lifted the *Marta Cielo* and set her down again, gentle as a mother's hand, as if the river were sorry for what it was about to show them.

"Two-oh-six," Wren said beside her at the wheel, compass open in one palm, rain streaming off their hood. "Past that gray face on the left. There."

Astrid saw it. A seam in the ice — not blue, not white, but a bruised gray-green, the color of old glass, wide enough to take a rowboat if you didn't breathe wrong. Meltwater poured from it in a hundred silver threads, and beneath the threads, impossibly, there was light. A cold, sourceless glow, like moonlight that had gotten lost and decided to stay.

"That's not natural," said Dov from the stern, arms crossed over his chest. He'd been arguing for turning back since Mile Forty, since the hull split, since Thorn died coughing blood into a rag at dawn. "Caves in glaciers collapse. Whole thing could come down on us."

"It's been standing longer than your grandfather's been dead," Astrid said. She eased the throttle forward. "Thorn walked out of it. He walked out and lived another twenty years."

"He walked out and it killed him anyway," Dov muttered.

But nobody asked her to stop. That was the thing about the crew she'd gathered — half of them hired for a delivery run that had become something else entirely — they complained like rivermen and obeyed like pilgrims. Even Dov, who wanted only to sell the map and be done with it, even he leaned over the rail as they approached the seam, and his complaints went quiet.

The entrance swallowed them by degrees. First the rain stopped — cut off mid-fall, as though someone had shut a door above their heads. Then the sound of the gorge faded to a low, resonant hum, the glacier singing to itself in a register below hearing. The air changed too: colder, yes, but also *cleaner*, scrubbed of silt and diesel and the green rot of the monsoon forest, so pure it hurt to breathe, like breathing winter itself.

And the light. Astrid had no name for it. It came from everywhere and nowhere, refracted through centuries of compressed snow, and it turned everything it touched the color of drowned jewels.

"Captain," Wren said softly. They hadn't called her captain until recently. She wasn't sure when it had started, or why she'd let it. "Look at the walls."

She looked.

The ice was full of things. Not debris — *things*. A rusted lantern, whole and perfect, suspended three feet inside the wall as if the glacier had plucked it from some climber's hand and filed it away for study. A boot. A tin cup. A bird, frozen mid-flight, wings spread in a final elegant arc, its feathers still iridescent after what must have been a century. The glacier kept everything. The glacier forgot nothing.

"Where the ice keeps time," Astrid whispered. Thorn's words, and now she understood them not as poetry but as directions.

They tied off at a shelf of gravel the meltwater had deposited sometime in the last decade — a little beach inside the mountain, absurd and impossible — and Astrid stepped onto it with the map in an oilcloth pouch against her chest and a hurricane lamp in her hand. The flame barely mattered. The ice gave its own light, patient and total.

Ahead, the cave narrowed, then opened again, and through the opening came a smell that did not belong: lamp oil, paper, iron.

"Wren," she said. "You're with me. Dov, Sefa — hold the boat. If anything comes down that corridor that isn't us, cast off and don't look back."

"And leave you?" Sefa asked.

"Especially me." She met the young man's eyes. "That's not a joke. That's the order."

He didn't like it. He nodded anyway.

**Wren**

Wren had been inside syndicate vaults before. That was the confession they still hadn't made — not fully, not in words — though they suspected Astrid had assembled the truth from pieces: the way Wren's hands knew padlocks, the way they'd patched a split hull with the bored efficiency of someone who'd done salvage work that wasn't always legal, the way they flinched at black hulls on the water. They'd broken into places like this. They'd carried things out of places like this for men who paid in unmarked notes and never learned your name.

So Wren thought they knew what a vault looked like.

They were wrong.

The passage opened into a chamber the size of a cathedral, and the cathedral was *full*.

Shelves. Rows upon rows of them, cut directly into the living ice or built of salvaged timber gone silver with age, receding into the glowing depths farther than the lamplight could follow. And on the shelves — ledgers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Bound in leather, in canvas, in oilcloth; some fat as bricks, some thin as hymnals; spines stamped with years going back further than Wren could read without counting twice. Dust lay on them like a second binding, undisturbed, because nothing rotted here, nothing decayed, nothing was ever thrown away. The cold preserved everything. Including the crimes.

"Oh," Wren said. It came out very small.

Astrid stood ten feet ahead, lamp raised, and did not move. In the glow her face had gone strange — lit from below and within, carved out of shadow — and Wren watched her take it in the way you take in a landscape after a flood: slowly, because rushing would mean admitting the scale of what was lost.

"The Vasquez claim," Astrid said at last. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used on the river when the channel was narrowing. "It'll be here. Somewhere in all this."

"It'll be here," Wren agreed. "And so will mine."

Astrid turned. The lamp threw her shadow long across the shelves. "You've never told me your family name."

"No." Wren's throat was dry. The air tasted of iron and centuries. "I haven't."

They walked the aisles together, boots crunching on ancient grit, and the scale of it kept unfolding. Wren pulled one ledger at random — a squat volume bound in cracked green leather — and opened it to the first page. A family name. A river frontage measured in chains and rods. A date of seizure. A signature. On the next page, the same name again, and a figure: what the family had been paid, which was nothing, and what the syndicate had recorded paying them, which was a number so small it was clearly a joke told among thieves.

Another ledger. Another name. Another river, another valley, another inheritance converted to paper and locked in the dark where no one it belonged to would ever find it.

"They didn't just steal from families," Wren said. They heard their own voice shaking and couldn't stop it. "They stole the *proof*. If the records are gone, the claims are gone. You can't fight for what you can't prove existed. That's why nobody ever came back for any of this. That's why it worked."

Astrid had stopped at a shelf near the center of the chamber. She wasn't touching anything. Her hands hung at her sides, curled slightly, as if around a wheel.

"How many?" she said quietly. "How many names do you think?"

Wren looked down the aisle — shelf after shelf after shelf, vanishing into the blue-green dark — and did the arithmetic they didn't want to do.

"Hundreds," they said. "Maybe thousands. Whole valleys. Whole generations."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down like the glacier itself. Somewhere far above, muffled through a hundred feet of ice, the mountain groaned — the calving, ongoing, the slow demolition of the route they'd come in by — and the sound moved through the chamber and made the lamp flame shiver.

Astrid reached out at last and drew one ledger from the shelf. She held it the way you hold a sleeping child you're afraid of waking. She didn't open it. Wren saw her thumb move once across the cover, tracing letters worn nearly smooth, and knew without looking whose name it was.

"Vasquez," she said. Just the word. Just the name, spoken aloud in a place where no one had spoken aloud in decades, and the glacier took the sound and kept it, the way it kept the lantern and the bird and every other small, lost, precious thing.

"We should take it," Wren said. "Take yours, take mine, burn the rest—"

"Burn the rest?" Astrid's head came up sharply. "These are the only proof these families ever had. You want to destroy the evidence?"

"I want to destroy the *weapon*." Wren heard the old training in their own voice, the syndicate logic they'd sworn they'd shed, and hated it. "As long as this vault exists, the syndicate owns every one of those families. Proof cuts both ways, Astrid. This isn't a treasure. It's a hostage."

Astrid stared at them. The light made her eyes unreadable, two dark stones in a face of ice and shadow.

"You sound like you've had this argument before," she said.

"I *was* this argument," Wren said. "Before I read Thorn's letter. Before I found my family's name in it." Their hand rose, almost without permission, toward the shelves. "Row nine. Third shelf. There's a ledger with a blue spine. I checked while you were reading the letter at the homestead. I told myself I was keeping watch." A breath. "I wasn't keeping watch."

Astrid was quiet a long moment. Then she did something Wren didn't expect: she held out the Vasquez ledger, the one she'd taken down, and offered it.

"Then we read them," she said. "All of them, if we have to. Every name. We learn what was taken and from whom, and *then* we decide what burning means — whether it's mercy or just another theft." Her jaw tightened. "I've spent ten years running from my name, Wren. I'm not going to free it by erasing everyone else's."

Down the long aisles, the ledgers waited in their thousands, patient as the ice that held them. Wren took the book. It was heavier than it should have been, or maybe that was only the moment.

And then, from the direction of the entrance — faint, distorted by the humming throat of the cave, but unmistakable to anyone who'd grown up around black-hulled boats — came the sound of an engine.

Not their engine.

Astrid's head snapped up. The lamp flame steadied in her grip because her hands had gone perfectly, terribly still.

"They followed the calving," she breathed. "They know the route's open. They know *we* know."

The engine sound grew. Under it, thinner, came another sound: voices. Boots on gravel. Men who knew exactly where they were going, moving through the dark with the confidence of owners coming home.

Wren looked at the shelves. At the thousands of stolen lives stacked in the cold. At Astrid, already reaching for the map pouch, already calculating, already the captain.

"Astrid," Wren whispered. "There's no back way out of here."

"I know," she said. And smiled — a thin, fierce, river-made thing. "Then we'd better make one."
